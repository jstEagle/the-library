## 8. The Inquisitor's Bargain

The vault door had no lock.

Mara had expected iron, wards, a garrison of the king's men—some final barrier worthy of the Crown of Covenants. Instead, the passage simply ended in an archway of black stone veined with silver, and beyond it the air itself changed. It hummed. Not with sound but with memory, the way her mother's court had hummed before the Sundering, when every wall remembered the oaths spoken beneath it.

Felix stopped at the threshold, rain still dripping from his coat, and Mara watched him feel it too. His gift answered the vault the way a tuning fork answers its twin—a shiver running through his shoulders, lightning flickering pale beneath the skin of his wrists.

"It knows you," she said quietly.

"It knows what I am." He didn't look at her. "Storm-caller. The last thing my kind was ever good for, according to the king."

"The last thing your kind was ever *needed* for," she corrected. "There is a difference."

He almost smiled. In the weeks since the ridge—since she had put a blade of hardened wind to his throat and demanded her brother's map—they had built something fragile between them, word by word, fire by shared fire. She did not have a name for it. Wraiths were not made for naming things. But when he stepped through the archway first, placing his body between her and whatever waited in the dark, she understood that he had already named it, even if neither of them would say it aloud.

The vault opened like the inside of a thunderhead.

Walls of storm-glass rose on every side, and within them moved shapes—clouds pressed flat and kept, lightning coiled like sleeping serpents, the captured breath of a sky that had once been a kingdom. And there, at the center, upon a plinth of fused sand where lightning had struck stone into glass, sat the Crown of Covenants.

It was smaller than Mara had dreamed. A circlet of silver branches, thin as frost, set with stones that were not stones at all but drops of solidified storm—each one holding a small eternal weather of its own. Rain that fell forever inside a single bead. Snow that never landed. A tiny sun trapped mid-simmer.

Her brother had died carrying the way to this. Her father had been unmade defending it. And here it sat, patient as grief, waiting for hands worth wearing it.

Mara reached out—

"Touch it, and I burn this place to glass."

The voice came from everywhere and nowhere, the way voices do in rooms built from storm. Mara spun. Felix's hands came up, sparks crawling along his knuckles.

Inquisitor Seraphine Vael stepped out of the storm-glass wall as though it were a curtain. She was not what Mara had expected. She was young—not much older than Felix—with close-cropped hair and the gray uniform of the King's Question worn without ornament, without pride, without anything at all. Her face was the face of someone who had burned out everything behind her eyes years ago and now ran only on procedure.

Behind her, filling the archway, came six more—inquisitors in gray, crossbows of storm-iron leveled, their bolts fletched with feathers dipped in salt and consecrated ash. Bolts made to kill things like Mara.

"You've been walking my map for two months," Vael said. Her gaze settled on Felix with something that might, in another life, have been respect. "Every waystation you slept in, I knew within a day. Every smuggler who fed you, I questioned. You are not good at being invisible anymore, Blackwood. Exile has made you sentimental."

"And it's made you predictable," Felix said. "You let us come all this way. Why?"

"Because the Crown cannot be carried out by force." Vael walked slowly around the plinth, her boots silent on the glass floor, and Mara tracked her the way a hawk tracks a snake. "It chooses. Or rather—it *judges*. My king learned that thirty years ago, when he tried to take it and it seared the flesh from his arm down to the bone. It will only pass willingly to those both realms would accept. So I needed you to reach it. I needed you to *want* it." She stopped. "And then I needed you to hand it to me."

"You're mad," Mara said. "It will never accept your king. He broke the covenant. He stole it. The Crown remembers."

"The Crown remembers nothing," Vael said. "People remember. People testify. And testimony can be arranged." She turned back to Felix, and now her voice shifted—softer, almost gentle, which was somehow worse. "Here is my offer, Blackwood. Full pardon. Restored standing. Your name cleared in every court from Saltgate to the capital. Your exile ended—not tolerated, *ended*. You walk back into the world a free man, a decorated man. All you must do is pick up that Crown and give it to me."

Mara felt the trap close before Felix said a word, because she could see it working on him—the terrible arithmetic of it. Freedom. Everything he had lost. Everything he'd told her about beside a hundred fires: the trial he never got, the order to call storms down on a rebel village, the choice that had cost him his name and his home and eleven years of rain-soaked anonymity.

*He will take it,* she thought, and hated how much the thought hurt. *Of course he will take it. He owes me nothing. He owes the storm realm less.*

"Felix," she said carefully, "you don't have to—"

"I know what I don't have to do." He hadn't taken his eyes off Vael. Lightning moved under his skin like something waking. "You killed Dain Hollow, in Greymoss. The old smuggler. You strung him up in the town square for giving me bread."

"He harbored a fugitive. The law is clear."

"The law is clear," Felix repeated. He laughed, and there was no humor in it, only a low rolling sound like distant thunder. "Eleven years I told myself the same thing. Stay small. Stay out of it. Let the world break whatever it breaks, so long as it isn't me." He looked at the Crown—at Mara—and something in his face settled, the way a storm settles just before it decides to become a catastrophe. "I'm done being clear."

Vael's expression did not change. "Then you refuse the pardon."

"I refuse *you*." Felix stepped forward, between Vael and the plinth, between the crossbows and Mara. "This Crown goes to the heir of the storm realm or it goes to no one. That's not a bargain. That's the whole point of it."

For one heartbeat, the vault was perfectly silent. Even the trapped weathers seemed to hold their breath.

Then Vael sighed, and Mara saw it—the flicker behind her eyes, cold and quick as a fish under ice. She had known. She had always known. The offer had never been an offer; it had been a mirror, held up to see what Felix Blackwood would do when offered his old life back.

"Take her," Vael said.

The inquisitors moved. Not toward Felix.

Toward Mara.

She should have seen it. She cursed herself even as the net of consecrated chain came whistling out of the dark—storm-iron links etched with binding-script, the same chains her people had been shackled in during the Purge. She twisted into mist, but the script caught her, pulled her back into flesh, and the first bolt took her through the shoulder. Pain like white fire. She went down on one knee among the glass roots of the plinth, blood spotting the floor, and heard Felix roar—a sound with weather in it, a sound that shook dust from the vault ceiling.

"No—" Vael's voice cracked like a whip. "Stand down, Blackwood, or the next bolt takes her heart. Storm-iron. Consecrated. You know what it does to her kind. You've seen the wounds."

Mara lifted her head. Blood ran warm down her arm. Through the haze of pain she found Felix's face, and braced herself for what she would see there: the calculation, the retreat, the exile's ancient instinct to survive. She had watched men make that choice her entire long half-life. She would watch it now, and she would understand, and she would hate him a little forever, and the realm would go on breaking.

Felix looked at her.

And she saw none of it.

What she saw instead was the man on the ridge who had stood between her and the inquisitors' arrows before they'd ever struck a truce. The man who had carried her when the salt-marsh fever took her legs. The man who had said, three nights ago, in a voice too quiet for anyone but her: *I spent eleven years learning what I could live without. Turns out it wasn't much.*

His hands came down. The lightning died.

"Let her go," Felix said, "and I'll give you the Crown."

"*Felix, no—*" The words tore out of Mara rawer than the wound.

"Quiet." He didn't look at her. He couldn't—or wouldn't—and that, more than the chains, more than the blood, told her exactly what it was costing him. "Vael. You have your leverage. Use it."

Vael studied him for a long moment, head tilted, and Mara realized with a fresh wave of horror that the inquisitor was *savoring* this—that somewhere beneath the procedural gray there lived a small cold thing that enjoyed watching good people fold.

"Drop your gift," Vael said. "All of it. Kneel. Bind your own hands—I'm sure you remember how we do it."

Felix knelt. The most dangerous storm-caller in the human realms knelt on the glass floor of the vault and began winding the binding-cord around his own wrists, and Mara screamed at him, at all of them, at the universe that kept writing endings like this one.

"That's enough," Vael said softly. "Now stand, walk to the plinth, and lift the Crown. Slowly."

Felix rose. He walked past Mara close enough that she could smell the rain still in his hair, and though his face was carved from surrender, his eyes—for one unguarded instant—were not. They were calm. They were certain. They were the eyes of a man who had already won a game no one else could see.

*Trust me*, those eyes said.

*You absolute fool*, she wanted to answer. *You beautiful, impossible fool.*

He reached the plinth. He wrapped his bound hands around the Crown of Covenants, and the vault exhaled—a vast, slow sigh through every wall of storm-glass, as if the whole room leaned in to witness.

The Crown did not burn him.

It flared—silver light pouring between his fingers, the trapped weathers waking all at once, rain and snow and sun swirling up the walls—and Mara felt it in her bones, in the old royal blood the wraith-flesh barely contained: the Crown was *reading* him. Weighing him. Asking its ancient question of the kneeling traitor with the prisoner's cord on his wrists.

Vael smiled for the first time. It did not reach anywhere near her eyes.

"Lift it," she commanded. "Hand it to me. And your pardon begins tonight."

Felix raised the Crown above his head.

And Mara, chained and bleeding on the floor of her ancestors' vault, saw the lightning gather in his bound hands—and understood, one heartbeat too late, that Felix Blackwood had never intended to surrender anything at all.

* * *

The light went white.

Not the silver of the Crown's judgment—white, blinding, total, the color of the sky the instant before it splits. Mara threw her chained arm across her face as the vault filled with the roar of a storm given permission, at last, after eleven years of leash and law, to be what it was.

When her vision cleared, Felix was standing.

The binding-cord hung from his wrists in ashes. The Crown blazed on his brow like a captive dawn, and every drop of solidified storm in the walls had broken free—rain falling upward, snow burning blue, lightning swimming through the air like fish through water. The six inquisitors were scattered against the far wall, groaning, their consecrated bolts slagged to uselessness in their quivers.

Only Vael had kept her feet. Only Vael had thrown up a ward in time—and even her ward was cracking, spiderwebbing, shedding light like a shattered mirror.

"You lied to me," Vael snarled, and for the first time Mara heard something human in her: fear.

"I offered you the truth," Felix said. The Crown's light ran down his arms and lit him from within. "You just preferred the bargain." He raised one hand, and the storm in the vault gathered to him like wolves to a whistle. "You took a hostage to control me. Bad arithmetic, Inquisitor. You should have asked what happens to a storm-caller's power when he stops being afraid of losing everything."

He turned his head—just slightly—toward Mara, and the ghost of a smile crossed his face.

"Now," Felix Blackwood said, "let's talk about justice."

The vault doors blew inward, and the storm came for them all.
