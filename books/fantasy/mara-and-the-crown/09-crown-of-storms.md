## 9. Crown of Storms

The vault doors were older than the kingdom that had built them.

Felix felt it the moment his palm met the stone—something vast and patient humming beneath the rock, like a held breath that had lasted three hundred years. The Crown of Covenants lay somewhere beyond, behind doors no human hand had opened since the old king stole it away, and every step he took toward it rang through him like a struck bell.

Above, in the high gallery of the vault's antechamber, the lead inquisitor watched him come.

Ser Aldric Vane had shed nothing of his authority in the crossing. His grey coat was immaculate despite the storm realm's ceaseless weather, his boots dry where Felix's were soaked through. He stood with his hands folded behind his back, a man waiting for a delivery rather than a fight, and that—more than the six crossbows trained on the stairwell—told Felix how thoroughly they had been expected.

"Blackwood." Vane's voice carried without effort, pitched to fill the chamber the way a preacher fills a nave. "You've made this remarkably easy. I confess I expected more running."

Felix kept climbing. The stairs were basalt veined with something that glowed faint gold when lightning crawled across the distant ceiling of the realm—the old covenant-light, Mara had called it, dying but not yet dead. Each vein he passed flickered at his approach, as if recognizing something in his blood.

"You have a pardon in your pocket," Felix said. "I could hear you rehearsing it from the pass."

"I have a *restoration* in my pocket." Vane descended one step, unhurried. "Your exile, lifted. Your name cleared. Your gift licensed under crown charter instead of hunted by it. Every storm-caller who came after you would owe their safety to your example." He smiled, thin and reasonable. "All of that, for one artifact placed in the king's hands. You'd be a hero, Blackwood. The kind they write songs about."

Below them both, at the foot of the stair, Mara hung between two wardens whose chains ran with suppression-glyphs, her storm-grey eyes fixed on Felix with an expression he had learned to read across a thousand miles of mountain: *don't you dare.*

He dared to look at her anyway.

She was fading again. He could see it—the edges of her going translucent where the glyphs bit into her skin, the living lightning of her blood dimming to embers. In the storm realm she should have been blazing, whole, more than wraith and less than ghost and entirely *alive*, and instead they had caged her light like a lamp under a cloth. Because they knew what she was now. Last royal blood of the storm court. The heir the human kings had spent three centuries pretending didn't exist.

"They'll never stop hunting you either," Vane said, following his gaze with clinical interest. "You understand that. A man who can call storms is only ever one bad harvest away from being a weapon or a corpse. We're offering you the chance to be neither."

"The last man who offered me that," Felix said quietly, "had my teacher hanged from his own lightning rod."

He reached the top of the stair.

Vane sighed, and the reasonable mask went away all at once, the way a pleasant sky goes away before a squall. What remained underneath was older and colder. "I was hoping for the song version," he said. "Take him."

* * *

The first bolt caught Felix in the shoulder and threw him across the antechamber floor.

He rolled with it—years of smuggler's instinct, taking the hit where it cost least—and came up with lightning already crawling along his knuckles. The wardens advanced in a wedge, crossbows reloaded with practiced speed, and above them Vane watched with the detached attention of a man timing a race.

Felix had spent seven years not doing this. Seven years of swallowing the pressure that lived behind his sternum, of walking away from fights, of letting small cruelties pass because intervening meant being seen, and being seen meant everything he'd built burning down around him. Neutrality had a body count—he'd learned that in a mining town over a dead friend—and he was done paying it.

He let go.

The storm came out of him the way water comes out of a broken dam—not graceful, not controlled, but *his*, answering him the way it always had, the way it had answered no one else since the covenant broke. It tore the nearest warden off his feet and slammed him into the wall. It wrapped the second man's crossbow in a corona of blue fire until he dropped it screaming. It turned the antechamber into the inside of a thunderhead, wind screaming, gold veins in the floor flaring brighter than they had in three centuries as if the vault itself remembered what a storm-caller's oath felt like.

Through it all, Vane walked forward.

That was the terrible thing about him, Felix thought, ducking a bolt and driving a fistful of lightning into a third warden's shield. Vane didn't flinch. He moved through Felix's storm like a man wading a river he'd forded a hundred times, coat snapping, face serene, and when he was close enough he drew a long blade of grey metal that drank the light rather than reflecting it.

"Cold-iron and nullstone," Vane said. "Forged for exactly one purpose. Your teacher died surprised, Blackwood. You won't."

The blade took Felix across the ribs before he finished turning. Not deep—a line of white fire—but where it touched, his storm *stopped*, snuffed like a pinched wick, and he staggered back gasping against the vault door itself, feeling its ancient hum die under his palms.

Vane raised the sword.

And the chains shattered.

It wasn't lightning. It was something deeper—a crack that ran through the floor, through the walls, through the marrow of the mountain, and every glyph-carved chain in the antechamber burst at once into drifting ash. Mara came off the floor like a rising gale, and for the first time Felix saw her as she must have looked three hundred years ago: fully alight, hair streaming with static, eyes twin cores of stormlight, the last heir of a broken realm wearing her grief like armor.

Vane turned—fast, for a man in a grey coat—and got one hand toward her.

Mara didn't strike him. She simply passed through his guard the way weather passes through an open window, took him by the collar, and put him into the far wall hard enough to crack the basalt. The nullstone sword clattered from his fingers. He slumped, dazed, alive—she left him alive, and Felix understood even then what it cost her, what mercy chosen over revenge always costs.

Then she pressed her hand to the vault door, and the door that had refused kings for three hundred years opened for its queen.

* * *

Inside, the Crown of Covenants floated above a pedestal of black glass, turning slowly, shedding light the color of dawn through rain.

It was smaller than Felix had imagined. A circlet of silver and storm-glass, delicate as frost, humble almost—and beneath it, carved into the pedestal in the old script of both realms, the terms of its binding. Mara read them aloud, her voice unsteady for the first time since he'd known her.

"*Two anchors, one crown. One soul of the storm, freely given. One soul of the earth, freely given. Bound in life, bound past death, until the realms forget their names.*"

She reached for it and stopped, hand trembling an inch from the silver.

"It needs a second," she said. "It always needed a second. That's why the old king's theft broke everything—he took the Crown but there was no anchor willing on either side, so the covenant just... bled out." She turned to Felix, and her stormlight eyes were wet. "I can bind myself. I'm the last of the royal line; the storm side is mine to give. But the earth anchor has to be *willing*. Has to be someone who chooses it knowing what it means." Her voice dropped. "It means the borderlands, Felix. Forever. You can't leave. You can't run. Everything you built your whole life around—your invisibility, your freedom—it ends at that altar. And I won't ask it. I won't. I've watched enough people be sacrificed to crowns."

The Crown turned slowly above them, patient as weather.

Felix thought of Saltgate, of rain-slick rooftops and a dying stranger's blood soaking into his sleeve. Of a waystation he'd never see again, of a life measured in safe distances and closed doors. Of neutrality, and its body count. Of a woman made of lightning who had every reason to hate his kind and had chosen mercy twice in one night.

Seven years he had run from being seen. He found, standing here, that he was tired of being invisible.

"Mara," he said, and stepped forward without hesitation, without ceremony, the way he did everything that actually mattered. "Shut up and crown us."

She laughed—or sobbed, it was impossible to tell—and lifted the Crown from its pedestal, and the moment her fingers closed around the silver the whole vault lit up like the inside of a star, and somewhere far below, in the human world, every storm-cloud on the horizon turned gold—

—and Ser Aldric Vane, bleeding quietly in the ruins of the antechamber, began to laugh too, because the vault doors had sealed themselves shut, and on the black glass of the pedestal, new words were rising in the old script, words neither of them had read:

*The binding requires a witness from each realm. Let those who broke the covenant answer for it.*

Outside, faint but growing, came the sound of boots. Many boots. And a voice Felix recognized—the young inquisitor who had hesitated at the mining town, the one who had looked away when the order was given—calling out, uncertain, to whoever was inside:

"The Crown is awake. Both realms felt it. We... we need to know what happens now."

Mara set the Crown in Felix's hands, stormlight and silver, heavier than any smuggler's cargo he had ever carried.

"Now," she said, "we teach them."
