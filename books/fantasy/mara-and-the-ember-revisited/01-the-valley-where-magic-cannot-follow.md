## 1. The Valley Where Magic Cannot Follow

There are three things you should know about Hollowmere before I tell you anything else.

First: the fog comes down off the rim of the valley every morning like an old dog settling onto a porch, and it does not leave until it pleases. Second: the dead here are polite. They whisper, they do not shout, and they never—never—ask about the world outside. Third: I have lived here thirty years, and in all that time not one drop of magic has managed to slip past the valley's edge and touch me. Whether that is a blessing or a sentence depends on the day, and on how much tea I've had.

I am Beatrix Winterbourne, and I carve bones for the dead.

Not *of* the dead—*for* them. There is a difference, and the difference is my whole livelihood, such as it is. When someone dies in Hollowmere—and they die here the way they do everywhere, slowly or suddenly, but mostly slowly—their family brings me a bone. A rib, usually, or a knucklebone if the family was poor and the rib was wanted for the cairn. And I sit at my bench with my knives and my burins and my little brass calipers, and I carve the dead person's name into that bone, along with whatever last words they left behind.

Because that is the other thing about me. The bones talk.

Not while the person is alive, mind you. Not while the body is even warm. But somewhere between the last breath and the first shovelful of earth, a bone will pick up what the dying carried out with them—the final word, the unfinished sentence, the name called into a dark room—and hold it the way amber holds a fly. I don't know why. I stopped asking why around my fortieth year, when I realized that asking why was a door, and doors open from both sides.

I carve the words into the bone so they can't leak out. That's the honest version. The flattering version—which I tell the families, because the honest version makes widows cry—is that I preserve them. Honor them. Give the dead their say, permanent as stone.

Both versions are true. Most truths are just two lies holding hands.

* * *

On the morning this story properly begins—a morning in late autumn, when the fog was thick enough to spread on bread—I was working on the rib of a woman named Hesta Marlowe, who had died at eighty-one with her hand in her daughter's hand and, according to the daughter, a look on her face like she'd just remembered where she'd put something important.

The rib said otherwise.

Bones don't lie, which is inconvenient, because people ask them to constantly. What Hesta Marlowe's rib held was not tenderness. It was a single word, spoken with the particular sharpness of a woman who had waited sixty years to say it:

*"Finally."*

I carved it anyway. It wasn't my place to edit. Though I did take the liberty of making the letters small, so that anyone reading them would have to lean close, and leaning close to a bone that says *finally* teaches you something about marriage that no sermon can.

"You're scowling," said the skull on my shelf.

I should explain the skull. His name was Aldous—or rather, the name carved into his occipital plate was Aldous, and since he'd been dead for eleven years and had long since stopped correcting me, Aldous he remained. He had been the valley's schoolmaster, and he had died mid-lesson, and his last word was *"again,"* which meant either that he'd been exasperated by a student or that he'd loved teaching so much he wanted to repeat it forever. I have always chosen to believe the second. He has never confirmed or denied. Dead men are excellent at keeping their mysteries; it's their opinions they can't hold onto.

"I'm not scowling," I told him. "I'm concentrating."

"Concentrating and scowling are the same thing on your face. You should know. You've had fifty years to study it."

"Fifty-three." I blew a curl of bone dust off the rib. "And you're one to talk. You've had eleven years of nothing but your own face to study, and you still haven't learned to keep quiet."

"That's because keeping quiet is for the living," Aldous said, "and I have retired from that profession with considerable relief."

This was our usual morning. Carving, dust, banter. If you had told me at twenty-five that this would be my life—that I would grow old in a valley the world forgot, arguing with a skull over tea I couldn't share with him—I would have laughed. Or wept. At twenty-five those were closer together than most people admit.

But that was before. Before is a country I don't visit.

* * *

Here is what I know about the outside world, in its entirety:

It exists.

That's it. That's the whole inventory. People come up the pass sometimes—peddlers, once a year, when the frost lets them; a surveyor maybe twice a decade; the occasional young person leaving for reasons they announce loudly and return for reasons they don't mention at all. From these travelers I accept payment, salt, lamp oil, and news of weather. Everything else they offer—kings, wars, new gods, old scandals, the price of silk in cities whose names I make a point of forgetting—I decline with the courtesy of a woman declining a second helping of something she suspects is poisoned.

"Beatrix," the peddler Tam Voss said to me once, years ago, spreading his wares across my table like a man laying out a bet, "there's a whole world up there. Ember-priests doing miracles in the capital. The Ashen Court with their grey hounds. Kingdoms rising, kingdoms falling. Don't you want to know any of it?"

"No," I said.

"Not even a little?"

"Tam." I picked up his bolt of blue cloth, felt its weight, set it down. "If a fire is burning somewhere far away, knowing about it does not warm me. It only teaches me to be cold here. I am content being warm."

He'd looked at me for a long moment with an expression I pretended not to see—the expression people get when they realize someone isn't brave or foolish but simply *closed*, like a house with every shutter nailed fast—and then he'd sold me the cloth and gone on his way.

Content. Yes. I have used that word so many times it has worn smooth as a river stone. Content with my bench and my knives. Content with the fog and the polite dead. Content with a life measured out in ribs and knucklebones, in names carved small enough to require leaning close.

And if some nights I woke with my heart going like a trapped bird, and a grief in my chest so large and so shapeless that I could not have told you what it mourned—if I lay in the dark cataloguing everything I had and found the sum somehow short, the way a purse feels light before you count it and find every coin accounted for—if that happened, well. Fog gets into everything. Even houses. Even hearts.

I told myself it was the fog.

* * *

Midday brought the Marris boy with a basket and a job. Colm Marr is fourteen, freckled, and possesses the unshakable conviction that death is primarily an administrative matter, which I consider a sign of great future wisdom.

"Gran's rib," he said, setting the basket down with more care than he gave anything else in his life. "She went Tuesday night. Da says whenever you're ready, but Ma says soon, because Gran talked enough when she was alive and nobody wants her going on in the box."

"Your grandmother," I said, lifting the rib out, "sounds like she and I would have gotten along."

"Oh, you'd have hated each other," Colm said cheerfully. "You're both certain you're right."

He stayed to watch, because he always stays to watch, because children always stay to watch, because there is nothing in this world so magnetic to a child as a grown adult doing precise work with sharp tools. I set the rib in the clamp and took up my finest burin and listened.

Most bones give up their words easily. A whisper, a phrase, occasionally a full sentence if the dying had time and the dying person was the sort who'd never wasted breath in life. Gran Marr's rib gave up four words, and they came slow, like a woman climbing stairs:

*"Tell him... it wasn't... his fault."*

I paused with the burin against the bone.

"Colm," I said carefully. "Who's 'him'?"

The boy shrugged. "Da, probably? Gran used to go quiet whenever Da came in the room. For years. Ma says it was the thing with the boat, but nobody talks about the boat."

"The boat."

"There was a boat. Before I was born. Uncle Wendel was on it." He said it the way children say things they've been told not to ask about—with total fluency and zero curiosity, a parrot of other people's silences.

I looked down at the rib. *Tell him it wasn't his fault.* Sixty years, maybe, that man had carried a boat on his back. And here was the cure, sitting in my workshop, waiting for a burin and a steady hand—and I could carve it, yes, but carving preserves a word, it doesn't deliver it. The dead get their say. The living have to come and read.

"Tell your father," I said, "that when the carving's done, he should come see it himself. Tell him I insist."

"He won't come. Da doesn't come here."

"Then tell him his mother insists."

Colm considered this with the gravity of a general weighing terrain. "That might work," he admitted. "Da's scared of Gran even now."

"Good," I said. "Fear of the dead is the beginning of listening to them."

Which is a thing I said, and meant, and did not examine too closely, because buried under it was another thing entirely: that I had spent thirty years making sure no dead person could ever say anything to *me*. I carved their words away from the world like a woman building levees. Every name I engraved was a small wall. Every finished piece, laid in its niche in the memorial hall, was one more voice sealed behind stone.

Thirty years of walls. You'd think I'd have noticed I was building a cell.

Evening came down with the fog, as it does, and I lit the lamps and fed the stove and did the small arithmetic of a small life: two carvings finished, one pending, flour enough for ten days, tea enough for six if I stretched it, which I would, because stretching tea is practically a civic duty in Hollowmere.

Aldous watched me from his shelf. He watches best in lamplight; I suspect the shadows bore him.

"You were quiet today," he said. "Quieter than usual, and your usual is a monastic vow."

"I'm fine."

"Mm. In my teaching days, 'fine' was what boys said right before they put a frog in the inkwell. Fine is never fine. Fine is a lid."

"Then appreciate the lid," I said, "because underneath it is nothing you want."

He was quiet a moment. Skulls are very good at dramatic pauses; they have nothing else to do with their faces.

"You know," he said at last, "in eleven years, you have never once asked me what my last lesson was. The one I died giving. 'Again,' the bone says—but again *what*, Beatrix? Arithmetic? Verse conjugation? You've never asked."

"And you've never told me."

"Because you haven't asked. There's a contract between us, you and I. You don't dig, I don't spill. It's worked beautifully." The lamplight moved on his brow ridge, which in a living face would have been a raised eyebrow. "But I'll tell you what worries me about contracts like ours. They only work until something arrives that doesn't respect them."

"Something like what?"

"I don't know," Aldous said. "That's what worries me."

I laughed at him—kindly, the way you laugh at an old man who enjoys his own ominousness—and banked the stove, and went to bed in my narrow house above the workshop, in my narrow bed, under a roof I had built myself from timber I had felled myself, in a valley that kept the world out and, I had always assumed, kept me safe.

I slept the way I always slept: heavily, dreamlessly, a coin dropped into a well.

* * *

The knock came three weeks later, on the winter solstice, in the dead season, when the fog froze to the trees and the whole valley rang like a struck bell if you walked through it.

But I am getting ahead of myself, and a story deserves to be told in order, the way bones deserve to be carved letter by letter, even when the letters say things you'd rather they didn't.

Let me instead end this part where it ought to end: with the ordinary evening before the extraordinary one. With tea stretched thin and the stove banked low and Aldous muttering conjugations to himself in the dark, because even after eleven years he could not sleep, having forgotten how. With the fog pressing its grey shoulder against my windows. With me, Beatrix Winterbourne, fifty-three years old, alone by choice and contented by practice, whittling the last curl of bone-dust off a dead woman's forgiveness and thinking, as I thought every night, *this is enough, this has always been enough.*

The dead in my workshop whispered their small preserved words. *Finally. Again. It wasn't his fault.* Thirty years of voices, all of them sealed, all of them safe, none of them mine.

Outside, far beyond the valley's rim, in a country I had made myself blind to, something had already begun to move.

But that, as I said, is the next part.
