## 2. A Knock in the Dead Season

The dead are terrible conversationalists, if you want the truth of it.

People imagine that hearing the last words of the departed is some great gift—final wisdom, dying secrets, the accumulated regret of a lifetime distilled into a whisper. What they don't imagine is that most people's last words are *pass the salt* or *I should have bought the brown boots* or, in the case of old Tam Wicker whose femur I carved every spring equinox for eleven years running, *the goat did it on purpose.*

The goat had done it on purpose. I believed him. Goats have that look.

So when I say the winter solstice began like any other day in Hollowmere, understand what that means: it meant I woke before dawn in a house built against the hillside, banked my fire, ate bread that was mostly rye and entirely stale, and walked through the frost to my workshop where forty-one bones waited on their shelves, each one humming its small dead song. The valley was white and silent. The mountains held the sky up the way they always did, patient as old men at a funeral they've attended too many times. Nothing moved except smoke from my chimney and, far off, a fox making its living.

This was my whole world, and I want to be clear: I had chosen it. Chosen it the way you choose a warm coat in winter, which is to say completely, and without ever once asking yourself what you might be choosing *against*.

Hollowmere sits in a fold of the mountains where magic does not go. I didn't know why, not precisely—I knew the shape of the fact without its reasons, the way you know a scar without remembering the fall. Ember-magic, hearth-craft, the old covenant bindings, all the glittering machinery of the living world beyond the passes: none of it crossed our boundary stones. Priests who came up the road found their charms went dull as river pebbles. Mages found their spells slipping off them like rain off oiled wool. They always left looking vaguely insulted, as though the valley had declined them personally.

Good, I say. Let the world keep its fire. We had woodsmoke and turnips and each other's business, which in a village of two hundred souls is more than enough entertainment.

And I had the bones.

Carving memorial bones is honest work. When someone dies in Hollowmere—or when their family sends down from the high farms a relic of someone long dead—they bring it to me, and I carve the name into the bone with a graver my own master taught me to hold, and while I carve, the bone speaks. Just once. Just the last words, whatever the dying mouth had been holding when it let go. I write them in the ledger. Sometimes the family wants to know. Mostly they don't. Mostly the words are for me, and after thirty years I have a workshop full of voices that will never say anything else, and I have made my peace with being the only ear they will ever have.

That morning, the first thing wrong was the silence.

I noticed it the way you notice a missing tooth—with your tongue, so to speak, before your mind catches up. I stood in the doorway with my coat half-buttoned and listened to nothing. Forty-one bones on their shelves, and not one of them murmuring. No Tam Wicker confessing his goat crimes. No grandmother Aldsey complaining for the ten-thousandth time about her daughter's choice of husband, a complaint she had apparently carried into death with both hands. Nothing. The workshop felt like a room where everyone had left just before I entered, and I could almost feel the draft of the door swinging shut behind them.

"Bones," I said, because I talk to my work the way lonely people talk to anything, "if this is a joke, it's a poor one."

Nothing answered. In thirty years, nothing had ever failed to answer.

I set down my satchel. I took down the nearest bone—a shin, belonging to a shepherd named Corren who had frozen on the high pasture twenty years back—and turned it over in my hands. Clean. Warm, even, though the workshop was cold enough to see my breath. A bone that has spoken goes cool and stays cool; it becomes only bone. This one was warm as a sleeping bird, and silent as one too, and when I held it to my ear the way I'd done ten thousand times, I heard something I had never heard in any bone, living or dead:

A hum. Faint, rhythmic, rising and falling. Not words. Not quite music. Something older than either—the sound a language makes when it's being thought rather than spoken, the ghost of speech, speech's grandmother.

I put the shin back on its shelf with hands that were not entirely steady, and told myself the sensible things. Told myself the cold was playing tricks. Told myself I was getting old, that thirty years of listening to the dead would make anyone start hearing songs in the quiet. Told myself that today was the solstice, the longest night, the day the old wives said the veil wore thin—which was nonsense, of course, because there was no veil in Hollowmere, no magic at all, that was the entire point of the place—

Which was when someone knocked on my door.

* * *

Nobody knocks on my door.

Let me amend that: nobody knocks on my door *in the dead season*. In summer, families come with their relics and their griefs. In autumn, the high farms send down their dead year's bones by mule cart. But between first snow and thaw, the pass closes, the village hunkers down like a bear in a den, and Beatrix Winterbourne sees no one but herself and forty-one chatty corpses. That is the arrangement. That has been the arrangement since I came down out of the mountains thirty years ago with a graver, a ledger, and a grief I did not discuss, and settled into the one place in the world where certain kinds of power could not follow.

The knock came again. Small. Almost polite. The knock of someone who had knocked on many doors and been disappointed by most of them.

I opened it, and the cold came in like a debt collector, and standing in it—standing in snow to her knees, because the drifts had risen around her while she stood there waiting, and how long had she been standing there?—was a child.

Six years old, perhaps seven. Small for either. Wrapped in a cloak that had once been fine and was now only ruined, grey wool gone stiff with ice, hem burned in places—not torn, *burned*, melted into hard glassy beads, as though it had been near a fire hot enough to make stone of wool. Her lips were blue. Her fingers were worse. Frost had climbed her cheeks in white fern-patterns, and her eyes—

Her eyes were the color of embers. Not brown. Embers. There was light in them that moved, that breathed, that had no business being in a child's face on a doorstep in Hollowmere, where no such light could exist.

She looked up at me. She did not shiver, which frightened me more than the eyes. A child half-frozen should shiver; the body insists. Hers had simply... stopped asking. As though she had been cold so long her body had given up its complaints and settled into endurance, the way old soldiers stand at attention.

"Well," I said, because someone had to say something, and it was clearly going to be me. "You'd better come in before the mountain decides to keep you."

She didn't move. She looked past me, into the house, then at the workshop door across the yard, and her head tilted—a bird's motion, quick and precise—and from her throat came a sound.

Not crying. Not words. A hum. Rising and falling, rhythmic, patient.

The same hum the bone had sung.

Every hair on my arms stood up under three layers of wool. Because here is the thing I have not yet told you, the thing I understood in that moment with the clarity of ice water poured down the spine: the hum was not a tune. It was a *language*. Or the shape of one, the breath of one, a tongue so old it had worn its words smooth the way water wears stone—and my bones recognized it. All of them. Through the workshop wall, through the cold air, I felt forty-one dead voices lean toward that sound like flowers toward sun, straining toward a language older than any kingdom in the world, older than the kingdoms whose graves had fed them to me.

Then the child stepped forward, past me, into my house, and closed her own door behind her with a small decisive click, and the hum stopped, and the silence that followed was the deepest I had ever heard.

* * *

Here is what I know about frostbite, because I live alone in the mountains and have had occasion to learn: you warm a body slowly. You do not plunge it into heat. You give it warmth the way you give a starving man food—in small mercies, patiently, or you kill them with kindness literally.

So I sat the child by my fire wrapped in my second-best blanket, and I warmed milk with honey because she was too far gone to chew, and I worked her fingers free of the ice with my own hands, rubbing life back into them finger by finger while she watched me with those ember-eyes and made no sound at all.

Mute, I decided. Or struck silent. There was no fear in her muteness—that was the strange part. Children who cannot speak still *try*, still gesture and point and make the desperate noises of the unheard. This child simply didn't speak the way stones don't fly. It wasn't an absence. It was a discipline. As though her voice were a thing kept locked away, precious and dangerous, and she was its keeper.

I talked enough for both of us. It's a habit of mine; the dead are poor company and the living scarcer, so I narrate my days to myself like a woman writing letters to no one. "There. Fingers first, then toes, and don't give me that look, I've done this before, though admittedly not on anyone who was breathing." She blinked at "breathing" as if it were a curious word. "Milk. Drink. Slowly. If you vomit into my fire I'm putting you back outside."

She drank. She did not vomit. We were making progress.

While she warmed, I studied her, because studying things is my trade. The burned cloak—glass-beaded hems, and now that I looked, the same melting along the cuffs, the pattern of exposure radiating outward from her *body*, as though she herself had been the heat source. The frost-ferns on her cheeks, already fading in the warmth, leaving skin beneath that was unmarked and pale as birchwood. And under the blanket, when she shifted, a glimpse of something at her throat: a cord, and on the cord a small dark shape, some kind of pendant or talisman, which she covered with her hand the moment my eyes touched it. Quick as a pickpocket. Decisive as a closing door.

"No, then," I said mildly. "Fair enough. Everyone's entitled to one secret. I've got several dozen myself."

She looked at me then—really looked, the ember-light in her eyes guttering like a flame deciding something—and lifted her hand, slow and deliberate, and pointed at my workshop.

"No," I said.

She pointed again.

"It's full of dead people's bones, child. It's no place for—" I stopped, because I was about to say *a girl your age*, and the sentence collapsed under its own absurdity. What age was a girl with embers for eyes who walked out of a blizzard without shivering? What place was safe for her? Certainly not anywhere I could name.

She slid off the chair. She crossed the room on legs that wobbled but did not quit, and she stood before the workshop door, and she laid her palm flat against the wood, and she hummed—one note, low, sustained, a note that I felt in my teeth and in the floorboards and somewhere behind my sternum.

From inside the workshop, forty-one bones began, very softly, to sing back.

I have heard the dead whisper their last words for thirty years. I have never heard them *harmonize*. The sound that came through that door was not forty separate voices; it was one voice made of forty, rising together, and the note they sang was the note she had given them, and the language—if language it was—was older than the hills that held us, older than the names carved into every shelf, and every hair on my body stood up and paid attention.

The child took her hand away. The singing stopped. She turned and looked at me, and her expression was not triumph. It was apology. It was the face of someone who has brought weather into a clean house.

"You'd better tell me who you are," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "Or if you can't do that—you can't, can you—all right. All right." I rubbed my face with both hands. Outside, the light was failing; the longest night was coming down over the valley like a lid. "You'll sleep by the fire. Tomorrow we'll see about... whatever this is. And child—if anything else in this house starts singing, I want to be awake for it."

She nodded, solemn as a magistrate, and I swear on my master's graver that the corner of her mouth twitched. Almost a smile. The first currency she'd offered me, and I found I was poorer than I'd thought, because I'd have traded the whole workshop for the full coin of it.

* * *

I did not sleep. I sat in the dark with my ledger open on my knees and wrote nothing, and listened.

The child slept curled in my chair like a cat in a sunbeam, blanket to her chin, ember-eyes banked behind her lids. And the workshop—my workshop, my quiet kingdom of the finished dead—was silent. Truly silent, for the first time in thirty years. Not resting. *Attending.* I could feel it through the wall, the sense of forty-one presences gathered close to their side of the door, the way villagers gather at a window when something strange comes up the road. Waiting. Listening to the child breathe.

Once, deep in the black hours, Tam Wicker's shin spoke. Just once, just a thread of sound, and I was across the room with my ear to the wall before I'd decided to move.

One word. Not his voice—it came through him, the way wind comes through a crack—but shaped by him, given to me the only way he could.

It was not in any tongue I knew. But I am a carver of bones and a keeper of last words, and I have a memory like a grave: everything goes in, nothing comes out. I reached for my ledger, wet my pen, and wrote the word phonetically, syllable by careful syllable, in the margin beside Tam's confession about the goat.

Two sounds. Two syllables. Soft as ash falling.

Through the wall, in her sleep, the child repeated it. Perfectly. Like an echo returning home.

I sat back down. I looked at the word I'd written, and the word looked back at me, and outside the snow began again, filling in the small footprints she'd left at my door as though the valley itself wanted no record of her coming.

Whatever had knocked on my door on the longest night of the year, it had not been an accident. Bones do not lie to me—they haven't the imagination—and every bone in my house was telling me the same thing:

The world outside Hollowmere had finally found a reason to break in.

Her name, I decided, watching her sleep. Tomorrow I would find out her name, or failing that, I would give her one, because a person without a name is a door without a hinge, and I had a feeling this particular door was about to be opened whether I liked it or not.

In the workshop, very softly, the bones began to hum again.
