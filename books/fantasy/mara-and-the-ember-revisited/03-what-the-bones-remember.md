## 3. What the Bones Remember

The dead do not sleep in my workshop. I want to be clear about that, because the priests who pass through Hollowmere every few years—when they bother to come at all—always ask it with their eyebrows: *Do they rest?* No. They murmur. They wait. They are patient the way water is patient with stone, and I am the stone, and after thirty years I have made my peace with being worn.

But on the morning after the solstice, for the first time since I took up my knife, the bones were silent.

Not quiet. Silent. There is a difference, and the difference sat in my chest like a swallowed coal. Thirty years of whispers—grievances, blessings, recipes for plum preserves, one truly memorable confession involving a neighbor's goat—and now nothing. The workshop felt like a room where a clock had stopped. You don't notice the ticking until it's gone, and then you notice everything else: your own breath, too loud; the frost creaking on the shutters; and beneath all of it, the small, even breathing of a child asleep in my bed.

I had put Mara there myself, which was its own small scandal. I have not shared my roof with anything warmer than a cat since the cat died, and the cat had been a poor conversationalist even by my standards. But the child had looked at me across the threshold of my door with those ember-colored eyes, humming that impossible language, and something in me—the part I keep locked, the part with no name on its door—had simply opened.

Foolish. I know. I carved bones for the dead precisely because the dead don't knock.

I fed the stove and set water to heat and tried to work, because work is what I do when the world misbehaves. There was a rib on my bench—a woman's ninth rib, delivered in autumn by her son, who had paid in turnips and apologies. I'd roughed out the memorial weeks ago: her name, *Maren Voss*, and the wheat-sheaf pattern she'd asked for through her son's clumsy translation of her dying wishes. All that remained was the finishing pass along the grain.

My knife touched bone, and Maren Voss spoke.

Not *at* me. Not the usual way, the whisper-through-the-grain I've heard ten thousand times, words arriving like cold air under a door. This came up through my hand, through the knife, through the bones of my own wrist, as if the dead woman were standing behind me with her mouth against my ear.

One word.

*"Hearth."*

I dropped the knife. It rang off the bench and rolled under the stove, and I stood there in my own workshop like a girl who'd never held a blade, listening to the silence where thirty years of murmuring used to live.

"Hearth," I said aloud, testing it. A common word. A hearth is a hearth. Every cottage in Hollowmere has one, including this one, currently heating water for tea. There was no reason for a dead woman's last word to be *hearth*, unless she'd died thinking of home, which most of them do, and I might have filed it away as exactly that if the word hadn't arrived wearing a weight I had no scale for. It hadn't sounded like a memory. It had sounded like an instruction.

Behind me, small feet on floorboards.

Mara stood in the doorway wrapped in my spare blanket, which fit her the way a sail fits a rowboat. She looked at the bench, at the rib, at me. Her eyes did that thing they'd done at the door the night before—that flicker, like light off moving water—and then she crossed the room without asking leave, which no one does in my workshop, and climbed onto the stool beside my bench, and looked down at the carving.

Her breath caught. I heard it. Small sound, enormous meaning, the way a single snapped twig can tell you the whole forest is moving.

She knew the pattern. Not recognized it—knew it, the way you know your own name when someone shouts it across a square. And here is the thing that turned my blood to well-water, cold and slow: so did I. Not from Maren Voss's instructions. From somewhere much older and much less explainable.

Because I have seen that wheat-sheaf before. I've seen it every day of my life, if I'm honest, though I've spent those same days not looking at it directly, the way you don't look at the sun or at grief. It was the pattern I carved when I wasn't thinking. Idle hands, idle knife, and out comes the sheaf—curved stem, bound middle, heads bent as if bowing to something just past the edge of the design. I'd found it in my earliest carvings, pieces I made as a child before anyone taught me letters, before my mother put a chisel in my hand and said, *bones remember, Bea, that's why we dress them plain.* I'd assumed I'd copied it from somewhere. A church lintel. A coin. Some forgotten thing.

Mara reached out and touched the rib, one finger, feather-light, the way you touch a sleeping animal you're afraid of waking.

Then she looked around the workshop—at the shelves, at the finished pieces hanging in their rows, at the bin of shavings—and her eyes went to the hearth itself, my actual hearth, blackened stone and iron crane, and stayed there.

"Tea first," I said, because I am a coward and tea is my shield. "Then whatever it is you're about to communicate at me."

She let me pour her a cup. She held it in both hands and drank like a small solemn old man, and I sat across from her and studied her properly for the first time in daylight. Frostbite healing on three fingers, poorly tended. Cheeks still carrying the windburn of hard travel. Maybe eight years old. Maybe younger; hunger edits children downward. Someone had loved her once—you could see it in the mended coat, the careful stitching, love expressed in thread—but someone had also lost her, or been lost, and neither possibility improved my mood.

"You're going to write something, aren't you," I said.

She set down the cup. She looked at the hearth again. Then she slid off her chair, crouched at the cold ash-pan below the firebox, and dipped her finger into the grey.

And drew.

I want to describe this accurately, because accuracy matters more than comfort in my trade. She didn't draw the way children draw—sun in the corner, stick figures, house with a smiling door. She drew the way scribes draw, or the way water finds a channel: fast, certain, each stroke placed as if it had been waiting behind her fingernails for years. Symbols. Perhaps twenty of them, arranged in lines that read left to right and then, without any transition I could follow, right to left, and then spiraling inward toward a central mark.

The central mark was a hearth. Stylized—three strokes for flame above a bounded square—but unmistakable, and flanked on either side by the wheat-sheaf. My wheat-sheaf. Bound middle, bending heads, bowing inward toward the flame.

I did not move for a long time. Long enough for the kettle to go quiet. Long enough for the frost on the window to begin its slow afternoon melt, one drop tracing a path down the glass like something escaping.

"Where did you learn that?" I asked, and my voice came out steadier than I deserved.

She looked up at me. She pointed at herself. Then at the drawing. Then—this is the part I will be turning over for the rest of my life—she pointed at *me.*

"No," I said. "No, little one. I don't speak whatever that is. I carve turnip farmers' names on ribs and I mind my own affairs, and my affairs have never included—" I gestured at the ash, at the spiral, at the whole silent screaming of it. "That."

She tilted her head. Then she got up, went to the shelf where I keep my practice pieces—the failures, the experiments, the things too ugly to sell and too honest to burn—and she began pulling them down, one after another, holding each up to the light of the window, comparing.

A knuckle-bone with a spiral I'd doodled at fifteen. A skull-cap scored with branching lines I'd always called *lightning* because I had no better word. A vertebra drilled with holes in a pattern I'd once spent a week trying to repeat and couldn't, and had finally decided was a flaw in my younger self's patience.

She laid them on the table beside her ash-drawing, arranged them in order, oldest to newest, and looked at me with an expression I recognized because I wear it myself when a client lies to me about how their grandmother died.

*You already know,* that expression said. *You've known longer than I've been alive.*

"I copied them," I said. "From somewhere. A church, a book, some—"

She shook her head. Firmly. The way you correct a child, except she was correcting me, and the terrible thing was that she was right to.

Because I remembered—I made myself remember, prying the lid off the box I keep in the back of my skull—my mother finding those early carvings. Finding them and going white. Taking the knife out of my hand harder than she'd ever taken anything, and saying, *not these, Bea, never these, where did you see them,* and me saying nowhere, nowhere, I didn't, they just came out, and her face doing something complicated and frightened before she smoothed it over and said we'd speak of it no more.

We never did. She died the next winter, and my father followed inside two years, and I moved to the far end of the valley where nobody knew me and took up the family trade with the family patterns quietly omitted. Mostly. Except my hands kept their secrets. Except the sheaf crept back in, and the spirals, and the branching lines, whenever I stopped watching myself.

Thirty years in a valley where nothing magical can follow, and I chose it—or told myself I'd chosen it—for the quiet, for the honest work, for the distance from a world I claimed not to care about. And the whole time, the proof that I had run *from* something rather than *to* anything was sitting on my own shelves, gathering dust, carved in my own childish hand.

The past doesn't stay buried. I say that to grieving families all the time, gently, as consolation: *they live in you, they're not gone.* I never once considered that it worked in both directions. That the buried things in me might also be alive, waiting, patient as water.

Mara tugged my sleeve. She pointed at the ash drawing—at the hearth-mark at the center of the spiral—and then out the window, north, toward the fells, toward the boundary of the valley, toward everything I had spent three decades declining to think about.

"Hearth," I said.

She nodded. Then she pointed at the word again, and at herself, and pressed her small fist flat against her own sternum, and her eyes did the ember-flicker, brighter this time, and for half a heartbeat I would have sworn the ash-drawing *glowed*—the hearth-mark kindling faint gold before fading, like coals breathed on.

Outside, the wind changed. I felt it before I heard it—a pressure shift, a wrongness, the valley itself seeming to hold its breath. The horses in Aldous Merrick's field, visible from my window, lifted their heads all at once and stared north.

And in the workshop, the silence among the dead broke—not into their usual murmur but into something I had never heard from them in thirty years. Fear. A low, rising chorus of it, dozens of voices overlapping, the dead of Hollowmere all speaking at once for the first time, and underneath the fear, threaded through it, repeated over and over in a hundred different voices, men and women and children who had died across four generations:

*Hearth. Hearth. Hearth.*

Mara's hand found mine. Her fingers were cold, and they were trembling, and she was looking at the north window with the absolute certainty of someone who has run a very long way and just heard the dogs.

I am not a brave woman. I want that on the record, carved somewhere, dated. I am a bone-carver who moved to the empty end of a valley to avoid her own history. But a child was gripping my hand, and the dead I have tended for thirty years were calling one word in a language older than kingdoms, and far to the north, past the fells, something was coming that made even ghosts afraid.

"Right," I said, mostly to myself, because someone had to say something. "Tea's gone cold anyway."

* * *

I spent the evening doing what I do best, which is listening.

I lit every lamp I owned and I went shelf to shelf, rib to skull to knuckle, and I asked the dead of Hollowmere what they knew. Most of them only had the fear, formless, spreading between them like damp. But the old ones—the deep ones, the bones that had been in my keeping longest—had fragments. A woman dead sixty years whispered about *a fire that remembers*. A shepherd's femur, older still, gave me the image of a door and the sensation of it closing, and the grief of the closing, vast and specific, like a funeral for a place instead of a person. And one piece, a wrist-bone so ancient the name on it had worn smooth, said only: *it opens from the child's side.*

I wrote none of it down. Some instinct, old and unchosen, told me not to put it in ink.

Mara sat on the floor by the stove the whole while, drawing in the ash again and again—the spiral, the hearth, the sheaf—until the pan was a grey blur and she swayed with exhaustion. I put her back in my bed. She took my sleeve before I could go and pointed, one last time, at the hearth-mark, then at the north window, then at me, and mouthed something with great seriousness, no sound at all, but I understood it the way I understand the dead: not the words, the weight.

*You have to come with me.*

"I haven't agreed to anything," I told her.

She closed her eyes, unconvinced, and slept.

I sat up with a lamp and my knife and the rib of Maren Voss, and I finished the wheat-sheaf, and as the blade moved, the dead woman's voice rose one final time through the grain—faint now, receding, like someone calling from a boat pulling away from shore—and she said, in the First Tongue or whatever tongue it was, a second word.

This one I didn't know. But my hands did. Before my mind caught up, they had carved it, letter-perfect, into the margin of the memorial where no client would ever notice: five symbols, flowing, certain, in an alphabet I had never been taught and could not read and recognized the way you recognize your own reflection in dark glass.

They matched the last line of Mara's ash-drawing exactly.

Far to the north, beyond the fells, beyond the valley's edge, a light bloomed against the clouds—brief, ember-red, gone again in a breath. Not lightning. Lightning doesn't pulse. Lightning doesn't repeat, twice, three times, steady as a signal, steady as something answering.

The dead went quiet again. All of them. Even the fear.

And in the silence, from the road—miles off yet, but coming, hooves and hounds and a sound like wind through a burned forest—I heard the valley's long peace finally, audibly, end.
