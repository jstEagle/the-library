## 4. Riders on the Ash Wind

The dead of Hollowmere had opinions about weather. Old Tam, whose femur I'd carved eleven years ago, grumbled through the bone whenever it rained—a low, wet complaint like a man turning over in a grave that had sprung a leak. The three sisters in the cedar box sighed together when the wind came down off the ridge. And the child who'd been dead two days now, the little boy from the ferry road, said nothing at all, because children's bones kept their words close, and I had learned not to pry.

So when I woke on the third morning after the solstice to a workshop gone utterly, perfectly silent, I knew before I opened my eyes that something was wrong.

Silence, in my trade, is not peace. Peace is the murmur of the finished dead, their last words worn smooth as river stones by decades of repetition. Silence is what you get when something is listening harder than they are.

I sat up on my cot with my knees cracking in chorus—sixty-one years old, and my body insisted on announcing itself like a town crier—and looked across the room at the shelves.

Three hundred and forty bones, give or take. Each one wrapped in linen, each one labeled in my own careful hand, each one holding its whisper of a final word: *forgive*, or *the blue door*, or *tell her I tried*. For thirty years they had murmured to me in the dark like a congregation that never quite finished its prayer.

Now they were silent as stones in a well with no bottom.

And the girl was standing among them.

Mara—I'd taken to calling her that, though she'd never confirmed it; she'd only watched me say it with those enormous grey eyes and touched her own collarbone twice, which I chose to interpret as agreement—stood in the aisle between the shelves with one hand raised, palm out, as if she were holding back a door. Her frostbitten fingers had healed into pink new skin over the past days, quick as anything, quicker than healing had any right to be. She wasn't looking at the bones. She was looking at the window.

At the valley beyond it.

I followed her gaze and saw nothing but Hollowmere doing what Hollowmere did in deep winter: smoke rising straight from chimneys, sheep standing in fields like furniture someone had forgotten to move indoors, the river Vell's tributary frozen into a long white ribbon stitched through the brown fields. Ordinary. Safe. The most deliberately boring valley in the world, if I did say so myself, and I had spent thirty years making it so.

But Mara was trembling.

"Right," I said aloud, because talking to her felt less mad than talking to myself, and I'd already done plenty of that before she arrived. "You're going to tell me what you see. Or draw it. Or hum it. Any of your three options is acceptable."

She turned to me. And then she did something she hadn't done since the night she appeared on my doorstep, frostbitten and mute and humming that language older than kingdoms.

She shook her head.

Not *no, I won't.* No, *you don't want to know.*

"Well," I said, reaching for my boots, "fortunately for both of us, I've never once in my life wanted to know something and let that stop me."

* * *

Here is what you must understand about Hollowmere, and about me, and about the arrangement we had come to over three decades: the valley was empty of magic the way a bell is empty of sound. Not broken. Deliberate. When I was nineteen years old and carrying a grief so large I couldn't see around it, I walked until my feet bled, and then I walked further, and the place where I stopped was a valley where the ember-magic of the old covenant could not follow. Where no ghost could reach me, no priest could find me, no power of any realm could touch the small hard knot of a life I built for myself out of bone knives and linen wrappings and deliberate ignorance.

I didn't know why the valley was quiet. I didn't ask. Asking questions is how you end up with answers, and answers are how you end up involved, and involvement was the entire country I had emigrated from.

For thirty years this worked beautifully. The world outside could burn itself down in whatever fashion it liked. In here, I carved memorial bones for the grieving, listened to the dead's last words so their families could have them back, charged fair prices, grew leeks, and knew nothing—*nothing*—of kings, courts, wars, or wonders. People sometimes arrived at my door with wild eyes and stranger stories, and I sent them home with a carved keepsake and a firm recommendation to take their apocalypse elsewhere.

It was, I want to be clear, a good life. It was a life I had chosen the way you choose a wall: stone by stone, on purpose, to keep something out.

Or possibly, whispered a voice I didn't listen to, to keep something in.

Either way. On the morning the bones went silent, the wall developed its first crack in three hundred years, and the crack had grey eyes and frostbitten fingers and stood in my workshop humming a language that made my oldest carvings itch in their wrappings.

* * *

By midmorning I had done everything a sensible woman does when her sanctuary goes strange. I checked the wards—or rather, I checked the absence of magic, which in Hollowmere meant walking the boundary stones and confirming that they still hummed that faint dead note, like a struck bell that has forgotten sound ever lived in it. They did. Mostly.

Mostly is a word doing terrible work in that sentence.

The easternmost stone—the one nearest the pass, the one facing the wide world I had spent half a lifetime declining to think about—was warm.

Stones in winter are not warm. Stones in winter are liars and traitors and cold as a creditor's heart. This one was warm as a sleeping animal, and when I laid my palm against it, I felt something I had not felt in thirty years: a pulse. Faint. Far away. Like hearing, through a wall, the heartbeat of something enormous deciding whether to wake up.

"Mara," I said, when I got back to the house, "we're going to play a game. It's called Point At What's Wrong. You're very good at it. I'm afraid you might win."

She was sitting at my table where I'd set her to grinding ochre for pigment, and she had stopped grinding. Her head was cocked toward the east window the way a dog cocks its head at a whistle only it can hear. When I spoke, she looked at me, and then—slowly, deliberately—she raised her hand and pointed at her own chest.

"You," I said. "What's wrong is you?"

She shook her head. Pointed at her chest again. Then swept her arm outward, taking in the valley, the hills, the whole shivering world, and made a gesture I understood in my bones before my brain caught up: *through me. Coming through me.*

Like a key. Like a door that walks.

I sat down across from her, because my legs had opinions of their own and they'd voted for sitting.

"Now here's my problem," I told her, keeping my voice level the way you keep a lid on a pot. "Thirty years ago I came to this valley because it was the one place in creation where *that*"—I gestured at all of her, the humming, the symbols, the impossible healing, the whole uncanny package—"couldn't exist. Whatever runs beneath the world, whatever the priests and the courts and the burning people fight over, it doesn't reach here. That was the entire point. That was the *architectural principle*."

She watched me with the patience of the very young and the very old, which is to say she waited for me to arrive at the part I didn't want to say.

"And three nights ago," I continued, "you walked up to my door through a snowstorm, humming a language that predates every kingdom whose coins I've ever refused, and the dead in my house went quiet for the first time since I hung the first shelf." I rubbed my face. "Which means either you broke in, or the lock died, and I've been telling myself for three days that it was you. That you were the anomaly. One strange child, one closed system, no harm done."

Her chin dipped. Almost a nod. *And?*

"And this morning the boundary stone was warm." I heard my own voice go flat, which is what it does instead of shaking. "Warm, Mara. Which means the valley isn't keeping the world out anymore. Which means either the valley changed, or the world did, and either way—" I stopped. Made myself finish. "Either way, you didn't bring trouble with you. You brought the *door*, and trouble doesn't need an invitation when there's a door."

Outside, the wind changed.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I had left by afternoon. The wind in Hollowmere comes down off the western ridge; it always has; it smells of pine and snow and the leek beds. At midday, the wind swung east. It came up the pass, over the boundary stones, across the frozen fields, and it smelled of—

Of ash. Of cold hearths and burnt letters and the inside of a fire after the fire has finished. Of a thing I hadn't smelled since I was nineteen years old and running, and had sworn never to smell again.

Mara stood up so fast her stool went over.

* * *

They came at dusk, and I will give them this: they were beautiful, the way a landslide is beautiful if you catch it from far enough away.

I saw them first from the workshop window while I was pretending, very strenuously, to grind pigment and not at all to watch the pass. Shapes on the eastern sky. Low. Moving without wings, without legs, without any of the honest machinery of motion—just *arriving*, further along, and further again, the way a thought arrives. Hounds. That was the word my mind supplied and refused to replace: hounds of grey smoke, tall as horses, lean as hunger, trailing behind them a wake of fine ash that fell on the snow and did not melt it but *greyed* it, bleached the white to the color of old paper.

Behind them, fainter, rode shapes I couldn't look at directly—not because they were bright but because they were *absent*, holes in the dusk shaped like riders, and my eyes kept sliding off them the way a nail slides off glass.

There were seven hounds. There were more riders than I could count, which I suspect was the point.

"They can't cross," I said to the empty workshop, in the tone of a woman reading her own eulogy. "That's the whole arrangement. Three hundred years, the covenant holds, the valley stays dull as ditchwater and safe as a grave, and nothing—*nothing*—rides the ash wind into Hollowmere."

The lead hound reached the boundary line where my warm stone stood.

And crossed it.

I watched it happen. I watched the grey smoke pour over the invisible line my whole life was built along, and nothing stopped it. No flash, no thunder, no ward flaring into desperate light. The protection of Hollowmere didn't fight the hounds. It simply wasn't there anymore, the way a bridge isn't there after the flood takes it—you can stand on the spot all day and argue with the water, and the water will not care.

The hound put one smouldering paw in the snow of my valley, and the snow under it went grey, and somewhere behind me three hundred and forty bones began, very softly, to scream.

Not the murmuring of the settled dead. Screaming. Every last word I had ever gathered—every *forgive* and *blue door* and *tell her I tried*—came off the shelves at once, a cacophony of the unfinished, and I understood with terrible clarity what I was hearing. The dead weren't frightened of the hounds.

The dead recognized them.

I got Mara out the back and into the root cellar in ninety seconds flat, which I consider my finest work as a hostess. She fought me—not hard, but with that eerie strength of hers, until I put my mouth against her ear and said, "If they take you, they take the door, and if they take the door, everyone behind it burns. Including the dead. Including mine." She went still. She let me fold her into the dark among the turnips and the salt pork, and I pressed my oldest carving into her hands—the rib, the one that had spoken the First Tongue, the one that said *hearth*—and I put my finger to my lips, and she nodded once, grey-eyed in the lantern light, and I climbed back up into my dying sanctuary and shut the trapdoor on the only innocent thing left in the valley.

Then I did what I do best. I picked up a bone knife, and I waited by the door, and I listened to the dead scream, and I thought about walls.

Because here is the arithmetic I couldn't escape, standing there in the grey falling ash-light: the hounds had crossed the boundary the same hour they'd begun hunting. Not before. The valley's protection hadn't faded on some slow schedule of centuries, quietly expiring like an old man in his sleep. It had failed *when she arrived*. The breach moved with her. She was the crack in the world, and everything on the other side of the crack wanted through, and I had spent three days feeding her porridge and teaching her to grind ochre while the lock on my door dissolved.

Sanctuary, I thought, watching the lead hound's shape resolve in my yard like smoke poured into a mold. Sanctuary is just a trap that hasn't noticed it's closed.

The hound turned its head. It had no eyes—it had two places where fire had been, banked low, patient—and it looked at my house, and then past my house, at the ground, at the cellar, at the small buried heartbeat of a girl who hummed in the First Tongue, and its jaw unhinged, and what came out was not a growl.

It was a word. In her language. In the language older than kingdoms, the language my bones knew, the language that could bind or unbind—and even mangled through a throat of smoke, I felt it pull at something behind my ribs, some thread I hadn't known was knotted there.

From below the floorboards, faint as a heartbeat, Mara answered.

And every hound in the yard turned toward my house at once, and the ash-wind rose, and down the eastern pass, through the failing dark, came hoofbeats—real ones, iron-shod, deliberate—the sound of whoever had sent the dogs arriving to collect what the dogs had found.

I gripped my bone knife, sixty-one years old, out of practice, out of sanctuary, and out of time.

And that was when the knock came at my front door—which was remarkable, given that the front door faced a yard full of smoke-hounds, and more remarkable still because the knocking had manners.

Three raps. Even. Almost apologetic.

A man's voice, hoarse as a struck match, said through the wood: "Boneseller. I need passage, you need answers, and neither of us has much night left. My name is Ovid Crane—and yes, before you ask, I'm the one they're looking for too."
