## 5. The Priest Who Burned His God

Let me tell you the first rule of fleeing the Ashen Court: don't look back. The second rule is that you will look back anyway, because the Court trains you from childhood to check your shadow, and by the time you realize the training was never for your benefit, your shadow has learned to check you.

My name is Ovid Crane. It was, once, a name with weight attached—Ovid Crane, Third Voice of the Cinder Choir, Keeper of the Ninth Reliquary, sworn ash-bearer of the Court of Cinders. Say it fast and it sounds like a title. Say it slow and it sounds like a sentence, which is what it turned out to be.

I had been running for eleven days when I saw the valley.

You have to understand what it means, for a man like me, to see a place where the ember-light doesn't reach. The Court's power is in everything now—every hearth in every city burns with their permission, every memory worth keeping is taxed, every child is taught the Litany of Ash before they're taught their own name. The world is a lamp they hold. And there, spread out below the ridge like something from a bedtime story the Litany forbade, was a valley with no lamp in it at all.

Hollowmere. I had thought it was a myth the priests invented to frighten novices. *There are places the fire cannot follow,* we were told, *and the things that live there are worse than the dark.* We were told this by men who ate memories for breakfast, which should have been my first clue that the lesson was less about geography and more about keeping us home.

I went down into it anyway. Fleeing men don't get to be choosy about their myths.

* * *

The dead woman's workshop sat at the valley's heart like a knuckle of bone in a bowl of gruel. Smoke rose from its chimney—ordinary smoke, honest smoke—and the smell of woodsmoke and tallow reached me before the door did. I stood outside it for a long while, doing what I do best, which is nothing, thoroughly.

I should describe what eleven days of flight had made of me. My vestments were gone; I'd burned them in a drainage culvert outside Vasterlin, which had felt at the time like the most honest act of my life and now felt like the stupidest, since a man in ash-grey robes is at least a *known* kind of trouble, and a man in stolen farm clothes with a Court brand hidden under his collar is an *interesting* kind, and the Court loves interesting. My hands shook. Not from fear—fear is a luxury the Court burns out of you early, along with most other useful feelings—but from the thing they'd done to me when I left. They call it *unbinding.* It sounds gentle. It is the ritual equivalent of being unwritten: they take back every gift they ever gave you, every borrowed warmth, every scrap of ember-sight, and they leave you with the original equipment, which for me was a bad knee, a worse conscience, and a memory that had begun, in the last three days, to leak.

That's what happens when a priest of the Court stops being fed. We don't eat food. Well—we eat food, but food is a courtesy. What we eat is memory. Our own, mostly, recycled through the Reliquaries, distilled from the tribute the faithful pay at every hearth-altar in the nine provinces. A lifetime of small recollections, poured into the fire, and the fire pours a spoonful back into the mouths of its clergy. It is a beautiful system. I mean that sincerely. It is elegant, efficient, and it works, which are the three qualities I was taught to worship before I was taught that they are also the three qualities of a well-designed slaughterhouse.

Eleven days unfed, and my memories were leaking back to me. All the ones I had paid into the fire over thirty years of service. They came back the way floodwater comes back—muddy, indiscriminate, and full of drowned things.

I knocked on the bone-carver's door because I had nowhere else, and because the brand under my collar had started, in the last mile, to itch like a thing waking up.

* * *

She opened the door the way I imagine a fortress opens a gate: fully, and with the clear implication that whatever came through would regret it.

She was perhaps sixty, grey to the eyebrows, with the forearms of a woman who has spent thirty years arguing with hard material and winning most of those arguments. Her eyes did a thing I had only ever seen done by inquisitors and my grandmother, which was to take my complete inventory in a single pass and file me somewhere between *disappointment* and *problem.*

"You're not from the valley," she said.

"No."

"You're not from anywhere near the valley."

"No."

"You're dying of something, and it isn't a wound."

I blinked. "Is it the smell? There's a smell, isn't there. The unbinding has a smell. Like a snuffed candle that's decided to hold a grudge."

"It's the way you stand," she said. "Like a man waiting for a blow he already deserves. Come in before the frost does. And if you're followed—"

"I'm followed," I said, "but not closely. Yet."

She stood aside. I have thought about that moment a great deal since—more than I think about most moments, and I think about most moments far too much, which is the occupational disease of a man who sold his past to a fire. She let me in. She had every reason not to. I have since learned that this is simply what Beatrix Winterbourne does, and that she would tell you it wasn't kindness, it was curiosity, and that she would be lying, and that she would know she was lying, and that she would say it anyway, because some people would rather be accused of hardness than caught at tenderness.

Inside, the workshop smelled of tallow and cold stone and—this is the part that made my knees actually buckle, and I want it noted that I caught myself on her doorframe with what I maintain was dignity—inside, it smelled of *nothing.*

Do you understand? Thirty years in the Choir, and I had forgotten what nothing smelled like. Every room in the Court's world is seasoned with ember-trace, that faint charred sweetness, the smell of memory being kept warm. This room had no trace at all. It was a room the fire had never touched. It smelled like the world before the covenant, which is to say it smelled like a word I had only ever read in forbidden commentaries: *before.*

"Sit," she said, "before you fall, and falling damages my floor."

I sat. There was a child in the corner.

I want to be honest about what I felt, because honesty is the only currency I have left and I've learned to spend it carefully. When I saw the child, I felt terror. Not the ordinary terror of a hunted man—my terror had a shape, and the shape was small, and frostbitten, and sitting cross-legged on a workbench carving symbols into a slab of ash with one of the bone-carver's own knives.

I knew those symbols. I had spent a decade of my life making sure no one else did.

"That's her," I said. My voice came out wrong, cracked down the middle like a reliquary seal. "That's the Vessel. That's the—she's supposed to be dead. I was told she was dead. I *made sure* they were told she was dead."

The bone-carver moved between me and the child with the unhurried certainty of a woman picking up a poker before a conversation rather than during one. "You'll explain," she said, "who *they* are, and who *you* are, and why a child who hasn't spoken a word in a week just looked at you like she's about to."

The child *had* looked at me. That was the worst part. Mara—the name came back to me then, one of the floodwater memories, *the Vessel's name is Mara, and if you write it down I will have you unmade*—Mara had looked at me with an expression I recognized because I had seen it in a mirror for thirty years. It was the look of someone recognizing a debt.

* * *

I told her most of it. I'm a priest; lying is liturgy to me, and I know exactly how much truth a story needs to stand up on its own.

I told her about the Court of Cinders. I gave her the history the way the Court tells it first, because I wanted to watch her face, and her face did not change, which told me she was either very wise or very tired, and I have since learned she is both. The Court, I said, were the original guardians of the covenant—the order sworn, at the First Hearth, to keep the boundary between the living world and the Ember Realm. The covenant required feeding. That was its design and its flaw: a wall between realms, mortared with memory. The willing sacrifice of recollection, freely given, generation after generation. The guardians' sacred duty was to collect the tribute and pour it into the wall.

"And then?" she said.

"And then," I said, "someone noticed that the wall didn't have to be the only thing the memory could feed." I told her about the Corrupting—how the guardians discovered that a memory poured into a person instead of a wall made the person *more.* Stronger. Longer-lived. Wiser, in the terrible way that a library is wise without understanding a single book. How the guardians began skimming. A trickle at first, a tithe of the tithe, and then not a trickle at all, and then the guardians were the Court, and the covenant was not a wall but a *larder*, and the realms it had been built to keep apart were being ground against each other like millstones with the Court's hands on the axle.

"And they eat memories," she said. Flatly. As though I'd told her the neighbors kept goats.

"The faithful give freely," I said. "That's the genius of it. Nobody is robbed. They *pay*. A memory of a bad winter for a blessing on the spring planting. The face of a dead parent, because grief is heavy and the fire promises to carry it. Small things, at first. And the Court keeps them, and grows, and the wall between the realms starves, because the Court is eating the mortar." I looked at my hands. They were still shaking. "I was a priest of that larder for thirty years. I fed it. I taught the Litany. I stood at hearth-altars and told weeping women that giving the fire their dead husband's voice was holy."

"And now?"

"And now there was a child," I said, "born with the First Tongue in her mouth, which hasn't happened since the covenant was forged, and the Court's own oracles named her the living key to it. The bridge. The one being in creation who could unmake the wall entirely—or remake it. And the Court decided, in its wisdom, that a key is best used by melting it down." I heard my own voice go somewhere I didn't like. "They were going to consume her, bone-carver. A child. They dressed it in liturgy—*the Vessel returns to the fire, the fire returns to the covenant*—but it was a child, and I had carried her out of the nursery myself often enough to know the weight of her."

"You took her somewhere," Beatrix said. It wasn't a question.

"I hid her lineage. I falsified the oracles. I gave the Court a dead Vessel and gave the child to a family of river-folk three provinces away, and I paid for the falsification with thirty years of my own stored memories, poured back into the fire, so the ledgers would balance and no auditor would look twice." I laughed, and it came out like a cough. "Do you understand what that means? I bought her life with my own past. And the fire took it, and I thought the transaction was over, and then eleven days ago the Court found the river-folk family, which means they found the ledger error, which means they found *me*, and they unbound me and set the hounds on my shadow, and every memory they took is leaking back into me now, all at once, like a debt collector emptying a drawer."

The workshop was quiet. In the corner, the child had stopped carving. She was watching me with those eyes, and I found I could not look at her and speak at the same time, so I looked at the workbench instead, at the rows of carved bones, each one a name, each one a voice kept warm by a woman instead of a fire.

"They're dead," Beatrix said at last. "The river-folk."

"Yes."

"The child ran."

"Yes."

"And the hounds followed her here. Into a valley where magic can't follow." Her jaw tightened, and I watched her arrive, in real time, at the arithmetic I had arrived at on the ridge: *the valley's protection is the covenant, the covenant is starving, and the child is the covenant's key, and where the key goes the lock goes, and where the lock goes—*

"They'll breach," I said. "Days. Perhaps a week. The valley has been dark to them for centuries because the covenant's wall was thickest here, at its root. But the wall is thin everywhere now, and she—" I nodded at the child, gently, the way you nod at a wound. "She's a door, bone-carver. A living one. And doors don't make good walls."

* * *

Here is what I offered her, and I want the record to show I offered it before she asked, because I am not entirely the coward I have worked so hard to become.

"Passage," I said. "I know the way to the First Hearth. Not the way the songs say—the true way, the priests' way, through the Ember Wastes and the ferry and the city that sold itself. I served the Court thirty years; I know its roads, its tolls, its blind spots. Take me with you when you run, because you *will* run, and I'll give you everything I know. The Court. The covenant. The child. And at the Hearth—" I swallowed. This was the part I had rehearsed on the road for eleven days, and it still came out wrong, too fast, the way confessions do. "At the Hearth, the covenant can be reforged. I believe that. I have to believe that. And if it can, then everything I burned—every memory I fed them, every sin I stacked up like cordwood—maybe it counts for something. Maybe penance is a thing you can *spend.*"

"Penance," Beatrix said, "is not a currency."

"No," I said. "But it's the only coin I'm carrying."

She looked at me for a long time. Behind her, the child—Mara, *Mara*, the name a splinter working its way back up out of my skin—set down the knife and did something that made the bone-carver's breath catch and made my own heart stop like a struck bell.

She carved a symbol into the ash-slab. One symbol. The oldest one, the root-sign, the first character of the First Tongue, the one we were forbidden to pronounce on pain of unmaking.

Then she turned the slab around so I could see it, and pointed—at it, and then at me.

"She's asking," Beatrix said slowly, "if you're the one who hid her."

I am a priest of the Court of Cinders. I have lied to oracles. I have lied to the fire itself, which is like lying to a mirror that keeps the reflections. And I looked at that child, that impossible, hunted, silent child, and I found that every lie I owned had gone out of me like heat from an open door.

"Yes," I said.

Mara looked at me for a long moment. Then she did the second thing, the thing I will remember—if I am permitted to remember anything, which is a wager I've stopped making—until whatever end finds me.

She picked up the knife and carved a second symbol beside the first. The root-sign, and then the mark for *thanks*, which in the First Tongue is not a word but a debt acknowledged, a knot tied between two people.

The bone-carver looked from the slab to me, and her expression did something complicated and final, like a lock deciding.

"Rest tonight," she said. "You'll tell me the rest in the morning. All of it—the roads, the tolls, the blind spots. And priest—" she paused at the hearth, firelight catching the grey in her hair, "—if your hounds come while we sleep, you'll stand with me at the door. Not behind me. With me."

"Behind you is traditional," I offered.

"I know," said Beatrix Winterbourne. "That's why it's not on offer."

I lay down that night on a pallet among the bones of strangers, in a room that smelled of nothing, in a valley that was about to stop being safe, next to a child who had forgiven me in a language older than forgiveness. And in the last hour before dawn, I heard it—faint, far off, at the valley's eastern edge where the frost lay thinnest.

Hounds. Not baying.

*Humming.*

And the child, in her sleep, hummed back.
