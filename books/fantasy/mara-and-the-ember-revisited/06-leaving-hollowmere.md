## 6. Leaving Hollowmere

I had always assumed that when I finally burned my workshop down, it would be an accident. A dropped candle, a careless ember from the hearth, old woman's hands gone clumsy at last. There would be neighbors—well, there would be the idea of neighbors, three miles off across the frozen fields—and there would be a small funeral for a small building, and I would move into the back room of the meetinghouse and be pitied in a comfortable way until I died.

Instead I stood in the snow before dawn with a lamp of lamp-oil and a flint, and did it on purpose.

There is a particular clarity that comes with deciding to destroy thirty years of your own work. It is not peace. It is not rage, exactly, though rage is in there somewhere, banked like coals. It is closer to what a surgeon must feel: this will hurt, and it is still the correct cut.

The hounds had been humming at the valley's edge all night. Ovid Crane had gone grey when he heard them—or greyer; the man was built mostly of grey already—and had said, in the tone of a physician delivering a diagnosis he'd rather not deliver: "They've found her. They'll take a day to work up their courage. Smoke doesn't like cold ground."

"A day," I said.

"Perhaps two."

"Then we have one." I had learned, in forty-odd years of dealing with the recently bereaved, that grief negotiates best with deadlines. Give a mourner forever and she will sit by the grave until she joins it. Give her until sundown and she will stand up, wash her face, and ask what needs doing. I gave myself until dawn.

Mara stood beside me in the snow, wrapped in my second-best shawl, watching the workshop windows glow faintly gold in the early dark. She did not know yet what I intended. Children understand fire well enough—they are not fools—but they do not understand *choosing* it, and I wanted one more hour of being, in her eyes, simply the bone-woman who fed her porridge and taught her letters, rather than the bone-woman who burned down the only home either of us had.

Ovid understood immediately. That is the trouble with priests, even disgraced ones: they recognize sacrifice the way dogs recognize thunder.

"You can't save the archive," he said quietly, coming to stand at my shoulder. His breath steamed. "If you're thinking you might carry out even a fraction—"

"I'm not thinking anything so stupid."

"Good, because—"

"Ovid. I have spent thirty years listening to dead people's last words. Do you imagine I don't know how to have one of my own?"

He shut his mouth. To his credit, it stayed shut for nearly a full minute, which for Ovid Crane was a personal record.

* * *

Let me tell you what was in that workshop, since no one else will ever see it now.

Four hundred and eleven carved bones, each one a memorial, each one holding the final whisper of someone who died in or near Hollowmere in the years since I came here as a young widow with nothing but grief and a talent I didn't want. Farmers, midwives, children taken by fever, old men taken by winter, one unlucky tinker taken by a bull named Duchess. The valley sent me its dead because the valley could not send them anywhere else—Hollowmere being a place where magic could not follow, where the covenant's long arm went numb at the boundary stones, where the dead's words simply *stayed*, caught in the bodies they left behind, unless someone carved them free.

That was my work. Not necromancy—I was always careful to say that, though the distinction mattered less every year—not prophecy, just... transcription. The dead get one sentence, usually. Sometimes only a word. *Tell Tomas the fence.* *It didn't hurt.* *She knew.* And once, memorably, from a man who had been gored by the aforementioned Duchess: *Should've sold the cow.*

People think last words are profound. Mostly they are logistics. Love arrives sideways, in the margins, in the fence-mending and the cow-selling, and if you listen long enough you learn that this is what love actually sounds like when it is true: ordinary, unfinished, addressed to someone who will never hear it.

I was going to burn four hundred and eleven unfinished sentences.

I want it noted that I stood in the cold for a good while before I lit the flint, and that I am not ashamed of the standing. Grief negotiates with deadlines, but it also stalls. It asks for extensions. It says: surely we can carry out just the rib of the miller's daughter, just the finger-bone of old Aldous who left me his house, just—

No. That was precisely the trap. The Ashen Court fed on memory—that much Ovid had told me, in his careful, guilt-ridden way—and a bone-archive was not a library to them. It was a *pantry*. Every carving I saved would be a door they could walk through, a whisper they could drink, a thread leading straight from their hungry mouths to everything Hollowmere had ever loved. I could not save the archive. So I would spend it.

There are worse things a whisper can do than end in fire.

* * *

Before the flint, the choosing.

I had told myself I would take three carvings. Three seemed a number a reasonable woman could defend. One for practicality, one for sentiment, one for whatever the third thing is that has no name but makes you keep a child's crooked drawing in a drawer for twenty years.

Practicality chose itself: the shoulder-blade of Maren Ostler, the midwife, whose last word had been *hearth*. Ovid had gone white as whey when Mara wrote the same symbol in ash on my floor—the same symbol I had found myself carving, unbidden, into a scrap of antler when I was nine years old. Whatever the First Hearth was, wherever it was, Maren's blade-bone hummed toward it like a compass needle. If we were going to walk into the world with nothing, we would walk with a compass.

Sentiment was harder. My hands hovered over the case of recent carvings—people I had known alive, people who had waved to me across fences—and then drifted, as my hands always drift, to the far shelf. The oldest box. The one I opened perhaps twice a year, and never all the way.

Inside, wrapped in linen gone soft as skin: a rib. Not human—deer, and old, older than my work, older than my arrival in the valley. I had carved it when I was a girl, before I knew I had any such talent, before the word *covenant* meant anything to me but a line in a harvest hymn. I had carved spirals into it because the spirals felt right, and I had carried it through every house I ever lived in without knowing why, the way you carry a scar.

And beneath it, also in the box: a small carving of applewood, done by my own hand at fourteen. A girl's profile. Round-cheeked, gap-toothed, laughing at something just outside the frame.

My sister. Wren.

Dead forty years. Dead before I came to Hollowmere, which was not an accident—though I did not know that yet, and would not know it for many miles and many chapters of my ruin, so let me stay here, in the snow, in the ignorance that was the last kind thing the world ever did for me. All I knew then was the shape of the loss: a girl who used to steal apples and blame the crows, gone in one bad autumn, mourned so long that mourning had worn smooth as river stone, a thing I carried in my pocket and worried with my thumb.

I took the applewood face. I took the deer rib. I left the rest.

Mara watched me wrap them. Then she tugged my sleeve and pointed—at the case of recent carvings, at the whole four hundred and eleven—and made a sound. Not a word. She was still nearly mute then; the First Tongue lived in her but had not yet learned to come out. The sound was low and questioning, and her eyes were enormous, and I understood her perfectly: *all of them?*

"All of them," I said. "They aren't ours to keep, little one. They're only ours to carry for a while. And carrying ends."

She frowned at me with the ferocious moral seriousness of the very young, and I had to look away, because she was right and we both knew it, and being right does not make burning a library any easier.

Then she did something I did not expect. She walked into the workshop—past me, past Ovid's warning hand—and stood among the shelves, and hummed.

Not the hounds' humming. Something gentler. A lullaby-shape, a falling-and-rising, and though I had no grammar for it yet, I felt it land on the bones like light through a window. For one moment the workshop did not smell of dust and lamp-oil. It smelled of every kitchen those four hundred and eleven people had ever stood in. The dead, if they had heard it, were not frightened. I choose to believe they heard it as a blessing. I will believe that until I die, and possibly after.

Then she came back out, and took my hand, and nodded once: *now.*

So I lit the flint.

* * *

Fire is faster than memory and slower than regret. Those are the only observations I will offer on the subject.

We stood in the dooryard and watched thirty years go up. The roof caught first, then the shelves, and the heat came out in waves that pushed against our faces like a living thing objecting. Ovid had turned away—he could not watch, he said, and I did not ask him why, because I suspected the answer involved altars and I was not feeling charitable toward altars that morning. Mara watched everything, unblinking, her frost-scarred fingers tight around mine.

And here is the thing I have never told anyone, because there was no one left to tell: as the bones burned, I heard them.

All of them. At once. Four hundred and eleven whispers rising together out of the smoke—not screaming, nothing so dramatic—the way a chord is not a note. *Tell Tomas the fence. It didn't hurt. She knew. Should've sold the cow.* Every unfinished sentence in the valley, released all at once into the freezing air, and for three heartbeats I stood inside the whole of Hollowmere's love for itself, its mending and its cows and its small kindnesses, and I thought: *this is what the Court wants to eat. This. Exactly this.*

Well. Let them come and find ash.

The grief did not leave me. I want to be honest about that, because stories like to pretend that resolve is clean. Resolve is grief that has been given a job. It stands up in the morning and puts on its boots and does not discuss its feelings, because there is no time, and because the feelings would lay it flat. My sister's wooden face rode against my breastbone under my coat, and every step I took toward the valley's edge, I took *away* from something, and the taking-away became a kind of engine. Hatred would have been simpler. I reached for hatred several times and found it thin gruel. What I had instead was worse and better: the absolute refusal to let one more unfinished sentence be swallowed.

"They're moving," Ovid said, near midmorning. He stood on the boundary path squinting east, where the frost thinned toward the hills. "The hounds. Or—" he paused, listening to something I couldn't hear, "—no. Not moving. *Waiting.* They know she has to come out eventually. Valleys only have two doors."

"Then we'll use the one they're not watching."

"There isn't—"

"There is," said Mara.

We both looked at her. She pointed south, past the meetinghouse, past the old stone quarry where the valley's dead had been cut for generations, to a fold in the hills I had known my entire life and never once thought about, because it was not a door. It was a sheep track. It was bramble and scree and a drop that had killed two goats and one unfortunate peddler in living memory.

But she pointed at it the way Maren's blade-bone pointed north: with certainty.

"The boundary stones," Ovid breathed. "The covenant runs along the stones, but the stones are broken at the quarry—there's a gap, there's always been a gap, the Court wouldn't watch a gap because nothing with sense would—" He stopped. Looked at the child. Looked at me. "She reads the covenant. Like text."

"She reads everything like text," I said. "Come along, priest. You can marvel while we climb."

* * *

The crossing itself I will describe plainly, because plainly is all it allows.

The sheep track was exactly as cruel as advertised. We climbed through bramble that took payment in blood, over scree that shifted like bad conscience, past the quarry where the pale scars of old cutting still showed in the rockface. Mara went first, sure-footed as a cat, turning back to check on us with an expression of polite concern that I found frankly insulting from a person half my height. Ovid prayed under his breath, which I pretended not to hear, and swore under his breath, which I pretended not to enjoy.

At the top of the fold there was a moment—a single stride—where the wind changed.

I have tried a hundred times to describe what happened next, and I have settled on this: it was like deafness ending.

For thirty years I had lived in silence and called it quiet. I had told myself I preferred it. I had built a whole life on the honest lie that a world without magic was a cleaner world, a kinder world, a world where the dead stayed decently dead and the living stayed decently unbothered. And then my boot came down on the far side of the fold, past the last broken boundary stone, and the world *turned on*.

Color first. The snow wasn't white anymore; it was silver, threaded with blue, singing faintly at the edges of vision. Then weight—my own body suddenly heavier and lighter at once, as if I had been walking through water all my life and had forgotten water was there. Then sound: a hum underneath everything, vast and slow, the covenant's dying heartbeat still audible now that I stood outside the valley's numbness, and it was *failing*, I could hear it failing, a great bell cracked but still ringing, and somewhere in that ring was a word I almost understood.

I sat down in the snow. I am not ashamed of that either.

"Oh," I said. Which was not adequate, but language rarely is, at the important moments.

Beside me, Mara stood with her face lifted to the sky, and she was crying without noise, tears freezing on her cheeks, and her mouth was open, and out of it—thin, wavering, unmistakable—came a word.

Not a hum. A word. Her first.

I did not know its meaning yet. I know it now, and I will not write it here, because some words are load-bearing and this page is not strong enough. But I felt its meaning the way you feel a struck bell in your sternum, and Ovid fell to his knees in the snow beside her, and the disgrace on his face rearranged itself into something older than disgrace, which was awe.

Down in the valley behind us, my workshop smoked its last. Ahead of us the hills rolled away south and west, silver-threaded, humming, full of roads I had never walked and dangers I could not count and a hearth, somewhere, waiting to be relit.

The hounds' humming rose behind us—east, at the proper door, patient and terrible. They had not seen us leave. But they would smell the smoke. They would read the ash. And smoke, Ovid had told me, remembers where it came from.

I put my hand on Mara's head, and my other hand on the applewood face of my sister over my heart, and I stood up.

"Well," I said, to the child, to the priest, to the dead, to the whole ringing treacherous beautiful world. "Onward, then."

And the world, humming, agreed.
