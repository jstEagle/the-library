## 7. The Road of Small Ghosts

The world outside the valley was made of doors.

That was the first thing Mara understood about it, walking behind the tall woman with the sawdust in her hair. Every hill was a door lying on its side. Every river a door that had been left open too long and now everything inside had spilled out. The dead woman in the workshop had shown her this once, in the last word she ever gave anyone: *hearth*, spoken through a rib like a secret passed hand to hand under a table.

Doors everywhere. And almost all of them broken.

Mara did not speak. Speaking was a door too, and hers had been shut for so long she had forgotten which side of it she lived on. But she could see. Oh, she could see what the tall woman could not, what the frost and the grey hounds and the burning workshop had all been trying to tell her in their clumsy loud ways: the road they walked was crowded.

* * *

They came out of the valley on the second morning, over a shoulder of stone where the pines bent away from something invisible, like hair combed by a giant's thumb. The tall woman—Beatrix, though Mara only knew her as *the one who smells of bone-dust and woodsmoke*—stopped at the crest and went very still.

Mara felt it before she saw it. A warmth crawling up her arms like sunlight remembered rather than felt. The air past the boundary shimmered faintly, the way water shimmers when a stone has just been pulled from it.

"Stay close," Beatrix said, and her voice had a crack down the middle of it, like a plate dropped once and glued.

Then they stepped through, and the world *roared*.

Not with sound. With everything else. Color came back so hard it hurt—the grass suddenly green the way fire is orange, insistent, showing off. Wind arrived with opinions. Somewhere below, a bird shouted a thing three times because no one had answered it the first two times. And underneath all of it, humming like a struck bell that would not finish ringing: the ember-light. Threads of it, gold and rust-colored, running through the ground, through the trees, through Beatrix herself, who stood swaying with her eyes closed and her mouth open as if she were drinking.

Mara did not drink it in. She recognized it. It recognized her.

The threads leaned toward her. All of them. Subtle, mostly—a field of wheat bending in wind that wasn't there—but she felt every stalk of it turn her way, the way you feel eyes on your back in an empty room.

She took Beatrix's sleeve. Not her hand. Hands were for people who trusted things.

They walked.

* * *

By midday, Mara had counted forty-one ghosts.

Not the proper kind—not the ones with faces and grievances and unfinished business, the kind that rattled chains if they'd read the right stories. These were smaller. Fainter. They hung in the low places of the land, in ditches and doorless doorways and the shade under bridges, like breath hanging on cold glass. They looked less like people than like the *idea* of people, the way you remember a room after leaving it: mostly edges, mostly absence, one bright detail refusing to fade.

One was only a pair of hands, folded, resting on nothing. One was a laugh—you could see it, a small silver shape like a fish, circling the same spot above a ruined well. One was a woman-shaped hole in the afternoon light, standing at the edge of a field, facing a house that had burned down so long ago even the burn had died.

They were leaking out of the world. That was what Mara understood, watching them, and the understanding sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. The covenant—the great woven thing, the bell that rang beneath everything—was fraying, and these small spirits were slipping through the gaps. Souls too little to be missed. A grandmother's hum. A dog's devotion. A child's whole self, maybe, if the child had been quiet enough in life.

No one else could see them. She checked. Beatrix walked past a ghost sitting in the middle of the road—an old man made of fog and patience—and stepped through him without a shiver, muttering about the weather.

But when Mara stopped, and looked, and *waited*—

The old man looked back.

His face was the least finished part of him, smudged like a thumbprint, but she felt his attention gather, slow as honey, until all of it was on her. Then he did something none of the fading ones ever did.

He bowed. Small. Almost nothing. The bow of someone who has been waiting a very long time to be seen by anyone at all.

Mara's throat tightened around the word that rose in her, the first word in months that wanted out badly enough to hurt:

"*Sela.*"

It came out as breath, not voice. But it was the First Tongue, and the First Tongue does not need volume. It means *I see you*, and also *you are not lost*, and also, in its deeper grammar, *stay*.

The old ghost straightened. For a moment he was more than fog—he had shoulders, a coat with buttons, a hat pushed back the way men push hats when they mean to stay awhile. He smiled with a mouth that was mostly suggestion.

Then the wind came up the road, and he thinned, and thinned, and was gone—not destroyed, just scattered, a hand of cards shuffled back into the deck of the world.

Mara stood in the empty road with her word still hanging in the air, and found that she was crying without having decided to, and that Beatrix had stopped twenty paces ahead and turned around, and was looking at her with an expression Mara had no name for yet but would later learn was called *recognition*.

"You said something," Beatrix said carefully. "Just now."

Mara touched her own lips, surprised. Had she? It hadn't felt like speaking. It had felt like opening a window.

"What was it?"

Mara crouched, found a stick, and wrote in the dust of the road. Not the letters Beatrix used—those she was still learning, crooked and shy. She wrote the First Tongue, the true shapes, the ones that predated ink.

*Sela. I-see-you.*

Beatrix stared at it a long time. When she finally spoke, her voice had gone quiet in the particular way it went quiet around the bones in her workshop, the way it went quiet around anything that deserved respect or fear or both.

"That's not any language I know," she said. "And I know the dead languages. That's the trouble with my life." She crouched beside the writing, not touching it. "Where did you learn it?"

Mara shrugged, which was honest. She hadn't learned it anywhere. It was simply there, the way hunger is there, the way knowing your own name is there—if you have a name. Hers had been taken along with her voice, or perhaps her voice had been taken along with her name; the two losses were braided together too tightly to pull apart.

Instead of answering, she pointed—at the spot where the old man had stood, then at her own eyes, then at Beatrix's.

Beatrix followed the gesture. Looked at the empty road. Looked at nothing, and understood that it was not nothing.

"How many?" she asked softly.

Mara held up both hands, twice, and then kept going, folding fingers down and up again, because forty-one had been hours ago and the number had grown.

Beatrix exhaled through her teeth. "Right," she said, in the tone of a woman adding another log to a fire that was already much too large. "Well. Keep talking to them, then. If they can hear you." A pause. Her jaw worked. "Someone should."

* * *

They walked for three days, and Mara learned the grammar of ghosts.

Not the First Tongue's grammar—that came later, and it nearly broke her, learning that words could be load-bearing beams holding up reality. No, this was older and simpler: the grammar of being seen.

The small spirits wanted nothing. That was the strange mercy of them. The big ghosts, the strong ones, the ones with names still attached—they wanted things. Vengeance, messages, one more hour, one more lie corrected. But these faded ones, the leak-throughs, the ones the failing covenant couldn't hold anymore—they only wanted witness. To be perceived once, truly, before the wind took them apart.

So Mara gave them that.

She learned their habits. They gathered in the morning in the hollows, where the night's cold pooled, drawn to anything warm-hearted the way moths are drawn to the wrong kind of flame. They liked crossroads, though they never chose a direction. They avoided running water—not out of fear, she sensed, but out of courtesy, the way you don't interrupt someone mid-sentence.

And they were starting to follow her.

At first it was one—the laugh-fish from the well, which detached itself from its ruin and drifted after them at the height of a person's chest, circling lazily whenever they stopped to rest. Then a pair of hands joined it, floating folded and patient. Then a small grey shape that might have been a cat, might have been a sock, might have been a very devoted cloud.

By the fourth day, Mara walked at the center of a procession of eleven small ghosts, visible to no one but her, silent as snowfall, keeping pace like children told to stay close in a market.

Beatrix noticed the third time Mara laughed.

It startled them both. The sound came out of her rusty and strange, half cough, half birdsong—a laugh at the grey-cat-sock-cloud, which had tried to sit on a ghost's lap that didn't exist and fallen through it, and tried again, and fallen through again, with the persistence of all small creatures who have not yet learned physics applies to them.

Beatrix turned. "What?" she demanded, not unkindly. "What's funny?"

Mara pointed. Described, with hands, the falling, the trying again, the falling.

Beatrix looked at the empty air beside the campfire. Looked at Mara. Looked back at the air.

"I'm going to choose to believe you," she said at last, "because the alternative is that I've adopted a child who laughs at nothing, and I refuse to examine that." She fed the fire another branch. But her mouth was doing something at the corner that Mara suspected, on other faces, was called a smile.

That night, Mara spoke again.

She hadn't planned it. The fire was low, Beatrix asleep sitting up with a carving knife still in her hand—she slept the way fortresses stand—and the ghosts had gathered close for the warmth of being near the living, arranged around Mara like stones around a hearthstone. The laugh-fish circled her head. The folded hands rested, for once, on her actual knee, feather-light, the weight of a sparrow's opinion.

And the word rose in her, wanting out, and this time she let it.

"*Nessa.*"

*I am here.*

The effect was immediate and enormous and entirely silent. Every ghost turned. The firelight seemed to lean in. Somewhere far off—miles, or realms—the great frayed bell of the covenant rang once, faintly, as if something vast had shifted in its sleep and noticed a small voice using its language.

The ghosts began, one by one, to press closer. Not touching. Never touching. Just nearer. And Mara felt it happen in her chest, the thing she had been missing without knowing its name since before her voice was taken:

Company.

Not family. Family was a door she wasn't ready to open. But company. Witnesses. Eleven small fading someones who knew she could see them, and stayed anyway.

She said "*nessa*" again, softer, and the folded hands squeezed her knee once, gently, like a promise made by someone with no mouth to keep it.

* * *

On the fifth day, the road changed.

Mara felt it in her teeth first—a wrongness, a note played flat. The ember-threads in the ground, which had run alongside them for days like loyal dogs, began to thin. To stutter. By noon they were flickering like a candle in a draft, and the color had gone out of the world again, leached to ash-grey and bone-white, and the small ghosts grew agitated, drifting close against her back as if she were a wall they could hide behind.

Beatrix stopped at the top of a rise and did not say anything for a long moment.

Below them, the land lay black. Not burned recently—burned *long* ago, generations past, and never healed. A scar across the world, miles wide, glassy in places where the heat had melted stone into mirror. Nothing grew. Nothing moved. Even the wind seemed to go around it rather than across.

And in the middle distance, at the edge of sight, something was standing in the black. Several somethings. Tall, grey, patient. Watching the road. Watching, Mara was quite sure, *her*.

The small ghosts pressed so close against her that she could feel them like cold silk. The laugh-fish hid behind her ear. Even the folded hands gripped her sleeve now instead of resting.

Beatrix's hand found her shoulder. Steady. Warm. Alive.

"We go around," Beatrix said quietly. "We go around, and we keep moving, and you stay where I can see you." A pause. Her thumb pressed once, briefly, against Mara's collarbone—a touch that was almost a word. "Can you do that?"

Mara nodded. Then, because the ghosts were listening, and because she was tired of doors staying shut, she raised her hand and wrote in the air between them, in firelight and First Tongue both, the smallest sentence she knew:

*Nessa. Sela. Umma.*

*I am here. I see you. We go together.*

Beatrix read it. Something moved behind her eyes—grief and resolve shaking hands.

"Together," she agreed.

And far off in the black glass, the tall grey figures began, without hurry, to walk.
