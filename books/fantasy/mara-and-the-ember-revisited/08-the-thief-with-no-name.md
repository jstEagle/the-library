## 8. The Thief With No Name

Here's the thing nobody tells you about selling your name: the debt-mage doesn't take it all at once. He takes it in pieces, like a butcher breaking down a carcass. First he takes the sound of it — the way your mother said it when you tracked mud through her kitchen. Then he takes the shape of it, the letters you'd scratched into fence posts and schoolroom slates. Last of all, he takes the *using* of it, so that when someone asks who you are, your mouth opens and produces nothing but a shrug.

I sold mine to a man called Ferrow Vane for eleven silver marks and a hot meal, which even at seventeen I recognized as a bad price. But I was hungry, and names are heavy things to carry when you've got nothing else in your pockets, and I figured I could always steal it back later.

That was nine years ago. I have not stolen it back. Not because I haven't tried. Because every time I get close to remembering what I was called, something in my chest goes cold and quiet, like a door closing in an empty house, and I find myself thinking about other things instead. That's the real cruelty of it. Vane didn't just take my name. He took my wanting it back.

So call me Sef. It's not short for anything. It's not anything at all. It's the noise I make when people insist on addressing me, and it has served me well enough across three cities and one very regrettable season on a fishing barge.

Which brings me, by a road I did not intend to travel, to the night I tried to rob a priest, a bone-carver, and a child, and got the worst surprise of my life: they were worth more than their purses.

* * *

I'd been watching them since Corvane's south gate. You learn to read travelers the way a fisherman reads water — where the ripples are, where the current runs slow. The tall one with the burned collar moved like a soldier pretending to be a scholar; his purse hung fat and careless, which meant either arrogance or bait. The woman walked like winter itself, all grey wool and closed doors, and she kept her hands wrapped around a bundle of carved bones the way other pilgrims clutch relics. And between them went the girl — small, silent, wrapped in a coat too big for her, her eyes doing a strange thing I couldn't place at first.

It took me half a mile to work out what it was. She wasn't looking at things the way you or I look at things. She was looking *through* them, at something underneath, the way I imagine a mole reads the world through its skin. Every few steps she'd stop and touch a wall, a fence post, a dead tree, and her fingers would move — little patterns, deliberate, like she was writing on whatever the world was made of underneath the world.

I told myself: easy marks. A madwoman, a defrocked priest, a mute brat. Three purses, maybe four if the priest wore rings under those gloves. I told myself that right up until the moment I had my hand in the priest's coat pocket and found it empty except for a scrap of paper that read, in neat clerical script: *Left pocket. Try the right.*

I want it noted for the record that I did not panic. I withdrew my hand with dignity, turned, and found all three of them looking at me. The priest had stopped walking. The woman had stopped walking. The girl was looking directly at me with those through-the-world eyes, and her head tilted, very slowly, like a bird considering a worm.

"Well," I said. "This is embarrassing."

"Is it?" the priest asked. He had a voice like a bell that had been dropped once. "In my experience, embarrassment is the least of a thief's problems."

"That depends entirely on whose thief he is," the woman said. Her voice was flat as slate, but there was something underneath it, some old current. "And whether anyone is coming to collect him."

And that — that is how I learned that the world had gone strange while I wasn't paying attention. Because the woman looked at me, really looked, the way you look at a lock before picking it, and then she said, quietly, almost to herself:

"He's already marked."

* * *

Let me back up. Three days earlier, I'd done a job in Corvane. Small job. Clean job. Lift a ledger from the counting-house of a merchant named Aldous Wren — no relation, presumably, to anyone important — who had been skimming from the city's relief funds, and deliver it to a widow's lawyer who wanted leverage. Standard moral arithmetic. I sleep fine.

Except when I cracked the ledger's clasp, there'd been a slip of paper inside that didn't belong. Grey paper. Ash-grey, the color of paper that's been burned and then un-burned, which I want to stress is not a thing paper can do. On it, in handwriting like frost forming, were words I couldn't read — but my eyes could read them, which was worse, because the reading happened somewhere behind my eyes rather than in them.

And ever since, I'd been followed. Not by men. By weather. There was a haze that came down certain streets ahead of me, a greyness in the air like smoke that hadn't decided to be fire yet, and dogs wouldn't look at me, and twice I'd caught my reflection in shop glass wearing an expression I hadn't made.

So when the bone-carver woman said *he's already marked*, I did what any sensible professional does.

I ran.

I made it eleven steps. I'm proud of the eleven steps. Then the greyness came off the rooftops like water off a table's edge, and the street went cold, and something stood up out of the haze at the end of the lane. I say *stood* because it had height and a kind of posture, but it was made of smoke the way a wave is made of water, and where its face should have been there was a suggestion of features, the way a suggestion of a face appears in wood grain if you stare long enough.

Behind me, the priest said something in a language I didn't know, and the air went warm. The smoke-thing flinched. Actually flinched, like a dog hearing a whistle only it can hear.

Then the girl stepped past me.

I grabbed for her — I swear I did, some reflex about children and monsters — and my hand closed on empty coat-sleeve, and she was walking toward the thing with her chin up and her fists at her sides, and she looked at it the way you look at a debt collector who has come to the wrong house.

She opened her mouth. Nothing came out. Of course nothing came out; she was mute, everyone knew she was mute, the priest had been signing to her for days.

But the *air* spoke.

There's no better way to put it. The space where her voice should have been filled instead, and the word that filled it wasn't a sound so much as a pressure, a single syllable that I felt in my teeth and behind my sternum, and the smoke-thing came apart. Not violently. It came apart the way fog comes apart when the sun finds it — unraveling, thinning, going politely back to wherever smoke goes when it's dismissed.

The silence afterward rang like a struck bell.

The girl turned around and looked at me. And here is the moment I will be honest about, because honesty is cheap now that everyone who could hold it over me is either dead or fictional: she looked at me, and I felt *seen*. Not seen like a mark sees a cutpurse. Seen like a locked room sees light under the door.

Because she was silent the way I was silent. Hers was on the outside, where everyone could pity it. Mine was on the inside, where nobody could even find it to pity. But it was the same silence. The same cold door in the chest. The same missing word.

She pointed at me. Then at her own chest. Then she made a gesture — small hands opening, like a bird releasing something.

The priest translated, sounding like a man reading his own death warrant: "She says you have a hole where a name should be. She says she can feel it." He paused. "She says the Court can feel it too. That's why they're following you. They collect... gaps. Gaps are where they pour themselves in."

"Wonderful," I said. "I'm a load-bearing absence."

"You joke," the woman said, and her slate-flat voice had an edge on it now, "because you don't yet understand what's hunting you. Give it a week."

* * *

They fed me that night, which is the oldest trick in recruitment and it worked on me anyway, because I hadn't eaten since morning and pride is a poor sauce. We camped in the shell of a waystation shrine off the north road — Beatrix, the bone-carver, built a small fire with the economy of someone who has built ten thousand fires and resents each one personally. Ovid, the priest, produced bread and hard cheese from a pack that seemed deeper than physics allowed. The girl — Mara, Ovid signed, though Beatrix pronounced it like a word in another language, careful, weighted — sat close to the fire and watched me eat with open, unnerving curiosity.

"So," I said, around cheese. "You're fugitives. From a court. Of ash."

"The Ashen Court," Ovid said. "Yes."

"And they eat memories."

"They consume them. To fuel themselves. Memory is power, and power must be—" He stopped himself, jaw tightening, like the sentence was a prayer he'd sworn off. "Yes. They eat memories."

"Right." I dusted crumbs off my knees. "Well. Good luck with all that. I'll be leaving at dawn, alone, in the opposite direction, and we'll all agree this never happened."

Beatrix didn't look up from the bone she was carving. "You'll be dead by midsummer."

"Cheerful."

"I bury people for a living. Or I did." The knife whispered along the bone's curve. "The Court doesn't chase thieves. It chases openings. You're an opening now, boy. A gap with legs. They'll pour themselves into you and wear you like a glove, and then they'll walk you back to us and ask us to trust you, and one of us will, because we're sentimental fools, and it will cost us everything." She looked up then, and her eyes were the exact grey of the valley mist I'd heard about in songs. "Unless you come with us and let the girl teach you to close the gap."

I laughed. I'd like that noted too. I laughed, genuinely, at the idea of a mute child teaching a professional cynic anything.

Mara reached over and touched my wrist.

Just that. Two fingers, light as ash settling. And the cold door in my chest — the one that had been shut for nine years, the one I'd stopped noticing the way you stop noticing a limp — creaked. Just barely. Just enough for a draft to come through. And in the draft, for half a heartbeat, I smelled my mother's kitchen. Woodsmoke and onions and the tallow candle she burned down to nubs because we were poor and she was stubborn.

I didn't cry. Professionals don't cry. But my hand shook, and Mara watched it shake with an expression of terrible gentleness, and then she pulled her fingers back and signed something to Ovid.

"She says," Ovid began, and his voice caught, "she says the wound is the same. Yours and hers. She says—" He looked at his own hands, at the faded burn-scars of an oath-mark being torn away. "She says the Court took your name the way they took her voice, and that means you both know what it is to be addressed by nothing."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, a dog was refusing to bark.

"Eleven silver marks," I said finally. "That's what I sold it for. Eleven marks and a meal. Do you know how many times I've done that arithmetic? Nine years of it. Some nights I figure the meal was good enough. Most nights—"

"Most nights you can't remember why it mattered," Beatrix said. "That's the design. They don't just take. They take the taking."

I stared at her. "How do you know that?"

For a long moment she didn't answer. The knife stilled against the bone. And I saw something cross her face — grief, yes, but grief with a strange seam in it, like a wall that had been plastered over in a hurry.

"Because I know what it is to lose something and not be able to find the shape of the loss," she said at last. "Eat your cheese, thief."

* * *

I stayed. Not because of gratitude — gratitude is for people with something to lose. Not because of the girl's eerie kindness, though I thought about that touch on my wrist more than I admitted, lying awake under the broken shrine roof, listening to Ovid snore like a landslide and Beatrix not sleep at all.

I stayed because of the smoke.

It came again two nights later, on the road above the river country. Not one of them this time — three, moving through the dusk like thoughts you try not to think, and they weren't even looking for us. They were sweeping the countryside, methodical, patient, and when their attention dragged across our campfire like a lantern beam, it snagged on me. On the gap in me. I felt it the way a tooth feels cold water: a bright, intimate wrongness, something reaching into the hollow where Sef-the-name-used-to-be and testing the fit.

Mara was asleep. Ovid was on watch and stood up fast, hands raised, and I saw the old fear on him — the fear of a man meeting colleagues. Beatrix put herself between me and the dark with a carved bone held out like a knife, and I remember thinking: *she doesn't even know me. Why is she standing there.*

The pressure lifted. The smoke-things moved on, unhurried, the way creditors move on — not gone, merely rescheduled.

And I sat there by the dead fire with my heart hammering and understood, with the clarity that only arrives at the worst possible times, that I was never going to outrun this. You can't run from a debt collector when the debt is written into your body. Nine years I'd survived by being unremarkable, unattached, unnamed — and it turned out unnamed was exactly the problem. My whole strategy had been the trap.

So I stayed. And the strange thing — the thing I keep turning over like a coin that shouldn't exist — is that staying started to feel less like surrender and more like choice. Beatrix taught Mara letters by firelight, and Mara taught Beatrix words that made the air bend, and Ovid cooked like a man apologizing, and none of them asked me for anything except to walk when they walked and watch the dark when it was my turn.

On the fourth morning, Mara pressed something into my hand. A sliver of bone, polished smooth, with a single symbol carved into it. I didn't know what it meant. But when I closed my fist around it, the cold door in my chest didn't open — it just felt, for the first time in nine years, like a door with a key somewhere in the world.

Ovid watched me pocket it and said, quietly, "She's named you, in her way. The symbol means *found*."

"Don't tell anyone," I said. "I have a reputation."

But that night, when the greyness gathered on the hills behind us — thicker than before, deliberate, a whole court of smoke deciding to take an interest — it was Mara who went still and pointed east, toward the river country, and Beatrix who said, "We need to cross the Vell," and Ovid who went pale and said, "The ferryman's toll," in the voice of a man naming his own executioner.

Nobody explained. Nobody had to. I'd spent my life around debts. I knew the tone.

"What's the toll?" I asked anyway.

Beatrix looked at the river-dark horizon, and her hand went to the bundle of bones at her chest, and she said, "Memories. True ones. The ferryman doesn't take coin."

I laughed again. Less convincingly this time.

Because I'd already done the arithmetic, see. Nine years of it. And I knew exactly which memory I'd pay, and which one I'd fight to keep, and I knew — God help me, I knew — that the ferryman would find a way to take the wrong one anyway.

That's the thing about debts. They always come due heavier than the contract said.
