## 9. The Court of Cinders

I was twenty-two years old the first time I fed a memory to a god, and I want you to understand that it felt like kindness.

That's the part no one believes. They imagine the Ashen Court as monsters from the beginning—crowned skeletons in robes of smoke, cackling over a cauldron of stolen childhoods. And yes, eventually, that's more or less what we became. But monsters are rarely born. Mostly they're *promoted*. Mostly they start as the person who volunteers for the terrible job because someone has to, and then discovers, one small compromise at a time, that the terrible job has made them terrible, and by then the job is all they know how to do.

I was a novice of the Ember Order, third-ranked in my cohort at memorization and last in humility, which my instructors said was the correct order of priorities. The covenant still held, then. Barely. It had been fraying for two hundred years even before I took my vows—everyone knew it, nobody said it aloud, because saying a thing aloud gives it weight, and weight is what the covenant ran on.

You don't know how the covenant works. Beatrix certainly didn't, back when she let me into her valley with a knife hidden in her sleeve and questions sharpened on both edges. So let me tell you, because this is the story of how I learned it, and learning it ruined me in ways I'm still cataloguing.

The covenant is a promise kept between the living world and the Ember Realm—the country of the dead, the warm dark where souls go to be remembered until they're ready to move on. Every soul that passes through leaves something behind: not ash, not essence, but *memory*. The memory of being loved. Of loving. The shape of a mother's voice, the smell of a particular bread, the exact blue of an afternoon in June when nothing hurt. These memories pool in the Ember Realm like rainwater in a cistern, and the cistern feeds the Hearth, and the Hearth holds the door between life and death open just wide enough.

A door held open by love. That was the original engineering. Sentimental, yes. Also load-bearing.

The problem—and there is always a problem—is that memories fade. Grief fades. The living forget their dead, gently, inevitably, the way a river smooths a stone. And every forgotten memory is a cup taken out of the cistern. For centuries the flow was enough. Then it wasn't.

* * *

The year I took my vows, the Hearthkeepers reported a shortfall. Not a collapse—a shortfall. The door between realms had narrowed by a hand's width. Souls were taking longer to cross. Ghosts were lingering who should have moved on, confused, hungry, unable to find the warm dark because the warm dark had grown dimmer.

The Ember Order convened. I remember the chamber: nine elders seated in a ring of hearthstones, each stone holding a flame that had burned since the First Forging. I remember Elder Maerin, who had taught me liturgy, standing to speak, her voice steady in the way that people's voices go steady right before they say something unforgivable.

"The shortfall is manageable," she said. "If we supplement."

Supplement. A clean word. What she meant was: the dead leave memories behind whether they intend to or not. What she meant was: we could take a little more than they left. Voluntarily, at first. We would ask the dying to gift one memory beyond their natural tithe—one happy memory, freely given, to keep the door open for everyone else.

And here is the horror, the true horror, the one I have carried thirty years like a stone in my boot: *they said yes.* Nearly all of them. Dying men and women, asked to surrender the memory of their wedding day or their daughter's laugh so that other people's dead could rest—they gave it up with tears and gratitude. Because we framed it as sacrifice. Because we told them it was holy. Because it *was* holy, in the way that a knife is honest.

I stood in that chamber at twenty-two years old and thought: what a beautiful thing we do.

God, I could strangle that boy. But he's me. He's always going to be me.

* * *

For fifty years, the supplementation worked. I rose through the Order. I learned the rites of gathering—how to stand at a deathbed with a vessel of ember-glass and draw the gifted memory out gently, without tearing, the way you'd draw a splinter. I got good at it. I got *proud* of it. There's a particular arrogance that comes to those who handle grief professionally; you start believing grief is a substance you understand better than the people drowning in it.

Then came the plague years. You may have heard of them—the Grey Sweats, the historians call them, though the historians were mostly writing afterward, from cities that survived. Three summers of fever. Whole villages emptied. And the covenant, which had been balanced on a knife's edge of exactly-enough, went into deficit overnight.

Too many deaths. Too many souls crossing at once, each owed their passage through the warm dark, and the cistern running dry because the living were too busy burying to remember, and the dead were arriving faster than their memories could pool.

The Hearthkeepers did the arithmetic. I did some of it myself—I was junior enough to be given the ledgers and senior enough to understand them. The numbers said: the door closes in four years. Four years, and the Ember Realm goes dark, and every soul in passage is lost, and the boundary between living and dead becomes a wall, and walls, in my experience, breed worse things than doors ever did.

Unless we supplemented more.

"From whom?" I asked Maerin, in the chamber, in the ring of stones. "The dying have already given everything they can spare."

Maerin looked at me with such gentleness. That's what I remember most—not cruelty, not ambition, but gentleness. She had been taking memories for fifty years and she had somehow remained kind, or something shaped like kindness, the way a wax figure is shaped like a man.

"From the dead themselves," she said.

* * *

Here is the doctrine she taught me, and I will teach it to you straight, because you deserve to hear the devil's argument at its strongest:

The dead do not need their memories. That was the whole thesis. A soul crossing into the Ember Realm carries its memories with it, yes—but what does it *do* with them? It remembers its life, it grieves its losses, it lingers on old joys. And then, given time, it moves on, and the memories dissolve anyway. Nothing is lost in the end. The river takes everything eventually.

So if the Order skimmed from the passing souls—if we drew off the memories *before* dissolution, harvested what would rot regardless—were we stealing? Or were we composting? Taking what the universe intended to waste and using it to hold open the door for ten thousand grieving families?

Memory is power. That phrase wasn't ours originally—it comes from the First Tongue, from the oldest liturgies, and it meant something gentler then: *to remember someone is to hold power over forgetting on their behalf.* But Maerin read it the other direction, and once you see it her way you cannot unsee it. Memory is power. Power must be fed. And the dead are, by definition, past caring.

We called ourselves the Court of Cinders within the decade. Not Ashen—that name came later, after the second corruption, the one I'll get to, the one I caused. At first we were Cinders: what remains after fire, still warm, still useful. We wore grey because grey is humble. We told ourselves grey stories about necessity.

I gathered for eleven years. Do you want to know what it feels like, drawing a memory out of a newly-dead soul? It feels like lifting a sleeping child. The memory doesn't fight. It doesn't even wake. It comes away soft and complete—the wedding, the bread, the blue afternoon—and the soul it belonged to continues on toward the Hearth none the wiser, lighter by one joy it will never miss.

That's the lie I lived inside: *none the wiser.* Lighter by one joy it will never miss.

Except the souls did miss things. Slowly, over years, the Ember Realm changed. Souls arrived lighter and lighter, because we'd been skimming deeper than the surplus—we'd crossed into principal long before anyone admitted it. And light souls cross fast, but they arrive *thin*. They forget why they're moving on. They forget who waited for them. The warm dark filled up with the half-remembering dead, ghosts of ghosts, drifting through a realm that should have been a home and had become a waiting room with the lights turned down.

And the living world noticed. People began reporting dreams of dead mothers who looked at them without recognition. Seances—folk customs, harmless ones—went strange, the spirits answering questions with questions. *Who are you? Why am I here?*

We told ourselves it was turbulence. Transition pains. The medicine working.

It was not the medicine working.

* * *

Now I come to my own part in it, and I'll tell it plainly because I've earned no softer telling.

By my fortieth year I was a Gatherer-Prime, which meant I supervised the harvests and trained novices in the gentle art of taking. And I discovered—or rather, I was the first to formalize, which in institutions amounts to the same thing—that memories could be drawn from the *living*.

Not stolen. Never stolen; we maintained that fiction to the end. *Gifted.* A farmer whose crops failed could bring us his memory of a good harvest and receive grain. A widow could trade her wedding day for a winter's firewood. A mother could sell the memory of her son's face—he'd died in the Sweats, she couldn't bear it, she *asked*, she wept with relief when it was gone—and walk home lighter, able to sleep, free of the grief that had been killing her by inches.

Do you see? Do you see how good it sounded? We weren't predators. We were *surgeons*. We removed pain. That people came to us voluntarily, gratefully, in queues sometimes—that was our proof of virtue. The Court grew rich in memories, and the memories fed the Hearth, and the door stayed open, and everywhere we went people blessed us.

And the villages where we worked most began to forget themselves. Not dramatically. A festival here whose purpose no one recalled. A song sung with the words worn off. Children who couldn't say why their grandparents' house was built facing east. Communities hollowed out from the inside, cheerful and vacant, like houses after a flood.

Beatrix saw one of those villages, later. Corvane. She'll tell you what it looks like when a place trades its history for safety. I never went back to check on mine. Cowardice has excellent logistics.

* * *

The Court's final corruption—the one that made us Ashen instead of merely Cindered—came from within, as these things do. A faction arose, led by a Keeper named Vess, who argued that if memory was power, and power must be fed, then the Court itself deserved feeding. That guarding the covenant entitled its guardians to sustenance beyond duty. That a Gatherer-Prime who spent his days handling the raw stuff of human joy ought to be permitted to *taste*.

Tasting. That's what Vess called it. Drawing a memory into yourself instead of into the vessels. Feeling a stranger's wedding from the inside. Wearing a dead man's pride in his daughter for an hour.

I refused, at first. I want that on the record, whatever the record is worth now. I refused Vess's invitation three times.

On the fourth occasion she didn't invite me. She simply performed the rite in front of me, on a novice who'd failed his examinations, and I watched the boy's face as she drank down his memory of his mother's kitchen, and I watched him smile as it left him—smile, because the rite was designed to feel like release—and I understood with total clarity that I was watching the future, and that the future did not require my consent.

I stayed eleven more years after that. Read that sentence again. I *stayed*. Because staying was comfortable, and leaving was dangerous, and because I told myself I could do more good inside the rot than outside it—the universal anthem of every collaborator since the invention of institutions. I rose. I administered. I signed the harvest quotas. I tasted twice, in weak winters, and I will not describe it to you except to say that they were the two finest hours of my life and I have hated myself with professional thoroughness ever since.

And then came the child.

* * *

I won't tell the whole story of Mara here—it isn't mine to tell, and parts of it I only learned afterward, from records I stole on my way out. But you need the shape of it, because everything I've described, all that slow rot across three hundred years, converges on one small mute girl who speaks the language the covenant was written in.

The First Tongue isn't a language, properly speaking. It's the covenant's own grammar, the syntax in which the original promise was forged, and it survives in the world only in fragments—in old bones, in certain bloodlines, in children born wrong-shaped for their time. The Court had been hunting those fragments for a century. Not to preserve them. To *consume* them. Because a fragment of the First Tongue is concentrated memory, distilled covenant-stuff, and Vess's faction had developed appetites that ordinary harvested joy no longer satisfied.

They wanted the source. They wanted the Hearth itself, opened wide, its cistern poured directly down their throats. And the prophecies—all of them corrupted by then, interpreted by people who'd eaten their own discernment years ago—said the key would come as a child who could not speak, carrying the speech of the beginning.

When the Court's diviners located the child's lineage, I was the one assigned to verify it. My last official duty. I looked at the records, and I looked at what the Court intended, and I did the only arithmetic that has ever made me proud: I falsified the report. I buried the lineage under three layers of misdirection and sent the hunters chasing shadows in the southern fens.

It bought her time. It did not buy her safety. Within a season the Court had unraveled my deception—Vess always suspected me; guilt has a scent, and I reeked of it—and I ran, an old man with a traitor's brand seared into his oath-mark, carrying the one truth I'd salvaged: the child existed, the covenant was failing, and somewhere in a valley where magic could not follow, there might still be a woman who carved bones and listened to the dead.

They say the dead's whispers reach her through the carvings. If any part of what I've told you tonight reaches her ears, let it be this:

Beatrix Winterbourne, when you ask me why I helped build the thing that hunts your child, the answer is: because it asked so politely. Because it wore kindness like a mask fitted so well the wearer forgot it was a mask. Because every step into the dark was framed as a step toward the light, and I walked all of them, and I counted them virtues.

Memory is power. Power must be fed. I believed it for forty years.

But here is what I learned too late, what I'm carrying to the First Hearth along with my guilt and my broken oath: the covenant was never meant to run on *taken* memory. Freely given, or not at all. That was the First Forging's whole secret, and the Court devoured its own founding truth rather than starve.

The Court is coming for the girl. I've seen the ash-wind change direction; I know what rides it. And when it arrives, it will offer bargains wrapped in kindness, and it will mean every gentle word, and that is precisely what makes it monstrous.

I only hope, when the hour comes, that I remember which side of the knife I'm standing on.

I heard the hounds again last night. Closer. And beneath their crying, faint as a memory of bread—

Mara was humming in her sleep. In the First Tongue.

She's starting to answer them.
