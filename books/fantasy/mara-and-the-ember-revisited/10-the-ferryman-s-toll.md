## 10. The Ferryman's Toll

The river Vell did not look like much. That was the first lie it told.

We came down out of the hills at dusk, five of us and one of Wren—though Wren didn't count for tolls, as we would shortly learn to our sorrow—and there it was: a broad grey ribbon of water moving with the patience of something that had never once been in a hurry. No rapids. No roar. Just a long, quiet slide toward a horizon the color of old pewter.

"That's it?" Sef said. "That's the famous river? My grandmother's washbucket has more menace."

"Your grandmother," Ovid said, "is not here to be insulted by the comparison."

"My grandmother is exactly the sort of person who'd want to be here for this."

Mara said nothing. Mara mostly said nothing, though her silences had begun to develop grammar. This particular silence—the way she stopped at the top of the bank and would not come closer, her small hand finding the hem of my coat and gripping it like an anchor line—said *I know what this is*.

The ferry was already waiting. Of course it was. It sat midstream, lantern-lit, drifting against the current in a way that water does not permit, and standing at its stern was the ferryman.

He was tall in the way that reeds are tall—thin, swaying slightly, as if he were less a man than the memory of one. His face was smooth. Not young-smooth. *Blank*-smooth, like bone before the carving knife touches it. I knew that face. I had made a hundred faces like it, out of femur and patience, for families who wanted somewhere to put their grief.

"Ah," I said. "Wonderful. A toll bridge with opinions."

* * *

Ovid explained it on the walk down to the landing, in the tone he used for theology, which was the tone of a man apologizing for a dog he'd once owned.

"The Vell marks the boundary of the Court's reach—or it did, when the covenant held. The ferryman is older than both. He doesn't take coin." Ovid's hands worked at each other, priest's hands gone soft at the knuckles. "He takes memory. True memory. Freely given. That's the whole of the law—he cannot take, only receive. And what he takes, no one gets back. Not even him. He forgets it too, the moment it crosses his palm. That's why his face is—" He gestured vaguely. "That."

"So we pay him our pasts," Sef said, "and in exchange he rows us forty feet."

"He rows us across the last unguarded border in the world. Everything on the far side of the Vell, the Court can't see. Can't follow. It's the only road to the First Hearth they don't watch."

"And if we refuse?"

"Then we swim, and the river decides what we're carrying that it wants."

Wren drifted through the middle of this conversation, which she did often, partly because she could and partly because she enjoyed watching people flinch when she spoke from inside their personal space. "I crossed here once," she said. "Two hundred years ago. I paid him a memory of my wedding day. I don't remember the wedding. I remember being told about it afterward, which is a strange thing to carry—a hole with a label pinned to it."

"What was your husband's name?" I asked.

She looked at me with those faint, stubborn eyes of hers. "You know, I've been dead two centuries and that question has never once occurred to me until this moment." She smiled, and it cracked something in me to see how easily she smiled around the holes. "See? The river's generous that way. It leaves you the label."

* * *

The ferryman received us without greeting. He stood at the gunwale with his blank face tilted down, and when we boarded—Sef first, because Sef volunteered to go first in everything, which I was beginning to understand was not bravery so much as a man racing his own second thoughts—the boat simply accepted us, the way a threshold accepts a foot.

The rules, delivered in a voice like water over stones:

One memory each. True. Whole. Given aloud, spoken into the lantern light, and the ferryman would hold out his hand and take it—not the words, but the thing itself, lifted out of you like a splinter drawn clean.

"You'll feel it go," Ovid said quietly. "Most people say it feels like relief. That's the cruelest part."

"I notice," I said, "that you're speaking as someone who has done this before."

"I notice," Ovid returned, "that you're asking questions instead of deciding. Take your time. The river's patient. It's the only thing about it that is."

Mara tugged my sleeve. She pointed at herself, then at the ferryman, then shook her head slowly—an entire argument conducted in three gestures.

"She's asking if she has to pay," Wren translated, unnecessarily, since I had understood perfectly. Being translated by a ghost when you speak fluent silence is a particular indignity.

"I think," I said carefully, "the ferryman will decide what he takes from her." I looked at him. "Will you?"

The blank face turned toward Mara. For a long moment nothing happened. Then the ferryman did something I have never seen another living—or formerly living—thing do.

He bowed.

A small bow. A bow of recognition, deep in the knees, the kind you give a queen or a grave. And then he straightened and held out his open hand toward Mara, palm up, empty, and waited.

Mara stared at the hand. Then she stepped forward, took a breath, and opened her mouth—

"No," I said, and heard my own voice come out wrong, too sharp, too loud over the water. "No. Whatever she was going to give, she keeps it. She's given enough. She's *been* given enough—taken enough—she's spent her whole short life being a vessel for other people's designs, and I will not stand on this boat and watch one more hand reach into her and lift something out."

The ferryman regarded me. The river regarded me. Even the lantern flame seemed to lean in.

Then, slowly, the ferryman closed his hand, turned his palm down, and inclined his head to *me*—a different bow this time, shallower, almost wry, if a face without features can be said to have a sense of humor. As if to say: *as you wish, carver*. As if he knew me. As if he'd ferried bones before.

Mara exhaled. Her grip on my sleeve loosened by one finger's width, which from Mara was a standing ovation.

"One of us owes double now," Sef observed. "River law. There's always river law."

"There's always river law," the ferryman agreed, in his stone-water voice, and I decided then and there that I disliked him enormously and trusted him completely, which is a combination I recommend to no one.

* * *

Sef went first, properly this time. He stood at the prow with his stolen-name wound showing through his shirt—a pale scar over his heart, shaped like a word nobody could read anymore—and he laughed, once, the laugh of a man stepping off a ledge.

"All right. Fine. Here's how this works, yes? You get the truth, because apparently that's the currency." He rubbed the back of his neck. "My mother. I sold a lot of things in my day—purses, names, other people's names, one regrettable horse—but I kept her. Kept her face. Every night before sleep, I'd put it up behind my eyes like a picture over a mantel. Brown hair going grey at the temples early, because she worried professionally. A gap between her front teeth she covered with her hand when she laughed, which meant she laughed with her hand up most of the time, which meant—" His voice caught. He pushed through it the way he picked locks: fast, before anyone could stop him. "Which meant I got very good at reading her eyes above her fingers. All right. Take it. Take the whole picture, gap and all. If I'm going to spend my mother, I'm not spending her in installments."

He spoke her name—I won't repeat it; it isn't mine to carry anymore either—and the ferryman extended his hand, and I watched it happen.

There was no flash. No drama. Sef simply stopped, mid-sentence, mouth open on the next word, and blinked. And blinked again. And touched his own chest where the scar was, frowning, like a man who'd walked into a room and forgotten why.

"Huh," he said. "Huh. I had—there was something. Wasn't there? Something about—" He looked at us, and his face did something terrible and ordinary: it moved on. "Well. Gone's gone. Who's next? Someone impressive, please, so I don't look weak."

Nobody laughed. But Wren drifted close to him and settled at his shoulder, and stayed there, and Sef—who claimed ghosts gave him hives—did not move away.

* * *

Ovid paid next, and paid fast, like tearing off a bandage: a memory of the first soul he ever burned as a priest of the Court, a farmer who'd begged, and Ovid at nineteen, certain he was mercy itself for doing it quickly. He gave it up with his jaw set and his eyes wet and his voice steady, and when it left him he sagged against the rail and whispered, "Good. Good. Let the river have that one. Let it sink."

Some memories are not treasures. Some are debts wearing treasure's clothes. The ferryman took Ovid's the way you take a knife from a child.

* * *

And then it was me.

I had known, from the moment Ovid explained the toll on the hillside, whose name was coming up in my throat. I had argued with myself the whole walk down—*you have others, Beatrix, you have thirty years of whispers, you have ten thousand carved bones and every last word they ever said, surely the river would accept a shelf of those*—but I knew. When it comes time to pay, you do not send the river your least valuable possession and call it sacrifice. The river knows. Rivers always know.

My sister. Lyra.

Dead forty years. Dead at nine years old, in the winter, in the fire—so I had believed, so I had grieved, so I had built my entire exile in Hollowmere on the foundation of that grief, brick by brick, year by year, until the grief was load-bearing and I lived inside it like a house.

Her name was the keystone. Her name was the one word I had never carved into any bone, because carving it would mean she was finished, and some part of me had refused for four decades to finish her.

I stood at the prow. The lantern swung. The ferryman waited with his open hand, patient as geology.

"Beatrix," Ovid said softly. "Wait."

"I know what I'm doing."

"You don't. That's rather the point of memories—you never know what you're doing with them until they're gone." He came to stand beside me, and lowered his voice to the register he probably thought was private. "Listen to me. I served the Court for twenty years. I know what they take and how they take it, and I am telling you that the shape of your grief is wrong."

"Excuse me?"

"Grief has a shape. Everyone's does. Yours is—" he searched for it, hands turning in the air, "—clean. Too clean. Forty years, and there's no rot in it, no guilt, no unfinished argument. People who lose children to fire carry the smell of smoke for the rest of their lives. You carry none of it. Your memory of her death is smooth as river glass, and river glass, Beatrix, is what water leaves behind after it's carried away everything rough." He gripped my arm. "Someone has edited you. I don't know who or why or what was taken—but if you give the river her name now, you're paying with a forgery, and worse, you're sealing the forgery forever. Whatever the truth is under there, you'll never dig for it again. You'll have sold the shovel."

The river slid beneath us, dark and unhurried.

I wanted to strike him. I wanted to tell him that no one edits a bone-carver, that I knew my own grief the way I knew my own hands. And underneath that wanting, small and cold and undeniable, was the thing he'd named: the smoothness. The terrible tidiness of it. Nine years old, winter, fire, gone—and no smoke in the memory anywhere. No scream. No sound at all. A death with the volume turned down, like a scene someone had trimmed for length.

The ferryman's hand stayed open. Waiting. He would wait forever; that was his nature.

Mara appeared at my side. She did not touch me—she had learned that I flinch—but she stood close enough that I could feel her warmth, and she looked up at me, and she signed, slow and deliberate, the sign she'd invented our first week together: the one that meant *true*, palm flat, pressed over the heart.

*Is it true?* she asked me. *What you're about to give—is it true?*

And I could not say yes.

I stood there with my sister's name in my mouth like a coin bitten between my teeth, testing it, and found the edge worn smooth, and could not say yes.

"No," I said finally, and my voice came out scraped hollow. "Not this one. Ask me again at the Hearth. Ask me anything else on this river—but not this one. Not yet."

The ferryman studied me for a long, long moment. Then he closed his hand, and bowed—that third kind of bow, the one I hadn't seen yet, deeper than respect, closer to pity—and when he raised his blank face, I would have sworn on any god still taking oaths that he looked sorry.

And then he held out his hand again, palm up, and turned it slightly, and I understood: the toll was still owed. The crossing was still owed. One memory, true, freely given, and my sister's name was back behind my teeth where it would stay.

I cast about inside myself like a woman patting her pockets for fare. Thirty years of whispers. Ten thousand last words. Which one could I live without?

None. That was the horror of my profession—I had spent my life collecting other people's endings precisely because I couldn't bear my own, and now the river wanted one back.

But there was one. There was one whisper I had carried thirty years without ever once carving it, because it wasn't a last word at all. It was a first word. Mine. My mother's voice, the night Lyra was born, saying to my father over my infant head: *This one will break your heart someday, Hartwell, look at her, she's already watching everything.*

I had hoarded it like a dragon. I had never told a living soul.

I gave it to the river.

It went out of me gently, the way Sef said—like relief, God forgive it, like setting down a weight I'd forgotten I was holding—and for one bright instant I remembered that I had remembered, and then I didn't, and there was just a warm place in my chest shaped like a voice, unlabeled.

The ferryman closed his fist. The lantern flared. And the boat, which had been holding station against the current all this while as if the river were politely waiting, began at last to move.

* * *

We crossed in silence. On the far bank, the world was subtly, unmistakably different—the air tasted of iron and possibility, and every few breaths I felt something brush past me like a cat in a dark room: magic, loose in the world again, unbound. Ovid breathed it in like a man surfacing. Wren flickered brighter. Sef kept touching his chest and frowning at the hole where his mother's face used to hang, and Mara watched him do it with an expression I couldn't read, and didn't try to.

We made camp above the waterline, and I sat apart, as is my custom, and took out the three carvings I'd saved from the burning of my workshop. The wolf. The door. And the small figure with the gap-toothed smile I'd carved the week after the fire, forty years ago, from a piece of applewood, because bone felt too final.

I looked at it and felt—nothing missing. That was the worst part. I felt nothing missing. The grief was all still there, smooth and tidy and load-bearing, and whatever Ovid thought was wrong with it, wrongness this deep doesn't ache. It just sits.

"Beatrix." Ovid crouched at the edge of my firelight, hat in his hands, looking older than the ruins we were walking toward. "When we reach the shrine at Corvane—the old one, the pre-Court one—there are rites. Ways of reading what's been taken from a mind. Traces. Scars in the grain." He hesitated. "If I'm right about you, you should prepare yourself. Because whoever edited you didn't take a memory, Beatrix. They took a *chapter*. And chapters have pages on either side."

Across the camp, Mara had fallen asleep against Wren's incorporeal shoulder—which she could do, apparently, and which Wren pretended to find inconvenient and treasured beyond speech. The river murmured behind us, carrying its cargo of other people's yesterdays southward.

And far downstream, so far it might have been imagination, so far it was certainly imagination, I saw lights on the water. Lanterns. Many of them, strung across the current in a line, like a court in procession.

Coming north.

Behind us, in the dark, Wren sat up very straight and said, in a voice stripped of all her usual humor: "They've found the crossing. We need to move. Now."
