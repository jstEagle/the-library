## 11. The City That Forgot Itself

I've stolen a lot of things in my life. Coins, mostly. A few knives, one horse—briefly, before the horse stole itself back—and once, for reasons I still can't explain, an entire wheel of cheese from a merchant who was already asleep and would never have missed it.

But I'd never seen anything like what Corvane had done to itself.

We came over the ridge at dusk, and there it was: a city built of pale stone that caught the last light like it was holding its breath. Towers with copper roofs gone green. Walls thick enough to make a thief's mouth water. And smoke rising from a hundred chimneys, which meant people, which meant food, which meant—for the first time since the river Vell took Sef's mother's face out of his head and left something raw and blinking in its place—the possibility of sleeping indoors.

"Corvane," Ovid said, and the way he said it made me look at him twice. He had the tone of a man naming a grave.

"You know it?" Beatrix asked.

"I know *of* it." He pulled his hood lower. "It's famous, in certain circles. The city that bargained."

"Bargained what?"

He didn't answer. Ovid has this habit where he goes quiet exactly when the information gets interesting, like he thinks silence is some kind of penance. Maybe for him it is. For the rest of us, it's just annoying.

Mara tugged my sleeve. She pointed at the city, then at her own chest, then made a gesture I didn't understand—a fist pressed flat against her sternum, fingers spreading open like something blooming. Wren drifted up beside us, translucent as breath on glass, and looked at the city with an expression I'd learned to read as her version of weeping.

"Oh, you poor things," she said softly. "You poor, stupid, brave things."

That should have been our first warning. Wren doesn't pity the living. She thinks we're all walking corpses who haven't gotten around to lying down yet, and she says so with a cheerfulness I find genuinely unsettling. But she pitied Corvane.

The gates were open. That was the second warning. No guards worth the name, no toll, no questions. Just a bored woman at a ledger who waved us through with the enthusiasm of someone shooing flies.

"Welcome to Corvane," she said. "State your business."

"We're travelers seeking lodging," Beatrix said.

"Fine. Fine." The woman made a mark in her book without looking at it. Then she paused, quill hovering, and frowned at nothing. "Sorry—what was I—right. Lodging. The Drowned Bell, on Chandler's Row. Tell them Marta sent you."

She smiled at us. It was a perfectly good smile. It reached her eyes and everything.

And then, halfway through turning back to her ledger, her face did something terrible. It crumpled. Just for a second—like paper crushed in a fist—and two tears fell onto the page and blurred the ink, and she wiped them away with the heel of her hand looking not sad but *confused*, the way you'd look at a door that had started bleeding.

"Damn eyes," she muttered. "Happens all the time lately. Must be the wind off the river."

There is no wind off the river in Corvane. I checked. I check everything; it's how I've stayed alive and nameless this long.

* * *

The Drowned Bell was warm and clean and wrong.

Oh, everything on the surface was right. Good stew, decent ale, a fire that actually burned instead of sulking. The innkeeper was fat and jolly and called everyone "love." There were merchants at the tables arguing about wool prices, and children underfoot chasing a cat, and a fiddler in the corner playing something cheerful.

But listen—I'm a thief. Reading a room is my trade the way carving bones is Beatrix's. And every room in Corvane read like a house where someone had died and no one remembered who.

The conversations had holes in them. You'd hear a merchant say, "—and that's why my grandfather always said—" and then stop, and laugh, and start a different sentence. A woman at the next table told her friend about a dream, got halfway through, and went silent so long her friend asked if she was ill. "No," she said slowly. "No, I just... forgot where the dream was going. Isn't that strange? I had it just this morning."

The children didn't sing songs. I noticed that first, because children always sing songs, usually ones designed to be maximally irritating. These kids played games with rules they clearly knew by heart—but when one little girl tried to teach Mara a skipping rhyme, she opened her mouth and nothing came out. Not mute-nothing, like Mara. *Empty*-nothing. The girl frowned, rubbed her throat, and said, "Funny. I knew it yesterday. Probably."

Probably.

Beatrix sat very still through all of it, holding her bowl of stew without eating, watching the room the way she watches bones before she carves them. Like she could hear something underneath.

"There's no history here," she finally said, quiet enough that only our table could hear. "Look at the walls. An inn this old should have marks, mementos, portraits of dead innkeepers. There's nothing. It's like the building was born last week."

"The whole city's like that," Ovid said. His voice had gone flat, the priest-voice, the one he uses when the truth is too heavy to carry any other way. "Corvane made a bargain with the Court. Forty years ago, maybe fifty. The Ashen Court came for them—or was coming—and the city fathers offered a trade. Their history. All of it. Every memory older than a day, given up freely, in exchange for being passed over."

"That's monstrous," Beatrix said.

"It was *effective*." Ovid turned his cup in circles. "The Court feeds on memory. A city that surrenders its past is a larder they don't need to raid. They took everything—the founding, the wars, the kings, the grievances, the names of the dead in the ground beneath their feet. In return, Corvane lives in perfect safety and perfect peace."

"And the crying?" I asked. "The wind-off-the-river tears?"

Ovid looked at me with something like pity, which from Ovid is a rare vintage. "Grief needs a reason, boy. Take the reason away and the grief doesn't leave. It just... wanders. Looking for a door." He gestured at the room, at the fiddler playing a tune nobody could remember learning, at the innkeeper humming a lullaby in a language he couldn't name. "They weep and don't know why. They wake mourning and can't say for whom. They stand in front of buildings they built and feel like strangers. The Court took the memories, but the feelings were baked into the stones. Into the *bodies*. You can delete a story, but the scar tissue stays."

Nobody said anything for a while. The fiddler finished his song and started it again from the beginning, because it was the only one he had left.

Then Beatrix stood up, sudden enough that her chair scraped, and said, "I need air," and walked out into the dark, and after a moment I followed her, because apparently I'm the type now. The type who follows. Who joins parties. Gods help me.

* * *

Corvane at night was worse than Corvane at dusk, and more beautiful, which felt like the same crime.

Lamps burned in windows. Couples strolled arm in arm. Everything orderly, everything safe, everything hollow. And everywhere—everywhere—the small private griefs. A man standing in a doorway wiping his eyes and shrugging apologetically at passersby. An old woman laying flowers on a random doorstep, then staring at the flowers like she couldn't remember buying them or why she'd chosen white. Two brothers walking in silence, both crying, neither looking at the other, because whatever they'd lost they'd lost together and couldn't share it anymore.

"They gave it up to be safe," Beatrix said. She wasn't talking to me, exactly. More to the street. "Forty years of safety. And look at them."

"They're alive," I said, because somebody had to say the stupid thing. "Safe. Fed. Nobody's burning their city down."

"Yes." She turned and looked at me, and in the lamplight her eyes were the color of old bone, and I understood suddenly that I wasn't looking at Beatrix Winterbourne anymore. I was looking at a mirror wearing her face. "And every morning they wake up weeping and cannot tell you what they paid. Is that safety, Sef? Or is it just a slower way of dying?"

I didn't have an answer. I thought about my own missing name—that hole in me shaped like a word, the thing I sold to a debt-mage for coin I spent in a week. I thought about how sometimes I reach for it in the night, the way a tongue finds the gap where a tooth was. How I can't even mourn it properly, because mourning requires remembering what you lost, and that's the one thing I can't do.

"Yeah," I said. "Okay. Point taken."

"It's not a point. It's a warning." She pressed her palm against the pale stone of a wall, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "This is what the Court offers everyone, eventually. This is what happens if we fail at the Hearth. They don't need to conquer the world, Sef. They just need the world to *trade*. And everyone trades, in the end, when they're frightened enough. Safety for memory. Peace for the past. Everyone thinks they'll be the exception."

Mara appeared between us like she'd been summoned—which, knowing Mara, maybe she had been. She looked up at Beatrix, then at the weeping strangers in the street, and her small hands moved fast, urgent, shaping words in the sign language Beatrix had taught her, words in the First Tongue that Beatrix translated in a whisper:

*"She says: they are not empty. She says the memories aren't destroyed. She can feel them. Under the city. Like embers under ash."*

Beatrix went very still.

"Under the city," she repeated.

Mara nodded, fists pressed to her chest again, fingers blooming. Then she pointed down, down, toward the foundations of the world, and her expression was the oldest thing I've ever seen on a child's face.

Wren drifted through a wall to join us—she does that, drifts through walls, mostly to show off—and she was wearing her serious face, which on a two-hundred-year-old ghost looks like a storm deciding whether to happen.

"She's right," Wren said. "I can feel it too. The Court doesn't eat memories, dears. It *stores* them. Hoards them. Everything Corvane gave up is still here, packed under the streets like grain in a silo. Which means—" she held up one translucent finger, and her eyes gleamed with two centuries of stubbornness— "which means it isn't gone. And what isn't gone can be *given back*."

The street around us seemed to hold its breath. Somewhere nearby, a man wept at his own front door and didn't know why.

And that was when I saw them—at the far end of the lane, three figures in grey, standing too still among the strolling crowds, faces like smoke behind glass. Not moving. Not needing to move.

Watching us.

One of them raised a hand—not a threat, worse than a threat. A *greeting*. And beside me, Ovid's oath-mark flared through his sleeve like a coal breathing, and he made a sound I'd never heard from him before, low and broken, half prayer and half apology.

"They know we're here," he whispered. "They've known since the gate. And they're not attacking because—" he swallowed— "because they don't have to. They're waiting to see if we'll trade."

Beatrix stepped forward, placing herself between the grey figures and Mara, and her voice carried down the empty-hearted street like a blade laid flat on stone.

"Tell your masters we're not selling."

The figure inclined its head, almost courteously. And the last thing I heard, drifting across the lamplit dark like ash on the wind, was a voice made of many voices, saying:

"*Everyone sells, bone-carver. Your sister did.*"

Then the figures were just three more shadows, and the crowd flowed closed behind them, and Beatrix stood frozen in the middle of the road with her hand pressed over her heart like someone trying to keep something inside it from getting out.

She didn't ask what the voice meant. I think she already knew. I think some part of her had known since the river Vell, since the moment she nearly traded her sister's name for passage and Ovid stopped her.

But she didn't speak. She just turned and walked back toward the inn, straight-backed, silent, with Mara's hand tucked in hers—and above us the lamps of Corvane burned on, beautiful and bright, lighting a city full of people who had sold their yesterdays and woke each morning grieving a loss they could not name.

We left before dawn. None of us slept anyway.

And none of us talked about the fact that, somewhere east of the city, the road bent toward the Ember Wastes—and that whatever waited for us there, the Court had just shown us its favorite weapon.

Not fire.

*Memory.*
