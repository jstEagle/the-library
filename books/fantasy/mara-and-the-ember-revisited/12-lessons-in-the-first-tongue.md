## 12. Lessons in the First Tongue

We wintered in a shepherd's hut below the shrine at Vell's Crossing, because Ovid said the Court would not look for us in a place with no name worth stealing, and Sef said any place with a roof was a palace if you squinted, and Wren said nothing, being dead, but drifted through the thatch and settled in the corner like a cat who had decided we were hers.

I had not planned to teach Mara letters. It happened the way most true things happen—by accident, and then all at once.

She had found my knife. Not stolen it; Sef steals things, and even he raised his hands when I looked around the room that first morning. Mara simply picked it up from where I'd left it by the fire, turned it over twice, and held it out to me handle-first, the way you offer something back to its owner. Then she pointed at the woodpile, then at my hands, then made a shape in the air—curved, patient—the universal sign for *you make things*.

"I carve bones," I said. "Memorials. Names for the dead so they aren't forgotten."

She tilted her head. She didn't understand the words, or maybe she understood them too well; there was a look on her face like someone hearing their own thoughts spoken by a stranger.

So I did what I'd done for thirty years in Hollowmere, which is talk to people who cannot answer. I took a scrap of birch—no bones here, the sheep were all still inconveniently alive—and carved a letter into it. The first one anyone learns. The one my own teacher scratched into tallow-slick slate while I sat swinging legs too short to reach the floor.

"This is A," I said. "It's the sound you make when the doctor presses your tongue down. Or when you fall off a roof."

Mara took the birch scrap and studied it the way scholars study scripture and thieves study locks: completely. Then she set it down, took up the knife, and cut—not an A. Something older. A spiral with a line through it, three strokes, each one placed like it had been waiting her whole life to be placed.

The fire popped. Wren, in her corner, went very still, which for a ghost is saying something.

"That's not a letter," I said stupidly.

Mara tapped the spiral. Tapped her own throat. Tapped the air between us, where words live once they've left a mouth. And I understood—or began to—that I wasn't looking at writing. I was looking at a knot. A way of tying sound to meaning so tightly it couldn't come undone.

That was the morning the lessons reversed themselves, and I became, at fifty-some years of age, the oldest student in a school of two.

* * *

Here is what I knew about language before that winter: it is a tool. You pick it up, you pry things open, you put it down. Words are hammers. Some are scalpels. None of them mean anything until a living mouth agrees they do, and the agreement can be revoked at will. That's why oaths have witnesses. That's why contracts are written. Language is only as strong as the memory of the people using it—and memory, as I would learn to my cost, is the one thing in this world that everyone spends and no one keeps.

Here is what the First Tongue is, as far as I could gather from a mute child drawing spirals in hearth-ash:

It is not a tool. It is a promise.

Ovid tried to explain it his way first, which meant forty minutes of theological scaffolding and a diagram in spilled tea that looked like a drunk spider's confession. "The covenant," he said, "was spoken into being. Not written—spoken. Every binding in the old magic descends from a single grammar. When the Ember Realm and ours were joined, the joining was a sentence. Subject, object, vow." He tapped the tea-spider. "The First Tongue isn't a language people speak. It's the language the world was spoken *in*."

"And if someone speaks it now?"

"Then the world listens," he said, "the way a sleeper listens to their own name."

Sef, from the doorway where he was ostensibly keeping watch and actually eating our reserves: "So it's a very old way of shouting."

"It's a very old way of *meaning*, boy. There's a difference."

"There always is," Sef said, "right up until there isn't."

But it was Mara who taught me, because Ovid could only tell me what the thing was, and Mara could show me how it lived. She had begun making sounds again—not words yet, more like a lute being tuned—but ash and birch and the frost on the window were her blackboards, and she was relentless. She had the patience of the dead and the stubbornness of the living, and she deployed both against me daily.

Her method was this: she would draw a symbol, say a sound—a hum, mostly, low in the chest, the kind of sound you feel before you hear—and then point at something true. The fire. The frost. My own two hands. And she would wait, with those grey eyes, until I stopped trying to translate and started trying to *understand*, which is a different muscle entirely, and one I hadn't used since before my sister died.

Because here is the thing about the First Tongue that took me eleven days to grasp and another four to believe:

There are no words for lying in it.

Not that lying is forbidden. That it is *impossible*. The language has no structure that permits a false statement, the way our alphabet has no letter for the smell of rain. You cannot say "the fire is cold" in the First Tongue, because the word for *fire* contains its burning and the word for *cold* refuses to hold it. To speak at all is to speak truly. The grammar itself is the oath. Every sentence is a small covenant, sealed the moment it leaves your lips.

"No wonder the Court wants her," I said one night, watching Mara draw the spiral-knot for *hearth* in the ash for perhaps the hundredth time, though she never seemed bored of it. "A weapon that cannot miss."

"She's not a weapon," Ovid said.

"I know what she is. I'm telling you what *they* think she is."

"And what do you think she is?"

I looked at the child bent over the hearthstone, tongue between her teeth, correcting a stroke she'd gotten slightly wrong, holding herself to a standard of truth no court in either realm could survive. What did I think she was?

"Lonely," I said. "And carrying more than loneliness should be asked to lift."

Ovid was quiet a moment. "That," he said, "is the most honest theology I've heard in thirty years."

* * *

The days found a shape, and I will not pretend I didn't love the shape.

Mornings: letters. I taught her ours, twenty-six little cages for sounds, and she learned them in a week, faster than any novice I'd seen, because she already understood that marks could hold meaning—she'd just been waiting for someone to show her this particular dialect of it. She wrote her first full word in our letters on the ninth day, scratched into birch bark and pushed across the table to me without ceremony:

FIRE

"Well done," I said, and she gave me a look that clearly said *obviously*, and I laughed for the first time since the valley, and Wren drifted over to see what the noise was, and Sef pretended not to smile and failed at it comprehensively.

Afternoons: the other direction. She taught me knots of the First Tongue, three or four at a time, never more, and drilled me without mercy. I learned *hearth*. I learned *bridge*—a symbol like two riverbanks reaching. I learned the knot for *name*, which is drawn differently depending on whether the name is held, given, or taken, and when I asked her how you drew a *taken* name she went still, and later I saw her draw it once, alone, staring at Sef across the room, and I understood something about the thief that he had never told us and we had never asked.

Evenings: stories. Or rather, one story, told in pieces, because it was the only story she had. She would draw it in the ash—the tower of cinders, the grey figures with their patient faces, the door she came through—and each night a little more of it, the way frost builds on a window: slowly, and then all at once. She had been kept somewhere. She had been *taught* somewhere, by things that wanted her to learn the First Tongue perfectly and never once learn why. And one night, in the middle of the tower-drawing, she added a small figure at the base of the wall—a stick figure, running—and touched it, and looked at me.

"Someone let you out," I said.

She didn't nod. But she didn't erase the figure either, and from Mara, that was a testimony sworn and sealed.

Wren watched these sessions from her corner with an expression I eventually learned to read, which is a strange skill to develop toward someone with no face to read. On the twelfth evening she drifted close enough that the candle flames leaned toward her, and said, in her voice like wind through a keyhole:

"You know what you're doing, don't you?"

"Teaching a child her letters."

"You're teaching her to be *less* alone. In a language only monsters speak fluently." A pause, the ghostly equivalent of clearing a throat. "My mother did that for me. After the plague took my brothers. Taught me letters by candlelight so I'd have somewhere to go that wasn't grief." Her outline flickered. "It worked. Mostly. For about sixty years."

"Wren—"

"Don't." But she stayed, and watched Mara draw, and when the girl glanced up and smiled at the empty corner—children and ghosts recognize each other, I've come to believe—Wren smiled back with everything she had left, which was not much, and was enough.

* * *

On the nineteenth day, I spoke my first true sentence in the First Tongue.

I want to be precise about what happened, because precision is all I have left of some things. Mara had drawn the knot for *stay*—a closed circle, unbroken—and pointed at me, and pointed at the floor, and waited. And I thought about Hollowmere, and the workshop I had burned, and the thirty years I had spent building a life shaped exactly like a hiding place, and I thought about how every road since had felt less like flight and more like return, though return to what I couldn't say.

And I said the sound. The hum, low in the chest, shaped by the knot. *Stay.*

The fire stood up.

I don't know how else to describe it. The flames went vertical, utterly still, like soldiers called to attention, and the warmth in the room doubled, and every hair on my arms rose, and Mara's eyes went wide—not frightened, *delighted*, the way a child is delighted when the dog finally does the trick—and Ovid came through the door at a run with his oath-mark flaring, and Sef fell off the woodpile.

"What did you do?" Ovid demanded.

"I said 'stay.'"

"To *whom*?"

"The fire, apparently."

He stared at the upright flames. He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "Thirty years I served the Court," he said, muffled, "and I never once heard it spoken truly. Do you understand what that means? They *have* the language. They've hoarded it for centuries. And not one of them—not the Regent himself—can speak a true sentence, because you cannot serve hunger and mean anything at the same time." He lowered his hand. He was looking at me the way men look at relics. "You're not gifted, Beatrix. You're just honest. It's rarer."

The flames settled. Mara patted my knee, once, gravely, the way you congratulate someone who has survived a fall, and went back to her ash-drawing as though the laws of nature had not briefly stood at attention in our kitchen.

But I sat there a long time after, turning it over. One word, spoken truly, and the world obeyed. Not commanded—*invited*. There was a difference, and the difference was everything. The Court consumed memories because they could not make promises; a thing that eats cannot give. But a promise, freely spoken—

"One word spoken truly can bind," I said slowly, working it aloud. "Or unbind."

"Yes," Ovid said quietly.

"The covenant at the First Hearth. If it's failing—"

"It isn't failing like a rope frays, Beatrix. It's failing like a vow goes unkept. Someone has to speak it again. Truly. Wholly." He looked at Mara's bent head, then at me, and whatever he saw in my face made him stop talking, which for Ovid Crane was a form of prayer.

* * *

That night I couldn't sleep, so I did what I always do with sleeplessness: I carved.

Birch, not bone—we were long past bone—but my hands knew the work regardless, and I let them go where they wanted while my mind circled the evening's lesson. *Bind or unbind.* The knot for *hearth*. The knot for *bridge*. The knot for *taken-name*, which I now caught myself drawing in the margins of everything, the way you worry a loose tooth.

The hut was warm. Sef snored with the theatrical commitment of a man who had never once been truly asleep on watch. Wren sat by the banked fire doing whatever ghosts do instead of dreaming. And Mara slept curled against my side like she'd been assigned there, breathing in the small fierce rhythm of the young, one hand fisted in my sleeve as if even unconscious she intended to keep me.

I looked down at the birch scrap in my lap and realized what my hands had made.

A spiral with a line through it. Three strokes. The knot for *hearth*—but altered, the closing stroke extended past the circle, unfinished, *open*, the way Mara drew it every night before bed. I frowned at it. I hadn't known that variation. I hadn't been taught it.

"Mara draws it open," I murmured to Wren. "Why would the knot for hearth be open? A hearth is contained. Contained is the whole point."

Wren was silent so long I thought she'd faded. When she spoke, her voice had gone strange and thin.

"Because it isn't finished," she said. "Beatrix—it isn't finished. The original forging. What I died defending. I've been circling it for two hundred years and I couldn't—" Her outline guttered like a candle in draft. "Ask me tomorrow. Ask me properly. Because I think I finally remember how it ends, and I think you're not going to like it, and I think—" She stopped. Across the room, Mara stirred in her sleep and made a sound—not a word, not yet, but nearly—and every candle in the hut leaned toward her at once, all together, like flowers finding the sun.

Outside, far off on the frozen hills, something answered.
