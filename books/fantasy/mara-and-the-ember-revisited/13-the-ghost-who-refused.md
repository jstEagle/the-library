## 13. The Ghost Who Refused

Here is what nobody tells you about being dead: it is mostly waiting, and the waiting is not even the bad part. The bad part is that you remember everything, and none of it will hold still. Two hundred years of watching the world go about its business, and my business—my one, single, stubborn business—was to keep a door shut that the whole world had forgotten existed.

I was very good at it, once. I was good at it the day I died, too, which is the only reason I am here to complain about it.

But let me back up, because a ghost's sense of sequence is a scandalous thing, and if I start in the middle you will think I am mad, and if I start at the beginning you will think I am mad anyway, so we may as well be comfortable.

I first saw them at the river Vell, which is a river with the manners of a tax collector. I was drifting along the eastern bank—there is a willow there, or there was, trees come and go like tenants—and I felt them before I saw them. You learn to feel the living. They are loud. Not with their mouths, mostly, but with their wanting. The old woman wanted nothing, which was remarkable. The child wanted everything, which was terrifying. The priest wanted forgiveness so badly it smelled like a house fire. And the thief—

The thief wanted his name back. He didn't know it consciously. But wanting a name is a particular kind of hunger, and I have had two centuries to learn the flavors of hunger, and his was the one I knew best, because it was mine.

I had a name once. I had a name the way you have hands: without thinking about it, until someone takes one.

* * *

I followed them. Of course I followed them. You would have too, if you had spent two hundred years watching a door and then one gray morning felt the hinges groan from a hundred miles away.

The child did it. I want that recorded, wherever ghosts record things, which is nowhere, which is why I am telling you. The child felt me at the ferry crossing—felt me the way you feel a draft under a door—and she looked directly at where I was standing, which is to say at nothing, and she did not look away. The living never look. Looking at a ghost is a courtesy, and the living stopped extending courtesies to the dead somewhere around the reign of the third Aldric, and I have kept count.

She looked. And then she hummed.

It was one note of the First Tongue—not a word, just the shape a word makes before it decides to be one—and I felt it go through me like a needle through wet paper. Two hundred years, and the first thing to touch me was a note hummed by a mute child who was not mute at all, only waiting for a language worth the trouble of speaking.

The old woman—Beatrix, I would learn, though I knew her kind immediately, the carvers, the listeners—Beatrix said, "What is it, Mara?"

And Mara pointed at me.

Now. I have been pointed at four times in two centuries, and I remember each one the way you remember the day your house burned. The priest went white. The priest, I should say, went whiter; he was already the color of old tallow, being a man who had fed his god and regretted the meal. He said a word in the ember-tongue that I had not heard since it meant something, and it was a word for *witness*, and I confess it stung, because I had witnessed a great deal and been thanked never.

"She's been following us," the priest said. "Since the river."

"How long since the river?" Beatrix asked.

He looked at me the way you look at a ledger you are afraid to open. "Since before the river," he said. "Possibly since the valley. Possibly—" and here he did the thing priests do, which is to stop speaking exactly when the truth arrives, "—possibly since the Hearth."

I did not know I could still feel cold. But I felt it then, the way you feel a hand on the back of your neck in an empty room.

* * *

They could not see me, most of them. Beatrix had the flat practical blindness of a woman who has spent thirty years listening to the dead through bone and has decided that is quite enough commerce with the departed, thank you. The thief—Sef—could see me, but only out of the corner of his eye, and every time he did he swore with a fluency I found genuinely impressive. I once heard a fishwife in Corvane curse a customs man for a full minute without repeating herself, and Sef was better.

"You're still there," he said to me on the third night, when the others slept and he sat up keeping watch, because thieves sleep in shifts with their own guilt. "The woman made of cold air. The one who's been making my neck itch since the ferry."

"I'm still here," I said.

He did not jump. I will grant him that. He went very still, the way small animals go still, and then he said, "Do you have a name?"

And there it was. The question. Two hundred years, and the second person to ask me my name was a pickpocket with a stolen one, and I could have kissed him if kissing were a thing I could still do, which it is not, which is a grief I have had time to get used to and have not.

"I had one," I said.

"Everyone says that." He turned a coin over his knuckles—stolen, obviously; it had a merchant's mark bitten into the rim, and he worried it like a tooth. "The debt-mage who bought mine said the same thing. *Everyone had one once, boy.* As if that makes it better. As if the having is the point." He looked at me properly then, both eyes, which took courage, and I have always respected courage in the underfed. "What happened to yours?"

"I spent it," I said. "On a door."

He waited. Thieves are good at waiting; it is most of the profession. But I did not say more, because the rest of the story was not his yet, and because I am a coward in the ways that matter, and two hundred years of cowardice makes a very smooth groove.

* * *

Here is what I remember of the day I died, and I remember it the way you remember your own hands, which is to say completely and without wanting to:

The First Hearth stood in a valley that is not on maps anymore, because maps are drawn by the living for the living, and the Hearth was neither. It was a place where the fire did not burn wood. It burned agreement. That is what the covenant was—not a spell, not a chain, an *agreement*, spoken in the First Tongue by people who understood that a word spoken truly can bind or unbind, and who chose to bind themselves to the keeping of both realms the way you choose to marry: knowing it will cost, and paying anyway.

I was a keeper's daughter. My mother was the last full keeper, and when the Ashen Court came—and they were not called that yet, they were still guardians then, still wearing their duty like a coat that fit—I stood in the doorway of the Hearth with a fire poker, because I was nineteen and stupid and brave in the way that only nineteen-year-olds and ghosts can be.

They did not kill me. I want that understood. They did something worse, which was to *unmake the agreement I had with my own life*. They took my death and made it into a hinge. I died defending the door, and my death was so stubborn, so entirely unwilling, that it stuck. I have been stuck ever since. A splinter in the world's hand.

The Hearth fell. The covenant held, barely, badly, held the way a roof holds when the beams are rotten and everyone in the house has agreed not to look up. And I stayed, because someone had to, and because I did not know how to leave, and because—here is the humiliating truth, the one I have carried for two centuries like a stone in my shoe—I was waiting for my mother to come back for me.

She never came. The Court took her, or the years took her, or the forgetting took her, and I stood at my door and waited, and the waiting hardened into duty, and the duty hardened into me.

* * *

I decided to stop following them on the morning I saw the hounds.

Grey smoke, moving against the wind, with the smell of a snuffed candle. The priest saw them first and made a sound I had not heard a man make in a hundred years, the sound of a dog that knows the whip by its shadow. The Court had found them. Of course the Court had found them—the child was a lit candle in a century of dark rooms, and you do not leave a lit candle unattended if you are made of hunger.

And I thought: *this is where I stop.* I had followed them out of habit, then out of curiosity, then out of the terrible hope that is worse than either. The child was the key. The key was going to the Hearth. The Hearth was my door. Everything I had waited for was walking toward me at the pace of a tired woman and a limping thief, and I could have simply drifted into the ground and been done, and no one—not one soul in either realm—would have known the difference.

Instead I did the most ghostly thing possible. I made myself useful.

They were arguing about the road. Beatrix had a map she did not trust and the priest had a memory he trusted less, and between them they were about to take the low road through the Cinderholt, which would have been picturesque and fatal, because the Court held the Cinderholt the way a spider holds a web it is embarrassed about.

"There is a pass," I said, to no one, to the wind, to the thief who was the only one who could half-hear me. "The Shepherd's Cut. North and up. Two days longer and no one has died there since I did."

Sef went still. "The pass," he repeated, slowly, the way a man repeats a word he suspects is a trap. "Nobody's died there since you did. And when was that?"

"Two hundred years ago," I said. "Give or take a bad winter."

He told them. I will say this for the thief: he told them, knowing they would think him mad, and Beatrix looked at him the way you look at a man who has started talking to the air, which is to say with concern and a hand near her knife. But the priest went to his knees in the frost. The priest knew. The priest had read the records of the Hearth's fall, the ones the Court had not burned, and he knew the name of the keeper's daughter who died at the door.

"Wren," he said, to the empty morning. "Wren of the Hearth."

I had not heard my name in two hundred years.

I want to be clear about what that was like. It was not warmth. It was not the stories where the ghost sighs and dissolves into light, grateful, finished. It was like being handed a coat you buried someone in. It fit. That was the horrible part. It *fit*, and I had spent two centuries telling myself I had outgrown it.

"Yes," I said. "That's mine. I'd forgotten the shape of it."

* * *

We traveled north after that, and I traveled with them, which is a different thing, and I will tell you the difference: following is what you do when you are afraid to arrive. Traveling with is what you do when you are afraid not to.

The child talked to me. Not in words, at first—in drawings, scratched in ash on a flat stone at the night fires. She drew the Hearth. She drew it *right*, which no living person had done in two centuries, because she had never seen it and yet her hands knew it, the way my hands knew the fire poker. She drew the door. She drew the door open.

I looked at that drawing a long time.

"You don't want it open," I said. "You want it *through*. There's a difference. The Court wants it open the way a throat is open. You want it through the way a road is through."

She looked at me—she could see me now, more each day, as if the failing covenant were thinning the wall between us, as if she were already half a bridge—and she tapped the drawing, twice, and then she drew herself standing in the doorway. Not holding it shut. Not holding it open. *Standing in it.*

Being the door.

I had spent two hundred years being a splinter, and this child, this frostbitten scrap of a girl, was proposing to be a *threshold*, and I laughed. I had not laughed since the third Aldric. It came out of me like a bat out of a cellar, all wings and wrongness, and the thief dropped his coin, and Beatrix looked up from the fire and said, "What?" and Sef said, "Nothing. The ghost is laughing at us," and Beatrix said, "Good. Someone should be," and went back to her carving.

I liked her immediately and permanently. There is a kind of woman who meets the impossible and responds by sharpening something, and those are the women who have held the world together since the beginning, and no one ever writes songs about them because the songs would all be the same: *she stayed, she worked, she paid.*

* * *

The night before we reached the high country, the priest came to find me. I was sitting—ghosts sit; it is a habit of the body we cannot shake, like grief—on a stone above the camp, looking north, where the mountains had begun to show their teeth.

"You knew the way," he said. "To the Hearth. All along."

"I knew the way," I said. "I died on it."

"Why didn't you say something sooner?"

"Because saying it makes it real," I said, "and I have spent two hundred years keeping it real all by myself, and I thought that was the same as doing my duty. It isn't. It's the same as hiding." I looked at him. He was a man built entirely of penance, a cathedral of guilt with a small frightened clerk inside. "You know something about that, ember-priest. You hid her lineage. You hid the truth the way I hid the road. We are both very good at keeping things the way keeping a wound is keeping it."

He flinched. Good. Flinching is the first honest thing a penitent does.

"The Hearth will ask something of you," I said. "It asked something of my mother, and she paid it, and the covenant held for a thousand years on what she paid. It will ask again. It always asks. The only question—the only one, priest, write it on your bones—is who will pay *willingly*."

"And you?" he asked. "What will it ask of you?"

I looked north. The mountains. The pass. The valley beyond it that is not on maps, where a door stands in a ruin, and my death is still stuck in the hinges like a nail that was hammered in angry.

"I don't know," I said, which was a lie, because I had known for two hundred years, and the knowing went through me every night at the hour I died, a small bell tolling in a room with no doors. "But I'll tell you what I told the Court, the day they came. I told them: *you can have my life, but you cannot have my yes.*"

I stood up. The stone did not notice, the frost did not notice, the world went on not noticing me, and it did not matter, because for the first time in two centuries I was not following.

I was going home.

Behind me, down in the camp, the child hummed in her sleep—one note, the shape of a word before it decides to be one—and far to the north, past the pass, past the mountains, something old and patient felt the note and began, very slowly, to open its eyes.
