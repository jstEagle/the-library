## 15. The Ember Wastes

There is a particular silence that comes off burned land. I don't mean quiet—Hollowmere was quiet, and I loved it, and quiet is a living thing with a pulse under it. This is different. This is the silence of a place that has been asked a question and answered by dying.

We stood at the edge of it on the morning after we left the ferryman's toll behind us, and none of us said anything for a long while, because there is a certain kind of country that makes even Sef shut his mouth, and this was it.

The Ember Wastes ran from horizon to horizon like a sea of grey-green glass. Not black, the way you'd expect burned ground to be. Glassed. Fused. The fire that came through here generations ago had been so hungry and so thorough that it hadn't left ash—it had left *vitrification*, Ovid said, in the tone of a man reciting scripture he no longer believed in. Whole forests turned to standing mirrors. Hills smoothed into waves. Here and there the glass had caught something mid-fall—a tree, a fence post, once what might have been a church bell—and held it forever, suspended, like flies in amber.

"It goes three days across," Ovid said. "Four, if the weather turns."

"What weather?" Sef asked, gesturing at the sky, which was the color of an old bruise and about as promising.

"That's my point."

Mara stood at my side with her hand in mine, looking out at all that terrible shine, and I felt her fingers tighten—not with fear, I thought then. With recognition.

I should have paid more attention to that.

* * *

We walked into the Wastes at midmorning, and by noon I had learned two things about walking on glass.

The first was that it sang. Every footstep rang a small clear note that went on far too long, rippling outward through the fused ground, so that our little company sounded less like travelers and more like a badly tuned orchestra rehearsing in an empty cathedral. There was no way to move quietly. There was no way to hide. If the Ashen Court wanted us, they would hear us coming for three days, and there was nothing any of us could do about it, which Ovid admitted cheerfully, as though cheerfulness were some kind of penance he'd assigned himself.

The second thing was that the glass remembered light strangely. When the sun broke through the bruised clouds—and it did, occasionally, in long greasy shafts—the whole plain lit up from underneath, every buried shape glowing, and you could see how much death the ground was holding. Roots. Roads. A village, once, its foundations visible beneath our feet like a diagram of itself, streets and doorways and a well, all of it rendered in faint gold light, all of it empty.

"Where did everyone go?" Sef asked. He asked it lightly, the way he asked everything, but his hand kept drifting to the place where his name used to live, behind his breastbone, the way it always did when a question got too close to him.

"The covenant failed here," Ovid said. "Generations ago. Before the Court learned to feed politely." He walked with his eyes down, which was unusual for him; usually he watched the horizon like a man expecting it to apologize. "When the binding between the realms frayed, the ember-magic didn't just stop flowing. It *spilled*. Everything within fifty miles burned in a single night. The dead couldn't cross over because the road between worlds was broken, so they—" He stopped. Started again. "They stayed. In the ground. In the glass. For a long time, travelers swore the Wastes whispered."

"Do they still?" I asked.

Ovid looked at me with an expression I couldn't read, and said, "Ask your bones."

Which was unfair, because I had exactly one bone with me that still spoke, and it was wrapped in wool at the bottom of my pack, and it belonged to my sister, and I was not going to unwrap it on a glass plain in front of four strangers just to settle a rhetorical point.

Except they weren't strangers anymore. That was the trouble. Somewhere between the river and the city that forgot itself, these people had stopped being cargo I was escorting and started being people I would grieve. I resented it. I want that on the record. I had spent thirty years building a life precisely engineered so that I would have nothing left to lose, and here I was crossing a burning desert with a priest, a thief, a ghost, and a child, cataloguing them—all of them—as things that could be taken from me.

Wren drifted alongside me, translucent feet not quite touching the glass, humming something tuneless. She'd been quieter since Corvane. Quieter for Wren meant she only talked half as much as a waterfall instead of twice.

"You're doing the face," she said.

"What face?"

"The one where you're writing everyone's eulogy in advance. You do it when you're frightened. It's very becoming. Terribly efficient. The dead of Hollowmere must have adored you."

"They did," I said. "That was rather the problem."

She hummed again, and drifted ahead, and I watched her go and thought: *not her too. Don't add her to the list.* And then, because grief is a habit before it's a feeling, I added her to the list anyway, just to have it done.

* * *

The first night, we camped in the lee of a glass hill that curved like a wave frozen mid-break. Ovid built a fire from wood he'd been carrying since the river—I never saw him gather any; the wood simply appeared in his pack when needed, which I chose not to examine—and we ate hard bread and dried apples and pretended the meal was more than fuel.

Mara sat close against my side, the way she had since the Waystation, since the night Ovid's oath flared and his hands went around her throat before his mind caught up. She had forgiven him faster than any of us. Children forgive quickly; it's one of the many ways in which they are braver than we are. But forgiveness is not the same as forgetting, and she had not forgotten, and so she sat pressed to me like a burr, and I found I did not mind.

It was while I was braiding her hair—she'd begun letting me do it somewhere outside Corvane, another small surrender I treasured more than I let show—that I noticed her lips moving.

No sound. Just movement. The same syllables over and over, quick and fluid, in the language older than kingdoms.

"Mara." I finished the braid and tied it off. "What are you saying?"

Her mouth stopped. She looked up at me with those enormous dark eyes, and for a moment I thought she wouldn't answer—she still went mute sometimes, when the world pressed too hard—but then she took my hand and wrote on my palm with one finger, letter by letter, in the ash-script she used when words in any tongue were too heavy:

*THEY ARE SINGING.*

"Who is?"

She pointed. Not at anything near us. At the horizon, eastward, deep into the Wastes, where the glass plain met the bruised sky in a line of dull silver.

And I listened—really listened, the way I listen through bone—and beneath the wind and the ring of settling glass, I heard it. Faint. Distant. A sound like a choir heard through a wall, if the choir had been singing for a hundred years without stopping and had forgotten there was ever anything else to sing about.

The Court. Calling. Not for me. For her.

"How long," I said, and my voice came out flat, the carver's voice, the voice I used for the dead because the dead appreciate steadiness, "have they been singing?"

She held up her hand. Fingers spread. Then, slowly, she folded three of them down.

Three days. Since the river. Since the ferryman's toll, where we had paid in true memories and crossed over water that remembered being holy.

Since we entered the Wastes' reach.

Ovid had gone very still by the fire. "That's not possible," he said. "The Court doesn't call. They take. They send hounds, they send agents, they send—"

"They send songs now," Sef said. He was lying on his back with his hat over his eyes, but his voice had lost its drawl. "Maybe they're learning manners. Or maybe—" he lifted the hat and looked at Mara, and his expression did something complicated, "—maybe they've figured out that you can't hunt something that walks to you."

"We are not walking anywhere," I said.

"Course not," Sef said, and put the hat back down, and I noticed his hand had gone again to the hollow where his name had been, pressing it, the way you press a wound to be sure it's still closed.

* * *

The second day, the singing got louder.

Not steadily—that was the worst of it. It came in tides. An hour of wind and ringing footsteps and ordinary dread, and then, without warning, the whole plain would fill with that distant choir, swelling like a tide coming in, and I would watch Mara's shoulders rise toward her ears and her hands curl into fists, and then it would recede, and she would breathe again, and we would walk on.

By midday she had stopped speaking entirely. Not just the First Tongue—everything. Her ash-words grew shorter. *Loud. Far. Home.* Each word cost her something visible, like a woman hauling buckets up from a deep well.

"She's fighting it," Ovid said quietly, beside me. We had fallen back a few paces from the others, out of habit or mercy. "The calling. Most people can't. The Court's summons isn't persuasion—it's gravity. You don't decide to answer it. You simply find yourself facing the right direction."

"And how," I said, "do you know so much about how their summons works?"

He had the grace to look ashamed. "Because I carried it once. Their mark." He touched his forearm, where the oath-sigil lived under his sleeve, the brand that had betrayed him at the Waystation. "It's quieter now. Dead-ish. But I remember what it felt like when it sang. Like your own heart beating in someone else's rhythm." He glanced ahead at Mara's small straight back. "She's holding it off better than I ever did. Do you understand what that means? She's a child, and she's out-resisting the Cinder Regent's own voice."

"I don't care what it means. I care when it stops meaning anything."

"That's the thing, Beatrix." He hesitated, and I learned later that Ovid Crane hesitating was worse than Ovid Crane confessing. "It doesn't stop. It waits. People who resist the call—they don't defeat it. They hold it at arm's length until their arms tire. Usually asleep. Usually on the third or fourth night."

I looked at the grey sky, and the glass singing under our boots, and the child walking ahead of me with her fists clenched like stones, and I began, in the private workshop of my mind, to build something. I am a carver. Give me a problem and I will start shaping it whether I mean to or not.

I needed bone. I needed a name. And I needed both before nightfall.

* * *

I won't pretend I knew what I was doing, because I didn't. What I knew was this: in thirty years of listening to the dead, I had learned that a name is a rope thrown across a chasm. The dead cling to theirs. The living forget how tightly. And whatever the Court's song was, whatever gravity it worked by, it worked by *unmooring*—by making Mara forget which shore she belonged to.

So I would give her a rope.

But there were no bones in the Wastes. Nothing had died here in generations except the land itself, and the land's bones were glass, and glass does not take a carving. I spent the afternoon's march turning this over, and it was Sef—of all people, Sef—who solved it, because thieves think like carvers and neither of them likes to admit it.

"Here." He dug in his coat and produced a sliver of something pale, polished, worn smooth at one end. Bone. Old bone. "Off a saint's finger, allegedly, from a reliquary in Corvane. Allegedly. I was going to sell it. Couldn't get a price I liked." He tossed it to me. "You carve things, right? Make it worth something."

"Sef. This is theft of holy relics."

"It was theft of holy relics. Now it's redistribution." He grinned, and for once the grin reached all the way up. "Consider it my tithe."

* * *

That night we camped in the shadow of a glass ridge shaped like a kneeling figure, which nobody commented on, because commenting would have required deciding whether it was comforting or horrifying, and we were all too tired for either.

I sat apart with the relic-bone and my knives, and I carved.

I carved the way I hadn't carved since leaving Hollowmere—not memorial-work, not the careful reverent grooves that give the dead a shape to speak through. This was anchor-work. I carved Mara's name.

Only I didn't know her name. Not truly. *Mara* was what we called her, the word she answered to, but names in the First Tongue were different—I'd learned that much from our lessons on the road, from the grammar that was really a covenant, from the way a single true word could bind or unbind. Her true name was hers, unwritten, unspoken even by herself.

So I carved what I had. I carved *Mara* in the letters I'd taught her. And beneath it, because my hands knew things my mind only suspected, I carved the shapes she had scratched in ash the first night she came to my door—the shapes that matched carvings I'd made as a child without knowing why. The hearth-sign. The bridge-sign. And around them, in a spiral, I carved the oldest pattern I knew, the one my own hands had made forty years ago in a valley workshop, the one I had never been taught:

*A thing named cannot be unmade. A thing held cannot be called away.*

I carved until the moon rose, and then I carved a hole through the bone's end and threaded it onto a cord, and I went to where Mara slept curled against Wren's faintly glowing form—the ghost had appointed herself the child's night-light, and neither of them acknowledged the arrangement, and it was the most tender thing I had seen in thirty years.

I knelt and slipped the cord over Mara's head and tucked the bone beneath her collar, against her skin.

And her eyes opened.

Not sleepily. All at once, wide and black in the firelight, fixed on my face, and her lips parted, and for one horrible moment I thought the song had her already.

Then her hand came up and closed around the bone through her shirt, and she breathed out, long and shaking, like a swimmer surfacing.

"Oh," I said, uselessly. "Oh, child. How long have you been awake?"

She didn't answer. She sat up, and she leaned into me, and she pressed her forehead against my collarbone the way she had that first frostbitten night at my door, and I held her and felt the choir singing out there in the dark, patient, immense, certain, and I understood that I had not saved her tonight.

I had bought a night. Maybe two. Ovid had told me plainly: the call doesn't stop. It waits. Arms tire.

Protection, I was beginning to learn, is not a wall you build once. It is a debt you pay hourly, and the interest is terrible, and you pay it gladly, and it will ruin you, and you pay it anyway.

Somewhere past midnight, I finally slept, with the bone warm against my own sternum—for I had carved a second mark on it, hidden, without quite admitting why: a spiral, and inside it, a name I had not spoken aloud in forty years.

*Wren woke me.*

That alone was wrong; ghosts don't wake people, they hover and comment. But her face, when I opened my eyes to the pre-dawn grey, had none of its usual performance. She was pointedly, urgently looking past me, eastward, and her voice when it came was stripped of all its music.

"Beatrix. The girl's gone."

I was on my feet before the sentence ended. The camp: Ovid snoring against the glass ridge, Sef a hat-and-coat-shaped lump, the fire down to embers. No Mara.

"She walked out maybe an hour ago," Wren said. "Straight line. Due east. Toward the singing. I followed as far as the ridge—I can't go further east, there's something in the glass that—" she shook her head, frustrated, furious at her own limits. "Beatrix, she wasn't stumbling. She wasn't sleepwalking like the drowned. She was walking like someone *going home*."

I ran.

The glass sang under my boots, every stride a struck note, alarm bells I was ringing against myself, and ahead of me the plain stretched silver-grey into the dawnless morning, and there—at the edge of sight, small and straight-backed and terrible—walked a child in a braid I had made, moving with the unhurried certainty of the summoned.

"MARA!"

She didn't turn. The choir swelled, and I heard it clearly now for the first time, heard *through* it the way I hear through bone, and beneath the vast patient harmony there were words, or the shapes of words, in the First Tongue, and my blood went cold because I knew enough of the grammar now to catch their meaning:

*Come home, little key. Come home, little door. We have kept your place. We have kept everything.*

And Mara walked on, and I ran, and the bone with her name swung against my chest like a pendulum counting down, and far ahead, rising out of the glass like a tooth from a gum of silver, I saw a structure I had no memory of ever seeing before—and yet my hands, traitorously, already knew its shape.

A shrine. Small, ancient, half-drowned in glass.

And carved above its ruined door, in patterns my own childhood hands had once made without knowing why, a single word in the First Tongue that I could suddenly, impossibly, read:

*Sister.*
