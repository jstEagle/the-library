## 16. What Was Taken From Me

The shrine found us the way trouble always finds me: by looking, at first, like shelter.

We had been three days in the hill country since the glass wastes, and Mara had not sleepwalked again, but she slept badly, curled against Ovid's pack with her brow furrowed as though the Court's calling were a sound only she could hear and she was being polite about it. Sef complained of his feet. Wren drifted ahead of us humming a tune two centuries out of fashion. And I walked with my sister's name carved into a sliver of bone in my pocket, turning it over and over like a stone I meant to skip across forty years of silence.

*Edith.*

I hadn't spoken it aloud since Hollowmere. In the valley, names of the dead are for the bones to carry, not the living. But we were out of the valley now, and the world outside had teeth.

The shrine sat in a fold of hills where the road bent around an old barrow — a low stone building with a roof of living turf, half-swallowed by hawthorn. It was older than any god I knew of, which in my experience means it is older than any god worth praying to. The door stood open. Not broken. *Open*, the way a house opens its door when it has been waiting.

"I don't like it," Sef said, which was how I knew it was probably fine. Sef didn't like anything that wasn't someone else's purse.

"It's a shrine," Ovid said. His voice did the careful thing it did whenever he talked about holy places, the voice of a man walking on ice over deep water. "Pre-covenant, maybe. Or early covenant. They built them at crossroads so travelers could leave a memory in exchange for safe passage."

"Leave a memory," I said. "Just hand it over, like a coin."

"The old rites were gentler than what the Court does." He said it quickly. Too quickly. Ovid always said things too quickly when they were true.

Inside, the shrine was one round room with a hearth at its center — cold, ash-swept, ancient — and niches cut into the walls, dozens of them, each holding a small carved token: birds, hands, doors, keys. Offerings left by travelers who had wanted something badly enough to pay in the only currency that mattered here.

Mara walked straight to the hearth and put her palm flat on the cold stone.

She made a sound. She had been making more of them lately — single syllables of the First Tongue, dropped like seeds into our company, most of them still growing meanings I couldn't yet harvest. This one I knew. We'd gone over it a dozen times by firelight, her fingers shaping it against mine until my stubborn tongue got the grammar right.

*Remember.*

"Yes," I told her. "I remember plenty. That's rather the problem."

But the hearthstone warmed under her hand. And the niches along the wall began, very softly, to sing.

I want to be precise about this, because precision was all I had left after what happened next. They didn't sing words. They sang the way my carved bones whispered — that dry, intimate rustle of the dead getting ready to say their piece. Every token in every niche woke at once, and the room filled with the accumulated wanting of ten thousand travelers, and all of it, somehow, all of it, was aimed at me.

"Oh no," I said. "No. I've paid my tolls. Ask the ferryman. Ask anyone. I am famously paid up—"

The nearest niche held a carving of a girl. Small, crude, the kind of thing a child makes with a soft stone and too much feeling. Two figures holding hands. One of them had hair scratched in long lines down her back.

My hands knew that hair. My hands had made that carving.

I was six years old. There was a river behind the house, and Edith — eight years old, gap-toothed, insufferable, *mine* — dared me to carve us into the soft shale at the water's edge, and I did, and she laughed and said I'd be famous someday, and I believed her because she was my big sister and big sisters are the first gods we worship—

"Beatrix." Ovid's hand on my shoulder. "Beatrix, look at me."

"I'm looking at a rock."

"You're looking at a memory that isn't yours."

And there it was. The sentence I would spend the rest of this story trying to survive.

* * *

Here is what I knew about my sister before that afternoon, and I want you to weigh it, because I weighed it for forty years without ever once noticing how little there was:

Edith died when I was young. That was the whole of it. Died of what? I don't know. Where? I don't know. How old was I? Six? Seven? What did we bury — where did we bury her — who told me — what did I feel?

Nothing. I felt nothing, because there was nothing to feel. A hole shaped exactly like a fact, and I had spent my entire life walking around it the way you walk around furniture in a dark room you grew up in. You never look at the furniture. You just know where it is.

I built a valley around that hole. I built thirty years of quiet around it. I told myself I came to Hollowmere for the silence, for the work, for the honest company of the dead. And the dead came to me through the bones and gave me their last words, and I took those words and made them beautiful, and everyone said what a gift I had, what a vocation, and I agreed, because agreeing was easier than asking why a woman who could hear everything the dead ever said had never once heard from her own dead.

Not once. Forty years, and Edith's whisper never came through a single bone.

I thought it was grief. I thought the silence was the wound.

It wasn't the wound. It was the bandage.

"They edited you," Ovid said. He had gone very pale, the way he went pale whenever the Court's methods came up, and I understood in that moment that he had recognized the shape of this before I had — that some part of him had known since the night he knocked on my door with a mute child in tow. "Memory-work. It's the oldest thing they do. It's the *first* thing they do. Beatrix—"

"Don't." I held up a hand. It was shaking. "Don't tell me what they do. Tell me what *they did*. To me. Start at the beginning and don't decorate it."

He looked at Mara. Mara looked at me. And the child — who could not speak, who had crossed a kingdom in silence while the Court hunted her like a lamp-flame in a wind — reached up and took my face in both her small cold hands, and looked at me with an expression I have spent my life carving into memorial bones for other people's families, and I understood that whatever came next, she had decided I could carry it.

So Ovid told me.

* * *

They tell you the Ashen Court eats memories, and you imagine something crude. A theft. A mugging in the dark of your own head. That's not what it is. What it is, is *editing*. They take a memory the way a censor takes a knife to a letter — cutting out the dangerous passage and stitching the edges so neatly you'd swear the letter was always that short. The mind heals around the absence. The mind *prefers* the absence. A missing memory doesn't ache; it just isn't there, and the self flows over the gap like water over a stone, and after a while the stone might as well never have existed.

Except the stone remembers being a stone.

That's what the shrine was doing. Old covenant shrines weren't just tollbooths. They were *archives* — places where the land itself kept copies of what passed through, in case the Court's scissors ever came through the neighborhood. The tokens in the niches weren't offerings. They were receipts.

And somewhere in that round cold room, the land had kept a receipt for a six-year-old girl named Beatrix Winterbourne, and the receipt was waking up.

It came back in pieces, and the pieces came back wrong-side-out, the way a word looks strange when you stare at it too long.

Edith at the river, laughing. Edith teaching me to carve, her hand over mine on the flint. Edith singing — singing *what?* Something about embers. Something about a hearth. Our grandmother's song, the one nobody else in the village would sing anymore, the one that made our mother go quiet and leave the room.

Edith, at fourteen, standing in the doorway at night, arguing with men whose faces I couldn't hold onto — men in grey, men whose voices sounded like wind through a burned forest. *"She's too young. Take the bargain or leave, but she stays."*

Edith, at sixteen, packing a bag in the dark. Kissing my forehead while I pretended to sleep. Whispering into my hair words I suddenly, horribly, remembered hearing and forgetting a hundred times: *"Whatever they tell you happened to me, little wren, don't believe it. Don't stop looking. Promise me—"*

And then — nothing. A seam. A place where the cloth of my childhood had been cut and stitched, and the stitching was so fine I had worn the garment my whole life without ever once feeling the join.

"She didn't die," I said. My voice came out flat and far away, like it belonged to one of my bones. "There was no body. There was no grave. There wasn't even a *story*. I just — accepted it. Forty years. I accepted a death with no details the way you accept weather."

"The Court took her," Ovid said quietly. "Sixteen years old, daughter of a line that still carried the old blood — the same line Mara comes from. Covenant-fuel. Living fuel keeps the ember bright longer than ash does. They've been feeding her to the First Hearth's failing flame for forty years, Beatrix. Bit by bit. Memory by memory. She may still be—"

"*Don't.*" The word cracked out of me like green wood. "Don't you dare say *may still be*. Don't give me hope like it's a bone you're tossing. You knew. You sat at my fire, you ate my bread, you watched me carve her name into bone and call her *dead*, and you knew."

"I suspected." His voice didn't flinch, and I hated him for it, and I loved him for it, and I have never in my life been so confused about another human being. "I suspected enough to hide Mara's lineage from them for years. I didn't know enough to break an editing that good. Beatrix — the work on you wasn't done by some field agent. This was the Regent's own craft. When they edit, they usually leave the grief. Grief is useful; it keeps people small. They didn't leave you grief. They left you *nothing*, and let you build a life in the nothing. Do you understand what that means?"

"That I'm hollow."

"That you were *dangerous*. That whoever arranged it looked at a grieving girl with your talent and decided the safest thing to do was remove the reason she'd ever use it." He stepped closer. "Your exile wasn't a wound, Beatrix. It was a cage. Built for you, measured for you, furnished with thirty years of quiet. And the door was never locked, because they made sure you'd never think to try it."

The vertigo hit me then, properly, the way it must hit a woman who learns the floor she has stood on for forty years was painted on air.

Every choice. Every one. Coming to Hollowmere — was that me, or was that the shape of the hole they'd cut? Learning to carve bones — or was the talent itself the leash, keeping me busy, keeping me *here*, keeping me listening to strangers' dead so I'd never listen for my own? The contentment I had wrapped around myself like a shawl — had I woven it, or had they woven it and handed it to me and called it my own?

I had spent thirty years believing I was a woman who had chosen peace.

I was a woman who had been *edited into choosing peace*, and the cruelest part — the part that made me laugh, actually laugh, a horrible sound that made Sef take a step back and Mara grip my sleeve — the cruelest part was that it had worked. It had worked beautifully. I had been *happy*. I had been so grateful for my small gray life, so proud of my contentment, and all of it, every warm coal of it, was a scar in the shape of a prison.

"Beatrix." Sef, of all people. The thief with no name, crouched a careful distance away like a man near a frightened animal. "Hey. Bone-carver. Look at me. Whatever they took — you're still the one standing here. They cut things out of you and you *still* ended up on the road, with the kid, heading for the Hearth. If their edit was supposed to keep you small, somebody's edit failed."

"Somebody's edit," I repeated.

"Mara's," Wren said, from the doorway, where she had been standing the whole time with an expression I'd never seen on her — no humor at all. "Or fate's. Or Edith's. Ghosts talk, Beatrix. Even edited ones. Especially edited ones. Why do you think a child who can't speak walked out of nowhere to *your* door, of all the doors in the world?"

I looked down at Mara. She was still holding my sleeve. Her eyes were wet and furious and certain, all at once, the way only children's eyes can be.

And I thought: *they took her sister too.* Whatever the Court had done to Edith, they had done it to make sure that a girl with the old blood would grow up alone, untaught, unguarded, easy to collect later if the first harvest ran thin. Two sisters. Two edits. One to feed the fire and one to keep the ashes tidy.

The rage arrived then. Not the hot flash I'd have expected — something colder and larger, rage with architecture, rage with a floor plan, rage that looked around my ruined sense of myself and started, methodically, taking inventory.

They had taken my sister. They had taken my grief. They had taken my search, my questions, my forty years. They had taken my *yes* — my consent, the one thing a person actually owns — and used it to build my cage.

Fine.

Then they could watch me take it back.

I knelt at the cold hearth, took out my knife, and pulled the oldest piece of bone I carried — the rib I'd taken from my workshop, the one that had spoken the word *Hearth* on the worst night of my life. I set it on the stone. Around us, the niches were still singing, ten thousand receipts rustling in their alcoves like leaves deciding whether to fall.

"Mara," I said. "Teach me the word for *return*."

She blinked. Then she smiled — a fierce, tear-streaked, terrible smile, the smile of a child who has been waiting her whole short silenced life for an adult to ask exactly that question — and she took my hands and began to shape the sounds.

Because here is what I understood, kneeling in a shrine full of the land's long memory, with my identity lying around me in pieces like a dropped sculpture: if they could edit me, then the edit was a *thing*, and anything that is a thing can be found, and anything that can be found can be carried, and anything that can be carried can be brought home.

They had made my whole life a lie. Very well. A lie has edges, and edges can be traced, and I had spent thirty years learning to read the truth out of dead material.

Let the Court keep its scissors. I was going to the First Hearth, and I was going to find every piece of myself they'd cut away, and my sister with it — alive, ash, or somewhere in between — and I was going to carve their theft into the one archive they could never burn:

the new covenant itself.

Mara's fingers moved against mine. Outside, far off across the hills, the wind changed, and it smelled faintly, unmistakably, of smoke.

Something had noticed us noticing.

"We move," Ovid said, already reaching for his pack. "Now. Before—"

The hearthstone beneath my hands went from warm to hot.

And every token in every niche turned, all at once, to face the door.
