## 17. The First Hearth

I have been dead for two hundred years, and in all that time I have learned exactly three things worth telling.

First: ghosts do not float. That is a lie invented by living people who find the alternative undignified. We walk. We trudge, mostly, because being dead is ninety percent trudging and ten percent spite.

Second: you cannot haunt a place you did not love. The dead cling to what they loved, and if they loved nothing, they simply go out like a candle in a draught. I have watched it happen. It looks like falling asleep standing up, except you never get the courtesy of actually sleeping.

Third—and this is the one that matters tonight—the First Hearth remembers everything.

I know this because I helped forge what it holds, and I have spent two centuries trying to forget it, and I have failed at both jobs magnificently.

* * *

We smelled it before we saw it. Or rather, Ovid smelled it, which surprised no one, because priests can smell their own god's house the way hounds smell home. He stopped so abruptly on the ridge that Sef walked into his back and swore about it in three languages, two of them invented.

"What," Beatrix said. She had Mara's hand in hers, and the girl was humming again—that low, wordless thread of the First Tongue she carried like a second pulse. The hum had been getting louder for two days. So had the light behind Mara's eyes, which was not a thing I enjoyed noticing, because eyes should not glow, and children's eyes especially should not.

Ovid didn't answer Beatrix. He was staring down into the valley below the ridge with an expression I recognized from my own funeral, which everyone attending wore when they realized the coffin was going to be heavier than expected.

"Smoke," he said finally. "Not ash-smoke. Hearth-smoke. Two hundred years, and the stones still—" His voice did something complicated and gave up.

"Lead the way, then," Sef said, "since apparently your nose knows the neighborhood."

"It knows the neighborhood," Ovid said quietly. "That's rather the problem."

I drifted ahead of them down the slope, because I could, and because I wanted to arrive first. Call it vanity. Call it the fact that I had been walking toward this place since the river Vell, since before the river Vell, since the night I died on its threshold trying to keep something out—or in; even now I flinch from deciding which—and I wanted to see it alone before anyone else's grief got smeared across it.

The valley of the Hearth opened like a cupped hand.

It had been beautiful once. I remember beauty; it's one of the few possessions the dead keep. Terraced gardens climbing the slopes, orchards, the long white road where pilgrims came from every kingdom to lay down a memory at the fire and receive a blessing back. The Hearth itself at the center: a ring of black stone wide as a village square, and in its heart the First Flame, which was not fire as you understand fire but agreement—living, burning agreement between two worlds that they would not devour each other.

Now the terraces were ribs. The orchards were charcoal sculptures of themselves, frozen mid-scream by some ancient heat. The white road was grey, and cracked, and grew nothing, not even lichen, as though the land itself had been asked a question and refused ever to answer again.

And the Hearth—

The Hearth still stood. Of course it did. It was built to outlast everything, including, as it turned out, its own purpose. The black stone ring was unbroken, uncracked, polished smooth by two centuries of wind and neglect. But the center was dark. Cold. A hearth with no flame is not merely empty. It is an accusation.

I stopped at the edge of the ring, where I had stopped once before, two hundred years ago, with a sword in my hands and a choice in my throat.

"Well," I said to nobody, because that's most of my conversation these days. "Home again."

* * *

They caught up to me at dusk. Of course they did; the living are slow but relentless, like weather.

Mara felt it first. She always feels things first now—I try not to resent that, and succeed maybe half the time. She pulled free of Beatrix's hand and walked to the ring's edge and laid both palms flat against the black stone, and the stone *answered*. I saw it happen. A shiver went through the whole circle, a ripple like a finger dragged across a drumhead, and every ghost within fifty miles—including me, thank you very much—felt it in whatever we use instead of spines.

"She's reading it," Ovid breathed. He had gone pale, which for him took effort; the man has the natural complexion of someone who has been apologizing for decades. "No. She's being *read*. The Hearth recognizes—"

"Don't say it," Beatrix said.

"Mara is covenant-stuff, Beatrix. She was always going to recognize it."

"I said don't say it."

Sef, wisely, said nothing, and busied himself with a fire that didn't need building, because a man who sold his name has learned when to make himself small. I hovered near him, as I do. He pretends not to notice me. He is bad at pretending. When he thinks no one watches, he glances at the space where I stand, the way you glance at a scar.

Mara lifted her hands from the stone and looked at Beatrix and made her gesture—the flat palm, the small urgent circles she used before her words came. *Show me. Tell me.*

Beatrix looked at Ovid. Ovid looked at the ground. And I thought: *oh, hang it all.* Two hundred years of trudging, and it was going to end with the truth coming out of *me*, the least qualified person present, the ghost who'd been dodging this story since the ferry.

"The Hearth doesn't want Ovid's confession," I said.

Everyone turned. They always turn. Being a ghost means being interrupted constantly and ignored completely, usually in that order.

"It wants mine," I said. "Because I was there. Because I'm the only one left who was there. And because—" Here I paused, which is a luxury the dead have; we can pause for years. "Because the stones remember, and they've been waiting two hundred years to replay it, and I'd rather tell it myself than watch it happen to all of you like a play none of us bought tickets for."

"Tell us what?" Beatrix asked. Her voice was careful. She is a woman who carves bones for a living; she knows how much weight a small thing can hold.

"How the covenant was forged," I said. "The real forging. Not the hymn version. Not the version Ovid's temple taught, with trumpets and a benevolent flame and everybody going home for supper." I looked at Mara, and her glowing eyes looked back, patient as winter. "The true version. The one with the price in it."

* * *

Here is what the stones remember, and here is what I told them, sitting on the cold black rim of the First Hearth while the stars came out over a valley that died for love:

The covenant was not forged by gods. There were no gods in it. That was the whole point, though the temples later found this embarrassing and edited it out. It was forged by people. Nine of them. Guardians, they called themselves, back before the word curdled into *Ashen Court*. Nine men and women who stood between the living world and the Ember Realm in the age when the boundary was thin as skin and things crossed it the way weather crosses a border—indifferently, destructively, often.

Nine guardians, and a problem: the boundary needed binding, and binding needs material, and there is only one substance in either world strong enough to hold two realities together.

Memory.

Not metaphorically. Literally. A memory, freely given, freely surrendered, becomes a thread. Enough threads, woven together, become a rope. Enough rope becomes a wall. The First Flame was nine people's entire remembered lives, burned willingly—not destroyed, never destroyed, memory is never destroyed, only *moved*—poured into the space between worlds as mortar.

"They didn't die for it," I told them. My voice sounded strange against the stones, which were listening; I could feel them listening, the way you feel a crowd go quiet. "Everyone assumes sacrifice means death. It doesn't. Death is easy; I've done it, I can recommend it. What they did was worse. They walked into that fire alive, each of them, one at a time, and they gave up everything they remembered. Their names last. Names burn slowest." I looked at my own hands, which do not exist. "The Flame kept those memories safe. That was the bargain. The memories weren't lost—they became the wall itself. Every guardian who stepped into the fire forgot their whole life, and lived on afterward as a stranger to themselves, tending a flame they couldn't remember why they'd lit."

Silence. Even the wind stayed out of it.

"And you?" Beatrix asked softly. She is not a fool. She had already done the arithmetic; I could see the answer sitting behind her eyes, waiting for permission.

"Ninth," I said. "I was the ninth guardian. Youngest. I drew the short straw, or volunteered—honestly, after two hundred years, I've told myself both stories so many times I've worn grooves in them." I laughed, or produced the sound that stands in for laughing when you have no lungs. "But here's the part the hymns also leave out, the part that matters, the part I need you to hear before tomorrow, because tomorrow you're going to be standing where I stood:

"The fire cannot take. It can only receive. That is the law underneath all the other laws. A memory given under compulsion is ash—a dead thing, good for nothing. The Court learned that the hard way; that's why they're starving, why they hunt, why they consume and consume and are never full. Everything they've taken was taken, and taken things won't burn clean. But a memory *given*—freely, knowingly, with open eyes—that burns forever. That's the fuel. That's the only fuel there has ever been."

I stood up, which for me is mostly a matter of deciding to be vertical.

"The original covenant is failing because the guardians' memories are finally burning through—two thousand years of borrowed time, spent. You cannot patch it. You cannot steal enough memories to refill it; the Court has proven that for a century. There is exactly one way to reforge it, and it is the old way, and it is terrible, and it works."

"And that way is?" Sef asked, though his face said he knew.

"Someone gives everything," I said. "Freely. All of it. Every memory they own, poured into the Hearth, woven fresh. And then they live on afterward as a stranger to themselves, tending a fire they can't remember lighting." I let that sit. I am crueler than I look, and I look like a draft with opinions. "I'm telling you now so that no one decides anything in the heat of the moment. Freely given means freely *chosen*, in advance, in cold blood, with time to run away. That's not a technicality. That's the whole law. The moment it's coerced, it's worthless, and we might as well hand the realms to the Court and save everyone the walking."

Beatrix was looking at the cold center of the Hearth. Her face did the thing I had seen it do over bones a hundred times—the listening face, the face of a woman who spends her life receiving other people's endings.

"That's why you followed us," she said. "Since the river. You weren't guiding us. You were deciding whether to warn us."

"I was doing both," I said. "Badly. In parallel. It's called multitasking."

Mara touched my arm. I felt it—ghosts feel almost nothing, but a covenant-child's touch goes through us like sunlight through water. She looked up at me and spoke one word aloud, her voice rusty and small and absolutely certain:

"*Given.*"

"Yes," I said. "Given. That's the word. That's the whole religion, sweetheart, in one syllable more than the Court ever managed."

* * *

We made camp inside the ring, because outside it the dark had teeth in it lately, and because the Hearth's stones hold a warmth that isn't warmth—it's remembering, and it keeps worse things out than cold does.

I took the night watch, which is a joke; I don't sleep. I walked the rim of the circle while the others lay in their bedrolls, and as I walked, the stones began to show me things. They do that here. The Hearth replays what it witnessed; it has nothing else to do with its evenings.

I saw the nine guardians arrive, two thousand years ago, young and terrified and certain. I saw the orchards green. I saw pilgrims on the white road, laying memories down like flowers. I saw the first guardian step into the Flame—a broad man, laughing, waving goodbye to his own name—and come out the other side blinking, a stranger, asking *why is everyone crying?*

And I saw the night I died.

I had forgotten—I use the word loosely; the dead misplace things constantly—that I did not fall defending the Hearth from invaders. There were no invaders. The Court did not yet exist. What attacked the Hearth that night was a *leak*: a tear in the half-made covenant, the Ember Realm pressing through like water through a bad seam, hungry and mindless. Someone had to hold the seam shut while the eighth guardian finished her burning. Someone had to stand in the gap and be, briefly, a wall.

I volunteered. I remember that much clearly, and I will tell you it was not bravery. It was arithmetic. Eight guardians nearly done, one seam failing, and I was the only one whose memories weren't already in the fire. If I died holding the gap, my death would seal it—death is a kind of giving, if you don't fight it—and the covenant would close complete.

So I stood in the gap. And it burned through me. And it hurt exactly as much as you fear, and less than you'd hope, and the last thing I thought was not *for the realms* or *for my comrades* or any of the noble nonsense I'd rehearsed.

It was: *I never got to see the orchards in spring.*

Two hundred years, and that's the debt I'm owed. One spring. One orchard. I have haunted my way across half a map collecting interest on it.

I stood at the rim until dawn grayed the valley, watching the stones replay my death on a loop, and I found—to my considerable irritation—that I wasn't sad anymore. I was *clear*. Terribly clear, the way ice is clear. The covenant had been made by ordinary people giving up extraordinary things, freely, and it had held for two thousand years, and it could hold again, and the price had not changed, and the price would be paid by someone in this camp, and every hour I didn't say the next part aloud was an hour of theft.

Because there was a next part. There always is.

At dawn, Beatrix rose and built a small fire and set water on to boil, and Mara sat beside her learning letters in the ash with a stick, and Ovid prayed badly, and Sef slept like a man hiding from creditors. Ordinary morning. Last ordinary morning, as it turned out.

I drifted to Beatrix's shoulder and said, quietly, so only she heard:

"There's one more thing the stones showed me. About the reforging. About *who* the fire will ask for."

She didn't turn around. Her hands went still over the kettle.

"And it isn't me," I said. "I'm already spent. The fire doesn't want a ghost's leftovers." I swallowed, which is theater, but some theaters deserve maintaining. "It wants the person here with the most to give. The deepest vault. The longest ledger of the dead."

The wind moved through the dead orchards, and far off—too far off, wrong-direction far off—the horizon over the Ember Wastes began, very faintly, to glow the color of a lit window.

"They're coming," I said. "And Beatrix—we need to talk about your sister."
