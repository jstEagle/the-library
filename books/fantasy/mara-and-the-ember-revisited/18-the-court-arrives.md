## 18. The Court Arrives

**Beatrix**

I had carved bones for thirty years, and in all that time I had never once heard the sky make a sound.

Now it did. It was not thunder. Thunder comes from below and rolls upward, all bluster and no intent. This came from above and pressed downward, patient as a hand closing over a candle flame. The clouds over the First Hearth had gone the color of old ash in a cold grate, and they were *descending*—not falling, settling, the way silt settles in still water, deliberate and total.

"They're here," Ovid said. He had his back to me, which I had learned was how he delivered his worst news. "All of them."

"All of what?"

"The Court." He turned. His oath-mark—the brand on his throat that had betrayed him at the waystation—was glowing through his collar like a coal wrapped in linen. He didn't try to hide it anymore. That frightened me more than the light did. "Not the riders. Not the hounds. The Court itself. They've come to a forging, Beatrix. They mean to be present for the birth of the new covenant so they can strangle it in the cradle."

Wren drifted up through the Hearth's broken floor, which she did when she was excited or alarmed, and she was doing it now with her hair standing out from her head like smoke deciding to be hair again. "The stones are singing," she said. "The old stones. They remember the first forging and they're—oh, that's not singing. That's screaming. Never mind."

"How long?" Sef asked. He had Mara's hand in his, and she had gone very still, the particular stillness of a child listening to something adults cannot hear yet.

Ovid looked at the sky. "Less than an hour. Perhaps much less."

I looked at my hands. They were steady, which surprised me, because everything else in me was shaking. Thirty years of carving bones teaches you one thing above all others: work is done with steady hands or not at all. Whatever came down through those clouds, I would meet it the way I met everything—with a blade in one hand and something true in the other.

"Then we have an hour," I said. "Someone put the kettle on."

Nobody laughed. I hadn't really expected them to. But Wren snorted, and that was nearly the same thing.

* * *

**Sef**

Here's what nobody tells you about the end of the world: it's mostly waiting.

The Ashen Court took its time coming down. The clouds lowered and lowered, and out of them came shapes like cathedral spires if cathedrals were built by things that hated cathedrals—black, jointed wrong, trailing cinders that fell upward instead of down. Behind them came the hounds, hundreds of them, grey smoke given legs and appetite. And behind *them*, walking where nothing should walk, came figures in robes the color of a fire's last hour.

And in front of everyone, alone, unhurried, came a woman in white ash-grey, and I knew her face, because I'd seen it carved into bone in Beatrix's pocket for weeks.

"That's her sister," I said. "That's the dead sister."

"She wears her," Ovid said quietly. "She isn't her. Understand that before you look at Beatrix. The Regent collects faces the way I used to collect relics. It will use that face like a knife uses a handle."

"Charming court you used to run."

"I said *used to*."

Mara tugged my sleeve. She pointed at the descending host, then at herself, then made a shape with both hands—a bridge, an arch—and shook her head hard. No. Then she pointed at the Regent and made the same shape and nodded slowly, and her eyes were wet and furious, and I understood: *they want me to be their bridge.*

"Then we don't let them ask nicely," I said, which was a stupid thing to say, because they were already asking, and the asking was the worst part.

Because the Regent stopped a hundred yards from the Hearth, in the trampled snow, and raised its voice—and it used the sister's voice, warm and cracked and human—and the whole valley heard it.

* * *

**Wren**

Two hundred years I have been dead, and in two hundred years I have learned that the living are loudest right before they break.

The Regent spoke, and its voice went into the stones of the Hearth and into our bones, and it said:

*Mara of the First Tongue. Child of the bridge. We do not come as enemies. We come as family.*

I watched Beatrix hear her sister's voice say the word *family*. I watched her hands close around the three carvings she carried—her sister among them—and I watched her knuckles go white as frost.

*You were born silent,* the Regent said, gentle, terrible. *You were born into a world that could not hear you. We can give you a world that speaks your language. Every word. Every name. Every memory ever surrendered to us, returned and multiplied—we will open the archive of everything the living gave away, and you will walk through it like a garden. You will know every mother's lullaby. Every dead man's last joke. You will never be lonely again, little key. Open the covenant to us, and hunger ends—not just ours. Everyone's. No one need ever lose anything again.*

Oh, it was good. I'll give the monster that. Two hundred years of watching liars, and this was the finest lie I'd ever heard, because every word of it was true except the last one.

No one need ever lose anything again—as long as someone else does the losing forever.

Mara stood at the lip of the Hearth with the wind pulling her hair and the whole burning sky leaning down toward her, and she looked at the Regent wearing a dead woman's face, and she looked at Beatrix, and I saw the exact moment the offer found the soft place inside her. Of course it did. She was a child. She had been mute in a loud world. Someone was finally offering to make the world quiet enough to hear her.

I moved to stand beside her. I couldn't touch her—I'd tried once; my hand went through her shoulder like fog through a fence—but I could stand where she could see me, and I could say, in my own voice, cracked and stubborn and two hundred years out of date:

"Don't listen, love. I refused to fade for two centuries out of pure spite, and spite has kept me warmer than any hearth. But even spite knows a bad bargain. That thing doesn't want to give you a garden. It wants to plant you."

She glanced at me. Almost smiled. And then the Regent played its cruelest card, and I wished to God I had a body again, if only to throw a punch with it.

*Ask your keeper,* it said, in the sister's voice, soft as snowfall. *Ask Beatrix Winterbourne what she would pay to hear her sister say her name one more time. Ask her what she is about to spend. And then ask yourself, child—if love costs this much, perhaps it should simply be free.*

* * *

**Ovid**

Every priest learns eventually that his god is a hunger with good manners. I learned it late, and I paid for the lesson with everything, and still the old liturgy rose in me when the Regent spoke—I felt my knees want to bend, felt the oath-mark heat against my throat, felt the beautiful rotten logic of it settle over my mind like ash on snow: *memory is power, power must be fed, feeding is holy.*

I bit down on it. I had practice now.

"The offer is real," I told Beatrix. We stood together at the Hearth's rim while the Court waited below, patient as debt. "That's what you must understand. They're not lying about the archive. Everything ever surrendered to them is still there—the memories, the names, the faces. Sef's mother. Your—" I stopped.

"My sister," Beatrix said. Flat. Steady. A chisel-strike of a voice.

"Yes. If Mara opened the covenant to them, the Regent would keep its word. It always keeps its word. That's the horror of it. It would give Mara everything, and it would take the *giving*—it would take the fact that anyone ever lost anything, and bury it so deep that grief itself would die, and with grief, everything grief makes. Love is mostly loss management, Beatrix. I learned that in their seminaries. Remove loss and you don't get joy. You get a world of well-fed ghosts pretending to be alive."

Below us, the Regent waited. It did not press. It didn't need to. The sky pressed for it, and the hounds circled, and the hour burned down like a wick.

"And if Mara refuses?" Beatrix asked.

"Then they take her, and they take the Hearth, and they unmake the forging before it happens. There is no third door." I hesitated. "Except the one we came here to build. And you know its price. You've known since the shrine."

She nodded once. She had been nodding like that since the Ember Wastes—small, final nods, a woman signing documents with her spine.

"There's one thing I don't understand," I admitted. "Why wear the sister's face? Cruelty serves no strategic purpose at a negotiation."

"It's not cruelty," Beatrix said. "It's a message. It's telling me it knows exactly what I'm going to feed the forge." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly wasn't. "It's saying thank you in advance."

* * *

**Mara**

*(She perceived it this way:)*

The sky was a mouth and the mouth was full of teeth made of other people's Tuesdays.

The Lady-with-Sister's-Face stood in the falling ash and held out her hands, and in her hands was everything: a garden of names, a river of lullabies, a room where every lost thing sat waiting to be picked up again. Mara could *smell* it, the way you smell bread through a wall. Warmth. Fullness. A world with no holes in it.

And the Lady showed her the other thing too, without words, because the Lady knew Mara's language better than anyone: the small grey woman who wouldn't fade, going out like a candle, smiling. The thief-boy getting his name back and walking away whistling. The priest kneeling in clean snow, forgiven. And Beatrix—Beatrix whole, Beatrix laughing, Beatrix with her sister's hand in hers in a kitchen that smelled of bread, no forge, no fire, no giving-away at all.

*You don't have to burn anything down,* the Lady said in the sister-voice, in the First Tongue, in the grammar that binds. *You only have to open the door the other way.*

Mara looked at the garden. It was beautiful. That was the trap's whole architecture—it was genuinely beautiful, and behind its beauty, thin as a wire through fruit, ran the truth: *nothing in the garden grows.* Picked flowers forever. Preserved laughter. A museum where the dead were kept polished and the living were kept fed, and nothing was ever born, because birth requires a hole to be born out of.

She thought of the bone with her own name on it, warm in her pocket. Beatrix had carved it without being asked. Had anchored her in the glass wastes with four letters and a steady hand.

She looked up at the sky-mouth and did not open the door.

But she did not close it either. Because there was a third thing, the thing the Lady could not see, the thing growing in her chest like a coal learning to be a sun—and the Lady leaned closer, hungry, certain, and that certainty was the last mistake the old world would let her make.

* * *

**Beatrix**

The Regent grew tired of waiting. Or perhaps it never intended to wait; patience is just a knife held politely.

It raised one hand—the sister's hand, I knew the scar on the knuckle, I had carved that knuckle into memorial bone forty years ago, I knew it better than my own—and the hounds came up the hillside like a tide of grey smoke, and the Court's spires began to descend in earnest, and the First Hearth's stones screamed their old scream, and Sef swore, and Ovid began the forging-rite in a voice that cracked on every third word, and Wren shouted something defiant and useless and wonderful—

And the Regent looked directly at me across the burning distance, wearing my sister's face, and smiled with my sister's mouth, and said, only to me, in a voice only I could hear, pitched under the chaos like a note under a song:

*"Hello, Bea. Miss me?"*

And I will tell you the truth, since truth is the only currency I have left to spend: for one full second, I wanted to say yes.

Then I stepped forward to the Hearth's edge, drew the carving of my sister from my coat, and held it up between us—her face in bone, her true face, the one no thief could wear—and I said the only words that mattered:

"You're not her. She's *mine*. And I'm about to introduce you to what that costs."

The ember in the Hearth's ruined heart flared once, like a held breath.

Mara opened her mouth—and this time, sound came out.
