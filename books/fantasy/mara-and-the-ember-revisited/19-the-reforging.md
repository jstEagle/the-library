## 19. The Reforging

I have spent thirty years listening to the dead tell me how they ended. I thought that made me an expert on endings.

It did not. It made me a woman standing in the ruin of the First Hearth with her hands full of bone and no idea what she was doing, which is, if I'm honest, the only position I've ever held with any real conviction.

The Hearth itself was smaller than I'd expected. Everything about it was smaller than I'd expected—the way grief is smaller once you finally set it down and look at it. A ring of blackened stones in a bowl of shattered mountain, each stone carved over with script so old it had worn into suggestion rather than language. The firepit at its center was cold. Had been cold for two hundred years, Wren said, since the night she died keeping strangers from putting it out for good.

Wren stood at the ring's edge now, translucent as breath on glass, watching the sky.

"They're coming," she said, unnecessarily. The sky had been telling us for an hour. It had gone the color of a bruise deciding whether to apologize, and the wind smelled of ash and something sweeter underneath—burnt sugar, my sister's kitchen, forty years gone. The Court knew exactly what it was doing. It always had.

Ovid knelt by the firepit, his oath-mark seared through his sleeve, both hands pressed flat to the old stones as though he could warm them by apology alone. "The structure holds," he said. "Barely. The covenant's skeleton is still here. It needs fuel."

"Fuel," Sef repeated from atop a fallen pillar, where he sat sharpening a knife he had no intention of using. "You keep saying that word like it doesn't mean *pieces of us*."

"It means pieces of us," Ovid said simply. "That's what the word has always meant. The first guardians knew it. They just forgot to mention it to anyone else." He looked up at me, and there was no flinching in him anymore; whatever the Court had lit in his blood at the waystation, Mara had burned out of him, and what remained was a man who had run out of lies to hide behind. "Beatrix. You already know what has to go in the fire."

I did know. I had known since the shrine, since the moment the truth of my sister cracked my memory open like an egg and showed me the yolk of my whole life: engineered exile, edited grief, thirty years of contentment built on a lie someone else had written into me. The Court took her because she was covenant-fuel. The Court erased her from me because a searching woman is dangerous and a grieving one is merely sad.

And now the only thing that could reforge what they'd broken was the same currency they'd been counterfeiting all along. Memory, freely given. That was the original price. Wren had told us in the stones' replay of her death, her voice steady and her hands shaking: *it required a willing sacrifice, freely given, or it doesn't bind.*

Freely given. There's the rub, as some dead poet probably whispered through somebody's rib once.

"Mara," I said.

She came to me across the ruined hearth-stones, small and deliberate, frost-scarred fingers laced with ash. She had stopped being mute somewhere on the Ember Wastes—not all at once, but word by word, the First Tongue surfacing in her like bones rising in a flood. She still said very little. But when she spoke, the world listened. I had watched hounds of grey smoke sit down and wait when she told them to. I had watched Ovid weep at a single syllable.

She looked up at me and signed, quick and fluid, the hand-language we'd built between us on the road: *Are you afraid?*

"Terrified," I said. "But I've been terrified since the solstice, and it hasn't improved my carving any, so I've decided to stop counting it as new information."

Her mouth did the thing it did instead of laughing—a small crook, private, like a door opening half an inch onto a lit room.

"I need you to understand what I'm about to do," I told her, and then, because she deserved better than a speech, I told her plainly. "Everything I have of the dead goes into that fire. Every whisper. Every name. Every hour I spent learning how grief sounds when it comes off a bone. And my sister. Whatever's left of her in me—even the edited version, even the lie—they get that too. All of it. And when it's done, I won't remember any of you. Not the road. Not the river. Not the ferryman taking Sef's mother's face. Not you."

Mara's chin came up. She signed back slowly, so I couldn't pretend to misunderstand: *Then I will remember for you.*

"That's not how it works, love. The covenant takes. It doesn't lend."

She stamped her foot—an actual stamp, the first childish thing she'd done since the valley—and then she grabbed my wrist and pulled me toward the firepit, and wrote in the ash with one finger, fast and fierce:

**NOT TAKING. BRIDGE.**

"I don't—"

Ovid made a sound like a man who has just remembered where he left something vital. "The grammar," he breathed. "Beatrix, the grammar. You told me yourself. The First Tongue doesn't describe—it binds. If she speaks herself into the covenant as its bridge rather than its fuel—" He stopped, because saying it aloud made it real, and real things in this story had a habit of costing limbs.

"What does a bridge do?" Sef asked from his pillar, and for once there was no cynicism in it. He'd sold his own name once; he knew what it was to be a gap people crossed over without seeing.

"A bridge," Wren said softly, "holds both sides at once. Without belonging to either." She looked at Mara with two hundred years of dead woman's knowledge in her face. "Child. Do you know what you're offering?"

Mara nodded. Then, after a moment, she shook her head. Then she shrugged, which I felt in my chest like a bell struck, because it was the most honest answer anyone had given in this entire wretched, holy business.

The sky tore.

Not lightning—tearing, like cloth, like skin, and through it came the Ashen Court, descending on the First Hearth the way a verdict descends on a courtroom: slowly, formally, with every intention of being final. They were beautiful, which is the worst thing I can truthfully say about them. Tall figures of grey smoke and cinder-light, wearing robes woven from other people's yesterdays—I could see faces flickering in the weave, mouths mid-laugh, mid-word, mid-goodbye, worn as decoration. And at their center, robed in white ash, crowned in cooling embers, walked the Cinder Regent.

Wearing my sister's face.

Not a copy. Not an approximation. Her actual face, aged the forty years she'd been denied, carried on a body of smoke with the patient cruelty of a thing that had studied my grief the way a scholar studies a text.

"Little sister," the Regent said, in a voice like my name spoken at bedtime. "You brought it home to us. The key. The vessel. We only ever wanted to spare you this part."

"You didn't want to spare me anything," I said. My voice held. Thirty years of talking to the dead teaches you to keep your voice level while your insides are screaming; turns out it's the same skill for talking to monsters. "You wanted me quiet. There's a difference, and I've had a long road to learn it on."

"The covenant is dying," the Regent said, spreading smoke-hands wide over the cold hearth. "We are what remains of its guardians. Let us feed, and both realms live—unequally, yes, but alive. Refuse, and everything ends. Is that not mercy? Is that not the gentler arithmetic?"

"It's theft with good posture," said Sef, dropping from his pillar.

"It's rot wearing duty's coat," said Ovid, rising, his oath-mark blazing.

"It's boring," said Wren, "and I died once already, and let me tell you, the second time is never as good."

The Regent's eyes—my sister's eyes, God help me, the exact grey of rain on the valley—moved past all of them and settled on Mara.

"And you," the Regent said, gentler now, gentler the way a snare is gentle until it closes. "They will spend you too, little key. The priest will use you. the thief will sell you. The ghost cannot touch you. And the carver—" the Regent smiled my sister's smile, and I felt it in my teeth— "the carver will give everything she is to the fire and forget you before the ash cools. Come to us. We remember everyone we take. Forever. You would never be alone again. You would be *kept*."

And Mara—frostbitten, mute-then-speaking, hunted since before she could walk—looked up at the Regent wearing a stolen face, and did the bravest thing I have ever seen anyone do.

She shook her head.

Then she stepped into the cold firepit, turned to face both realms at once, and began to speak.

* * *

I will try to render what happened next, though rendering is a carver's word and this was music, or weather, or both.

The First Tongue poured out of her—not the single words she'd been finding on the road, but the whole river of it, and I understood suddenly why the language was called First. It wasn't old. It was *original*. Every word she spoke was the word that thing *was*: when she said *stone*, the stones remembered being mountains; when she said *ash*, the ash remembered being oak and pine and green. The covenant hadn't failed because it was weak. It had failed because everyone who tended it had treated it as a wall, a lock, a leash—and it was none of those things. It was a sentence. And sentences need speakers on both ends.

"She's opening it," Ovid whispered, agonized. "Beatrix, if she opens it to them—"

"She isn't opening it to them," I said. "Watch."

Because the Court had surged forward when Mara began speaking, hungry, certain—and found they could not cross the hearth-ring. Not because it barred them. Because Mara's words were rebuilding the sentence of the covenant around her, and the sentence had a subject, and the subject was *her*, and the Court could no more enter her covenant than a debt can enter a paid account.

The Regent understood one heartbeat before I did. My sister's face went white with fury.

"*Fuel*," the Regent snarled at me. "It must be fed! What will you burn, carver? What do you have?"

"Everything," I said.

And I meant it, and meaning it is the whole of the magic, and so I knelt at the edge of the firepit and I gave.

I gave the whisper of the drowned ferryman who'd asked me to carve *she waited* on his oar-bone. I gave the soldier's last word, which was *marmalade*, and the laugh I'd had to swallow so he wouldn't hear it. I gave thirty years of Hollowmere mornings, the smell of marrow-dust, the particular silence of a workshop full of the finished dead. I gave the edited memory of my sister, the false one, the soft-focus lie the Court had left me—and beneath it, cracking open as I surrendered it, the true one: her real laugh, her terrible singing, the day she taught me to hold a chisel, the last thing she ever said to me, which the Court had cut out of me and kept, and which came home to me now for one burning instant—

*"Keep the fire small, Bee. Big fires eat what feeds them."*

—and I gave that too, I gave it gladly, I fed it all into the cold pit between Mara's bare feet, and the pit caught.

Not flame. *Remembering.* Light rose from the hearth the way heat rises off a living body, and every mote of it was a memory returning to the world—the Court's hoard tearing loose, decades and centuries of stolen yesterday streaming out of those smoke-woven robes like birds out of a burning barn. The lesser Ashen came apart first, unraveling into crowds of faces, each face flying home to a town, a grave, a grieving family waking at dawn with a name back on their tongue. The realm itself drank. I felt it drink, through the soles of my feet, through the bones in my hands: land going from grey to merely wintered, glass-cracked fields remembering how to be soil.

The Regent fought it. Of course the Regent fought it. Clutching my sister's face like a drowning woman clutching driftwood, shrieking in a voice that was suddenly many voices, suddenly *no one's*—and then the last of the hoard ripped free, and there was nothing left to wear the face with, and the Cinder Regent came apart into ordinary ash, and my sister's face—her true face, freed—rose up out of the ruin for a moment, unaged, unafraid, and looked at me.

She mouthed something. I was already forgetting what her mouth looked like. But I read it anyway, the way you read a word you'll lose by morning:

*Thank you. Go home.*

Then she was light, then she was everywhere, then she was the dawn coming up over the shattered mountain, and I was kneeling by a warm hearth with empty hands and a head full of clean snow, and I could not have told you my own sister's name to save my life.

Because I no longer had one to save it with.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about sacrifice: afterward, it's quiet.

The realms mended around us with a sound like a long exhale. Mara stood in the hearth, glowing faintly, alive, *herself*—a bridge, not a burnt offering, holding both worlds in her small body the way a doorframe holds two rooms. She stepped down out of the firepit and walked to me, and I watched her come with the polite attention one gives a stranger.

She knelt. Took my empty hands. Signed to me—*are you hurt?*—and I understood the signs, because hands are hands, but I didn't know why this child cared, or who she was, or why the tall sad priest was crying, or why the transparent woman was laughing and fading at the edges like sugar in tea, waving at all of us with both arms like a departure I should apparently grieve.

"Beatrix," Ovid said, hoarse. "Do you know me?"

"No," I said honestly. "Should I?"

He laughed, wet and broken and relieved, which seemed an excessive response to a stranger's ignorance, but grief does strange bookkeeping.

Sef crouched in front of me, turning his own hands over and over, mouthing something silently—two syllables, three—and when he looked up his eyes were shining and furious and free. "My name," he said. "I got my name back. Do you—" He stopped. Whatever he saw in my face, he swallowed the rest of the question, and grinned instead, crooked and real. "Doesn't matter. You'll want to. Eventually."

I did not know what he meant. I want that noted for the record: I did not know what any of them meant.

Wren was nearly gone, a shimmer with a smile in it. She bent close, close enough that her voice arrived more as memory than sound—which, given the day, felt fitting. "You did it, carver," she said. "You won't remember doing it. That's the whole trick of it, isn't it? Love that doesn't need the remembering." She straightened, spread her arms at the sunrise, and faded with the sheer theatrical satisfaction of a woman who had waited two hundred years for a good exit and intended to enjoy it.

And then it was morning, properly, and the four of us—or the three of them and I, a stranger among them—stood in the ruins of the First Hearth with the covenant humming in the ground like a hearth banked for the night, and Mara took my hand, and pointed east, and said one word in the First Tongue that I did not understand but felt in my sternum like a compass needle settling.

Home, it meant. I would learn that later. I would learn all of it later.

Because that was the morning we started walking back to a valley I had no memory of, with three people whose names meant nothing to me and whose faces I somehow could not stop looking at—and in my pocket, though I had no idea how it got there, a single carved bone, smooth as a riverstone, bearing letters I did not recognize and a shape my thumb kept returning to like a question it already knew the answer to.
