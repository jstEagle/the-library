## 20. Ashes Giving Back

I want to tell you that the world ended with a bang, because I've always trusted a good loud ending. Fireworks. A last word. Something you could put on a headstone and feel was honest.

It didn't end with a bang. It ended with rain.

We were all still standing in the ruins of the First Hearth—what was left of us, which is to say: a bone-carver who'd just fed her entire life to a fire, a priest on his knees in the wreckage of his god, a ghost two hundred years overdue for retirement, a mute child glowing like the inside of an ember, and me, a thief with no name standing in a circle of ash, wondering if anyone had thought to bring breakfast.

The Court had come apart like a bad stitch. One moment the Cinder Regent had been wearing Beatrix's sister's face and offering Mara everything she'd never had; the next, Mara had spoken a word I felt in my teeth rather than heard, and the Regent had simply... unraveled. Not died. *Returned.* That's the part nobody warns you about with villains. Sometimes they aren't monsters at all. Sometimes they're stolen goods, and when the theft is undone, so are they.

And then the sky opened, and it started raining memories.

I don't have better words for it, and believe me, I've spent my whole life finding words for things people would rather I didn't name. It looked like rain. Grey-gold, fine as flour, falling slow over the Ember Wastes, over the glass fields, over the dead land where nothing had grown since before my grandmother's grandmother learned to lie. But where it touched, things happened. Grass came up through glass like it had been waiting at the door this whole time, politely, hat in hand. A dead tree fifty feet tall shrugged off its charcoal and stood there blushing green. Somewhere behind us, a river that had been a dry scar on the map remembered how to run, and we heard it start before we saw it—a long, relieved sigh of water.

"It's going back," Ovid said. He was still kneeling. He hadn't stood since the Regent came apart, and his voice had the flat, stunned quality of a man reading his own eulogy and finding it generous. "The memories the Court consumed. They're returning to their owners."

"Owners," Wren said, drifting beside him. "That's a strong word for what most of them did with them. Half those people traded theirs away for safety. You watched them do it."

"I know." Ovid's hands were open in his lap, palms up, like he was waiting to be arrested. "I know what I watched."

I left them to it. Guilt is a private ceremony; I've never liked attending other people's.

* * *

Here's what you should understand about me, if you've read this far without giving up: I sold my name when I was nineteen years old, to a debt-mage in Corvane named Halloway Fen, for enough coin to buy my mother a winter she didn't survive anyway. That's the joke I've told myself for eleven years, the one that keeps the wound from going septic. Sold my name, wasted the price. Ha ha.

What I've never said out loud—not to Beatrix, not to Wren, not even to myself at three in the morning—is what it's actually like, living nameless. People think it's a convenience. Walk into a room, nobody knows your sins. And it is convenient, right up until someone asks who you are and the answer is a hole where a person should be. Every introduction was a small amputation. Every time a friend—I'm looking at you, Beatrix, you stubborn woman—every time a friend said my name, and there wasn't one to say, and we both pretended the silence was nothing—

Well. Pretending is also a kind of work. I was very tired.

The rain found me before I found shelter. There wasn't any shelter; the Hearth's roof had been ash for centuries. So I just stood there like an idiot with my face turned up, and the grey-gold came down, and one drop landed on my tongue.

And I knew my name.

Not remembered it. *Knew* it, the way you know your own hand in the dark. It arrived whole, wearing its old clothes, smelling of the kitchen it was born in. My mother saying it across a table. My mother saying it angry. My mother saying it soft, the winter before the winter, when she already knew what she was trading her son's name to pay for and did it anyway because pride is cheaper than watching your child starve.

Sef.

No. That's the shape of it, the shell. The real thing—the sound of it, the weight, the way it fit against my mother's voice—I'm not writing that down. Some property you get back, you keep behind lock and key. But I'll tell you this much: I stood in the rain in the middle of a wasteland and said it out loud, once, just to hear the air agree to hold it, and then I sat down in the wet grass like a man whose legs had been recalled by their manufacturer, and I laughed, and then I did something undignified that I will deny under oath.

Wren drifted over and hovered above me, which is her version of tact.

"You got yours back too?" I asked her.

"Got mine back? Darling, I never lost mine. Stubbornness is free." She settled cross-legged in the air, which always looked like sitting on an insulted chair. "But I can feel it ending. The rain's touching me too. Can't you see?"

I could, actually. Now that she mentioned it. She was thinner than she'd been at the river. Thinner than she'd been yesterday. Edges going soft, like chalk in damp weather.

"No," I said. "Absolutely not. We just won. You don't get to die at the victory party. That's terrible manners."

"Two hundred years, Sef-with-no-name, and you're the rudest person I've met, and I met the Ashen Court." She smiled. It was a real smile, not the performance she wore like jewelry. "I held the Hearth door for six hours while the first covenant was forged. Did I ever tell you why?"

"Because you were paid handsomely and had no other prospects."

"Because my little brother was inside." Her voice went quiet, and quiet from Wren was rarer than honesty from a magistrate. "He was one of the memory-keepers. I bought him time. I didn't save him—he faded anyway, decades later, when the covenant started leaking—but I bought him time, and time was the only currency that mattered that night. And I've been standing at that door ever since. Two hundred years. Nobody relieved the watch." She looked up at the rain coming down through her, gold threading through grey. "Somebody just relieved the watch."

"Wren—"

"Don't you dare make a speech. I've seen your speeches. They're mostly complaints with better posture." She reached out and put her hand against my cheek, and it didn't pass through, and it was cold as river-stone and I would remember it forever except that's the thing about forever, isn't it, it has a shorter contract than advertised. "You're going to be unbearable now that you have a name. Good. Be unbearable loudly. It's the only way to be."

She was fading as she talked, going translucent from the edges in, and she didn't fight it, and that was the worst part. I've watched men fight death with their fingernails. She greeted hers like a guest she'd been expecting and had finally gotten around to tidying up for.

"Tell Beatrix—" she started, and stopped, and I watched her realize it at the same moment I did. Tell Beatrix what? Beatrix wouldn't know who Wren was. Wouldn't know any of us. The carver had poured every memory of every dead soul she'd ever tended into that fire, and Wren had been dead two hundred years, and dead was dead was dead in Beatrix's ledger.

"She'll carve you anyway," I said. "She won't know why. But her hands will. I've seen them do it."

Wren's smile cracked wide open, all the way to whatever she'd been before she was a ghost. "Oh, that's the best thing anyone's said to me in two centuries, and I once got a marriage proposal from a tax collector." She stood—or rose, or simply decided upward—and the rain fell through her and she let it, and the last thing to go was her voice, faint and delighted, like a bell rung once far away:

"There's my brother. He waited."

And then there was just rain, and grass, and me, crying in a field like a man with a name and no shame about using either.

* * *

Ovid knelt in the ruins of his god for a long time. Hours, maybe. The ember-priests had served the covenant's guardians once, before the guardians rotted into the Court, and Ovid had carried that service like a stone in his chest since the day he'd hidden Mara's lineage and burned his own ordination marks off with a coal shovel. I'd seen the scars. They weren't the kind you regret. They were the kind you wear to remind yourself you chose something once.

Now the god he'd betrayed and the god he'd fled were both gone, unravelled into weather, and he knelt in the ashes of both and I honestly couldn't tell you if he was grieving or praying or doing inventory.

Mara solved it, the way she solved most things, by walking over and sitting down next to him without asking permission. She'd been speaking more every day since the reforging—single words first, then phrases, the First Tongue coming out of her like water finding an old channel—and she said something to him then, low, in that language older than kingdoms.

Ovid flinched. Then he laughed. It was a wrecked sound, half sob, but it was a laugh.

"What did she say?" I asked.

"She said—" He had to stop and press the heels of his hands into his eyes. "She said the word for 'forgiven' and the word for 'begin' are the same word. In the First Tongue. They've been the same word all along, and none of us noticed."

"That's suspiciously convenient."

"That's covenants," Ovid said. "They're built out of true things. Convenience is just truth wearing work clothes." He got up, finally, knees cracking like green wood in a fire, and looked around at the greening waste, the running river, the rain still sifting down gold. "Someone will need to rebuild the priesthood. Properly, this time. Or disband it properly. Either way, someone who was inside it has to be the one holding the broom."

"Volunteering?"

"I'm the only one who knows where all the bodies are buried." He almost smiled. "Literally. Several of them are ones I buried."

Mara tugged his sleeve and pointed north, toward the road, toward Hollowmere, and made a small impatient sound—the universal grammar of children everywhere, in every tongue including the First.

"Yes," I said. "Home. Let's go home. I want to sleep somewhere the floor doesn't remember being a battlefield."

* * *

Beatrix stood apart from all of it, at the edge of the Hearth's foundations, and she was the reason I couldn't enjoy anything cleanly.

The rain touched her too. Of course it did. But her memories hadn't come back down with it—they'd gone *in* as fuel, burned at the forging, spent. Every whisper she'd ever caught in carved bone. Every name she'd ever cut into memorial ribs. Thirty years of the dead, given freely, and her sister most of all, the sister whose face the Cinder Regent had worn, the sister whose name Beatrix had nearly paid to a ferryman and then, in the end, had paid to a fire instead.

She remembered none of it. Worse—she remembered none of *us*. Wren had died ten feet away from her and Beatrix had watched a stranger fade. Ovid had fought beside her through the wastes and she'd thanked him with the polite distance you give a helpful innkeeper. And me. Eleven days on the road, and I'd told her things I've never told anyone, and she'd held my hand through the Ferryman's toll, and now when she looked at me I saw it happen behind her eyes: the search for recognition, the polite failure, the small apology of a stranger's smile.

She caught me watching her and walked over. Rain in her grey hair. Hands stained with ash and, absurdly, already nicked from carving—she'd picked up a shard of Hearth-stone on the road and her fingers had started working it without her permission, some habit deeper than memory.

"You're staring," she said. Mild. Almost amused.

"I'm a thief. Staring's how I work."

"Mm." She looked at the shard in her hand, turning it over. "Strange thing. I woke up this morning knowing how to do this"—she flexed her fingers—"and not knowing why I'd want to. Knowing the valley, and not knowing why I lived there. Knowing grief." Her jaw tightened, just slightly. "Knowing grief like a house I grew up in, with all the furniture gone. Do you know what I mean?"

I knew exactly what she meant. I'd lived in that house for eleven years. But I said, "Something like it," because you can't explain a person to themselves, and because my throat was doing something unsupportable.

"The child says you were friends," Beatrix said. "Her and the priest. They keep telling me things. What I did. Who I was." She paused. "Who *we* were."

"And do you believe them?"

"I believe my hands." She opened her palm. On the Hearth-shard, half-finished, were lines of carving—three names, in a style I recognized from her workshop, from bones I'd watched her make. I couldn't read them. I didn't need to. I'd seen her carve Wren's name once, laughing, on a lark, *so somebody remembers me properly*. I'd seen her carve Ovid's into a walking stick after the waystation. And the third—

The third was mine. My name. The one I'd gotten back an hour ago, out of the rain, that no living soul in this world had spoken in eleven years.

She couldn't have known it. She'd lost it with everything else. But her hands had kept the ledger her mind had burned, and there it was, cut clean and careful into stone, like it had never gone anywhere at all.

"That's—" I said, and stopped, and started again. "That's mine."

"Is it?" Beatrix looked at the carving, then at me, and something moved behind her eyes—not memory, but the shadow memory leaves on a wall. "Then I suppose I must have thought you were worth keeping."

* * *

We walked north the next morning, the four of us—five, counting the child, though Mara counted double these days, being a bridge between realms and all. The rain followed us for a while and then thinned into ordinary weather, blue sky, the boring kind, the kind I'd have killed for a year ago. Behind us the Ember Wastes were turning green in patches, and ahead of us the road ran home to a valley I'd never seen, where a woman with no past was going to have to learn who she'd been from four strangers who loved her.

Strangers. Right.

I rolled my name around my mouth the whole walk, quietly, like a coin I was afraid of dropping. And near evening, when the rebuilt towers of Hollowmere finally showed above the hills—smaller than I'd imagined, humbler, smoke rising from chimneys like the valley was breathing easy for the first time in centuries—Beatrix stopped on the road and frowned at the horizon, at the place where her life had supposedly happened.

"I feel like I've been away a long time," she said.

"You have," I said.

"From where?"

And I put my hand on her shoulder, the way a friend does, the way I'd earned the right to do across eleven days she no longer owned, and I told her:

"From home. But it's close now. And the people there—" I glanced back at Ovid, at Mara riding his shoulders and conducting the birds in the First Tongue, at the empty air beside me where a stubborn ghost used to hover, and at the shard in Beatrix's pocket with three names on it and a fourth half-begun. "The people there have been keeping your seat warm."

She looked at me for a long moment, this woman who had given away everything and kept only her hands, and then she nodded slowly, and her fingers, without her mind's consent, without her knowledge, began tracing a new name against her thigh as she walked.

A fourth name. Mine again. Practicing.

Like she was learning it by heart.
