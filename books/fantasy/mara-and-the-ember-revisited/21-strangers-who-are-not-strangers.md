## 21. Strangers Who Are Not Strangers

The valley of Hollowmere, in the spring after the world was mended, smelled the way it had always smelled to me: wet stone and pine resin and the particular sweetness of bone dust settling into fresh shavings. I had rebuilt my workshop on the same foundation as the old one, because apparently even a woman with no past trusts her feet to remember where home is.

I say *rebuilt* loosely. The walls were new timber. The roof was new slate. Everything else—the bench worn smooth by forty years of hands that were not mine anymore, the racks of carving knives, the little stove that smoked when the wind came from the east—everything else had been carried back up the mountain by people I did not know, who insisted they knew me.

That was the arrangement now. I lived among strangers who grieved me like family.

"You're staring at the ribs again," said the one called Sef.

He appeared in my doorway most mornings, ostensibly to deliver bread, actually to watch me work with an expression I'd learned to read despite having no right to: the look of a man checking whether a fire he'd tended all winter was still burning.

"I'm not staring," I said. "I'm listening."

"To what? You said the dead don't speak through them anymore."

"They don't." I turned the rib over in my fingers. It was elk bone, old, bleached white, and utterly silent—had been silent since the night at the First Hearth when I poured every whisper I'd ever heard into the covenant's flame. "But silence has textures. This one's not empty. It's *waiting*."

Sef leaned against the doorframe. He was a lean, quick-eyed man with a scar across his palm that he worried like a rosary, and when he thought no one was looking, he mouthed his own name to himself, tasting it, making sure it stayed. He'd told me once—I was told many things about myself, none of which I could verify—that before the Hearth, he hadn't been able to do that. That there had been a hole where his name should be, and that I had helped him get it back, though how exactly, nobody would say. They were all very careful about what they told me. It was maddening.

"Waiting for what?" he asked.

"For whatever comes next, I suppose." I set the rib down. "Bones are patient. It's their whole profession."

He laughed, but it came out wrong, half-strangled, and he looked away toward the window where the morning light lay across the valley floor. Below, Hollowmere was coming back to life—not just the village, which had never entirely died, but the land itself. The ash fields beyond the ridge had gone green at the edges. The river Vell ran clear. And far off, if you knew where to look, you could see the faint warm shimmer above the First Hearth, like heat rising off summer stone, except it never stopped.

"Mara comes tomorrow," Sef said. "For the season."

"I know. Ovid told me. Twice."

"He'll tell you a third time anyway. He's Ovid."

"He's also standing behind you holding a basket," I said, "which I assume contains more bread, since apparently this household believes I cannot survive without being fed by committee."

Ovid Crane filled the doorway beside Sef, grey-bearded, solemn as a headstone, carrying a basket that did indeed contain bread, honey, and—because the man had no sense of proportion—a jar of preserved plums. He had been an ember-priest once; he'd told me that much himself, freely, the way a man confesses to a crime he's already served time for. What he'd done, whom he'd served, why his hands shook sometimes when he touched anything warm—all of that lived in the part of my story other people carried for me.

"Lady Winterbourne," he said, with the formality he used precisely because I'd told him to stop.

"Ovid. If you bring plums again I'm charging rent."

"The plums are from Wren's tree," he said quietly.

And the room went still, the way it always went still when her name came up. Wren. The ghost who refused. Dead two hundred years, freed at last when the covenant was reforged—and before she faded, so they told me, she had pressed her hand to the earth outside the workshop and asked it, very politely, to grow something. The tree stood there now, young and absurdly healthy, blossoming out of season. They said it was like her. Stubborn. Cheerful at inappropriate moments.

I had carved her name into a piece of birch the first week I was home. I didn't know why. My hands had simply done it.

They did a great deal of things without consulting me.

* * *

It happened again that afternoon, and this time I let myself see it clearly.

I had been working a length of deer antler—no commission, no purpose, just the restless itch in my fingers that had plagued me since I woke in the ashes of the Hearth with thirty years of memory burned away and a stranger's calluses on my palms. The knife moved. I watched it move. And when I surfaced, the antler bore four names in the old flowing script I somehow knew but couldn't read:

*Sef. Ovid. Wren. Mara.*

I sat very still for a long time.

Here is what nobody tells you about losing your memory to a fire you lit yourself: your body keeps the receipts. My mind was a swept floor. But my hands—my hands remembered the weight of a child's frostbitten fingers going still under a blanket. My shoulders remembered bracing against a compulsion that wasn't mine. My throat remembered singing, badly, to keep someone's spirits up on a road full of ghosts. I was a house whose owner had left, and the furniture had opinions.

I wrapped the antler in cloth and put it in a drawer, and then I took it out again, because hiding felt like lying, and I had apparently spent enough of my life doing that.

When the four of them gathered that evening—Sef and Ovid by the stove, and the tree's shadow long through the window, and tomorrow's promise of Mara hanging in the air like woodsmoke—I set the antler on the table between us.

"I need to ask you something," I said. "And I need you not to manage me while I ask it."

Ovid folded his hands. Sef went carefully, deliberately casual, which was his version of standing at attention.

"My hands carve your names," I said. "All of them. Yours—" I nodded at Sef, "—and yours, priest. Hers." The tree's shadow. "And hers. I don't know you. I want to be clear about that. When I look at you, I see kind strangers who bring bread and plums and hover like anxious relatives. But my hands know you. My hands have been carving your names since the day I got home, onto everything that holds still. So somebody tell me plainly: who was I to you?"

The silence stretched. Outside, wind moved through Wren's tree, and the sound it made was almost, almost like laughter.

It was Sef who broke first—he always broke first, they told me; I was apparently the one who held out longest, which figures, since I was the one with nothing left to lose.

"You were the one who didn't give up on me," he said. "When everyone else had. Including me." His thumb found the scar on his palm. "You looked at a thief with no name and decided he was worth dying for. Nobody had ever done the math that way before. I've been trying to repay it ever since, and I'll be trying the rest of my life, and honestly? Best debt I ever owed."

Ovid spoke next, slowly, each word laid down like a stone in a wall. "You gave a coward a chance to be brave. I betrayed my god, my order, and nearly all of you, and you forgave me before I finished apologizing. I don't understand forgiveness, Lady Winterbourne. I only know that yours was real, and I have built what remains of my life on top of it."

"And Wren?" I asked, though the tree was already answering, blossoms opening in the dark window like small pale flames.

"She followed us two hundred years' worth of stubborn miles to finish what she started," Ovid said. "And when she faded, she wasn't afraid. She said she'd finally gotten to be part of something that lasted." He smiled, and it hurt to look at, the way beautiful sad things hurt. "She said you'd understand. She said you were the best listener she'd ever haunted."

I laughed. It came out wet. Somewhere in the middle of it, my hand had found the antler again, and my fingers were moving, tracing the fourth name. *Mara.*

"And her?" I whispered. "The child?"

Sef and Ovid exchanged a look over my head, the kind of look that contains an entire negotiation.

"She's not a child anymore," Sef said gently. "Not entirely. Bridges grow fast."

"But she's still hers," Ovid said. "Still ours. Tomorrow, when she comes down from the Hearth, she will walk into this valley where magic cannot follow, and she will knock on your door, and she will tell you—in the First Tongue, which you taught her to write and she taught you to speak—who you are. Because we agreed." His voice roughened. "We agreed that you should hear it from her. That you'd earned the telling."

"Why not just tell me yourselves?"

"Because," said Sef, with the ghost of his old pickpocket's grin, "some stories you have to be given by the right storyteller. Otherwise they don't take root."

I looked down at the antler. Four names. My hands had known them for months. Had known, I realized, the exact pressure of each letter, the way the knife wanted to turn at the end of *Wren*, the double stroke in *Mara* that was not in any alphabet I'd learned but fit my grip like it had been waiting for me all along.

Love, I thought, does not live in memory. Memory is just where we keep the paperwork.

* * *

Mara knocked at dawn.

Of course she did. Even stripped of the story, some part of me must have known she would—must have kept the kettle warm and the good chair cleared and the door unlatched, the way you leave a lamp in a window for someone crossing dark country.

She stood on my step in the grey light, taller than I'd imagined, though I hadn't known I'd imagined her at all until that moment. Sixteen now, maybe. Dark-haired. Eyes the color of banked embers—not glowing, not burning, just holding their warmth the way a hearth holds it overnight, patiently, ready for morning.

She didn't speak. Not at first. She looked at me the way you look at a house you grew up in after a long war—checking the roofline, the chimney, whether the light was still on.

Then she raised her hand, and in the air between us, in letters of faint gold that only existed because the covenant let its bridge bend its own rules, she wrote a single word in the First Tongue.

My mind didn't know it. My hands rose to answer it anyway, shaping the sign that went with it, the one my fingers had been practicing on every quiet surface in Hollowmere for months.

Mara's face crumpled and reassembled itself into a smile, and it was the most devastating thing I had ever seen, and I had, apparently, seen it a hundred times before.

"Come in," I said, my voice not entirely steady. "There's tea. There are plums. There's a man downstairs who mouths his own name when he thinks no one's watching, and a priest who cries at bread, and a tree outside that blooms out of season, and I am told—" I gestured at the antler on the table, at the four names gleaming in the morning light, "—that all of them are mine."

Mara stepped inside. She set down her pack. And then, instead of speaking, she did something better.

She picked up a blank length of bone from my scrap pile, took a carving knife, and began, with clumsy, careful strokes, to cut a fifth name into it.

Mine.

The shavings curled and fell. The kettle sang. Outside, Wren's tree scattered blossom across the threshold like a blessing, and somewhere below, two men I did not remember loving climbed the stairs two at a time because they'd heard the door.

I watched the child—the bridge, the key, the girl my sister's face had tried to buy and my sacrifice had set free—carve my name with my own knife, and I understood at last what the dead had been trying to tell me through all those silent bones.

Some things, once given, cannot be taken back. Not by fire, not by gods, not by forgetting.

They can only be handed on.

Mara finished the last stroke, blew the dust away, and looked up at me with ember-warm eyes. Then she opened her mouth, and in the First Tongue—the language that binds, the language that unbinds, the language older than any kingdom—she began, word by true word, to tell me a story.

It was, she would later tell me, a very long one.

It was about a woman who listened to the dead.

And it was, for the first time, entirely mine to hear.
