## 22. Mara and the Ember

The First Tongue has no word for *goodbye*.

I learned this in my third year at the Hearth, when I went looking for one and found instead a word that means *the fire remembers you*, which is not the same thing at all, and which I have come to believe is better. The old speakers of the Tongue understood something we have forgotten: that parting is only a story we tell ourselves about distance. The covenant does not recognize it. Nothing truly leaves the fire. It only changes what it is burning.

I am getting ahead of myself. Beatrix says I do that — says it with her hands, mostly, since her hands have always been more honest than her face. She'll be carving and I'll be talking and she'll hold up a half-finished bone and give me *the look*, the one that means *you've skipped four seasons and a war, child, start again from the beginning.*

So. From the beginning.

* * *

Spring comes to Hollowmere later than it comes to anywhere else in the valley-lands, as if the mountains hoard the cold out of stubbornness, or perhaps affection. I always arrive before the thaw finishes. It's become a ritual: I walk the road down from the pass with my hood back and my breath steaming, and the valley opens below me like a cupped hand, green and grey and smelling of woodsmoke and wet stone, and something in my chest unclenches that I didn't know was clenched.

I was mute for the first eleven years of my life. People assume that silence was a wound. It wasn't. It was a held breath. Somewhere in me, even before I knew what I was, I understood that the first true thing I said would matter enormously, and so I said nothing until I could say everything. When I finally spoke — when I stood in the ruined Hearth with ash raining upward and spoke the word that reforged the covenant between the living world and the Ember Realm — I did not stop speaking after that. Ovid says he has never met anyone who made up for lost time so thoroughly. He says this while covering his ears.

Four years now I have tended the Hearth. Four springs, four summers of ember-light, four autumns of carrying the season's first frost down into the deep places where the old stones still remember being warm. And every winter — every dead season, the season that once nearly killed me on a stranger's doorstep — I come home to Hollowmere.

Home. There's a word the First Tongue also lacks, incidentally. Not because the old speakers had no homes, but because they considered the concept too large for a single word. They used a phrase instead: *the place where your name is kept.* I find this unbearably lovely and I refuse to apologize for it.

The workshop stands rebuilt on the same foundations, though Beatrix let Sef design the additions, which means there are three secret doors, two of which serve no purpose whatsoever. Sef maintains they serve the purpose of *being secret doors*, and honestly, having watched him disappear through one mid-argument and reappear behind his opponent, I concede the point.

She's outside when I crest the hill. Of course she is. She's always outside on the day I'm due; she pretends otherwise, claims to be surprised every single time, and every single time her surprise is betrayed by the fact that there are two cups of tea steeping on the workbench and the good chair has been cleared of bone-dust.

"Mara Winterbourne," she calls up the slope, using the surname she gave me herself, the one that isn't legally anything and is therefore the truest name I own. "You're late."

"The mountain snows were deep," I say, in the common tongue, because we perform this ritual too.

"You're late," she repeats, in the First Tongue, and the words land in my chest like embers finding dry tinder, and I run the rest of the way down the hill like the child I was and the child I will apparently always be, arriving at her door.

* * *

Here is what I do at the Hearth, for those who imagine tending a covenant is dramatic work. Mostly it is sweeping. The Hearth sits in a high mountain fold where three valleys meet, a ring of black stone around a fire that has burned without fuel since the night I lit it — no, not lit it. *Reminded* it. The First Tongue distinguishes between these things the way our tongue distinguishes between striking a match and recognizing an old friend.

The fire needs nothing. That is the whole miracle and the whole difficulty of it. A covenant freely given does not consume. The old Court never understood this — could not understand it, having been built by guardians who mistook their duty for their identity and then mistook their hunger for their duty. They fed the boundary between worlds with memories because memory was the richest fuel they knew. It never occurred to them — or it occurred to them and they killed the thought, the way you kill a thought that would end you — that willingness itself might burn clean. That a gift costs nothing and gives everything.

So my work is not feeding the fire. My work is *witnessing* it. I sit with the flame each morning and I speak to it, in the First Tongue, telling it what has changed in the world it holds together. A child born in Corvane who can now say her grandmother's name without weeping and not knowing why. A ghost crossing a river without toll, because the ferryman's booth stands empty now, its ledger of extracted memories burned in the last days of the Court. A city remembering why it weeps, and discovering the reasons were worth weeping, and weeping anyway, and being glad of it.

The fire listens. I know this because sometimes it answers — not in words, but in images that rise off it like heat-shimmer: a hearth in a farmhouse, a campfire of travelers, a candle in a sickroom, all the small fires of the living world flickering in sympathy. The covenant is not the great ring of stone. It never was. It is every fire anyone has ever kept against the dark, and my job is simply to remind them all that they are one fire.

Wren would have loved this job. Wren, who refused for two hundred years to fade, who followed us out of pure spiteful loyalty and died — finished, completed, *released* — smiling in the rain of returning memories, would have sat with that flame and told it such stories. Sometimes I tell them for her. I tell the fire about a woman who died defending a hearth and then spent two centuries refusing to admit she'd lost, and the flame burns a little taller, the way a person sits up straighter when someone they loved is mentioned.

But this chapter is not about the Hearth. This chapter is about the valley.

* * *

Beatrix's workshop smells the way it has always smelled, which is to say the way safety smells: cedar shavings and lamp oil and tea gone slightly oversteeped because she forgets it when she's working. She has not slowed. She is sixty-eight years old and her hands are steadier than mine, and she still carves memorial bones for the valley's dead, though the bones speak differently now. Before, they whispered the dead's last words — fragments, grief-catches, the unfinished business of unfinished lives. Now they hum. Now, when she sets a finished piece in a grieving family's hands, the bone carries not the last word but the *best* one, the memory the dead themselves would choose to leave behind, drawn up out of them gently by a covenant that houses rather than hungers.

She did not remember designing this. Her hands remembered. That is the sentence I want you to hold onto, the one the whole story turns on: *her hands remembered.*

We drink tea. We perform the ritual complaints — my lateness, her stubbornness about the drafty window, Sef's most recent scheme (he runs messages and moves cargo along the river Vell now, legitimate as sunlight, and complains constantly that honesty is ruining him while quietly becoming the most trusted man on the water). Ovid sends letters full of theological arguments with himself; he is rebuilding the ember-priesthood from scratch, from apology, writing new rites that begin with the words *we were wrong* and go upward from there. He asked me once if penance ever ends. I told him in the First Tongue there is no word for penance either — only a phrase meaning *carrying the fire back to where it came from*. He wept. He pretends he didn't. His letters pretend harder.

And then, as the light goes long and gold through the workshop windows, I do the thing I came to do. The thing I do every visit, and will do every visit for as long as she'll allow it, which I suspect is forever, because Beatrix Winterbourne has never once turned away from a hard truth that arrived politely and meant well.

I tell her the story.

* * *

It takes hours. It has taken hours every time, and it grows longer each year, because the story keeps happening — the story is not over, the story is just the part where I talk. I tell it in the First Tongue, always, for two reasons. The first is practical: the Tongue cannot lie. Spoken truly, each word binds to what is real, and so the story comes out of me shaped exactly as it happened, no softer, no crueler. The second reason is hers. She asked for it that way, that first spring, four years ago, when she sat across from me in a workshop she'd rebuilt from ashes and said, in careful, halting syllables she'd practiced alone for months:

*Tell me what I gave. I want to hear it in the language that can't make it smaller.*

So I begin where the story begins, which is a knock in the dead season. I tell her about a frozen child on her doorstep, and how the dead in her workshop went silent for the first time in thirty years, and how she — this woman who had built a life out of never being needed by anything larger than a grieving neighbor — took that child in without hesitation, because hesitation was simply not among her available responses.

I tell her about the bones. About the rib that whispered *Hearth*, and the symbols in the ash that matched carvings she'd made as a girl without knowing why, her hands moving under instructions her mind had been forbidden to keep. I watch her face as I tell this part. She knows it now, has known it for years, but every time her expression does the same thing: a small flinch, then a settling, like a house accepting weather.

I tell her about the riders on the ash wind, and the burning of the workshop — *your* workshop, I say, the one you built to hide from the world, and you put the torch to it yourself rather than let them have one bone of your dead — and the crossing of the valley's edge, where magic flooded back into her like water into a dried riverbed, and she stood at the boundary line and wept and swore in the same breath.

I tell her about the ferryman. This part I tell slowly, and I watch her hands, not her face. The toll of the river Vell was true memory, and she stood at the rail ready to pay her sister's name — the sister whose face she carried in carved bone, the sister dead forty years, or so the edited shape of her grief insisted — and Ovid caught her wrist and paid in his own coin instead: the memory of his ordination, the single happiest day of his life, spent so that she would not spend the last thing holding her together.

"And then," I say, and here my voice always does the thing voices do, "we found the shrine. And the shrine showed the truth. Your sister did not die, Beatrix. She was taken. Taken by the Court, taken as fuel, and your memory was cut so you would stop searching, and your exile in that valley was built for you like a cage built for a bird that wouldn't stop singing." The First Tongue makes even this gentle; it cannot help it; it binds words to truths and the truth underneath all of it was *they were afraid of you*. "Your whole quiet life was their design. And it failed. Because you heard a knock, and you opened the door."

She is quiet. She is always quiet here. Then she says what she always says, which is: "Go on. You haven't gotten to the good part yet."

The good part. As if there is a good part. As if the whole thing is not one piece, grief and fire and found family braided past separating.

But I go on. I tell her about the Ember Wastes, and the bone with my own name on it that anchored me when the Court called. I tell her about the betrayal at the waystation, and how Ovid's oath flared and he turned on us with smoke in his eyes, and how my hand on his face broke it, and how trust is a bone that heals stronger at the break. I tell her about Wren, and I make it funny first, the way Wren would demand — the ghost who heckled us across three provinces, who called Sef "the thief" for so long it became endearment, who knew the way to the First Hearth because she had died there and considered this a professional credential. Then I make it sad, because Wren deserves both, and the First Tongue holds them in the same breath without contradiction.

And then the Hearth. And then the Court coming down out of the dark sky wearing her sister's face — the Cinder Regent, grown fat on centuries of stolen memory, wearing the face of a woman named Lira Winterbourne who had been burning inside it, aware, imprisoned, for forty years.

I tell her what I offered Mara-that-was: everything I never had, a family, a name, a self, if only I would open the covenant to the Court instead of closing it. And I tell her what I chose. I chose the bridge. I chose to stand between the realms and let the fire pass through me rather than be the fist that held it shut or the door that flung it open. I chose the word. The true word, the one the whole language exists to carry, the one that means *freely given*.

And I tell her the cost. Every time, I tell her the cost, and every time I watch her receive it.

"You stood in the ring of stones," I say, "and you offered the fire everything. Every whisper you'd ever heard from the dead. Thirty years of last words. Every carving, every vigil, every grief you'd kept like coins in a closed fist. And Lira. Your sister. Her face, her voice, the forty years of mourning that had been built on a lie — you gave the true mourning and the false one both, because the fire needed truth and only truth, and you did not hesitate." The Tongue will not let me soften it. "You did not hesitate, Beatrix. I have thought about that every day for four years. They took everything from you and engineered your whole life around your loss, and when the moment came, you gave the loss away *willingly*, and that is the thing the Court could never do, and it is the thing that unraveled them. Their stolen memories tore free and flew home. Their faces melted off them like frost in sun. And the Regent — the Regent came apart, and underneath the ash, for one moment before the wind took her, there was a woman's face, and it was at peace, and it mouthed your name."

The lamp has burned low. Outside, the first stars are out over the valley, and somewhere down in the village a hearth-smoke rises straight in the still air, one thread of the one fire.

Beatrix sets down her tea. She looks at me with the face of a woman hearing a story for the first time — because she is, every time, she remembers none of it, the fire took it all and gave back only this: the world, mended, and people in it who love her — and she smiles.

"That sounds like someone worth knowing," she says.

"She is," I say. "She's impossible. She oversteeps her tea and she lies about being surprised when I visit and she once threatened a god's regent with a carving knife."

"A sensible woman," Beatrix says, wry as ever, and reaches across the workbench, and her hand finds mine, scarred and steady and sure.

* * *

Later, when she's gone to bed, I sit alone in the workshop among the bones and the shavings and the smell of cedar, and I do the last thing. The inheritance.

On the bench lies a rib of pale wood-bone, unfinished, that she set aside for me weeks ago — she plans these things, my Beatrix, she plans surprises she won't remember planning. I pick up the knife. My hands know the grammar of this work now almost as well as my mouth knows the other kind.

And I carve her the story. Not all of it — no bone could hold all of it, and she wouldn't want a relic, she wants a companion. Just the heart of it. A door, opening. A fire, small and stubborn. Five figures on a road: a priest bent under guilt, a thief clutching a borrowed name, a ghost walking backward to watch the horizon, a child with ash on her palms. And at the center, a woman with a knife and steady hands, giving away the only treasure she had left, and the fire taking it gently, the way you take something from someone who is smiling.

When I finish, I breathe on it, the old way, the way the First Tongue teaches, and I speak the phrase that has no single-word translation: *the fire remembers you.*

The bone hums.

In the morning she'll pick it up, this stranger's gift from a stranger's guest, and her fingers will read it the way they read everything, and her mind will know nothing and her hands will know everything, and she will look at me across the workbench with tears she won't understand and say, "Have we met before?"

And I will say, "No. But we're going to."

Because that is the covenant now. That is the covenant I tend and the covenant I am: nothing truly leaves the fire. Not the dead, not the given-away years, not the sister whose name is written in returning rain on a thousand stones across the valley-lands. It only changes what it is burning. And what it is burning, this spring, this morning, in a rebuilt workshop in a green valley, is *this*: a family teaching a woman who she was, one story at a time, forever, freely given.

The kettle is on. The light is coming up over the pass. Somewhere far away, a fire that needs no fuel is keeping every small flame in the world company, and it knows my name, and it knows hers.

I go to make the tea. I make it weak on purpose. Some traditions you honor; others you improve.
