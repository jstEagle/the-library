## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

There is a particular hour at the Guildhall of Impossible Things when the candles burn lowest and the ledgers seem to breathe. I have spent eleven years in that hour. It comes after the last clerk has gone, after the great iron doors have been barred against the city's fog, and it belongs to me and to the small debts.

That was my title, in the way the Guild grants titles the way a miser grants bread: grudgingly, and with an eye toward what you'll owe later. Junior Reconciler of Small Debts. It meant I sat alone in the undercroft with the minor accounts—the widow who'd wished her fence mended and hadn't read the fine print about her hens' dreams, the cooper who'd bought three days of perfect weather and was still paying them off at a shilling a season. Impossible things are expensive. The Guild's genius was realizing that impossible *small* things, sold cheap and often, would keep a city indebted forever.

I liked the work. That's the shameful truth of it. Numbers don't lie to you, they only record how you've been lied to, and there's a cold comfort in that. My father had been a gambler; my mother had died owing an apothecary for a cure that didn't work. I grew up fluent in debt the way other children grow up fluent in prayer. When the Guild found me—fourteen, orphaned, quick with a quill—and offered indenture in exchange for board and instruction, I signed. Of course I signed. Kindness always arrives wearing the clothes of a bargain, and by the time you notice the stitching, you've already worn it home.

So: the undercroft, the eleventh year, the third night of Deepwinter. Rain on the high slit windows like someone tapping to be let in. I was reconciling the account of a midwife who had purchased one impossible thing—a single painless birth for a stranger's daughter—and I remember thinking, as my pen scratched, that the Guild had charged her fairly, all things considered. Eleven years will teach you to think like that. It should frighten you more than it frightened me.

The knock came at the coal chute.

Not the door. Not the bell above the door, which rings a specific chord the Guild has tuned so that no honest visitor can imitate it. The coal chute—a square of blackness at floor level, meant for deliveries no one makes anymore since the furnace was converted to ember-glass. Someone was knocking on it from outside. Politely. Three little raps, like a child asking permission.

I want to say I did not go to it. I want to say eleven years of Guild training held me in my chair with the sensible paralysis of a man who knows that anything arriving uninvited at a guildhall is either trouble or a test, and both are billed to you. But there was something in those raps—their smallness, their patience—that got under my ribs before my mind could object.

I opened the chute.

A child looked up at me out of the dark. Perhaps eight years old, perhaps younger; hunger edits age. She was mud to the eyebrows, mud in her hair, mud packed into the creases of her knuckles as though she'd been made of it and only partly fired into flesh. Her eyes were the color of banked coals—not red, nothing so dramatic, but that deep orange-brown you see when a fire has gone quiet and isn't finished.

"Please," she said, in trade-tongue, and then she said something else, a long spill of syllables that meant nothing to me and yet—

And yet the ledger on my desk turned its own page.

I have thought about that moment many times since, and I still cannot tell you which frightened me more: the child, or the page. The paper moved with a sound like a sigh, settling flat, and the ink—I wrote that account myself, I know my own hand—shivered as if I'd breathed on wet letters.

She said the words again. Not louder. Just again, patient, the way you repeat yourself to someone slow.

The ledger responded. A column of figures rearranged itself, gently, like a sleeper turning over. Where the midwife's balance had stood, new characters surfaced in the margin—characters I had never seen, angular and old, each stroke looking less written than *grown*, the way frost grows on glass.

"No," I told the ledger, because one must establish authority early with insubordinate stationery. "No, we don't do that."

The child watched me with her banked-coal eyes and said, in trade-tongue, "It's answering wrong. I can fix it."

"You can—" I stopped. Looked at the chute behind her, the rain, the alley beyond, black as spilled ink. "Where is whoever came with you?"

"With me?" Genuine puzzlement. As if the idea of accompanying anyone, or being accompanied, were some exotic custom from a far country.

Eleven years in the Guild teaches you many things, most of them regrettable, but chief among them this: never let the impossible thing stand in your doorway where the masters can see it. I did the arithmetic of fear in about half a second—the cost of sheltering her against the cost of what Veyra would make of her if she found the girl first—and I am not proud that the sum came out in the child's favor mostly because I was afraid of Veyra. Pity wears whatever armor it can find.

"In," I said. "Quickly."

She fit under my desk the way small debts fit between large ones—invisibly, if you didn't look too hard. I gave her my supper roll, which I regretted immediately and completely, and pretended I hadn't. She ate it in the careful way of children who have learned that food can be taken back, watching me the whole time as if I might revoke the transaction.

"Mara," she said, when I asked. Then, pointing at the ledger: "It's still wrong."

"It's not wrong, it's balanced."

"It's lying." She said it without heat, the way you'd report the weather. "The numbers are a cage. Underneath, it wants to say something else." And then she spoke again—that language, that First Tongue, though I didn't know to call it that yet—and the ledger *wept*. There is no better word. The ink ran in thin dark lines down the page like a face giving up, and beneath the ruined columns, faint as breath on a mirror, other words rose. Old ones. The angular script, growing now in whole lines.

I snatched the book away from her. Which, I note, was exactly the wrong instinct, and exactly the instinct eleven years had trained into me: protect the asset, secure the account, hide the anomaly. Somewhere in the undercroft above us, a clock the Guild kept for reasons it never explained began striking midnight, and each strike made the candleflame lean toward Mara as if listening.

"Stop talking," I whispered.

"Why?"

"Because everything you say does *that*, and if the masters hear of it, they will do things to you that I cannot write down even in my own head." I heard my own voice and hated it—hushed, urgent, complicit. Already I was spending myself on her. Kindness is a debt with interest; I knew that better than any man alive. Every kindness I had ever extended had come back around with collectors attached. And here I was, opening another account.

Mara considered me from under the desk with those ember eyes, chewing the last of the stolen-from-myself roll, and said, with terrible child-clarity: "You're already kind. You just keep writing it down as something else."

I had no answer for that. I was still assembling one when the bell above the great doors rang its tuned chord—an honest visitor, at midnight, in Deepwinter—and boots crossed the hall overhead, and Veyra's voice came down through the floorboards like water finding cracks.

"*Nystrom.* We know something entered the building. Open the undercroft."

I looked at the coal chute, still ajar, rain blowing through. I looked at the ledger, its pages full of language that predated every contract I'd ever copied. I looked at the mud-streaked child who had spoken a dead tongue and made dead paper live.

Then I blew out the candle, and in the dark, I lied to the Guildmaster of Impossible Things for the first time in eleven years.

"There's nothing down here," I said, "but me and the small debts."

Above me, silence stretched—long, considering, priced. And then, softly, from under my desk, Mara began to speak again, and every ledger in the room turned toward her voice at once.
