## 2. What the Ink Remembers

*Edmund*

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its ledgers the way other institutions kept their sins: locked away, alphabetized, and never spoken of in daylight. I had spent six years among them, cataloguing wonders I was contractually forbidden to believe in. A jar of last winter's thunder. The confession of a man who had traded his shadow for a pension. Receipts.

So when Mara woke on my cot the next morning and said something in that impossible language of hers, I did what any trained scribe would do.

I checked the paperwork.

"Slowly," I told her, dipping a pen. "Again."

She frowned at me—children frown with their whole bodies, as though disappointment were a physical labor—and repeated it. Three syllables, soft at the edges, like water finding the crack in a wall. And there, on the page before me, my own handwriting began to move.

Not smudge. Not bleed. *Move.* The ink lifted from the paper in fine dark threads, gathered itself like startled birds, and settled into her three syllables rendered in a script older than the guild, older than the city, older, I suspected, than paper's better judgment.

I dropped the pen. It rolled off the desk. Neither of us picked it up.

"That's not supposed to happen," I said, with the calm of a man whose entire profession was things not supposed to happen.

Mara looked at the page, then at me, and shrugged the shrug of someone for whom moving ink ranked somewhere below breakfast in the order of alarming phenomena. She pointed at the ledger shelf—the old ones, the covenant folios chained behind glass that even the guildmaster needed two keys and a permission slip to touch—and said something else in the First Tongue.

Every book on the shelf rustled.

I want to be clear about what I felt in that moment. It was not wonder. Wonder is for people whose livelihoods don't depend on the world staying legible. What I felt was the specific cold dread of an accountant watching the numbers start to disagree with each other. Because I had copied those covenant pages once, years ago, as an apprentice exercise. I knew every word of them. And if Mara's voice could rewrite them—

Then everything the guild claimed to preserve was, at best, a rough draft.

"Mara," I said carefully, "how did you learn to speak like that?"

She considered the question with the gravity of a magistrate. Then she touched her own throat, then her chest, then pointed at the hearth where the fire had gone strangely still, listening.

Born knowing, then. Wonderful. The guild would want to dissect the concept, if not the child.

I hid the rewritten page under my coat, told the night clerk I'd be working late on inventory, and prepared to commit what the guild's bylaws called Irregular Custody and what I called keeping a small person alive until I understood why she shouldn't be.

*Mara*

The ink-man—Edmund, he was called, which sounded like a sneeze held politely in—asked too many questions, but he asked them quietly, and quiet was rare. The gray building hummed with men who spoke loudly about small things. Edmund spoke softly about large ones, or maybe he just thought loudly and let his mouth rest.

The words came out of me whether I wanted them or not. They had always been there, coiled under my tongue like a sleeping thing, and since the mud and the rain and the running, the sleeping thing had started turning over in its sleep. When I spoke, the world paid attention. Doors remembered being open. Candles leaned toward me like sunflowers.

Here, in this room full of books, the books listened hardest.

Edmund took down a big old book from behind the glass, handling it like something recently deceased. He opened it to a page near the front and went very still.

"This," he said slowly, "is the founding covenant. It's supposed to say—" he squinted—"it's supposed to say how the human realm and the Ember Court agreed to keep apart. Boundaries. Terms. It's the oldest document we have."

I looked. The page was mostly empty. Not blank the way snow is blank—blank the way a room is empty after the furniture has been carried out. You could see where words had been, faint ghosts of them, like bruises on the vellum.

"It's fading," Edmund whispered. "It's been fading." He turned pages faster. Ghost-bruises everywhere, whole paragraphs evaporated, and here and there—new marks. Marks I recognized. Marks shaped like the sounds under my tongue.

He pulled another book from his desk drawer. His own contract, the one that bound him to the guild until his debt was paid, all forty years of it. He flattened it on the desk and stared.

The script on it was the same as mine. The same as the new marks ghosting through the covenant.

"I signed this," he said. "I remember signing it. It was in Common Script. I read every line—I *always* read every line." His laugh came out wrong, like a hinge that needed oil. "It was never in this language. Or it was, underneath, and they only ever showed me the translation."

He sat down heavily. I climbed onto the chair across from him because sitting seemed contagious.

"They didn't erase the language," he said, mostly to himself. "They've been *harvesting* it. Every scribe who copied these pages, every contract written in the old script—the words didn't die, they moved. Into the paper. Into us." He looked at me, and his eyes were doing something complicated. "You're not speaking a dead language, Mara. You're speaking the part they haven't eaten yet."

I didn't understand all of it. But I understood *eaten*, and I understood the way he said *us*, like the word tasted of iron.

Then the door opened, and the humming of the building changed key.

*Edmund*

Veyra did not knock. Guildmasters treat doors as a formality observed by lesser beings. She entered the way frost enters a window: gradually, totally, and with the implication that spring was cancelled.

"Scribe Nystrom." Her gaze went from me to Mara to the covenant folio lying open on my desk, and something flickered behind her eyes—not surprise. Confirmation. "You've been busy."

"The child wandered in during the storm," I said. "I was assessing her for the registry."

"Assessing." She crossed the room and looked down at the blanking covenant the way a jeweler looks at a flawed stone she intends to recut anyway. "Do you know what you're looking at, Nystrom?"

"A dying document."

"A *molting* one." Veyra turned a page with one gloved finger. "The covenant was never meant to live on vellum. Paper is a cage. Words want a body." She looked at Mara, and her expression did not soften so much as reorganize itself around a new purpose. "Every few centuries, the language chooses an anchor. Someone born with the whole of it under their tongue. The last anchor died four hundred years ago, and the boundary between realms has been thinning ever since. Leaking. Ember-light in our cellars. Our debts in their skies."

"And her?"

"The current anchor." Veyra said it the way one says *the current vintage*. "Which means the covenant can be rewritten while it lives in her. Properly, this time. With terms favorable to the guild." She smiled, and I was reminded that smiles are tools, and some tools are for prying things open. "You will record her, Nystrom. Fully. Every syllable, every inflection. We are preparing her for transfer."

"Transfer," I repeated. The word sat in my mouth like a coin from a country that no longer existed.

"To the Court. As consideration. The old covenant required balance; the new one requires collateral." Veyra was already moving toward the door, already elsewhere in her mind, signing things. "Your pen has done fine work for this guild, scribe. Continue to do it, and your debt may find itself... abbreviated."

The door closed behind her. The candles straightened up slowly, like soldiers after an inspection.

Mara tugged my sleeve. She pointed at herself, then at the door, then made a small walking gesture with two fingers, and raised her eyebrows in question.

"No," I said. "She doesn't mean you'd walk anywhere. She means—" I stopped, because the honest version was obscene. She means they will carry you across the threshold the way one carries payment across a counter, and no one will ask the coin its opinion.

Mara watched my face the way children watch weather, reading the storm before it breaks. Then she said something in the First Tongue, very quietly, and every contract in the room—including mine, including hers, including the guild's charter itself—turned its pages toward her like flowers toward a bad sun.

I understood then that I had exactly one night to decide what my pen was for.

And somewhere below us, faint but unmistakable, the guildhall's hunters were being given their boots.
