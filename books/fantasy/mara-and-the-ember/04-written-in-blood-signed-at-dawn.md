## 4. Written in Blood, Signed at Dawn

The guildhall was burning, and I found I could not bring myself to care.

That is a strange confession for a man who spent eleven years dusting its shelves and licking its ink. But fire has a way of reordering one's priorities, and the fire tonight had been set by hunters carrying guild lanterns—green-flamed things that burned cold and ate memory instead of wood. Veyra wanted the threshold destroyed before dawn. She wanted Mara with it.

Mara stood at the center of the great hall, in the scorched ring where the old covenant had hung for three hundred years. The frame was empty now. The pages had gone blank weeks ago, eaten by her own voice, unmade by the First Tongue they were written to contain. All that remained was the space where words had been—a absence shaped exactly like a promise.

"Edmund." Her voice was small in all that smoke. "I don't know all of it. The Court only gave me half."

"Then we'll write the other half," I said. "That's what scribes are for."

I had a knife. Guild steel, ceremonial, meant for trimming quills and, on occasion, for trimming oaths that grew inconvenient. I opened my left palm along the old callus where the pen had rested for eleven years, and the blood came up dark and obedient, pooling in the lines of my hand like it had been waiting there all along, patient as any debt.

* * *

From Mara's side of the night, the hall looked different. She told me this later, when telling was possible again. She said the burning threshold looked like a mouth, and the mouth was singing. She said every word she spoke rose out of her like sparks off a struck flint, and the air caught them, and the air *answered*.

She said she was not afraid until she saw Edmund's face when he cut his hand—and then she was afraid, because he looked calm, and she had learned by then that calm men were the most dangerous kind.

But his blood hit the stone and did not pool. It moved. It crawled toward the empty frame in thin bright threads, and where it went, the blankness began to remember itself.

"Speak," Edmund said. "Whatever comes. Don't hold it back."

So Mara spoke.

The First Tongue does not sound like language. It sounds like the moment before language—the breath drawn, the name almost chosen. When Mara used it, the hunters' green flames guttered. The ledgers in the burning shelves began to turn their own pages. And the covenant, dead these many weeks, began to grow back through Edmund's blood like grass through a cracked flagstone.

* * *

Veyra came at the hour before dawn, as I knew she would. Masters always arrive precisely when the accounting closes; it is the one law they never break.

She came through the smoke with six hunters behind her, and she stopped at the edge of the covenant's growing light, and I watched her do the arithmetic. Six against two, one of them a child. Easy numbers. Except the numbers were wrong, because the child was speaking, and the speaking was rewriting the room.

"Nystrom," she said. My surname. She hadn't used it in years; I'd been *scribe*, *boy*, *asset*. "You understand what you're doing. You're spending your life on a contract you haven't read."

"I've read everything you've ever given me," I said. "That's rather the point."

"The covenant binds both realms. Reforge it and you bind yourself to it. There is no clause for release. No term of service. You will stand at that threshold until the worlds end or you do."

"I've done the arithmetic," I said. "Eleven years of indenture, and I never once belonged anywhere. This is the first contract anyone's offered me where I get to choose the terms." I held up my bleeding hand. "I find them acceptable."

Something flickered across Veyra's face—not anger. Recognition. The look of a woman seeing an old mistake of her own walking around wearing a younger man's coat.

"Hold him," she said.

The hunters moved, and Mara raised her voice, and the First Tongue filled the hall like water filling a hull.

Here is what I understood in that moment, standing in another man's—or another *age's*—language: the covenant was never a wall between the realms. It was a sentence. A single, enormous sentence, spoken at the beginning of things, and the Ember Court had spoken it, and the human guilds had written it down, and somewhere in the writing-down, the guilds had decided the sentence belonged to them. That they could edit it. That they could erase a word here, a clause there, and pocket what fell out.

Veyra had spent thirty years erasing. And now the sentence was being spoken whole again, in a child's voice, in my blood, and every erased word was coming home to roost.

* * *

Mara says she didn't choose what happened to Veyra. She says the words chose, the way a river chooses—it simply goes where the ground tells it.

The hunters reached the edge of the light and stopped, not from obedience but because the light had become a place, and places have rules. Veyra did not stop. She walked into the covenant's regrowth with her ledger held out before her like a shield, reciting transfer-clauses, ownership-claims, the legal architecture of a theft three centuries deep.

And the First Tongue heard her, and answered in kind.

Every word she had ever erased returned to her. Not as punishment—I believe that, whatever else she was, this was not cruelty. It was bookkeeping. The language balanced its accounts. The blankness she had made was hers to carry, and it settled over her like snow, and she kept reciting even as the recitation emptied her, clause by clause, claim by claim, until the last thing she said was a number—an indenture price, eleven years old, paid in full—and then there was no Veyra at all. Only a ledger falling open on the stones, its pages finally, mercifully, blank.

The hunters set down their lanterns. Green flames died. One of them, a woman with ash in her hair, looked at me and said, quietly, "We were told you'd be the easy one," and I confess I laughed, which surprised us both.

* * *

Dawn came the way it does in stories and almost never in life: exactly on time, and meaning something.

The covenant stood complete in the empty frame—written in my blood, spoken in Mara's voice, holding. Through it I could see the Ember Court on the far side, dim as coals under ash, watching. They did not cheer. Courts don't. But the oldest of them inclined her head, the smallest degree, and I understood it to be the largest gesture their dignity allowed.

Mara turned to me. Her eyes were wet and furious about being wet.

"You're staying," she said. Not a question.

"Somebody has to keep the door," I said. "It's a long sentence. I'm rather good with sentences."

"You could come with me. There's a family—" She stopped, because the Court had found one for her, a widow in the river quarter who took in strays and asked no questions about where they came from, and Mara had seen her in the covenant's turning and chosen her herself. Found, not assigned. "You could be assigned too."

"I could," I said. "But I've spent my whole life being assigned. Let me try belonging somewhere instead. Even if the somewhere is a doorway."

She cried then, briefly and without apology, and then she hugged me hard enough to crack a rib I probably needed anyway, and then she walked through the threshold into the grey-gold morning of the human world, and did not look back, which I counted as the finest thing she ever learned from me.

* * *

They ask me sometimes—the travelers, the lost ones, the occasional guild apprentice who finds the hall rebuilt around its burning heart—what it's like, keeping the threshold.

I tell them the truth. It is quiet work. I sweep. I mend the frame when the words wear thin. I keep a small ledger of my own now, and in it I record only debts forgiven, which take far less ink than debts incurred, and dry faster, and smell better.

Some nights, when the veil thins, I hear a girl's voice on the far side of the door, older now, laughing at something in a language only I would recognize. I do not open the door. That is not my office. But I leave the lamp lit, and I write in the margin of my ledger, in a hand steadier than it has any right to be:

*Still here. Still holding. Still, at last, home.*

And some nights, the margin writes back.
