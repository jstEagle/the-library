## 1. The Valley Where Magic Cannot Follow

The bees did not care that Wren Blackwood was having a crisis.

They went about their golden business in the warm June light, threading in and out of the white clover, and Wren crouched beside the hives with his sleeves rolled to the elbow—then thought better of it and rolled them back down. Old habit. The scars on his hands were pale now, seven years faded, thin silver lines like the ghosts of writing across his knuckles and wrists. But habits of hiding outlive their reasons.

"Mind your own affairs," he told the nearest bee, which was regarding him with what he chose to interpret as judgment. "I'm allowed one good day a decade."

The bee declined to comment and returned to the clover.

Wren had lived in Vell for six years, three months, and eleven days—not that he counted—and in all that time he had perfected the art of being unremarkable. He kept bees. He sold honey at the Saturday market, where Mrs. Tallow complained about the price and bought four jars anyway because his honey was, as she grudgingly admitted, better than anyone else's in the valley. He mended fences. He sat in the corner of the Drowsy Fox on market nights and laughed at other people's jokes without ever quite becoming the subject of one. He was, by every measure that mattered, invisible.

He had worked very hard to become invisible. It had not come naturally. That was the trouble with running away from an apprenticeship to a hedge-witch who'd once been something considerably more than a hedge-witch: you learned that being seen was dangerous, and you learned it so thoroughly that even after the danger passed—if it passed—you still flinched from eyes.

And yet here he was, having arranged to be seen. Deliberately. By a stranger.

He pressed his palms flat against the warm wood of the hive and felt the low thrum of the colony through his skin, and tried not to think about how the thrum felt like something else entirely, something he hadn't let himself feel in years.

* * *

The letters had started in autumn.

It had been Tam Osgood's fault, really. Tam ran the valley's only post office out of the front room of his house, along with a lending shelf of books nobody borrowed and a standing offer of tea that nobody accepted twice, because Tam talked through the entire cup. One gray afternoon Wren had come in for a parcel of wax and found Tam holding up an envelope with the air of a man presenting evidence in a murder trial.

"This came for you," Tam said. "From beyond the pass. Nobody writes to you. I've been your postman six years. You get seed catalogs and a bill from the cooper. This is a letter."

"Perhaps the seed catalog has escalated," Wren said, and took it.

The handwriting on the envelope was slanted and sure, ink the color of deep water. Inside, on paper that smelled faintly of smoke and something sweeter—myrrh, though he wouldn't know that until later—a single page:

*Dear Mr. Blackwood,*

*I hope you will forgive the strangeness of this letter. Your name was given to me by a friend of my family, who says you keep the best bees between here and the mountains. I am trying to learn beekeeping myself, or rather, I am trying to convince my brother to let me try, and he is stubborn as a mule with a library card. I am told you are patient with difficult creatures.*

*Would you tell me about your hives?*

*Yours,*
*Mara*

He should have written back a polite paragraph and been done with it. Any sensible man would have. Instead Wren sat down that same evening at his kitchen table, turned the letter over twice, and wrote four pages. About the hives. About how you never rush a swarm, how you listen for the change in their note when they've decided something, how the queen is less a ruler than a rumor the whole colony agrees to believe in.

She wrote back within the fortnight. Then again. Then, through the winter, nearly every week, her letters crossing the pass with the mail cart while snow sealed the high road and the valley grew quiet and white and safe.

Her letters were nothing like his life. She wrote about cities he'd never seen, books he couldn't find, a garden she described so vividly he could smell it—roses that bloomed in frost, she claimed, and night-blooming silverbells, and an orchard of trees that fruited in winter. She wrote about her family with a strange carefulness, love and distance tangled together like briars. And she asked him questions no one had asked him before. Not *what do you do*, but *what do you notice*. Not *where are you from*, but *where do you go when the valley feels too small*.

He answered honestly, mostly. He left out the parts that mattered—the scars, the reason for them, the border stones at either end of the valley that hummed faintly if you touched them, the way magic slid off this place like rain off oiled canvas. He left out the whole buried country of himself. But he found, over the months, that he was leaving out less. That some part of him, letter by letter, was coming up out of the cellar where he'd stored it and blinking in the light.

In March she had written: *I will be passing near Vell in early summer. It occurs to me that we have been friends for half a year and strangers for all of it. Would you let me buy you a cider at the Drowsy Fox and remedy the second condition?*

He had read that line perhaps forty times. He had read it standing up, sitting down, lying awake at two in the morning with the letter held above his face like a man reading scripture upside down. And every time, the same two voices spoke in him, the way they always had.

The first voice said: *She wants to meet you. You. The actual you, not the honey-seller, not the quiet man in the corner. Go.*

The second voice said: *She has built a picture of you out of ink. When she sees the real thing—the flinching, the hiding, the hands—she'll know she wrote to a fiction. Stay small. Stay unseen. Unseen is safe.*

For six years the second voice had won every argument. Wren had long ago stopped noticing it spoke, the way you stop hearing a clock.

But in March, for the first time, he had written back: *Yes.*

* * *

Now it was the day itself, and the second voice was winning on points.

He had changed his shirt three times, which was absurd, because he owned four shirts and two of them were for funerals. He had swept the cottage floor he had already swept. He had considered, seriously considered, wearing gloves to hide his hands, and then imagined explaining gloves in June, and abandoned the plan with a shudder. In the end he settled on the blue shirt, the least-worn one, and stood before the small mirror above the washstand telling himself he looked like a man and not like a scarecrow that had been promoted.

"You look fine," he told his reflection.

His reflection looked skeptical.

The cottage was tidy in the way of a man who lived alone and liked it that way: herbs drying from the beams, jars of honey ranked on the shelf like soldiers, a kettle, two chairs, one table. Two chairs. He looked at the second chair for a long moment. He had bought it in the spring without letting himself think why, and had used it since as a convenient surface for boots and books and one memorable occasion, a hen. Tonight it would hold a person. The thought made his chest do something complicated.

He spent the afternoon with the bees because the bees were easier. There was no pretending with bees. They told you exactly what they thought, in a language of hum and motion, and they never once asked him about his past. He checked the supers, eased a stuck frame free, and let the work settle him the way it always did—the slow rhythm of it, smoke and wax and sunlight, his hands moving with a competence that needed no words.

It was only when a bee landed on the back of his bare wrist, right across the oldest scar, and simply rested there, wings folded, that he stopped.

The scar tissue was different from ordinary skin. He knew that. The hedge-witch had marked him there herself, in another life, ink and ash worked into lines that had once—before Vell, before the border stones—held power the way a channel holds a river. Here in the valley the marks were just marks. Dead channels. He had told himself for six years that this was a mercy.

The bee walked slowly along the old line, and under its tiny feet, Wren would have sworn—would have sworn on his mother's grave, if he'd known where that was—that something flickered. A warmth. A thread of feeling, faint as a memory of heat, moving under dead skin.

Then the bee lifted off and rejoined its sisters, and his wrist was just a wrist, and the warmth was gone so completely he could believe he'd imagined it.

"Too much sun," he muttered, and pulled his sleeve down anyway.

* * *

By late afternoon he was walking the lane toward the village, and the second voice had regained the lead.

*Turn around,* it said, reasonably. *Send word you're ill. People will understand. People always understand; that's the problem with people, they keep understanding things about you that you'd rather they didn't.* 

But his feet kept going, past the millpond where the ducks conducted their eternal corruption schemes, past the smithy with its smell of coal and iron, into the village square where the Drowsy Fox leaned comfortably against its neighbors like a drunk leaning on friends.

He arrived too early, of course. He sat in the Fox's dim front room with a cider he wasn't drinking and watched the door, and rehearsed opening lines, and discarded them all. *Hello, you're taller than your letters.* No. *I brought you honey.* Desperate. *The weather.* God, no.

The innkeeper's daughter, Bess, refilled his untouched cider and gave him the look women in Vell gave men who were visibly dying of nerves. "Expecting someone?"

"A friend," Wren said. "From beyond the pass."

"Ah." Bess nodded sagely, as though this explained everything, which in a sense it did.

And that was when the peddler came in.

Wren noticed him first as a jingle of harness bells, then as a shape filling the doorway—a broad, weathered man in a coat of many mended colors, dust of five roads on his boots, leading a handcart stacked with pots and ribbons and, improbably, books. The whole room turned to look, because a peddler from beyond the pass was an event in Vell, and events were scarce.

"Good evening, good evening!" the peddler boomed, in the voice of a man who had made his living being heard. "Books! Ribbons! A mirror that tells no lies, which is more than most mirrors can say!" He began dealing his wares onto a table like a gambler laying out a hand, and the room gathered around him the way rooms do, grateful for anything new.

Wren stayed in his corner. Staying in corners was his profession.

But then the peddler, sorting through his stack of books with quick dirty fingers, paused. Pulled one out. Frowned at it, the frown of a man who has carried a thing for years without knowing why. It was a heavy volume bound in green leather gone dark with age, its spine cracked, its title worn to a ghost.

"Now this," the peddler said, half to the room and half to himself, "this I've had since my grandfather's day. Can't read a word of it. Old script, maybe. Cursed, maybe." He grinned. "Any scholars in the house? I'll let it go cheap. Or dear, if any of you are rich."

Someone laughed. Someone else asked to see the mirror. The moment passed, the book went back on the pile, and the peddler moved on to selling ribbons to Bess, who was buying them for reasons everyone politely ignored.

Wren told himself he wasn't looking at the book.

He was looking at the book.

There was something about it—or perhaps about the peddler, or the dust on his boots, or the fact that he had come from beyond the pass on the very day, the exact day, that everything in Wren's carefully small life was scheduled to change. The border stones hummed at the valley's edge. Nothing crossed them easily. Things that came from outside Vell came for reasons.

*Stay small,* said the second voice. *Don't touch it. Don't ask. Invisible men don't buy cursed books.*

Outside, footsteps on the lane. A traveler's stride, unfamiliar, coming toward the inn. His heart slammed once against his ribs like a fist on a door.

Mara was coming. Any moment now, the door would open, and the second chair would stop being furniture.

And the peddler, humming, set the green book out on the table again—right beside the ribbons, right in Wren's eyeline—with its cover facing the room, and Wren felt the bottom drop out of his stomach.

Because through the wear and the shadow, he could read the title after all.

It was in old script, the script he'd been taught in another life, the script he'd sworn never to read again.

And it said: *The Book of Doors.*
