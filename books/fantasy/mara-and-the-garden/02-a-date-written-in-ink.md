## 2. A Date Written in Ink

Wren had swept the doorstep three times, which was two times more than the doorstep required.

He stood back to inspect his work anyway, because his hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking. The cottage at the edge of Hollowbrook was small and crooked and smelled of beeswax and woodsmoke, and he had spent the entire morning pretending it didn't matter how it looked. He had also spent the entire morning rearranging the table by the window, moving the honey jars left, then right, then back to the left again, as though a woman he'd never met would judge him by his jar placement.

She might. He knew nothing about her except her letters.

Mara. Even her handwriting had been strange and lovely—slanted letters like wind-bent grass, ink the color of deep water. She wrote about things no one in Vell wrote about: the taste of snowmelt in high places, the way starlight behaved differently over old stone, the particular grief of leaving somewhere you could never name aloud. Wren had written back about bees. It seemed safer. Bees were honest work; they asked nothing of him but calm hands and a steady heart, and he had both, most days.

*Most days.* His scars ached when the weather turned, and today the weather had turned. He pulled his sleeves down over his wrists out of habit, though there was no one here to see. The marks ran from palm to elbow in pale ridges, the signature of an apprenticeship he'd fled at nineteen, and in seven years of valley life he had shown them to exactly no one. In Vell, magic couldn't cross the border stones. Whatever the Order had written into him stayed dormant, quiet, a banked fire under wet ash. He intended to keep it that way until he died of respectable old age surrounded by hives and unanswered questions.

The sun leaned toward evening. She would arrive on the dusk coach from Millford, if she arrived at all. People said they would come and then did not; Wren had learned not to bet his heart on coach schedules.

He lit the lamps anyway. Both of them. Then, feeling ridiculous, he blew one out. Then, feeling lonelier, relit it.

* * *

The coach came down the valley road at half past six, groaning like an old man climbing stairs, and Wren's stomach performed a maneuver usually reserved for falling off roofs.

And she stepped down.

He had imagined her a hundred ways and been wrong in all of them. She was tall, with dark hair escaping its braid in defiance of several pins, and she stood on the road a moment looking up at the border stones on the far ridge as though checking for something. Then she saw him in the doorway, and her whole face changed—not the polite smile strangers give each other, but something startled and glad, like someone finding a door where they'd expected a wall.

"You're taller than your letters," she said, coming up the path.

"You're real," Wren said, and immediately wished to be struck by lightning. "I mean—I'd wondered. Whether you were. Real. Since we've only—" He stopped. "Cider? I have cider."

"Please," Mara said gravely, "before you injure yourself."

They sat at the table by the window, both lamps lit after all, and the terror of it faded faster than he'd dared hope. She laughed easily. She asked about the bees and actually listened to the answer, and when he told her about the hive that had swarmed into Mrs. Tallow's chimney last spring, she laughed so hard she had to press her sleeve to her mouth. She had a scar too—a thin one along her jaw—and she didn't hide it, which made him feel, absurdly, less alone in his own skin.

"I should tell you," she said, halfway through her second cup, "that I almost didn't come. I read your letters so many times I wore soft spots in the paper. And I thought—if he knew what I really—"

The door banged open.

A peddler stood there, round as a barrel, wrapped in a coat that had clearly survived several wars and one house fire. Snow dusted his shoulders despite the mild evening, which Wren noted distantly and failed to note properly, which was unlike him.

"Begging pardon!" the peddler boomed. "Saw the light! Cold night for traveling, warm night for buying! Books, missus—books, master! Old ones, good ones, cheap as sin and twice as entertaining!"

"We're—" Wren began.

"I'll see them," said Mara.

Wren looked at her. She was watching the peddler with an expression he couldn't read—something taut beneath the pleasantness, like a bowstring dressed in lace.

The peddler unloaded his pack onto their table without asking, right on top of the honey jars, and spread out perhaps twenty books in various states of ruin. Most were sermons or farm almanacs. One was a child's primer with a woodcut of a bear wearing trousers. And one—

One was bound in grey leather gone soft as cloth, its spine cracked, its pages the color of old cream. No title. Wren's hands moved toward it before his mind agreed to anything, and the moment his fingers touched the cover, his scars woke.

Not burned. Not yet. But *warm*, the way a hearthstone stays warm hours after the fire dies. He snatched his hand back.

"That one's strange," the peddler said cheerfully. "Found it in a dead man's attic up past the stones. Can't read half of it myself. Prophecies and such—rubbish, mostly, but rubbish sells."

"How much?" Mara asked quietly.

"Mara," Wren said, "it's rubbish."

"Everything true starts as rubbish," she said, and paid the man before Wren could argue, pressing coins into his fat palm while he beamed and repacked his wares and let himself out into the dark, whistling something that Wren would spend years trying to forget.

* * *

The book fell open in Mara's hands as though it had been waiting to do exactly that.

She went still. Wren watched her stillness spread across her face like frost across a window, and his own cup paused halfway to his mouth.

"What is it?" he asked. "What does it say?"

She didn't answer. She turned the book around and set it in front of him, and pointed with one finger that was not entirely steady.

The script was old-fashioned but legible, cramped and certain, the hand of someone writing fast because the words were arriving faster:

*A stranger comes to the keeper's door at dusk, by the coach from Millford, and he will light two lamps and put out one and light it again, for he has always been afraid of being seen.*

Wren's mouth went dry.

*She will laugh at the bees in the chimney, and press her sleeve to her mouth, and he will think: I am less alone in my own skin.*

*There will be a peddler at the door with snow on his shoulders, though no snow has fallen.*

*And she will buy the grey book, and the cider will spill.*

The last line landed a heartbeat before the cup did. Wren knocked it with his elbow—it was his elbow, he would swear to it forever, some traitorous part of his body obeying the text rather than his will—and cider sheeted across the table, over the prophecy, soaking the page so the ink bloomed and swam.

*And the cider will spill.*

"It's a trick," Wren heard himself say. His voice came out very small. "It's a joke. Someone wrote this recently. Someone watched me—the peddler, he watched the house, he—"

"Wren." Mara's voice was gentle and terrible. "Look at the date."

Beneath the final line, in smaller script, was a notation. Not a year in any calendar Wren knew—but Mara clearly knew it, because when she translated it for him, her voice shook.

"A hundred and four years ago," she said. "This was written a hundred and four years before tonight."

The lamp flames bent, though there was no draft. Outside, the bees in their hives had gone silent—all of them, every hive, at once, the way they went silent before a storm or a death.

Wren looked at the page, at his own evening lying there in ink older than his grandfather, at the spilled cider soaking through a hundred years of patience. Wonder rose in him first—that was the cruel part. Wonder, bright and childish: *someone wrote me down, someone saw me coming, I was always going to matter.* For one shining moment he felt like a character instead of a coward, like a man whose life had a plot.

Then the wonder curdled.

Because if tonight was written, then everything was written. Every careful smallness, every year of hiding, every time he'd chosen the safe path and called it wisdom—it hadn't been choice at all. It had been a script, and he had delivered his lines faithfully, and the author had known he would. Knew he'd light two lamps and blow one out. Knew he'd knock the cup. Knew him better than he knew himself, this stranger a century dead, and Wren felt suddenly naked in a way no invisibility had ever protected him from.

"I wanted to be unseen," he whispered. "That was the whole point. That was the *whole point*, Mara. You build a life where nothing can find you, and then you find out you were found before you were born."

Mara reached across the ruined table and took his scarred hand—his bare hand, sleeves shoved up in the chaos, marks exposed in lamplight—and she didn't flinch, didn't ask, just held it like it was a hand.

"There's more," she said.

"Don't read it."

"There's more," she repeated, "and I think you need to hear it, because it isn't only about tonight." She turned the page carefully, peeling the wet vellum back, and her breath caught. "Wren. There are chapters. Whole chapters. About a doorkeeper. About a country behind the winter. About—" She looked up at him, and in the lamplight her eyes were frightened and fierce at once. "About you. It names you. Not your name—the *title*. 'The doorkeeper, born on both sides of the stones, who will open what was closed.'"

Outside, far off on the ridge, something howled. Not a wolf. Wolves sounded hungry; this sounded *cold*.

And beneath Wren's skin, under seven years of wet ash, something answered it—something that stretched, slow and delighted, like a cat waking by a fire, and his scars warmed from wrist to elbow, and he understood with perfect clarity that the valley's peace had never been peace at all.

It had been a held breath. And tonight, something had begun to let it out.

"Mara," he said, staring at his glowing wrists, at the prophecy, at the woman the century had sent to his door, "what exactly did you come here to find?"
