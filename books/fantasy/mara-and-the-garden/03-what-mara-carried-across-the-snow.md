## 3. What Mara Carried Across the Snow

I had rehearsed the confession forty times on the road north.

In the first version, I was dignified. I stood by the window of whatever inn had taken my coin, silhouetted against the snow, and said something like, *Wren, there is much you do not know about me.* In the second version, I wept gracefully. In the third, I did not confess at all but simply vanished before dawn, leaving a letter and the seed, which was cowardly and also the version I liked best, because it required no courage whatsoever.

None of the versions accounted for the cider.

The cider was not in any plan. The cider happened because Wren Blackwood laughed — really laughed, startled out of him like a bird from a hedge — when I told him about the goat who had eaten my only good hat somewhere outside Marrow Fen, and his elbow caught the table, and the mug went over, and we both lunged for it and knocked our heads together instead, and suddenly there was cider everywhere and a lump rising on my brow and Wren apologizing to me, to the table, to the mug, and to the inn at large.

And I thought, with a sinking feeling that had nothing to do with cider: *the book said this would happen.*

*The cup will spill,* the peddler's prophecy had read, in ink gone brown as old blood. *And she will know, then, that she has led him to it.*

So there it was. I had led him to it.

* * *

He mopped up the mess with his sleeve, still stammering apologies, and I watched his hands — those scarred, careful hands — and felt the guilt settle onto me like the snow settling onto Vell: soft, silent, and deep enough to bury a person.

Because here is what I had not told him. Here is what I had carried across three hundred miles of frozen country, wrapped in oilcloth against my heart:

Aldermere is real.

They say here, in the valley and beyond it, that Aldermere is a story — a country swallowed by the long winter a hundred years ago, a fable grandmothers tell, a name on no map. And they are right that it is lost. They are wrong that it is gone. Aldermere endures under the winter, gray and starving and stubborn, lit by hoarded candles, ruled by an Order that came out of the cold one year and never left. And its last princess walked out of it through a door that should not have opened, carrying a seed and a prophecy and a lie she told herself every night: *I am not using him. I am saving everyone. Those are different things.*

Wren set a fresh mug in front of me. "You've gone somewhere," he said. "Your eyes do this thing. Like you're reading a page just behind my head."

"You'd laugh if I told you where."

"Try me. I've been laughed at plenty; I'm owed some reciprocity."

I looked at him — this beekeeper with the burned hands and the careful voice, who had written me letters for eight months, letters so funny and so lonely that I had fallen in love with them before I ever fell in love with him, though I had told myself those were different things too — and I understood that the dignified version was dead, and the vanishing version was dead, and all that remained was the truth, which I had been carrying even longer than the seed.

"Wren," I said. "Do you believe in Aldermere?"

His face did something complicated. "It's a story."

"It's a country. It's *my* country." My voice came out lower than I meant it to. "And it is dying under a hundred years of winter, and I am the last person who walked out of it alive, and I came here because of you."

The inn went quiet around us, or perhaps it only seemed to. Somewhere a fire popped. A dog sighed.

"Me," Wren said carefully, "specifically?"

"Specifically."

"Not, say, the valley generally? Because I should tell you, the valley's lovely but the rent—"

"*Wren.*"

"I'm sorry." He pressed both scarred hands flat on the table, as if steadying himself against weather. "I'm sorry. Go on. I'm listening. I'm — go on."

* * *

So I told him.

I told him about the winter that came the year my grandmother was a girl, and how it never lifted — how spring forgot the road to Aldermere entirely, and the rivers froze to their beds, and the Order arrived wearing white and calling itself salvation. I told him about the border stones, the ring of standing rocks that marked the edge of the valley of Vell, and how magic could not cross them — how the Order had built that silence deliberately, a prison shaped like a peace, and how anyone with power in their blood who reached the valley found it simply... gone. Quiet. Safe.

"That's why you're here," Wren said slowly. "That's why you ran *here*. Not away from something. Toward the absence of it."

"The only place in the world the Order can't follow," I said. "Or couldn't. We'll come back to that word."

"And the letters?" His voice cracked on it, just slightly, and I hated myself for the crack. "Was that part of it? Finding me?"

Here is the truth, and I will set it down plainly because I have lied enough in this story: the letters were the mission. The Order kept records, even in exile, even across the stones. There was a boy born in Aldermere's shadow-country, born on the wrong side of a door during the last crossing, raised in the valley where his borrowed magic could not wake. A doorkeeper. The prophecies were old and half-mad, but they agreed on one thing: when the way home opened again, it would open for him, and only him.

I was sent to find him. I found him. And then he wrote to me about bees.

"I answered the first letter because it was duty," I said. "I answered the second because your handwriting slants like it's trying to escape the page, and it made me laugh. By the fourth letter I'd stopped telling myself it was duty. By the eighth—" I stopped. Started again. "By the eighth letter I would have crossed the winter for you with no prophecy at all."

He looked at me for a long moment. Outside, the light was going blue and gold the way it does in Vell in late autumn, honey-colored and ending soon.

"The spilled cup," he said at last. "The peddler's book. It knew. It knew before either of us did."

"Yes."

"So my whole life—" He laughed, but it wasn't a happy sound; it was the sound of a man finding out the floor is thinner than he thought. "I spent ten years making myself small so nobody would ever look twice at me. And the entire time, I was a line in a book."

"A line in a very old book," I said. "Which means you matter, Wren. Which means the way home might actually open. Do you understand what that—" I caught myself, heard the mission in my own voice, and made myself stop. Made myself say the other thing, the true thing, the thing I'd been strangling since the cider hit the table. "But I need you to hear this part more than any of it. If you tell me no — if you want to keep your bees and your valley and your invisible life — then I will burn the prophecy myself and find another way home or die in the snow trying, and you will never be asked again. You are not a door, Wren. You're a person. I have to keep remembering that, because everyone else in this story has forgotten it, including me, most days."

* * *

He didn't answer right away. Instead he asked, quietly, "What did you carry? Across the snow. You said — you said you came because of me, but you said it like there was something in your arms the whole way."

My hand went to my collar without my permission. Of course it did. Some truths announce themselves.

I drew it out from beneath my shirt: the oilcloth packet, worn soft from three hundred miles against my skin. Inside it, the seed.

It was the size of a plum and the color of dawn — not any single color of dawn, but all of them, shifting, warm. Warm. That was the impossible part, the part that had kept me alive on the worst nights of the crossing, when I lay in drifts and told myself: *it is still warm, therefore Aldermere is not yet dead, therefore get up.* It was cut from the mother tree in the royal garden the night I fled, from the last living branch in the last living garden in a country where nothing else had grown in a hundred years. The gardeners — what remained of them — had pressed it into my hands and said, *Take the spring with you. Find the doorkeeper. Bring back the summer.*

I set it on the table between us, on the cider-damp wood, and unwrapped the cloth.

Wren leaned over it, and I watched his face as the warmth reached him — watched him feel it, the way you feel a candle's heat from a foot away. His breath caught.

"It's alive," he whispered.

"It's Aldermere," I said. "All that's left of it that isn't people. It hasn't cooled once. Not once, in all the miles." I turned it gently with one finger, and the colors turned with it, rose and gold and green, impossibly green. "Every night on the road I held it and thought: this is what I'm for. Not the crown. Not the blood. This. Getting one small piece of spring out of the dark."

He looked up at me then, and his eyes were wet, and he didn't wipe them, and I loved him so much in that moment that the guilt and the love finally stopped fighting each other and simply sat down together at the table like two old friends sharing a meal.

"Mara," he said. "I don't know anything about prophecies or doors or countries under snow. But I know that whatever this needs — whoever it needs — I'll help. I don't know what that costs yet. But I'll help."

And that was when I saw it, over his shoulder, through the frost-feathered window at the edge of the lamplight.

Down the lane, at the far bend where the road left the village, the snow had begun to fall differently. Not falling. *Hanging.* Suspended in the air like a held breath, glittering white, in a long slow line that stretched away toward the border stones.

Moving. Coming closer.

"Wren," I said, and my voice came out flat and cold as the thing outside. "How attached are you to helping *right now*?"
