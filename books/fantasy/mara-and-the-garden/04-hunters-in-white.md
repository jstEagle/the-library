## 4. Hunters in White

The morning after the prophecy, Wren did the only sensible thing: he went to work.

Bees, at least, had never been written about in any book. He was fairly sure of it. He'd checked the peddler's volume twice before Mara woke—once by candlelight and once with his heart hammering so hard he could barely hold the pages—and there was nothing in it about hives or honey or a man in Vell who smelled of smoke and clover. The bees were his. Unscripted. His own small country of six-legged citizens who asked nothing of him except steadiness.

"Steady," he told them, and meant it as advice to himself.

The hive at the far end of the row had gone quiet overnight. Not dead-quiet—the kind of quiet that comes when something has frightened them. He crouched beside it, bare hands despite the cold, because gloves made him clumsy and the bees knew his hands better than his face. The hum inside rose to meet him, thin and uneasy, like a song sung through chattering teeth.

"That's it," he murmured. "I know. Something's wrong. I felt it too."

He had felt it since dawn, actually—a pressure behind his breastbone, faint and wrong, like weather coming from the wrong direction. He'd blamed the cider. He'd blamed the prophecy. He'd blamed, briefly and unfairly, the peddler. But standing there with his palms flat against the hive's warm wood, he let himself admit what the feeling actually was.

It was the border stones.

Everyone in Vell learned them as children the way you learn not to touch a stove: the gray standing stones ringed the valley, mossy and unremarkable, and magic could not cross them. Couldn't. It pooled against them like rain against a window. That was the whole reason Wren had run here eleven years ago, half-starved, his hands wrapped in rags to hide the scars the Order's training had burned into him. Inside the stones, his magic couldn't follow. He'd spent a decade being gloriously, blessedly ordinary.

And now the ordinary was leaking.

"Wren?" Mara's voice came from the cottage door, sleep-rough and careful. She'd slept on the settle—he'd insisted, with the gallantry of a man terrified—and she emerged now wearing his spare coat, which fit her like a tent and somehow still looked elegant. "You're talking to bees again."

"It's a conversation," he said. "They're very opinionated."

"What are they saying?"

He hesitated. Lying to her had gotten complicated since the book. "That something's coming."

* * *

They walked the valley's edge together, which Wren would have called romantic under almost any other circumstances. Frost silvered the pasture grass. The hills beyond stood white and silent, and the border stones rose out of them at intervals, old and patient as teeth.

At the third stone, Mara stopped walking.

"What is that?" she whispered.

Wren looked. The stone was sweating. There was no other word for it—beads of moisture crawling down its face despite the hard frost, except the moisture wasn't water. It caught the light wrong, too bright, too sharp, and where it dripped onto the ground the grass had frozen into little spikes, like the earth itself had grown afraid.

"It's never done that," he said. "In eleven years, it's never—"

The stone cracked.

Not loudly. A sound like a knuckle popping, deep in the rock, and then a hairline fracture ran from crown to base, and through the fracture came air that was not valley air. It was the cold of high places, of long nights, of somewhere that had forgotten spring on purpose. Wren's breath stopped. Beside him, Mara made a small sound he'd only heard once before—in the tavern, when she'd read her own name in a century-old book.

"They're failing," she said. "The stones are failing."

"Why now? They've held for a hundred years."

"Because they were built to hold against magic." Her eyes stayed fixed on the crack, and her voice went flat in the way it did when she was reciting something she'd rather not know. "Not against *him*. The winter isn't magic, Wren. It's older than the Order's rules. It doesn't knock. It seeps."

Somewhere up-slope, in the white silence of the hills, something moved.

It might have been snow shifting off a branch. It might have been a heron taking flight. But herons didn't move in straight lines, and snow didn't move *toward* you, and whatever it was crossed the ridge with the unhurried confidence of something that had never once needed to hurry.

"Mara," Wren said quietly. "Inside. Now."

"Wren—"

"*Now.*"

She went. He watched her go—all the way to the cottage door, all the way until the latch clicked—because some stubborn farmer's instinct in him refused to send anyone into a house while he stood watching the hills alone. Then the figures crested the rise, and every instinct he owned changed its mind entirely.

There were three of them, and they were wrong in the way that dreams are wrong. They wore white—not cloth-white but frost-white, coats that seemed grown rather than sewn, rimed at the shoulders, trailing breath that hung in the air long after they exhaled. Their faces were human-shaped and mostly human-colored, but their eyes were pale as skimmed milk, and where their boots touched the ground the frost spread outward in fern-patterns, delicate and total.

Frost-bound. He'd heard the word in the Order's halls when he was a boy, spoken in the hushed tone reserved for punishments and saints. Hunters who had given their warmth to the founder and received purpose in exchange. He'd thought they were a story adults told to keep apprentices obedient.

One of them raised a hand, and the air between them and the valley *bent*, and Wren understood with animal clarity that the stone hadn't cracked on its own. They had been working at it for hours. Maybe days. Patient as winter.

"Hallo," Wren called, because terror had always made him polite. "This is private land."

The middle hunter tilted its head. When it spoke, its voice was beautiful, which was somehow worse than if it had rasped. "Wren Blackwood. Apprentice Third Circle, fled the year of the red comet. You have something we want."

"I really don't," he said. "I've got bees and a leaky roof. Take either."

"You have the doorkeeper's blood." The hunter took a step down the slope. The frost ran ahead of its feet like a dog off its lead. "And you are harboring the runaway princess of Aldermere. Surrender her, and the Order will forget your desertion. There is a place for you. There has always been a place for you."

And here was the terrible thing, the thing Wren would lie awake examining for years afterward: for one whole heartbeat, he *wanted* it. A place. Recognition. To walk back through those doors not as a failure dragged home in chains but as someone sought after, named, *wanted*. Eleven years of being nobody pressed up against that offer like a cold man pressing against a lit window.

Then he thought of Mara asleep on his settle in his ridiculous coat, and the wanting curdled into something much more useful.

"No," he said.

The hunters moved.

They didn't run—they *glided*, horribly, the frost carrying them, and the world went slow and silver at the edges. Wren had time to think, absurdly, *the bees*, and then the lead hunter raised its hand and the cold came down the slope like a wave breaking, and every blade of grass it touched turned to glass.

Wren threw out his arms.

He didn't decide to. There was no decision in it, no plan, no clever hedge-witch trickery. His body simply moved the way it had moved once, long ago, in a training hall when another student's spell went wild—an instinct beaten into him so deep that eleven years of ordinary living hadn't reached it. He stepped between the cold and the cottage, spread his scarred hands, and said, in a voice that wasn't quite his own:

"*No.*"

The word hit the frost-wave like a bell struck with a hammer.

Light broke over him—not gentle light, not candlelight, but the white-hot glare of power with nowhere left to hide, pouring up out of the buried places in him like water finding a crack in a dam. It ran along his scars and *ignited* them. He felt every mark the Order had ever burned into his skin light up in sequence, wrist to elbow to shoulder, a ladder of fire, and the pain was enormous and familiar and—gods forgive him—

*Sweet.*

That was the part that frightened him most. Not the power. The sweetness. It felt like coming home to a house he'd sworn he'd never return to, like a key turning in a lock he'd thrown away, like being *seen*, finally, wholly, by something that approved of him. The cold shattered against the shield he hadn't known he was holding and sprayed outward as glittering dust, and the hunters stopped, and for a moment the three of them simply regarded him across the smoking grass with those milk-pale eyes, and he could feel them *reassessing*.

"There he is," the middle one said softly. Almost tenderly. "The founder will be pleased."

"Don't—" Wren started, and his voice came out doubled, layered, his own voice with something vast underneath it like a river under ice, and the sound of it scared him so badly he clamped his mouth shut.

The hunters withdrew. Not fled—winter didn't flee—but they flowed backward up the slope, unhurried again, and the last thing the middle one said drifted down to him on air that was already warming:

"We do not need to take you today, doorkeeper. The stones are dying. We need only wait. Everything freezes, given time."

Then the hill was empty, and the frost-ferns on the grass began, slowly, to melt.

* * *

Mara found him on his knees in the pasture.

He didn't remember falling. His hands were in his lap, and they were glowing—faintly, guttering, like coals remembering fire—and the scars stood out livid and new, fresh-burned over old burns, eleven years of healing undone in a single word. It hurt less than he expected. That was the worst part. It hurt like a *relief*.

"Wren." She dropped beside him, took his face in her cold hands, made him look at her. "Wren, look at me. What did you do?"

"I don't know." His voice shook. "I said no. I just—I said no, and it *answered*."

"Your magic. You said it couldn't cross the stones. You said—"

"So did everyone." He laughed, and it came out wrong, giddy and ragged at the edges. "Eleven years, Mara. Eleven years I built a life on the idea that I was safe from it, that it was safe from me, that I could just be a beekeeper with bad hands. And it was *here* the whole time. Under the floorboards. Asleep." He looked down at his burning palms. "And it liked waking up. That's the thing I can't—Mara, it *liked* it. And so did I."

She was quiet for a moment. Snow had begun to fall, fine and dry, though the sky above the valley was clear—it was falling only on them, only on this field, as if the hills were already composing their report.

"When I was a girl in Aldermere," she said slowly, "before the winter closed in, my grandmother told me that the strongest wards aren't walls. They're locks. And a lock doesn't destroy what it holds. It just... keeps it. Keeps it safe. Keeps it *ready*." Her thumb traced one of his scars, feather-light, and the burn beneath it pulsed like a heartbeat. "Your teacher didn't bury your magic, Wren. I don't think anyone could. I think she hid it somewhere even you couldn't find it."

"The valley," he whispered. "The stones. The whole valley is—"

"A box," Mara said. "And I think somebody just heard you knocking."

Far away, at the top of the white hill, the border stone sang—one clear, cracking note, like ice on a pond beginning to give way.

Wren got to his feet. His hands still glowed. He looked at them for a long moment, at the sweet warm terrible light of the thing he'd spent half his life running from, and then he looked east, toward the hedgerow lanes and the crooked cottage where a hedge-witch named Old Nan Sallow had once hidden a frightened boy behind a wall of ordinary things.

"She'll know what to do," he said. "She always did. And I think—" his voice caught, "—I think she always knew this day was coming. She told me once, the last time I saw her: *when your hands start singing, boy, come find me before they find you.*"

"Then we go tonight," Mara said.

"No," Wren said, watching the snow fall upward off the melting grass, defying the wind, drifting toward the hills like letters being returned to their sender. "We go now. While my hands still remember how to be quiet."
