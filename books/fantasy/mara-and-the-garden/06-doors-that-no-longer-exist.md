## 6. Doors That No Longer Exist

The key made of thorn and glass did not point. It *itched*.

Wren had expected—hoped, really—that it would behave like a compass needle, swinging dutifully north, saving them days of argument about maps. Instead it sat in his palm like a splinter of winter itself, cold as a held breath, and every few minutes it prickled against his skin, sharper or softer, depending on which way he turned.

"It likes the east road," Mara said, watching his face rather than the key. She had gotten good at reading him in the weeks since the peddler's book had upended his life. He wasn't sure how he felt about that. Being read was dangerously close to being seen.

"The east road goes through Hollowmere," Wren said. "Hollowmere has a miller who still owes me for three jars of honey and a very personal conversation about his wife."

"Then we'll buy honey and mind our own business."

"That's not how conversations with Bram work. He's a man who treats silence as a debt to be paid with interest."

Mara laughed—the real laugh, the one that crinkled her eyes and made something in Wren's chest go loose and warm and terrified all at once. He was beginning to suspect he'd do anything to keep hearing it, which was exactly the sort of weakness that got hedge-witches' apprentices killed.

They took the east road.

* * *

The key led them off the road by midmorning, into a wood so old the trees seemed embarrassed by the notion of fences. Beech mostly, silver-trunked and patient, with a floor of copper leaves that hushed under their boots like a crowd settling before a story. The prickling in Wren's palm grew steadier, then urgent, then insistent, until he was walking with his hand half-raised like a man dowsing for water.

"She said thorn and glass would know its own door," Mara murmured. "Her exact words were 'it'll drag you by the wrist if it has to.'"

"I'm choosing to follow it," Wren said. "There's a difference."

"There is," Mara agreed gravely. "It's called dignity."

He was about to reply with something witty—he'd been composing it for a full minute, and it involved bees—when the key went hot through the glove he'd taken to wearing, and stopped.

The oak stood alone in a clearing where no other tree would grow. It was vast in the way of things that had been old when the border stones were young: hollow-hearted, lightning-scarred, its great limbs bare even though it was barely autumn. And in the middle of its trunk, where a knot should have been, there was a shape.

A door. Small, human-sized, set into the living wood as if the tree had grown around it for centuries the way an oyster grows around grit. There was no handle. There was no hinge. There was only a keyhole, narrow and vertical, shaped—for Wren's stomach dropped as he saw it—exactly like the key in his hand.

"Well," Mara said softly. "That's not ominous at all."

"Mara."

"Yes?"

"If this is the door to Aldermere, and Aldermere is on the other side of the border stones—" He stopped. Started again. "Magic can't cross the stones. Nothing crosses the stones. That's the whole point of them. So what's *through* there?"

She didn't answer right away. When she did, her voice had gone quiet in the way it did when she spoke of home. "Winter," she said. "And everything the winter hasn't managed to kill yet."

* * *

The key fit the way keys fit in dreams: perfectly, and with a click that sounded less like a lock turning than like a decision being made.

Nothing happened.

Wren tried pulling. The door didn't have the decency to rattle. He tried pushing, which felt foolish immediately but he committed to it anyway, shoulder against the oak, breath fogging in air that had gone suddenly, impossibly cold. Frost feathered out from the keyhole across the door's surface, delicate as lace, and stopped at the edges of the wood as though respecting a boundary.

"It's not locked," Wren said. His voice came out strange. "I mean—it is, but it's not the lock you're thinking of. I can feel it." He could. Something behind the door was pressing against the other side of the keyhole, patient and enormous, the way deep water presses against a dam. "It's not asking for a key. It's asking for me."

"What do you mean, *you*?"

"My blood built the prison. Her words." He flexed his scarred fingers. Under the glove, the old marks from his apprenticeship—the ones he'd spent seven years hiding under honey-smoke and long sleeves—had begun to ache in a low, familiar register. "I think it wants the whole inheritance. Not just the blood. The magic too."

Mara stepped closer. Close enough that he could see the frost-light reflected in her eyes, close enough that backing out was going to require actual courage instead of mere cowardice. "Wren. Whatever it's offering—you don't have to take it."

"You don't know what it's offering."

"No," she admitted. "But I know you. And I know that look. You get it when Bram talks about his wife and you pretend you don't want what they have."

"That's—completely different—"

"Wren." She took his gloved hand, scars and all, and held it between both of hers. "Whatever happens next, I need you to hear this. You walked into a valley where magic couldn't follow because you wanted a life that was *yours*. Small and warm and chosen. If the price of this door is giving that up—if the price is becoming whatever the Order trained you to be—then we find another way. There's always another way. We've got a seed that's still warm and a witch who taught you stubbornness, and I refuse—" her voice cracked, just slightly, "—I *refuse* to let a tree bully you into anything."

For one shining moment, Wren believed her.

Then he pulled off the glove.

* * *

The magic had been waiting. Of course it had. Seven years of burying a thing alive only works if the thing cooperates, and his had never once cooperated; it had simply grown patient, the way roots grow patient under pavement.

It rose through him like water finding an old channel. The scars on his hands lit—not painfully, not at first—with a pale gold light, and Wren gasped, because oh, he had forgotten. He had *made himself* forget. The sweetness of it. The rightness. Every spell he'd ever fumbled as a boy, every cantrip the Order's masters had sneered at, came back to him whole and effortless, and underneath it all, quiet as a voice through a wall, something spoke.

*You could have this always.*

The door's frost spread. Not toward the hinges—there were no hinges—but outward, up the oak's scarred trunk, silvering every branch, and through the narrowing gap of the keyhole Wren could see snow falling on the far side, slow and endless, and beyond the snow, faint as memory, the towers of a city he had never seen and somehow recognized.

*You could open this with a thought. You could open every door. The valley kept you small, little keeper. The valley was never your home—it was your cage with better manners.*

"That's not true," Wren whispered.

*Isn't it? Seven years of hiding your hands. Seven years of being nobody. Here, you would be the doorkeeper in truth. They would write prophecies about you—and they already have. You've read the book. Your name is in it. Doesn't some part of you want to walk through the world and be* known?

And here was the terrible thing, the thing he would carry like a stone for the rest of his life: some part of him did. Some part of him stood in that cold clearing and wanted it with a hunger that made him dizzy—to be powerful, to be named, to never again flinch when someone looked too long at his hands. The whisper knew that. The whisper had been built, he suspected, by someone who understood wanting very well indeed.

"Wren?" Mara's voice, far away. "Your eyes are doing something I don't like."

He reached for the door.

He actually reached for it—hand out, fingers spread, gold light streaming from his scars like water from a broken jug—and in the last inch before his palm touched the frost, he saw himself clearly. Saw the man on the other side of that touch: robed, ringed, revered, opening doors across the world while the valley of Vell went back to being a place he'd escaped. Saw the hive on the hillside going untended. Saw Bram's unpaid honey debt. Saw a woman standing behind him in a freezing wood, loving a man who had just chosen a door over her.

Saw, most of all, that the voice promising him belonging had never once mentioned love. Power never does. That's how you know it's lying.

"No," Wren said.

The word came out small. Then he said it again, louder, tearing his hand back from the door as if from a stove. "No. I won't. I fled you once and I'll flee you forever, do you hear me? I am a beekeeper. I am *nobody's vessel*. Get out of my head!"

The gold light guttered. The magic sank back down through him like a tide going out, leaving him hollow and shaking and colder than the frost deserved credit for. The whispers faded—not gone, he could feel them retreating rather than departing, patient again, biding—but the door—

The door stayed shut.

The frost stopped spreading. The keyhole sat dark and empty. Somewhere above them, a branch creaked in a wind that smelled, suddenly, of nothing magical at all—just leaf-rot and coming rain.

Wren stood there with his empty hand hanging at his side, and the failure landed on him with its full weight. He had done everything right. He had refused the corruption, rejected the temptation, held onto himself—and the door hadn't cared. The prophecy hadn't said *the doorkeeper will nobly decline power.* It had said he would open the way into Aldermere. And he couldn't. All that refusing, and the door wouldn't so much as shiver.

"I failed," he said. His voice cracked on the second word. "Mara, I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I thought—I thought if I just held onto myself, it would be enough, and it's not, the door wants—"

"Wren."

"—and now the hunters are coming and the seed is dying and your whole country is on the other side of a tree that won't open for a coward who—"

"*Wren.*"

He stopped. She had both his hands now, the bare scarred ones, and she was looking at him with an expression he couldn't decipher at first—until he realized it was the same one she wore looking at the seed from her garden. Grief and tenderness in equal measure, aimed entirely at him.

"You heard it offer you everything," she said. "Everything you've ever wanted. And you said no. Do you understand how rare that is? How *hard*?" Her thumb traced one of his scars, gentle as reading braille. "The door didn't open. Fine. Doors are doors. But you opened something else tonight, and I watched it happen, and I am not—" her voice wobbled, steadied, "—I am not going anywhere. Failed quest and all. You're stuck with me."

"That's a terrible bargain. I come with frostbite and unresolved prophecy."

"Terrible bargains are the only kind worth keeping."

He laughed—a wet, broken sound, but a laugh—and leaned his forehead against hers, and for a moment the clearing was almost warm.

Which was when the key, lying forgotten in the snow at their feet, began to glow.

Not gold. Not the pale silver of the frost. A deep, steady red, like a coal breathing, and with it came a sound neither of them could mistake: hoofbeats. Many. Coming fast through the beechwood, and beneath the hoofbeats, faint but unmistakable, the ringing crack of ice forming on living wood.

Mara's head came up. "How did they find us?"

Wren looked down at the glowing key, and the answer arrived in his stomach like a dropped stone.

"They didn't find us," he said. "Someone told them exactly where we'd be. The witch's key isn't leading us to the door anymore, Mara. It's leading them to *us*."
