## 7. The Founder's Bargain

They took her at the ford, where the river ran thin as a knife and the reeds had frozen mid-bend, still curved as if bowing to something that had passed through days ago.

Mara had known the hunters were close. You learned to know it in Aldermere—the way the air went glassy and quiet, the way your breath hung around you like a debt unpaid. She had felt that hush gathering behind them for two days now, patient as frost on a windowpane, and she had said nothing to Wren because saying it aloud would have made it real, and because she was selfish in one small, specific way: she wanted three more hours of his laugh before the world came for him.

She got two.

The captain stepped out of the trees without sound, which should not have been possible—a man in white furs and pale armor, broad as a doorframe, crossing snow that did not crunch beneath him. Behind him stood six more, identical in their stillness, their faces the color of old candle wax. The border stones were failing; she could feel it in her teeth, a low ache like a storm coming. Magic was leaking back into Vell, thin and cold and wrong, and these creatures drank it like wine.

"Queen Mara of Aldermere," the captain said. His voice was pleasant. That was the worst part. It was the voice of a man who read bedtime stories to children he would later inventory. "You've been difficult to find."

"I've been easy to find," Mara said. "I've been exactly where I said I'd be. Your master simply doesn't listen."

Something moved behind the captain's face—amusement, perhaps, or the memory of it, worn smooth by long use. "He listens," the captain said. "He listens to everything. That is rather the problem with him."

* * *

They did not bind her hands. They did not need to. The cold itself was the rope—one of the hunters breathed out, slow and deliberate, and the air around Mara thickened into a shell of frost that held her arms at her sides and lifted her off the ground when they walked, so that she traveled through the winter forest like a lantern carried at a funeral.

Wren was not with them. That was the only mercy, and she suspected it wasn't mercy at all but strategy: take the bait, let the hook set itself. The founder wanted Wren walking into the winter under his own power, unforced, believing himself the rescuer. Free will made better vessels. She had read enough of the Order's histories—stolen pages, smuggled chapters—to know how their founder thought. He had been a gardener once, before the crown and the corruption. He had believed that anything living could be transplanted into any soil, given enough cruelty and patience.

The hunters carried her north through the afternoon, and the world grew whiter and quieter until sound itself seemed to freeze, and then there was a place where the snow stopped falling upward and began, impossibly, to fall inward—toward a point ahead—and she understood they had arrived.

There was no castle. There was no tent, no hall, no throne. There was a clearing, and in the clearing stood a single bare tree, black as a burnt match, and beneath it sat an old man on a chair of ice, as though he had been waiting there since before chairs were invented.

His eyes were open. They were the blue of deep glacier ice, and they were looking at her before the hunters even stopped moving.

"Mara," he said. "Little queen of a dead garden. Do you know, I remember your grandmother? She bit me." He smiled, and his teeth were very white and very even, like piano keys. "I have missed being bitten. Everyone up here has become so agreeable."

"You're him," she said. "Aldric Vane. The Founder."

"I'm the winter," he corrected gently. "Aldric Vane was what I wore while I worked. The winter is what I became when the work was finished. It's much more efficient. A man must sleep; a season does not." He rose from the ice chair with the creak of a lake freezing, and came closer, and she hated that she could not step back. "And you crossed my border carrying a seed from my garden. My garden, Mara. I planted every tree in it. I remember each root the way you remember your own fingers. Did you think I wouldn't feel it leave?"

"It isn't yours anymore," Mara said. Her voice shook, and she let it. Let him hear the fear; fear was honest, and honesty was the one weapon they couldn't confiscate. "Nothing in Aldermere is yours anymore. That's why you need Wren. That's why you've been hunting us instead of simply taking the seed back. You can't touch it. You can't touch anything alive anymore, can you? The winter doesn't grow things. It only keeps them."

The smile stayed exactly where it was, but something behind it shifted, tectonic and slow. "You are your grandmother's granddaughter," he said.

* * *

"So here is what happens next," he said, pacing now, hands clasped behind his back, a lecturer strolling before a class of one. "In a day, perhaps two, the boy will come north after you. He cannot help himself; I have read the prophecy too, and prophecies are such useful things—they do half our recruiting for us. He will come, and he will find me, and I will offer him a choice, and he will make the wrong one, because everyone does. Everyone chooses power when it is offered kindly enough." He paused. "Do you know what he is?"

"A doorkeeper," Mara said.

"He is a hinge," Aldric said. "Born on both sides of the border—the last person in the world who belongs to Vell and Aldermere at once. Do you understand what that means, little queen? When his bloodline helped build my prison—" he gestured vaguely southward, toward the valley, toward the stones, "—they built it around themselves. Their magic didn't die in Vell. It was locked in with everything else. And this boy walks around with a hundred years of stolen power sleeping in his scars, and he thinks he's ordinary. He thinks he's *safe*." He laughed, softly, delightedly. "When I open him, I will not merely regain what was taken. I will gain a vessel that can walk through doors that no longer exist. Every border in the world. Every kingdom. And the first door I'll open is the one between me and my garden."

Mara's heart was doing something painful and complicated, because she could see it—she could see Wren hearing those same promises, wanting them the way you want water, secretly, ashamedly. He had spent seven years making himself small. What would a man who had practiced invisibility say to a voice that promised he could be *seen*?

"You won't get him," she said.

"No?" Aldric tilted his head. "And why not?"

"Because I'm going to buy him first."

The clearing went very still. Even the inward-falling snow seemed to pause, flakes hanging like held breath.

"Your blood," Aldric said slowly, tasting the words. "Royal blood. The line of the garden's keepers. Yes. That would do. That would do beautifully—as a stopgap. A vessel of the old line could hold the winter's edge together another century while I work on the boy." He studied her the way she had seen merchants study cloth, looking for flaws. "But you fled your crown, Mara. You crossed a country of ice rather than wear it. Why would you hand yourself to me?"

Because duty eats love, she thought. Because someone taught me that a long time ago, in a warm room, by people who meant it kindly, and I ran all the way to Vell to escape the lesson, and here I am teaching it to myself.

What she said was: "Because he'd do it for me. And one of us should get to be the coward's opposite."

* * *

The bargain took less time than buying a loaf of bread, which seemed obscene. Her royal blood, freely given, freely flowing—the founder was very particular about *freely*, the old laws mattered to him, some gardener's superstition he'd never managed to prune—in exchange for Wren Blackwood walking free of the winter, unmarked, unhunted, forever. The frost shell around her arms melted away, and the captain produced a knife of grey glass, and Mara looked at the blade and thought, absurdly, of spilled cider on a table in a valley tavern, of a nervous man with scarred hands laughing at his own joke, and she thought: *this is what I fled to protect. Not a seed. This.*

"One condition," she said, as the captain took her wrist.

"You are in no position—"

"The seed stays with him. Whatever happens. Swear it on the garden."

Aldric's glacier eyes narrowed, and for a moment—for just a moment—she saw the gardener underneath the winter, the man who had once loved growing things before he decided he preferred owning them. "Sworn," he said. "Though I confess I don't understand the sentiment. It's a seed, little queen. Seeds are common."

"That one," Mara said, "is the last thing in the world that remembers Aldermere being *warm*. You should be more careful with the things you don't understand."

The knife touched her palm. The pain was bright and clean and strangely welcome—proof she could still feel, proof she was still flesh and not yet furniture. Her blood fell onto the snow, and where it fell the snow did not melt. Instead it darkened, and hardened, and took the shape of a crown, small and perfect and terrible, and Aldric picked it up with both hands, reverent as a priest.

"There," he said softly. "Welcome home, Majesty."

And far to the south, Mara knew without knowing how, a hollowed oak had just begun to hum—and somewhere in the valley, Wren Blackwood woke from a dreamless sleep with his scars burning, already reaching for his coat, already coming for her, walking straight into the trap she had spent her whole life trying not to build.

She had become her grandmother after all: a piece on the board, sacrificed by her own hand.

The only thing left to decide was whether the game ended here—or whether pieces, sometimes, got to bite.
