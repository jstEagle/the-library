## 10. Mara and the Garden

### Wren

The bees came back in the third week of spring, and Wren took it personally.

"You could have knocked," he told them, lifting the lid off the first hive. The comb inside was a ruin—honey crystallized to amber glass during the long winter, the cells sealed like a hundred tiny doors that had never been opened. But at the bottom of the hive, clustered around their queen like courtiers who had refused to believe the worst, a handful of survivors stirred and lifted their heads to look at him.

He knew how they felt.

The valley of Vell had changed the way a sleeping man changes position: slowly, then all at once. Magic had come flooding back when the prison broke—not as a flood, really, though everyone called it that afterward, but more like water finding an old channel. It ran through the border stones now instead of pooling against them. The stones themselves had gone from grey to a deep mossy green, and children had already begun the ancient and universal tradition of daring each other to touch them.

Wren touched them every day. He touched everything now. That was the strange inheritance of being the doorkeeper: doors answered him. Not just the great one in the hollowed oak—all doors, everywhere, in the small way that all things acknowledge the thing they are made of. A latch would turn a half-second before his hand arrived. A gate would swing wide on windless mornings. The innkeeper's cellar door, which had stuck for forty years, now opened for Wren so eagerly that he'd taken to using the window instead, out of embarrassment.

His hands had healed, too. Mostly. The old scars from his apprenticeship—the ones he'd spent seven years hiding under gloves and sleeves and careful angles—had faded to thin silver lines, except where the new magic had burned through them, and there the skin shone pale and clean, like frost melting off a pane. He still caught himself hiding them sometimes. Old habits. But more often now he let them show, because Mara liked to trace them with one finger and say, *There's my doorkeeper,* in a voice that made the word sound less like a job title and more like an endearment.

She'd been gone three weeks. Queen business. Letters arrived by crow—a real crow, not a messenger bird, but an actual crow named General Pemberly whom Mara insisted had volunteered, and whom Wren suspected had simply decided she was interesting and appointed himself.

*The garden is waking,* her latest letter read. *You would laugh at the state of it. Three centuries of winter and the roses came up anyway, stubborn as tax collectors. The council wants me to hold a coronation feast. I want you here for it. I want you beside me, Wren. Permanently. There is a chair with your name on it. Literally—the carpenter asked, and I said yes before I could be sensible about it.*

He read the letter four times. Then he went out and sat among the hives until dark, turning the word *permanently* over like a stone in his pocket.

Here was the trouble, and he was honest enough with himself to name it: he had spent his whole life learning to be small. First the Order had made him small—a vessel, a tool, a boy whose only worth was what could be poured into him. Then Vell had taught him a different smallness, a chosen one, the safety of the unseen. And now the world wanted to make him large again. Doorkeeper of the threshold. Hero of the thaw. The man who broke the founder's winter with nothing but surrender. They sang songs about him in Aldermere already; General Pemberly reported this with what sounded suspiciously like pride.

But Wren had learned something in the winter's heart, kneeling before the founder while power whispered its sweet promises: that belonging bought with bigness was just another kind of hiding. You became so large, so important, so *necessary*, that no one ever saw the actual person underneath. The founder had been the largest thing in the world, and the loneliest.

What Wren wanted was smaller than a crown and harder to win. He wanted to be seen. He wanted to keep things—bees, thresholds, a garden gate, a promise. He wanted to be visited, not installed.

And he wanted Mara. That was the complication. Because he loved her, and she loved him, and love made a liar out of every clean answer he'd constructed in the hive-yard.

So he did what he always did when he couldn't decide: he wrote back honestly, packed a bag, and walked north through the green border stones to ask her in person. Some questions deserved a face.

### Mara

Queen Mara of Aldermere was late to her own garden, which the council had opinions about, and she was carrying dirt under her fingernails, which the council had stronger opinions about, and she did not care, which the council had given up having opinions about entirely.

The royal garden had been her grandmother's, and her grandmother's grandmother's, going back past the long winter into the bright country Aldermere had been before the founder closed the sky. For three hundred years it had slept under snow—and yet the seed Mara had carried across the border in her coat pocket, the living seed that had led her to a beekeeper in a valley where magic couldn't follow, had come from the last tree in it still holding on. She had planted it the week after the thaw, in the exact center, and now it stood taller than she was, budding green, as if three centuries were nothing but a long nap.

"Your Majesty." Old Tam, the head gardener, materialized at her elbow the way he did, smelling of loam and disapproval. "The feast. The hall. The hundred guests."

"The feast can wait an hour."

"An hour," Tam repeated, in the tone of a man writing *forever* in his ledger.

Mara knelt by the young tree and pressed her palm flat to its trunk. Warmth. Still warm, faintly, the way it had been warm in her pocket through all those cold miles—the little heartbeat of home that had kept her walking. She had carried a lot of things across the snow: guilt, duty, a seed, a lie she'd told a kind stranger over cider. She had set most of them down since. The guilt was the hardest. She still caught herself rehearsing apologies to Wren—*I used you, I found you with a prophecy and I didn't tell you*—and each time, he'd look at her with those patient eyes and say, *You also chose me. Nobody scripted that part.*

Which was true. The prophecy had written their meeting, the spilled cider, even the peddler with his terrible timing. But it hadn't written the second date, or the tenth, or the night they'd sat up talking about whether bees dreamed. Prophecies, it turned out, were terrible at the parts that mattered.

A shadow crossed the garden wall, and her heart did the undignified leap it always did, because she was a queen now and queens were supposed to have steady hearts, and hers had never once obeyed.

He came through the gate without touching it—it swung open for him, smug as anything—and stood there in the evening light with his bag over his shoulder and his scarred hands visible, no gloves, no hiding, and Mara thought: *there he is.* Not the doorkeeper. Not the hero of the thaw. Just Wren, travel-dusty and nervous, looking at her the way he'd looked at her across that table a lifetime ago, terrified and hopeful, both at once, forever.

"You're late," she said.

"I'm punctual. Your garden is early."

They met in the middle, among the waking roses, and held on, and neither of them said anything clever for a while, which was its own kind of eloquence.

Then, later, sitting on the low wall with the tree between them and the feast-hall lights beginning to glitter down the hill, she asked. She'd promised herself she wouldn't beg, and she mostly managed it.

"The chair has your name on it," she said. "Literally. I've seen it. It's very nice oak. Come be a king-consort or whatever we're calling it. Come be *large*, Wren. You'd be good at it, once you stopped flinching."

He was quiet for a long time. When he finally spoke, his voice was gentle, which is how she knew it would hurt.

"I spent years being nobody, and it nearly killed me. And then I spent a season being everybody's savior, and that nearly killed me faster." He turned his hands over, silver scars catching the light. "I don't want either one. I want the thing in between. I want to keep the threshold, Mara. The door in the oak, the border stones, the road between your country and mine. Somebody has to tend it—not guard it, *tend* it, the way you tend a garden. And it turns out I'm the only person alive who can."

"And me?"

"You," he said, and smiled, and the smile undid her completely, "are the reason there's a road at all. You'll come through it. Often. That's rather the point."

"I could order you to stay."

"You could. And I'd remind you that doors open for me whether you like it or not, and I'd be in Vell by morning, and you'd have to explain to the council why their queen lost her consort to a beehive."

She laughed despite herself—laughed until Tam, lurking by the wall pretending to prune something, gave up pretense and glared at them both.

So it was settled the way good things settle: not with a decree but with a compromise, which is the only treaty that lasts. Wren would keep the threshold. Mara would rule. And between the two countries, in the garden that straddled the old border—for the border stones had shifted after the thaw, and no one, least of all the stones, seemed inclined to move them back—there would be a place that belonged to both.

They built it together that summer. A cottage, small and stubborn, with a garden spilling out on either side. On the Aldermere side, Mara planted roses and the herbs her grandmother's recipes demanded. On the Vell side, Wren set out hives, six of them, and the bees—traitorous, wonderful creatures—pollinated straight across the border without the slightest regard for national identity, which Wren declared the finest diplomacy he'd ever witnessed.

And in the middle of the garden, where the young tree grew warm-barked and tall, they put two chairs.

One faced south, toward Aldermere, toward the white towers of the capital and the feast-hall where a queen held court. One faced west, toward Vell, toward the valley and the border stones gone green and the small life Wren had built and finally, finally stopped apologizing for.

Some evenings Mara sat in the southern chair with her crown set aside and her feet bare in the grass, and Wren brought her cider, and spilled a little, deliberately, because some prophecies deserved to be honored. Some evenings he sat in the western chair and watched the light go gold over his valley, and she came and stood behind him with her hands on his shoulders, tracing the silver scars, saying nothing, needing to say nothing.

And some evenings—the best ones—they pushed the chairs together, facing nowhere at all, and simply watched the garden grow.

One such evening, near midsummer, General Pemberly landed on the arm of Wren's chair with a letter clamped in his beak and an air of tremendous self-importance. Mara opened it, read it, and went very still.

"What is it?" Wren asked.

She handed him the page. At the bottom, in handwriting he recognized—the hedge-witch's handwriting, the teacher the hunters had taken, the woman he'd mourned for a year—were five words:

*Thorn and glass. Come collect.*

Wren looked up. Mara was already smiling.

"Well," she said. "Doorkeeper. Someone left a door unlocked."
