## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The grimoire sat on Mara's desk like a sleeping animal, and Bram Quintana had been watching it breathe for three years.

Not literally, of course. Books didn't breathe. But there was something about that particular book — bound in grey leather gone soft as mothwing at the corners, its spine cracked in seven places from being opened and opened again — that made him think of a thing curled up, waiting. He had dusted around it. He had oiled the hinges of the cabinet where it lived when it lived anywhere at all, which was rarely, because Mara carried it against her ribs like a second heart. He had watched her read it by candlelight with her lips moving over words she never spoke aloud, and he had wanted it the way you want water when you've been walking all day: quietly, constantly, without any hope of being reasonable about it.

Tonight he was going to take it.

Bram stood in the doorway of his own small room above the workshop and listened to the house settle. The valley pressed close outside the window — the terraced orchards going silver under the half-moon, the river muttering to itself on its way down toward the lowlands it was never permitted to reach, the far border hills standing black against a sky so crowded with stars it looked like someone had spilled salt across black wool. Twenty-two years he had lived inside this bowl of mountains, and every night the same stars, arranged in the same constellations, telling the same stories. The Shepherd. The Drowned Girl. The Long Road, which no one in the valley had ever walked.

He knew them all by heart. That was the problem. He knew everything here by heart.

Below him, Mara's lamp still burned. He could see the light through the floorboards' seams, a thin gold line across his dark room, steady as a pulse. She worked late most nights now. Lately she'd been working later and stranger — up until the small hours with the grimoire open, sometimes writing, sometimes just sitting with her hand flat on the page as though taking a pulse of her own. Twice this week he'd come down for water and found her staring at nothing, and both times she'd looked at him a moment too long before saying goodnight, as if she were checking something. As if she were counting.

He told himself it didn't matter what she was doing. In an hour it would be her problem and not his.

His pack was already packed. It had been packed for eleven days, actually, hidden under a loose board beneath his bed: a spare shirt, a knife, hard cheese wrapped in waxcloth, dried apples, the good flint, and forty-one coppers sewn into the hem of his coat because he did not trust pockets or luck. Eleven days ago he had decided, and once Bram Quintana decided a thing, the deciding part was easy. It was only the living between deciding and doing that was hard, and he had been living in that gap like a man holding his breath underwater, and tonight he was finally going to come up for air.

There was a letter on his desk. He'd written it four drafts deep and burned three of them, and the survivor said:

*Master — I'm sorry. I know you'll say the valley needs hedge-witches more than it needs wanderers, and I know you'll be right, but I can't spend my whole life learning how to keep things in. Someone has to find out what's out there. Don't follow me. — B.*

It was a coward's letter and he knew it. He should have said it to her face. He had tried, twice, and both times she'd looked at him with those grey eyes that saw straight through skin and muscle and intention, and both times the words had turned to porridge in his mouth. So: a letter, left on the desk beside the cold hearth, weighted down with the river-stone she used to press her maps flat. She would find it at dawn. By then he'd be over the first ridge, and by the time her fury caught up with him he'd be a day gone, two, and anger cools faster than grief if you keep moving.

That was what he told himself, anyway. His hands disagreed. They shook as he pulled on his boots, and they shook worse as he went down the stairs on the sides of his feet, each tread placed like a lie.

* * *

The workshop smelled of rosemary and hot iron and the particular dry sweetness of old paper, a smell Bram had slept inside for three years and would carry in his nose for the rest of his life whether he wanted to or not. Moonlight came through the south window in a long pale pane across the worktable. Mara's lamp had finally gone out. Her door at the back stood closed, and behind it he could hear her breathing — slow, even, the breathing of a woman asleep, or of a woman lying very still and listening.

He stopped with his hand on the cabinet latch and made himself count to sixty. Her breathing didn't change. Sixty more. Nothing.

*She's asleep,* he told himself. *She's fifty-three years old and she was awake past midnight wrestling with whatever's in that book, and she sleeps like a stone, and in six hours she will be furious, and in six weeks she will be relieved, because Mara Quintana does not waste fury on people who are coming back.*

The cabinet opened without a sound. He'd oiled the hinges himself, months ago, telling her it was general maintenance. Even then — even then, some part of him had known what he was oiling them for. That was the thing about wanting. It got into your hands early and waited.

The grimoire was heavier than it looked. It always was. Three years of carrying it to and fro and he had never once gotten used to the weight of it, the way it seemed to pull not downward exactly but *inward*, as though its center of gravity were somewhere behind the world. He slid it into his pack, felt the straps bite his shoulders, and closed the cabinet.

That was when the candle stub on the worktable lit itself.

Bram went very still. He hadn't touched it. No draft, no spark — the wick simply bloomed, a small fat flame standing up out of the dark like a question mark, and he knew that trick, he'd seen Mara do it a hundred times, and Mara was asleep behind a closed door and had been for an hour.

"Put it out," he whispered to it, absurdly, and the flame leaned toward him, attentive, and stayed lit.

From behind her door, Mara's voice came, quiet and level and terrible: "Take your hand off my book, Bram."

Every story he'd ever rehearsed — the explanations, the justifications, the wounded dignity — left him at once. "I wasn't—"

"You were. You have been for a year." The door didn't open. Her voice came through the wood, unhurried, and somehow that was worse than shouting. "The hinges. The questions about the lowlands. The way you look at the border hills when you think no one's watching. Did you think I hired a boy who watched borders and never wondered why?"

"I'm not a boy."

"No," she agreed. "That's rather the problem." A pause, and then, softer, which was worst of all: "Why tonight?"

And Bram, who had prepared answers for every question except the honest one, heard himself give it anyway. "Because I can't learn anything else here. Because I know every star in that sky by name and I've never seen anything new under any of them. Because you're teaching me to be a wall, and I don't want to be a wall, I want to be a road."

Silence. The candle flame bent, steadied.

"The wards need tending," Mara said at last. "The valley needs—"

"The valley needs a hedge-witch and you know six candidates and I am not the best of them and we both know it." His voice cracked on the last word and he hated it. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry, but I'm going. Put it on my grave that I was polite about it."

Behind the door: a breath that might have been a laugh, or might have been the other thing. "At least wait until morning. There are things you should—" and then she stopped, sharply, as if she'd bitten through the sentence, and the silence that followed had a different texture, a listening texture, and Bram stood in the moonlit workshop with a stolen book against his spine and understood that he had just heard his master decide something.

"Bram." Her voice had changed entirely. Flat. Urgent. "How far were you planning to walk tonight?"

"To the ferry crossing. Maybe the first village past it, if—"

"Don't go past the wards tonight."

"Why? What's tonight?"

Another silence, longer, and when she spoke again her voice had gone carefully, deliberately calm, the voice she used for delivering bad news to families, and it frightened him more than shouting ever had. "Nothing. Old ward-calendar nonsense. Go tomorrow, in daylight, if you must go at all. Take the east road. And Bram — leave the book."

He looked down at his pack. Through the canvas he could feel the grimoire's strange inward pull, faint and constant, like a hook set in his flesh. "You know I can't."

"I know," Mara said, and there was something in her voice he had no name for yet, something that sounded almost like surrender. "Then go. Now. Quickly. And whatever you see when you get there — whatever happens — keep walking, and don't look back at the sky."

"What happens to the sk—"

"*Go.*"

He went. Out the kitchen door, across the herb garden where the rosemary brushed his ankles like fingers trying to hold him, over the stile, onto the sheep path that climbed away from the lamplit windows. At the top of the first field he broke his own resolution immediately and looked back once: the workshop small and golden, Mara's silhouette in the upstairs window, watching him go, making no move to stop him, raising neither alarm nor hand.

Just watching. Like a woman seeing off a ship she was fairly sure would sink.

* * *

The night turned strange within the hour.

Bram noticed it first as a wrongness in the dark itself — a thinning, as though someone had drawn a cloth across the world one layer too sheer. The sheep path glimmered pale ahead of him, but the darkness on either side of it had acquired a texture, a grain, like the black of a printed page rather than the black of night. And the sounds were wrong. Crickets, yes, but spaced oddly, with gaps between their chirrings as though the night were forgetting, briefly and repeatedly, how crickets went.

By the time he reached the shepherd's hut below the ridge, the moon had developed a halo, and the halo was moving. Not drifting the way halos drift — *contracting*, tightening around the moon like a noose being drawn by patient hands, and the moonlight through it came down yellowed and unsteady, the color of old paper.

Bram stopped and stared up, and the wanting that had carried him out of the valley curdled in his chest into something colder.

He thought of Mara's voice through the door. *Don't go past the wards tonight.* He thought of her silhouette in the window, letting him go. He thought: *she knew. She knows what tonight is. She let me steal the book anyway.*

"Why?" he asked the empty hillside, and the hillside declined to answer, and he kept climbing, because the alternative was to go back, and going back meant admitting that the whole shape of his life — the wanting, the packing, the eleven days of resolve — had been a child's tantrum, and twenty-two-year-old men cannot afford to learn that about themselves on a mountainside at midnight.

The pass below the border crest was a saddle of scree and old snow, and the wardline ran along it: a seam of pale stone markers, hip-high, each carved with the valley's knotwork sigil, marching up the slope and over the crest and out of sight. His family had tended farms inside that line for nine generations. Every child in the valley learned the marker song before they learned letters. *Stone to stone the promise goes; what walks without it, wardstone knows.*

As Bram came up the last stretch of path, the nearest marker began to sing.

It was a sound he'd heard all his life and never listened to — a hum, felt more than heard, the sound of the wards idling, the valley's held breath. Except now the hum was stuttering. Skipping. A note held, lost, found again, like a singer losing the thread of a song sung ten thousand times. And with each skip, the halo around the moon tightened another degree, and the grainy dark pressed in another inch, and somewhere below and behind him, faint as memory, he heard a dog begin to bark in the valley and then stop mid-bark, and not start again.

Bram stood at the wardline with his heart slamming and did the arithmetic he'd been refusing to do since the candle lit itself. The grimoire against his back. Mara's sleepless nights. Her hand flat on the page like a woman taking a pulse. *Don't go past the wards tonight. Leave the book.* The wards weren't failing. The wards were being *unmade*, and they were being unmade from the keystone outward, and the keystone was the book, and the book was in his pack, and he had carried it out of the valley like a thief carrying off the load-bearing wall of a house.

"Oh," he said aloud, to the scree and the stuttering stones. "Oh, you idiot."

He turned for the valley. He got three steps.

The wards shattered.

There was no explosion. That was the horror of it — the sheer *quiet* of it. The hum simply stopped, all along the line as far as he could see in both directions, and in the sudden silence Bram felt the change pass through him like cold water, the way you feel a door close somewhere in a house you thought you knew. Above him the halo snapped tight around the moon and snuffed it, and then the stars went out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded. *Out* — one by one, in no order he could see, the way candles die in a draft, the Shepherd first, then the Drowned Girl, then the Long Road vanishing star by star until the sky overhead was a smooth and absolute black, a ceiling instead of a heaven, and the darkness came down close, closer than night had ever hung over him, close enough that he could feel it on his face like the approach of something inhaling.

In the valley below, lights were waking — doors opening, lamps kindling, voices rising thin and confused up the slopes. He heard someone scream, far off, and then several someones, and then, cutting through it all, Mara's voice, pitched to carry the way only she could pitch it, calling the valley's folk out of their houses with the old gathering-words, steady as bedrock even now, even betrayed, even robbed.

Bram stood on the broken wardline with the stolen keystone on his back and wept, briefly, furiously, without making any sound at all. Then he wiped his face with his sleeve, because grief is a luxury and movement is not, and he did the only thing left that made any sense, which was the thing he'd set out to do: he crossed the line, into the dark, away from the valley, away from her voice, carrying the ruin with him.

If the Court — if whatever this was — had come for the book, then the book needed to be elsewhere. That was the logic. He clung to it. It was thin logic, spider-silk logic, but a man can cross a mountain on spider silk if he doesn't look down.

He was a hundred yards beyond the wardstones, descending into the lowland dark, when he heard it behind him.

A sound like a page turning. Vast, slow, deliberate — the sound of something very large beginning to read.

Bram turned, and looked back at the sky after all, because he couldn't help it, because looking back was the whole of his nature.

On the black ceiling where the stars had been, something was moving. Not descending — *entering*, the way ink enters water, a slow unfurling of a shape that had no business fitting through a sky, unfolding downward through the broken line the wards had died defending. It had edges like the margins of a burnt page. Where it passed, the scree below it went grey and featureless, smoothed of detail, the way a face goes in a bad dream when you try to look at it directly. It did not roar. It did not reach. It simply advanced at the pace of something that had never once been hurried, because nothing it hunted had ever escaped by running.

Bram ran.

Down through the lowland dark with the vast page-turning sound unfolding behind him, over ground that felt wrong underfoot — too smooth, too quiet, the grass silent where it should have whispered — the grimoire hammering against his spine with every stride, pulling inward, pulling *home*, and Bram Quintana, runaway apprentice, thief of keystones, fled into a country with no stars above it, carrying the only light anyone had left.

Behind him, soft as a closing book, the Hollow crossed the line.
