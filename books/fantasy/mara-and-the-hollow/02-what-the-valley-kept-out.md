## 2. What the Valley Kept Out

The wards broke behind me like a held breath finally let go.

I felt it through my boots before I heard it — a shudder in the ground, deep and low, the kind of tremor that comes from something vast turning over in its sleep. Then the sound arrived: not a crash but an *unmaking*, a long sighing tear, as though the whole rim of the valley had been stitched from light and someone had drawn out every thread at once.

I was three hundred paces past the last wardstone when it happened. Close enough to see the boundary shimmer die — that faint silver haze I had lived beneath for nineteen years without ever truly seeing, the way you never see the air. It hung over the mountain passes like breath on glass, and then it didn't. It simply went, all along the valley's crown, a ring of frost melting off the world.

And above me, the stars went with it.

I had been walking toward them, if truth be told. Toward the lowlands, toward the river road, toward everything Mara Quintana had spent seven years teaching me to fear. I had the grimoire strapped against my chest beneath my coat, warm as a second heartbeat, and I had told myself a dozen lies on the climb down from her tower: *I'll only look at it. I'll bring it back by morning. She'll never know.* But my feet had known better than my mouth. My feet had carried me downhill in the dark, past the goat pens, past the shrine where the valley folk left bread for ancestors whose names no one could quite recall anymore, and out beyond the standing stones where the wardlight thinned to nothing.

Now there was nothing between me and the sky.

I stopped on the shoulder of the fell and looked up, and the absence was so complete it made a noise in my head, a ringing like a struck bell that would not finish fading. I knew those stars. Every apprentice did. We had learned their names before we learned our letters — the Shepherd, the Nine Sisters, the Long Oar that steered the dead downriver. Mara used to make me recite them on winter nights until my breath froze, saying, *Names are how the sky holds itself together, Bram. Forget that and you'll forget everything else worth knowing.*

They were gone. Not clouded, not veiled — *gone*, as if the night had been peeled back to reveal a blacker night underneath, a darkness with texture to it, a darkness that pressed. Where the Shepherd should have stood there was nothing, and the nothing had weight. I felt it on my shoulders like wet wool. I felt it leaning close, the way a large animal leans to smell you.

My first thought — and I am not proud of this — was that I had done it. That the grimoire under my coat had somehow pulled the stars out of the sky when it crossed the line, that my theft had cost the world its lights, and that somewhere behind me Mara was already climbing down from her tower with fire in her hands.

But the wards had broken first. I was sure of it. The tearing had come from the valley's rim, all around, not from the book against my ribs. Whatever had happened, it had happened everywhere at once.

That should have sent me home. Any sensible person would have turned around. Instead I stood there in the starless dark with my heart hammering and thought: *this is what's outside. This is what she kept me from.* And beneath the dread, shamefully, unmistakably, there was wonder. The world beyond the wards was supposed to be ordinary — farms, roads, towns, the same dull earth we had, just more of it. No one had ever said it would be *dark* like this. No one had ever said the dark would feel like a held note, like a throat about to speak.

I walked on.

* * *

The descent into the lowlands took most of what remained of the night, though night had become a strange currency now, since there were no stars to spend it by. I navigated by the grimoire's warmth, which pulsed faintly stronger whenever I faced south, and by the smell of woodsmoke that drifted up from the valley floor, promising hearths and people and the ordinary world.

The first village announced itself by its dogs. They began barking long before I saw the rooftops — a ragged chorus, frantic in a way dogs shouldn't be, and then, abruptly, silent, all at once, as if a hand had closed over the sound. I remember thinking the owners must have quieted them. I remember thinking a great many foolish things that night.

The village sat in a fold of the hills beside a stream, a scatter of stone houses with turf roofs, exactly like a dozen villages inside the valley. Smoke rose from chimneys. A light burned in one window. Everything looked right, and everything was wrong, and it took me a while to understand why, because the wrongness was not in the village at all.

It was in the way the dark lay over it.

Inside the valley, night is a blanket. It settles; things sleep under it; morning lifts it away. Here the darkness *pooled*. It sat in the doorways and the wells and the spaces between houses like water filling the low places of a field, and where it pooled deepest, the details of the world grew vague. A fence post near the well wasn't shadowed — it was *unfinished*, its grain smoothed away, as if someone had begun carving a fence post and lost interest halfway through. The churchyard wall had gaps in it, not broken gaps but absent ones, places where the eye slid off and found nothing to report.

And the people. The people were awake.

I came into the village along the stream path, and the first thing I saw was a woman standing in her doorway holding a lamp, calling a name into the dark. "Tamsin," she called. "Tamsin!" Her voice had the frayed quality of a voice that has been calling for hours. "Tamsin, love, come inside, it's late—"

A man sat on the step beside her, and he was the one who saw me. He looked up, and his face in the lamplight was the face of a man who has been crying for so long that crying has become simply the shape of his face. "You've come from up-valley," he said. Not a question. "You've come from behind the stones."

"I—" I had prepared a story, on the walk down. A lie about being sent for medicine. It evaporated. "What's happened here?"

He laughed, or made a sound near enough to laughing that it hurt to hear. "Happened." He said the word like he was tasting spoiled milk. "Come and see, then. Come and tell us, if you can, because we can't."

They gathered in the largest house, maybe twenty of them, and they were not hurt. That was the thing I kept returning to, the thing my mind circled like a dog circling a trap: none of them were hurt. No wounds, no sickness, no marks of any violence. They fed me barley broth because that is what people do, and they talked, and within a quarter hour I understood that I had walked into a village of amnesiacs, and that it was worse than that, so much worse, and I will try to set it down plainly even though plainness fails.

They had not forgotten everything. They remembered how to churn butter and mend nets and say their prayers. They remembered each other's faces. What they had lost was narrower and deeper: they had lost the *meaning* of each other.

The woman who had been calling Tamsin — her name was Sorrel, the others supplied it for her, patiently, the way you remind a child — could not remember who Tamsin was. "There's a girl missing," she told me, gripping my wrist across the table. "There's a girl missing and everyone says she's mine, and I believe them, I want to believe them, but when I try to picture her—" Her face worked. "I get a shape. A shape and a smell, soap and woodsmoke, and nothing else. It's like someone reached into me and took the middle out."

Her husband tried to comfort her and got her name wrong twice. An old man by the fire wept quietly and steadily and could not say why, only that he felt he'd lost something enormous, something that had once taken up most of him, and now there was a hollow where it lived, and the hollow ached like weather coming.

"The stars," I said. "Did you see the stars go out?"

"Stars went out years ago, boy," someone said, and then they argued about it, some saying three years, some saying three months, one old woman insisting the stars had never existed at all, that the sky had always been a lid, and the terrible thing was that she believed it, wholly, and worse — that looking at her, hearing her certainty, I felt my own memory of the Shepherd and the Nine Sisters go briefly slippery, like a name on the tip of the tongue that retreats when you reach for it.

I put my hand flat against the grimoire beneath my coat, and the memory steadied. The warmth of the book ran through my palm and up my arm, and the stars came back to me, all their names, bright and exact.

The villagers watched me do it. Sorrel's eyes fixed on my chest, on the place where my hand pressed the coat, and something moved through the room — not hope exactly, but hunger's cousin, the shape hope makes when it has been starving a long time.

"What have you got there?" the old woman asked softly.

"Nothing," I said, and stood, and knocked over my stool, and left.

* * *

I walked fast after that. The road south followed the stream down through country that grew steadily stranger, and I use *strange* precisely: not frightening, at first, just incorrect, the way a dream is incorrect. Milestones gave distances to towns I couldn't find and omitted the village I'd just left. A bridge crossed a stream that, on the far side, became a different stream entirely, flowing from a different direction, and neither version seemed aware of the other. Once I passed a crossroads where four roads met, and stood there a full minute trying to count them, because every time I looked away and back, one of them had shifted its bearing slightly, like a compass needle sulking.

The land itself was thinning. I don't know how else to say it. Where the pooled dark lay thickest, grass grew gray-green and generic, blades without variety, a lawn printed from a single stencil. A hill ahead of me changed height between one glance and the next. Birds flew overhead and I found I could not afterward say what kind they'd been — not that I lacked the knowledge, but that the birds themselves seemed to resist being known, slipping out of memory like fish out of wet hands.

And always, underneath, that pressure. The dark leaned. All through that day it leaned on me harder and harder, and I noticed a pattern, and the pattern frightened me more than anything yet: the dark leaned hardest on the things I loved best. When I thought of the valley, of the tower, of Mara's voice saying *names are how the sky holds itself together* — the memory dimmed, softened, went distant, like a letter read through fogged glass. When I thought of nothing in particular, of bread, of boot leather, of the road, the dark ignored me.

It wasn't eating light. Light was incidental. The stars going out hadn't been the disaster; they'd been the *symptom*. The dark ate meaning. It drank the names out of things, and when a thing had no name left, the dark owned it completely, and the thing faded to whatever the dark cared to leave — a shape, a smell, a smooth unfinished blank.

I understood, walking that road with my hand pressed constantly to the grimoire like a man holding a wound closed, why the valley had wards. Not to keep the dark out. To keep the *names* in. The valley was a jar with its lid on, and the lid had just come off, and everything inside it — every child's name, every grave, every recipe and grudge and lullaby — was now sitting in the open air of a world that drank such things.

And I had run *toward* this. I had stolen the one book that might matter and run straight out the broken door, carrying it like a lantern, like a bell tolling *here, here, here*.

* * *

Near dusk — real dusk, the sun still setting in the west, the one honest light left — I came down to the river.

It was wide and slow and brown-gold, and on its bank stood a ferry: a flat barge moored to a post, a rope strung across the current sagging between two poles. Beside the landing stood a hut with a stovepipe, and outside the hut, splitting wood with slow mechanical strokes, stood the ferryman.

He was older than my father would have been, broad as a door, with a beard gone to iron-gray and hands like root balls. He saw me coming and set down his axe, and I opened my mouth to ask about crossing, about the road south, about anywhere that might be less wrong than here —

and he raised one finger to his lips.

Then he pointed, deliberately, at his own mouth, and shook his head.

"You can't speak," I said. He nodded. "Since when?"

His face did something complicated then. Grief, and embarrassment, and a strange sheepish patience, all at once. He held up both hands, fingers spread, and folded them down one at a time — counting — and when he ran out of fingers he started again, and again, and I realized with a cold slide in my stomach that he was counting days, and that he had run out of numbers, and that he did not know how long it had been because counting requires remembering where you began.

He gave up on the arithmetic. Instead he took me by the sleeve — gently, the way you take a child — and walked me ten paces up the bank, and pointed across the river.

On the far side, the fields continued. There was a lane, a hedgerow, a distant chimney smoking. Ordinary. Then he turned my shoulders with both hands and pointed back the way I had come, north, up-valley, toward the mountains and the broken wards and everything I had crawled out of the world to escape.

"Back?" I said. "You want me to go back?"

He nodded, emphatic, and then he crouched, and drew in the dirt with one blunt finger. He drew a circle. Inside the circle he drew small figures — stick-people, a stick-dog, rows of little marks that might have been crops. Then around the circle he drew a second line, a ring, and tapped it twice: the wards, the shining ring I had watched die.

Then, slowly, watching my face the whole time, he dragged his palm across the drawing, smearing the inner circle — the people, the dog, the crops — into a gray blur. Nothing left. Just the ring, empty, and the dark pressing in around it.

"That's what happens if they stay broken," I said. "If the names get out."

He nodded. His eyes were wet. He wiped them with the back of his wrist, impatient, and drew again: the circle, the ring — and this time he added a small mark at the center of the circle, a dot, a point, and looked at me with an expression of fierce, exhausted significance, tapping the dot once, twice, three times.

I didn't understand. I said so. He made a sound of frustration deep in his chest, and then he did the strangest thing of that entire terrible day: he reached out and laid his huge rough hand flat against my chest, over the grimoire, over the warmth pulsing there like a second heart.

And the warmth answered him.

I felt it happen — felt the book lean into his palm the way a cat leans into a known hand, felt a pulse pass between them, and the ferryman's eyes went wide and filled, and he stepped back and pressed both fists to his mouth, and shook his head, and shook it, and pointed at me, and pointed north, and mouthed words he had no breath to make: *take it back. Take it back.*

"How do you know what it is?" I demanded. "How do you know what I'm carrying?"

He couldn't answer. Of course he couldn't. He could only stand there in the failing light with his fists against his mouth, a man full to bursting with the most important knowledge in the drowning world and no way to pour out a drop of it — and I understood suddenly that this was what the Hollow did, that taking your words was only the beginning, that somewhere inside that mute careful giant was a man who remembered having a name, having children perhaps, having a life, and remembered it the way you remember a dream after waking: perfectly, and uselessly, and fading.

Behind him, across the river, the pooled dark lay in the hedgerows like floodwater rising.

I looked north, at the mountains going blue-black under a sky with no stars, at the gap in the ring where the wards had torn, at everything I had betrayed and abandoned and run from. I looked south, at the road that forgot where it led.

The ferryman took his fists from his mouth. Slowly, deliberately, he pointed one last time — not at me, not at the mountains, but at the grimoire, at the shape of it beneath my coat, and then he pressed that pointing finger against his own heart, and held it there, and looked at me with an expression I would carry the rest of my life:

*Whatever you think you stole,* that look said, *it was never yours to run away with.*

Up-valley, faint on the wind, I heard — or thought I heard — a voice calling a name into the dark. Calling it the way Sorrel had called for Tamsin. Frayed. Hopeful. Getting closer.

It sounded, impossibly, like my own name.

And it sounded like hers.
