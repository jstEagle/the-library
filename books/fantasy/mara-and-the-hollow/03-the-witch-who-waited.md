## 3. The Witch Who Waited

Mara Quintana had been waiting at the ruined wardstone since the stars went out.

Not because she had guessed he would come — though she had, hours before the sky broke, when she had watched Bram's shadow slide along the wall of her cottage with a shape too careful to be innocent. She had waited because there was nowhere else to go. The wards were dying, and the only place in the valley where their death could be felt plainly, like a fever through a palm pressed to a brow, was here: the First Stone, the anchor-root of everything she had spent thirty years pretending was merely a fence against wolves and weather.

She sat on the flat crown of the wardstone with her boots off, her bare feet against the cold granite, and she listened to it die.

It did not die loudly. That was the horror of it. A ward of this size should have gone out like a struck bell, a sound that would crack crockery in every kitchen from the mill to the high pastures. Instead it went out the way a name goes out of an old woman's mouth — one syllable at a time, each one softer than the last, until what remained was not silence exactly but the absence where sound had learned to live. The stone under her feet had been warm for as long as anyone could remember, warm even in January, warm as a sleeping animal. Now it was cooling by degrees, and she could feel each degree leave, the way you feel a hand loosen its grip on yours in a crowd.

Above her, where the stars had been, there was nothing. Not cloud. Not mist. Cloud has texture; mist has weight. This was a smoothness, a black so complete it seemed less like night than like the memory of night, described to someone who had never seen it. The valley's other lights still burned — hearth-glow in the low town, the lantern at the ferry crossing, the moon doing its poor best — but they looked suddenly small and provisional, candles in a house whose owner had forgotten to pay the rent.

Mara counted her breathing. In for four. Hold for two. Out for six. The old hedge-craft rhythm, the one she taught apprentices in their first week, back when she still took them, back when she had taken *him*.

*You knew,* she told herself, and the thought had no comfort in it. *You have known for eleven years. You knew the night you pulled the grimoire down from the shelf and felt the wards lean toward it like wheat toward sun.*

She heard him coming long before she saw him. He had never learned to walk quietly in the dark — not truly quietly, not the way the old ones walked, weight rolled heel-to-toe, breath swallowed into the ribs. Bram walked the way he did everything: earnestly, hurriedly, apologizing to the ground with every second step. She heard him stumble on the sheep-track, curse under his breath, catch himself on a hawthorn whose thorns she had charmed soft years ago and which now, with the wards failing, pricked him sharply enough to make him yelp.

That yelp nearly undid her. Eleven years of his voice in her house — asking questions, mispronouncing cantrips, singing badly while he chopped herbs — and it came down to this: a boy yelping at a hawthorn in the dark, running away from her with a stolen book against his chest.

She did not turn around. She let him climb the last stretch of the track, let him see her sitting on the stone with her back to him, a small figure in a grey shawl, barefoot on the dying heart of the world. Let him stand there in the terrible starless dark and decide, for three full seconds, whether to run.

He decided wrong.

"Mara." His voice cracked in the middle of her name. "I can explain."

"Can you." She said it flatly, to the horizon, to the smooth black nothing where Cassiopeia should have been. "Go on, then. Explain. I have nothing but time. The wards are dead, the stars are eaten, and my apprentice is standing behind me holding forty years of my life in a goatskin bag. Explain."

The silence behind her had a texture. Guilt has a texture — she had felt it often enough in her own chest to know its grain, rough as uncarded wool. She heard him shift his weight. Heard the bag rustle. Heard him take a breath to speak, and then another, because the first one hadn't held.

"You knew," he said finally, and it wasn't an explanation at all. It was an accusation, which was so perfectly Bram that she almost laughed. "You knew I took it. You let me take it."

Now she turned.

The moon gave her just enough light to see him by, and what she saw hurt worse than fury would have. He was still in his nightshirt, half-tucked into trousers thrown on in panic, his coat over it all buttoned wrong. His hair stood up where he'd slept on it. He had the grimoire clutched to his chest with both arms, the way children hold bread in famine stories, and his eyes were wet and wide and utterly unprepared for the world that had ended while he slept.

He looked sixteen. He was twenty-three. She had known him since he was twelve years old and had set fire to her herb garden trying to charm the slugs out of it.

"Yes," she said. "I knew."

* * *

She made him sit. It was an old habit — when a lesson was hard, you put the student down on something solid first, because hard lessons knocked people off their feet and it was better to be sitting when they landed. There was a fallen menhir near the wardstone, older than the wards themselves, mossed green on its upper face. She pointed at it and he sat, obedient even now, even fleeing, and that obedience twisted something in her that she refused to look at directly.

"Tell me why," she said. "And don't tell me you wanted to see the world. I've known you wanted to see the world since you were thirteen and drew maps of the lowlands on my table in charcoal. That's not a reason tonight. Tonight the reason matters."

"Because I couldn't breathe here anymore." It came out of him fast, like blood from a cut. "Every day the same valley, the same fences, the same nine villages and their nine hundred problems — a cow with a cursed udder, a bride who wants a love-charm, old Tammas wanting to talk to his dead wife again and me having to tell him no, again. And you —" He stopped. Started again, gentler, which was worse. "You never went anywhere either. Thirty years in this valley and you never once crossed the line, and I kept thinking, if I stay, that's me. That's me in thirty years. The whole world out there and we're guarding a fence around nine hundred people who barely notice the fence exists."

"The fence," Mara said, "is the only reason those nine hundred people exist."

"I know that *now*." His voice broke upward. "I didn't — I thought it was old magic. Maintenance magic. I thought — gods, Mara, I thought the worst thing the book could do was make me rich in a city somewhere."

"And instead?"

"Instead the sky went out." He looked up at the smooth darkness and she watched the understanding move through him like cold water through cloth. "The moment I crossed the boundary line. The wards fell and the sky went out, and the two things are the same thing, aren't they. They're the same thing."

"They are the same thing," Mara said. "Sit with that a moment. You're better at hard truths sitting down."

He sat with it. She watched his hands, which had always been his honest part — clever hands, ink-stained, quick — tighten on the goatskin bag until the knives of his knuckles showed white.

"How long," he said. "How long have you known what the book is?"

"Since before you were born." She lowered herself onto the menhir beside him, because her knees were fifty-four years old and the night was long. "My teacher was a woman named Odile Ferran, and she was the keeper before me, and her teacher before her, all the way back to the founding. We are not hedge-witches, Bram. We never were. There is no such thing as a hedge-witch. There is only the Order of the Keystone, and we are what's left of it, and our entire purpose — the entire purpose of every witch in this valley since the valley was founded — is to keep one book safe and to keep one question unasked."

"What question?"

"*What happens if the book leaves?*" She let it hang in the cold air. "Eleven generations of women memorizing that question and passing it down like a lit coal. Because the grimoire isn't a spellbook. It looks like one — half of it is, herbs and charms and the little domestic magics I've taught you, all real, all fine. But underneath, sewn into the binding, written on pages that only show themselves to the keystone-bearer — underneath, it is the lock. The wards aren't a fence, Bram. The wards are a seal. And the book is the keyhole."

Bram was very quiet. When he spoke, his voice had gone thin. "A seal against what? Wolves don't eat stars."

"No," Mara agreed. "They don't."

* * *

She told him. She had rehearsed telling him a hundred times across eleven years — in the garden, at the hearth, walking the ward-line in autumn — and every rehearsal had failed, because there was no way to say it that didn't sound like madness or myth. So she said it plainly, the way Odile had said it to her, kneeling by this same stone with snow coming down.

"There was a court once. Before the founding. Before names were written down everywhere and taken for granted. It lived in the spaces between remembering — in the pause when you walk into a room and forget why, in the fog after a dream, in the moment on the stairs when your own childhood goes strange and far away. It fed on memory. Not thoughts — memories. Names, faces, the particular blue of a particular door. And it grew, the way anything grows that eats without being eaten, until whole regions woke up unable to say who they'd married. The old kingdoms fought it. They lost. You cannot fight a thing that makes you forget you're fighting it — armies turned around halfway to the battle, kings signed treaties they couldn't remember signing.

"So the survivors did the only thing left. They found the Court's hunger a single door and locked it, and they built a valley around the lock and filled the valley with ordinary life — cows and weddings and slugs in gardens — because the seal runs on attention. On being unnoticed. A ward nobody thinks about is a ward at full strength. Every generation of keepers has known that the price of the seal is smallness. We stay. We mend. We do not cross the line, and above all we do not let the key wander."

The words came out of her steady and cold, and inside she was shaking, because saying it aloud made it true in a way eleven years of silence had not.

Bram stared at her. "The ancient war. The one in the ballads. The one about the King Under the Hill and the—"

"The ballads are the war," Mara said, "with the teeth filed off. Songs survive forgetting better than chronicles do. Rhythm is an anchor. Why do you think I made you sing the ward-litany every morning, badly, while you complained? It wasn't ritual, Bram. It was maintenance."

He looked down at the bag in his lap. "So when I carried it over the line—"

"You unlocked a seal that has held for four hundred years, and the thing it sealed began to pour through, and the first thing it ate was the sky, because the stars are the oldest memory the world has. Everything else forgets. Stars remember. They burn the same fire they burned when the first creature looked up and gave them names. The Hollow eats outward from the newest memories to the oldest, and when it reaches the stars—" She stopped. Made herself finish. "When it reaches the stars, it doesn't stop. There's nothing older. It starts eating backward through time itself. People won't just forget their children. Their children will never have been."

The wind moved through the hawthorn behind them, and with the wards down it sounded different — thinner, somehow, as if the wind itself had fewer things to say.

"Why didn't you stop me?" Bram asked, and his voice was small. "You saw me take it. You said you watched me. One word, Mara. One word and I'd have put it back and hated you for a year and gotten over it. Why let me steal the keystone of the world?"

And there it was. The question she had been waiting for, dreading, the one she had no clean answer for, because the clean answer was monstrous and the true answer was worse.

"Because I couldn't carry it alone anymore," she said.

* * *

She stood up while she said the rest, because some things could not be said sitting down, and walked to the edge of the wardstone's crown and looked out at the valley she had guarded for thirty years. The hearth-lights. The ferry lantern. Nine hundred lives going about their morning unaware that the morning was borrowed.

"Do you know what the keeping costs?" she said. "Not the danger. The danger I could do forever. It's the *smallness*. Odile warned me and I didn't believe her. Thirty years, Bram. Thirty years of mending charms and lying to Tammas about his wife and watching the road south and knowing — *knowing* — that the world out there was eating itself alive and I was the only one who understood why, and I could tell no one, and I could do nothing, because the whole duty was to sit still. To be a doorstop. Do you know what it does to a person, to be a doorstop for thirty years?

"The Court is getting stronger. It has been for a decade — I've felt it through the stone, little tugs, little tastes, like something testing a jar lid. The seal was built by a hundred witches at the height of the art, and it has been maintained by one tired woman and her apprentice for a generation. It was going to fail, Bram. Soon. Maybe this winter, maybe in ten years, but soon, and when it failed I would be here alone, fifty-four years old, with a book I could read and no one strong enough to carry it where it needs to go."

"Where it needs to go," he repeated slowly.

"The Starwell." She turned to look at him. "There is a place where the seal was first cast. The wellspring of it. If the wards fall, the seal can be remade there — or better than remade. The founders left a door in the design, in case the worst happened. But the book must be carried there, through country that the Hollow has already begun to unmake, by someone the book will answer. And I am not sure that's me anymore. And I am certain it cannot be me alone."

"So you gambled." His voice had gone strange — not angry yet, but circling anger the way a hawk circles. "You gambled *me*. You let me rob you and run, because you knew I'd run straight at the disaster, and then you'd follow, and then — what? You'd have your carrier. Your strong young pair of legs. You used me."

"Yes," Mara said.

He flinched as if she'd struck him. She let him. She owed him that much honesty, at least — more honesty than she had ever given him, all at once, on the worst night of both their lives.

"I could have asked you," she went on. "I drafted the speech a hundred times. *Bram, the book is the keystone, the world depends on it, will you help me carry it to the Starwell.* And do you know what you would have said? You'd have said yes. Of course you'd have said yes — you'd have said yes with that look you get, the dutiful one, the one that breaks my heart, and you'd have come because you were asked, and you'd have resented it your whole life, and the resentment would have leaked into the work like rot into timber. A keystone-bearer who feels like a prisoner guards a prison. I needed someone who chose the road." She laughed, short and bitter. "Or someone who thought he chose it. Gods forgive me, Bram, I needed you to steal it. I needed your want. It's the strongest thing about you — stronger than your craft, stronger than your sense. You wanted out of this valley so badly you glowed. And I took that want and I aimed it."

"That's not choosing," he said hoarsely. "If you rig the choice, that's not—"

"No," she agreed. "It isn't. Welcome to the family trade. Every keeper for eleven generations has done arithmetic like this with other people's lives. Now it's your turn to learn how much it weighs."

He was silent a long time. Above them the smooth dark held its terrible patience. Down in the valley, absurdly, a dog barked twice and stopped, and the ordinariness of it was almost unbearable.

"If I put the book back," he said at last, "on the stone. Right now. Would the wards come back?"

Mara shook her head. "The seal is broken, not unlocked. Putting the key back in a shattered lock mends nothing. The only road now runs forward, to the Starwell. There is no home version of this, Bram. There is no putting it back."

"Then I'll carry it." He said it to the bag, not to her, his jaw tight. "Not because you gamed me into it. Because the sky is gone and I'm the one who — " He stopped. Swallowed. "Because it's mine to carry now. That part's true whether you planned it or not."

Something in Mara's chest loosened by a single degree — the first degree of warmth since the stars had gone out. She smothered it immediately. Warmth was for afterward, if there was an afterward.

"Then we do this properly," she said, climbing down from the menhir. "Rules. First: the book stays between us, literally — one strap over each of your shoulders and one over mine whenever we sleep. Second: you take instruction now without argument. Everything you thought you knew about the craft is a child's version. Third—" She hesitated. This rule was for herself, and she said it anyway, because saying it might make it hold. "Third: no more secrets between us about the book. Either of us. I have spent eleven years keeping secrets from you and it has bought the world exactly nothing. We lie to everyone else. Never to each other. Agreed?"

He looked at her for a long moment, and in the moonless dark his eyes were just two catches of light, and she could not read them, and she realized with a small cold shock that she had never quite been able to read them, and that perhaps he had learned hiding from watching her.

"Agreed," he said.

She held out her hand — the ward-hand, the left, scarred across the palm from the binding-oath she'd taken at nineteen. He took it. His grip was warm and steady and younger than hers by thirty years, and together, hand to hand over the goatskin bag, they stood on the cooling heart of the valley while the first grey suggestion of dawn crept up behind the eastern hills — dawn without a single star above it to fade.

"We walk tonight," Mara said. "Sleep by day, move by dark. The Hollow hunts remembered shapes, and daylight makes us too visible. South, then east, along the river to the ferry crossing — there's a man there who owes me, and a boat, and beyond the river the lowlands where we'll learn quickly what we're walking into."

"The ferryman," Bram said. "Old silent fellow, scarred up his arms?"

"You know him?"

"He wouldn't take my coin last night." Bram's voice dropped. "Mara — he grabbed my wrist when I got off the boat. He couldn't talk, but he kept pointing back at the mountains. Kept pointing until I walked away. Like he was trying to warn me about something."

Mara went still. The ferryman — Doss, his name had been, before — had been mute for six years, struck dumb and half-blank by a fever the year the Court's tugging had first begun. She had told herself it was fever. She had told herself many things.

"He wasn't warning you about something," she said quietly. "He was warning you about *someone*. The Court doesn't only eat, Bram. Sometimes it sends things out ahead of it. Things wearing shapes it remembers." She looked south, toward the river, toward the dark water and the lantern burning small and stubborn at the crossing. "Get your coat straightened. Eat something. We have until full dark, and then we find out what the world outside has become — and what, if anything, is already following us."

Behind them, unseen by either of them, the ruined wardstone gave a final faint tick of cooling stone, like a clock running down — and far to the south, at the edge of hearing, something in the river reeds began, very softly, to hum a tune that neither of them had ever been taught, and could not possibly have forgotten, because neither of them had ever known it at all.
