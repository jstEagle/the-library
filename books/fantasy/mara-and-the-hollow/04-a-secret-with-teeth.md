## 4. A Secret With Teeth

The grimoire did not want to be read.

Bram had carried stolen books before — a psalter from the shrine at Lowmark, a herbalist's folio with pages soft as moth wings — and every one of them had opened for him like a hand unclenching. This one resisted. The cover was grey leather gone smooth as riverstone, cold to the touch even inside his coat, against his chest where it rode like a second heartbeat. When he finally laid it on the flat stone beside the dead fire and thumbed back the clasp, the first page showed him nothing at all: blank vellum, pale as skimmed milk, staring up at him with the patience of something that had waited a very long time and could wait longer.

"You're joking," he told it.

The valley behind him was a bruise on the horizon, a smudge of green under a sky with no stars in it. He had walked most of the night, downhill through pine shadow, following the old drover's road until the trees thinned and the land opened into the lowlands — and then he had seen what the lowlands were now, and he had run, and run, until his lungs burned and the book hammered against his ribs like a trapped bird. Now he crouched in the lee of a fallen watchtower, hands shaking, talking to a blank book like a madman.

He tried again. Turned past the empty first leaf, the second, the third. Blank, blank, blank. Then, somewhere around the tenth turning, he felt it — a faint tug behind his eyes, the sensation of a hook finding flesh.

*What do you remember of your mother's voice?*

Bram went still. The question hadn't arrived as words. It had arrived the way a memory arrives, unbidden, whole, smelling of bread and woodsmoke — except he hadn't summoned it. Something else had. And when he reached, instinctively, for that memory, to hold it close, it *shifted*, sliding sideways like a fish in shallow water, and the blank page before him shivered, and a single line of ink bloomed across the vellum as if rising through water:

*Payment is memory. Reading is spending.*

He slammed the book shut so hard the sound cracked off the tower stones and went skittering away over the grass.

For a long while he just breathed. A lark sang somewhere above the ruined tower, absurdly ordinary, and Bram clung to the sound of it the way a drowning man clings to driftwood. His master's voice came back to him unasked — Mara, dry as chalk dust: *Nothing is given, apprentice. Everything is traded. Learn the price of things before you learn their uses.* He had thought she meant herbs and charms. He had thought she meant coin.

He thought about his mother's voice. It was still there. He checked — carefully, the way you probe a tooth — and it was there, bread and woodsmoke and all. Whatever the book had done, it had only *shown* him the shape of the price. Not taken. Not yet.

"Right," he said aloud, because the silence was worse than speaking. "But you'll show me the true pages if I pay."

The book said nothing, which he chose to take as yes.

* * *

He paid small, at first. That was the trick of it, he realized — or the trap. The book accepted small memories the way a moneylender accepts coppers: without comment, without mercy, recording each transaction in ink that surfaced like bruises. A memory of the taste of plum wine at the harvest fair, two years gone. Gone now; he knew there had been a taste and could not find it, a socket where a tooth had been. A memory of falling out of the walnut tree at nine years old and the specific white shock of the sky spinning. Spent. A memory of the name of the boy who'd dared him to climb it — spent, and with it, unsettlingly, the boy himself, reduced to a dare with no darer, a gap shaped like a friend.

Each payment bought him a page. And each page made the last night of the valley make sense, and each page made it worse.

The wards were not walls. That was the first thing. He had grown up inside them the way a fish grows up inside water, never once thinking about the water, and Mara had taught him ward-craft as she taught him everything — precisely, impatiently, with the assumption that he would one day need it more than she had. He had always imagined the border wards as a fence: something built to keep the world's dangers out of the valley's soft interior. Every child in the valley learned the rhyme. *The wards keep the dark, the dark keeps away, sleep safe till morning, sleep safe till day.*

The rhyme was a lie told to children by people who had forgotten they were lying.

The true pages called the wards by another name: *the Seal of Quiet*. They had not been raised to protect the valley. They had been raised to protect the world *from* the valley — no. Bram read the passage four times, tracing the archaic script with a fingertip, feeling the words resist meaning the way a knot resists untying. Not from the valley. From what slept beneath it. The wards were a lid. The valley was a pot. And the fire under the pot had been burning since before the rhyme, since before the wards, since before —

Since the war. The ancient war, the one every valley child learned as fable: the war of the Star Kings, fought so long ago that its battles had worn down into nursery stories, its heroes into constellations. Except the constellations were gone now, weren't they? Every star above the valley had gone out on the night the wards broke, all at once, like candles snuffed by a single breath. He had thought it was an omen. He had thought it was grief.

It was a ledger being settled.

The Hollow Court had not been defeated in the ancient war. It had been *starved*. That was what the stars were — he read it three times to be sure, and the book seemed almost eager now, ink flowing faster the deeper he went, as though it had been waiting centuries for someone foolish enough to pay its toll. The stars were names. Each one a name the Court had tried to eat and the world had refused to surrender — pulled up out of reach, hung in the sky where nothing without wings of light could follow, kept burning as proof and promise both. *As long as the stars burn, the eaten are not digested,* the page said. *As long as the stars burn, the Court goes hungry, and hunger remembers its chains.*

And the Court had spent all the long centuries since learning patience. Leaking through cracks. Wearing down the Seal of Quiet grain by grain, the way water wears stone, waiting for some fool to carry the keystone out of the lock.

Bram looked down at the book in his lap and felt the full weight of it settle onto him at last — not the physical weight, which was nothing, less than nothing, a book should weigh more than this — but the other kind. The kind Mara must have carried every day of her life without ever once letting him see her stoop.

The keystone. The grimoire wasn't a record of the wards. It was the wards' heart, cut out and bound in grey leather, the way you bind anything that might otherwise walk off on its own. Every ward-stone in the valley's border was a finger pointing at this book. Pull the keystone, and the fingers uncurl. The lid comes off the pot.

He had done it. He had walked out through the border on the very night the stars died, clutching the thing under his coat like treasure, and he had thought the darkness following him was punishment for theft.

It was simpler than that. It was appetite.

* * *

By the time the sun stood at its height, Bram had stopped reading for pleasure, if he had ever been reading for pleasure, and started reading the way a man reads a diagnosis. There was a section — pages bought with the memory of his father teaching him to whistle, a small price, a cheap one, he told himself, and hated how easily the rationalization came — titled in the old script *On Beacons and Their Bearing*. He almost skipped it. He didn't.

A keystone, the book explained, in the maddeningly patient tone of all its true pages, does not stop being a keystone when it leaves the lock. It remains what it is: the point toward which every part of the broken structure still leans. The Seal of Quiet was shattered, but shattered is not destroyed; the fragments still remembered their center, the way a broken mirror remembers the face. And the Court — hungry, patient, blind in the normal way but seeing perfectly in the way that mattered — could feel that lean. Could feel the pull of every fragment toward the grey-bound heart of the wards.

Carrying the grimoire was not hiding from the Hollow Court. Carrying the grimoire was standing on a hilltop at midnight holding a lit torch and wondering why the wolves kept coming.

Bram closed the book gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping animal, and sat for a moment with his palms flat on the warm stone. The lark was still singing. Below the ruined tower, the lowlands stretched away south, golden-green in the afternoon light, dotted with villages whose chimney smoke rose straight and peaceful into the starless sky. It looked like home. It looked like anywhere. Two nights ago he would have looked at that view and felt the old ache, the hunger for beyond, the conviction that his life was happening somewhere else, always somewhere else, and the valley was just the anteroom.

Now he knew what lived in the anteroom.

He thought of the ferryman who couldn't speak. Of the woman in the first village who had smiled at him with wet eyes and asked, politely, kindly, whether he had seen her son, describing him in loving detail — and then, mid-sentence, forgetting why she was crying. Of the road that had unspooled behind him like thread off a spindle, so that the way back was not hidden but *unmade*, and he had walked forward into country that grew less certain with every mile, hedgerows going vague at their edges, hills soft-focus, the world itself starting to forget its own outlines.

That was what the Court ate. Not light. Light was just what it looked like from inside the valley, where people had mistaken the wards' glow for protection and the stars' burning for beauty. The Court ate *memory* — and not the way a thief steals, taking the thing and leaving the room. The way a fire eats, leaving the room itself changed, hollowed, structurally unsound. The villages weren't dark. They were *eaten*. The people in them still walked and worked and smiled, because the body remembers its habits longer than the mind remembers its reasons, but the names were gone, and the griefs, and the reasons, and what remained was a house with all the furniture carried out, beautiful and bare and echoing.

And he was carrying the one object in the world that every hungry thing in that country could smell.

"Why," Bram said to the book, quietly, "did she let me take you?"

The book, naturally, said nothing. But the question wouldn't leave. Mara Quintana missed nothing — not an unlatched gate, not a lie told badly, not an apprentice's eyes lingering too long on the iron-bound chest in the workroom's corner. He had rehearsed the theft for a month and she had never once turned her back at the right moment by accident. She had known. She had *let* him. And she had let him go on the night the wards fell, which meant she had known what night it would be, which meant she had known what the book was, which meant —

Which meant she had sent him out of the valley carrying the keystone like a lantern, into a country full of the starving, alone, ignorant, armed with nothing but hedge-craft and arrogance.

Or she had gambled that he would do exactly what he'd done: run, panic, open the book, pay its prices, and learn in one terrible afternoon what she had apparently known for years and never told him. Because telling him would have made him refuse to take it. And someone had to take it. Somewhere in those true pages — he was sure of it now, sure the way you're sure of a splinter in your skin — there was a reason the keystone needed to travel. A direction. A destination. The book had shown him fragments: a well, a mountain, a place where the first star had been hung and might be hung again. But those pages were expensive, and his pockets of memory were not bottomless, and the afternoon was dying.

He repacked the book against his chest, and felt it pulse once against his ribs — or imagined he did — like something satisfied.

Then he heard the horses. No — not horses. He heard the *absence* of sound coming up the drover's road behind him, a silence moving with purpose, eating birdsong as it came, the lark cutting off mid-phrase as though a hand had closed over the whole sky. The golden light didn't dim. It simply became uninteresting, drained of the quality that makes light worth looking at, and Bram understood with a lurch that this was what it looked like when something that fed on remembering came near enough to touch the edge of your mind.

He ran. There was no strategy in it, no craft; he was an apprentice with a stolen god in his coat and his heels suddenly understood everything his head had been slow to learn. Behind him, at the edge of hearing, something that was not a voice said — courteously, distinctly, in the tone of a herald announcing dinner —

"You're carrying what belongs to the Court, little thief. And we are *so* hungry."

The book burned cold against his heart, and every fragment of the broken Seal leaned toward it in the dark, and Bram Quintana ran west, toward the mountains, toward the well he half-remembered from pages he'd already spent, carrying the only light left in a country that had forgotten what light was for.
