## 5. The Ferryman's Name

The river found us before we found it.

We had been walking since the grey hour, down out of the foothills where the burned forest still smoked at its edges, and all that time I had felt the water the way you feel a tooth you know is going to ache — a low, patient wrongness somewhere ahead of us in the mist. When it finally appeared through the reeds, wide and slow and the color of old pewter, I understood why. Rivers remember. That is their whole nature: they remember every stone they have worn smooth, every bank they have abandoned, every name ever shouted across them from a ferry deck. And this one was trying very hard to forget.

The current moved strangely, in long hesitations, as if the water kept starting a thought and losing it. Reeds stood along the near shore in perfect ranks and then, twenty paces on, simply stopped mid-growth, their stalks ending in nothing, as though someone had drawn a line and said *no further*. On the far bank, a village should have been — I could see chimneys, roofs, the pale rectangles of windows — but no smoke rose from any hearth, and no dog barked, and no light burned, though the hour was one when even the poorest house keeps a candle against the dark.

Bram stopped beside me at the reed-line and pulled his cloak tighter. He had not spoken more than a dozen words since we left the ash road, and most of those had been apologies shaped like questions. *Are you all right. Did you sleep. Should we turn back.* As if there were a back to turn to. As if the valley were anything now but a wound with a fence around it.

"He's here," I said.

"Who?"

"The ferryman." I could feel him the way I had felt him three days ago, when Bram and I first crossed this water fleeing the broken wards — a presence like a held breath. "He never leaves the boat."

* * *

We found him moored at the rotted jetty, exactly where memory said he would be, and that was the cruelest part of it. The Hollow takes names first — I had learned that much watching the lowlands come apart — but it is not greedy the way fire is greedy. It does not consume. It *subtracts*, cleanly, leaving the shape of a thing standing in the space where the thing used to be. The man who sat in the stern of his flat-bottomed ferry was still a man: broad hands, weathered face, a wool coat gone soft with decades of river fog. His eyes were open. They tracked us as we came down the jetty planks. And there was nothing behind them. Not emptiness — emptiness would have been a mercy, an absence with clean edges. What looked out of his face was the *outline* of a person, the way a glove looks like a hand.

Bram crouched at the edge of the boat, careful not to touch it. "Sir? Do you remember us? We crossed here. Three days ago. You took us over — you pointed back at the mountains when—"

The ferryman's head turned toward the sound of his voice with terrible attentiveness, the way a sunflower turns toward light it cannot feel. His mouth opened. Nothing came out. His mouth opened again, working, straining, and what emerged was not silence exactly but the *ghost* of speech — a breath shaped around the place where words go, a hollow hush like wind through a doorway.

I had seen it before, in the villages. Mothers mouthing at children they could no longer name. Priests conducting rites whose meanings had been eaten out from under the motions, hands moving through blessings that blessed nothing. But it was different, watching it happen to someone whose face I knew. Three days ago this man had gripped my wrist to steady me into the boat, and he had looked at Bram with an expression I had recognized too well — the look of a man seeing a boy run away from something he shouldn't, and remembering, perhaps, having once been that boy himself.

"Bram," I said quietly. "Give me the book."

* * *

He hesitated. Of course he hesitated. He had been carrying the grimoire against his chest for two days like a stolen child, and every mile he loved it more and trusted me less, and I could not entirely blame him for either. He thought I wanted it back because it was mine. He did not yet understand — how could he — that I wanted it because I was afraid of what it was doing to him, and more afraid of what I might have to do with it, and most afraid of the thing I had already decided to do and had told no one, least of all myself.

"The book," I said again, and held out my hand, and after a moment he passed it over, and our fingers touched on the cover's leather, warm from his body, and neither of us pulled away as quickly as we should have.

The grimoire is not beautiful. People expect a keystone to be beautiful — gilt corners, star-charts, a spine that sings. Mine was a workman's book, bound in brown calf gone bald at the corners, swollen with a century of marginalia in six different hands. My mother's hand was in it. Her mother's before her. Every witch of the Quintana line had added her page, her corrections, her warnings, until the thing had become less a text than a sediment — layers upon layers of women writing *do not do this* above *I did this anyway*. Somewhere in its middle pages, pressed flat beneath everything else, was the true purpose of the valley wards, written by the first of us in ink that had gone the color of dried blood: that the wards were not raised to keep the Hollow Court out, but to keep the world's grief *in* — sealed, contained, given a shape and a border so it could not spread. And that the seal required a living keeper. And that the keeper's book was the key. And that if the key walked out of the valley of its own free will—

Well. It had.

But the grimoire held more than seals. My grandmother's pages held the counter-craft, the hedge-magic older than the Court itself: the law of names. A thing eaten by the Hollow is not destroyed; it is *stored*, folded into the dark like linen into a chest. And a name, spoken truly, by someone willing to pay, can be a hook to draw it back out.

I found the ferryman's page by touch, in the dark, without opening my eyes. That is the other thing about the book. It knows its keepers. Under my thumb the pages turned themselves past wards and weather-charms, past the birth-records of goats and the death-records of neighbors, past my own apprentice contract with Bram Quintana's signature crooked at the bottom like a bird with a broken wing — and stopped at a page I had read a hundred times without reading.

There was no name written there. There was a *space* where a name had been, a pale rectangle in the ink like frost on a window, and around the space the old instructions curled in my grandmother's hand: *When the river-man gives up his voice to the crossing-oath, write it here, for a name given away can be borrowed back only by one who pays in kind.*

The ferryman had given his name to the river. That was the old covenant — the crossing-oath, older than the wards, older than the Court: the ferryman surrenders his name to the water he serves, and the water holds it safe, and in exchange the river remembers for him, carries him, keeps his boat upright through floods and ice. The Hollow had not eaten his name. It had eaten everything *around* the name — his mother's face, his trade's pride, sixty years of mornings — until the name sat alone in him like a coin at the bottom of a drained well, untouched because unfindable.

But the book could find it. The book remembered the space where it lived.

"Mara," Bram said. He had come to stand at my shoulder, and his voice had that careful quality it took on whenever he watched me work, half awe and half fear, as though I were something that might bite. "What are you going to do?"

"What your master trained you to watch," I said, "and what you ran away before you learned." I heard the bitterness come out sharper than I intended. Two days of walking beside him had not softened it; it had only taught me to aim it elsewhere, mostly at myself. "Stand back. Whatever happens, don't speak. Don't say my name, don't say yours, don't say *stop*. Names are currency tonight, and I won't have you spending any."

* * *

I knelt at the gunwale of the ferry, and I took the ferryman's broad hand in both of mine, and his fingers closed around mine reflexively, gently, the grip of a man who had spent his life pulling strangers out of cold water. Even hollowed, the kindness stayed. That was worth knowing. That was worth *everything* knowing. The Hollow strips the story of a person but not always the grain of them — and if the grain survived, then the people in the lowlands were not gone, only locked, and every lock has a key, and every key has a price, and I was done pretending I hadn't already resolved to pay it.

I set the open grimoire on my knee. I put my finger in the pale space where his name had been. And I spoke into the river-dark:

"*Ossian.*"

The word left me and the world *tilted*.

Not visibly — the reeds did not bend, the pewter water did not stir — but I felt it the way you feel a floorboard give underfoot, a lurch in the deep structure of things. The name went out of me like a hook on a line, out over the black water, and for one heartbeat nothing. Then the river answered.

It came up out of the water the way heat comes off summer stones — a shimmer, a pressure, a sound below sound. All along the far bank the dead village flickered. In the chimney-flues, smoke. In the windows, one by one, candles. The river's current steadied, its hesitations smoothing into the long confident pull of water that knows its course, and I understood with a clarity that hurt: the river had been forgetting too, holding its cargo-name in waters that were being unpicked around it, and now the whole slow animal of it exhaled.

And the ferryman breathed in.

His chest lifted. His shoulders squared. Color climbed his throat like dawn climbing a wall. His eyes — I will remember this as long as I remember anything, which is a sentence I no longer trust — his eyes filled from the outside in, the way a well fills, bottom first, and what rose in them was not confusion but *grief*, whole and immediate, the grief of a man surfacing from deep water into a world on fire. His mouth worked. This time sound came.

"—the stars," he rasped. His voice was gravel and rope, unused, rusted. "Boy. Witch. You're the ones. Three days past — I put you over — I tried to tell you—" He coughed, wet and hard, and gripped my hands harder. "The stars went out and I couldn't say it. Couldn't say *anything*. Knew it, knew it plain as rain, and couldn't say it." He looked up past us, at the blank sky where the constellations should have been wheeling, and his face did something I had not seen it do before. It broke. "What's happened to the sky?"

"The wards fell," I said. "And worse things than the sky are dark. Can you pole us across, Ossian? We need the far bank. We need the north road."

He laughed — a short, disbelieving bark — and swung his pole off its brackets with the ease of sixty years' habit. "Witch, I'd pole you to the moon's anchor if you asked. Only—" and here he paused, pole dripping, and looked at me with eyes gone suddenly, piercingly clear, "—only it costs you, doesn't it. That kind of speaking. I can smell it on you. Something's missing. Something's *gone*."

Behind me, Bram made a small sound, half question.

"It doesn't matter," I said, and stood, and took my place in the bow. "Push off."

* * *

The crossing was silent except for the knock of the pole against the gunwale-stakes and the lap of water that remembered how to move. Ossian worked the stern with the unhurried power of his trade, and I watched the far village resolve out of the mist — real now, lit now, a woman's silhouette crossing a window with a lamp, a dog finally barking at nothing — and I took inventory of myself, the way you check your pockets after a crowd.

Something was gone. I knew that the way you know a tooth is missing: by the tongue finding the gap again and again, unable to leave it alone. I could not tell what. That is the treachery of it — the spent memory does not leave a label behind, only a smooth-walled absence, and the mind, resourceful liar that it is, papers over the hole with plausible plaster. I searched backward through myself. My mother's kitchen: there. The night the wards were raised, or rather the night I learned what they were for, my grandmother's dying whisper: there. The day I signed Bram's apprenticeship, the boy with the hungry eyes and the forged letter of recommendation he thought I hadn't noticed: there, intact, every detail, the rain on the window, the ink drying under his crooked signature.

All present. All accounted for. So what was missing?

That was the trap of the question, and I stepped around it deliberately, the way you step around a snare you've set yourself. Because I knew — some cold practical knot at the base of my skull knew — that if I ever identified the loss, I would have to decide whether it had been worth it. And I had made the bargain in advance precisely so I would never get to make that decision. Pay first. Count never. That was the only way the arithmetic worked. If I let myself weigh each memory against each stranger saved, I would grow miserly, and miserly witches let ferrymen drown in their own skulls.

"You paid," Ossian said quietly, from the stern. Not a question this time.

"Pole," I said.

"I buried a wife on this bank," he said, as if I hadn't spoken. "Forty years back. River fever. And I'll tell you what nobody tells you about grief — it isn't heavy. Heavy you can put down. Grief is *load-bearing*. Take it out and the whole house comes down different." The pole knocked, knocked. "Whatever you gave, don't go digging after it. Some cellars are better left shut."

I did not answer. Ahead of us the jetty planks of the far bank slid close, silvered with mist, and beyond them the north road climbed away into hills that the darkness had not yet reached — hills we would walk tomorrow, and the day after, all the way to a ruined Starwell that existed now only in the grimoire's oldest pages and my grandmother's most stubborn convictions.

Bram helped Ossian make fast, and I saw the boy watching me sidelong, and I saw the exact moment he decided not to ask. He was learning. Stars help me, he was learning the craft's first lesson — that the strongest magic is the kind nobody talks about — and I did not know whether to be proud or heartsick.

"North road's bad past the second milestone," Ossian said, shrugging his pole back onto its brackets. "Travelers coming south say there's a lady on it. Fine lady. Grey dress. Smiles like she's sorry for you." He spat over the side, an old habit restored along with everything else. "They say she asks your name. Politely. Like she's doing *you* the honor."

The mist closed behind us as we climbed away from the water, and I did not look back at the ferryman, because looking back is how you start counting costs.

But I heard him call out once across the water, my name — *Mara Quintana!* — spoken full and free, a gift returned, and I carried it up the dark road like a coal cupped in both hands, and I did not tell Bram that it was the first time in two days anyone had said my name aloud, or how much a name weighs when you have just spent something you cannot name to buy one back for a stranger.

Ahead, at the second milestone, the mist had begun to take a shape. And the shape was smiling.
