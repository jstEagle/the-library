## 6. Hollow Court

The road out of the lowlands climbed through country that had begun to forget itself. Bram felt it in his teeth first — a faint wrongness, like a word on the tip of the tongue that never quite arrived. The hills here were the color of old ash, not burned but *unremembered*, their slopes soft-edged as things seen through water. Grass grew in patches and then gave up mid-blade, as if the ground could no longer recall what grass was for.

Mara walked ahead of him with the grimoire strapped to her back, and even from behind he could see how she carried it: not like a burden but like a wound she was refusing to favor. She had barely spoken since the ferryman. Since she had spoken a name into that poor man's hollow face and watched him bloom back into himself like frost reversing, and then turned away so quickly that Bram hadn't seen what it cost her — only noticed, later, that she'd stopped humming. She had always hummed when she worked. He'd known her six years. The silence where the humming had been was louder than anything she said.

*She spent something,* he thought, for the hundredth time. *And she won't tell me what.*

He hated her a little for it. He hated himself more for noticing how much he didn't want to lose whatever else she might spend next.

The road bent around a hill that had no name — he tried to think of one and his mind slid off the attempt like rain off glass — and there, at the bend, stood a carriage.

It should not have been possible. There was no road wide enough for it, no track at all, just the forgotten footpath they'd been following since dawn. Yet the carriage stood square in their way, black-lacquered, drawn by two horses so pale they seemed cut from milk-glass, and they did not breathe. Bram watched for the steam of their breath in the cold morning air and saw none. Their eyes were the color of the sky had been before the stars went out: nothing at all.

Mara's hand closed on his wrist. Her grip was iron gone cold.

"Don't," she breathed.

"I haven't done anything."

"You were about to be curious." Her eyes stayed fixed on the carriage door. "Curiosity is how it gets in. Curiosity is a door you open from the inside."

The door opened anyway.

* * *

She stepped down without touching the ground, or so it seemed — one moment inside the dark of the carriage, the next standing on the ash-colored road, and between the two there was no motion Bram's eye could catch. She was beautiful the way a portrait is beautiful: perfect, composed, and flat. A tall woman in dove-gray silk, hair the white of undyed wool pinned up with what looked like slivers of bone, and a face that made him think of a page someone had written on and then carefully scraped clean. You could see the ghost of the writing if you looked hard enough. That was the worst part. Something had been there once.

"Good morning," she said, and her voice was lovely and warm and completely without anything behind it, like music played in an empty house. "I do hope I haven't startled you. Travelers are so rare these days. Most people forget where they're going long before they get anywhere worth arriving."

Mara stepped in front of Bram, one arm out, the other hand already moving through the opening shapes of a warding sign. "Lady Vess."

"You know me." Vess pressed a hand to her breastbone, delighted. "How flattering. How *rare*. Do you know, witch, that being known is the last luxury left in this world? Everyone else has spent themselves. But you—" her empty eyes moved over Mara with something almost like appetite, "—you are still full."

"Stay back from my apprentice."

"Oh, I intend to speak *to* your apprentice." Vess's gaze slid past Mara as though she weren't there, which Bram understood suddenly was not rudeness but accuracy — to Lady Vess, Mara was furniture, scenery, an obstacle of no more account than the hill. "Bram Quintana. Yes? We know your name. Names are rather our business."

His own name in her mouth felt like cold water poured down his spine. Not because it hurt. Because it *fit*.

"What do you want?" he managed.

"To talk. Only to talk." She gestured gracefully at the road, the hills, the pale horses. "You've seen what we've inherited, I expect. The forgetting. I know how it must look to you — a plague, a devouring, some grand horror out of the old stories your mistress tells by firelight. And the Court does have its reputation." She smiled, and the smile was flawless and meant nothing. "But reputations belong to the remembered, and we are, after all, the opposite. May I tell you what we actually are?"

"No," said Mara.

"Yes," said Bram.

Mara's head snapped toward him. He didn't look at her. He couldn't. If he looked at her he would step back behind her arm like a good apprentice, and something in him — the same thing that had carried him over the valley wall with a stolen book under his coat — refused.

Vess's smile deepened by a single degree. "Thank you."

* * *

They walked. That was the strange part; he'd expected menace, coercion, the black carriage rolling after them like a verdict. Instead Lady Vess strolled beside him along the forgotten road while Mara paced three steps behind, close enough to grab him, far enough that the conversation belonged to him alone. The pale horses followed the empty carriage at a walk that made no sound. Even the hooves on the road were silent. Even the wheels.

"The Court doesn't eat memories," Vess said. "That's the fable. Fables need monsters, and monsters need appetites, and appetites are so *messy*. What we do is closer to... custodianship. Consider." She spread one white hand at the hills. "Every memory ever held by every mind that has ever died — where does it go? All those childhoods, all those griefs, all the names mothers whispered over cradles ten thousand years ago. Burned? Wasted? Scattered? The world throws away everything it loves, Bram Quintana. Every single day. It calls this *time passing* and builds little monuments to console itself."

"And the Court keeps them," Bram said. His master's voice came out of his mouth — her lecture-voice, the one she used when he'd almost understood something. He heard it and flinched.

"We keep them. Perfectly. Nothing loved is ever lost with us. Nothing forgotten is ever gone." Vess turned her scraped-clean face toward him, and for a moment — a heartbeat, no more — he thought he saw depth in her eyes, like a well you could fall into. "Your valley built walls against us. Your witch's order sealed its borders and called it protection. But ask yourself, apprentice: protected from *what*, exactly? From grief? From loss? Or from the simple truth that keeping something safe and keeping it forever are the same act, and the world has simply been too cowardly to perform it?"

"The wards kept people whole," Bram said. "I've seen what your custodianship looks like. Villages full of mothers who can't remember their children's faces."

"A regrettable side effect of the seal's collapse." She waved it away like smoke. "When the keystone fell, everything the wards had dammed up came through at once. An accident. A flood. Floods are not the river's malice." She stopped walking, and he stopped too, because stopping felt like the natural thing to do beside her, and that frightened him more than anything she'd said. "But you didn't come out here to discuss hydrology, did you?"

"No."

"You came out here because the valley was too small and your life in it was already written and you wanted — oh, I know exactly what you wanted, Bram Quintana, we are *very* good at wanting, we collect it — you wanted a world big enough to matter in." Her voice softened, and the softness was worse than any threat. "And instead you're walking an ash road beside a woman who let you steal from her because she needed a pawn, carrying a book that makes you a target, toward a ruin everyone who remembers it fears. Is that the bigness you imagined?"

Behind him, Mara said, low and dangerous: "Vess."

"One moment, witch. I'm nearly finished." Vess reached into the air beside her — into it, her hand disappearing to the wrist as though the morning were a drawer — and drew out a small object. A jar. Glass, stoppered with wax, no bigger than her palm. Inside it swam a light like late afternoon through honey, and shapes moved in the light, and Bram knew — the way you know your own name — that the shapes were moments. A kitchen. Hands kneading bread. Someone laughing off-frame.

"This is a preserved memory," Vess said. "A baker's, from a town that no longer recalls baking. We hold thousands. Tens of thousands. And here is what no fable will tell you: we can give them back. Any of them. To anyone." Her empty eyes held his. "We can give them to *you*."

"I don't want someone else's—"

"Not someone else's." She stepped closer, and the smell of her was nothing at all, which was somehow worse than any perfume. "Yours. A past of your own choosing, Bram. Crafted, seamless, true in every particular. You want a world where you matter? We can build you one. A past where you never stole, and so never betrayed. Where you and your mistress parted warmly on the valley road, with respect, with pride even, and no guilt sitting in your chest like a swallowed stone." Her voice dropped to a confidant's murmur. "I know about the stone. We know about all the stones. Guilt is just a memory that refuses to heal, and we are very, very good with memories."

The jar turned slowly in her fingers. In its honeyed light, the hands kneaded bread, and someone laughed, and Bram's throat ached.

Because he wanted it. That was the obscene truth of it, the thing he would never say aloud: he wanted it the way a starving man wants bread, instantly and entirely and without dignity. Six years of guilt over leaving — no, over *how* he'd left, sneaking out with her book under his coat like a thief, like a coward, after she'd taught him everything, after she'd trusted him with her whole solitary life — six years compressed into one night, and here was a woman offering to lift it out of him like a splinter. A past where he was good. A past where he'd never done the thing that made him unable to meet Mara's eyes across a campfire.

All it would cost was a book.

Just a book. Just the thing that had started all of this. Let the Court have their keystone, let the stars stay dark, let the lowlands keep forgetting — he'd never known those villages, those mothers, those children. But he knew the weight of Mara's disappointment. He knew it better than he knew his own heartbeat.

"Bram." Mara's voice, sharp as a drawn wire.

He raised his hand — *wait* — and heard himself say, "If I gave you the grimoire. What happens to her?"

Vess tilted her head, and the gesture was so perfectly human that it took him a moment to see the wrongness underneath: she tilted it the way a portrait might, if the painter had guessed at the angle but never seen a real neck bend. "The witch? Why, whatever she chooses. The Court holds no grudges. Grudges require remembering."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one anyone will give you today." She held out the jar, and the honey-light washed up over both their faces, and somewhere inside it the unseen person laughed again, and Bram thought: *that could be me in there. Kept. Safe. Forever.* "Take it. Take the past you should have had. Take a life with no betrayal in it. No one will ever know the difference — least of all you."

His hand rose.

* * *

Later — much later, on the far side of the hills, in the dark, with the ash road finally behind them — he would try to understand what had stopped him. It wasn't courage. He wanted to believe it was, but he'd felt no courage in it, only a lurch, like a man waking on stairs. It wasn't Mara's voice either, though she'd said his name twice more, each time sharper.

What stopped him was the laugh in the jar.

Because he'd listened to it — really listened, the way you listen to a stranger's conversation through a wall — and under the warmth of it he'd heard the shape of the silence around it. One laugh, looping. One kitchen, one pair of hands, one afternoon, kept perfect and changeless and *alone* in the glass forever. No one to laugh with. No tomorrow after it. The Court didn't preserve lives, he realized. It preserved specimens. And the woman holding the jar out to him with such courtesy, such grace, such bottomless politeness — she was the most preserved specimen of all. Somewhere inside that scraped-clean beauty there had been a person, and the Court had kept her so perfectly that nothing of her remained.

"Bram," Mara said, third time, and this time her hand closed on his raised wrist and pulled it down, and the touch broke the spell like ice cracking on a pond. "Look at me."

He looked at her. Her face was gray with fury and something else, something that wasn't fury at all, and her eyes — her eyes were full. Full of six years, full of anger, full of the humming she no longer did, full of every ordinary morning in the valley that he had thrown away and she had mourned. Full was the whole difference. Full was everything the Court would never be.

"No," he said, to Vess.

Her smile didn't falter. That was the terrible thing — it didn't falter at all. "No," she repeated pleasantly, as though tasting the word. "Of course. They always say no the first time." She returned the jar to the air beside her, into the drawer of the morning, and stepped back toward her silent carriage. "Do give my regards to the Starwell, apprentice. And do sleep badly. Guilt keeps best in the tired." At the carriage door she paused, half-turned, and for one flickering instant her composure slipped — not into menace but into something smaller and sadder, a woman glancing back at a house she couldn't remember living in. "The offer doesn't expire," she said quietly. "That's rather the point of us."

The door closed. The carriage was simply elsewhere, between one blink and the next, horses and all, and the road stood empty except for two travelers and the wind moving through grass that couldn't remember being green.

Mara rounded on him. "You were going to take it."

"I wasn't—"

"Your hand was *up*, Bram. I watched you weigh my whole life against your guilt, and your hand was *up*."

"And yours wasn't?" It came out of him hot and ugly, six years of it. "You let me steal the book. You *planned* it. You gambled me against the end of the world because you couldn't face it alone — don't look surprised, you told me yourself at the wardstone. So don't stand there and judge my hand for rising when yours has been in the till since the night the stars went out."

The words landed. He watched them land, watched them go into her like arrows into snow, and immediately wished them back, but the road was silent now and there was no unsaying anything. Mara looked at him for a long moment, and whatever moved behind her eyes she folded away, the way she folded away everything, and turned up the road.

"Walk," she said. "We've lost half a day, and it knows where we're going now."

He followed. But all that afternoon, and into the failing light, one thought kept pace with him like the soundless carriage had: *she always says no the first time.* And beneath it, quieter, colder, the thought he couldn't make go away:

*Then why did it feel, for one moment, like coming home?*

Behind them, far down the ash road, something was following. Not the carriage. Something patient, keeping just past the edge of sight, and as the dark came down Bram caught it once — a pale shape at the crest of the last hill, watching, no longer hunting.

Waiting to see whether they'd change their minds.
