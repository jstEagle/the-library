## 7. Ash Road

The forest had been green once. You could feel it the way you feel a scar on someone else's skin — a wrongness under your fingertips, a story the body still tells.

Mara felt it most strongly through her boots, of all things. The ground beneath the ash was not dead ground. Dead ground was silent; she had walked enough of it in her life to know its particular hush. This ground *ached*. Somewhere below the char and the cinder-loam, roots remembered drinking. Trunks that stood like blackened fingers against a bruise-colored sky still held, deep in their heartwood, some stubborn ghost of sap. When the wind moved through what remained of the canopy — a canopy of broken spears, leafless, lightless — it did not whistle the way wind whistles through bare branches. It *mourned*.

"Stop staring," Bram said behind her. "You keep stopping."

"I'm listening."

"To trees."

"To trees," she agreed, and did not explain, because explaining would mean admitting how much the place unsettled her, and she had spent three days cultivating the impression that nothing unsettled her. She started walking again. The ash rose around their ankles with each step, fine as flour, gray as old snow, and clung to them the way grief clings — invisibly at first, then everywhere at once.

They had put the better part of two valleys between themselves and Lady Vess since the crossing at the ferry, and Mara did not trust a single furlong of it. The Hollow Court did not chase. That was the thing no one understood about being hunted by the Hollow: there was no pursuit, no baying, no crashing through underbrush. There was only the slow certainty that whatever direction you fled, you would arrive where they wanted you, eventually, because they were patient in the way that emptiness is patient. A hole does not run after the water. It waits for the water to come to it.

Still. Vess had looked at her over the ferryman's restored tongue, and something in that porcelain face had flickered — surprise, or its fossil — and Mara intended to spend every hour of that flicker putting distance between them and the thing wearing a lady's shape.

"Bram." She said his name without turning. "Tell me what you remember about your mother's kitchen."

A pause. Ash crunching as he stopped walking. "What?"

"You heard me."

"I remember—" He caught up to her, and she could hear the wariness in his stride, the way he moved now, always half-turned toward her like a dog deciding whether the hand was going to feed it or strike. Three days of alliance had not cured him of that. She wasn't sure she wanted it to. "Why my mother's kitchen?"

"Because I asked. Because we're going to stop for the night in an hour and I'd rather hear about bread than listen to you rehearse arguments you're too frightened to start. What do you remember?"

He was quiet long enough that she glanced back. In the failing light he looked younger than he had any right to look, ash dusted across his dark hair, the grimoire slung against his chest in its sling of oiled leather where it had lived since the wardstone — since she had let him take it, which was a sentence she kept locked in a room in her mind and did not enter.

"The window over the basin," he said finally, slowly, feeling his way. "It faced east. Morning came in sideways and made the water in the basin go gold. And there was a crack in the corner of the pane that my father said he'd fix for six years and never did, and my mother said she liked it because—" His voice did something complicated. "Because the frost made feathers on it in winter. Only on the cracked side. She used to say the glass was lonely where it was broken, so winter was kinder to it."

Mara walked four more steps before she trusted her voice. "Good," she said. "That's exactly the kind of thing I need you to learn to hold on to."

"Hold on to? It's mine. Nobody can take my mother's kitchen from me."

She stopped then, fully, and turned, and let him see her face without armor, because the lesson was too important to teach gently and he was too proud to learn it gently anyway.

"Yes," she said, "they can. They already have. Half the lowlands we crossed couldn't tell you their children's names, Bram. Do you think the Hollow broke into their heads and tore those memories out screaming? No. It doesn't work like that. Memory isn't a stone in a wall. It's a knot in a rope. Pull the right thread and the whole knot loosens — quietly, painlessly, so gradually you don't even notice you've forgotten until you reach for your child's face and find smoothness. The Court doesn't steal. It *unties*."

The wind mourned overhead. Somewhere off the road, a blackened trunk groaned and settled, remembering weight it no longer had.

"So we tie knots," she said. "Hedge-craft. The oldest craft there is, older than wards, older than the grimoire, older than whatever pretty story your master told you about why the valley needed walls. Anchoring. You take a memory — small, specific, loved — and you drive it into yourself like a tent stake. Not the big memories. Never the big ones. Big memories are doors; the Hollow loves doors. Small ones are stones buried in the earth. It can't get purchase."

Bram's arms had come up across his chest, the old defensiveness, but his eyes had gone sharp and hungry in the way that had made him, despite everything, worth taking as an apprentice. "Show me."

"Not here. Light's dying, and anchoring leaves you open while you do it — wide open, like sleeping with your door unlatched. We make camp first."

"We've been making camp at dusk and walking at dawn for three days and you haven't taught me anything but walking."

"And you're alive," she said, "which is the entire curriculum so far."

* * *

They made camp in the lee of a fallen giant — a tree so vast that even burned and hollowed it formed a wall taller than Bram, its interior scorched to a cavern of charcoal that still smelled, faintly, impossibly, of resin. Mara built no fire. Fire drew eyes, and besides, the forest gave its own dim illumination: along the dead branches, pale lichen bloomed in patches, faintly luminous, feeding on something that was not quite decay. Ghost-light. Her grandmother had called it candle-of-the-dead and forbade her to touch it, which meant Mara had touched it extensively at nine years old and learned it was cold as riverstone and tasted of nothing at all.

She shared out the last of the hard cheese and the flatbread Ilsa— no. Not yet. The bread from the ferryman's wife, then, back when the ferryman's wife had still known she had a wife. The thought snagged on something and she let it pass, the way you let a shadow pass over you on a road: don't look directly at it, and it goes by.

"Eat," she told Bram. "Then we work."

He ate. He watched her eat. She was beginning to catalog the ways he watched her — this one meant he was working up to a question, that one meant he'd found a flaw in something she'd said and was savoring it, this other one, the one she liked least and thought about most, meant he had forgotten to be wary.

"All right," she said, before the question could land. "Anchoring. Give me your hand."

"My—"

"Wrist. Give me your wrist. This is hedge-craft, not court dancing, stop looking scandalized."

He gave her his wrist. His pulse jumped under her fingers, quick and warm and alive, and she fixed her attention on the working so hard that her knuckles ached. "Close your eyes. Take the kitchen. The window, the crack, the frost-feathers. Hold all of it at once — the gold water, the lonely glass, your mother saying it. Can you?"

"Yes." His voice had gone quiet and strange.

"Now say it. Out loud. All of it, in order, like a story you're telling someone who's never been cold."

"Why out loud?"

"Because the voice is the deepest groove we have. Words cut channels in us that even forgetting runs along instead of across. Say it."

And he said it. *The window faces east. Morning comes in sideways. The water goes gold. There's a crack in the corner my father never fixed. My mother says the glass is lonely where it's broken, so winter is kinder to it.* Twice, three times, his voice dropping into rhythm, into litany, and Mara felt the change come through his wrist before she saw it in his face — a settling, a density, as if the boy had been smoke and was becoming, word by word, a stone.

"That's the anchor laid," she said softly. "Now the hard part. Every night, you say it again. Tired, terrified, bleeding, it doesn't matter. You say it. Because the Hollow works slow, and repetition is the only rope it can't untie — a knot tied fresh every day has no loose threads."

His eyes opened. In the ghost-light they were very dark. "You do this too."

It was not a question, but she treated it as one, because answering questions cost less than admitting things. "Every night since the wardstone fell. Since before."

"What's yours?"

"No."

"Mara."

"Apprentices don't audit their masters."

"I'm not your apprentice anymore. You fired me. Or I quit. We never established which."

"You stole my grimoire and ran into the apocalypse. I believe the traditional term is *thief*."

"*Alleged* thief. You let me take it." The words came out of him fast and then he went white, visibly white even in the lichen-light, because that was the sentence, wasn't it, the one that sat between them like a drawn blade. He knew. Of course he knew — he'd known since the ruined wardstone, since she'd stood over him furious and unsurprised and told him exactly what she'd done and why. But knowing a thing and saying it aloud in a dead forest at night were different animals.

Mara took her hand off his wrist. "Yes," she said. "I let you take it. And if you want to hate me for it, hate me in the morning, when we can walk while we argue. Tonight you're going to lay your anchor and sleep, because tomorrow we cross the burn-line and the country past it doesn't hold a person's shape unless the person is tied down tight."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one you're getting." She wrapped herself in her cloak and turned her shoulder to him, and heard him shift, and settle, and — after a long time, so quiet she almost missed it — begin to murmur. *The window faces east. Morning comes in sideways.*

She waited until his breathing lengthened. Then, into the dark, barely voiced, she said her own.

Not the choosing. She had checked for the choosing that morning, the way you check a coin purse, and found the purse lighter than expected and could not say what had been in it, and had decided — firmly, deliberately, with the full force of a will honed by forty years of hedge-craft — not to think about that. Instead she anchored on the small things. Rain on the washhouse roof. The smell of the grimoire's leather. A girl's laugh on a mountainside, years ago, the day she'd pulled a scrawny, furious, hungry-eyed boy out of a river he'd sworn he hadn't fallen into.

*The window faces east,* Bram murmured in his sleep, and Mara anchored her stones, and the dead forest mourned around them, and neither of them noticed the lichen nearest the road had begun, very faintly, to dim.

* * *

The burn-line came at midmorning, and it was not a line at all but a gradient, the way drowning is a gradient. First the trees stood black but standing. Then they leaned. Then they lay in rows, all pointing the same direction, like wheat after a storm — except no storm burns in rows, and Mara did not say what she thought, which was that these trees had been *pushed*, and that whatever pushed them had been tidying.

Past the leaning dead, the country changed character entirely. The ash thinned. Color began to leak back in — a dirty olive scrub, a stream the color of pewter — but everything was slightly wrong the way a portrait is wrong when the painter worked from description rather than sight. The scrub grew in spirals. The stream ran uphill for thirty feet before thinking better of it. And the sounds were worst: birdsong that repeated itself every eleven seconds, precisely, like a music box winding down.

"Is it doing that?" Bram whispered. "Or are we?"

"It's doing that. This country's been skimmed. Fed on shallowly, over years. The Court takes the top layer first — names, faces, recent joys — and leaves the deep structure, mostly. Mostly." She stepped over a spiral of scrub and tried not to think about what fed on a place so thoroughly that even its birds forgot the ends of their songs.

They walked. The bickering resumed by noon, which was how she knew he was recovering from the morning, and possibly how he knew she was.

"You're limping."

"I'm favoring my left ankle, which is a strategic decision."

"You're limping because you twisted it on the wardstone steps three nights ago and refused to let me look at it."

"And if I let you look at it, what exactly would you do? You have the healing arts of a turnip."

"I have excellent hands."

She stopped walking. The words hung there, absurd, unintended, radiating heat. Bram went the color of brick. Somewhere off to the left, a bird sang its eleven seconds and started over.

"That came out wrong," he said.

"Everything you say comes out wrong. It's practically a discipline you've mastered."

"I meant I'm good with splints. Bindings. Wrapping things."

"Stop talking."

"I'm stopping."

But he was grinning — actually grinning, ash-streaked, exhausted, hunted, and grinning — and Mara found, to her considerable alarm, that the muscles of her own face were attempting treason. She crushed the rebellion immediately and walked on, faster, favoring her left ankle with tremendous dignity.

They made the ridge by late afternoon, and from the ridge they saw the town.

It sat in the fold of the next valley like a coin at the bottom of a purse: slate roofs, a crooked church tower, chimney smoke — real smoke, woodsmoke, the smell of which reached them on the wind and made both of them stand very still, breathing it in like starving men. People. Unskimmed people, maybe, or skimmed lightly, or people who had found some way to fight. Smoke meant fires meant cooking meant living.

"There," Bram said. "Food. Splint supplies. Possibly a map."

"Maybe." But Mara was looking at the town with narrowed eyes, because something about it nagged at her — the layout, the tower, the way the roads bent around a central square as if avoiding it. She had seen a bend like that before, in her grandmother's oldest charts, in ink faded to the color of weak tea. Charts of places that had gone dark in the first Hollow years. Places that survived.

Places that survived because someone in them was fighting.

"Come on," she said, and started down. "Carefully. And Bram — tonight, add a second anchor. Your mother's kitchen won't be enough where we're going."

"What's in the town?"

"I don't know yet." Below them, in the gathering dusk, a single lamp was being lit in a high window of the church tower — placed there, she realized as she watched, not to illuminate the room, but to be seen from the road. A signal. An invitation. Or a lure. "But somebody down there remembers how to leave a light burning."

Behind them, far back along the ash road, so far it might have been imagination, the lichen-light guttered — and something patient began, unhurried, to follow.
