## 9. What Mara Forgot

The fire had burned down to a red heart by the time Mara noticed the hole.

They had made camp in the lee of a fallen watchtower, one of the old ward-towers that had once studded the valley's rim like beads on a string. Its stones still held a faint warmth of purpose, a hum she could feel through her palms when she pressed them flat against the rock — or she imagined she could. The wards were broken. Whatever hum remained was only memory in the stone, and memory, these days, was currency that everyone spent and no one minted.

Bram slept across the fire from her, the grimoire tucked against his chest like a child, like a shield, like the thing it actually was: a keystone wearing the shape of a book. Even asleep he held it as if someone might take it. Someone had taken things from him before, she supposed. She had been the one to teach him that the world took things. She had never taught him that she might be one of the takers.

She should have been sleeping too. Her body ached from three days of hard walking, from the ash road behind them and the forgotten country ahead, and sleep was the only place where her own mind did not turn traitor. But she sat instead with her hands around a tin cup of pine-needle tea gone cold, and she went looking for a memory, the way you go looking for a tool you are certain you left in a certain drawer.

Why had she taken an apprentice?

It seemed a small question. It should have been a small question. She had been Mara Quintana for sixty years; she had been witch of the valley for forty of them; she had taken apprentices before Bram — two, both long gone now, one married into the river towns and one dead of a fever that even hedge-craft could not argue with. She remembered those two with perfect clarity. The first had come to her door barefoot in autumn, chasing a runaway hen, and Mara had thought: *this one has hands that want work.* The second she had chosen herself, plucked from the harvest festival because the girl had looked at the bonfire the way Mara once looked at it, hungry and afraid of her own hunger.

But Bram —

She reached for it the way you reach for a stair in the dark, certain of its height, certain of its place. And her foot found nothing. No stair. No door. No barefoot boy at her threshold, no moment of decision, no reason rising up to meet her like steam off warm bread.

There was nothing there.

Not fog, not blur, not the soft erosion of ordinary forgetting, which wears an edge but leaves the shape. This was smoothness. This was a wall where a window used to be, plastered over so neatly that if she hadn't known — if some stubborn animal part of her hadn't *known* — she would never have guessed anything had ever hung there at all.

Mara set down the cup very carefully, because her hands were not steady, and she made herself think it plainly, the way she made apprentices name a poison aloud before they learned its antidote:

*You spent it.*

At the ferry. At the gray water where the mute ferryman sat like a knot of driftwood, his eyes full of someone else's weather. She had opened the grimoire to the page of restorations and spoken his name — *Ondrej* — and the name had gone out of the book and into him like a coal dropped into a frozen pond, cracking him open, flooding his face with light. And the book had taken its price from the nearest purse, which was hers. One memory, freely given, for one name freely restored. She had felt it go. She had felt it the way you feel a tooth leave your jaw: a wrongness, a gap the tongue keeps returning to.

She had told herself it didn't matter which memory the book chose. She had told herself she would check, later, when there was time. Later had become the ash road, and the ash road had become Ilsa's town, and Ilsa's town had become this cold fire under a sky with no stars, and *later* had arrived, and here was the bill.

The book had not chosen at random. Books like that never do. It had taken the memory closest to the working — the memory of names given and kept, of choosing a person and binding yourself to them. It had taken the day she chose Bram Quintana.

And because that day was gone, everything downstream of it had gone strange. She remembered teaching him. She remembered correcting his grip on the sickle-charm, scolding his handwriting, watching him fail and fail and then, one gray morning, succeed — the first clean ward he ever wove, trembling in the air between his hands like something newborn. She remembered all of that. What she did not remember was why she had let him stay. Why she, who had turned away worthier petitioners, who had sworn after the fever took her second apprentice that she would not do it again, had looked at a runaway boy with more ambition than sense and said *yes*.

Every teacher remembers the choosing. It is the hinge of the whole craft. She had a hollow where her hinge should be, and the door swung on nothing.

Across the fire, Bram stirred. "Mara?" His voice was rough with sleep, younger than he liked to pretend he was. "You're awake."

"Someone has to be."

He sat up, pushing hair out of his eyes, and looked at her the way he always did in the dark hours — as if checking that she was still real, still there, still his. She hated how much she loved being looked at like that. She hated that she could not remember whether she had ever decided to love it.

"You were talking in your sleep," he said. "Earlier. You said a name."

"Whose?"

"I don't know. It wasn't mine." He smiled a little, trying to make it light. "Should I be jealous?"

*Yes,* said something in her, treacherous and warm. *Be jealous. Be anything. Fill the silence before it fills itself.*

"No," she said. "Sleep, Bram. Dawn comes early, and the road ahead doesn't hold its shape."

He lay back down obediently, the way he never had as a boy, and she watched his breathing slow, and she said nothing.

That was the whole of it, she thought. That was the shape of her cowardice: she said nothing. Because to tell him — *I cannot remember why I took you; the book ate my choosing of you; I am carrying you toward the Starwell on legs that stand on air* — would be to hand him a knife and show him where her ribs were softest. He already carried enough guilt to bend his spine. She had seen it every day since the wardstone, in the way he flinched from her anger and leaned into her approval, a boy trying to pay down a debt she had never named. If she told him the truth, he would make it his fault. He would decide that her sacrifice was his penance, and he would start spending himself to refill her, and she knew Bram Quintana well enough — better than she could prove, now — to know exactly where that spending ended.

So she said nothing, and the silence grew teeth.

* * *

They walked the next morning through country that was forgetting itself in layers.

First the fences went — not fallen, but *unfallen*, their posts standing in rows that no longer agreed they had ever enclosed anything. Then the orchards, trees bearing apples and pears on the same branch, confused about what they had promised to be. A well stood in the middle of a field with no village around it, drawing water for no one, its bucket rope worn smooth by hands that had been subtracted from the world. Bram touched the rope and pulled his fingers back as if burned.

"It's still wet," he whispered.

"The water remembers longer than the people did," Mara said. "Water's honest. It just does what it's told."

He glanced at her — that quick, searching glance, the one that had grown more frequent over the last two days, the one she had begun to dread. "You're quiet today. Quieter than usual."

"Witches ration words. You know that."

"I know when you're rationing." He fell into step beside her, close enough that their sleeves brushed, and lowered his voice. "Mara. At the ferry. When you gave Ondrej his name back — what did it cost? Really?"

The question went through her like a splinter finding its depth. She kept her face still, the way she had taught him to keep his face still, and heard her own voice answer from somewhere calm and practiced: "A memory. I told you."

"A memory of *what*?"

"Something small." She stepped over a fence rail that had forgotten its fence. "A taste. A color. The kind of thing the book takes because it thinks it won't be missed."

He stopped walking. She felt him stop, felt the weight of his attention settle on her back like a hand, and she kept walking three more steps before pride let her turn around.

"You're lying," he said. Not accusingly. Worse than that — gently. "You're lying the way you taught me to lie, with true details around a false center. Which means it wasn't small. Which means you know exactly what it was, and you're not telling me because—" He stopped himself. She watched him wrestle the sentence down like a man wrestling a door shut against wind. "Because you think I'll break."

"I think," Mara said, "that you carry enough."

"That's not your choice."

"No," she agreed. "It's mine. That's what makes it my choice."

He stared at her, and she saw the hurt move through him and be mastered, and she thought: *he learned that mastering from me, and I cannot remember why I wanted him to learn anything at all, except that I must have wanted it more than I have ever wanted anything, because look what the book took when it got hungry.*

"Fine," he said quietly. "Keep it. But when we get to the Starwell, Mara — whatever the price is, we split it. Evenly. Promise me."

"I promise nothing on roads like this. Roads like this eat promises."

"Mara."

"Walk, apprentice."

The word came out of her by habit, and landed strangely, because *apprentice* was a word whose meaning lived in the very memory she had lost. She was calling him by a relationship she could no longer account for. It was like a woman saying *my sister* while unable to recall a single shared childhood — the word true, the ground beneath it gone.

He walked. She followed. Neither of them spoke, and the silence between them had teeth now, and it bit at both of them all day.

* * *

Near dusk they found a shrine.

It was a small thing, roadside, the kind farmers built in the valley's fat years: a stone hood over a niche, a candle stub, a bowl for offerings. The niche was empty. Of course it was empty. But carved into the lintel, deep enough to survive shallow forgetting, were words in the old valley script, and Mara knelt and traced them with her thumb, reading aloud because reading aloud was how she anchored — hedge-craft's oldest trick, give the memory a voice and it grips harder.

"*We remember so they don't have to*," she translated. "A grandmother's prayer. They carved it over the offering bowl. You'd leave bread for your dead here, and the shrine would hold the remembering of them so your own head stayed light."

Bram crouched beside her. In the last light his face was all planes and shadow, older than his years, and she caught herself studying it — hunting her own features in his expression the way you hunt a reflection in dark water. *Did I choose this face? What did I see, that first day? What did I—*

Nothing. Smoothness. Wall.

"Mara," he said. "Say something true. Right now. Anything. Ilsa said the anchors work best out loud, and you've barely spoken since noon, and I—" His jaw worked. "I keep feeling like something's pulling at me. Like the Hollow found a loose thread. And when you go quiet I can't tell if it's you or it."

The honesty of it undid her a little. Here was the boy she could not remember choosing, asking her to anchor him, trusting her with the very thread the darkness pulled. And she understood, suddenly and completely, what the silence was costing — not her, but him. He thought her distance was judgment. He thought she was regretting the alliance, regretting the rescue at the wardstone, regretting *him*. Every hour she kept her secret, the Hollow didn't need to eat his memories; it was feeding on worse. It was feeding on doubt.

So she gave him a true thing. Not the whole truth — she was not brave enough for that, and perhaps not cruel enough either — but a true thing, spoken aloud, into the empty shrine, where the old script could hold it.

"When I was seven," she said, "before the valley, before any of it, I lived by the sea. My mother sold fish. I remember the smell of the scales on her apron, and I remember that she sang while she cleaned them, badly, songs from a country I've never found on any map. That's my anchor. Scales and bad singing. Whenever the dark pulls at me, I put my hands in that smell and I sing one of her songs, badly, and I am still Mara Quintana, and nothing hollow can have me."

Bram's eyes had gone bright. "I didn't know you were from the sea."

"There's a great deal you don't know about me." She meant it lightly. It came out heavy. "Your turn. Say a true thing."

He thought. The sky above them was black and starless, and the ruined country breathed around them, and he closed his eyes like a child praying.

"The night the stars went out," he said, "the first thing I felt wasn't fear. It was relief. I was outside the wards, I'd stolen from you, I'd betrayed everything, and for one whole breath I thought — good. Now I don't have to be the valley's hope. Now I'm just a thief, and thieves get to be small." He opened his eyes. "Then I saw the first Hollow thing cross the line, and I understood what my smallness cost, and I've been walking toward the Starwell ever since to pay it back. That's my anchor. Not a good memory. A true debt."

"Bram—"

"Don't." He shook his head, smiling, and the smile broke her. "Don't forgive me again. Just — say another true thing sometime. Soon. Whatever it is you're not saying. I'll wait. I'm good at waiting. You taught me that."

*I taught you that,* she thought, *and I cannot tell you when, or why, or what I saw in you that made me spend forty years of patience on your name.*

"Come on," she said aloud, standing, knees cracking. "There's a barn past the next rise that still half-believes in itself. We'll sleep under a roof that mostly exists."

They walked, and behind them the little shrine held its carved prayer in the gathering dark — *we remember so they don't have to* — and Mara carried her hollow carefully, the way you carry a cracked jar of water, knowing that every mile spilled a little more, knowing that soon she would have to decide which was heavier: the secret, or the thing the secret was protecting.

She did not yet know that the choice would be taken out of her hands. She did not yet know that the road ahead bent toward a town where the Court's herald waited with a courteous smile and a bargain shaped exactly like Bram's guilt — that within two days, Lady Vess would step out of the forgetting like a lady stepping from a carriage, and offer him any past he wanted, even one where he had never betrayed anyone at all.

Mara only knew that the night was coming on, and the stars were still gone, and somewhere inside her, in a room she could no longer find the door to, a younger woman was saying yes to a boy at her threshold — and Mara would have given anything, anything at all, to hear what she said.
