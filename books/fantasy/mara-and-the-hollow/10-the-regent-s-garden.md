## 10. The Regent's Garden

They did not bind my hands.

That was the first thing I noticed, and the last thing that made any sense. Lady Vess's riders — if you could call them riders; their horses had the look of things remembered rather than seen, all suggestion and no detail — took the grimoire from my pack with a courtesy that was worse than violence, and then they simply walked me. No rope. No blade at my throat. Vess rode beside me through the ash-dark country with her hands folded in her lap like a woman on her way to a wedding, and when I tried to bolt, somewhere past a river that flowed uphill, she didn't chase me. She just waited. The dark came down around me like a lid, and inside it I heard my own name being peeled away, syllable by syllable, the way you'd strip bark from a green branch, and then she was there again, offering her hand, saying *there you are* as though I'd only stepped behind a tree.

After that I stopped running. Not because I was beaten. Because I understood, finally, that there was nowhere to run *to*. The Hollow did not pursue. It was the ground you stood on.

"You're taking me to the Regent," I said, on the third day or what I guessed was the third day, because out here the sun rose when it pleased and sometimes not at all.

"I'm bringing you to the garden," Vess said. "The Regent will come to you, if it pleases. It rarely pleases." She smiled, and the smile was beautiful the way an empty house is beautiful — everything in its place, nothing living in it. "You should be flattered. Most people are never invited."

"Most people are eaten first."

"Eaten." She turned the word over like a coin she suspected of being foreign. "Is that what Mara told you? That we devour?" She shook her head slowly, and for a moment — one strange, flickering moment — her face did something I had no name for. Not sadness. The place where sadness would have gone, if the room hadn't been cleared out years ago. "We keep. There is a difference, Bram Quintana. You'll see it soon enough."

I hated that she knew my name. Then I hated myself more, because some small starving part of me liked hearing it said in that voice, liked being *known*, after weeks of being hunted and hollowed and afraid. That is how they get you. Not with teeth. With recognition.

* * *

The garden grew in a valley that had no right to exist.

I felt us descend for hours, down switchback roads cut from stone so old it seemed embarrassed to be stone, and then the walls opened and there it was: light. Real light, warm and gold, falling from a sky that shouldn't have been there — a sky painted, I realized as we passed under the gate, or perhaps grown, arch by arch of pale ribbed bone. Beneath it stretched rows and rows and rows of glass.

Not greenhouses. I thought greenhouse at first, because the shapes were humble, domestic, the kind of thing a village herbalist might keep. But there were no plants. There were jars.

Jars by the thousand. Jars on shelves that climbed into the golden haze until I couldn't see the tops of them. Jars laid in long beds like seedlings, each one cradled in soil that glittered faintly, and every jar held something that moved — a slow turning shimmer, a color that breathed, a small weather all its own. One glowed the particular bruised violet of dusk over a wheat field. One flickered with lamplight and laughter I could almost hear. One, near the path, held nothing but the smell of bread, visible somehow, hanging in the glass like smoke that had decided to stay.

"What is this place?" My voice came out smaller than I wanted.

"The harvest," Vess said. She had dismounted and walked ahead of me, and now she stopped, and her hand hovered over the nearest jar without touching it, the way you'd hover over a sleeping child. "Every name the Court has gathered. Every memory we've taken from your kind since before your kind had words. They don't die, Bram. Nothing here dies. That's what your witch has never understood." She looked back at me, and the almost-human flicker crossed her face again, quicker this time, like a fish under ice. "We are not the end of memory. We are its *archive*."

I walked the rows because she let me, and because I couldn't not.

Here was a childhood: a boy of maybe six, chasing a dog through summer grass, the whole scene folded into a jar no bigger than my fist, and the grass so green it hurt, greener than anything left in the world outside, because outside the world had forgotten what green was for. Here was a love — two figures dancing in a kitchen, and the jar around them warm to the touch, warm like a hand. Here was grief, and grief was the heaviest of all; the jars that held mourning sat lower on their shelves, dense, dark-cored, and when I leaned close to one I felt my own chest tighten with a sorrow that wasn't mine, a woman's sorrow for a son lost at sea, so complete and so perfectly preserved that I understood in one lurching instant that the woman herself must be walking somewhere out in the gray country right now, smiling politely at strangers, unable to remember why the ocean made her cry.

"That one came in forty years ago," Vess said softly, watching me. "A fisherman's wife, up the coast. She gave it freely, in the end. They usually do, near the end. Grief is heavy, and we offer to carry it." A pause. "She visits, sometimes. Stands where you're standing. Looks at the jar. She doesn't know what it is anymore, but she cries, and then she thanks us, because she feels lighter, and she doesn't ask why."

"You call this mercy."

"I call it *keeping*." For the first time her voice lost its courtly smoothness, and something underneath it showed through — old, and tired, and terribly patient. "Do you know what happens to a memory when a person dies with it still held? It goes into the ground. It rots with the body. All those loves, all those summers, all those names — poured out into dirt, forever, and the world forgets them twice. Once when the person dies and once when everyone who knew them dies." Her hand finally settled on the jar with the dancing couple, and the warmth of it seemed to reach her, seemed to make her almost solid. "We do not rot anything. We lose nothing. Every child who ever chased a dog through summer grass is *here*, Bram. Whole. Safe. Forever. Your Starwell would burn them as fuel. We build them a museum."

"And the people they came from?"

"Lighter," she said.

It was the horror clarified, standing there among ten thousand glowing jars under a sky made of bone. I had thought the Hollow Court was hunger, was darkness, was the thing that ate the stars. But hunger at least wants and lacks and rages. This was worse. This was *curation*. Somewhere in these rows was a jar holding my mother's laugh — I knew it the way you know a wound, suddenly and completely — and somewhere was the taste of the bread Mara baked on my first morning as her apprentice, and somewhere, maybe, the exact weight of the grimoire in my hands the night I stole it, and all of it filed, labeled, dusted, *kept*, while the people those things belonged to wandered the world with smooth places where their lives used to be, thanking their thieves for the lightness.

Victory, for the Hollow, meant this. Not an ending. An ending would at least be honest. Victory meant the whole world became this garden — every love potted, every grief shelved, every name pinned under glass like a moth, and above it all a Regent walking the rows, admiring the collection, adding to it, forever, while nothing anywhere ever happened again, because nothing that happens can matter once it's only been *preserved*.

"They were people," I said. "The Court. Before. Weren't they?"

Vess went very still.

It was the smallest thing — a stillness, half a heartbeat, a breath not taken — but after days of her flawless, gliding composure it rang like a dropped plate. And I saw it then, looking at her properly for the first time instead of through fear: the way her beauty was assembled rather than grown, the seams of it, the way her eyes held light without giving any back. She wasn't empty the way a bottle is empty. She was empty the way a house is empty after the family leaves. Something had lived here once. Something had *left*, or been taken, room by room, and the rooms had been kept so well that even the absence looked furnished.

"That question," she said at last, and her voice had found its smoothness again, but slower, like someone reassembling a mask piece by piece, "is not one you should bring to the Regent."

"Why not?"

"Because it remembers being asked before." She turned away from me, toward the endless glowing rows, and her hand had curled closed at her side, away from the jars, as if she'd caught herself reaching. "By someone who also walked here. Also asked. Also—" She stopped. The almost-human flicker came and stayed a moment longer than before, and I swear the gold light bent around her differently, warmer, wrong for her. "There was a gardener here once," she said, very quietly, as if reporting a fact about the weather. "Before me. I don't remember her name. I remember that I don't remember, which is a cruelty the Court does not intend." Her eyes found mine, and for one unguarded instant they were not beautiful at all. They were frightened. "Do you understand what I'm telling you, apprentice? I am troubled. I have been troubled for a hundred years, and I do not know by what, because whatever troubled me is in one of these jars, and I am not permitted to open them."

* * *

The Regent came at dusk, or what the garden offered as dusk — the bone-sky dimming to a deep amber, the jars brightening in answer, ten thousand small fires answering a signal I couldn't see.

It did not walk so much as arrive, row by row, the way night arrives: one moment the far shelves were empty, and the next there was a shape among them, tall and narrow, wearing robes the color of unwritten paper. Its face — I keep writing *its*, and I keep hesitating, because that was the horror of it, the final turn of the knife — its face was kind. That's the word. Kind. The mild, patient, gently smiling face of a grandfather at a bedside, of a priest at a confession, of every soft authority that ever told you your suffering was for the best. And beneath the kindness, when it turned its head and the amber light slid across it, I saw the structure underneath: a face worn down by forgetting, feature by feature, century by century, until what remained was only the *shape* of gentleness, the groove where a person had knelt so long that the kneeling had become the face.

"Bram Quintana," it said, and its voice was my own voice, roughly, the way a recording is your own voice — familiar and unbearable. "You carry a book that does not belong to you. You love a woman who taught you to steal it. And you have spent the whole of your walk here asking yourself one question." It drifted closer, robes whispering against the soil beds. "*Am I like them? Am I already becoming this?*"

My mouth was dry. "I haven't—"

"You have. Since the ferry. Since you watched that poor mute man and felt the first small relief that it wasn't you." The Regent gestured, and the garden opened around us, aisle after aisle, horizon after horizon of preserved lives. "Look at what we are, boy. Look carefully. Because you are the first visitor in three hundred years who has looked at this garden and wanted to *burn* it, and I find that fascinating, and I want you to tell me why, before Lady Vess decides for herself what you are worth."

Behind the Regent, half in shadow between two shelves of children's summers, Vess stood with her hand pressed flat against a jar I couldn't see the contents of, and her shoulders — a body's honest language, older than any mask — were shaking.

And I understood, standing in the museum of everything stolen, that the crack in her ran deeper than mercy. That somewhere in ten thousand sealed glasses was the memory of a mortal daughter, or a mortal name, or a mortal afternoon, and that the Regent's perfect archive had made one mistake no curator should ever make:

It had kept *her* too.

The Regent extended a hand like an invitation to dance. "Walk with me," it said. "Tell me about the Starwell. Tell me everything your witch believes. And perhaps — if you please me — I'll show you a jar with your own name on it. We've had it ready for years."

Vess's fingers, against the glass, curled closed.

And she did not step aside when I began to run.
