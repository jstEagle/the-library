## 11. Anchors

The cellar beneath Ilsa's house smelled of cold earth and old apples, and it was the only place in the town of Vell that still remembered what it was for.

Mara felt it the moment they descended the ladder — a hush like held breath, a sense of *keptness*. The walls were fieldstone mortared with something older than mortar; her palms read centuries of small devotions pressed into them, jar-seals and threshold-blessings, the hedge-craft of people who had understood, long before the stars went out, that memory was a thing you had to store against winter.

"Your father built this?" Bram asked Ilsa, ducking under a beam.

"He built the shelves." Ilsa lit a stub of tallow candle and set it on a crate between three dusty jars of preserved plums. "The cellar was here when he bought the house. The man who sold it to him said his grandmother used to sit down here and talk to the walls. He thought she was mad." She looked around at the stone, at the faint silver traceries only visible now, in candlelight, running through the mortar like frost through a windowpane. "She wasn't mad."

"No," Mara said quietly. She laid her palm flat against the wall and let the old workings answer her, thread by thread. "She was an anchor-keeper. This is a remembering-cellar. One of the last ones left, I'd wager."

Ilsa's face did something complicated — hunger and grief sharing the same expression. "Can it be used? Now?"

"It can be taught," Mara said. And then, because Bram was watching her with that look he got when he thought she was about to spend something she couldn't afford: "It will have to be."

* * *

They had been in Vell two days. Above them, the town went about its forgetting — the baker who greeted his wife each morning as though meeting a pleasant stranger, the children whose names their parents wrote on slates and hung around their necks because paper burned too easily and ink faded and the Hollow took written things first. Ilsa went out each day with her father's charts under her coat and came back with more blank spaces than she'd started with, marking not roads but absences: *here there was a mill, I think; here someone's grandmother is buried, or was.*

Mara had spent the days teaching. It steadied her, teaching — gave her hands work while her mind circled the thing she could not say aloud, the smooth place inside herself where a memory should have been, the shape of an absence she had learned to walk around the way one walks around a missing stair in the dark.

She had not yet told him. Every morning she woke intending to tell him, and every morning she found a reason — he was tired, they were hunted, the road ahead was hard enough without grief added to it. But reasons were just fear wearing practical clothes, and she knew it, and the knowing sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

Tonight the stone would come up or choke her. Because tonight she was going to teach him anchors, and anchors required truth, and truth required showing him exactly how much of herself she no longer had.

"Sit," she said, when Ilsa had gone up to sleep and the candle had burned down to a thumb's width. "Both of us. This lesson goes both ways."

Bram sat on the crate across from her, knees nearly touching hers in the low space. Twenty-two years old and he still sat like a boy called before the magistrate, shoulders up, hands folded, waiting to find out what he'd done wrong. He had been doing that since the night at the wardstone. Guilt had settled into his body the way silt settles into a riverbed, and she hated that she found it endearing, and hated more that she couldn't remember — not precisely, not anymore — the moment she had first found anything about him endearing at all.

"Do you know why the Hollow takes memories and not lives?" she began.

"Because memories are easier to carry?"

"Because memories are *food* and lives are only fuel." She turned her hands palm-up between them. "A life burns once. A memory can be savored. The Court doesn't kill the towns it passes through — it grazes them. It leaves the bodies walking so the bodies can go on forgetting, year after year, and every forgetting is another meal. That's what Vell is. A pasture."

Bram's jaw tightened. "And us?"

"We're not cattle tonight. We're carrying the keystone. The grimoire burns their tongues when they reach for us — you've seen the herald flinch." She had seen it, Lady Vess's perfect face flickering for half a heartbeat into something rawer, hungrier, angrier. "But the book protects itself, not us. So we protect ourselves. The way the old hedge-witches did, before the wards, before anyone believed the Court was real."

"How?"

"You choose a memory and you drive it deep — deeper than the Hollow can root for. You make it an anchor. Not hidden — the Hollow finds hidden things, hiding is just a scent trail — but *held*. You hold it so fiercely, so completely, that pulling it would pull all of you, and the Hollow doesn't want all of you. It wants loose threads." She met his eyes. "The way you hold it is you speak it. Aloud. To another person. A memory spoken and witnessed becomes load-bearing. That's the whole craft. That's the secret the valley forgot."

"That's it?" Bram said. "You just — say it out loud?"

"To someone who will keep it. Witnessing is the working. Which means—" and here the stone in her chest turned over, "—it means we have to trust each other with the truest things we have. Tonight. Both directions. Because if either of us is holding back, the anchor won't set, and the Hollow will know it, and it will pull until it finds the soft place."

He was quiet a moment. The candle flame leaned and righted.

"You first," he said.

* * *

She had planned this part. She had rehearsed it in the dark of the last three nights, lying awake in Ilsa's spare room listening to the town forget itself above her head. She would give him something true but survivable — her mother's kitchen, the smell of beeswax, the summer of the comet. Something warm enough to anchor, small enough not to cost.

What came out of her instead, when she opened her mouth, was the truth, because apparently some part of her had decided the matter without consulting her.

"I don't remember why I took an apprentice."

Bram blinked. "What?"

"The year you came to me. I remember the valley, the cottage, the wards humming along the ridgeline. I remember deciding I needed an apprentice — the wards needed a second pair of hands, everyone said so, the Council said so. But I don't remember *choosing you*. Do you understand the difference? There should have been a moment. A day when your name crossed my path and I said yes. There's nothing. It's like walking into a room and finding the window bricked over where a door used to be." Her voice stayed level, which took everything. "I've been telling myself it's age, or the wards leaching me, or ordinary forgetting. It isn't any of those. I know what my own forgetting feels like, Bram. This has edges. This was taken."

"When?" His voice had gone very quiet. "When did it happen?"

"At the ferry." She made herself say it plainly, no ceremony, because ceremony would be a kind of lie. "The ferryman. When I spoke his name out of the grimoire and gave him back to himself. The working needs a counterweight. A name restored requires a name risked. I paid with—" she gestured vaguely at her own temple, at the bricked-over door, "—with whatever that was. With the memory of choosing you."

The silence went on so long the candle guttered twice.

"You've been spending yourself," Bram said finally, and his voice had changed — flattened, careful, the voice of a man walking carefully across ice he already knows is cracking. "Since the ferry. How many?"

"Mara—"

"*How many.*"

"Three," she said. "The ferryman. The miller's daughter at Harrow Ford — the Hollow had her whole childhood, I gave her back her mother's face. And the watchman on the ash road, the one who'd forgotten which side he was on. I gave him his daughter's wedding." She lifted her chin. "Strangers. All strangers. People who would have been pasture otherwise. That's the arithmetic, and the arithmetic is sound."

"Is it." He stood up. In the low cellar he couldn't pace properly, so he stood there vibrating, hands opening and closing, and she watched six years of apprenticeship curdle into something hotter. "Let me check the arithmetic. You gave away the memory of choosing me. Of taking me in. Of — whatever that day was, whatever I was to you before I was a burden you inherited. You gave it to a ferryman who can't even thank you."

"He can. He does. Every time he poles that boat he's thanking me."

"Don't." His voice cracked on it. "Don't do that thing where you make sacrifice sound tidy. I've watched you do it since the wardstone. You hand out pieces of yourself like bread crusts and you smile and you never once ask whether anyone wants to be fed that way." He crouched suddenly, bringing his face level with hers, and his eyes were wet and furious. "Do you know what it's like learning that the person you — that the person keeping you alive is spending herself down like a candle to save people who'll never know her name? While the person who actually knows her, who *chose* to follow her even after she lied to him for six years, sits there watching?"

"You didn't choose to follow me. I dragged you out of the lowlands by the collar."

"You know that's not what happened."

She did know. She remembered the wardstone, his face white in the starless dark, the grimoire clutched to his chest like a stolen child — and then the moment he could have run, truly run, toward the world he'd wanted his whole gilded life, and hadn't. She remembered thinking: *he stayed. Why did he stay?* And then remembering, with a lurch, that she had no earlier page to turn back to, no origin, no reason. Just the fact of him, unexplained, like a lamp burning in a room no one had lit.

"Why didn't you tell me?" he was saying. "At the ferry. At Harrow Ford. On the ash road, when you taught me anchoring — you taught me the craft and never said you'd been bleeding into it for weeks—"

"Because it changes nothing!"

"It changes *everything*—"

"It changes nothing," she repeated, louder, and the stones overhead seemed to lean in, the old silver traceries brightening faintly, the remembering-cellar listening. "The well is ahead of us. The Regent is behind us. The book eats a piece of me every time we feed someone back to themselves, and it will eat more before the end — you know that as well as I do. So the only question is which memories I spend, and I will *not* apologize for choosing strangers' mothers over my own—"

"Over your own *what?*" he demanded. "Finish it. Over your own what, Mara?"

The candle flame stood perfectly still, as if the room were holding its breath.

"My own past," she said.

"No." He shook his head slowly. "No, you were going to say something else. Say it."

"I said—"

"You were going to say *my own heart*, or something near it, and you swallowed it, and that's the whole problem, isn't it? That's the entire rotten architecture of you." He laughed, and it was an awful sound, hurt dressed as humor. "You think I haven't noticed? You keep a ledger of what you're allowed to want. Duty gets everything. You get scraps. And when the scraps look like they might amount to something — to *someone* — you find a stranger who needs saving and you pay with the very memory you should have kept."

"And what would you have me do?" She was on her feet now too, close enough that the candlelight couldn't fit between them. "Keep it? Hoard myself? Walk past the ferryman and the miller's girl and the watchman and arrive at the Starwell *whole*, having saved no one, so that I can offer the well a complete life at the end — is that your plan for me, Bram? Is that what this anger is? You're angry because I'm spending myself in installments instead of saving it all up for one grand gesture?"

"Yes!" he shouted, and then, immediately, quieter, wrecked: "Yes. Because grand gestures end. Installments are just dying politely. Slowly. In pieces small enough that no one's allowed to grieve."

The words dropped into the cellar and lay there.

Mara heard her own heartbeat. Heard, above them, through the old stone, the town of Vell settling into its forgetting — a dog barking at nothing, a shutter closing, someone laughing at a joke they wouldn't remember by morning.

"Dying politely," she echoed.

"That's what you're doing."

"And what are *you* doing?" She stepped closer. "You think I can't see it? You've been polite since the wardstone. Polite at breakfast, polite on the road, polite when the herald offered you your pretty counterfeit past and you stood there wanting it — don't look surprised, I saw your face, I saw how long it took you to say no. You're polite the way a man is polite at his own funeral. You've already decided how this ends for you. You've decided you're the one who pays the final bill, and you're being *gracious* about it, and you think I don't notice because I'm busy bleeding."

"That's not—"

"You carry the book like it's already a headstone. You hold doors. You say *after the Starwell* like it's a place we're both going to arrive at. You've been writing your goodbye in the margins of everything you say, and you have the gall—" her voice finally broke, and she let it, "—you have the *gall* to accuse me of dying politely."

The cellar rang with the quiet afterward.

Then Bram sat down on the crate, slowly, like a puppet with cut strings, and put his face in his hands.

"She offered me a life where I never betrayed you," he said into his palms. "That night. Lady Vess. A past with no theft in it, no running, no wardstone. And God help me, Mara, the reason I said no wasn't courage. It was that every version of the past she showed me had no *you* in it. Not this you. Not the one who drags me by the collar and spends herself on strangers and lies by omission and teaches me craft by candlelight in a dead woman's cellar. I said no because losing a clean conscience was cheaper than losing you." He looked up. His eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady. "So no, I'm not planning to die politely. I'm planning to live impolitely, loudly, at your side, for a very long time, and I need you to stop making that impossible."

Mara stood over him and felt the argument go out of her like heat from a stone at dusk, leaving something rawer underneath.

"You realize what you're asking," she said. "You're asking me to stop paying for other people. To keep myself. To arrive at the Starwell whole."

"I'm asking you to stop deciding alone."

"That's not the same thing."

"It's the only version of the thing I'll accept."

She looked at him for a long time — this boy she had taken in and could no longer remember taking in, this man who had stolen from her and stayed anyway, who had been offered every erased sin he wanted and chosen the guilty version because the guilty version had her in it. The bricked-over door inside her ached. Behind it, she understood now, was not just a memory. It was the proof that loving him had been a decision, made once, freely, by a woman who perhaps had better judgment than she currently possessed — or worse, and happier.

"All right," she said.

His head came up.

"Anchors. Both of us. Truth for truth, all the way down, no ledgers." She knelt so they were level, knee to knee, the candle between them burning low. "You first. Your truest thing. The one you'd anchor the world to. Speak it, and I'll witness it, and it will hold."

He swallowed. For a moment she thought he'd refuse, or deflect, or reach for something safe — the comet-summer equivalent, the survivable warmth.

Instead he looked at her the way he'd looked at the vanished stars on the night everything broke, and said:

"The night before I stole the book. You fell asleep at the worktable over the ward-ledgers, and there was wax on your fingers, blue wax, from the seal-sticks, and you'd pushed your sleeves up and there was an old burn scar on your forearm shaped like a crescent moon, and I stood in the doorway for — I don't know how long. An hour. More. Just looking. And I thought: *when I leave tomorrow, this is the thing I'll take. Not the book. This.*" His voice frayed. "I thought I was memorizing it to keep. I didn't know yet that keeping and stealing were the same motion, or that I'd spend the rest of my life trying to give it back."

The old silver in the walls brightened — a slow tide of it, rising through the mortar, and Mara felt the memory settle somewhere beneath the cellar floor like a foundation stone sliding home. Anchored. Held. Witnessed.

"Kept," she said, and her voice was not entirely steady. "It holds."

"Now you." He wiped his face with the heel of his hand, unashamed. "Truth for truth. Your truest thing. And Mara—" a ghost of the old crooked smile, "—if you try to pay for a stranger with it, I'll know."

She closed her eyes.

And reached — carefully, the way one reaches into a dark drawer for a blade — past the bricked-over door, past the smooth hollow where the choosing used to be, down to what remained. Down to the one memory she had guarded without admitting she was guarding it, through three spendings, through the ferry and the ford and the ash road, through every rational arithmetic that said *this one, spend this one, it costs least.*

It did not cost least. That was the point. That had always been the point.

"There's a night I've never told anyone about," she said. "Two years ago. Before you ever stole anything. You'd been with me a year, and that evening you fell asleep in the herb loft and I came up to wake you for the midnight ward-check, and the light through the slats was falling across your face, and you were smiling in your sleep at nothing, at some dream I'll never know." She opened her eyes. "And I stood there and I thought — I thought, *oh no.* Oh no, oh no, not this, not him, not the boy I'm responsible for. And I went down the ladder and I checked the wards and I told myself it was exhaustion, and I spent two years telling myself that, and I was lying every single day." Her breath caught. "That's my anchor. That's the truest thing I have. The night I knew, and chose to pretend I didn't."

The cellar held it. The silver in the stones glowed like dawn under a door.

For a moment neither of them moved. Then Bram reached across the space between them — not far, inches, a distance six years wide — and took her wax-scarred hand, the one with the crescent burn, and laced his fingers through hers.

Neither of them said the word. They didn't need to. It was anchored now, witnessed, load-bearing, spoken into old stone by two people in a pasture town under a starless sky — and it would hold, or it would not, but it was *said*, and saying it had cracked open something in the air between them that no duty and no fear was going to reseal.

Above them, faintly, through the remembering-cellar's ancient stone, Ilsa's floorboards creaked. Then again. Deliberate. A warning rhythm they had agreed on — three slow steps meaning *someone's coming.*

Mara and Bram rose together, hands still linked, and blew out the candle.

Through the gap under the cellar door, in the dark, came a smell that had no business in a forgotten town in the middle of the night: cold air, high altitude, pine snow. The smell of the mountain pass. The smell of the Starwell road.

And beneath it, courteous as a knock, a voice Mara knew — a voice like an empty room agreeing to be beautiful:

"Forgive the hour. I've stopped hunting you, I promise. I only wish to *watch* you carry it the rest of the way."
