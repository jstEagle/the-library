## 12. The Starwell Road

The world began to come apart three days past the ash road.

I noticed it first in the stones. We had been climbing since dawn through a pass that Ilsa's charts called the Shepherd's Stair, and the path beneath our boots kept changing its mind about what it was. One moment it was flagstone, ancient and lichen-crusted, laid by hands that had known masonry as a form of prayer. The next it was raw scree, then packed earth, then something that wasn't quite any of these — a suggestion of ground, a rumor of footing, like walking on a word someone had almost finished saying. Twice my boot sank into hillside that had forgotten how to be solid, and I felt the mountain's indifference to my weight the way you feel a stranger's gaze: present, and wrong.

"It's not crumbling," Ilsa said behind me. She had her father's charts spread across a board strapped to her chest, and she was frowning at them with an expression I was coming to know well — the look of a girl watching the world disagree with ink. "Crumbling is a thing stone does. This is stone *unbeing*. Look —" She turned the chart toward me. The contour lines she'd drawn that morning had faded to gray ghosts, and where the peak of the Starwell's mountain should have risen in confident strokes there was only blank paper, as if the map had looked up at the country ahead and declined to describe it.

"The Hollow got here first," Mara said.

She was walking ahead of us, as she had walked ahead of us for days, and she did not turn around when she spoke. She never did anymore, when the talk turned to the Hollow. I had learned to read her by the set of her shoulders instead: the way they squared before bad news, the way they dropped half an inch after, as though each truth were a small weight added to a load only she could feel.

"How far ahead?" I asked.

Mara considered the question the way she considered everything now — too long, and with her eyes somewhere I couldn't follow. "Far enough that the forgetting has had time to soak down into the roots of things. Near enough that we're standing in it." She lifted one hand, palm out, fingers spread, and I saw the faint silver shimmer of hedge-craft gather along her knuckles like frost forming in reverse. "Stay anchored. All of you. Small memories, held loose but held. If the country tries to take your name, give it your grandmother's kitchen instead. Give it the smell of bread. It can't eat what you won't hand over."

I reached into myself for an anchor and found my usual one waiting: the evening Mara first let me hold the grimoire, back when I thought it was just a book, back when I thought she was just a witch and I was just a boy who wanted the world. I held it loosely, the way she'd taught me on the ash road — tight enough to keep, loose enough to breathe around — and felt it steady me against the wrongness of the ground.

What I did not let myself think about, not fully, was how many times I'd caught Mara doing the same thing and seen her hand hesitate. Seen her reach for an anchor and find, for a heartbeat, nothing there.

* * *

We camped that night in a hollow between two hills that could not agree on their own height. The hills shifted while we watched, subtle as breathing, one swelling as the other subsided, and Ilsa finally turned her back on them and declared she would rather trust her tent pegs than her eyes.

There was no wood to burn. There were no trees; there hadn't been trees since the ash road, and even the burned forest had at least remembered being a forest. Here the country had forgotten vegetation altogether, and the ground grew only a kind of pale moss that gave off light instead of absorbing it — a cold, greenish glow, like moonlight that had given up and settled into the dirt. We ate cold provisions by corpse-light and did not speak much.

It was Ilsa who broke the silence, because Ilsa always broke silences; she had inherited her father's terror of empty space, whether on paper or around a fire.

"My mother used to say the mountains up here were where the old gods went to be forgotten," she said. She was copying the day's observations onto a fresh sheet, working by moss-glow, her pen scratching in the quiet. "She said that's why the Starwell was built here. Not to be hidden. To be *forgotten on purpose*. A thing so important it had to be taken out of every mind that ever held it."

"That's not how forgetting works," I said. "Forgetting isn't a place you put things."

"No?" Ilsa looked up at me, and in the green light her face looked older than her years, cartographer's patience worn over a child's fear. "Then what is this country, Bram? What do you call a land where the roads don't remember themselves? My father charted this pass thirty years ago. He named eleven villages between here and the lowlands. I've found two. And one of those was just a shape in the fog that I *wanted* to be a village." Her pen hovered. "Either my father lied, or the world has been eating its own margins."

Neither of us said the third possibility: that both things were true, and that the eating had been going on far longer than the Court's recent hunger, longer than the fallen wards, longer than any of us. That the Starwell had been built in a wound.

Mara spoke from the edge of the camp, where she sat wrapped in her cloak facing downslope, watching the dark. "Your mother was closer than you know, Ilsa. The well was built to be forgotten. That was part of the working. A star that everyone remembers is a star that can be argued with. A star no one remembers simply *is*." She paused. "The wards hid it from the world so the world couldn't unmake it. And now the wards are down, and everything the hiding cost has come due all at once."

I watched her silhouette against the moss-glow and thought about the price of hiding things. I thought about a grimoire disguised as a mere book of hedge-craft, sleeping on a shelf for years while it held up the sky. I thought about a woman who had spent a memory to save a ferryman and would not say which memory, and how the not-saying had grown teeth between us, and how I loved her anyway, or perhaps because of it, or perhaps love and dread had become the same muscle in me and I could no longer tell them apart.

"Bram," Mara said quietly. "You're staring."

"I'm anchoring," I said.

A pause. Then, softer: "On what?"

"On the night you first trusted me with the book."

She didn't answer. But her shoulders came down from around her ears, just slightly, and I counted that as an answer, and I hated how much it meant to me, and I held the memory tighter.

* * *

The next day the maps failed entirely.

It happened at noon, under a sky the color of dirty pewter — the stars still gone, the sun a pale coin that gave light without warmth, as if it too had been hollowed and left running. Ilsa stopped mid-stride and made a sound I had never heard from her, small and strangled, and when I turned she was holding her father's master chart in both hands and the chart was *blank*.

Not faded. Not smudged. Blank — every line, every annotation, every careful village-name in her father's cramped hand, gone, the parchment smooth and white as an eggshell. She turned it over. The back was blank too. Thirty years of a man's life's work, erased between one footstep and the next.

"No," she said. "No, no —" Her voice climbed. "This is vellum, this is treated, this has survived rain and fire and *me*, this doesn't —"

"Ilsa." Mara crossed to her fast and took the girl's wrists. "Look at me. Not the map. Me."

"He drew every line himself. He carried it across the Stair twice. He said a map was a promise that places would still be there when you came back —"

"*Ilsa.*" Mara's voice cracked like a whip, and then gentled immediately, which was the most frightening thing about it. "Breathe. Anchor. Now. What's your father's name?"

Ilsa's mouth opened. For one horrible second nothing came out of it, and I saw the exact moment the panic found the hollow where the answer should have been — saw her eyes go wide and blind with a loss so fresh it hadn't yet learned its own shape.

And then her jaw set. I watched her do what Mara had taught us on the ash road, watched her reach down into herself with the deliberate ferocity of someone grabbing a drowning person by the hair, and when she spoke again her voice shook but it held.

"Tomas. His name was Tomas Ilsa Vey, and he smelled of ink and pipe smoke, and he hummed when he drew, badly, off-key, and he called me his little compass because I always knew which way was north." She was crying, but she said all of it, every word, like driving stakes into the ground. "He is *in me*. Whatever took the map didn't get him. It got the paper. Paper forgets. People don't have to."

"No," Mara agreed softly. "They don't have to."

I stood there holding the grimoire against my ribs and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, why the Court wanted it so badly. Because people *could* forget. People forgot every day, gently, without meaning to, and the Hollow had only ever been that gentle forgetting grown vast and hungry and organized. And the book — the keystone, the seal, the last argument the old world had made against oblivion — was warm against my side like a living thing, and I was its courier, and I was so tired.

"We navigate by memory now," Mara said. "Mine." She looked at the blank chart in Ilsa's hands, and something moved behind her eyes that I couldn't name — something that looked almost like relief, which made no sense, unless... "I walked this road once. Long ago. Before the valley closed. I'll hold the route."

"You walked to the Starwell?" I asked. "When? Why?"

"Later," she said, which was what she always said, and which had lately begun to sound less like deferral and more like a woman checking whether *later* still existed for her.

* * *

She navigated by memory, and I watched her spend them.

That was the only way I could describe what I saw. At each fork in the un-road, Mara would stop, close her eyes, and go inward — and I learned to see the signs of it, the slight catch in her breath, the way her lips moved around words that weren't spoken aloud. She was searching herself for the route the way you search a dark house for a candle, and sometimes she found the candle, and sometimes her hand closed on empty air where a candle should have been, and each time that happened her face went carefully, deliberately blank in a way that told me more than any expression would have.

At the second fork she paused a long time. When she opened her eyes she chose the left-hand path, but she stood there afterward looking at her own hands as if they belonged to a stranger, flexing her fingers slowly.

"Mara," I said. "What did you just remember?"

"A lesson," she said. "My teacher's voice. The cadence of it. How she made me repeat the warding litany until the words wore grooves in me." She smiled, and it was the worst smile I had ever seen on her, bright and brittle as ice on a puddle. "Useful. The route runs along the litany. Seven verses, seven landmarks. I only need the words, not the woman."

"Not the—" I caught Ilsa watching us and lowered my voice. "Mara. You're spending them. You're spending memories to find the way, and you're choosing ones you can afford to lose, and you're doing the arithmetic in your head right now about how many you have left."

"Don't," she said.

"—and you won't tell me the sum—"

"*Don't.*" Louder this time. Ilsa pretended very hard to study her blank chart. Mara stepped close to me, close enough that I could see the exhaustion sitting in the hollows under her eyes like bruises, and her voice dropped to something fierce and low. "You want to know what I'm spending? Fine. Here is what I am spending: the taste of a plum I ate when I was nine. The name of a neighbor's dog. The color of a dress I owned at fifteen. Small change, Bram. Copper coins. I am paying for our road in copper coins, and you will not make a tragedy of it, because we are walking out of the world and I will not arrive at the Starwell having spent anything that matters."

"And when the copper runs out?"

Her jaw tightened. "Then I'll spend silver. And after that, gold. And I will decide what counts as gold, and you will not get a vote, because you are carrying the keystone and I am carrying the map, and neither of us gets to be noble at the expense of the mission. Is that understood?"

I understood. That was the terrible thing. I understood her completely, because it was exactly what I intended to do myself, and we stood there in the forgetting country, two people planning their own quiet economies of loss, each certain the other couldn't afford the price.

"Yes," I said. "Understood."

She searched my face for a moment, as if checking the word for counterfeits. Then she turned and led on, and I fell into step behind her, and I did not tell her that I had already decided what my gold was. Some bargains you keep by keeping them secret.

* * *

Vess appeared at dusk on the fourth day.

I felt her before I saw her — a wrongness in the air behind us, a sensation like a door opening onto an empty room. I turned, hand already moving to the grimoire, and there she was: fifty paces back up the slope, standing on ground that rippled faintly around her boots, as though the country itself couldn't decide whether to hold her or not.

Lady Vess of the Hollow Court. Herald, huntress, the beautiful emptiness who had once offered me any past I wanted, even one where I never betrayed Mara. I had nearly said yes. I still dreamed about saying yes.

But she wasn't hunting. That was the first thing I understood, and it frightened me more than pursuit would have. She stood with her hands folded, her pale gown unmoving in a wind that moved everything else, and she simply *watched* us — the way you watch a fire you lit and cannot decide whether to feed or smother.

"She's been there since noon," Ilsa whispered. "Maybe longer. I keep catching her at the edge of my eye and then she's not — no, she is. She's not following. She's *keeping pace*."

"Why?" I breathed.

Mara had gone very still. She stared at the herald across fifty paces of unfaithful ground, and I saw her do something I had never seen her do: she inclined her head. Slightly. The way you greet someone whose nature you distrust but whose presence you acknowledge.

And Vess — the empty, courteous, utterly hollow Lady Vess — inclined her head back. And in the moment her chin lifted, I swear on whatever stars used to be up there, I saw something flicker across her face. Not expression. Expression requires a self to express. It was more like the *place* where an expression might someday grow — a shadow of a shadow, a scar where feeling had been cut away.

"She's changed," Mara murmured. "Since the garden. Since you escaped and she let you." Her eyes narrowed. "Or she's learning to want to change, which for one of them is worse. Or better. I genuinely don't know which."

"What do we do?" Ilsa asked. "Do we run?"

"Running spends strength we need for the Stair," Mara said. "And she isn't blocking the path. Look — she stands aside. She's *letting* us climb." She was quiet a moment. "So we climb. And we watch her watching us. And if that changes — Bram, you carry the book and you run, and you don't look back for either of us. Swear it."

"I won't swear that."

"Swear it anyway."

"I won't."

Something passed over her face — anger, and beneath the anger, unmistakably, terribly, tenderness — and she turned away from it before either of us had to survive it. "Then walk faster," she said, "and maybe it won't matter."

* * *

We climbed through the night, because sleep had become a country none of us trusted. The moss-glow thickened as we rose until the whole mountainside seemed lit from below, a green ghost-light that threw our shadows upward instead of down, so that we climbed beneath our own dark doubles. The air thinned and sharpened. Somewhere above us, though the summit stayed stubbornly invisible, folded away behind ridges that rearranged themselves when unobserved, the Starwell waited — the place where the sky had been bolted on, the place the grimoire ached toward. I could feel it now through the book, a pull in my sternum like a fishhook of light, stronger with every switchback.

Once, near midnight, I stopped to drink from a spring that ran uphill, which felt like the sort of thing this country would do, and while I drank I heard Mara behind me, speaking very quietly to Ilsa.

"—and when you anchor, don't grip. Gripping is fear, and fear has a shape the Hollow recognizes. Hold it the way you'd hold a bird you intended to release. That's the whole craft, girl. Everything else is commentary."

"What was yours?" Ilsa asked. "Your first anchor. On the ash road. You taught me with mine, but you never said yours."

Silence. Long enough that I stopped drinking and listened with my whole body.

"I don't remember," Mara said at last, and her voice was level in the way that ice is level. "Isn't that funny. I've spent so many teaching anchors that I've lost track of the first one. But I remember *that I had one*, and I remember it was enough, and some nights that has to be the whole prayer: I had one, and it was enough."

I stood there in the uphill-running water with my heart breaking in a way I had no right to allow, because I had secrets of my own, and hypocrisy is a heavy thing to carry alongside a grimoire.

* * *

Near dawn we came over a shoulder of rock and saw, for the first time, the summit — and the summit was wrong.

The Starwell's mountain rose ahead of us like a broken tooth, black stone streaked with veins of something that glimmered faintly, dully, like embers buried under ash. And above it, where the peak should have met the sky, there was no sky. There was a hole. Not darkness — darkness would have been honest. A hole in the color of the world, a ragged absence shaped vaguely like a crown, through which I could see... nothing, and in the nothing, faintly, arranged in a ring like a court seated in judgment, shapes. Tall. Patient. Waiting for us the way a web waits.

"The Court's seat," Ilsa whispered. Her pen was moving on fresh paper, recording it, because recording was how she prayed. "They've gathered. They know we're coming."

"They've known since the wards fell," Mara said. "This was always the appointment. We're merely arriving punctually." She stood with the wind pulling at her cloak, staring up at the crowned hole in the world, and I watched her take inventory of herself — I could see her doing it, the way a traveler counts their remaining coin — and whatever total she arrived at, she kept it behind her teeth.

Fifty paces below us, on the path, Lady Vess had stopped.

She stood at the border of some line only she could see, hands folded, head bowed — and I realized with a jolt that she was not stopping because she must. She was stopping because she was giving us the last stretch of road unwatched. Whatever she had become since the garden, whatever seed of the human had begun, impossibly, to regrow in her hollow — it was enough to make her grant us this: the final approach, unwitnessed.

She raised her head and looked directly at me, across the distance, across the difference between her kind and mine. And she mouthed something. No sound crossed the gap. But I read her lips the way you read a language you didn't know you knew:

*Hurry. Before I am myself again.*

Then the mist came up off the glacier fields and took her, and took the summit, and took the crowned hole in the world, and we stood alone on the last honest ground any of us would stand on for a long time.

Mara touched the grimoire at my side — one finger, brief, almost tender, though whether she touched the book or me I couldn't have said.

"One day's climb," she said. "Rest while resting is possible. Eat while eating means something." She looked up at the mist where the hole in the sky waited behind it. "Tomorrow we walk out of the world. Let's not arrive hungry."

Behind us, far below, faint as a memory of a memory, I heard the first verse of a warding litany being sung in a voice I almost recognized — and could not, try as I might, place at all.
