## 13. Ilsa's Choice

The Starwell did not look like a well.

That was the first thing Mara understood when they crested the last ridge and saw it: a bowl of white stone sunk into the mountain's shoulder, wide as a village square, its rim carved with names so old the letters had worn to suggestions — a lip of almost-words, a circle of what had once been said aloud. Inside the bowl there was no water. There was sky. Or rather, there was the place where sky should have been, a darkness so complete it seemed less like absence than like attention, something looking back up at them from below with the patience of centuries.

No stars reflected in it. Of course not. There were no stars left to reflect.

Mara stood at the rim with the wind pulling at her cloak and felt the grimoire shift against her chest where it was strapped beneath her coat, warm as a sleeping animal. It had been warm for three days now. Warm the way a kettle is warm just before it sings.

"We made it," Bram said behind her, and his voice cracked on *it*, and she did not turn around because if she turned around he would see her face, and her face was not yet ready to be seen.

Ilsa said nothing at all. The girl stood at the edge of the ridge with her father's charts bundled against her ribs, and she was staring down into that patient dark with an expression Mara had seen before — on the ferryman's face, in the burned forest, in the eyes of every person they had passed who had lost someone to the Hollow. It was the expression of someone doing arithmetic with grief. Counting what remained. Deciding what it was worth.

Mara had spent too many memories by now to mistake that look for anything innocent.

* * *

They made camp inside the rim itself, in the lee of a fallen wardstone half the size of a house, because the wind above the tree line had teeth and the stone broke it into something merely cruel. Ilsa built the fire with the competence of someone who had been cold often and recently. Bram unrolled the charts across a flat shelf of rock and weighted their corners with pebbles, though there was nothing left to navigate to; the Starwell was the end of every road, the last mark on the last map.

Mara sat apart, as she had been sitting apart for days now, and tried to inventory herself.

This was hedge-craft at its most brutal: the nightly audit. She closed her eyes and walked the corridors of her own past, touching doors, testing latches. Her mother's kitchen — still there, the smell of scorched barley bread, the burn scar on the doorframe shaped like a comma. Her first spell — still there, the taste of copper and rain, the way the candle flame had leaned toward her like a curious bird. The valley. The wards. The long gray years of keeping them.

She reached for the memory of taking an apprentice and found, as she had found every night since the ferryman, a smooth wall where a door should have been. Not gone, exactly. *Sealed.* As if some careful hand had plastered over it and painted the plaster to match the rest of the wall, so that only the absence of a handle told you anything had ever opened there.

She knew what she had spent. She had known since the night she lay awake in Ilsa's cellar and heard Bram breathing in his bedroll and realized she could remember teaching him everything except why she had agreed to teach him at all. She could remember his first day — could see him standing in her doorway, all elbows and hunger, clutching a letter of introduction written by some hedge-witch two valleys over. She could remember deciding to say yes.

She could not remember *why* she had decided. And the shape of that missing reason, when she pressed her tongue against it, was exactly the size and shape of something she was afraid to name.

So she didn't. She sealed the audit, banked the fire of her own attention, and opened her eyes to find Ilsa watching her across the flames.

"You do that every night," the girl said. "Count yourself."

"And what do you see when you watch me do it?"

Ilsa shrugged. Her face in the firelight was all angles and ash-smudge, fifteen years old and already carved by the country they'd crossed. "Someone checking whether she's still home."

Mara laughed — a short, surprised sound, more breath than voice. "Your father taught you that?"

"Nobody taught me that." Ilsa fed a stick to the fire. "You learn it, living out here. You check your people every morning. Da used to say the Hollow doesn't kick doors down. It just moves in room by room while you're asleep, and one day you wake up and can't remember why the house mattered." She looked up, and her eyes were very steady. "He's been gone four years. I don't remember his voice anymore. I remember the maps. That's all that's left of him — ink."

Bram, half-asleep against the wardstone, murmured something that might have been Ilsa's name or might have been a dream. The girl's expression softened and shuttered in the same instant, and Mara thought: *ah.* Thought: *there it is.*

She had watched this happen over six hundred miles of forgotten road. Watched Bram and Ilsa fall into the easy rhythm of the young — the shared jokes, the divided chores, the way they moved around each other in small spaces without collision, as if choreographed. And she had told herself it was nothing, because telling herself otherwise would have meant admitting what she felt watching it, which was jealousy, which was monstrous, which was also — she was honest enough, alone at night, to admit — proof of the other thing. The thing without a name. The thing whose memory she had spent to save a stranger who ferried boats.

Love, she had learned, was not a feeling. Feelings were weather. Love was a load-bearing wall. You only noticed it when it was gone and the ceiling came down.

* * *

The Court came at moonrise, and it came politely, because the Court always came politely first.

There was no fanfare. One moment the darkness inside the well was simply dark; the next it was *occupied*. Shapes rose through it the way reflections rise through black water — tall, robed, wrong at the edges, faces like pages from which the print has faded. They did not climb out. They arranged themselves around the rim at intervals, standing on nothing, and the wind died, and the fire bent flat as if bowing.

At their center, where the fallen wardstone met the well's edge, Lady Vess appeared the way a thought appears: suddenly, completely, already finished.

"Mara Quintana." Her voice was lovely. It was always lovely. It was the loveliest thing about her and the most horrifying, because it held no texture at all, no grain, no history — a voice like polished glass, like a bell rung in a room where no one had ever lived. "And the apprentice. And the cartographer's daughter. How industrious you've all been."

"We're not negotiating," Bram said. He was on his feet with his hand on the grimoire through his coat, and Mara loved him for the tremor in his voice that he refused to let reach his spine.

"Oh, but we are." Vess folded her hands. Behind her, the robed shapes stood motionless as furniture. "You've come all this way to relight the well. We've come to make sure you understand the terms. The Regent is scrupulous about terms — it always has been. It was a lawyer, once, before it was anything else." A smile, perfect and vacant. "The well feeds on willing memory. Willing. Given freely, with full knowledge of the price. It cannot be forced, cannot be tricked, cannot be paid from a purse the giver doesn't own. So here are the terms, plainly: one whole life, freely given, or memories equal in weight to every name we have eaten. Do you know how many names that is, witch?"

"I know you've been hungry a very long time," Mara said.

"Longer than hunger has had a name." Vess tilted her head. "So. Choose. Choose tonight, choose slowly, choose never — the Court is patient. But understand that every day you delay, we harvest elsewhere. Every village you pass through on your way home will be a little emptier. Every child a little harder to recall. The arithmetic does not stop because you find it distasteful."

"You could stop it," Bram said. "You could give it back. All of it. You were people once—"

Something flickered across Vess's face then, fast as a fish under ice, and was gone. "We were," she agreed softly. "That is rather the point of the museum." And the Court withdrew — not fled, not vanished, simply receded into the well's dark like tide going out, leaving the fire guttering and the three of them alone under a starless sky that felt, tonight, less like a blanket than like a lid.

* * *

Mara woke before dawn to an empty bedroll.

She knew before she opened her eyes. Some part of her, the hedge-witch part, the part that read weather in the behavior of smoke and animals, had been counting heartbeats in her sleep, and the camp's rhythm was wrong — two pulses where there should be three. She rose without sound. Bram slept on, exhausted past waking, his face young and defenseless in the gray light, and something in her chest pulled tight at the sight of him and she did not examine why, because examining it led to the sealed wall, and the sealed wall led nowhere.

Ilsa was at the well's rim.

She stood at the very edge, toes overhanging the inner curve of the bowl, her father's charts clutched to her chest, and she was talking — low, steady, the way people talk to the dying or the newly born. Mara drifted closer through the blue pre-dawn, boots silent on ancient stone, and caught the words.

"—so that's the whole of it. You take the charts too, if you want them. Ink remembers better than people do. He drew every river twice because he never trusted the first line, and the second line was always truer, and I used to think that was just how he was, but now I think it was a kind of prayer." A breath. "I'm not afraid. That's the strange part. I thought I would be. But there's nothing left in me that anyone would miss having, and there's everything out there that everyone else is missing, and it seems like simple sums."

*Ilsa,* Mara thought. *Oh, you foolish, brave, unbearable girl.*

"Ilsa." She said it aloud, and the girl flinched but did not turn. "Step back from the edge."

"It has to be willing," Ilsa said, in the tone of someone reciting terms she'd memorized. "That's what the lady said. Freely given. Full knowledge. I know everything. I've known since the cellar — I read your notes when you were sleeping, I'm sorry, but I did, and I understood it before either of you did, I think. You two keep looking at each other like the answer is each other. It isn't. It's me. I'm the lightest thing here. My life weighs almost nothing—"

"*Stop.*"

Mara crossed the distance between them in four strides and took the girl's shoulder and turned her, and the look on Ilsa's face stopped her breath — not defiance, not fear, but a terrible adult calm, the calm of someone who had done the arithmetic and found the remainder acceptable. Fifteen years old. Four years orphaned. A head full of other people's geography and a heart with a hole in it shaped like a voice she couldn't remember.

"Ilsa. Listen to me. I am about to do something, and afterward you are going to feel something return to you, and I need you to trust it. Can you trust me?"

"Why?" The girl's calm cracked, just at the corner. "What are you going to spend?"

"That," said Mara, "is not your concern. It is the entire point that it is not your concern."

She set down her pack. She drew the grimoire out and laid it open on the stone between them, and the book fell open — as it always did now, as if it had learned her — to the page of restoration, the working she had used on the ferryman, the one that pulled a stolen name out of the Hollow's grip and put it back where it belonged. The ink moved under her fingers like something breathing.

But this time she did not speak a name from the book.

This time she reached into herself, past the sealed wall, past the audit-corridors, down to the newest room in the house — the one built on the road, in the burned forest, in the cellar, in the small hours of a hundred camps. The room where Ilsa lived. The memory of finding her: the half-forgotten town, the girl on the doorstep with her father's charts and her chin up like a drawn line, saying *I can get you to the Starwell, I've been mapping the dark for four years, take me or I'll follow you anyway.* The exact weight of that moment. The exact stubbornness. The exact moment Mara had looked at this feral, brilliant, grieving child and felt something she had not let herself feel since —

Since the sealed wall. Since the apprentice in the doorway. Since the yes she could no longer explain.

*I choose this one too,* Mara thought, holding the memory up to the light of her own attention, letting it fill her, letting it become bright enough to burn. *I chose you. Both of you. Whatever duty says, whatever the craft costs — I chose, and choosing is the whole of the law.*

"Ilsa Varga," she said aloud, and her voice shook, and she let it shake, because willingness was the whole of the terms and she would meet them honestly. "Daughter of Anders Varga, who drew every river twice. Hear me: you are not the lightest thing here. You are the map. Maps are how the lost find their way home. And I give this freely — my memory of the day I found you, the day I chose you — to buy back what the Court took from you. Not your life. Never your life. Your *father's voice.*"

She spoke the working.

It was like opening a vein made of afternoon light. The memory poured out of her — the doorstep, the chin, the charts, the fierce tired eyes — and she felt it go the way you feel warmth leave a bath, gradually and then all at once, and she held onto the shape of it with both hands as it thinned, refusing to let it tear, feeding it out slow, because a memory given in terror was worth less than one given in tenderness and the well, the book, the whole rotten generous universe knew the difference.

On the stone, the grimoire flared. Ink rose off the page like a flock of black birds and hung in the air between them, and the birds became letters, and the letters became a sound — a man's voice, roughened by wind and pencil shavings, saying:

*"Ilsa. Supper. Come in before the light goes, and bring the west sheets, the damp's getting at them—"*

Ilsa made a sound Mara had never heard from her, would never be able to describe afterward — a sound like a door breaking open after years locked against a storm. She went to her knees on the stone. "*Da.*" And then, wildly, to Mara, tears cutting tracks through the ash on her face: "What did it cost you? What did you spend? Tell me — tell me so I can carry it too—"

"No." Mara knelt and took the girl's face in both hands, and her own vision was blurring, and she found she did not mind. "That is the lesson. Write it in your charts if you write anything. There is *always* something worth keeping — and the proof is that there is always something worth giving. They are the same coin, child. The Hollow eats because it has forgotten that. We remember. That is the entire war, fought in one sentence."

"But you'll forget—"

"Yes." Mara smiled, and it cost her everything, and she let it cost her, because that was the craft, the real craft, the one no grimoire held. "Yes. And tomorrow I will find you insufferable and brave and I won't know why I'm fond of you, and I will be fond of you anyway, because fondness outlives its reasons. That's what memory is *for*, Ilsa. Not keeping. Passing on."

Behind them, the sun cleared the eastern ridge and struck the well's dark water, and for one heartbeat — one — the surface did not swallow the light. It held it. A single coin of gold trembling on all that patience, like an eye remembering how to blink.

And in that heartbeat, Mara felt the hollow where the memory had been, and in the hollow, faint as a watermark, she felt the shape of the sealed wall beside it stir — the wall behind which the other choice, the first choice, the *yes*, was plastered over and waiting.

Two choices now, side by side in the dark of her. One spent. One hidden.

She was beginning to suspect they were the same choice. She was beginning to suspect she had chosen Bram on that long-ago morning for exactly the reason she had chosen Ilsa on the doorstep: because someone had to, because duty was a wall and love was the door in it, and she had spent her whole life guarding walls.

The light on the well faded. The Court's shapes stirred below the surface like sleepers turning over.

And from the bottom of the Starwell, soft as a secret, the Regent's voice rose through the stone and said her name.
