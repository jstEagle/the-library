## 14. The Regent Unmasked

They did not bind him. That was the first thing Bram noticed, and the thing that frightened him most.

Chains he could have understood. Iron, salt-warded rope, a cell with a door that locked — any of those would have meant the Hollow Court feared him. Instead they walked him through corridors of pale stone that seemed to have been grown rather than built, two silent attendants drifting at his shoulders like thoughts he couldn't shake, and no one touched him at all. There was no need. The grimoire had been lifted from his pack somewhere between the garden gate and the inner court; he felt its absence the way you feel a tooth gone from your jaw, tongue returning again and again to the smooth gap.

*They don't need to hold me,* he thought. *I'm already held.*

The attendants were not empty like Lady Vess. They were worse — half-full, the way a cup is half-full when someone has been drinking from it for a very long time. One of them hummed as they walked, a lullaby in no key Bram knew, and he caught himself trying to place it, and caught himself failing, and understood with a cold slide of horror that the tune was familiar and the memory of where he'd heard it was simply *gone*. Eaten. They passed close enough to breathe on him, and things fell out of his head like leaves out of a shaken tree: the name of the village where he'd been born — no, wait, that was still there, *Quintana's valley, the long road down* — but something else, some small bright thing, some afternoon, was missing, and he would never know which one.

"Please," he said, and hated how thin his voice sounded. "What did you take?"

The humming attendant turned its head. It had a face, more or less — features arranged the way a scarecrow's are arranged, in the right places but without conviction. "Nothing," it said, and its voice was kind, which was unbearable. "You gave. They all give, in the end. It only feels like taking because you're still counting."

Then the corridor opened into the Regent's hall, and Bram stopped counting anything ever again.

* * *

It was not a throne room. He had expected a throne room — every story about ancient courts had one, a high seat and banners and a floor polished by centuries of kneeling. What he got instead was a garden under glass.

The ceiling arched overhead in panes of something clearer than glass, and beyond them there were no stars. Of course there were no stars; there were none anywhere now. But here the darkness seemed deliberate, curated, a black velvet backdrop hung so that what grew below could be seen properly. And what grew below —

Bram had seen Lady Vess's courtesy. He had thought he understood what the Court did with what it took. But this —

Rows. Rows and rows of them, beds of dark soil stretching away into the gloom, and rising out of the soil on pale stems, flowers. Not flowers. He knew that even before he knelt at the edge of the nearest bed and looked properly. Each bloom was a sphere of soft light the size of an apple, veined with color that moved, that *shifted*, gold shading into green shading into a blue so specific it hurt — and inside each one, if you leaned close, if you let your eye unfocus the way Mara had taught him to read the grimoire's true pages, there was a scene.

A kitchen. A woman's hands kneading bread, flour to the wrists, a child tugging her apron. The light pulsed gently, in and out, like breathing.

A riverbank. Two boys skipping stones, one laughing so hard he had to sit down in the shallows.

An old man at a window, watching snow, and the look on his face was not sadness exactly, and not peace either, but that third thing that has no name, the one you only wear when you have outlived everyone who knew why you built the life you built.

"They're beautiful," said a voice behind him, "aren't they? Everyone says so. Even the ones being grown."

Bram did not turn around. Partly from fear. Mostly because he already knew, in the marrow-deep way you know a storm is coming before the first drop falls, whose voice it was, and he wanted one more moment of not seeing.

It was not a frightening voice. That was the trap of it. It was a warm voice, unhurried, the voice of someone who has sat up all night with the sick and the dying and learned to speak softly forever after. It was, he realized with a lurch, almost exactly the voice he imagined for himself when he rehearsed being old and wise.

"You can look at me," the voice said. "Everyone does, eventually. It's the last curiosity anyone has left."

He turned.

The Regent stood among its garden wearing a shape it had clearly assembled rather than been given. It was tall and slight, robed in grey that moved like smoke deciding to be cloth, and its face — Bram searched the face for a long time before he understood what was wrong with it. Nothing was wrong with it. That was what was wrong with it. It was a face with no history, smooth as an egg, with eyes that held nothing back because there was nothing in them to hold. It was the face of someone who had never been a child, never been embarrassed, never lost anything, because it had never *had* anything, and Bram felt his stomach turn over, because he recognized the expression. He'd worn a version of it for years, looking east over the valley wall, hungry for a world he'd never seen.

The Regent was homesick for everything. That was the horror of it. That was the whole horror, all of it, in one face.

"I'm told," the Regent said, drifting closer along the row of glowing blooms, "that you read the grimoire. The true pages. So you know what my Court is, or you think you do. A court of thieves. Harvesters of names." It paused beside a flower showing a wedding — a small one, poor people, borrowed chairs, real joy. "May I show you what you actually know? Which is nothing?"

Bram said nothing. His silence felt like agreement, and he suspected it was meant to.

"The wards around your valley," the Regent said, "were raised eleven hundred years ago. You know that part. Your histories say they were raised against us." It smiled, or performed the shape of a smile. "Your histories were written by the people who raised them, which is the first thing anyone should ask of a history: *who profited from writing it this way?*"

"The grimoire says—"

"The grimoire says what the warders wrote into it, yes. Prison seal. Ancient war. Very dramatic." The Regent bent over the wedding-flower and adjusted something at its stem with one long finger, tenderly, the way Mara adjusted seedlings. "Let me tell you what actually happened, apprentice. Eleven hundred years ago, the world ended. Not with fire. Not with flood. It ended the way a person ends, slowly and then all at once, under the weight of everything it remembered and could not put down."

* * *

It walked as it talked, and Bram walked with it, because the alternative was standing alone in the dark among other people's lives, and somehow walking beside the thing felt less terrible than that.

"There was a war," the Regent conceded. "There is always a war. But not the one in your book. The war was against grief. Do you understand what I'm saying? Grief itself. The world had accumulated so much of it — plagues, famines, wars within wars, children buried by parents until burying children became ordinary — that it could barely turn beneath the weight. Whole provinces went silent. Not dead. Silent. People stopped speaking because every word they owned was attached to a memory of loss, and saying *bread* reminded them of the famine year, and saying *mother* reminded them of the grave, and so they sat in their houses and remembered, and remembering became a country you could live in, and most of them moved there permanently."

The Regent stopped at a bed near the center of the garden, and this flower was different. Dimmer. Older. Inside it, barely visible: a field, and figures burning something — a pyre, many pyres, a horizon of smoke.

"This was the first," the Regent said quietly. "This is me."

Bram's mouth was dry. "You were a person."

"I was a person. A hedge-witch, actually, though we didn't use that word — a comfort-woman, a keeper-of-small-remedies, the one you called when the fever came. I lived through the year the sickness took the valley's children. All of them. Every child under ten, in one season." The Regent's smooth face did not change, but its voice developed, for just a moment, a crack in it, a hairline fracture running through the warmth. "And I discovered something that the wise men and the kings and the warders never discovered, because they never had to. I discovered that a mind can hold only so much grief before it begins to spill. And what spills is not the grief. What spills is everything else. The grief gets heavy and the rest goes over the sides, and you keep the sorrow and lose the Sunday afternoons, and you keep the graves and lose the names, and in the end you are a vessel shaped entirely by what broke you, holding nothing but the breaking."

"And so you forgot," Bram said. "On purpose."

"I forgot *one thing*. One memory. I cut it out of myself with a working older than any of your books, and I chose the worst day of my life, and I set it down." The Regent gestured at the dim old flower. "And do you know what happened? I woke the next morning and the sky was bluer. The bread tasted like bread. I could walk past the pyres. I had room again." Its voice softened into something dangerously like confession. "So I did it again. And again. And other people begged me to teach them the working, and I did, and they cut out their worst days too, and they made room, and they lived. We were not thieves, Bram Quintana. We were surgeons. The world was dying of remembering, and we amputated."

"But it didn't stop there."

"No." The Regent resumed walking, and the crack in its voice sealed over, smooth as its face. "It never stops there. That is the lesson. That is the *only* lesson, and no one learns it until they've done it. Because once you have taken one memory, you know it can be done, and once you know it can be done, every remaining memory becomes a thing you *could* lose, and fear is also a weight. So you take another, to stop fearing the second one. And the taking gets easier — mercy always does — and soon you are not cutting out grief at all. You are cutting out anything inconvenient. Anything painful. Anything that *aches*. And an ache, apprentice, is how a person knows they are still attached to the world." It spread its grey hands. "We hollowed ourselves out and called it healing. And when we had hollowed ourselves completely, when there was nothing left in us but the taking, we discovered that emptiness does not die. Emptiness *persists*. It is the most persistent thing there is. And it gets hungry."

* * *

They had come, without Bram noticing, to the far end of the garden, where the glass ceiling met a wall of raw stone, and against the stone stood a single pedestal, and on the pedestal lay the grimoire.

Open. To the true pages. Laid open with a care that made Bram's chest hurt, the way you'd lay open a sleeping child's door.

"So now the truth," the Regent said, "the truth your Mara has probably already guessed and cannot bear to say aloud, because she is braver than I ever was and bravery is mostly the ability to keep carrying what should be set down." It rested one finger on the page, not touching the ink. "The wards were never a prison for us. Prisons are for things that might escape, and we cannot escape ourselves; we are everywhere forgetting happens. No. Look at the working. Look at what the keystone actually anchors."

Bram looked. He had read these pages a dozen times, and now, with the Regent's words loosening something in him, he saw it — saw the structure of the great ward-spell the way you suddenly see the figure hidden in a pattern of leaves. The wards drew inward. Every line of them, every anchor-point, every knot of the working converged not outward at the border but inward, down, into a single deep chamber beneath the valley, and the chamber was not empty. The chamber was a vessel. The largest vessel ever made.

"It holds grief," Bram whispered. "The wards hold the world's grief."

"Eleven hundred years of it. Everything the warders could gather, drawn out of the world the way a physician draws poison, and sealed under your pretty valley, and your valley has been light and green and gentle ever since, hasn't it? The safest place in the world. Everyone says so. And no one asks *why*, no one asks what it costs to keep one corner of the world clean while everywhere else drowns." The Regent turned to him fully, and its empty eyes were wet, and Bram recoiled from the sight of tears on that blank face more than he had recoiled from anything in the garden. "Someone must hold it. That is the whole of the secret. Someone must hold the grief, or it spreads, and the world drowns again, and this time there will be no warders left with the strength to build a vessel. My Court holds it now, badly, greedily, the way a starving man guards bread — we were supposed to be its keepers and we became its hoarders, and I will not pretend otherwise. But the office remains. The burden remains. *Power is just pain with somewhere to go.* Give the pain somewhere to go, and you are a healer. Nowhere to go, and you are a god of emptiness, alone in a garden full of other people's Sundays."

"Why are you telling me this?" Bram's voice came out rough. "Why show me the garden, the truth, all of it?"

"Because you are going to have to choose," the Regent said simply, "and I refuse to win by lying. That is the one vanity I have kept. Let the others hunt and herald and bargain — Vess means well, in whatever's left of her meaning. But you, Bram Quintana, runaway, thief, boy who loved the world outside his walls enough to break them — you deserve to choose with your eyes open. Take the book. Carry it to the Starwell. Relight the stars, free every stolen name, save every village that has forgotten its children. And then ask yourself: who carries the grief home afterward? Where does it go? Into whom?" It stepped back, and the grey robes settled, and it looked for one strange moment very small. "Mara will offer herself. She has been offering herself since the ferry, piece by piece; surely you've noticed the holes in her. And you love her, which means you will try to stand in front of that offering, and one of you will succeed, and the world will have its vessel, and the vessel will be someone who is loved. It is always someone who is loved. That is how the world pays its bills."

The attendants appeared at the edges of Bram's vision, drifting forward, gentle as tide.

"Rest," the Regent said. "Eat. We will not take anything from you tonight — I want you whole for the choosing. And apprentice—" It waited until he looked back. In the dim garden light, surrounded by the soft breathing glow of a thousand stolen afternoons, the first person who ever forgot wore an expression that Bram would carry longer than almost anything else: not menace, not triumph, but the exhausted tenderness of someone handing a stranger a weight and apologizing for it.

"When you see her again," the Regent said, "look at her hands. Count what's missing. Then decide which of you the world deserves."

They led him away down corridors of pale stone, and behind him the garden breathed its slow light, and somewhere above the glass ceiling the starless dark pressed down like a hand, patient, waiting to see what he would carry.
