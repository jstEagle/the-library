## 15. Vess Remembers

The Starwell's threshold was a wound in the mountain, and the Court had stitched it shut with silence.

Mara felt it before she saw it — the way the air thickened as they climbed the last switchback, how the stones beneath her boots went smooth and nameless, worn by no one's feet because no one remembered walking here. Behind them, Ilsa clutched her father's charts against her chest like a shield that had already failed. Ahead, where the well should have opened its throat to the sky, stood Lady Vess.

She was alone this time. No heralds, no hollow riders fanning out across the ash road behind her. Just Vess, standing at the mouth of the threshold with her hands folded and her beautiful empty face tilted toward the starless dark above, as though she were listening for something.

"You've made good time," Vess said, without turning. Her voice was still that terrible courtesy, silk drawn over nothing. "The Regent expected you tomorrow. It is pleased. Pleased things are careless things."

Mara stepped in front of Bram without deciding to. The grimoire hung heavy against her ribs, warm as a sleeping animal. "Then we'll disappoint it."

"Oh, I hope not." Vess turned then, and Mara saw what she had refused to see at their first meeting: that there was nothing behind the beauty. Not cruelty — cruelty required appetite, preference, a self that wanted. Vess was a lamp with the flame removed, lit anyway, shining on nothing. "The Regent has prepared a place for all of you. In the garden. There are jars enough."

Ilsa made a small sound. Bram's hand found his knife, useless and human.

But Mara had spent three days watching this creature shadow them instead of hunting them, and she had learned to read emptiness the way she read weather. Something in Vess had changed. She stood wrong for an ambush — weight settled, patient, like someone waiting out a storm rather than bringing one.

"You're not here to take us," Mara said.

"I'm here to hold you until the Regent arrives." A pause. The first hesitation Mara had ever heard in that voice. "That is what I am here to do. Whether I do it is another matter entirely."

* * *

They had told stories in the cellar under Ilsa's town, the night before the final ascent. That was the anchor-craft Mara had taught him — hedge-witch work, old as hearthsmoke: speak your truest memory aloud, give it weight and witness, and the Hollow cannot pull it loose. Bram had gone last, and he had been generous with himself, the fool, the dear fool, spending his anchors like a man scattering coins to beggars.

One of those stories had been about a girl on a ferry dock.

Not Mara. Not Ilsa. A stranger — a woman Bram had never met, seen only once through the mist on the lowland river, years ago when he'd first run from the valley. He'd described her in the cellar with the particular tenderness boys waste on strangers: how she'd stood at the rail of the ferry with a child asleep against her shoulder, and how she'd hummed to it, some tuneless thing, half lullaby and half lament, and how the sound had carried across the water and made Bram, alone and frightened and far from everything he knew, feel for one moment that the world was not entirely lost.

He hadn't known why he kept the memory. He'd only said, *it's mine, and it's kind, and I'm not giving it up.*

Now Mara watched Vess stand at the threshold of the Starwell, and she understood.

"That story," Mara said quietly. "On the ferry. You heard us tell it."

Vess's hands unclasped. Refolded. "The Hollow hears everything its heralds walk past. We carry sounds home like bees carrying pollen. Most of it dies in us. There is nowhere for it to live." Her empty eyes fixed on Mara with something that was almost focus. "Hers didn't die."

"Hers," Mara repeated.

"The woman on the ferry." And then Vess said a name — a small, ordinary name, a name like bread or rain or any child's name, and the saying of it cracked her voice down the middle. "Her daughter was called that. Before. When I was—" The sentence failed. Vess looked down at her own perfect hands as if they belonged to a stranger. "I don't remember being anyone. That is what the Court does. It doesn't kill you. It *tidies* you. Every grief, every love, every ugly human weight — removed, for your comfort, forever. I have been comfortable for four hundred years."

The wind came down off the mountain and moved through the dead grass, and even the wind seemed to be listening.

"But the story," Vess said. "The humming. My daughter used to—"

She stopped. Pressed two fingers to her lips. And Mara watched something impossible happen on that vacant face: moisture gathering at the lash line, the body remembering weeping before the self could remember why.

"I recovered one thing," Vess whispered. "Out of everything the Court ever took from me, one thing grew back, from a stranger's kindness, told aloud in a cellar by a boy who didn't know what he was giving away. One memory. My daughter, asleep against my shoulder, humming to her doll because I used to hum to her." Her composure finally broke — not into sobs but into a terrible stillness, like ice before it goes. "Do you understand what the Regent is? It isn't a god of forgetting. It is the first person who ever forgot, and it cannot bear that anyone else might *keep*. If I bring you to it willingly, perhaps it will give me back my tidiness. Perhaps it will take this away again, this awful—"

"Or," Mara said, "you let us pass."

* * *

It did not happen quickly. Nothing true happens quickly.

Vess stood in the threshold's mouth for a long time while the dark pressed down and Ilsa's breathing went shallow and Bram's hand stayed frozen near his knife. Mara did not push her. You cannot rush a soul back into a body; you can only keep the door open.

"They will dissolve me," Vess said at last. "When the Regent learns what I've done. Do you know what dissolution is? It isn't death. Death leaves a shape. Dissolution is being poured out. Four hundred years of service, and my reward will be to become weather."

"Yes," Mara said, because lying to her would have been its own kind of hollowing. "Probably."

"And if you fail anyway. If the well stays dark and the Regent takes every name in the world—" Vess's chin lifted, and for one moment the herald's mask slid back over the mother's face, and both were unbearable. "Then I will have traded eternity for nothing."

"You'll have traded it for a chance," Mara said. "Which is more than the Court has ever offered anyone."

Bram stepped forward then. Mara felt him move beside her rather than saw him — she always knew where he was now, some new sense the mountains had taught her, or some older sense she refused to name. He looked at Vess the way he looked at hard pages in the grimoire, working them out loud.

"When the Regent comes," he said, "it will come fast. It always sends its power ahead of itself, the way a wave sends its shine. But a wave has to gather before it breaks. If you stand in the threshold and refuse it — really refuse, with your whole whatever-you-have-left — it has to stop and deal with you. It can't just pour past a herald who stands against it. The wards worked that way. One will, holding a line."

"You know this how?" Vess asked.

"I read the book that was the lock," Bram said. "Somebody had to."

Something happened to Vess's face then that Mara would carry for the rest of her life, however long the rest of her life proved to be. It was a smile — but not the courteous lamp-glow she'd worn at their first meeting. This was crooked, and pained, and utterly human, the smile of a woman remembering that her mouth had once known how.

"My daughter," Vess said, "was afraid of thunderstorms. She would climb into my bed and press her ear against my heart and say, *tell the sky to wait.*" She drew a breath she did not need and had not taken in four centuries. "Tell the sky to wait. Very well. I am done telling it to hurry."

* * *

The Regent came at moonset, which was no longer a time so much as a direction.

It did not walk. The darkness simply arrived along the ridge like frost arriving on glass, and where it touched, the mountain forgot its own stone — boulders going gray and featureless, the path behind them softening into unmarked snow. Mara felt it pulling at her already, patient fingers hooking into the edges of her memories, testing each one's knots. She gripped her anchors the way Bram had taught her to grip rope in floodwater. *The ferryman's face when he spoke his name. Ilsa laughing over her charts. Bram, younger, furious, stealing what he thought was a book.*

And Vess turned to face her master.

What followed, Mara had no words for at the time and has never fully found words for since. The Regent did not shout. It offered. The dark around Vess filled with murmurs — Mara caught fragments, sweet as rotting fruit: *your tidiness, restored; the ache removed; you need not feel this, none of us need feel anything, come home.* The night bent toward Vess like a crowd bending toward a fallen rider.

And Vess stood in the threshold of the Starwell with her arms spread wide, filling it, a wall of one.

"No," she said.

The murmurs rose.

"*No.*" Louder now, ragged, magnificent. "You took my daughter's name and I lived four hundred years without missing it, and that is the most obscene thing you ever did to me — not the taking, the *not missing*. Well, I miss her now. I miss her with one memory and it burns like a brand and I will not give it back. I am your herald no longer. I am her mother. And her mother says: *tell the sky to wait.*"

The Regent struck her.

There was no other word for it. The dark came down on Vess like a sea coming down on a candle, and Mara screamed and Bram shouted and Ilsa buried her face in the charts — but Vess held. She held the way the old wardstones must have held, the way one will holds a line, and the blackness broke around her in slow tides, unable to pour past, unable to unmake her all at once because she was refusing it with everything she had, and everything she had, it turned out, was one memory, burning.

"She cannot last," Bram gasped. "Mara — the threshold — *go*—"

Mara went. She seized Ilsa's wrist and Bram's sleeve and hauled them both past Vess — past the terrible bright ruin of her, the dissolving edges, the hair lifting away like smoke, the face still turned toward the Regent with its crooked human smile — and through the wound in the mountain, into the dark throat of the Starwell.

At the entrance Mara stopped, one step inside, and looked back.

Vess was mostly light now, or mostly absence — Mara couldn't tell which, and suspected there was no longer a difference. The herald's beauty was gone, poured out, and what remained was smaller and plainer and infinitely more real: a woman-shaped stubbornness, holding the door.

"Go," Vess said, and her voice was almost gone too, thin as the last ring of a bell. "Feed your well. Relight your stars. And if you find, in all the names the Court has eaten — if you find a girl who hummed—" The smile flickered, the last of her. "Tell her the sky waited."

Then the dark closed over her, and the threshold behind them sealed like water closing over a stone, and Mara ran into the heart of the Starwell with a dead woman's errand folded into her chest alongside the grimoire, alongside the hollow where her own spent memories ached like weather, alongside the boy she loved and could not save and would not stop trying to.

Ahead, the well waited. Below, something vast began to climb.

And above them all, in the country of the Court, a single point of light appeared in the starless sky — small, stubborn, human-bright — and did not go out.
