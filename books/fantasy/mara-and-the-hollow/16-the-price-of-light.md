## 16. The Price of Light

The Starwell did not look like a well.

It looked like the place where a well had been dreamed and then abandoned halfway through the dreaming — a shaft sunk into the crown of the mountain, ringed with stone so old its carvings had worn to suggestions, to the memory of memory. Bram stood at its lip and felt the cold come up out of it like breath, like something sleeping far below had turned over in its sleep and exhaled.

Above them, where stars should have been, there was nothing. Not cloud, not haze. Nothing. He had lived eleven days under that nothing now, and he still could not make his eyes stop searching it. Every time he looked up, some animal part of him flinched, expecting light, finding absence, and every flinch was a small wound he was learning to stop feeling, which frightened him more than the wound itself.

*You get used to anything,* he thought. *That's what the Hollow counts on.*

Ilsa slept by the fire they'd dared at the threshold, curled around her father's charts like a child around a doll, her pencil still tucked behind her ear. She had refused to go further down. "Maps end here," she'd said, with the flat finality of someone who has thought about it. "I'll wait where the world is still shaped."

So it was Bram and Mara alone who descended the worn stair that spiraled the well's inner wall, their boots ringing on stone that rang back wrong — too long, too deep, as though the mountain were hollow as a bell and something below was listening for the note.

Mara went first. She always went first. He watched the set of her shoulders in the grey witchlight she carried cupped in her palm like water, and he thought: *I have followed this woman out of one life and into another, and I have never once told her why.*

The stair let them out onto a ledge above the well's heart, and there the grimoire woke.

Bram felt it before he understood it — a warmth against his chest like a second heartbeat, then a pulling, insistent, the way a hound pulls at the leash toward home. He unslung the satchel and drew the book out, and it opened itself in his hands, pages turning without wind, until it lay flat at a spread he had never seen before.

The ink on those pages was not ink. It was starlight, pressed flat and dried into legibility, and it moved when he wasn't looking directly at it, rearranging itself the way dreams rearrange when you try to hold them.

"Read it," Mara said quietly. "Out loud. The well will want to hear it in a voice."

He read.

*What was extinguished may be rekindled,* the grimoire said, in Bram's own voice, in words older than any tongue he knew but which he somehow understood the way you understand your mother's footsteps on a stair. *Light is not made. Light is remembered. Each star is a name, held burning by those who loved the bearer of it. When the Court eats a name, the star does not die — it goes dark, waiting, as a lamp waits for oil.*

Bram's mouth went dry. He read on.

*To relight the sky requires remembering. Willing remembrance, freely given, in measure equal to all that was taken. For every name the Court has swallowed, a memory must be fed to the well — a true memory, held with love, surrendered without coercion or regret.*

He stopped. The arithmetic arrived in his head all at once, cold and enormous, and he stood there doing it while the witchlight trembled in Mara's hand.

Every name. Every person the Court had eaten since the beginning — and the Court had been eating since before the valley's wards were raised, since before the ancient war, since before anything Bram had learned in Mara's schoolroom. Thousands of years of harvest. Villages. Kingdoms. Generations whose descendants no longer remembered they had ever existed.

"How many?" Mara asked. Her voice was steady. Her hand was not; he saw the witchlight shiver.

"I don't—" Bram turned the page, hoping the book would do the counting for him, and the book obliged, cruelly.

*A whole life, freely given,* the next page said, *may stand in place of the sum. One soul's every remembering, poured out entire, outweighs the ledger of the taken. This is the price of light. There is no lesser coin.*

The silence after that was the loudest thing Bram had ever heard.

Below them, far down the shaft, something moved — a glimmer, faint as fish-scale, turning slowly. The well was not empty. The well was *waiting*, patient as only very old things are patient, and Bram understood suddenly that it had been waiting a long time for someone to climb down to this ledge and read these pages aloud, and that it did not care which of them it was.

"No," Mara said.

He hadn't said anything yet. He hadn't even looked at her. But she had known — of course she had known, she had been reading him for eleven days the way she read weather, and whatever she'd seen in his face just now had answered a question she'd been dreading.

"Mara—"

"No." She set the witchlight down on the ledge, and it burned there between them like a small captive sun. "I've watched you do the sums, Bram Quintana. I've watched you do them since the ash road. You think I haven't noticed how you count things? How you keep a running tally of what everything costs? You've been pricing yourself since the day I met you, and I am telling you now, before you say one more word: the answer is no."

"The book says a whole life—"

"The book says a whole life *freely given*, and I am telling you it won't be yours." She stepped closer, and up close her eyes were the grey-green of the valley in early spring, the valley he had betrayed and she had forgiven, or hadn't, or both. "Because I got here first."

And Bram saw it — saw it the way you see lightning before you hear it, the whole shape of the strike laid out in the sky — and he lunged for the grimoire at the same moment she did, and neither of them got it, because the grimoire chose that instant to close itself with a sound like a door shutting, and the witchlight guttered, and in the half-dark he heard her whisper the first word of a working he knew.

"Mara, don't—"

She spoke the second word.

He threw himself at her, arms around her shoulders, dragging her back from the ledge, from the shaft, from the slow glimmer turning below, and she was stronger than she should have been — hedge-craft, anchor-work, the small fierce magics she'd taught him on the ash road all bent now toward one purpose — and they went down together onto the stone, grappling like children, like enemies, like two people who loved each other too much to let the other one win.

"Let me go," she said through her teeth. "Bram. Let me *go*."

"It's supposed to be mine," he gasped. "It was always going to be mine. I stole the book, I broke the wards, I led Vess to the ferry crossing — everything the Court has taken since we left the valley traces back to me—"

"That is the stupidest arithmetic you have ever done," she snarled, "and you once tried to price a healing against three days' labor," and despite everything, despite the terror, something in him almost laughed, because she was furious at him in exactly the way she'd been furious at him over botched salves and lazy warding-chalk, and fury that familiar is its own kind of love.

They broke apart, breathing hard, sitting in the wreckage of each other's grip, and the witchlight steadied, and the well went on waiting below with its fish-scale patience.

"You don't get to decide this alone," Bram said finally. His voice came out rougher than he meant. "Neither of us does. That's the whole lesson, isn't it? That's what you taught me. Magic done alone is magic done badly."

Something crossed her face then — grief, or fear, or the ghost of the memory she'd spent at the ferry and never named, the choosing-him she no longer had and couldn't miss because missing requires remembering. He watched her reach for an answer and find the shelf where it should have been standing empty.

"Then we decide together," she said at last. "We sleep. We anchor. And tomorrow, at dawn, we talk about it with clear heads, and nobody moves toward this well without the other one watching. Swear it."

"I swear."

"Swear it properly. Anchor-word and all."

He took her hands. Hers were cold. He anchored the oath the way she'd taught him in the burned forest — a small memory, held up to the light of the mind and spoken aloud so it could not be pulled loose or quietly set down: *the smell of her kitchen the first morning, bread and rosemary and woodsmoke.* "I swear I will not give myself to this well without you knowing, without you awake, without your hand in mine if it comes to that. So witnessed."

Her turn. She was quiet a long moment, searching, and he realized with a lurch that she was searching for a memory to anchor the oath with, and coming up short, because she had spent so many — the ferryman, the threshold, the girl in the cellar town whose name none of them could recall anymore — and what remained was precious and few.

"Rain on the schoolhouse roof," she said finally. "My first year teaching. I swore I'd never grow tired of the sound, and I never did. So witnessed: I will not give myself to this well without you knowing, without you awake, without your hand in mine."

"So witnessed," he echoed, and the oath settled over them both like a weight, like a rope tying two climbers together over a crevasse, and he thought: *a rope works both ways.* He thought: *she has tied herself to me, and she believes that protects her.*

He thought: *there is more than one way to be awake.*

They climbed back up the stair as the false night deepened. Ilsa stirred at the fire and asked, sleep-thick, "Did you find it? Did you find how?"

"We found the price," Mara said.

"Is it bad?"

Mara looked at Bram across the fire, and he looked back, and between them passed the whole unsaid sum of it — the ledger, the life, the well turning patiently below.

"It's the worst kind of bad," Mara said. "It's fair."

Ilsa nodded slowly, the way children nod at things they don't understand but can tell matter, and rolled over, and slept.

Bram did not sleep. He lay with his back to the stone and watched Mara watch the empty sky, and he turned the grimoire's words over in his mind like stones in a riverbed until they were smooth: *one soul's every remembering, poured out entire.* A whole life. Freely given. No lesser coin.

But the book had not said whose life. The book had not said the giving had to happen in daylight, or with witnesses, or with the other climber's hand in yours. The book had said *willing*, and *free*, and Bram had spent eleven days learning from Lady Vess exactly how wide and soft those words could stretch.

Mara's breathing slowed toward sleep. The witchlight burned low. Above the mountain, the nothing went on being nothing, vast and patient as the well.

*I'm sorry,* he thought, not knowing yet to whom — to her, to the oath, to the boy he'd been who stole a book and ran. *There has to be another way. And if there isn't, then it has to be me, and it has to be soon, before you wake.*

Far below, the Starwell turned its slow glimmer, and waited, and kept his secret the way it kept everything: perfectly, and in the dark.
