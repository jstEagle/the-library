## 17. Love Against Duty

The Starwell did not look like a well from below. It looked like a wound in the mountain.

Mara stood at the edge of the last shelf of stone and felt the cold come up through her boots — not the honest cold of altitude, which she had known all her life, but a colder thing, a *subtractive* cold, as though the mountain itself had been hollowed out from within and what remained was only the memory of rock, holding its shape out of habit. Above them, where the sky should have been, there was nothing. Not cloud. Not dark. Dark was something; dark had texture, had weight, had the smell of coming rain. This was absence pressed flat against the world like a hand over a mouth.

She had climbed toward it for six days. Her legs remembered every one of them, even now, even trembling on this ledge of black glass that had once been, Ilsa said, a stair.

"Here," Ilsa said behind her, voice small in the vastness. The girl unrolled the last of her father's charts across the stone and weighted the corners with pebbles, though there was no wind up here to move them. There was no wind anywhere. "This is as far as the maps go. Past this line he wrote *here be kept things* instead of *here be monsters.* He crossed it out twice."

"He knew," Mara said quietly.

"Everyone knew something." Ilsa folded her arms against the cold she could not feel properly either. "They just didn't know they knew. That's worse, isn't it?"

It was worse. It was the whole shape of the age, folded down into one sentence: a world that had forgotten it was forgetting, going about its business in the dark like a house whose family has died but whose clocks still keep the hours.

Bram crouched at the chart's edge, tracing the line of the old stair with one finger. In the grimoire-light — the book open on his knee, its true pages shedding a pale gold that was the only warm color left in creation — his face looked older than his twenty-two years. It had been aging all journey, she thought. Every mile had taken something soft off him and left something harder underneath, and she could not decide whether to mourn the softness or be grateful for what it had become.

"The stair holds for another hundred paces," he said. "Then it's the shaft. Then the heart of it." He looked up at her, and the light caught the gray in his eyes and made it silver. "Mara. Before we go down. There are things—"

"There are always things," she said, too quickly, and hated herself for it.

He closed the book. The gold went out of the world and the absence rushed back in, and in that sudden darkness she heard him stand, heard the scrape of his boot, felt him come close enough that she could feel the warmth of him — real warmth, living warmth, the kind the Hollow Court could never make and therefore could never stop wanting.

"Mara Quintana," he said, "you have been about to tell me something since the ash road. You start and stop like a woman counting coins she's afraid to spend. So I'll say mine first, and you can spend yours after, or not at all, or throw me off this mountain, whichever seems best."

"Bram—"

"I love you."

Three words. She had faced down heralds and hounds made of unremembered grief; she had walked roads that forgot themselves beneath her feet; she had spent pieces of her own mind like a miser spending blood, and none of it had frightened her the way those three words did, said plainly, in the dark, by a boy she had chosen and then deliberately forgotten choosing.

Because she had forgotten. That was the rot at the center of her, the secret she had carried since the ferryman opened his eyes and spoke his own name and wept. She had gone into herself afterward, into the ledger of her own remembering, looking for what she'd paid — and found the entry blank. Not hidden. *Blank.* A smooth place where something had been, the way a riverbed is smooth where a stone sat for a thousand years and then was lifted away.

And then, three nights ago, in Ilsa's cellar, when they had traded their anchors aloud like children trading treasures — *the smell of bread in my mother's kitchen, the first time I flew a kite, the day my master called my work good* — she had understood. Because Bram had given an anchor she had never heard before: *the day Mara Quintana chose me.*

He remembered being chosen. She did not remember choosing.

The ferryman's name had cost her that. The book had reached into her and taken the single memory most bound to the working — and the working had been mercy for a stranger, so the price had been the root of everything else. She had lost the moment a younger Mara had looked at a hungry, brilliant, runaway-hearted boy and said *yes, this one, I will teach this one,* and out of that lost moment had grown every lesson, every argument, every evening by the fire, every slow terrible tenderness she had refused to name because naming it would mean admitting she had let duty slip.

She had thought, at the time, that she was being careful. She had thought she was being *good.*

"I know," she said at last. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "I know you do. And I have to tell you why that frightens me, and it isn't the reason you think."

"It's the reason where you think you don't deserve—"

"Bram." She put her hand flat on his chest, over his heart, feeling it hammer against her palm like a bird against a window. "Let me finish. I've finished nothing in my life. I've deferred everything. Let me finish this."

He went quiet. Even the mountain seemed to lean in.

"When I took you as apprentice," she said, "I chose you. Do you understand? Out of forty petitioners that spring, I chose you — the runaway with the stolen spellbook and the burned fingers and the mouth that never stopped asking *why.* I chose you because you were hungry in a way the others weren't. Hungry for the world. And I told myself it was because hunger makes the best students." Her throat tightened. "But that wasn't all of it. Somewhere in those first years — I don't know when, the memory won't give me the date, only the shape of it — I began to love you. And I *felt* myself beginning, the way you feel rain starting, and I did what I always do with anything that threatens the valley's safety."

"You buried it," Bram said softly.

"I warded it." She laughed, and it came out cracked. "Of course I warded it. I'm a hedge-witch; warding is what I *do.* I built a little border around my own heart and posted signs on it: *duty this way, feeling that way, apprentices keep to the path.* And I told myself it was discipline. I told myself a witch who loves her apprentice is a witch whose judgment bends, and a bent judgment gets people killed, and the valley cannot afford dead people. Do you see? I made my love for you into a *dereliction.* A failure of office. Something to be guarded against like blight in the wheat."

"And then the ferryman," Bram said.

"And then the ferryman." She drew a breath that hurt all the way down. "The book took the memory of choosing you. And do you know what I felt, when I found the hole where it had been? Relief. For one shameful heartbeat, *relief* — because the choice was gone, and if the choice was gone then the love growing out of it was orphaned, rootless, and I could set it down without breaking my vow. The Hollow didn't just take a memory, Bram. It took my excuse. It handed me back my heart with the duty-ward broken, and I have been pretending ever since that I didn't notice the door standing open."

The silence stretched. Far below them, somewhere in the shaft, something sighed — a long, empty sound, like wind through a house with no one in it.

"I noticed," Bram said. His voice was rough. "God help me, Mara, I noticed years ago. The way you'd stand too close to the fire and never ask me to move. The way you said my name when you thought I was asleep. I thought — I told myself I was flattering myself. An apprentice's vanity. But I watched you build those walls, brick by brick, and I loved you *for* it, which is either very noble or very stupid, and I've never been able to tell which."

"Both," Mara said. "It's both. That's what nobody tells you about duty — that done honestly, it's indistinguishable from foolishness right up until the moment it saves someone."

"And when it doesn't?"

"Then it's just a beautiful way to lose."

She turned from him at last and faced the shaft. The Starwell's throat opened in the mountainside like a pupil blown wide, and down inside it, faint as a coin at the bottom of a fountain, something glimmered. Old light. Waiting light. The last unspent coin of the sky.

"I refused once already," she said. "Duty asked me to choose between the valley and my heart, and I chose the valley, and I told myself it was right, and maybe it was, maybe it was, I'll never know now because I paid the memory of knowing. But here's what I do know." She turned back and took his face in both hands, and his skin was cold from the climb and stubbled and *alive*, and she held it like something rescued from a fire. "Duty is not going to win twice. I am done letting it spend me by installments. If we go down into that well and the price is a life, I will pay it — not because duty demands it, but because *you're* in the world, and Ilsa's in the world, and the ferryman's grandchildren will be born into it, and I choose them. Do you hear the difference? Duty *commands.* Love *chooses.* And a choice is worth more than an order. Any god worth worship knows that."

"Mara—" His hands came up over hers. "If it comes to a price, it's mine to pay. I stole the book. I broke the wards. I started this."

"You were a boy who wanted to see the stars up close."

"I was a coward who ran."

"You were nineteen," she said, "and the valley was small and your hunger was enormous, and those are not crimes. I should have told you what the book was. I should have trusted you with the truth instead of trusting you to fail. We have both been paying interest on each other's silences for years, my love, and I am *done* with silence."

His breath caught on the word. *My love.* She felt it catch, felt his hands tighten over hers, and she did not take it back. She would not take it back. She had spent enough of her life taking things back.

"Say it again," he whispered.

"My love." Steadier now. Warmer. The words fit her mouth like a key she'd owned all along and never tried in the lock. "My Bram. My runaway. My hungry-hearted fool who walked into the dark carrying the keystone of the world because he couldn't bear to leave it behind. I love you. I loved you before the ferryman took the proof, and I love you now without it, which means it wasn't in the memory at all — it was in *me*, it survived the spending, it's the one thing that Hollow cannot eat because I keep giving it away faster than they can steal it."

He kissed her.

It was not gentle. Six days of ash road and forgetting and fear came out of both of them at once, and the kiss was salt and desperation and the taste of the last clean water they'd drunk that morning, and his hands slid into her hair and hers fisted in his coat, and above them the starless nothing pressed down and below them the well glimmered and neither of them cared, because this — this warmth, this *witness*, two people choosing each other in full knowledge of the cost — this was the exact opposite of the Hollow. The Court ate memory. This was memory being made, bright and new, in the teeth of the dark.

When they broke apart, foreheads touching, breathing hard, Bram laughed — a wet, cracked sound.

"We're doing this now," he said. "At the mouth of hell. Under a dead sky."

"Now is the only time there is," Mara said. "You taught me that, actually. Third year. You ate the offering bread before the rite because you were hungry and the gods, you said, would understand hunger better than ceremony."

"They did understand. The rite worked better that year than any year after."

She laughed too, and the laugh hurt, and she let it hurt, because she was done numbing things. Whatever came next, she would feel all of it. That was her vow now, sworn on the lip of the Starwell: *feel all of it, spend nothing unwillingly, refuse to let the dark make you small.*

Ilsa cleared her throat from the chart. "I hate to break up the—" she gestured vaguely at the two of them, "—but the light down there is getting brighter. Or closer. I can't tell which, and both options frighten me."

Mara looked down the shaft. The glimmer had strengthened — a pulse now, slow as a sleeping heart. The well knew they were there. Perhaps it had always known. Perhaps it had been waiting, patient as a seed, for someone willing to climb down and wake it.

She straightened. Took up the grimoire from where it lay and pressed it into Bram's hands, folding his fingers over the cover.

"Together," she said.

"Together," he agreed.

They came to the shaft's edge, the three of them, and the cold breathed up out of the deep like the exhalation of something enormous and sleeping, and Mara felt her heart go quiet and clear as a bell struck once.

Whatever waited below — the Regent, the price, the arithmetic of names and lives — it would find them unarmored and unashamed. It would find a witch who had finally stopped warding her own heart, and an apprentice who had finally stopped running, and a girl with her father's charts and nothing left to lose.

It would find them *choosing.*

Mara stepped down onto the first stair of the deep, Bram's hand warm in hers, and behind them, far above, in the absolute dark where stars should have been—

—one point of light flickered. Just one. Small and white and trembling, like a candle relit in a window.

Like something answering.

Or like something watching, and learning their names.
