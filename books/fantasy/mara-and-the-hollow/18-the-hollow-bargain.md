## 18. The Hollow Bargain

Mara slept the way she did everything: stubbornly, as if sleep were a duty she had finally consented to perform and would perform perfectly or not at all.

She lay in the lee of the Starwell Road's last standing wall — a farmhouse that had forgotten it was a farmhouse, its stones grown soft and shapeless as old bread, its doorway opening onto nothing in particular because the country around them no longer held enough memory to decide what a door should lead to. Ilsa had spread her father's charts over the two of them like a blanket, weighting the corners with pebbles, and now she sat a little apart with her back against the wall, sharpening her mapping pencils with a knife gone thin from use. The scratch of the blade was the only sound. That, and Mara's breathing.

Bram watched her breathe.

He had been watching for an hour. He knew this because he had counted — four hundred breaths, give or take, each one a small proof that she was still here, still hers, still carrying whatever remained of herself after all she had spent. He had memorized the rhythm of it without meaning to, the way he had once memorized the rhythm of her footsteps on the tower stairs, the way she said *again* when his warding sigils came out crooked, the way lamplight caught the grey in her hair when she bent over the grimoire at night and pretended not to notice him noticing.

He had memorized everything. That was the problem. That was, he understood now, sitting in the forgetting dark of the world's last road, the entire problem.

The grimoire lay open on his knees.

It had opened itself. That was the only way to describe it. Somewhere past midnight — if midnight still meant anything in a sky with no stars to mark it — the book had stirred against his chest like a waking animal, and when he drew it out, the pages turned without his hand, past the ward-cant and the seal-lore, past the prison-truth they had learned on the ash road, down into a section neither of them had ever reached: pages written in an ink that was not ink, a brown so deep it was nearly black, in a hand that changed as you watched it. Sometimes it looked like his master's hand. Sometimes it looked like his own.

At the top of the page, in letters that seemed to be written slightly beneath the surface of the paper, as though the words were drowning:

*A bargain may be struck.*

He had read it eleven times. It did not change. Some part of him had hoped it would.

"Bram." Ilsa's voice, low. She didn't look up from her pencils. "You've been staring at that book like it owes you money."

"It might," he said.

"Does it?"

He didn't answer. Ilsa nodded slowly, the way she did when a map confirmed what the ground already told her, and went back to sharpening. She was fourteen and had already lost a father to the Hollow — not dead, worse, walking somewhere in the lowlands with a smile and no name behind it — and she had learned early not to ask questions whose answers would require her to put down her knife.

* * *

The remnant of Lady Vess came at the hour when the dark was deepest, which was every hour now, but this one felt deepest anyway.

Bram heard her before he saw her — not footsteps; Vess had never made a sound he could point to — but a kind of pressure, a hush in the air like the moment before snow. He rose carefully, easing the grimoire shut, and stepped out beyond the ruined wall into the open dark.

She stood perhaps ten paces away, and she was less than she had been.

When they had first met her, on the ferry road, Lady Vess of the Hollow Court had been beautiful the way an empty house is beautiful: perfect proportions, perfect courtesy, nothing home. Now she was translucent at the edges. Her outline wavered like heat-shimmer, like a reflection in water someone had dropped a stone into. Where her eyes had been there were still eyes, but they had begun, very faintly, to hold something — a smudge of color, a suggestion of grief — and Bram understood with a lurch that this was what dissolution looked like from the inside. She was coming apart. And in the places where she was coming apart, something mortal was leaking through.

"You should not be awake," she said. Her voice had lost its music. What remained was quieter, rougher, almost — almost — human. "The witch anchored you both. I felt the working. Old craft. Good craft."

"She's asleep. You're talking to me."

"I am talking to the book," Vess said. "It is merely using your ears."

The grimoire grew warm against his ribs. Not painfully. The way a hand grows warm when another hand covers it.

"You're dying," Bram said. "The Regent is unmaking you."

"I betrayed it. There is only one punishment, and it is thorough." She said it without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. "I have perhaps a day. Less. I wanted to see the three of you before—" A pause. In the pause, the smudge of color in her eyes deepened, and Bram thought, absurdly, of a candle guttering before it goes out, flaring bright on its own burning. "Before I stopped wanting to."

"Why?" The question came out harder than he meant. "Why defect? Why throw away whatever a hollow thing has instead of a soul, just to buy us an hour inside the well?"

Vess tilted her head. The gesture was so human it hurt to watch.

"I remembered my daughter," she said simply. "One memory. Out of everything the Court took, out of six hundred years of harvested names, one memory survived in me — buried so deep even the Regent could not find it. A girl in a yellow dress, laughing at rain. I do not know her name anymore. I do not know mine. But I know she laughed at rain, and I know I loved her, and that has been enough to burn me alive from the inside ever since Bram Quintana told his stories in the Regent's garden." She smiled, and the smile cracked her face like ice on a pond. "You cannot unknow a daughter. Did you know that? The Court never learned it. They thought emptiness was safe. Emptiness is just pain with nowhere to go."

*Power is just pain with somewhere to go.* The Regent's words, spoken in its garden among the jars of stolen childhoods. Hearing them again in Vess's ruined mouth, Bram felt the truth of them settle into his bones like cold water finding its level.

"The book wants something," he said.

"Yes."

"And you're here because—"

"Because the book asked me to witness." Vess lifted one fading hand, and the grimoire answered it, sliding free of Bram's cloak and hanging open in the air between them, pages riffling though there was no wind. "A bargain requires a witness who can enforce terms. I am — I was — the Court's herald. Terms are my oldest craft. Even dying, I can bind a bargain. Even dying, especially dying."

"What bargain?"

"Ask it," Vess said. "Not me. I only carry the pen."

* * *

The grimoire spoke to him the way Mara had once taught him the wards spoke: not in words, exactly, but in understandings that arrived fully formed, the way you wake some mornings already knowing what you dreamed.

It showed him the arithmetic first.

The Starwell needed feeding. Willing memories, freely given, equal in weight to every name the Hollow Court had eaten — six centuries of harvest, villages upon villages, the whole drowned ledger of the forgotten. No handful of recollections could balance that scale. Not Mara's remaining years of spent and spendable past. Not Ilsa's charts, precious as they were. The well demanded either a river or a life: one whole existence, surrendered entire, its every memory poured out at once. One person, giving everything, in place of everyone.

And Mara had already decided it would be her. He had seen it in the way she'd looked at the well's threshold that afternoon, the way she touched the grimoire's spine like a woman touching a door she intended to walk through. She was spending herself by inches — the ferryman's name, the reason she'd taken an apprentice, whatever else she had fed silently into strangers along the ash road — and she thought he hadn't noticed, and she planned to make the last payment before he could stop her. Die politely. She would call it duty. She always called it duty.

Then the book showed him the alternative, and his whole body went cold.

*His memories of Mara. Every one.*

Not his life entire — the book was precise about this, and the precision was its own cruelty. The well's scale weighed names, and the Court's greatest single theft, the keystone theft, the theft the whole harvest had been bent toward since the beginning, was the bond between the witch and her apprentice: the love the Regent had scented across the valley the night the stars went out, the one living thing in the world bright enough to relight the sky alone. A whole life given freely was one currency. A whole love, given freely, was another. Either would balance the scale. The book did not say which weighed more. The book did not have to say. Bram already knew.

He stood in the starless dark with the open book hanging in the air before him and understood exactly what he was looking at.

If he gave his life, Mara would live and grieve him, and grief was a country she knew how to survive; she had been surviving things her whole hard life. If he gave his memories of her — every lesson, every argument, every night by the grimoire's light, every moment of the falling-in-love they had never said aloud until the dark beneath the starless sky — then he would live. He would walk out of the Starwell alive, breathing, whole, and he would look at Mara Quintana and see a stranger. A powerful stranger, a witch he had apparently served, a woman with grey in her hair and a hollow where something used to be. He would not know why his chest ached when she spoke. He would not know why the word *again*, in her voice, could undo him. He would stand beside her under the reignited stars and feel nothing, and she would know everything, and she would spend the rest of her life being loved by a man who could not remember loving her.

That was the price. That was the whole price. Not death — something the book seemed to consider gentler, and which Bram considered far worse.

"No," he said aloud.

The pages waited.

"I'm not doing that to her. I'm not standing next to her for forty years as a ghost wearing my face. Find another way."

The book turned a page. The drowning ink shifted, and new understanding arrived, and it was worse.

There was no other way. The scale did not negotiate. The Court's harvest was too vast, the well's hunger too exact. Memories of Mara, or Mara entire. Those were the only two weights that would tip it. The book had carried this knowledge since before Bram was born — since the first witch bound it as the wards' keystone and hid the terms in ink that only showed itself to someone standing at the end of the world with no good choices left.

Behind him, through the broken wall, he could hear Mara breathing. Four hundred and twelve. Four hundred and thirteen.

"Would she even know?" he asked quietly. "What I did. Would she find out?"

The book's answer was gentle, which was the cruelest thing about it. She would know. The well does not accept secretly. Every memory given must be given knowingly, willingly, named aloud before a witness — that was the law that kept the Hollow from simply stealing, the law the Court had spent six centuries learning to circumvent. He would have to speak each memory as he surrendered it. *The night she found me sleeping in the library. The day she taught me the anchor-craft. The word she used when she was disappointed in me.* He would say them all, into the well, while Mara slept fifty feet away — and afterward, when the stars burned and the world remembered itself, she would piece together what he had done, because everyone else would remember him loving her, and he alone would not.

She would spend her whole life knowing. He would spend his whole life not knowing what she knew.

"That's monstrous," Bram said.

Yes, agreed the book.

"She'd hate me. She'd hate herself for letting me—"

Yes, agreed the book.

"Then why are you offering it?"

The pages stilled. When the understanding came, it came not from the book at all but from behind him, from the ruined wall, from the sound of a woman breathing in her sleep — and Bram realized the book had never been offering anything. The book was a lock. He was the key turning himself.

Because you would rather be empty than let her be nothing, said the understanding. Because that is what love is. The Court never learned it. The Regent never learned it. Six hundred years of harvesting names, and they never once understood that this — this exact thing, this willingness — is the only weight the scale truly measures.

* * *

Lady Vess waited while he walked, alone, to the edge of the forgotten country and stood there for a long time doing nothing but listening to the dark.

Ilsa had fallen asleep against the wall, her father's charts rising and falling with her breath. Above them all the sky was a black so complete it felt less like night than like a held breath — the whole heavens waiting to see what the small figures on the road would do. Somewhere behind them, down the mountain, the Hollow Court was gathering for its last attempt to stop them. Somewhere ahead, above the tree line that no longer quite existed, the Starwell waited in its ring of standing stones, hungry and patient and honest about what it cost.

Bram thought about running. He examined the impulse honestly, turned it over like a stone: take the grimoire, take Ilsa, flee south into whatever countries still remembered themselves, let the valley go, let the world go dark at its edges the way it had been going dark for six hundred years, and keep her. Keep every memory. Grow old beside Mara Quintana in a shrinking world and call it love.

But he had seen the Regent's garden. He had walked between the shelves of jarred childhoods, the labeled griefs, the loves pickled in glass. He knew what a world looked like when the people in it chose keeping over giving. It looked like a museum with nobody left to visit it.

And he thought about Mara waking tomorrow and walking into the well with her chin up, calling it duty, spending six hundred years of other people's salvation on the altar of her own discipline — and something in his chest closed around the thought like a fist.

No. Not twice. She had told him, in the dark, why she had spent the memory of choosing him: because loving an apprentice felt like dereliction. Duty had won once. He would not stand aside and let it win twice.

He walked back to Vess.

"Terms," he said.

Her ruined face did something that was almost tenderness. "Speak them, and I will bind them. Know what you bind yourself to, Bram Quintana. The well does not refund."

"My memories of Mara Quintana. All of them. From the first day to the last — everything, freely given, in place of her life." His voice held. He was surprised by that; it seemed like the sort of voice that should break. "In exchange: the stars relight, the Court releases every stolen name, the Regent unravels, and Mara lives. Lives long. Lives whole. And Ilsa—" his voice did crack, slightly, on the girl's name, "—Ilsa gets her map under real stars."

"Noted and bound," Vess said softly. Faint light gathered around her dissolving hands, the last magic of the Court's herald turned to the service of its enemy. "One condition more, from the witness. Speak the memories aloud at the well. All of them. Do not rush them. Let her hear them in her sleep if she must — let some part of her keep them, even if you cannot." The smudge of color in her eyes flared, guttered. "I never got to say my daughter's name. Don't make her mistake."

"When?" Bram asked. "Tomorrow? At the threshold?"

Vess shook her head, and the shimmer of her grew thinner as she moved, as if the motion itself were costing her what little she had left.

"Soon," she said. "Sooner than you want. The Regent knows what you intend — it has always known; it watched you fall in love the way a spider watches a web tremble. It will force the moment before you choose it. Be ready to step into the well, Bram Quintano. Be ready to speak fast, and mean every word." She was nearly transparent now, a woman-shaped absence with two embers floating where her eyes had been. "And tell the witch nothing. That is the hardest term, and the kindest. If she knows, she will find a way to counter-bid, and the scale will take you both, and six hundred years of names will stay eaten. Your silence is the load-bearing wall of the whole bargain. Can you bear it?"

Bram looked back at the ruined wall. At the shape of Mara beneath the charts, breathing. Four hundred and sixty. Four hundred and sixty-one.

He thought: *I will get one more morning. She'll make tea badly and correct my pronunciation and pretend she wasn't worried, and I will look at her and know it's the last time I will ever know her, and she won't know, and I have to carry that alone for one whole day.*

"Yes," he said.

"Then it is witnessed, and it is bound." Vess's voice was fading with her, growing distant though she hadn't moved. "One kindness more, apprentice, from a mother who forgot: when you stand in the well and the memories leave you — they do not die. Nothing the Hollow takes dies. They go up. They become the light. Somewhere in the sky there will be one star that burns with the shape of her laugh. Learn to find it. You'll have a long life of looking."

And she was gone — not dramatically, not with light or thunder; she simply thinned into the general dark, the way frost leaves a window, leaving the glass clearer and colder than before.

* * *

Bram sat down against the soft stones of the forgotten farmhouse and took out the grimoire and did not open it. He didn't need to. He knew its terms now the way he knew his own heartbeat.

Above him the sky stayed black. Below him the earth stayed half-remembered. Beside him, Mara slept the sleep of a woman who believed she had already chosen, who believed the worst decision of her life was behind her, who trusted him — trusted him completely, trust being the one thing she had never learned to ration — to let her.

Tomorrow, or the day after, the Regent would come, and the moment would come, and Bram Quintana would step into the Starwell and begin to speak.

Until then, he decided, he would count her breathing. He leaned his head back against the wall, opened his eyes in the dark, and started from one.
