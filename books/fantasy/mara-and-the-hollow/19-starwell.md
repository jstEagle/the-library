## 19. Starwell

**Bram**

The well did not look like a well.

It looked like a wound in the shape of a circle — a ring of black stone at the summit of the forgotten country, and inside the ring a darkness that was not empty but *expectant*, the way a held breath is not silence. The stars should have been down there. Bram could feel them the way you feel a word on the tip of your tongue: present, pressing, refusing to surface.

He stood at the rim with the grimoire under his arm and Mara's sleeping weight against his shoulder, and he let himself have one minute. One minute to look at her face in the lightless dark, lit only by the faint grey seep of the well itself.

She had argued with him for three days about sacrifice. She had called him *polite* — the worst thing she knew how to call anyone — and she had been right, and that was the whole trick of it. He had learned politeness from her. He had learned how to give things away so smoothly the giving looked like nothing at all.

Vess's remnant stirred at the edge of the circle, a woman-shaped absence fraying at every border, and her voice came like wind through a keyhole. *The bargain is struck. The book consents. Wake her and it fails, apprentice. She would spend herself before you finished drawing breath.*

"I know," Bram said.

*Then know this too.* Vess's emptiness flickered — and inside the flicker, for half a heartbeat, there was a girl's laughter, a daughter's name shaped by a mouth long since hollowed. *I held one memory against the dark for two hundred years. One. It was enough to turn me. What you carry will be enough to turn the sky.*

Bram knelt. He laid Mara down gently on the black stone, arranged her cloak beneath her head the way she never allowed anyone to arrange anything for her, and pressed the grimoire into her arms.

"Keep this," he whispered, though she couldn't hear him. "You'll want something of mine to be angry about."

Then he stepped off the rim into the well.

There was no falling. That was the first mercy. The darkness took him the way water takes a stone — completely, without malice — and then the memories began to leave.

Not all of them. That had been the bargain's architecture, worked out in the small hours while Mara slept anchored: not his whole life, only the parts with her in it. Every memory of Mara Quintana, freely given, equal in the well's cold arithmetic to a constellation of stolen names. He felt them go the way you feel teeth pulled — one, then another, then a rushing series, each leaving behind a socket of smooth nothing.

Her hands measuring chalk lines on the schoolroom floor. Gone.

The taste of her terrible plum brandy. Gone.

The night the stars went out, and her face at the broken wardstone, furious and unsurprised. Gone — and even as it left he could feel the shape of its leaving, a hole exactly her-sized.

He had thought he would weep. Instead he laughed, because the last memory rising through him was the oldest one, the one he'd hidden deepest even from himself: the day he'd first seen her, standing in the market with flour on her sleeves, telling a lie about the price of eggs with such total conviction that he'd known — known instantly, helplessly — that he wanted to spend his life near someone who lied like that. He hadn't remembered it in years. The well found it anyway. It went up out of him like a bird released, and he thought: *there. Let that one be a star.*

Above him — above everything — the darkness cracked.

* * *

**Mara**

She woke to light.

That alone told her something had gone catastrophically wrong, because there had been no light in the world for weeks, and certainly none here, at the top of the Starwell road, where she had fallen asleep anchored against her own sacrifice with Bram keeping watch.

She woke to light, and Bram was gone, and the grimoire was open in her arms to a page written in his handwriting that said only: *Forgive me. I was polite after all.*

"No," Mara said. It came out very small. Then she stood up and made it fill the whole mountain. "*No.*"

Below her, inside the ring of stone, the well was burning.

Not fire — she knew fire; fire was honest. This was memory given mass, rising in columns of soft gold, and she could see them, could see *him*, suspended in the heart of it with his head tipped back and his mouth moving. Saying names. Her name, over and over, or what used to be her name — except that as each memory tore free of him, the word *Mara* meant less and less to the mouth saying it, until it became only a sound, a coin worn smooth, spent and spent and spent.

And above the well, where no stars had been for a season, the sky began to heal.

The first star came back like an eye opening. It flared white, then settled into a steady burn, and Mara felt the release of it in her chest like a bone setting — somewhere far away, a name the Court had eaten came home to its owner. A village remembered its children. A road remembered where it led. The second star ignited, and the third, and then they were coming faster, a constellation assembling itself out of one runaway apprentice's surrendered past, and Mara stood at the rim of the well screaming his name into the light because it was the only spell she had left and she had always taught him that a name, truly spoken, can call anything home.

He turned. He looked up at her. And he smiled — that particular smile, the polite one, the one that gave things away so smoothly the giving looked like nothing at all — and his lips shaped words she could not hear, and she understood, with a clarity worse than any horror the Hollow Court had ever shown her, that he could no longer read them either.

* * *

**Bram**

By now he was mostly light.

He had expected terror at the end, or grief, or at least regret. What he felt instead was spaciousness — the strange clean freedom of a room after the furniture is carried out. He was running low on Maras. He counted what remained the way a miser counts coins in the dark: the market, the wardstone, the ash road where she'd taught him to anchor himself with small memories, the cellar where they'd shouted at each other about dying politely, the confession beneath the starless sky — *I spent the memory of choosing you because loving you felt like failing my duty, and I refuse to let duty win twice.*

That one he saved. He held it clenched in whatever was left of his hands, the last coin, and the well circled it hungrily, and he said, "Not yet. There's someone you have to meet first."

Because the Regent had come.

It rose out of the deep dark beneath the well's floor the way a stain rises through cloth — vast, patient, older than the wards, older than the war, the first person who had ever forgotten and made a kingdom of it. Bram felt its attention settle on him like snowfall. When it spoke, it did not use sound. It simply placed understanding in him, the way you place a jar on a shelf.

*You are spending yourself to refill the sky. I am the reason the sky needed refilling. Do you see the joke?*

"I see it," Bram said. His voice sounded thin, a reed. "I don't find it funny."

*Nothing is funnier than grief,* said the Regent. *I was a person once. I forgot my own name, and the forgetting did not hurt — that is the secret no one tells. Forgetting is rest. I built a court on rest. I offered the world a bed, and it screamed at me.*

"You ate their children's faces from their memories."

*I kept them safe,* the Regent said, with something almost like tenderness. *Nothing hurts, in my garden. Nothing ends. Come and see—*

"No." Bram lifted the last memory like a lantern. "But I'll trade you. You want to understand why they scream? Here. Take the final one. Take what I'm keeping. See what a person is worth, from the inside."

The Regent hesitated. In two thousand years of harvesting, it had never once been *offered*.

The memory left Bram's hands.

* * *

**Mara**

From the rim, Mara saw the sky nearly whole — hundreds of stars now, constellations she had taught from charts as a girl suddenly real again overhead, the Bear, the Sisters, the Wandering Blade — and she saw the darkness below the well convulse.

The Regent rose fully into view for the first time in the history of the valley: a figure like a person seen through frosted glass, wearing the suggestion of robes, the suggestion of a crown, the suggestion of a face. And into that suggested face, Bram's last memory poured like wine into a cup.

Mara felt it happen before she understood it. Felt the well take hold of something that was hers — the goodbye, the one she had said without knowing why, kneeling beside his sleeping body on the ash road, smoothing his hair back and whispering, *"Whatever happens, I chose you. Whatever I forget, I chose you"* — felt it pass through Bram's offering into the Regent, and watched the Regent *taste* it.

The frosted face cracked.

For one instant, the Regent was not a god of emptiness. It was a person — the first person, some ancient shepherd or mother or child on a hillside ten thousand years dead, holding a single memory of being loved and discovering that the holding hurt, that love was a wound that refused to close, that everything it had built was an attempt to stop hurting and had instead stopped *everything*. Its crown dissolved. Its robes unraveled into names — thousands of them, murmured all at once, going home.

"No," the Regent said, in a voice like the last breath of a fire, and it was not a threat. It was a person, finally, saying no to the dark. "I remember. I remember, I remember—"

And it came apart into the light.

* * *

**Bram**

He barely witnessed it. He was nearly done.

The well was full of him now — every version of Mara he had ever carried, rising and igniting, becoming stars. He could see the sky through himself. He could see the rim of the well, and the small fierce silhouette of a woman standing there with her fists raised against heaven, shouting a name he no longer recognized but still, in the marrow of what remained of him, answered to.

*That's her,* he thought, with the last scrap of self. *Whoever she is. Whoever I was. That's her.*

The well asked for the final payment. There was one memory left — he had thought the confession was the last, but the well had found one more, buried deeper than the market, deeper than the flour on her sleeves: the goodbye. His copy of it. The moment on the ash road when he had woken, briefly, feigning sleep failed, and heard her voice saying *I chose you* and had lain perfectly still and let her believe he hadn't heard, because hearing it had changed everything and he hadn't known yet what to do about that.

He gave it up gladly. It went up. Somewhere above, a new star kindled, brighter than the rest, and he felt the Court's grip on the world slacken everywhere at once, every eaten name coming loose like fruit in autumn.

Then there was very little of Bram Quintana left, and what remained was not afraid.

The last thing he thought was not a memory at all. It was a wish, and wishes are the one currency the well cannot count:

*Let her keep the sky. Let her teach under it. Let her be angry at me for a hundred years — anger means remembering.*

The light took him the way water takes a stone.

* * *

**Mara**

Dawn found her on her knees at the rim of the Starwell, and the sky above her was so full of stars going pale into morning that it looked like the world had grown a second dawn.

The well was quiet. The Regent was ash and rumor. Far below, at the bottom of the golden light, she could just make out the grimoire's twin lying open on the well's floor — no. Not the grimoire's twin. A book she didn't recognize, bound in grey, its pages fluttering though there was no wind. And beside it, folded neatly, with a care that broke her heart precisely because she could not say whose care it was, lay a man's coat.

She climbed down. Of course she climbed down. She was Mara Quintana, and she had never in her life left a thing undone because it hurt.

The coat was his. She knew that the way you know your own hands. She put it on, and it fit, and that was somehow the worst fact of the entire ruined night. The grey book was warm. On its first page, in handwriting that made her chest seize around a name she could no longer quite attach a face to, were the words:

*If you're reading this, it worked. Don't come looking for me — there's nothing left to find, and everything worth finding is up. Look up. I'm the bright one, third from the Sisters' left hand. I always did want to matter.*

Mara read it four times. Each time, the grief arrived fresh, with no history attached — a wound that healed and reopened, healed and reopened, because the memories that would have let her mourn in order had been spent buying back the sky.

Ilsa found her there at sunrise, at the bottom of the well, a hollow-eyed girl with her father's charts clutched to her chest, staring up through the ring of stone at the impossible, mended heavens.

"Mara," Ilsa whispered. "We did it. We — where's Bram?"

Mara opened her mouth to answer, and found the smooth hollow where the answer should have been, and stood in the gold light with her fists closed, unable even to weep properly, because mourning requires a shape and they had taken the shape with them.

"Up," she said finally, and pointed at the bright star third from the Sisters' left hand, burning steady in the paling dawn. "He's up."

And in her pocket, unknown to her, pressed flat between pages she had not yet reached, a single memory waited — hidden there by a boy who had planned even his erasure politely — her own voice, saying his name.
