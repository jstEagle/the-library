## 20. Afterlight

Mara woke to birdsong.

Not the thin, borrowed song of the last weeks — the desperate, half-remembered calls of birds that had forgotten what they were singing for — but a full chorus of it, riotous and unashamed, spilling down the mountainside like water over stones. She lay on the cold shelf of rock outside the Starwell's mouth with her cheek pressed to stone and her eyes closed, and she listened, and she did not move, because moving would mean beginning the day, and beginning the day would mean looking for him.

The stars were still out.

That was the first impossible thing. Dawn was coming — she could feel it in the east, a gray-green breath of light behind the far ridges — and yet the stars had not fled it. They hung in the paling sky like nails of silver driven into blue wood, stubborn, luminous, *present*, as if they had decided after ten thousand years of absence that they were done with leaving. One by one through the night she had watched them kindle: not all at once but in slow constellations, each new star arriving with a faint sound she felt more than heard, a chime deep in the chest, like a name being spoken in a room you could not see.

Each one a name the Court had eaten. Each one given back.

She knew which star he was. She knew without knowing how she knew — the way you know your own hand in the dark. Low in the southern sky, just above the shoulder of the mountain where the Starwell road began its climb: a white point, steady, unremarkable to anyone who had not stood at the well's heart and felt the moment the light went up through him. The others flickered, some of them, still settling into their old places like cats returning to a house after a long absence. His did not flicker. It burned the way he had burned — quietly, obstinately, with an attention that made you feel seen whether you wanted it or not.

Mara opened her eyes at last and looked at it, and said nothing, because there was nothing in her throat to say.

* * *

They came down from the Starwell road in the full gold of morning: Ilsa walking ahead with her father's charts rolled under one arm and her chin lifted like a woman entering a hall she owned, and Mara behind her, and behind them both the long descent into a world that was remembering itself.

She heard it before she saw it. A village in the valley below — Ashford, or what had been Ashford, the place where the ferryman's sister had lived before the Hollow took her children's names out of her — rose up in a sound like a dam breaking. Not screaming. Crying. The particular crying of people who have spent months unable to weep because grief requires a memory to grieve *for*, and then been handed their grief back whole.

By midday they reached it. The village square was full of people holding each other the way drowning people hold driftwood, and in the middle of them a woman knelt in the dust with two children crushed against her, saying their names over and over — *Tamsin, Osric, Tamsin, Osric* — not because the children needed to hear it but because she did, tasting each syllable like a woman drinking after a long thirst, checking that it was real, that it was hers, that it would stay.

When she saw Ilsa's charts under her arm and the ash of the burned forest still on their clothes, she understood — everyone in the square seemed to understand at once, the way crowds do — and they turned toward Mara, and Mara felt the weight of their turning like a physical thing, a hundred pairs of hands pressing on her shoulders.

She raised her palm. *No.* Not yet. Perhaps not ever. Let them thank the stars; the stars had earned it.

But the kneeling woman came anyway, hauling her children with her, and pressed something into Mara's hand — a small carved bird, whittled pine, a child's toy worn smooth by love — and said only, "You'll eat with us. You'll sleep under a roof. I won't argue about it." And Mara, who had argued with gods and regents and heralds of the empty court, found she had no argument left for a mother with pine shavings still on her skirt.

* * *

They stayed three days in Ashford, though Mara had meant to stay one.

It was strange, the work of a remembered world. Everything took longer than forgetting had. Forgetting had swept through the lowlands like a flood, fast and total; remembering came back the way a field comes back after fire — green shoots first, then tangles, then a slow laborious harvest. The miller could recall his trade but not his wife's face, and sat of an evening with her hands cupped around his, asking her questions a husband should never have to ask. Children who had been strangers to their own parents for a season had to be taught, gently, that it was safe to be loved again. There was a boy of six who flinched every time his mother said his name, as if the word were hot, until one night she stopped saying it and simply held him, and in the morning he said it himself, tentatively, like testing ice.

Mara walked the lanes and did what she could — small hedge-craft, mending the frayed edges where memory had torn loose and healed crooked — and told herself that this was why she had survived. Someone had to teach the mending. That was the arithmetic she offered the hollow inside her, and the hollow did not answer, because hollows never do.

Ilsa, meanwhile, had claimed the miller's best table, spread her father's charts across it, and begun to kill them.

That was how she put it herself, ink to her elbows, hair escaping its braid in dark wings. "Killing the map," she said, when Mara asked. "All these lines my father drew from other people's memories — guesses, secondhand roads, places named wrong because the namers had already been nibbled. They're beautiful and they're lies. I'm drawing over them." And she showed Mara the new chart taking shape beneath the old: the same country, but drawn now from her own feet and eyes, the roads bending where they actually bent, the villages marked not with the cartographer's neat symbols but with tiny annotations in her fierce slanted hand. *Miller here. Good water. Woman who lost three days and got them back.*

At the bottom edge of the chart, where the old maps had shown the valley as a smudge labeled HERE BE THE SEA OF NO STARS, Ilsa had drawn the mountains in careful profile, and above them, in fresh black ink still shining wet, she had drawn stars. Dozens of them. Small circles, each one labeled with a name she was collecting as she traveled — names the villages were reclaiming, names read aloud from the grimoire's index by anyone strong enough to hold the book.

"Someone has to map the sky too," Ilsa said, not looking up. "Everyone always maps the ground. But the ground doesn't go anywhere."

Mara looked at the girl — sixteen, hollow-eyed once, and now lit from within like a lamp — and thought: *he would have loved you.* And then she thought nothing at all, because the hollow opened under the thought like a door in a floor.

* * *

On the third evening she finally opened the grimoire.

She had carried it since the Starwell — had carried it, in truth, since the night Bram stole it, though for a while it had carried itself, dragging them both along its spine like beads on a string. She had not opened it since the stars came back. She had told herself this was prudence: the book was exhausted, its pages scorched, its binding cracked from the heat of the relighting; it deserved rest. She had told herself many things. She was, she admitted, sitting alone in the miller's loft with the last light coming amber through the shutters, a very good liar when the truth was unbearable.

The truth was that she was afraid of the book. Because the book had been his hands. Because opening it would be the first time since the well that she touched anything he had touched, and she did not know what that would do to her, and she suspected — correctly, as it turned out — that it would do everything.

She set it on her knees. The leather was warm. That was the first cruelty: it should have been cold, hours away from any fire, and instead it lay against her thighs with the warmth of a sleeping animal, of a living thing, of a hand.

She opened it.

The true pages were quiet now. The great seal-script that had blazed across them — the prison-wards, the war-marks, the architecture of the world's oldest grief — had faded to a faint silver ghosting beneath the surface of the vellum, like writing seen through milk glass. The book had spent itself. What remained was the ordinary grimoire it had always pretended to be: hedge-recipes, weather-charms, the little domestic magics of a valley witch's long apprenticeship. Her own handwriting appeared in the early pages, young and cramped. Then, further in, another hand grew up alongside hers — uncertain at first, letters leaning like windblown wheat, then steadying year by year until it was a hand she knew better than her own.

Bram's notes. In the margins. Everywhere in the margins.

*Master says wards fail from the edges inward. Why? What's at the center?*

*Copied the keystone cipher wrong twice. She didn't scold me. She laughed. I'd burn the valley down for that laugh, probably will.*

*She thinks I don't see her watching the border at night. She watches it like a woman watching a wound.*

Mara read them all. She read them slowly, by shutter-light, and let each one land where it landed, and did not permit herself to look away from any of them, because she owed him that much — to be a witness even now, especially now, when witnessing was all that remained of love's duties.

And then, near the end, she found the page that was not a page.

It was bound differently — she felt it before she saw it, a thickness, a seam — and when she worked it loose with her thumbnail, a folded leaf slipped free and fell into her lap, and the moment her skin touched it she knew. Hedge-craft teaches you to know things through your hands before your head consents. This was anchored memory. Wrapped, warded, tied off with a knotwork she recognized because she had invented it — because she had taught it to him on the ash road, in the burned forest, in the worst week of their lives, laughing at his clumsy fingers while the trees remembered being green around them.

He had learned it perfectly. Of course he had. He had learned everything perfectly except how to stay.

Her hands shook so badly she nearly tore the wrapping. She made herself be slow. She untied the knot the way he had untied it a hundred times in practice — thumb here, pull there, the little twist at the end that she'd told him was the whole secret — and the memory unfolded into her like warmth into cold fingers.

A kitchen. Her kitchen, years ago, lamplit, rain on the roof. She was younger in it; she could feel her own younger body, the ache in her back from a day of ward-work, the irritation of a woman whose supper is late. And she was speaking — she heard her own voice, alive, unguarded, amused in the way she had almost never permitted herself to be aloud —

*"Bram Quintana, if you burn that bread I swear by every star in the sky—"*

—and then his laugh, off-frame, delighted, and the smell of burning bread, and her own laughter breaking loose despite her, helpless, and the memory ended there, mid-laugh, mid-kitchen, mid-life.

Her voice. Saying his name.

He had kept it. Out of all the things the well had taken — out of every hour of him that she had lost, every conversation, every argument, every morning — he had reached into the treasury of his own dissolving self and saved *this*: not his memory of her, but hers of him. A scrap of ordinary evening. Bread burning. Rain. Her voice, young and cross and happy, wrapped around his name like a hand around a coal.

Because he had known. He had known what she would search for, in the smooth hollow where he used to be. He had known she would find nothing — that the well takes cleanly, leaves no edges, gives back no echo — and so he had smuggled one piece past the price, hidden it in the one place he knew she would eventually have to look: the book. The keystone. The thing between them from the first night to the last.

Mara held the leaf of vellum against her sternum and made a sound she had not made since childhood, some raw animal note that had no dignity in it at all, and the miller's wife, hearing it through the floor, wisely put a kettle on and did not come up.

* * *

Later — much later, when the loft was dark and the new stars turned slow and patient beyond the shutters — she sat with the memory unwrapped in her lap and considered it the way a miser considers a single coin in a ruined vault.

She could spend it. That was the terrible knowledge sitting beside her in the dark, patient as Lady Vess had been patient. She knew the craft now, knew it better than anyone living: how to open an anchored memory, how to pour it out, how to feed it to a purpose. The Starwell was lit, but the world was only half-mended; there were villages deeper in the dark that had not yet remembered, roads still lost, names still missing from Ilsa's sky. One memory, freely given, could call a child's name home. Could mend a mother. Could do real, measurable, undeniable good.

One memory. Hers. His voice in it, if she listened closely — she could almost hear the laugh behind her own words, the rattle of the pan, the rain.

No one would know. No one could stop her. That was the seduction of sacrifice, she thought, and wondered bleakly whether Bram had stood exactly here, exactly thus, in the dark before dawn at the well's heart, weighing her life against the world and finding the arithmetic irresistible. *Each accusing the other of planning to die politely.* She had said it to him in Ilsa's cellar like a curse, and he had denied it with his whole face lying, and she had believed him because wanting to believe is its own kind of magic.

She looked at the memory a long time. Then she rewrapped it, carefully, the way you wrap a wound or a gift, and retied the knot — thumb here, pull there, the little twist at the end — and slid it back into its hiding place in the grimoire's binding.

"Not yet," she said aloud, to him, to the dark, to the steady white star low over the southern ridge. "I'm keeping this one. Find another way to make me useful."

The star did not answer. But it did not flicker either, and somehow that was enough — that was, for tonight, everything — and Mara Quintana blew out the lamp and lay down in a stranger's loft under a remembered sky, with a book full of a dead man's handwriting warming against her heart like a hand.

In the morning, Ilsa would ask her which road they took next, and Mara would look at the girl's chart — at the wide blank country beyond the last annotated village, the country the Court had emptied deepest, where whole towns might still be standing in the dark not knowing their own names — and she would already know the answer. Some part of her had known it since the kettle sang downstairs. The hollow inside her ached like weather, and weather, her grandmother used to say, always moves somewhere.

There were more names to carry home. And she had a voice in a book, and a star in the south, and a new apprentice-shaped question walking beside her with ink on her hands — and the road, as it turned out, was not finished with any of them.
