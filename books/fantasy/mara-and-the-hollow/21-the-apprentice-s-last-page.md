## 21. The Apprentice's Last Page

The grimoire had never been a book, not really. It had been a door wearing the shape of one.

Mara knew this now. She had known it in fragments for years — known it when she set it on its shelf with the reverence other witches gave to knives, known it when she caught Bram looking at it the way he looked at the horizon past the valley's rim, hungry and guilty about being hungry. But knowing a thing in fragments is not the same as knowing it whole, and it was only now, sitting in the cold ruin of her own kitchen with dawn coming gray through the shutters, that she understood what she had been keeping all those years: not a spellbook. A seal. A keystone. A promise made by dead hands and left for her to carry, the way you carry a debt your grandmother signed.

And now Bram was gone into it, or through it, or of it — she could not decide which preposition hurt least — and the stars were burning again overhead like a city relit after plague, and she was alone with a book that had finally stopped being anything but paper.

She turned the last pages by candlelight because she could not bear daylight yet. Daylight would mean the world going on. Candlelight meant the night still held her, still let her grieve inside it like water holds a stone.

His handwriting began where the old wards-script ended, cramped and slanting, pressed hard into the vellum as if he'd feared the words might be taken from him before he finished them.

*Mara.*

*If you're reading this, then I did it, and you didn't stop me, which means either you were asleep or you were kind, and I know which one I'm hoping for.*

*You taught me that magic is just memory with somewhere to go. You said it the first winter, when my hands shook too badly to hold a flame and you put your own over mine and said, stop trying to make fire, remember warmth instead. I have thought about that every day since. I think about it now.*

*The well wanted a life. It would have taken yours. I want you to understand that I did not do this for duty. I did it because I spent three years learning from you how to love things properly — patiently, without flinching — and I could not stand there in the dark beneath the sky we'd relit and watch you unmake yourself out of some idea that grief is a tax you owe the valley. You don't owe anyone your whole self. You never taught me that, but you should have, so consider this page the lesson, delivered late.*

*I am asking you not to chase me. Not into the well, not into the grimoire, not into whatever hollow rituals the old books keep for calling back the dead. I read them too, Mara. I know what's in the appendix. The price is always memory, and yours are already half-spent, and I will not be the reason you forget your own name trying to hold onto mine.*

*So instead I am asking you to do the thing you are worst at and best at, which is the same thing: teach someone.*

*You took me in off the ferry road with frostbitten feet and a head full of stories about the world outside, and you must have known even then what I was — restless, ungrateful, in love with everywhere that wasn't here. You taught me anyway. That was the bravest thing you ever did, braver than the Starwell, braver than facing Vess down on the ash road. You gave what you had to someone who might waste it.*

*Someone will come. Someone always comes, hungry and half-broken, wanting the world. Give it to them. Give them the valley and the hedge-craft and the anchoring, and give them the small memories first, the taste of bread and the sound of rain on the wardstones, because those are the ones that hold. And when they ask you about the stars — and they will ask, everyone will ask, the whole world woke up under a lit sky and wants to know who paid — tell them the truth if you can bear it, and if you can't, tell them a witch and her apprentice carried a book up a mountain, and leave the rest in the margins.*

*Don't spend yourself chasing me. Spend yourself staying. It's harder. You always said the valley was the difficult work.*

*Yours — I don't have a word for what I am. Yours will have to do.*

*Bram*

The candle guttered. Mara sat very still, the way she had once taught him to sit when a spell was settling, when any movement might spoil the shape of what was forming. Outside, a bird began the idiotic, ordinary business of morning. Somewhere down the lane a cart rattled, and someone laughed, and the sound of it went through her like a splinter because laughter was a thing the Hollow had eaten from whole villages and here it was again, cheap and free and everywhere, and Bram had bought it with everything he was.

She read the letter twice more. Then a third time, hunting the margins, because he had told her to leave things in the margins and Bram Quintana had never once in his life given an instruction he didn't intend to be obeyed literally.

There, at the bottom corner of the final page, in ink so faint she had to tilt the page against the flame: a single line, smaller than the rest, almost apologetic.

*P.S. — Check the binding.*

* * *

She checked the binding.

It took her an hour. She worked with a bone folder and a lampwick needle, easing the cords loose along the spine where the leather had been resewn — resewn recently, within the last year, in stitches she recognized because she had taught them, because she had watched his big clumsy careful hands learn them on practice scraps until his fingers bled. He had hidden a pocket between the false back board and the true one, no wider than two fingers, and inside it lay a slip of vellum rolled tight as a seed.

She unrolled it with hands that would not stay steady.

No writing. Just a memory, pressed into the vellum the way the grimoire pressed memories into itself — she could feel it before she understood it, a warmth rising off the little scroll like heat off a stone in evening sun. And when she held it to her ear, the way you'd hold a shell, she heard it:

Her own voice. Younger than she felt now, roughened with cold, saying his name.

*"Bram."*

Nothing else. Two syllables. The way she'd called him in from the ferry road the day he arrived, or the way she'd called him to dinner a thousand evenings after, or the way she'd called him the night the stars went out and he was running and she was shouting into the dark — she couldn't place it, and that was its own small mercy, because it meant it could be all of them at once. Every time she had ever said his name lived somewhere in those two syllables, folded up like light in a prism.

He had kept one. Out of everything he'd fed into the well — every shared meal, every lesson, every argument, every night on the ash road when their shoulders touched and neither of them moved away — he had kept one, and hidden it in the binding, and asked her not to spend herself chasing him.

She sat with it against her ear until the candle drowned in its own wax.

Then she got up, opened the shutters, and let the daylight in.

* * *

They came through the autumn, the way he'd promised. They always came.

The valley's border was open now — open by design, the wards unmade, the wardstones pulled and laid flat as paving so that anyone could walk in from the lowlands and find bread and beds and no questions asked until morning. Mara had argued for it herself, at the council of the rebuilt villages, standing where she used to stand arguing for walls. *A wall is just a fear you've built a roof over,* she'd told them, and hated how much she sounded like him when she said it. *We tried keeping the world out. Let's try letting it in.*

They came thin and hollow-eyed, mostly. People from the forgotten country, from the towns where roads forgot their own destinations and mothers had spent years unable to recall their children's faces. Some of them came back wrong in ways that could not be fixed — the ferryman among them, though Mara had restored his name and he policed the river crossing now with a ferocity that made travelers smile. Others came back only dented, only scuffed, carrying gaps in themselves like missing teeth: a man who could not remember his wedding but remembered loving his wife; a woman who had lost her childhood entire and was teaching herself to make new ones for her own daughters out of whole cloth.

And in the second week of the leaf-fall, a girl.

She came up the ferry road barefoot in frost, maybe twelve years old, maybe fourteen — it was impossible to tell, because the Hollow had gotten into her early and eaten the years off her the way it ate names. Her eyes were the worst part. Not empty — Mara had seen empty, had stood in Lady Vess's beautiful vacant face and known what empty looked like — but *scoured*, scrubbed down to something raw and waiting, the way a house looks after a fire, when the walls still stand and nothing in them is yours anymore.

Ilsa brought her in. Ilsa, who was mapping the recovered country now under real stars, whose charts hung in the council hall and whose father's lost work had become scripture for the road-builders. Ilsa found the girl huddled in the ruins of a waystation on the ash road, sitting perfectly still among burned trees that remembered being green, and had wrapped her in a coat and walked her home talking the whole way, because Ilsa had learned that the hollow-eyed ones needed voices around them even when they couldn't answer.

"She hasn't spoken," Ilsa reported, setting the girl down by Mara's fire. "Not a word. But look —" she held out a scrap of charred birch bark, and on it, in lines scratched over and over, was a drawing. A house. A small figure in the doorway. "She keeps drawing this. Same house, every time. I think it's hers. I think it's the last thing she has."

Mara crouched by the fire and looked at the girl, and the girl looked back, and Mara saw in that scoured face the exact expression Bram must have worn the night he stole the grimoire — hunger and guilt braided together, wanting the world and ashamed of wanting it.

"What's your name?" Mara asked gently.

The girl's mouth worked. Nothing came. She looked down at the birch bark and her fingers curled around it like a talisman.

"It's all right," Mara said. "Names can be found again. I know where some of them live."

She fed her. Put her to bed in the apprentice's room — Bram's room, unchanged since the Starwell, because Mara had not been able to make it into anything else and had stopped pretending she intended to. And in the morning the girl was standing at the window watching the wardstone-paved road, and when Mara came in with bread and honey the girl pointed at the mountains and made a sound. Not a word. A question.

"The Starwell," Mara said. "That's where the stars were relit. That's where—" She stopped. Started again. "That's where someone I loved gave everything he had so the world would remember itself. You'll see it from the ridge on clear days. Everyone does now."

The girl looked at her for a long moment. Then she held up the birch bark, the drawing of the house and the small figure in the doorway, and pointed at the figure, and then pointed at herself, and her eyes said what her voice could not: *this is all I am. Is it enough?*

And Mara, who had buried an apprentice in a well of starlight, who had read his last letter by candlelight and heard her own voice hidden in a book's binding, heard herself say the words before she had decided to say them:

"It's more than enough. It's a beginning. Would you like to learn what to do with it?"

* * *

She gave the girl three days to eat and sleep and wander the garden touching things, because you cannot build memory on an empty body. On the fourth morning she sat her down at the kitchen table — the same table, scarred with the same burn rings, where Bram had once melted a hole through a practice sigil and sworn he hadn't — and set the grimoire between them.

The girl stared at it. Her hand came out, hesitated, hovered.

"You don't touch it yet," Mara said. "Nobody touches it yet. First you learn why it exists."

She opened it to the first page, the old wards-script, dense and glittering faintly even now, though its power had gone out of it the way power goes out of a key once the lock is broken. She had thought about burying the book. She had thought about burning it. She had done neither, because Bram had asked her to teach, and you cannot teach from an empty shelf.

"A long time ago," Mara said, "there was a war. Not a war of armies — a war of remembering. There was a power that fed on names, on memories, on the selves of people, and it grew fat while the world grew thin. And the witches of the valley built a seal to hold it, and the seal was this book, and the seal held for a thousand years until the night it broke." She paused. "And a boy ran out into that broken night carrying it, because he was hungry for the world, and because he was brave, and because he didn't know yet that the bravest thing isn't running toward danger. It's staying where you're needed and doing the small work, day after day, when nobody's watching."

The girl's eyes had not left the page. In them, for the first time, something moved — not light exactly, but the *place* where light would go, the way a socket waits for a lamp.

"Now," Mara said. "The first lesson. Are you listening?"

The girl nodded.

Mara reached across the table and covered the girl's small chapped hand with her own, the way another hand had once covered hers, the way she had once covered a shaking apprentice's hands over a stubborn flame. Outside, the wind moved through the open border where no wall stood, and the frost was melting off the garden, and somewhere down the valley a child was shouting to another child, both of them alive, both of them named.

"Stop trying to make fire," Mara said. "Remember warmth instead."

The girl frowned — and then her face changed. Something surfaced in the scoured depths, some fragment the Hollow had missed, some crumb of a hearth or a mother or a morning. Her lips parted. And from somewhere down in the ruined country of herself, in a voice cracked from disuse and grief and the long walk through the forgotten lands, the girl spoke her first word in the valley:

She said a name. Her own.

Mara closed her eyes. *There you are,* she thought, to the girl, to the name, to the boy in the well of stars who had told her to spend herself staying. *There you are.*

When she opened them, the girl was looking at the grimoire with her raw waiting eyes, and her mouth was moving around the shape of her name again, tasting it, keeping it.

"Again tomorrow," Mara said, and began to turn the page.
