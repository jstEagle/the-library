## 22. Mara and the Hollow

The valley had no walls now.

Mara stood at its open edge on a morning in late autumn, when the mist came down off the mountains like something poured, and watched her students cross the old wardline without ceremony. There was nothing to feel anymore — no prickle of wards against the skin, no hum in the teeth, no sense of stepping between one world and another. The border was just a place where the grass changed color slightly, where the mountain soil gave way to lowland loam, where a stone that had once been a wardstone sat half-buried in moss with its runes worn smooth by six years of children climbing it.

She had refused to have it removed. Let them climb it. Let them sit on it and eat their bread and argue about whose turn it was to carry the water skins. A stone that had held back the end of the world deserved to be sat upon.

"Teacher." Pell, the youngest, tugging at her sleeve. He was nine and had been born after the stars came back, which meant he thought of the night sky as a settled thing, a ceiling that had always been there, studded and reliable as a roof. "Are we really going past the line today? All the way to the ferry?"

"All the way," Mara said. "And you'll each speak to the ferryman yourself. He doesn't like being pointed at."

"He still can't talk?"

"He talks fine. Just not with his mouth." She smiled and turned up the path, and eleven students fell into line behind her the way ducklings follow a shape they've decided is their mother.

This was the lesson she taught now, the only one that mattered, though she dressed it in a dozen forms: herb-craft by the river, star-reading on the summer solstice, the long walks into the forgotten country where the world had gone thin and was slowly thickening again. Under all of it ran a single thread, and she spoke it aloud so often that her older students could recite it in their sleep, and did, mockingly, when they thought she couldn't hear:

*Memory is not a thing you have. It's a thing you do.*

She had learned it from a book. Or rather, from what a boy had hidden inside a book, in his last living hour, knowing she would find it and knowing — this was the part that undid her, some nights, the sheer unbearable *consideration* of it — knowing exactly how much she would need it and exactly how little of it there would be.

* * *

The grimoire lived on her desk, under glass she had made herself, because there were people who would steal it if they knew what it was and people who would worship it if they knew what it had done, and Mara wanted neither. It looked ordinary. That was the joke of it, the terrible private joke: the keystone of the valley's wards, the prison-seal of the Hollow Court, the book that had unmade a god — bound in scuffed brown leather with a cracked spine, smelling faintly of tallow smoke and river water, looking for all the world like a hedge-witch's ledger full of recipes for fever tea.

Most of it was. Bram had never gotten past the first third. She'd checked, afterward, reading every page he might have read, trying to reconstruct what he'd known when he ran, and found that the true pages began at the point where a careful reader would have stopped for the night. He had stolen a book he didn't understand, from a woman he did understand completely, and both facts had saved the world. She had stopped trying to make moral sense of that years ago. Some things were simply true the way weather was true.

Each night, before she slept, she opened the glass, opened the book, and turned to the page near the end where the paper felt different under her fingers — thicker, warmer, the way a stone stays warm after an animal has been lying on it. And she read the memory once.

Never twice. Never more than once.

That rule had cost her three years to make and kept her alive ever since. Because the memory was real, whole, alive in the way only Bram's memories had ever been alive — she could step into it, stand in it, be nineteen again and furious at him across a kitchen full of steam while he tried to explain why he'd fed her best drying herbs to a sick child from the lowlands without asking. She could hear his voice. She could smell the rosemary. She could feel the exact temperature of her own anger, which had been love wearing armor, though neither of them had known it yet.

And memory spent was memory gone. She had spent enough of herself to know. Every restoration at the ferry, every stranger pulled back out of the hollow, every proof that the grimoire could fight the Court — each one had taken a piece of her, and she had told herself at the time that she chose carefully, that she gave up small things, forgettable things. She had been lying. She knew now, with the terrible clarity of hindsight, that she had never once chosen a small thing. The hollow in her was the size of everything she'd given, and the largest piece of it was shaped like a kitchen, and steam, and a boy saying *I'm sorry* in a voice that meant *thank you for letting me be someone who apologizes.*

So: once a night. Read it, live it, close the book. Let it ache.

The ache was the point. She had understood this eventually, the way you understand anything important — slowly, reluctantly, and only after every easier answer failed. The hollow in her ached like weather, like an old break before rain, and for the first year she had treated it as a wound to be healed, and then as a penance to be endured, and finally, one gray morning on the Starwell road's last mile, as what it actually was: the sensation of holding something. An empty hand aches too, if it held something long enough. The ache was Bram's weight, still registered by arms that would never carry him again.

She read the memory once a night the way other people said prayers, and the hollow ached, and she counted both as evidence that she was still whole enough to grieve.

* * *

The ferryman met them at the river, as he always did, and his boat was new-built but his hands were the same hands, scarred and sure, and when he saw the children his face did the thing it always did — the slow sunrise of a man who remembered being unable to remember, and had decided that gratitude was a full-time occupation.

He had a name now. Of course he did. Mara had given it back to him on the second day of the end of the world, pulled it out of the grimoire at a cost she hadn't let herself calculate, and he had wept on the deck of his own boat like a man being born. His name was Odo. He ferried pilgrims now, and refugees, and once a season he ferried a class of apprentices across the river and back, and every single time, without fail, he touched two fingers to his heart when Mara stepped aboard, and said nothing, because he had decided long ago that his mouth was for other work and his silence was his monument.

"Teacher says you used to be empty," Pell announced, with the tact of the very young.

"Pell." Mara's voice came out sharper than she intended.

But Odo laughed — a big, unhurried sound, oars creaking, river running. "It's true, small one. I was a house with nobody home. This woman walked in and lit a lamp." He looked at Mara over the children's heads, and his eyes were wet, and he didn't pretend otherwise. "Sixteen years I rowed that river without knowing why. Now I know why. That's the whole difference between a life and a route."

The children took turns speaking with him, as instructed, and got in return a string of river-stories so embroidered that Mara suspected Odo invented a new one each crossing, and the current carried them, and the far bank came on green and ordinary, and above them the sky did what the sky now always did: it held.

She still checked. She doubted she would ever stop checking. Every clear night, some old reflex tipped her chin upward, and there they were — the stars, all of them, or nearly; there were gaps, constellations with missing teeth, and the astronomers of the lowland towns had begun calling those gaps *the Given*, and mothers pointed them out to children and said, *someone paid for that light,* and the children said *who?* and the mothers said *no one knows,* and Mara kept her mouth shut and her eyes on the middle distance, because the truth was hers, and grief was allowed to be private even when heroism was public.

One star, low in the autumn sky, burned slightly wrong — a warm color, amber instead of white, and it flickered sometimes, irregularly, like a pulse. The astronomers had theories. Mara had a different theory, which she shared with no one: that somewhere in the great ledger of relit names, one entry had been written in a hand she knew, and the sky had never quite figured out what to do with it, and so it glowed the color of a kitchen fire and flickered like someone laughing.

She called it nothing. Naming it would have been spending it.

* * *

They were three days out, camped in the burned forest where the trees remembered being green — the forest had come back wrong and wonderful, new growth threaded through with ghost-growth, saplings standing beside translucent shapes of the old pines that only showed at dusk, like the forest's own memory refusing to fall — when the girl arrived.

Mara heard her before she saw her: a wrongness in the perimeter sounds, birdsong resuming a beat late, the particular hush of something moving carefully. Six years of teaching had not dulled the war-craft. She was on her feet with a warding word half-formed before her mind caught up with her body and reported: small. Alone. Human.

The girl stood at the edge of the firelight like a heron at the edge of water — thin, dark-eyed, maybe twelve, with the look Mara had learned to dread and had never stopped loving on sight: the look of someone carrying more past than future. A refugee from the deep forgotten country, where the Court's shadow lingered longest, where villages woke with holes in themselves and no way to know what had been taken.

"You're the witch," the girl said. Not a question. "They said in Ferrow's Crossing there's a witch who teaches at the open border, and she can give you back your name if it's gone, and she doesn't charge."

"I charge," Mara said quietly. "You just wouldn't see the bill."

The girl's chin came up. Whatever else had been hollowed out of her, defiance remained, sitting in her chest like a coal. "I don't want my name back."

Mara went still. In eleven years — no. Six years. Six years since the well, since the amber star, since the smooth hollow where a boy used to be — in six years of this work, no one had ever said that.

"They took my brother's name," the girl said. "Last winter. He woke up and didn't know me and the neighbors said take him to the witch in the valley, and everyone talked about it like getting a name back was the same as getting *him* back, and it isn't, is it? You give a man his name and he's still a stranger wearing it." Her voice cracked and steadied, cracked and steadied, the way young voices do when they've practiced being brave alone. "I want to know how to fight them. The ones who take. I want to learn what you know. I'll chop wood. I'll carry things. I don't care about my name."

The fire popped. Somewhere behind Mara, one of her sleeping students murmured and rolled over.

And Mara stood in the burned forest where she had once taught a runaway boy to anchor himself with small memories — *the smell of this road after rain, the way your mother said your name, hold one, hold it like a rope* — and felt the future arrive, the way it always does: disguised as an inconvenience, wearing the face of a decision she'd already made without knowing it.

"What's your name?" she asked. "The one you still have."

"Sefi."

"Sefi. Sit down. Eat first. Lessons start at dawn, and the first one's free." She handed the girl a bowl, and watched her eat like someone who had learned not to trust the next meal, and thought: *Bram, you absolute fool, look what you started.* The thought ached, and she let it, and the ache was warm tonight, banked-fire warm, hearth-warm.

Later, when the camp slept, she took out the grimoire and read the memory once — the kitchen, the steam, the rosemary, the boy's voice saying sorry like thank you — and closed the book, and sat a long time watching the amber star through the ghost-pines, flickering its patient flicker.

In the morning she would teach Sefi the anchors. In a year, maybe, Sefi would teach someone else. The wards were gone by design and the border was open and the world was full of holes that filled slowly, by hand, one name and one memory and one stubborn student at a time. Nothing was kept by walls anymore. Nothing ever really had been.

Some things are kept not by walls but by choosing, daily, to remember.
