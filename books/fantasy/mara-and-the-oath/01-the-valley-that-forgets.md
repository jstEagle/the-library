## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The valley forgets you the moment you stop fighting it.

That's what I told every new recruit, back when we still took recruits. You come down off the pass with your ghosts and your debts and whatever small magics the crown has decided to hate this decade, and the valley takes them. Not gently—it grinds them out of you like a whetstone wears down a blade—but it takes them. The air here is dead to the Gift. Spells curdle in your mouth. Charms go cold as river stones. Whatever you were on the other side of those grey peaks, inside the valley you are only meat and memory, and after a while even the memory thins.

I have lived here eleven years, and I still sweep the shrine every morning.

Not because anyone asks. Not because the god answers—no one has heard from the Silent God in three generations, and the Order that once kept its shrines is scattered or burned. I sweep because the broom is honest work, and because if I stopped, I would have to admit what I actually am: not a mercenary who happens to keep a shrine, but something else wearing a mercenary's boots.

The shrine sits at the valley's heart, a squat stone building older than the company, older than the war that brought us here. Its god-statue lost its face before my grandmother was born. I light no candles—the god never asked for fire—and I leave no offerings but the swept floor and the silence it deserves. Some mornings I kneel anyway, out of habit, and press my palm flat against the cold altar, and wait for the old ache in my blood to stir.

It never does. Eleven years of nothing. Which is exactly how I like it.

* * *

"Vesper." Dain's voice carried across the training yard the way it always did, flat as a dropped plank. "You're wanted. Herald at the gate."

My hands went still on the practice dummy's rope-wrapping. "Crown herald?"

"Crown herald." He chewed the words like gristle. "In full livery. With an escort of six and a sealed letter."

Eleven years, and the crown had never once sent so much as a tax assessor into the Vale of Sorrows—that being the name some long-dead optimist had given this place, though everyone just called it the valley, the way you'd call a grave a bed if you slept in it long enough. We were the Oathbound Company, forty-odd soldiers who'd fought for a god that stopped speaking and then for whoever paid, and finally for no one at all. We'd settled here because the valley ate magic, and most of us had reasons to want magic eaten.

"Herald wants you specifically," Dain added, watching me too closely. "Said the shrine-keeper."

"I'm not the shrine-keeper. I'm the person who owns a broom."

"You're the closest thing we've got to holy, Vesper. Don't make that face. It's a compliment."

"It's a diagnosis," I said, and went to wash my hands.

* * *

Captain Rennike received the herald in the long hall, which was generous of her, since the long hall smelled of tallow smoke and forty men's boots. I stood at the back with the others, arms crossed, doing my best impression of a soldier with nothing to hide—which is a performance I've rehearsed so long I almost believe it myself.

The herald was young. That was the first thing I noticed, and the thing that frightened me most. Young meant zealous. Old inquisitors burn you out of procedure; young ones burn you out of conviction. His livery was spotless white over grey, the crown's new colors since the Regency took power, and at his throat hung the sigil I'd hoped never to see outside of broadsheets: a candle with its flame turned downward. The Snuffed Candle. The mark of the Office of Purity.

He read the proclamation standing, in a voice trained for courtyards, and the hall went very quiet around me.

"By order of the Lord Regent, protector of the realm and guardian of its purity: be it known that the hunt for the Gifted is renewed throughout all provinces. Those who harbor bearers of unlicensed magic commit treason against body and soul alike. Bounties are set. Reports are owed. The flame of corruption will be sought wherever it hides—in cities, in wilds—" and here his eyes moved slowly across our faces, "—and in valleys."

Nobody breathed. In the valley, magic couldn't follow us. That had been the whole point. That had been the covenant, unwritten but understood: the crown didn't waste riders on a place where sorcery starved to death on its own.

But the herald hadn't finished. He produced a second document, smaller, and this one he did not read aloud. He walked the length of the hall instead, unhurried, letting his boot heels count the silence, until he stood directly in front of me.

"Vesper Crane," he said. "Shrine-keeper of the Silent God."

"Janitor," I said. "Of a shed."

Something flickered behind his eyes—not amusement. Recognition. "Your name appears on a list compiled by the Office of Purity. A list of persons who took service-oaths to the Silent God prior to the Silence." He held up the paper between two fingers, delicately, as one holds a dead bird. "All such oath-bound persons are hereby summoned to renew their vows under crown supervision, or to formally renounce them. The Regent does not trust oaths made to gods who no longer answer."

"And if we refuse?" Rennike asked from her chair, her voice level as a frozen lake.

"Then refusal is recorded," the herald said pleasantly. "Everything is recorded now, Captain. That is rather the point of the new age." He tucked the list away and smiled at the room, a smile with all the warmth of a ledger. "You have thirty days. The Office returns at month's end to collect names and oaths. Serve the crown's truth, and the valley remains beneath notice."

He left before noon, six white-and-grey riders trailing him down the pass like a thread being pulled out of a hem.

* * *

That evening the company drank, because that's what companies do when the ground shifts under them—drink first, think later, panic on schedule. I sat apart with my cup untouched, which fooled no one, least of all Dain, who dropped onto the bench beside me smelling of cheap ale and expensive worry.

"He knew your name before he came," Dain said. "Lists. Records. When did the crown start keeping records on us?"

"When we became worth recording." I turned my cup in a slow circle. "There's a bounty schedule posted in every market town south of the pass. Fire-gifts pay best. Then storm, then green. The quiet gifts—" I shrugged. "Mine wouldn't fetch enough to buy the horse they'd ride in on."

Dain went still in the particular way of a man deciding whether to pretend he didn't hear something. "Your gift's been dead since you crossed the pass. Everyone knows that."

"Everyone knows that," I agreed, "because I've spent eleven years making sure everyone knows that."

He looked at me then, really looked, and I watched him do the arithmetic every soldier in that hall was doing somewhere behind their eyes: *Vesper keeps the shrine. Vesper took the oath. Vesper's name is on a list.* And underneath it, quieter, the arithmetic none of them could finish because they lacked the numbers: *what exactly did Vesper give up when the god fell silent, and is it truly gone?*

"The vote'll come," Dain said quietly. "Rennike won't want it, but Brannoc's been talking to the southern boys. Bounty money's bounty money, and the company's thin. If there's a gifted hiding anywhere in this valley—"

"There isn't."

"—then handing them over might be the only coin that keeps us fed through winter." He drained his ale and stood. "I'm telling you as a friend, Vesper. Sweep your shrine. Keep your head down. And pray—if that's still a thing you do—that nothing comes over that pass looking for shelter."

* * *

I went to the shrine that night, because where else does a liar go but to the altar she lies beside?

The moon came through the broken roof in a slanted bar, laying itself across the faceless statue like a blade of bone. I knelt. I pressed my palm to the altar the way I have ten thousand times, expecting the usual nothing—the dead-air hush, the absence with teeth.

Instead, something stirred.

Faint. Far off. Like a coal remembered rather than felt—a warmth in the blood, a whisper of the old current that used to run through me before I buried it under eleven years of valley dirt. My gift, waking in its sleep. Turning over. Reaching toward—

Toward what? There was nothing in the valley to reach toward. Nothing had crossed the pass. Nothing—

Far away, at the edge of hearing, at the edge of the world, something screamed.

Not a person. Not an animal. Something older, and burning as it fell.

I stayed kneeling long after the echo died, my hand still pressed to the stone, my heart going like a drum in a retreat. Outside, the wind moved through the pass, and for the first time in eleven years it carried a smell I had no business recognizing:

Smoke. Dragon-smoke. Faint as a rumor, and coming closer.

The valley forgets, I've always said. But tonight, kneeling in my godless shrine with my buried gift humming like a struck bell, I understood that forgetting runs both ways.

Something was coming down the mountain that the valley would not be able to forget—and neither, gods help me, would I.
