## 2. Ash on the Wind

The valley had three rules, and we'd all memorized them before we could hold a sword straight: no magic, no maps, and no questions about why.

I was good at all three. Especially the first one, because I had to be. My gift—the thing coiled under my ribs like a banked coal—hadn't so much as warmed my fingertips in eleven years. That's what the valley did. It smothered things. Crops grew slow, fires burned low, grudges lasted forever because nobody had the energy to settle them. The old-timers called it holy ground. I called it a wet blanket laid over the whole world, and I was grateful for it every single day.

So when the wind changed on a gray morning in late autumn and brought me the smell of smoke that wasn't woodsmoke, I knew our wet blanket had sprung a leak.

I was at the shrine when it hit me. That's my job, or half of it: I keep the shrine to the silent god. Sweep the stones, refill the oil lamp nobody's lit since my grandmother's grandmother, recite the oath into air that hasn't answered in four generations. *Keep the flame. Guard the vessel. Wait.* It's a strange prayer for a god who can't hear you, but habits are cheaper than hope, and I've never been able to afford much hope.

The smoke smell curled through the shrine like a cat looking for a warm lap, and something under my ribs *twitched*.

I put my hand flat against my sternum and stood very still, the way you do when you feel your heart skip and you're deciding whether to panic. Eleven years of nothing. Eleven years of being the most boring mercenary in a company of mercenaries, and now, on an ordinary Tuesday, my gift rolled over in its sleep like a dog catching a scent.

"Vesper." Dree's voice from the treeline, sharp. "You'd better come see this."

Dree is our scout, which means she finds trouble professionally and then acts surprised by it. She was standing at the edge of camp pointing down the goat track that winds up from the lowlands, and at first I couldn't see what she was pointing at. Then the shape at the bottom of the track moved, and I realized it wasn't a shape. It was a child.

She walked like a marionette with half its strings cut—one shoulder lower than the other, each step placed with enormous care, as though the ground might lie to her. Her hair had been dark once. Now it was singed to the scalp in patches, and her left arm was wrapped in bandages gone gray with ash. She couldn't have been more than eight.

And her hands were burning.

Not bleeding, not blistered—*burning*. Small, steady flames sat in both her palms like birds that had decided to roost there, orange shot through with a core of white so bright it hurt to look at sideways. They didn't consume her. They didn't spread. They just *were*, patient as sunrise, and every hair on my body stood up and paid attention.

"That's not witchfire," Dree said quietly. "Witchfire flickers."

This didn't flicker. This burned the way the sun burns, the way old stories said dragons burned, back when dragons were more than embroidery on tavern signs. I'd spent my whole life around magic's absence, and I recognized what I was looking at the way a deaf man recognizes thunder: not by hearing it, but by feeling it in his teeth.

The child looked up at us. Her eyes were the color of a fire's last hour, and she said, in a voice scraped raw: "They're coming. Please don't tell them where I am."

Then she fell over.

* * *

We did the sensible thing. We put her in the healer's tent, bound her burns—which were real, whatever impossible fire lived in her hands—and called a company vote.

Here's how the Grey Company governs itself: badly, but loudly. Forty-one mercenaries arranged in a circle around the cook-fire, Captain Brannic standing with his arms crossed like a man watching weather he doesn't trust, and everyone shouting their opinions because shouting is free and wisdom costs extra.

"The bounty," said Osk, who can smell coin the way sharks smell blood, "is five hundred crowns. *Five hundred.* For a child. We could retire."

"She's eight," said Marta, who has daughters and therefore lost the argument before she opened her mouth.

"She's eight *on fire*," Osk said. "You saw her hands. You want that sleeping twenty feet from your bedroll?"

"It's not spreading. I checked."

"It's *dragon-fire*, Marta. You think it spreads? You think it needs to?"

I stood at the edge of the circle and said nothing, which is my specialty. But I'll confess something here, since this account is for whoever finds it after we're all dead or famous: I wasn't listening to the vote. I was listening to my own chest.

Because the twitching hadn't stopped. From the moment they'd carried the girl past me into the tent, that dead coal under my ribs had been glowing. Not awake—nothing so dramatic. Just warm. Warm for the first time in eleven years, like a hand pressed against a window in winter. And the closer I stood to her, the warmer it got, until I caught myself drifting toward the healer's tent mid-vote like a moth with poor judgment.

I made myself stop. I made myself go back and stand in the cold part of the circle, where my gift went quiet again, sulking.

That should have frightened me more than it did. What actually frightened me was how much I wanted to walk back.

"—and Vesper keeps the shrine, so Vesper gets a say," Brannic was saying, and forty heads turned toward me. Wonderful. "Well? You're the closest thing we have to a priest. Is the god going to object if we sell her?"

There it was. The trap inside the question. The silent god hadn't spoken in four generations; the last recorded miracle was a rainstorm that may or may not have been coincidence. Nobody alive had heard that voice. And yet the oath still hung around all our necks, cut into the cord each of us wore, because a promise doesn't need a listener to bind you. *Keep the flame. Guard the vessel. Wait.*

A child carrying living fire walks into a valley dedicated to guarding flames, and the company wants to sell her to the men who burned her.

"No objection," I heard myself say. My voice came out steady, which felt like a betrayal of everything happening under my sternum. "But I'll sit with her tonight. If she's going to be handed over at dawn, someone should make sure she doesn't burn the tent down and cost us the bounty."

Osk laughed. Brannic nodded. The vote carried, thirty-one to ten, and the meeting broke up with the brisk efficiency of people who have just done an ugly thing and would like to wash their hands before supper.

I went to the healer's tent.

* * *

She was awake. Of course she was awake. Children who carry fire in their hands don't get the luxury of sleeping deeply, I suspect.

"They voted," she said. Not a question. She'd heard it through the canvas; sound carries in a valley that has nothing else to do.

"They voted," I agreed. I sat down on the stool across from her cot, at a respectful distance, the way you'd sit near a hearth you weren't sure was banked. "I'm Vesper."

"Mara." She studied me with those ember-colored eyes, and I had the distinct feeling of being read like a cheap pamphlet. "You're lying."

"About my name?"

"About not caring." She held up her bandaged arm. "Your heart's doing something. I can see it. Everything warm shows, when—" she flexed her fingers, and the flames rose obediently out of her palms, small and white-hearted and utterly silent, "—when you're like me."

And there it was. The coal under my ribs flared—not painfully, not dangerously, just *gladly*, the way a locked room brightens when someone finally opens the shutters. For eleven years I'd kept it buried so deep I'd almost convinced myself it was dead. In front of this burned child I couldn't even keep it quiet for a full minute.

"Don't," I said, too fast.

Mara tilted her head. "Don't what?"

"Don't tell them. Whatever you see in me." I leaned forward, and my voice dropped to something I barely recognized—urgent, naked, the voice of a man whose illusion of safety had just cracked clean down the middle. "Listen to me. There are people coming for you. Crown men, with papers and chains and a very organized way of hurting children. If they find out what I am, they won't stop at you. Do you understand? You'd be signing two death warrants instead of one."

"You'd be signing mine either way," Mara said. "At dawn. Your captain said."

"Yes."

"And you're telling me to keep *your* secret." She said it without accusation, which was somehow worse. Just laying the fact on the table between us, plain as bread. "Okay."

"*Okay?*"

"I'm good at secrets." She closed her hands, and the fire went out—not extinguished, *folded away*, tucked somewhere behind her skin like a knife in a boot. "I kept theirs for a long time. Everyone in my village did. Right up until the night it stopped mattering." Her jaw tightened. "The men who came had a machine. A cage of black iron, and it drank the fire right out of the elders. Out of my mother. It couldn't drink mine, though." She looked down at her palms. "It tried. This is what happened when it tried."

I thought about the crown herald who'd ridden through the valley a month ago, all velvet courtesy and wax-sealed notices: *By order of His Majesty, all bearers of unlicensed gifts must present themselves for assessment.* Assessment. I'd watched them load a cage of black iron onto a wagon outside the valley's edge and told myself it wasn't my problem, because nothing past the ridgeline was ever my problem.

The valley forgets. That's its whole nature. It had let me forget too—let me believe the oath was a formality, that waiting was the same as keeping faith, that hiding was the same as safety.

"Vesper?" Mara said.

"Hm?"

"You're doing the warm thing again."

I looked down. Faint light was leaking between my own fingers, gold-white, trembling. My gift—my long-dead, long-buried, carefully forgotten gift—was rising toward her like a plant toward a window. Eleven years of discipline, undone by proximity to a child who smelled of smoke and stubbornness.

I clenched my fist and killed the light. Mostly.

"Dawn's a long way off," I said.

Mara smiled for the first time. It was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of someone who has already survived the worst day of her life and found it survivable, and it scared me far more than any bounty hunter ever could.

Outside the tent, boots crunched on frost. Brannic's voice, low, talking to Osk—and then a third voice I didn't know, clipped and official, asking questions.

The crown hadn't waited for dawn after all.

Neither, it turned out, had I.
