## 3. Brothers at the Door

Captain Dree gave me until dawn, which was generous of her. She could have given me nothing at all.

"You've been with this company nineteen years," she said, standing in the doorway of my tent with her arms crossed and her shadow falling across the shrine I'd spent the evening tending. The little altar to the silent god sat between us like a third party to the conversation, its candle burned down to a thumb's width of wax. "Nineteen years, Vesper. You've bled beside us. You've buried beside us. And now you'd throw it away for a child you've known three days."

"She's not a bounty," I said. "She's a person."

"She's a person worth four hundred crowns." Dree said it flatly, without relish, which somehow made it worse. Dree had never relished anything in her life. She was the kind of woman who could read a casualty list aloud and make it sound like a grain inventory. "The herald rides back to the capital tomorrow. If we hand the girl over before then, the company eats through winter. If we don't, we're all guilty of harboring. You know what harboring means now. It means the pyre."

"I know what it means."

"Then you know I can't protect you anymore." Something moved behind her eyes—something old and tired that might have been affection, if affection were allowed to have a face in this valley. "Dawn, Vesper. Bring the girl to the square, or don't come to the square at all."

She left. The candle guttered. I sat on my bedroll and listened to the camp settle around me—the clink of mess tins, someone laughing too loudly near the cookfire, the ordinary music of men and women who had decided, over stew, that a burned child was currency.

Here is what nobody tells you about oaths: they don't feel like chains. They feel like bones. You forget you have them until something asks you to bend in a way they won't allow.

I had sworn mine at seventeen, kneeling on cold stone with a cord of grey wool around my wrist, speaking words to a god who had already stopped listening. *Keep the flame hidden. Keep the valley's peace. Hold the door shut from the inside.* Every mercenary born with a gift took the same oath—it was the price of sanctuary in a land where magic couldn't follow. We cut ourselves off from everything we were, and in exchange, the witch-hunters rode past our borders without a glance. Nineteen years I'd kept it. Nineteen years of feeling nothing where there should have been something, like a man living with a hearth bricked over and telling himself he never liked the warmth anyway.

And then Mara had walked into camp with fire cupped in her palms like a wounded bird, and for the first time since I was a girl, something under my skin had answered.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I went to see her instead.

They were keeping her in the supply tent—not out of cruelty, but because it was the only one with a lock, and locks are how kind people do unkind things without having to watch themselves do them. Sera stood guard outside, a broad-shouldered woman who'd taught me to throw a knife when I was twelve. She looked at me for a long moment, then stepped aside.

"Three days ago I'd have called you family," she said quietly.

"What would you call me now?"

"A fool I'm going to pretend not to see."

Inside, Mara sat cross-legged on a sack of oats, watching her own hands. The burns up her arms had scabbed into dark rivers, but her palms glowed faintly, gold light leaking between her fingers no matter how tightly she clenched them. She didn't look up when I came in.

"They're going to give me to the men in red coats," she said. Her voice was very small and very steady, the way voices get when a child has decided crying is a luxury she can't afford. "Sera told me. She thought I was asleep."

"Sera talks too much."

"Is it true?"

I crouched down in front of her. Up close, the light in her hands wasn't just light—it moved, curled, breathed. Dragon-fire. The last of it, maybe, in all the world, sitting in the palms of an eight-year-old who smelled of smoke and river water.

"Mara," I said, "when I was your age, a priest tied a cord around my wrist and told me that if I ever used what was inside me, I'd lose everything. My home. My people. Everyone I loved." I turned my wrist so she could see the grey wool, worn soft and dark with nineteen years of sweat. "So I hid it. I hid it so well I forgot it was there. And you know what happened? The people I loved are about to sell me anyway."

Her brow furrowed. "That's a bad story."

"It gets better. There's a part where we leave."

Something flickered across her face—hope trying to remember how its own muscles worked. "Where?"

"Out." I stood and offered her my hand. "There's a place past the valley's edge where the world remembers itself. Where magic comes back like the tide. It's dangerous, and it's far, and I'm not going to lie to you—I don't know what's on the other side. But I know what's here at dawn, and it isn't staying."

She put her small scorched fingers in mine. Even through the wool cord, I felt it: that answering pulse beneath my skin, faint as a second heartbeat, waking after a lifetime of sleep.

* * *

Getting out of camp was insultingly easy. That's the thing about nineteen years of being trustworthy—nobody watches you. I carried Mara on my hip like she weighed nothing, which was a lie; she weighed exactly what she was, four hundred crowns and a dead dragon's inheritance, and every step toward the treeline felt like walking out of a country with stolen gold sewn into my coat.

We made the rocks above the eastern pass before the moon set. From there I could see the whole valley laid out below—the company tents, the cookfire dying to embers, the shrine where I'd knelt every morning for two decades. Someone was standing at it. Small figure, grey cloak. Anselm, the old lay-brother who swept the shrine floor and asked no questions. He couldn't have seen me in the dark. But he raised one hand anyway, slow as a tree growing, and held it up until I turned away.

Traitors always imagine they'll feel triumphant. I felt like a bone being reset.

Because here was the truth I hadn't said aloud to anyone, least of all myself: cutting the oath-cord wouldn't just free me. It would open me. Everything I'd walled off for nineteen years—all the gift, all the danger, all the thing the crown burned people for—would come flooding back the moment I crossed the valley's edge. I'd be walking out of sanctuary carrying the exact crime I'd fled into it to escape. And I'd be carrying her.

Mara tugged my sleeve. "You're doing the face again."

"What face?"

"The one where you look like you swallowed a sermon."

"I'm deciding something," I said. "It takes effort."

"Decide faster. Your hands are shaking."

Cheeky creature. I almost smiled. Then I drew my knife, and I wasn't smiling at all.

The oath-cord had been tied by a priest's hands, blessed by a silent god, and soaked in nineteen years of my sweat and cowardice. It should have taken a blade to cut it. Instead, when I sawed the wool against the edge, it parted like breath—like it had only ever been waiting for permission. The severed ends drifted down into the dark below the rocks, and I watched them go and felt absolutely nothing break, because apparently there had been nothing left in me to break. Just space. Just room.

"Vesper?" Mara whispered. "What did you cut?"

"A leash," I said. "Mine."

* * *

The valley's edge is not a wall or a line. It's a place where the air changes texture, where the world goes quiet in a different key—a hush like the inside of a bell. The company called it the Shallows. Children dared each other to walk into it and stick a toe past the boundary, where nothing happened, because nothing ever happened there. Magic couldn't follow you in. That was the whole covenant. That was the whole mercy.

It also meant magic was waiting right outside it. All of it. Patiently. Like a dog left at a door for nineteen years.

I stepped across with Mara's hand in mine, and the tide came in.

It didn't hurt. That surprised me most. I'd imagined pain, punishment, the silent god's disapproval raining down—but there was no god in it at all, only the raw returning of myself, pouring back into the spaces I'd hollowed out. Light under my skin. Heat in my palms. A sense of the world suddenly loud with texture and meaning, every stone humming its own low note, the night sky close enough to touch. I staggered. Mara caught my arm with both hands, and her dragon-fire flared bright—and met mine, whatever mine was, and for one impossible moment the two lights recognized each other like old friends shouting across a market square.

"Oh," Mara breathed. Her eyes were enormous. "Oh, you're *lit up*. You're like me."

"Not like you," I managed. My voice shook. "Nothing is like you. But—" I flexed my fingers, watching the glow answer, watching nineteen years of exile dissolve like frost in sun. "But I'm not nothing anymore."

Behind us, far below, a horn sounded in the camp. Once. Twice. The alarm note. Dawn was still an hour off, but someone had checked the supply tent, or noticed the empty bedroll, or simply woken with the particular instinct that soldiers have for betrayal. Torches began to bloom across the valley floor like a rash.

"They're coming," Mara said.

"Yes."

"Are you scared?"

I considered lying to her, and decided she'd earned better. "Terrified," I said. "But it's a good terrified. It's the terrified of a door opening instead of closing. Can you tell the difference?"

She thought about it with her whole face. "I think so."

"Good. Because we're going to run on that feeling all the way to the river country." I lifted her onto my back, and she settled there, warm as a coal, her fire tucked against my neck like a secret. Below us, torchlight spilled up the pass. Above us, the stars hung strange and near, brighter than any sky I'd known inside the valley—as if the whole heavens had been waiting for me to come out and look.

"Hold tight," I told her. "And Mara—if anyone asks, you found me. Not the other way around. A mercenary doesn't rescue a bounty; a clever child recruits a bodyguard. Understood?"

A pause. Then, against my ear, deadly serious: "You're a terrible liar, Vesper Crane."

"So I've been told. Run anyway."

We ran—out of the pass, out of the silence, out of the long grey sleep of my oath—while behind us the horns kept calling my name into a valley that had already begun, gently and without malice, to forget me.
