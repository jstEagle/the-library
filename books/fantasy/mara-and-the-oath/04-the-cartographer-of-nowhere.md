## 4. The Cartographer of Nowhere

The river town was called Ferrow's Landing, which was a lie twice over—there had never been a Ferrow, and the landing was a mudbank where boats went to die. I decided I liked it immediately. A town built on two lies felt like a town that understood me.

Vesper did not like it at all.

"Too many eyes," they said, hunching their shoulders inside a borrowed fisherman's coat three sizes too big. Vesper had spent the whole week since we fled the valley trying to become smaller, which was a strange thing to watch someone do on purpose. They were tall in the way that fence posts are tall, all angles and stubbornness, and no amount of slouching could make them forgettable. "Towns have bells. Bells mean soldiers."

"Towns also mean bread," I said, because my stomach had been making arguments since dawn and it argued louder than Vesper ever could.

We had crossed out of the valley four days ago, and I still hadn't gotten used to what the world did outside it. In the valley, everything was flat and quiet, like soup left out too long. Out here, things *hummed*. The river hummed. The willows hummed. And under my skin, in the palms of my hands, something answered the humming like a dog hearing its name from another room.

I kept my hands wrapped in rags. Vesper had taught me that much before anything else: *wrap them, keep them down, never let anyone see you warm your fingers by your own fire.* It was the first kindness anyone had done for me since my village burned, and it came wrapped in orders, which is how most kindness comes when it comes from mercenaries.

We found an inn called the Drowned Eel, which promised neither drowning nor eels and delivered on both counts, depending on the stew. Vesper counted out coins earned hauling crates on the dock road—mercenary work reduced to dock work, which they pretended not to mind and minded constantly—and bought us a room with one bed, one stool, and a window that looked out on the corpse of a rowboat.

"You sleep nearest the wall," Vesper said.

"I always sleep nearest the wall."

"Because I say so this time. Not because of the fire."

I looked at them. They were studying the window latch very hard, as if it might be planning something.

"The fire doesn't hurt people," I said. "It only hurts what I tell it to hurt."

"I know." A pause. "That's not what worries me."

They didn't explain, and I didn't ask, because that was our arrangement: Vesper gave me the edges of truths and I learned not to fall off them. We ate the eel stew—it tasted mostly of drowning—and I fell asleep listening to the river hum through the floorboards, and for the first time since the burning, I dreamed about something other than smoke.

* * *

In the morning, Vesper went to find work and told me to stay in the room, and I stayed in the room for approximately the length of time it takes bread to cool, which is to say: not long enough.

It wasn't disobedience, exactly. It was curiosity, and curiosity is just obedience pointed the wrong direction. There was a building near the docks with bars on its windows—not a jail exactly, more like a place where the town kept things it didn't know how to throw away. Old rope. Broken traps. And, sitting in the barred window like she'd been installed as part of the architecture, a woman with grey-streaked hair drawing on the wall.

Drawing on the *wall*. With charcoal. Great sweeping lines, rivers and ridges, coastlines curling into places I had no names for.

I should have walked on. Vesper would want me to walk on. But the woman drew a mountain range and then, without pausing, drew a country beyond it—a whole country, with cities marked in careful little squares and roads stitched between them like seams—and I stopped dead in the street, because everyone knew there was nothing past the Graywatch Mountains. Everyone knew it the way everyone knows the sun rises in the east. There was nothing there. There had never been anything there.

"There isn't anything there," I said, before my brain could catch up with my mouth.

The woman turned her head. She had ink on her fingers, charcoal on her cheek, and eyes the color of a storm deciding whether to bother. She looked at me the way a jeweler looks at a stone—quick, thorough, filing me away somewhere.

"That," she said, "is the single most interesting thing anyone has said to me in three years. And I once had a conversation with a goat."

"It's true, though. There's nothing past the mountains. My gran said—"

"Your gran said what she was told, and whoever told her was told the same, all the way back until nobody remembered being lied to." She tapped the charcoal against the wall, right on the little square city she'd drawn. "I mapped that country. Every river, every road, every door. Do you know what they gave me for it?"

"A reward?" I guessed, because I was young and stupid and hopeful.

"A cell." She smiled, and it wasn't a nice smile but it was an honest one, which I would learn was rarer. "Well. This. Bars instead of walls, the town's too cheap for a real jail. Ilsa Vane, formerly Royal Cartographer, currently decoration for a warehouse. And you're the child who smells like woodsmoke and is standing far too close to a woman the crown officially declares does not exist."

My stomach dropped. I looked down at my wrapped hands. "I don't smell like—"

"Dragon-fire has a smell, girl. Like a hearth the morning after, like rain on hot stones. I mapped the old borders; I've read what sleeps past them." Her voice dropped low, and though the street behind me was empty, she glanced both ways like a woman crossing herself. "You're carrying the last of it, or I'm a worse cartographer than the crown thinks."

Every sensible part of me said *run*. Vesper's voice in my head said *run*. But my feet had gone traitor, rooted to the cobblestones, and my mouth—my mouth was apparently run by the same traitors—asked:

"What door?"

Ilsa Vane went very still. Then she laughed, short and sharp, delighted and unsettled by her own delight. "Oh, you're dangerous. You're exactly the kind of dangerous I can't afford." She leaned close to the bars, close enough that I could see the map scars on her knuckles, old cuts from drafting knives. "There's a door, girl. The last door into the erased country. The crown burned every map of it, exiled every soul who remembered it, and called it mercy. But doors don't stop existing because you burn the drawings of them. Doors are patient. Doors wait."

"And you know where it is."

"I know where it *was*. Which, in that country, may not be the same thing." She straightened up, and something in her face closed, like a shutter going down over a lit window. "Go home, child. Tell your keeper I said hello—the big one with the guilty conscience and the soldier's walk. And if you ever decide you want to stop running *from* something and start running *toward* it, you know where I'll be. Nowhere else to be, after all."

I ran. Of course I ran. I was eleven years old and a stranger had just named the thing I carried in front of God and an empty street, and running was the only prayer I knew.

But even as I ran, some small treacherous coal in my chest glowed warmer than it had in days. Because she hadn't looked at my hands with fear. She hadn't reached for a bucket of water or a priest or a bounty notice. She had looked at me like I was a *map*—like I was a country worth knowing, worth the drawing of, worth the risk of a cell.

Nobody had ever looked at me like that before.

* * *

Vesper was back at the inn when I returned, smelling of rope and river water, counting coins that weren't enough. I tried to look innocent. I have been told my innocent face looks like a cat that has recently been in the cream, so it went poorly.

"Where were you?"

"Walking."

"Mara."

"There's a woman," I said, because lying to Vesper was like lying to a fence post—you got nothing back and felt foolish doing it. "In the warehouse by the docks. Behind bars. She draws maps on the wall. She says—" I took a breath. "She says there's a door. Past the mountains. Into the country that got erased. And she knows where it is."

The coins stopped moving. Vesper's hands went flat on the table, and for a long moment they just breathed, slow and controlled, the way they did when they were choosing not to break something.

"No," they said finally.

"You didn't let me finish."

"There's nothing to finish. It's a trap, or it's madness, or it's a trap made of madness. The crown erased that country for a reason. People who talk about doors get burned, Mara. You've seen what gets burned." Their voice caught on the last word, just slightly, and I knew they were seeing my village, the pyres, the sky full of ash. They saw it more often than I did, I think. Survivors carry each other's fires whether they mean to or not.

"She wasn't afraid of me," I said quietly.

Vesper looked up. Something moved behind their eyes—something old and tired and hungry all at once.

"Everyone's afraid of you eventually," they said. "It's safer if it starts early."

"Well, she hasn't started. And she's been locked up for three years for drawing a picture, which means she's either the bravest person in Ferrow's Landing or the maddest, and I've met enough brave-and-mad to fill a boat." I sat down across from them and put my wrapped hands on the table between us, deliberately, the way you lay cards down. "Vesper. We can't run forever. The hunt doesn't get tired. The heralds don't get tired. Eventually some town turns us in, or my fire flares at the wrong moment, or—" I swallowed. "Or they find us. There's a door, Vesper. An actual door. That's not running anymore. That's *arriving* somewhere."

For a while, the only sound was the river humming under the floorboards, and the creak of the inn settling, and Vesper breathing.

"They'll hang me for breaking my oath already," they said at last, and it wasn't a yes, but it wasn't a no, and with Vesper those were the same country separated only by a river. "Cutting the cord was supposed to be the end of it. Free and clear. And now you want to walk toward the one place in the world where oaths might actually matter."

"Doors are patient," I said. "Doors wait."

Vesper stared at me. "Where did you hear that?"

"The cartographer. Ilsa Vane." I stood up, heart hammering, feeling less like a fugitive and more like a compass needle finding north. "Come meet her. If she's a trap, we'll know. If she's mad, we'll know. And if she's right—"

"If she's right," Vesper said slowly, "then everything changes."

"Everything's already changed," I said. "It changed the night you cut the cord. We're just catching up to it."

They looked at me for a long time—that appraising, mercenary look, weighing odds, measuring exits—and then they sighed the sigh of a person surrendering to something they'd already lost, and grabbed their coat.

"One hour," they said. "And if she so much as mentions a bounty, we're gone."

But when we reached the warehouse, the barred window was empty. The charcoal map still covered the wall inside—rivers, ridges, the impossible country stitched in careful lines—but Ilsa Vane was gone, and in her place, nailed to the sill where she'd been sitting, was a scrap of paper with a sketch drawn quick and sure: a door, standing alone in a mountainside, and beneath it three words in a cartographer's precise hand:

*Graywatch. Third pass. Come armed.*
