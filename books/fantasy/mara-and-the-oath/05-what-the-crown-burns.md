## 5. What the Crown Burns

I have drawn maps of executions before. Not on purpose—cartographers record what exists, and in the capital, the scaffolds are as much a landmark as the aqueduct. But there is a difference between inkling a gallows into a corner of a city plan and standing close enough to smell the pitch.

We came to Corren's Ford because I needed paper and Vesper needed silence, and because Mara needed neither and wanted sweet buns, which is how children negotiate. The town sits where the river bends lazy around a hill, all slate roofs and fish-smoke, the kind of place that believes itself too small for history. There was a fair on. Bunting strung between eaves. A man selling whistles shaped like birds.

I should have known. Crowns do not burn witches at their leisure; they burn them as spectacle, and spectacles require fairs.

Vesper spotted it first—the scaffold, or rather the platform built at the river's edge with a great iron cage at its center, and around the cage, stacked like cordwood, bundles of something pale. Not wood. Bone-white rods, each taller than a man, bound with copper wire.

"Quicksilver ash," I said, before I could stop myself. "Cured willow soaked in it. It's how you make a fire that burns *cold*."

"And why would anyone want that?" Anselm asked quietly. The priest had been quiet since the Reach, quieter since his confession about the god's devouring, wearing his grief like a coat two sizes too small.

"Because cold fire doesn't destroy what it burns," I said. "It *draws* it out first. Alchemists use it to separate ore." I looked at the cage again, at the figure huddled inside—a woman, gray-haired, wrists bound with iron. "That's not an execution pyre. That's a crucible."

Vesper's hand found my shoulder. Not gently. "Explain. Slowly."

So I did, in the low voice we'd all learned to use in crowds: the crown's relic-houses, the ones I'd mapped as a younger woman with a royal seal and a fool's faith; the dead relic beneath the capital that the old kings had called the Ember Throne—not a throne of embers but a throne *for* ember, a vessel built to hold fire that no longer burned. I'd seen its chamber once, during my survey years. A vast hollow thing of black iron, cold as a well in winter, and the court alchemists whispering around it like priests around an empty tomb.

"They're not hunting the gifted to punish them," I said. "They're hunting them to *harvest* them. Every gift they burn out of a person goes into that throne. They're trying to reignite it."

"With people," Mara said. She was holding her sweet bun and not eating it, which from Mara was a sign of catastrophe on par with plague.

"With people," I confirmed.

* * *

The herald read the charges from a scroll he clearly couldn't fully see over his own nose. The woman in the cage was named Maren Ostwick, accused of "the crime of unlicensed flame"—she'd heated her neighbor's bathwater, apparently, after the neighbor's husband lost both hands to a reaping accident. Three witnesses testified. Two wept. The third looked proud of himself in the way of men who inform for coin and call it piety.

The crowd was the worst part. Not cruel, exactly. Worse than cruel: *comfortable*. They'd brought lunch. Children sat on fathers' shoulders. A vendor moved through the throng selling roasted chestnuts, doing brisk business, because nothing drives commerce like a public burning.

Mara's hand crept into mine. Her palm was hot—always hot now, hotter since the Reach, since she'd flared bright enough to paint us across the sky for every hunter in three counties. I squeezed it and felt her tremble and thought: *she is looking at her own future.* And then I thought the worse thought, the one I'd been carrying since the pyre-wood came into view: *she is looking at her own future and the crowd brought chestnuts.*

"I can't watch this," Vesper said.

"Then don't," I said. "But look anyway. We need to know how many soldiers. How they move the prisoners. Whether there's a wagon."

Vesper looked at me then, and I watched the mercenary arithmetic happen behind those eyes—the cold counting that keeps hired swords alive—and I watched it lose to something else. "You want to take her off that platform."

"I want to know if it's possible," I said, which was true, and also a lie, because Ilsa Vane has never once mapped a road she didn't intend someone to walk.

"There are forty spears," Vesper said flatly. "Two crossbow teams on the mill roof. And that cage is wrought iron an inch thick. No."

"I didn't say today."

"You were thinking it loudly enough that the whole town heard."

Anselm cleared his throat, soft as snowfall. "Perhaps," he murmured, "we might discuss logistics after the poor woman is either dead or not, whichever the crown decides."

But none of us left. That's the thing I'll carry—the fact that none of us walked away, though every sensible instinct screamed at us to melt into the fair and forget the gray-haired woman whose only crime was kindness with a side of arson. We stood among the chestnut-eaters and we watched.

They lit the quicksilver ash at noon.

Cold fire is a lie of a thing. It gives no light worth having, no warmth—it burns blue-white and *hungry*, and the smoke it makes doesn't rise so much as get *pulled*, streaming toward the cage in slow silver ribbons. And the woman—gods, the woman. She didn't scream at first. She arched, and from her open mouth came light, actual light, gold and flickering, torn out of her in threads like wool from a spindle. The ribbons of smoke drank it. Carried it up. And above the platform, where the smoke gathered, there hung—for just a moment—a shape. A shimmering jar of glass and copper, warded and sealed, filling with stolen gold.

The crowd gasped at the pretty lights. Someone applauded.

Mara made a sound I hope never to hear again. Small. Like a door closing in an empty house. Then her hand went furnace-hot in mine and the chestnut vendor nearest us yelped and stared at his smoking tray, and Vesper moved—fast, mercenary-fast—putting their body between Mara and the crowd, muttering, "*Not here, little dragon, not here,*" and folding the girl against their chest like hiding a lantern under a coat.

The woman in the cage stopped screaming when there was nothing left in her to scream with. What they took down afterward wasn't a person anymore. It was a shape that had been one. The soldiers handled it with tongs.

And the jar—the jar went onto a black wagon drawn by two gray horses, escorted by six riders in crown livery, rolling east. Toward the capital. Toward the empty throne.

* * *

We rented a room above a dyer's shop because it smelled so strongly of mordant that no hunter would expect anyone to choose it voluntarily. I spread my maps on the floor while Vesper paced and Anselm prayed to nobody and Mara sat by the window with her knees pulled up, watching the black wagon's dust still hanging over the eastern road like a bruise.

"The wagons run east twice a month," I said, tapping the river road. "I know the schedule because I drew the supply routes myself, back when I thought I was mapping roads and not building a machine for grinding up gifted people. Six riders per wagon. They change horses at Harrow Bridge. From there—" my finger traced north, "—they cut through the King's Mile, and the King's Mile has exactly one gate, and I have the original gate plans memorized."

"You're proposing to rob the crown," Vesper said.

"I'm proposing to rob a *wagon*," I said. "The crown won't miss it for days. And inside that jar is everything they took from Maren Ostwick, who heated bathwater for a grieving widow. If we can crack the warding—and Anselm knows the old liturgies, and wards are just prayers with locks—we can free what's in it. Give it back to the wind. To the god that isn't there anymore. To *anything* but that throne."

"It won't bring her back," Anselm said gently.

"No," I agreed. "Nothing brings anyone back. I've made my peace with being useless that way. But I'll be damned—" I caught myself, glanced at Anselm, amended, "—I'll be further damned if I let them feed her to a dead king's furnace without costing them something."

Silence. The dye-vats stank below us. Somewhere a child was practicing a whistle-bird, badly.

Then Mara spoke from the window, and her voice had changed—had gone from the small closed-door sound to something with iron in it, and I remembered that dragon-fire doesn't only live in palms. It lives in spines.

"They counted the crowd," she said. "Did you see? A man on the mill roof with a tally-stick. He marked every time the light came out. Like counting *yield*."

I hadn't seen that. Vesper hadn't either. Only the child who knew exactly what was being weighed had seen it.

"Mara," Vesper started, in the voice adults use when they intend to say *no*,

"He wrote a number," she said, turning. Her eyes had gone gold at the edges, banked coals, and for once I didn't want her to dim them. "Forty-one. Forty-one jars this season. I'm going to be forty-two unless we stop them."

Nobody argued. That was the terrible grace of it—horror does what sermons cannot. Vesper stopped pacing. Anselm folded his hands. And I, who had lost my name and my guild and my country to a crown that erased things it disliked, felt the old cartographer's fever rise in me at last for a worthy purpose: not to record the world, but to *unmake* one small piece of the crown's design.

"Harrow Bridge," I said, pulling a fresh sheet toward me. "Wagon changes horses at dusk on the third day. Which gives us two days to get ahead of it, and one night to build a plan."

Vesper looked at the map, then at me, then at Mara's burning eyes, and sighed the sigh of a person whose oath died weeks ago and whose conscience has been making noise ever since.

"Third day," they said. "Dusk. And Ilsa—if this goes wrong, you're drawing our escape route *first*."

"Naturally," I said, and reached for my ink.
