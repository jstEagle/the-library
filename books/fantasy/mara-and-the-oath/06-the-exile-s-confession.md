## 6. The Exile's Confession

The rain found us three days out of the pyre-town, and by then none of us had the strength to pretend we weren't running.

We walked with our hoods up and our heads down, four figures on a road that had stopped pretending to lead anywhere. Ilsa's half-burned map rode against my chest, wrapped in oilcloth like a wound dressing. Mara rode on Ilsa's back when her legs gave out, which was often, and complained about it even in her sleep, which was always. The fire in her palms had gone quiet since the crossing—banked down to embers, the way a hearth sleeps—but I could feel it anyway. Like standing near a lit window on a cold night. Like being watched by something warm.

I hated how much I'd come to rely on that feeling. Twelve years an oath-bound mercenary teaches you to trust nothing warmer than a whetstone.

The chapel appeared out of the rain the way grief appears out of ordinary days: suddenly, and as if it had been waiting for you all along. It crouched in a fold of hills where the road forgot itself—a squat stone thing with a roof of turf and a bell tower that ended in a stump, snapped off clean, like the sky had taken a bite and found it wanting. No sigil above the door. No god's name carved anywhere. Just a door, and smoke rising from behind it, and the smell of woodsmoke and something else underneath. Something old. Something that made the hair on my arms stand up and my oath-cord—the stub of it, the cut end still knotted around my wrist—go strangely, achingly quiet.

"Someone lives here," Mara said, sliding off Ilsa's back.

"People who live in broken bell towers are either saints or hiding," Ilsa said. "In my experience the two overlap more than the Church would like."

"We're hiding," Mara pointed out.

"Then we'll get along famously."

I knocked anyway. Habit. My mother raised me polite before the company raised me armed, and some lessons outlast the teacher.

The door opened on a man who looked like his order had died and he hadn't gotten the message—or had gotten it and decided to keep wearing the uniform out of spite. Grey robe, patched at the elbows. A tonsure grown out to grey fuzz, as if he'd started letting it go and lost his nerve halfway. He was perhaps sixty, perhaps older; hard living writes its own arithmetic. His eyes were pale as river-washed stone, and they went from me to Ilsa to Mara and stopped on Mara the way a man stops at a grave he dug himself.

"Well," he said. "You'd better come in before the road changes its mind about you."

"You know what she is," I said. Not a question.

"I know what's following you." He stepped aside. "Come in, come in. Anselm. Brother Anselm, though there's no brotherhood left to be brother of. Mind the third floorboard; it bites."

* * *

The inside of the chapel was one room, and it was the saddest kind of holy I've ever seen. A stone altar worn smooth by hands that no longer came. Shelves of books swollen with damp. Candles burned down to puddles and relit so many times their wicks were black forests. And everywhere—everywhere—small signs of a man keeping faith alone: a broom worn short from sweeping a floor no congregation dirtied. Three cups set out every morning for guests who never came. A wall covered in little chalk marks, tallies of days, going up past counting until the plaster ran out of room and he'd started over in a corner.

He fed us barley soup that tasted like patience. Mara ate three bowls and fell asleep sitting up against Ilsa's shoulder, her cupped hands cradled in her lap like something rescued from a fire—which, I suppose, is exactly what they were.

Anselm watched her sleep the way I'd once watched the shrine in the valley: with the particular devotion of a man who has nothing left to lose but the watching itself.

"She's the last," he said quietly. "Isn't she."

"I don't know what she is."

"You do." He turned those stone-pale eyes on me. "You've known since the valley. That's why you cut your cord. That's why you're here, in the rain, in my ruined parish, instead of spending her for the bounty that would buy you a cottage and a long cowardly life." He smiled without much humor. "Forgive me. Exile makes a man blunt. No one to flatter."

Ilsa snorted into her soup. "He's not wrong, Crane."

"He's wrong about the cottage. I'd have wanted a boat."

That got a real smile out of him, brief as a struck match. Then it went out, and he said, "Show me your wrist."

Every instinct I owned stood up and told me to refuse. I showed him my wrist.

The oath-cord's cut end was a knot of braided hemp, twelve years of sweat and weather worked into it, frayed where my knife had gone through. Anselm didn't touch it. He held his palm above it, close enough that I felt warmth—not his warmth, something else—and the strangest thing happened. The cord *sighed*. There's no better word for it. The fibers relaxed, the way a fist relaxes, the way a body relaxes when someone finally tells it the fight is over.

Anselm drew his hand back like he'd touched a stove.

"So it's true," he whispered. "They said the last oaths were given to soldiers. I never believed it. I thought it was a story we told to make the silence bearable." He looked at me with something I couldn't name—pity, or awe, or the specific horror of a scholar whose worst theory comes home wearing boots. "Vesper Crane. Do you know what you're carrying?"

"A knot and a bad attitude."

"Do you know why the god went silent?"

I'd had twelve years to build an answer to that question, and I built walls around it the way the company built palisades: fast, functional, and never examined too closely. "Gods go silent. Everyone knows it. The world moved on. People stopped believing, stopped tithing, stopped—" I heard myself reciting the catechism and stopped, because Anselm was shaking his head, slow and sorrowful, and my voice died in my throat like a bird hitting glass.

"The god did not go silent," he said. "The god was *eaten*."

* * *

Mara slept through the first part of it. Ilsa didn't. She sat with her back to the wall, arms crossed, wearing the expression of a cartographer being shown a map that contradicts everything she ever drew—which, in a sense, she was.

"It was a hundred years ago," Anselm said. He'd moved to the altar and lit a candle off the last one, a small ceremony of two flames touching. "My order kept the records. The real ones, not the ones the crown burned. The god of the oath—the Quiet One, the Binder, whatever your company called Him—was not a distant power. He was a *doorpost*. Do you understand? He was the hinge between what magic is and what magic does. Every gift in the world passed through Him the way water passes through a lock. And something came—from outside, from beneath, from wherever things come from—and it devoured Him. Not killed. Devoured. Swallowed whole, hinge and all."

"And the silence?" Ilsa asked.

"The silence was the swallowing." He turned. "Imagine every bell in the world ringing at once and then, mid-note, being stuffed with wool. That was the Silence. We felt it in our teeth. The gifts didn't die—they went *deaf*. Cut off from the hinge, they scattered like sheep without a shepherd. Some guttered. Some went feral. Some—" he nodded at Mara's sleeping hands— "went into hiding, generation after generation, smaller each time, waiting for a door that no longer opened."

"The witch-hunts," Ilsa said slowly. "The crown's relic. The harvesting."

"The crown found a bone of the devourer, buried deep, and they think if they feed it enough gift-fire it will wake and give them power. They are feeding the thing that ate our god." Anselm's voice cracked on the word *our*, and I understood, suddenly, that this exile wasn't punishment. It was mourning. He'd been sitting in this ruin for thirty years, keeping candles lit for a corpse nobody would admit was dead. "But they're wrong about the bone, and they're wrong about the flame, and most of all they are wrong about the spark."

"What spark?" I asked, though my wrist already knew. The cord had gone warm against my pulse, warm as a sleeping animal.

"When the god was swallowed, the last of Him did not go down that throat." Anselm crossed the room and knelt in front of me, and I want it recorded that I let him, because I was too busy forgetting how to breathe. "The High Rite, in the last years. The binding of oaths. Every sworn soldier received a thread of the god Himself, spun into the cord, tied around the wrist. A promise and a piece, both. When the Silence came, the threads went dark. The order assumed they'd died with Him." His eyes lifted to mine. "But a spark doesn't die in the dark, Vesper. It *sleeps*. And yours has been sleeping against your skin for twelve years, and three nights ago, near that child, it woke up."

"That's not possible," Ilsa said. "His gift is dormant. It's been dormant for years. The valley—"

"My gift is dormant," I agreed. My voice sounded like someone else's. "I'd know. It's mine."

"Is it?" Anselm asked gently. "Or have you been carrying something all along that isn't a gift at all, but a *guest*? You thought you were a mercenary with a broken oath. You are a lantern with the last light of a god folded inside it, and you have spent twelve years cursing the dark for not letting you see."

The rain filled the pause. Somewhere under the floorboards, water dripped with the patience of all dripping things.

I looked at my wrist. The frayed cord. The knot I'd tied at fourteen, when the captain laid the braid across my arm and spoke the words I'd repeated every night since: *Hold the door. Keep the faith. Though He is silent, hold.*

I'd thought the words were a memorial. A prayer to something absent.

They'd been instructions.

"Hold the door," I said aloud.

"Until it could be opened again," Anselm finished. "Yes. That was always the whole of the oath. Your company forgot. The world forgot. But the spark remembered, Vesper. It has been holding on inside you the way a drowning man holds a rope—not because he believes in the far shore, but because letting go is unthinkable."

Mara stirred in her sleep. Her hands uncurled, just slightly, and in the cup of them a coal of dragon-fire breathed—one slow pulse of gold, like a heartbeat seen through skin—and the oath-cord on my wrist pulsed in answer. In time. In *time*, like two hearts finding the same rhythm across a dark room.

Anselm made a sound low in his chest, half prayer, half sob.

"They're calling to each other," he breathed. "The last of Him and the last of the fire. Oh—you poor, magnificent fool. You haven't been hiding from the hunt at all. You've been *carrying* it. The crown isn't hunting the girl. They're hunting the pair of you together, because together—" He stopped. Pressed his lips together. Whatever he'd calculated next, he couldn't make himself say it.

"Together, what?" Ilsa demanded.

"Together they can do what the god did," Anselm said. "Open the lock. Or break it forever. And the crown means to be holding them both when it happens."

I sat very still. Rain on the roof. Ember-light in a child's hands. Twelve years of silence reassembling themselves in my chest into something that wasn't silence at all—it was a held breath. A door, held shut from the inside, by me, all this time, without knowing.

I had inherited a duty I never understood from a god I never met, and I had carried it faithfully into the one place magic couldn't follow, and I had called that safety.

It had never been safety. It had been storage.

"Get some sleep," Anselm said softly, rising, taking the candle with him. "All of you. Tomorrow we talk about maps and doors and what must be fed to what. Tonight—" he paused at the foot of the stairs to his broken tower, and the candlelight made his face young for a moment, the way grief sometimes does— "tonight, let the poor devil rest. He's been holding a god in his fist for twelve years, and nobody even thanked him."

He climbed into the dark. I stayed by the banked fire, wrist upturned, watching the cord rise and fall with my pulse.

Somewhere north, the crown was burning people to feed a bone of the thing that ate our god. Somewhere east, a door stood in a country erased from every map. And somewhere south, in a valley that forgets, men I'd bled beside were probably telling stories about the traitor who ran.

I closed my hand around the spark of a dead god and, for the first time in twelve years, felt it close back.

In the morning, Anselm woke us before dawn, grey-faced, listening to the eastern hills.

"Bells," he said. "Witch-hunters ring them when they've picked up a scent." He looked at me, and then at Mara, whose hands were already glowing in her sleep. "And these bells are ringing *our* name."
