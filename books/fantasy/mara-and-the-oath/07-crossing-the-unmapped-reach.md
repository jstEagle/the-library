## 7. Crossing the Unmapped Reach

The first thing you learn about a country that doesn't exist is that it doesn't want to be found.

Ilsa had warned us at the river town, unrolling her forbidden maps by candlelight like a woman showing off her sins. "The Reach isn't hidden," she'd said, tapping a place where the parchment simply stopped. "It's *forgotten*. There's a difference. Hidden things can be searched for. Forgotten things have to be remembered."

I didn't understand what she meant until we crossed the border stones and the world behind us went soft at the edges, like a painting left out in the rain.

Vesper went first, because Vesper always went first, cutting the tall grey grass with a knife that had seen more oaths than battles. Anselm walked second with his broken god tucked under his arm in the form of a splinter of shrine-wood wrapped in cloth. Ilsa came third, muttering bearings to herself, checking a compass that spun like a drunk. I came last, which suited me fine. Last meant nobody was behind me. Last meant I could watch all their backs at once.

"You're supposed to be in the middle," Vesper said without turning around. They always knew where I was. It was unnerving, the way they tracked me—by sound, by the heat I gave off, maybe, though they never said that part aloud.

"Middle's for people who trust the front," I said.

Vesper snorted. "Smart child."

"I'm eleven."

"Smart child," they repeated, and there was something almost warm under the dryness, like a coal under ash.

* * *

We lost the mule on the second day.

Not to wolves or weather or any of the dramatic deaths Ilsa kept predicting for the poor animal. We lost him to the country itself. One moment he was hauling our food and tents and Anselm's books through a field of pale grass; the next, Ilsa turned to check his lead and the rope ended in nothing. Not cut. Not dropped. Just *nothing*, fraying into air, as if the mule had been subtracted from the sum of the world.

"The Reach eats what isn't held onto," Ilsa said quietly. She was very still, staring at the rope-end. "Memories. Names. Supplies." She looked at each of us in turn. "Hold on to each other. Literally, if you have to."

So we tied ourselves together with the mule's abandoned lead-rope, four people strung along thirty feet of cord like beads on an abacus, and we walked into the white interior of a country no king had ever claimed because no king could remember claiming it.

That night we ate the last of the hard bread and Vesper gave me the bigger half without making a show of it, which was its own kind of show. Anselm said grace to a god who wasn't listening, then caught my eye and winked, which I think was his way of saying grace to one who might be.

"What happens if the Reach forgets us?" I asked.

Nobody answered fast enough, so I answered myself: "Then we stop existing."

"No," Anselm said gently. "Worse. Everyone else stops remembering you existed. You'd keep walking. You'd just be walking alone forever, and not know why the road felt so empty."

I decided I hated the Reach.

* * *

On the fourth day the witch-hunters found our trail anyway.

We heard them before we saw them—the baying of hounds that didn't bark so much as *sing*, long and wrong, harmonizing with each other across miles. Ilsa went grey. "They've got a rememberer," she said. "Someone who can hold a thought of us in their head and walk straight toward it. The Reach can't hide us from a mind that won't let go."

"How close?" Vesper asked.

"A day. Maybe less."

Vesper looked at the sky, at the flat white ceiling of cloud, and made a decision the way other people swat flies. "We run for the pass. Ilsa, how far?"

"Three days' walk."

"Then we make it two."

We made it two by throwing away everything that wasn't water or rope or the maps themselves. Anselm's books went first—he kissed each one before dropping it, murmuring apologies to authors three hundred years dead. Then the tent. Then the spare boots, the salt, the little iron pot Ilsa had carried since her disgrace. Each thing left behind seemed to vanish faster than it should, the Reach swallowing it whole, and I understood that we were feeding the country to buy passage. A toll paid in belongings.

By the second night we could see the pass—a black notch in a ridge of mountains that weren't on any map, because maps are drawn by people who remember, and this ridge had been forgotten down to its roots. And behind us, closer now, the hounds sang.

Anselm fell at moonrise.

He didn't trip. He just—stopped, mid-stride, and folded up like a chair, and when we got to him his eyes were open but looking at something very far away, and his lips were moving. I put my ear close.

"—and the light of the seven lamps, and the mercy of the quiet one—" He was praying. He was praying and his hands were blue and the cold coming off him wasn't mountain-cold. It was the Reach, pulling at him, trying to file him away among the forgotten things. He was old, and tired, and he had spent forty years carrying a grief too heavy to set down, and the country had noticed.

"He's being forgotten," Ilsa whispered, horrified. "While he's still *here*. Mara, talk to him. Make him real to you. If someone holds you in mind, the Reach can't take you—"

I grabbed his face in both hands. "Brother Anselm. Anselm. Look at me. You owe me a story about the shrine bells. You promised. You *promised*—"

His eyes drifted toward me and past me.

And the fire woke up.

I hadn't called it. That's the truth I'll tell for the rest of my life: I didn't call it, it answered. It came up my arms like sunrise, gold-white and furious, and poured out of my palms into the old man's chest—not burning, never burning him, warming him the way a hearth warms a room—and somewhere inside the Reach's grey hunger, something met something else, and the country flinched.

Because dragon-fire doesn't get forgotten. Dragon-fire is the thing fire remembers *being*.

Anselm gasped like a man pulled from deep water. His eyes found mine and focused. "Mara," he said. "Child. Your hands—"

"It's fine," I said, and my voice came out strange, doubled, ringing faintly like a struck bell. "It's fine, get up, we have to move—"

But Ilsa was staring upward, and her face told me it was not fine.

Above the clouds, directly over us, a light bloomed. White-gold, searing through the cloud-cover from above, hanging there like a second dawn. The mark of what I'd done, written across the sky for anyone with eyes—or instruments, or a rememberer—to read.

"They know where we are," Ilsa said. "Exactly. To the footstep."

The hounds changed key. Closer. Faster.

Vesper hauled Anselm upright, slung his arm over their shoulders, and looked at me—looked at the fire still guttering along my arms, dying down now, leaving me shaking and empty and terrified of myself.

"Well," Vesper said. "Now they know we're coming. Let's not disappoint them."

* * *

We ran the last miles to the pass with the sky-light burning over us like a brand, and I'll tell you what I learned about found family in those miles: it isn't made around fires and songs. It's made when an exiled priest who can barely stand refuses to let go of your hand because letting go would mean you might be forgotten, and when a disgraced mapmaker walks backward the whole way so she can watch the road behind, and when a mercenary with a broken oath carries a man twice their weight and never once says *we should leave him, we'd go faster.*

Nobody said it. That was the family. The not-saying-it.

We reached the notch in the ridge as true night finally fell, and Ilsa pressed her palm against the black stone and breathed a word she'd been imprisoned for knowing, and a door-shape of older darkness opened in the rock—narrow, breathing cold air that smelled, impossibly, of green growing things.

Beyond it lay the heart of the erased country. Beyond it lay whatever the crown would burn towns to reach.

Vesper went through first, then Anselm, leaning hard. Ilsa paused at the threshold, looking back down the valley we'd crossed—at the distant pinprick lights of the hunt, converging on the sky-mark I'd left burning above the pass like a signature.

"They'll follow us through," she said. "That light told them everything. Where the door is. What you are."

"I'm sorry," I said.

"Don't be sorry." She stepped through and held out her hand to me. "Be ready."

I took her hand, and the fire stirred in my palms again—not frightened now, but awake, attentive, like a dog that has finally heard its name—and together we left the Reach behind us, and the door began to close, and somewhere below us in the dark, the singing of the hounds did not stop.
