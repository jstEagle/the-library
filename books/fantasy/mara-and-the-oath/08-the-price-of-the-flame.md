## 8. The Price of the Flame

The border ruins announced themselves the way old debts do—suddenly, and with everyone pretending they'd known all along.

Ilsa stopped at the crest of the ridge, her half-burned satchel clutched to her chest like a sick child, and said, "Well. There it is. The edge of the map."

Below us lay what remained of a fortress that no longer had a name to defend. Walls like broken teeth, towers slumped into their own foundations, a gatehouse split open as if something enormous had once yawned its way through. Beyond it, the land went strange—the grass silvered, the air shimmering faintly, the horizon refusing to commit to any particular shape. The erased country. Even from here I could feel it pulling, the way a river pulls at your boots when you stand too long in the shallows.

And between us and it, camped in the ruins like they owned the deed: witch-hunters. A dozen tents, cook-fires, picket lines. And at their center, a pavilion of white silk that had no business being this far from any capital.

"They were waiting," Anselm said quietly.

"Of course they were waiting," Ilsa said. "I mapped this crossing twenty years ago. If the crown wanted the door, they only needed to sit on the one road to it and grow old." She looked at me. "They're not here for us, Vesper. They're here for the door."

"They're here for whatever walks through it," I said. "Which is us."

Mara tugged my sleeve. Her palms were wrapped in linen again—Ilsa's doing, after the Reach, after the flare that had painted us against the sky for every hunter within fifty miles—but I could feel the heat of her even through the cloth, standing next to her. Like holding your hands near a hearth you couldn't bring yourself to leave.

"Vesper," she said. "What do we do?"

What we did was what I always did. I did the arithmetic.

Twelve hunters, maybe more. One disgraced cartographer who hadn't held a sword since her trial. One priest whose god was dead and whose knees weren't far behind. One child made of fire. And me—an oath-breaker with a dormant gift I'd spent thirty years strangling in its sleep, carrying a spark inside me that apparently used to be a god.

The arithmetic was ugly. But ugly arithmetic has always been my native language.

* * *

We watched them for most of the day from the rocks above, and by late afternoon I knew three things.

First: the white pavilion belonged to someone important. Servants came and went; nobody else entered without ceremony.

Second: they had dogs. Not hunting dogs—*finding* dogs, big pale things that circled the perimeter in slow patterns, and every time they passed downwind of our hiding place their hackles rose and their handlers murmured. They could smell the gift. They could smell *her*.

Third: there was a pyre already built in the courtyard, stacked neat and patient, waiting for wood that hadn't arrived yet.

"Ilsa," I said, "how fast can you get us off this ridge and around their eastern flank?"

"Not fast enough," she said. "Not with Anselm's hip, not with the girl's hands, not with those dogs. Vesper, if they catch her scent properly, we're done. All of us."

"So we don't all go together."

Anselm turned his head slowly, the way old men turn when they already know what's coming. "No," he said.

"Somebody has to give them something to look at," I said. "Something worth looking at. While the rest of you cut east through the gully and make for the door."

"You are not volunteering yourself," Ilsa said flatly. "You are the worst possible volunteer. You have nothing they want."

That was the moment I decided not to lie to them. It felt strange in my mouth, honesty—like a sword hilt I'd never once gripped correctly.

"I have exactly one thing they want," I said. "I just haven't told you what it is."

Mara looked up at me. Anselm closed his eyes. Ilsa's jaw tightened, because she was smart, and smart people hate the pause before bad news.

"The oath I broke," I said. "When I cut the cord. You remember I told you the god didn't answer anymore. That it fell silent generations ago." I took a breath. "Anselm knows. He figured it out on the Reach, or he knew before. The god wasn't silent. The god was *eaten*. And what was left of it—the last spark, the last ember of something that used to be a power in this world—it went into the oath itself. Into the ones who swore. Into me."

The wind moved through the ruined grass below. Somewhere in the camp, a dog began to bark, then thought better of it.

"The crown isn't harvesting gift-bearers for pyres," I said. "The pyre is theater. They're taking the gifted somewhere, draining them, feeding the magic into a dead relic—and the relic is hungry for exactly one flavor of power. Old power. Divine dregs. Me."

"And you mean to hand yourself over," Anselm said. It wasn't a question. His voice had gone very quiet, the way it did when he quoted scripture he no longer believed in.

"I mean to *bargain*. There's a difference. Bargaining is just lying with paperwork."

"Vesper—" Mara started.

"Mara." I crouched so we were eye to eye. Her eyes were the color of coals under ash, and I hated how much I'd come to love looking into them. "Listen to me. You carry the last dragon-fire in the world. Do you understand what that means? Everything else burned out. Everything else got taken, or starved, or killed. You are the only ember left, and embers don't get second chances. So you're going to walk east through that gully with Ilsa and Anselm, and you're going to keep your hands wrapped, and you're going to reach that door, and you are going to live. That's the whole mission. Living."

"That's not fair," she whispered.

"No," I agreed. "It isn't. Fairness is for people with better arithmetic."

She threw her arms around my neck then, and I let her, and I will tell you honestly that I stood there in the cold wind of a country that had been erased and let a child set fire to whatever was left of my heart, because there was no one left to see it and no reason left to pretend.

Then I stood up, and I became a mercenary again, because that was the shape I knew best.

* * *

I walked down into the camp at dusk with my hands open and empty, which is how you teach soldiers not to shoot you. A sentry shouted. Dogs strained at their leads, whining—they could smell me now, smell what slept in me, and they didn't like it any better than I did.

The white pavilion smelled of ink and lilies. The man inside sat behind a folding desk, writing, and did not look up until I had stood there long enough to be rude.

Inquisitor Serane. I'd heard the name whispered even in the valley, where news came to die. A narrow face, gray eyes like ledger columns, hands folded with terrible patience.

"Vesper Crane," she said. "Oath-sworn of the Silent Order. We've been reading your file for eleven years. It's very thin. You hid well."

"Hiding was my best skill."

"And yet here you are. Walking into my camp. Carrying—" her nostrils flared, delicately, "—quite a lot. More than your file suggested. Where is the child?"

"Gone. Miles gone, by now, with two companions who know the way better than you ever will."

Something flickered across her face—not anger. Interest. "You're offering yourself in exchange for their escape."

"I'm offering myself in exchange for their *continued* escape. There's a difference. Send riders after them and I'll burn out my own spark before your relic gets a taste. Dead power feeds nothing. You know that. It's why you need me breathing."

Silence. Then she smiled, thin as a paper cut. "You've done this before."

"Once or twice. Usually for money. This time I want terms."

"Name them."

"The girl is struck from every list. Every registry, every warrant. She doesn't exist. The cartographer's pardon stands—full, public, her maps returned. The priest is free to pray to whatever corpse he likes. And none of you follow them past the border stones. Ever."

Serane leaned back. "And in return?"

"In return you get the last living piece of a dead god, delivered willingly, which I'm told burns brighter than anything you can squeeze out of some terrified miller's daughter."

She studied me for a long moment, and I understood that I was being read the way Ilsa read terrain—for routes, for weaknesses, for the places where the ground might give.

"You cut your oath-cord," she said. "Why would the spark still live in an oath-breaker?"

"Because oaths are like scars," I said. "Cutting them doesn't undo them. It just changes what they look like."

And beneath my breastbone, in the place where the spark slept, something stirred. Not warmth. Something older than warmth—a pressure, a presence, like a hand closing around mine from the inside. And in the silence of my own skull, faint as a voice through a wall, I heard it. For the first time since the valley. Since childhood.

*Renew,* it whispered. *Swear again. Live. Serve. It is not too late.*

The ghost of my god, whispering surrender dressed up as salvation. Thirty years of silence, and it chose *this* moment to find its voice—here, in the enemy's tent, while its last vessel sold itself.

*I know what you are,* I told it silently. *You're the leash remembering it was a leash.*

It didn't answer. But it didn't stop pressing, either, soft and patient as snowfall.

"Terms accepted," Serane said, and drew a document toward her, and I signed my name for the second-worst reason of my life.

* * *

They took my weapons, naturally, and put me in a wagon cell with iron bars that hummed faintly against my skin. Through the gaps I watched the camp settle into night. Watched the pyre stand dark and patient in the courtyard.

And then I saw the thing I will carry longer than any scar.

Torchlight at the eastern gully. Shouting. Dogs loosed, streaming into the dark with their handlers cursing behind them. And rising above the ridgeline, brief and terrible, a bloom of orange light—fire where no fire should be, shaped like a girl's desperation.

They'd been closer than we thought. Slower than we hoped. The gully had not saved them.

Ilsa was dragged into camp first, wrists bound, her satchel torn open, and I watched a lieutenant feed her forbidden maps page by page into a brazier while she screamed words at him that would have gotten her excommunicated from three faiths. Half the atlas went up before an officer thought to save the rest—half a map of a country that no longer existed, curling into black butterflies.

Anselm they threw down beside her, bleeding at the temple, praying anyway, because that was the whole of him.

And Mara—

Mara they put in a cage wagon opposite mine. Small. Wrapped hands pressed to the bars. Coals-under-ash eyes finding mine through the dark, wide with a betrayal she was too young and too fierce to name.

"You said *live*," she said.

"I know," I said.

"You *lied*."

"I know," I said, and my voice cracked on it, and I didn't fix it, because some things shouldn't be fixed.

Across the courtyard, Inquisitor Serane emerged from her pavilion and regarded the night's harvest with her ledger-column eyes. Three gifts. A cartographer. Half a map. And the door still waiting beyond the border stones, unguarded now, unclaimed.

She caught me watching and smiled her paper-cut smile.

"Don't despair, Master Crane," she called, pleasant as a hostess. "The relic hungers. And tomorrow, you'll finally learn what your god tasted like."

In the cage, Mara's fire breathed against the bars—small, furious, alive.

And inside me, patient as snowfall, the dead god whispered: *Renew.*
