## 9. The Last Door

The door was smaller than I had imagined.

I'd spent the whole crossing picturing something grand—a gate of black iron taller than a cathedral, wreathed in the ghosts of a country that no longer existed. What we found instead, at the heart of the border ruins where Ilsa's half-burned map said *here* and nowhere else in the world agreed, was a slab of grey stone set into a hillside. Man-high. Unremarkable. The kind of door you'd walk past a thousand times without seeing.

Except that it hummed.

I felt it in my teeth before I heard it with my ears, a low vibration like a struck bell that never quite finished ringing. The fire in my palms woke and pressed against my skin, eager, like a dog smelling home.

"It's real," Anselm whispered. He was leaning on Vesper's arm, still grey-faced from the crossing, from the fever the Reach had tried to drown him in before my fire burned it back. His voice cracked on the second word. "After all these years. It's real."

Ilsa didn't say anything. She just stood there with her ruined map held in both hands like a relic, looking at the door the way you look at someone you buried coming back up the road. Her eyes were wet. She'd deny it later. She always did.

Vesper said nothing at all.

That should have been my first warning. Vesper always had words—dry ones, wry ones, the kind that made the dark feel less dark. But they stood apart from us, at the edge of the ruin, and their hand kept going to their chest, to the place where the oath-cord used to sit against their skin before they cut it in the valley. They weren't touching the cord anymore. There was nothing there to touch. They were touching what lived underneath it.

I knew about that thing underneath. I'd known since Brother Anselm told us by the fire, since he said the words *devoured* and *last spark* and *sleeps inside them*. Since then, sometimes at night, when I dreamed too close to Vesper, I dreamed of a small grey light guttering in a ribcage like a lamp in an abandoned chapel.

I hadn't understood, until this moment, what the light was for.

* * *

"The door doesn't open for fire alone," Ilsa said. She'd spread what remained of her map on a fallen lintel, weighting the corners with stones. The burn across its lower half had eaten the borderlands whole; only the door itself survived, drawn in her precise, defiant hand. "I found the records before they took me. The erased country sealed itself with a lock keyed to two things. A flame to wake it—" she glanced at me, apologetic, as if my existence were an inconvenience she regretted— "and a god's consent to permit it."

"Consent," Anselm repeated softly.

"The old rites are explicit. No door of the Nameless Land opens while its guardian god lives silent. Either the god speaks—or the god dies, and the flame speaks in its place."

The wind moved through the ruins, cold and smelling of ash that had been ash for centuries. Somewhere behind us, far down the valley of the unmapped reach, I could still hear it—that faint wrongness in the air that meant witch-hunters, that meant the inquisitor, that meant the sky might split open at any moment with wings and fire and the crown's long reach.

"And if the god speaks," Vesper said. Their voice was flat. Careful. The voice of someone reading terms aloud before signing. "How does a dead god speak?"

Anselm looked at them for a long time. Then he looked away, which was worse.

"You know how," he said. "You've always known. That's why you kept the shrine. That's why you maintained the rites even after the company stopped believing, even after the god went quiet. You were keeping the vessel ready."

"No," Vesper said.

"The spark sleeps inside you because the oath made you its lantern. If you feed it—if you give it everything, all of you, the way the first oath-bearers gave themselves—it wakes. It speaks. It consents." Anselm's hands were shaking. "And then it goes out again, Vesper. Gods don't survive being lit twice. Not gods that were devoured. The spark is the last of it. When it burns, it burns *once*."

The silence that followed was the worst silence I have ever stood inside. Worse than the pyre square. Worse than the night the company voted. Because this silence had a shape, and the shape was Vesper, standing very still, already deciding.

"No," Vesper said again, but softer, and softness in Vesper's mouth had always meant surrender.

* * *

I want to tell you I argued brilliantly. That I found the perfect words, the ones that mattered, the ones that changed everything.

I didn't. I said, "Don't."

Vesper turned and looked at me, and their face did the thing it did—the thing where all the wry armor slid off at once and left just a person, tired and scarred and older than their years. "Mara."

"Don't." My voice cracked. The fire in my palms flared and I clenched my fists to smother it, and the heat crawled up my wrists anyway, angry, wanting out. "You're not doing that. You're not trading yourself for me. That's not—I didn't ask for that."

"You didn't ask for any of this." Vesper came closer. This close, I could see the grey light under their skin, faint as moonlight through smoke. It had been getting brighter ever since we reached the ruins. It knew where it was. It wanted to go home. "Nobody asks. That's the whole rotten joke of inheritance, Mara. You get handed a thing before you're tall enough to carry it, and everyone calls it duty so you won't notice it's a cage."

"Then break the cage," I said. "Break it. You already cut the cord. You already chose—"

"I chose to run." Vesper smiled, and it wasn't a happy smile, but it was honest. "Running isn't choosing. Running is just dying slowly somewhere else. This—" they gestured at the door, at the humming stone, at all of it— "this is the first real choice the oath ever offered me. Feed the god or fail it. Light the lantern or let it gutter. Those were always the options. Nobody ever wrote down a third one."

"There's a third one," Ilsa said quietly.

We all turned. She was standing over her map, and there was something in her face I'd learned to read on the crossing—the look she got when the world disagreed with her maps and she decided the world was wrong.

"The rites require a god's consent," she said. "They do not specify *which* god. Or—" her eyes came to me, and I saw it land in her, the thought arriving whole, terrible and bright— "or *what*. The Nameless Land sealed itself against the crown, against the hunt, against everything that burns countries off maps. It asked for a flame that could not be harvested. A flame that chooses."

"Mara," Anselm breathed.

"She's not a vessel of dragon-fire," Ilsa said. "She's the last of it. Living, willing, *herself*. If she opens the door—not as fuel, not as a sacrifice, but as the one who decides—it may answer. It may not need a god at all. It may need exactly what the old god can never be again: something alive."

"But the spark," Anselm said. "If the door opens without the god's consent, the spark dies anyway. Unfed, unchosen, it gutters out. Vesper would lose—"

"Everything I inherited," Vesper finished. Something moved through their face—grief, and relief, and grief again, tangled together like roots. "Everything that was handed to me before I could carry it."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being nine years old and carrying the last fire of the dragons: everyone assumes the choice will be taken from you. The company assumed it when they voted. The crown assumes it every time it builds a pyre. Even the people who love you assume it, in their kinder ways—they assume you'll be protected, carried, decided-for, right up until the moment you're spent.

So when Vesper knelt down in the ash in front of me, eye to eye, and said, "It has to be your choice, Mara. Not mine. Not anyone's. Yours,"—it was the first time in my whole life anyone had handed me a decision instead of a sentence.

"If you open that door," Vesper said, "and it works, the spark inside me dies. The last of the god that kept me alive through every bad year of my life—it ends, and I end with it, in the way that matters. Everything I was. Everything I was supposed to be. And if it doesn't work—" their mouth twisted— "then we're standing in front of a locked door with hunters at our backs, and I've killed my god for nothing."

"And if you feed it instead?" I asked.

"Then the door opens, and you walk through into a new country, and I'm not there to see it."

The fire in my palms was very quiet. Listening.

I thought about the shrine in the valley, and how Vesper had tended it alone for years, sweeping ash that never answered. I thought about the cord, and the cut, and the blood. I thought about how a person can hold a thing their whole life without knowing whether it's a gift or a wound, and how maybe it's neither—maybe it's just a hand, waiting to see what you put in it.

"What happens to you," I asked, "if the spark dies?"

Vesper considered the question honestly, the way they considered everything. "Lighter," they said finally. "Emptier. Alive, probably. Just—for the first time since I was younger than you—not owned."

I looked at the door. It hummed. Behind us, far off, the sky was starting to bruise with the crown's approach.

"Okay," I said.

"Mara—"

"*Okay.*" I opened my hands.

* * *

The fire came out gently. That surprised me most of all—all my life it had been panic and burning and the smell of my own village—but now it rose off my palms like breath in winter, gold-edged and calm, and drifted toward the door the way a bird comes home to a roost.

The stone drank it.

Light spread across the grey surface like dawn moving over water, and lines appeared—old lines, carved lines, a script that hurt to look at because it was written in the grammar of a country that had chosen to be forgotten. The humming deepened into something almost like speech. Almost like a question.

*Who opens?*

"Me," I said. My voice shook and I let it. "Mara. Of the fire. Of what's left of it. I choose this door. I choose what's behind it. Nobody sent me. Nobody spent me. I'm here because I decide to be."

The light pulsed. Waited.

And beneath my feet, beneath the ruins, beneath the whole erased world, something vast and patient turned over in its sleep—and answered not with thunder but with warmth, with the feeling of a hearth in a house you've never seen but somehow remember.

The door swung inward.

Beyond it: green hills. A river. Smoke rising from chimneys in a valley where people were living, actually living, in a country with no name. Air rolled out carrying grass and woodsmoke and rain, and it hit my face like forgiveness.

Behind me, Vesper made a sound.

I turned. The grey light under their skin was fading—not violently, not in agony, but the way a lamp goes out when morning comes. Fading, and Vesper watched it go with tears running freely down their face, and they were smiling.

"Goodbye, old man," they whispered to whatever it had been. "Thank you for the years. I'm sorry about the rest."

Then the light went out entirely, and Vesper swayed, and I caught them—caught all of them, all that weight, all those years—and for a moment we just stood there, the mercenary and the fire-child, holding each other up in the doorway of a new world.

"Come on," I said, my voice thick. "Found family carries both ways."

Vesper laughed—wet, broken, free. "Insolent child."

"Yours," I said. "If you want."

Behind us, the bruise in the sky broke open, and the first of the hunters' fires bloomed on the horizon. Ilsa was already hauling Anselm toward the light. Vesper took my hand.

Together, we stepped through—and the door began, slowly, gratefully, to close.
