## 10. A Country With No Name

The door closed behind us with a sound like a book shutting after a very long read.

I stood on the far side of it for a while, which is to say I stood in a country that had been erased from every map ever drawn, and waited for the world to notice the error and correct it. It didn't. The grass stayed green. The wind stayed warm. Somewhere downslope, a river ran loud and unhurried, as if it had been running the whole time we'd all been pretending this place didn't exist.

Which, I suppose, was exactly what it had been doing.

"Say something," Mara said. She was holding my hand hard enough to grind bone, her other palm cupped against her chest where the fire lived now—dimmer than it had been at the border ruins, banked like a hearth instead of a siege engine. "You always say something. Usually something annoying."

"I'm composing," I said. "It's not every day a man walks into a country that isn't there."

"It is there."

"I know. That's the annoying part. Everyone who told us otherwise—the maps, the crown, the whole weight of recorded history—they were all just wrong. Do you know how much paperwork that invalidates? Somewhere there's a royal archivist having a stroke right now and he doesn't even know why."

She laughed. It came out cracked and wet, because she was crying, and she was eleven, and she'd earned both. I let her grind my hand another minute before I pulled her into a hug, because I am a mercenary of twenty years' standing and I have a reputation to maintain, and also because she needed it more than my reputation did.

Behind us, the door stood in its frame of black stone, and it was, I noticed, only a door. No glow, no hum, no whisper of the silent god leaking through the seams. Whatever had powered it, whatever spark Anselm said slept inside my broken oath—it was quiet now. Spent, or sleeping, or finally, blessedly, done with me.

I didn't look at it too long. Some doors you thank by walking away from them.

* * *

We found the first village three days in, following the river down.

Ilsa had warned us about this—or rather, she'd theorized about it, at length, with diagrams scratched in dirt, because Ilsa Vane does not experience wonder like a normal person; she experiences it like an auditor. "A country erased from the maps doesn't stop existing," she'd said, back when we were starving in the Unmapped Reach and she was still calling her own cartography a sickness. "It stops being *administered*. No census. No garrisons. No tax rolls. Whatever's in here has been governing itself for however long the crown pretended it away."

What was in here, it turned out, was people. Ordinary ones. Farmers, mostly, and smiths, and a woman who ran a ferry across the river and charged us two coppers apiece and didn't blink at the burned child or the disgraced priest or the mercenary with a severed oath-cord still knotted around his wrist like a bad tattoo.

"They'll want to know where we're from," Anselm murmured as we crossed.

"We're from nowhere," I said. "It's a big country. Lots of room."

The ferrywoman looked at me the way old soldiers look at young ones wearing their old wars on their faces, and said, "Nowhere's got good soil this time of year," and that was that. First rule of an unmapped land: nobody asks questions they don't already know the answers to, and everybody knows the answers anyway.

Mara's fire behaved itself around these people. That was the thing I kept watching for, the thing I'd been watching for since the border ruins, since the night she lit the sky to save Anselm and painted a target on all our backs. Around strangers, around the ferry, around the innkeeper's children who adopted her within an hour of arrival the way children do—her flame sat low and golden in her palms, warm enough to toast bread, gentle enough to hold a moth without scorching its wings.

"You're doing that on purpose," I told her that night, on the inn's porch, while she practiced making the fire do small stupid tricks—a flower, a bird, once, disastrously, a fish.

"I'm not doing anything," she said. "That's the point. Back home—" she meant the valley, meant the company, meant everything we'd cut loose, "—it was always either hiding or fighting. There wasn't a middle. Here it's just... mine. It can be small if I want it small."

I thought about my own gift, the one I'd buried under twenty years of oath and ash and the valley's dead air. The one that flickered awake beside her and then, somewhere past the door, went quiet again—not gone, I could feel it, but resting, like a dog that had finally been let off duty. Inherited things, oaths and gifts and grudges. They sit in you like stones. You spend your whole life deciding whether to carry them or set them down.

"Small is underrated," I said.

* * *

Ilsa rode out on the fourth week.

Not rode—was delivered, which is a different verb entirely and one I intend to use correctly. A crown courier came up the river road with a sealed writ and a face like a man delivering a bill he personally found offensive, and handed over a document releasing one Ilsa Vane, former Royal Cartographer, from custody upon payment of a ransom sum that made me glad we hadn't asked what it was until after we'd paid it.

"How," I said, counting out coin we'd earned ferrying cargo and mending roofs and doing the assorted honest work of men with no history, "did the crown even find us?"

"The crown didn't," Ilsa said, shouldering her pack, which contained, I would later confirm, forty pounds of paper and nothing else. "The inquisitor did. The one at the border ruins. He let me go."

"He *let* you—"

"He ransomed himself, technically. Filed it as an exchange of assets." She said it flatly, but her hands were doing something complicated with a strap that didn't need doing. "He wanted to know if the door opened. If the country was real. I told him yes."

"And he just... accepted that?"

"He wrote it down," Ilsa said. "In his report. Sealed it. Filed it in the archive under a designation no one will ever retrieve." She looked at me then, and behind the auditor's face there was something older and tireder. "Vesper. He spent his whole life burning magic for a relic that never lit. You showed him a door that actually opens. What do you think a man like him does with that?"

I thought about the inquisitor's face in the last moment before I barged into his crossbows, the look of a man who had been promised a resurrection and given a ledger instead. I thought: he goes home. He sits in his office. And every night for the rest of his life he looks at that sealed report and knows the truth is sitting in a drawer, alive, and he chose to leave it alive.

"Some men you beat," I said. "Some men you just hand a better story."

"Put that on my headstone," Ilsa said, and then, because sentiment physically pained her, "Also put the map on my headstone. The corrected one. I've started redrawing the region. All of it. Every river, every village, every hill your so-called erased country has been quietly maintaining without imperial supervision. It'll take years."

"You could stay here and do it," Mara said, appearing at the porch rail with the terrible timing of children and cats. "Map it properly. From inside."

Ilsa went very still, the way she did whenever someone offered her the exact thing she wanted, as though accepting it might be a clerical error. Then she said, "Someone will have to," and started unpacking her paper, and that was that settled.

* * *

Anselm built his shrine in the spring.

He built it small, which surprised everyone including Anselm, who had spent thirty years tending a god's house and had strong opinions about scale. One room, stone walls, a slate roof, a door that faced east. No altar. No idol. No name carved above the lintel.

"People will ask whose shrine it is," I said, hauling stones for him because apparently I'd become a man who hauled stones, and worse, didn't hate it.

"Then they'll have to answer it themselves," he said. He set the last stone with hands that shook—not from age, or not only from age, but from the particular tremor of a man building something he'd been told his whole life was impossible. "The god is dead, Vesper. Devoured, eaten, ended—I've made my peace with the word. But I watched a man break a thousand-year oath to save a child, and I watched the child carry the fire gently afterward, and I have decided that whatever holiness exists in this world does not come down from above. It gets built. Stone by stone. Choice by choice." He wiped his mortar-covered hands on his robe. "So. A shrine without a god. Just a roof, and a door, and whoever needs it."

"And you'll preach?"

"I'll listen," he said. "Mostly. Occasionally I'll fail and give a sermon. I'm only human."

The valley folk came, eventually. Word travels slowly in an unmapped country, but it travels. They came with sick children and dead husbands and questions too heavy to carry alone, and Anselm listened, and the shrine filled up the way a hearth fills—with ordinary warmth, no miracle required.

I went sometimes. Not to pray. I'd done my praying, decades of it, into a silence that turned out to be a grave. I went because Anselm kept a bench by the door and never asked me anything, and because some evenings a man wants to sit in a holy building and feel absolutely nothing, and have that be enough.

* * *

Mara stopped flinching at her own hands in the second year.

I mark it because I remember the first winter, when she'd wake screaming from dreams of the pyres, of witch-hunters, of the company captain's face the morning we ran—and the fire would leap up wild off her skin before she was fully awake, and she'd have to smother it in the water basin, shaking, apologizing to nobody, to the dark.

By the second spring she was teaching the innkeeper's daughters to bake with it. Held low, held slow, bread rising golden in palms that had once lit the sky like a war banner. The girls called her the Ember Lady, which she pretended to hate and visibly did not.

"You know what I figured out?" she said to me one evening, flour to her elbows, fire cradled easy as a kitten. "Everyone kept telling me what the fire was *for*. The crown wanted it for their relic. The company wanted it for the bounty. Even the dragon-fire itself, maybe—it wanted burning, wanted big, wanted sky." She dusted her hands. "Nobody ever asked it if it wanted to be a hearth."

"And does it?"

"It's a fire," she said, with the magnificent condescension of the young. "It wants what you feed it. Same as everything."

I looked at her—at this child I'd stolen out of a vote and carried across an erased country, this last vessel of a dead age who had decided, at eleven-going-on-thirteen, to be a baker's warmth instead of a weapon—and I felt the old oath stir somewhere under my ribs, faint as a scar aching before rain. Not calling. Just remembering. Oaths are like that. They don't die when you break them; they just become history, and history is only heavy if you insist on carrying it.

I set it down. Again. It gets easier. Nobody tells you that part—that betrayal and liberation are the same door, and once you walk through, you get to keep walking.

* * *

We built the house in the third autumn, on the river bend where the ferrywoman said the light came in best at evening. I say "we" generously. Ilsa surveyed it. Anselm blessed it, godlessly. Mara fired the bricks herself, carefully, proudly, and only exploded four of them. I did the carpentry badly and the arguing well, and between the five of us—six, counting the ferrywoman, who appointed herself foreman and tolerated no dissent—we raised walls and a roof and a chimney, and Mara lit the first fire in the hearth with one cupped palm, small and gold and entirely hers.

No one named the house. No one named the road it sat on, or the village upriver, or the country around it all. Names were the crown's business, and the crown was on the far side of a shut door, filing reports no one would read.

But some evenings, when the light came in the way the ferrywoman promised, and Ilsa spread her half-finished maps across the table, and Anselm argued theology with a farmer over Anselm's own excellent wine, and Mara's hearth-fire ticked and settled like a sleeping animal—I thought that if anyone ever did draw this place, they'd need a name for it eventually.

And I thought I knew what I'd write in the margin, small, in pencil, the way cartographers note the things that aren't official yet:

*Here lives a family. None of them inherited it. All of them built it.*

It wasn't a country with no name, in the end.

It was a country with a hundred names, one per person, spelled differently in every mouth—and for the first time in my long, armed, oath-bound life, mine fit.
