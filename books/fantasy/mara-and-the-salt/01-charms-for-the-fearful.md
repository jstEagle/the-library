## 1. Charms for the Fearful

The trick to selling magic in a city where magic is illegal is to make it look like nothing at all.

I learned that lesson the hard way, back when I still had an army behind me and thought that counted as protection. Now I sell luck-knots from a folding table in the fish market of Brasse-les-Salines, and every knot is just cord and salt and patience — ask anyone. Ask the magistrates. Ask the crows on the warehouse roofs who have watched me for twenty years and never once seen me do anything worth burning for.

The crows know better, of course. Crows always do. But crows don't testify.

* * *

Market day began the way it always begins: with herring, and with men lying about herring. Brasse-les-Salines smells like money that has been left out too long — brine and tar and the sweet rot of fish guts — and by midmorning the salt flats beyond the seawall had already begun their daily work of bleaching the sky white. The city sits on the flats like a bone someone forgot to bury. Everyone here is pale in the same way. Even the children have gray eyes, or close enough.

I set up my table between a woman who sold rope and a man who sold nothing but opinions, which in this city is a licensed trade. My stock was modest: eleven luck-knots, three "soothing" sachets of salt and lavender for bad dreams, a jar of what I called blessed foot-powder and what was actually just salt I'd talked to for a while. Small things. Deniable things. If a magistrate seized my entire inventory, he would find cord, herbs, and seasoning.

What he wouldn't find was the work underneath.

Here's how you make a luck-knot without technically making one. You take your cord, and while your hands tie it — and tying is all craft, all honest labor, no law against knots — you let something else settle into it. Not a spell. Spells leave residue; Inquisitors can smell residue like dogs smell fear. What I do is closer to... leaning. You lean your attention against the cord the way you'd lean against a doorframe, casual, like you're only resting there. And whatever is left of the old listening — the thing I used to call prayer, before prayer became a word for kneeling in mud waiting for a god who had stopped answering — whatever is left of that settles into the fibers and stays.

Does it work? Sometimes. That's the beauty of luck: nobody can prove a negative. A fisherman buys a knot, comes home dry through a season of storms, and credits the knot. Another drowns anyway, and his widow doesn't come demanding refunds, because everyone knows luck is luck. It is the perfect crime. I sell hope with plausible deniability built into the stitching, and I have kept myself fed and invisible for two decades doing it.

Twenty years. Say it fast and it sounds like nothing. Say it slow and you can hear every dawn I woke with my hand already curled around a knife that wasn't under the pillow anymore, because I'd made myself stop sleeping armed, because unarmed women don't get reported.

* * *

He came to my table an hour past noon, when the market was at its loudest and therefore at its safest. Loud markets are good cover. Nobody remembers faces in a crowd that smells like herring.

He was a fisherman — obviously, they're all fishermen here — young, maybe twenty-five, with the sun-scalded neck and the particular way of standing that men have when the sea has recently tried to kill them and they've agreed to pretend it didn't. His hands were shaking. Not sea-shaking. Land-shaking. Fear-shaking.

"Madame." He didn't look at my face. Smart. Looking at a charm-seller's face is how you end up described in someone's report. "They say you sell knots."

"They say a lot of things about a lot of people." I kept sorting cord while I talked, giving him something boring to watch. "Most of them are flattery."

"My brother went out Tuesday." His voice dropped to almost nothing. "The *Maren-Jeanne*. Four men. Only two came back."

"That's the sea," I said. "The sea does that."

"The sea did that." He finally looked up, and I saw it in him then, plain as a brand: not grief. Grief he could carry. This was the other thing, the worse thing. "The water went wrong, madame. Old Tomas — the one who drowned — he said the water went *quiet*. Like it was holding its breath. And when it started again, the current had turned, and we lost him over the side, and—" He stopped. Swallowed. "My mother says you can make a knot that keeps a man on a boat."

"Your mother says a lot of things too, apparently."

"Please."

There it was. The whole word, carrying everything. I've heard *please* said a thousand ways in my life — begged, commanded, sobbed, screamed across battlefields — and I will tell you the truth about it: it never gets easier to refuse, and it should never get easy to grant. Both of those facts are traps.

I looked at his shaking hands. I looked past him, at the market, doing the arithmetic I always do. Rope-woman, watching her stock, not us. Opinion-man, watching everything, always. Two city guards at the fountain, bored, facing away. No gray coats. No Inquisition black. Just a frightened boy whose mother had heard rumors about a stall that sold more than cord.

One knot. One small, deniable leaning of attention. Twenty years of practice compressed into thirty seconds of work that would look, to any observer, like a woman tying string.

"Sit down," I said, "before you fall down. You look like a man the sea already got."

He sat on the crate I keep for exactly this purpose. I took a length of cord — undyed, ordinary, bought in bulk from rope-woman herself so that even my materials had witnesses — and I began to tie.

And here is the part I have never told anyone, the part I am telling you now because a book is the only confessor I have left: while my hands worked, I listened. Not with ears. With the place behind my sternum where the oath used to live, the hollow that twenty years hasn't filled so much as furnished. I leaned against the world the way I used to lean against Veyl, and I asked — quietly, deniably, the way you ask a stranger for directions — *keep this one. Keep this one, whoever's minding the store.*

Nothing answered. Nothing ever answers. But sometimes — and this is the heresy I live inside, the small treason I commit daily — sometimes the silence itself seems to lean back. Like the pause before a door opens. Like held breath.

The water went quiet, the boy said. Holding its breath.

I tied the knot. Nine crossings, salt worked into the third and seventh, and under it all, the leaning, the asking, the old muscle memory of a battle-sworn woman doing the only magic she has left, which is the magic of hoping very hard in a specific direction.

"For your brother," I said, and put it in his palm, and closed his fingers over it. "Cord and salt. Costs four sous."

"Mother said—"

"Your mother said four sous, or she's been talking to opinion-man, who charges eight." I took his coins. "Tell your brother to wear it under his shirt where no one sees it. Not because it's anything. Because men who show off charms get talked about, and men who get talked about get visited, and you don't want visits. Trust me. Visits are terrible."

He almost smiled. Then he didn't, because he remembered his brother was dead, and I watched the smile die and felt the usual thing I feel, which is nothing I'm allowed to name.

"Thank you, madame."

"Don't thank me. Thank the cord." I waved him off. "Go. Buy your mother flowers on the way home, she clearly needs the credit."

He went. I watched him go, the way I watch everyone go, checking his gait, checking who checked him. Nobody did. He dissolved into the crowd with his little knot of borrowed hope pressed against his chest, and I turned back to my table and my eleven remaining knots and my long, gray, careful afternoon.

That's the job. That's the whole job. I make peace in nine crossings of cord, and I charge four sous, and I tell myself it's enough.

Some days I almost believe it.

* * *

You want to know about the god. Everyone eventually wants to know about the god, though in this city nobody asks aloud, because asking about Veyl is like asking about a relative who disgraced the family — technically he exists, officially he's never mentioned, and the less said the safer for everyone's inheritance.

Veyl, the Quiet One. The Silent God. Once he had other names, back when the kingdom had a name, but names were the first casualty. He was the god of soldiers, which sounds wrong until you understand what kind: not the god of killing. The god of the moment after. The god of the held breath, the steady hand, the silence a squad keeps before the wall is breached. We swore our oaths in silence — no words, because words are noise and noise is fear wearing clothes. You knelt, and you opened yourself, and you *felt* him, vast and quiet as deep water, and you knew you were held.

Ten thousand of us felt him, once. Every dawn, every dusk, every hour of every battle. He wasn't a voice. He was a presence, and his presence was the closest thing to safety I have ever known, and I include in that category my mother's kitchen, my first lover's arms, and the specific warmth of a fire when you're the one on watch and everyone else is asleep.

Then, one morning, mid-prayer, mid-*breath*, he was gone. Not faded. Gone, the way a tooth is gone — a hole with edges, aching at the weather. Ten thousand sworn soldiers knelt in the mud of a battlefield I will not name and waited for a god who had stopped existing between one heartbeat and the next, and the silence where he had been was so loud that men screamed.

The kingdom broke within the year. Kingdoms are just gods wearing geography, and ours had lost its god. Magic was outlawed — the gifted blamed, naturally, because crowds need a throat to cut and "the divine mystery" cuts poorly. The country's name was struck from maps, treaties, memory. Officially, the land I'm sitting in does not exist. We are a clerical error the size of a nation.

And I ran. I ran from the army, from the oaths, from the hole in the world, and I came to Brasse-les-Salines because salt preserves things and I wanted to be preserved, and I folded myself down small, and small, and smaller, until I was a charm-stall. Until I was cord and salt and four sous.

People say the god died. People say he abandoned us. I say neither, because I was there when the silence landed, and I'll tell you what it felt like: it didn't feel like death. Death has a direction — something goes *away*, somewhere. This felt like being swallowed. Like the presence hadn't vanished but had been *taken into* something, the way a shout is taken into a closed mouth.

But that's madness talk, and madness gets you registered, and registration gets you a pyre, so: the god is dead, long live the cord, four sous please.

* * *

Evening came down on the market like a lid. I packed my unsold knots into their box — seven left, a slow day, the fear-economy fluctuating — and paid rope-woman for next week's cord, and endured opinion-man's theory about why the herring run had shifted north (taxes, according to him; everything is taxes if you've never been north). The guards changed shift at the fountain. The crows went wherever crows go to complain about us.

I was folding my table when the crier came through.

You hear criers often in Brasse-les-Salines — tide schedules, grain prices, the occasional execution, delivered in that flat official bellow that makes even joy sound like a summons. This one pushed through the thinning crowd with a lantern and a scroll, and I did what I always do when officials appear, which is become furniture. I bent over my table. I fumbled with a strap. Furniture doesn't listen.

But of course I listened.

"By order of the Crown," the crier read, "and the Office of the Inquisition: be it known that the harboring, concealment, or exercise of forbidden arts is henceforth subject to inquiry throughout the realm—"

*Realm.* Interesting word for a country that doesn't exist.

"—all persons possessing or suspected of possessing such arts shall present themselves for registration within fourteen days, at the Salt Court, where their cases shall be weighed. Those who register may expect mercy proportionate to their honesty. Those who are discovered unregistered shall expect—"

He paused. Criers love that pause. It's the theatrical high point of their entire career.

"—the pyre."

The market went quiet in that special way markets do, a hundred conversations dying at once and pretending they were never alive. Somewhere a gull laughed, because gulls have never once understood the room.

Registration. Mercy proportionate to honesty. I stood there with my strap half-buckled and did the oldest arithmetic I know: *am I seen? Am I known? Am I safe?*

Fourteen days. The Salt Court. I have spent twenty years building a life out of deniability — knots that are just knots, salt that is just salt, a woman who is just tired — and some cold practical part of me started calculating odds, escape routes, the weight of coin I could carry, whether rope-woman would talk, whether anyone had ever truly watched me lean.

And another part of me — the part behind the sternum, the furnished hollow, the ten-thousand-strong kneeling ghost I carry everywhere — that part heard *the pyre* and felt something I haven't felt since the morning the god went out.

Not fear.

Recognition. The way you recognize your own name called in a crowd.

Because here's the thing I've known for twenty years and told no one, the thing I fold into nine crossings of cord every time my hands tie: the silence leans back. The water holds its breath. Something is minding the store, and it is hungry, and it has been patient, and now the Crown is sweeping the country for everyone like me — everyone who ever felt that lean — gathering us up, registering us, weighing us.

Almost like something wants a full count.

I buckled the strap. I picked up my box of unsold luck. Around me the market resumed its noise, carefully, the way people resume breathing after a near-miss, and above the rooftops the first stars were coming out over the flats — pale, salt-white, indifferent.

Fourteen days.

I turned toward home, and I noticed — for the first time all day, though it had probably been true all day — that the air smelled wrong. Not herring, not tar, not rot.

Smoke. Faint and far off, from the direction of the warehouses by the seawall.

Someone, somewhere in Brasse-les-Salines tonight, was already burning.
