## 2. The Proclamation

The criers came at dawn, which was rude of them. Dawn in Brasse-les-Salines belongs to the fishwives and the gull-flocks and the men who sweep salt dust off the quay stones before the city wakes enough to dirty it again. It does not belong to the crown. But crowns have never been much for manners.

I heard the first bell from my room above the chandler's shop on Rue des Cordes, where I had lived eleven years under the name Margot Aumont, widow, seamstress, unremarkable in every way a person can be unremarkable. I had chosen the name the way you choose a coat: plain, well-worn, unlikely to be stolen. I had chosen the room because its window faced away from the harbor, toward the warehouse district, so that no one standing in the street could see me watching them.

Twenty years of small precautions. You'd be surprised how much of a life you can build out of not being seen.

The bell rang again—three strokes, pause, three strokes—and my hands went still over the mending basket before my mind caught up to what my body already knew. That cadence. Three and three. The last time Brasse-les-Salines had heard it, I was nineteen years old and kneeling in the mud at Veyl's Ford with ten thousand other soldiers, waiting for a god who never answered.

I dressed quickly. Not in panic—I want that understood, even if only by me and this page. Panic is a luxury for people whose names are real. I dressed the way I had practiced dressing for two decades: wool the color of ash, no jewelry, hair pinned under a kerchief like any tired woman going out early to haggle for herring. Then I went down the stairs past the chandler's sleeping dog and out into a morning that smelled of brine and smoke, and I followed the bell.

* * *

There were four criers that day, one posted at each corner of the Marché-au-Sel, and they wore the crown's livery—grey and gold, the colors of a kingdom that had spent twenty years pretending it still had a god's blessing to spend. The nearest one stood on the steps of the tithe-house with a scroll he clearly could not read himself, bellowing the words some clerk had fed him, words polished smooth as sea glass by a hundred drafts of legal ink.

"By order of His Majesty Aldric the Second, of that name, protector of the marches and keeper of the faith—" the usual litany, the usual flattery packed around nothing "—let it be known that the practice of unsanctioned arts is hereby declared treason against the realm and blasphemy against the silent throne."

Unsanctioned arts. There was a phrase with teeth in it. For as long as I'd lived in this city, magic had been illegal the way jaywalking is illegal—a law everyone knew existed and no one expected enforced, because enforcing it would mean admitting the gifted existed, and admitting *that* would mean admitting the silence had left something behind when it fell. Better to pretend the old blood had all run dry. Better to let women like me sell luck-knots to fishermen and call it superstition.

"Let all persons possessing such arts present themselves for registration within nine days," the crier droned on. "Those registered shall be examined by the Office of Inquiry and assigned according to their usefulness to the crown. Those who fail to register shall be taken. Those who resist shall be burned."

*Burned.* He said it the way he said every other word, flat as a ledger line, and that was the worst part. Not hatred. Arithmetic.

I stood in the crowd with my basket on my arm like any goodwife, and I listened to my death sentence read aloud in a voice bored of its own echo, and around me the market did what markets do. Some people muttered. Some people laughed—the young ones, the ones born after the silence, who thought the whole thing was theater, another tax with a different costume. An old woman near me crossed herself the old way, thumb to brow to breastbone, the way soldiers used to do before a charge, and then caught herself doing it and looked around guiltily, as if the gesture itself might be an art requiring sanction.

And some people—this is what I will remember, what I already knew I would remember when I was an old woman or a dead one—some people began, very quietly, to look at their neighbors.

Not accusingly. Not yet. Just *looking*. The way you look at a house after an earthquake, checking whether it still stands. I felt those glances pass over me and slide off, and I blessed the grey wool and the kerchief and twenty years of deliberate dullness, and I hated every one of them too, because they should not have been necessary, and they were, and they always had been.

* * *

They took Gaspard Toussaint from his boat at midmorning.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left of him. Gaspard mended nets two piers down from mine—or rather, two piers down from where I rented space under a false name to mend sails, because a widow who mends nets has no reason to know knots that hold against a storm-gale, and Gaspard had once watched me tie one and said nothing, only looked at my hands for a long moment and then gone back to his work. He knew. Of course he knew. Half the waterfront knew, in the way that poor people know things: silently, mutually, with the understanding that knowing was safe only as long as it stayed unwritten anywhere, including on faces.

Gaspard had a gift so small it was almost a joke. He could find lost things. Keys, rings, a child's wooden bird blown off a roof. He'd walk the shore with his palm open like a man carrying water, and sooner or later his fingers would close on whatever grief someone had paid him a copper to recover. Twenty years he'd done it. Twenty years of coppers, of gratitude, of children running up to him on the quay shouting *Monsieur Toussaint found it, Monsieur Toussaint found it*—and never once, in all that time, anything bigger than a lost thing. He wasn't dangerous. He wasn't powerful. He was a kind old man with salt in his beard who made sad people slightly less sad for a living.

Four soldiers took him off his own deck while his daughter screamed from the pier.

I was forty feet away, close enough to see everything and far enough to do nothing, which is the exact distance at which most of history happens to ordinary people. They didn't beat him. That surprised me, and then I understood why it shouldn't: the proclamation had come with instructions, and the instructions wanted him alive. Registration or the pyre—but first, registration. They walked him up the pier between them, and Gaspard went quietly, the way men go when they've decided that dignity is the last thing anyone can't take from them, and at the top of the pier he stopped and turned and looked back—not at his daughter, though she was screaming his name, but along the row of faces, searching.

Looking for lost things. With his gift, maybe, or without it. Looking for whoever among us would speak.

Nobody spoke. I didn't speak. I stood with my basket and my borrowed face and I let a kind old man be carried off to be *examined*, and his eyes passed over mine without stopping, because I had spent twenty years making sure they wouldn't stop, and God forgive me—God, or Veyl, or whatever is left in the spaces where gods used to be—they worked.

His daughter threw herself at the soldiers and was thrown back, and a fishwife caught her and held her, and the crowd closed around the little drama like water around a dropped stone, and by noon the pier was just a pier again, and Gaspard's boat rocked empty at its mooring, and the gulls argued over something in the shallows as if the world were a place where gulls' opinions mattered.

* * *

I did not go home. That was the first decision, and I made it before I knew I was making it, my feet taking me the long way through the tannery lanes where the stink keeps casual company away, my mind already moving through rooms I hadn't opened in years.

Here is what twenty years of caution buys you, when the axe finally falls: nothing. Less than nothing. I had told myself for two decades that invisibility was survival, that if I kept my head down and my gift smaller than small, the tide would never reach me. And it was true, right up until it wasn't. Caution is a wall built against the weather. It does nothing against the flood. The flood doesn't care how quiet you've been. The flood comes for the whole street.

Nine days. Registration or the pyre. And I knew—I knew with the cold certainty of a woman who had once knelt in mud and waited on a god's answer—what examination meant. They would not ask Gaspard to find a lost ring. They would strap him to something and hurt him until he found bigger things, until his little gift of finding was stretched into a tool for finding *people*, gifted people, hidden people, until the kindest man on the waterfront became the Inquisition's best bloodhound. Usefulness to the crown. That was the phrase. They would make his mercy useful or they would make his ashes instructive.

And me? What would they make of a battle-sworn sister of Veyl hiding in a seamstress's skin? I had killed men with prayer on my lips. My oath had been written into consecrated salt the day I took it, and there were priests—former priests, now called Inquisitors—who could read such things. If they ever got my hands in theirs, they wouldn't need torture. They'd need ten minutes and a jar of holy water.

So: nine days. Which meant, in practical terms, fewer than nine days, because the first people to flee would be the first people hunted, and the roads would thicken with soldiers by the third day, and after that—

After that, the country ended. That was the other thing everyone knew and no one said. Somewhere beyond the blight fields, past the drowned villages, there was a border, and borders meant checkpoints, and checkpoints meant names checked against lists, and my name—my true name, Marguerite Joubert, sworn of the Order of the Quiet Hand—had been on a list for twenty years. Probably at the top of it by now, dusted off and moved forward, the way old debts get moved forward when the debtor finally dies rich.

I bought things. That's what I remember of that afternoon: the strange domesticity of terror. Rope, good hemp. Dried fish wrapped in oilcloth. A flint and steel, because I'd sold mine years ago—why would a seamstress need fire on demand? A needle and heavy thread, and I let myself smile at that one, thin and bitter, because the thread was for stitching a false seam into a coat lining, and some skills you keep sharp your whole life without ever wanting to use them. A knife that cost more than I'd have paid a month ago; the cutler saw my face and didn't haggle, which told me his afternoon had been full of women with my face.

At the last stall before the tannery lane I stopped, because the stall was selling charms.

Luck-knots. Little braided cords with a bead of grey salt glass at the center, hung in rows like fish. The woman selling them couldn't have been older than nineteen, and her knots were wrong—pretty, but wrong, the crossings inverted, more decoration than intention. She'd learned the pattern from a book or a grandmother who'd half-remembered it. There was no craft in them at all, and certainly no art.

She smiled at me. "For luck, madame? Times being what they are."

Times being what they are. I looked at her bright, ignorant face and thought: in nine days, someone may drag you off your own doorstep for selling these. And I thought: buy one anyway. Buy one because if the world is going to end, let it catch you holding something a foolish brave girl made with her hands.

"One," I said, and paid her double, and she beamed, and I tucked the clumsy little knot into my pocket next to the knife, and walked home through the tannery stink with my heart going like a drum.

* * *

My room, when I finally returned to it, looked different. That's the thing no one warns you about. The room hadn't changed—I had. Every object sat where it had always sat, and every object was suddenly evidence. The mending basket: why does a seamstress earn coin inconsistent with her trade? The second blanket: why does a widow sleep alone but prepare for two? The loose floorboard under the bed, beneath which I had kept, for eleven years, a bundle that I had told myself I kept out of sentiment.

I knelt and pulled up the board.

The bundle was wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age. Inside: a soldier's kit, stripped to essentials. A whetstone. A vial of the old anointing oil, thick and dark, smelling of myrrh and something colder underneath. And folded at the bottom, soft as a shed skin, the grey sash of the Order of the Quiet Hand, embroidered at one end with a single word in the old script: *Veyl*. I had taken it off the night the god went silent, kneeling in the mud with ten thousand others, and I had carried it through twenty years of smallness because throwing it away had been impossible and keeping it had been deniable.

Neither of those things was true anymore. Deniability died this morning on the steps of the tithe-house, along with Gaspard Toussaint's quiet life and the fishwife's crossed thumb and every comfortable lie this city told itself about what the silence had left behind.

I sat back on my heels on the floorboards and let myself feel it—all of it, the whole weight, because I knew that once I started moving I would not stop to feel anything again. Fear, yes, but underneath the fear something worse and cleaner: relief. Terrible, shameful relief. Twenty years I had lived folded up inside a life too small for me, telling myself the folding was peace, and now the crown had torn the walls down and I was free to be exactly what I had always been, which was a hunted woman with a dead god's name in her pocket.

Veyl. I said it in my head, the way you test a bad tooth. Are you anywhere? Are you watching this? Your faithful are burning each other in your name, old friend, and your silence has become a doctrine, and somewhere out there men in grey and gold are writing lists. If you're listening—if there's any listening left in you—now would be the hour.

Nothing answered. Nothing has answered for twenty years. I had stopped expecting otherwise, and yet some stubborn splinter of me still tilted toward the silence like a plant toward a window, and I think that splinter is the last honest thing about me.

I repacked the bundle. Whetstone, oil, sash. Then I added the day's purchases, and the luck-knot, and the small store of coin from the tin behind the loose brick in the hearth—seventy-one crowns, the savings of a decade of careful, deniable, deliberately dull work. It would not be enough. I already knew it would not be enough, and that I would need things money couldn't buy: a road, a guide, a door.

A door.

I sat very still on the floor of my small grey life, with the bundle in my lap, and I let the thought finish forming, because it had been forming since dawn, since the bell, since the word *burned* spoken in a bored man's voice.

Odile. The hedge-witch who lived in the walls of the salt warehouses, old enough to remember the kingdom before it lost its name, cracked as riverbed clay and twice as deep. Odile, who traded in secrets the way I traded in luck-knots, and who had once—drunk on plum brandy and nostalgia, ten years ago, an evening I had filed away as drunken nonsense—spoken of a Door. The last way out of a country that no longer officially existed. A passage that opened for one thing only.

Consecrated salt.

Outside my window, the evening bells rang the hour, and beneath them, fainter, from the direction of the Marché-au-Sel, I could still hear a crier's voice wearing itself hoarse: *nine days, nine days, registration or the pyre.*

I stood up. I put on the grey wool and the kerchief, and I took the bundle, and I blew out the lamp, and I left the door of Margot Aumont's room unlocked behind me—let them search it, let them find a widow's tidy nothing—because I was done being careful, and I went down the stairs and out into the dark, toward the warehouses, toward the walls full of salt, toward the oldest woman I knew and the one question that mattered now:

How do you steal a miracle?
