## 3. Salt in the Walls

I have lived inside a salt warehouse for thirty-one years, and in that time I have learned three things worth teaching: salt keeps what it's given, walls remember who leans on them, and no one comes down the Ruelle des Entrepôts after dark unless they want something they're ashamed of.

Marguerite Joubert wanted something she was ashamed of. She'd wanted it for two decades, and every year on the feast of the Silent God she came to my door with bread and a question she never quite asked. This time she came at dusk, three days after the criers, with no bread and her hood up like that would fool anyone.

"Odile," she said through the door. "It's me."

"I know it's you. The door knows it's you. The whole warehouse knows it's you." I unbolted the thing anyway. "Come in before the street decides to remember you."

* * *

Let me tell you about my house, because you can't understand what I told her without understanding where I told it.

The warehouses of Brasse-les-Salines are built of brick and salt-blocks pressed so hard they've gone glassy, and when the crown dammed the flats and drowned Sainte-Avoie and half the coast besides, the water table rose under the city until the warehouses wept brine from their foundations. Everything down here is furred white. The stairs are white. The beams are white. My kettle is white. I am, I suspect, slowly becoming white myself, which suits me fine — a hedge-witch should be hard to see against any wall.

The salt remembers. That's not poetry; that's trade knowledge. Every block in these walls was cut from consecrated ground back when the god still answered, and the old cutters blessed each one going into the kiln. So the walls hold prayers the way a riverbed holds water long after the river's moved on. Press your palm flat against them and wait, and sometimes you'll feel something come up through your hand — not words, exactly. More like the shape of words. Like hearing someone talk through a wall in a language you almost knew as a child.

I've spent thirty-one years listening to my walls. It's kept me fed and it's kept me sane, though not necessarily in that order.

Marguerite came down my stairs the way she does everything — quiet, economical, like a woman crossing a courtyard full of sleeping dogs. She set her lantern on my table and looked around at the jars and bundles and the ceiling hung with drying herbs gone mostly to dust, and she said, "You haven't changed anything."

"You have," I said. "You've got a soldier's walk again. You lost it for a while there. Twenty years of pretending to be a fishwife, and now look at you, all shoulders and caution."

"The proclamation—"

"I heard the proclamation. Half the city heard the proclamation. The other half will hear about it by morning, because that's how Brasse works." I put the kettle on. Habit. Hospitality is also a kind of camouflage; nobody watches a woman making tea. "Sit down, girl. You didn't come here for tea."

"No," she said, and then sat anyway, because whatever else Marguerite Joubert is, she was raised polite. "Odile. Is it true? About the Door."

And there it was. The question she'd been carrying up my stairs for twenty years, finally set down on my table between the teapot and the mouse-chewed bundle of wormwood.

* * *

Now. Before I tell you what I told her, understand what it cost me to know it, and why I'd kept it shut behind my teeth for longer than she's been alive.

When the god went silent — and I use "went silent" the way everyone does, though I'll tell you now that phrase is doing more work than a mule in harvest season — when he went silent, the kingdom didn't fall all at once. Kingdoms don't. They rot from the edges inward, like fruit. First the far provinces stopped believing, then the roads went unsafe, then the tax collectors stopped coming back, and then one day the cartographers sat down in their warm offices and agreed among themselves that the country had never really been a country at all, just a stretch of coast with ambitions. They drew the border around it like a man drawing a sheet over a face. And that was that. No war, no conquest. A country erased by consensus.

But borders drawn on paper are still borders, and paper has doors in it whether kings admit it or not.

There is a place where the salt flats meet the old wall — not the new customs wall, the old one, the cyclopean thing out past the blight that nobody built and everybody maintains, because some stones you don't ask questions about. In that wall there is a seam of black glass, smooth as a frozen pond, tall as a cathedral door and shaped like nothing anyone builds. The first time I saw it I was nineteen and full of certainty, and I put my hand on it, and it was warm. Doors aren't warm. Walls aren't warm. Whatever that seam is, it isn't stone.

It opens. Once in a great while, for people carrying the right key. And the key is salt — not the salt in your cellar, not the salt you buy by the sack. Consecrated salt. Salt blessed by Veyl's own priests in the old rite, the long rite, the one that took three days and most of a herd of lambs. There were jars of it in every temple once. There is exactly one jar left that I know of, and I know where it is, and knowing that has been the heaviest thing I own, heavier than the roof over my head.

Why do I carry it? Because I'm old, and old women end up as reliquaries whether they mean to or not. Things get left with us. Secrets, relics, grudges, children. We're the last shelf things settle on before they fall off the world entirely.

So when Marguerite asked me if it was true, I did what I'd sworn I wouldn't do, which was answer.

* * *

"It's true," I said.

She didn't move. She's good at not moving; it's the mercenary in her, or maybe just the motherless child she was before the mercenary. "Where?"

"Ah." I poured the tea. "See, now you've asked the second question, and the second question is where it gets expensive."

"I'll pay."

"You'll pay with what? You sell luck-knots to fishermen, same as I sell fever-charms to their wives. Neither of us has two coins to rub together, and we both know it." I pushed the cup across the table. "Drink. It's terrible. It's always terrible. Drink it anyway."

She drank. Good girl.

"Here's what I know, and I'll give it to you plain, because you're the only person in this city I'd trust with plain speech, and that's saying something, since you betrayed me at cards in the spring of — never mind, you know what you did." She had the grace to look sheepish. "The Door is real. It's in the old wall, west of the blight, four days' walk if the weather holds and six if it doesn't. It opens for consecrated salt. There is one jar of consecrated salt left in this country, and I have it, and before you ask — yes, I'm telling you because of the proclamation, and yes, I hate myself for it."

"Why?" she said. "Why tell me?"

"Because they burned the registry office yesterday and started on the houses today, and because I'm seventy-one years old and can't run, and because—" I stopped. The kettle ticked on the stove. Somewhere above us, in the walls, the salt did its slow remembering, and I felt it the way you feel weather coming in a bad knee. "Because the jar was never mine to keep. It was mine to keep *until*. That's how relics work, girl. They sit with whoever's holding them until the moment comes, and then they leave, and the holding was the whole point."

"And the moment is now?"

"The moment is a woman sitting in my kitchen with a soldier's walk asking about doors." I looked at her properly, the way I hadn't let myself in twenty years. She'd gotten lines around her eyes. Her hands were wrong for a fishwife and always had been. "Marguerite. What were you, before? Really?"

The silence went on long enough that I thought she wouldn't answer. Then she said, "Sworn. Battle-sworn. Tenth cohort, the Salt Lances." And she said it the way you say the name of a dead parent — flat, careful, all the grief packed down underneath like gravel under frost.

"I know," I said. "I've known since the third year you came here. You think an old witch can't smell an oath? These walls are made of blessed salt. You walk in and the whole house stands up straighter." I tapped the table. "That's why it has to be you. Not because you asked. Because the jar won't open for just anybody, and it especially won't open for somebody who isn't sworn. Consecrated salt knows its own."

"That's not possible. The god is silent. He's been silent for twenty years."

"Mm." I wrapped my hands around my own cup, mostly for something to do with them. "About that. Sit tight, because this is the part where I tell you something I've only ever told my walls."

* * *

Here is what the walls taught me, in thirty-one years of listening.

Prayer goes somewhere. Everyone agrees on that much — even the crown, even the Inquisition, even the clever men who redrew the maps. Where it goes is the argument. The church said heaven. The philosophers said nowhere. The walls say: *here*. Prayer soaks into what it touches. It soaked into these bricks. It soaked into the flats outside, mile upon mile of it, twenty years of terrified whispered prayer from a city that wasn't allowed to pray, all of it sinking down into the salt like rain into a dry field.

And salt holds what it's given. I said that already. Salt keeps.

So when people tell me the god is dead, I want to laugh, except laughing about it makes the walls hum in a way I don't care for. The god isn't dead. The god is *stored*. He's in the flats and the foundations and the old wall and the jar in my back room, scattered thin as butter over too much bread, but present. Present the way a man is present in a letter he wrote before he died. You can't talk to him. But you can read him, if you know how, and if you're very brave, and if you don't mind what reading him might read back into you.

That's what the Door is, girl. That's what nobody understands. It's not a gate out. Or — it is, but that's the least of what it is. It's the place where the stored-up country runs thinnest, where the wall between *here* and *elsewhere* wears through like the elbow of an old coat. Pour consecrated salt on it and the salt remembers being blessed, and the blessing remembers the blesser, and for a little while — minutes, no more — the worn place opens, and a person can step through into whatever's on the other side of a country that doesn't exist anymore.

"What's on the other side?" Marguerite asked. Of course she asked. She was a soldier; soldiers scout the far side of every bridge.

"I don't know," I said, and it was the truest sentence I'd spoken all night. "Nobody who's gone through has come back to describe it. There was a smuggler, maybe fifteen years ago, paid his passage with a stolen psalter, went through with his woman and their boy. The Door closed behind them and that was the last anyone can find written or remembered. Could be another country. Could be nothing. Could be the inside of the god's silence, which for all I know is the same thing."

"That's not comforting, Odile."

"I'm seventy-one. Comfort is for the young. I deal in possibilities." I leaned forward. "But hear the rest, because the rest matters. The jar holds enough for one passage. One. Maybe two if the opener is small and doesn't breathe heavy. It is not enough for a family, it is not enough for a congregation, it is not enough for the hundreds of poor devils who are going to start walking west when the pyres start, and they *will* start walking west, because hope is a rumor and rumors travel faster than soldiers. One passage, Marguerite. Whoever takes it takes it alone, and leaves everyone else standing in front of a black glass door in the middle of a salt desert, knowing now that there's a way out and that they can't fit through it."

I watched that land on her. I watched her do the arithmetic — I could see her doing it, lips barely moving, the way she probably did casualty counts once. One jar. One door. Hundreds running. And underneath the arithmetic, something older and worse: the oath. Twenty years buried and the walls had already dug it up and shown it to her, I'd bet my kettle on it. Oaths soak in deep.

"There's a price," she said quietly. "You keep circling it. What's the price?"

And here is where I have to be honest with you, reader, whoever you are, whenever you are, if the walls ever let my voice out of this kitchen: I didn't know. I know things about the Door that no living person knows, and the price was not among them. The walls hum when I ask. The salt gives me shapes without words. The closest I ever got was the night I asked directly, palm flat on the coldest block in the foundation, and what came up through my hand was not a word but a *weight*, like a hand pressing back, and a feeling I can only call *accounting*. As if something on the other side were weighing me. As if the toll hadn't been set yet, and would be set by whoever opened the door, according to what they carried.

"Unknown," I told her. "That's the truth. The Door takes something. I don't know what. Nobody knows what. The salt won't say, and the salt says everything else."

"Then why go at all?"

"Because staying is a known price," I said, "and it's fire. Girl, they posted the list today. Names. Yours isn't on it yet, which means they don't know yet, which means you have days, not weeks. Tomas Ferreol knows what you were. Cassel knows what you were. How long do you think two old comrades keep a secret when the Inquisition starts asking with hot irons?"

Her face did something complicated and painful at Tomas's name, and filed it away for later, and I made a note of that too, because I make notes of everything; it's the privilege of the old.

* * *

I got the jar.

It lives behind a false brick in the back room, wrapped in oilcloth inside a flour crock, because the best hiding places are boring. I'd sworn I'd die before handing it over. I'd also sworn, thirty-one years ago, kneeling in the ruins of the last temple with the last priest's burned hands pressed over mine, that I'd give it to the right person when the moment came, and oaths made over burned hands don't lapse just because you get comfortable. The walls reminded me of that too. The walls are insufferable that way.

I set it on the table between us. Plain crockery, plain cloth. Marguerite looked at it the way you look at a sleeping animal that could be a dog or could be something wearing a dog.

"That's it?"

"That's it. Three days' rite, forty pounds of salt, one priest who knew what he was doing, and everything he loved poured into the blessing. All of it in that jar." I pushed it toward her and my hands shook, and I let them shake; she should see what it costs. "Take it."

"Odile—"

"Don't thank me. Thanking me means it's done, and it's not done until you're through that wall or dead on the flats, and I'd take either over the alternative." I stood, knees cracking like green wood, and went to the window slit that looks out over the loading yard. Down the Ruelle, a patrol was passing with torches, unhurried, thorough, the way patrols are when they're not hunting yet but learning the streets for later. "Four days west. Follow the old pilgrim road until it drowns, then follow the drowned road. The dead in Sainte-Avoie will point you true if you're respectful — they're bored, not hostile. Avoid the chapel at the crossroads; the choir that squats there now will either help you or unmake you and I genuinely don't know which. And when you reach the wall, don't touch the glass until you're ready to pour. It's warm, and warmth like that makes promises."

She stood. She picked up the jar and held it against her chest with both arms, and the lamp flame bent toward her, just slightly, just once, and I pretended not to see it and so did she.

"Odile. Come with me."

I laughed. It came out wrong, more cough than laugh. "Child, I haven't walked four miles in four years. This house and I have an arrangement. Besides—" I nodded at the walls, at the white fur climbing the beams, at the thirty-one years of listening soaked into every block. "Somebody has to stay and remember. That's the other thing salt does. It keeps what it's given. I'm given to keeping."

She hugged me. Quick, hard, smelling of lantern oil and fear, and then she was at the door with her hood up, and I said the thing I'd been not-saying all evening:

"Marguerite. The jar chose you before tonight. Ask yourself sometime why a hedge-witch in a salt warehouse recognized an oath you never spoke aloud. Ask yourself what your mother's people were, before they were anything else." I smiled, and it felt like a crack in old glaze. "Go. And girl — whatever the Door asks you at the end — remember that you're allowed to refuse to pay. Remember that refusing is also a choice the salt respects. I only wish I knew which choice it respects *more*."

She went out into the dark, and I bolted the door behind her, and I put my palm flat against the wall, and the salt was humming like a struck bell that hasn't decided whether to ring.

Three days later they came for me with torches, and I stood in my doorway and let them see the smile, because by then the jar was already west of the city, and everything I was given to keep, I had kept.
