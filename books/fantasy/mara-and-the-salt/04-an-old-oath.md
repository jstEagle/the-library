## 4. An Old Oath

I have kept the same ledger for twenty-two years, and in all that time the columns have never once balanced.

Guildmaster Ferreol, they call me now. Master of the Saltwrights' Guild, holder of three contracts with the Crown, owner of a house with a door that locks from the inside and a wife who believes the story I told her about my shoulder scar. Respectable. That is the word people use, and every time they say it I hear it as an accusation dressed in Sunday clothes.

The truth is simpler and uglier: I was a soldier once, sworn to a god who stopped answering, and when he went quiet I did what most soldiers do. I found a new flag to stand under and pretended the old one had never meant anything.

Marguerite Joubert is the reason that pretense has never quite held.

* * *

She came at dusk, which was like her. Marguerite never arrived anywhere at a convenient hour; she considered convenience a form of vanity. I was closing the guildhall, checking the wax seals on the day's ledgers, when the door opened without a knock — she had a talent for doors, for gaps, for the small unwatched seams of any room — and there she stood in the lamplight looking like a rumor of herself.

Twenty years had done to her what twenty years do. She was thinner. Her hair had gone iron-grey at the temples, and she wore it under a fishwife's scarf, of all things. But her eyes were the same eyes that had watched my back through the marsh campaigns, the same eyes that had knelt beside me in the mud at Varenne when the god's voice cut off mid-blessing like a snapped rope.

"Tomas," she said.

"Nobody calls me Tomas anymore," I said, because apparently my first instinct upon seeing a woman I'd have died for was to correct her grammar.

"Master Ferreol, then." She smiled, and it was the old smile, crooked, unimpressed by everything including itself. "You look prosperous."

"I look fat."

"Prosperous," she repeated, and came inside, and closed the door behind her with the particular care of someone who has spent two decades making sure no door ever closes loudly behind her.

* * *

Here is what you must understand about me, whoever ends up reading this — if anyone does, if this confession survives whatever is coming, which I doubt, because paper burns faster than loyalty and both burn faster than guilt.

I loved her. Not the way the ballads mean it. The way soldiers mean it, which is worse and less speakable. I loved her the way you love your own hands. For eleven years she was the left half of my shield wall. When Veyl fell silent at Varenne and ten thousand of us knelt in the mud waiting for a voice that never came back, it was Marguerite who stood up first. It was Marguerite who said, quietly, so only I could hear: *Get up, Tomas. He can't order us to die anymore.* And it was Marguerite who walked away from the army, from the oath, from all of it, into a city built on salt where she hid herself so thoroughly that even I lost track of her.

I found her again four years ago. Purely by accident — a charm-seller in the market whose luck-knots actually worked, tied in a pattern I'd seen her fingers make a thousand times around a campfire. I could have reported her then. Registration wasn't law yet, but the Inquisition paid informants, and I was not yet guildmaster, and money was money.

I bought a luck-knot instead. I still have it. It hangs over my desk and it has never once brought me luck, which I consider fair.

* * *

We sat in the guildhall kitchen because it has thick walls and one window, and because I wanted tea between me and whatever she was about to say. People who arrive at dusk wearing fishwife scarves are always about to say something.

"You've heard the criers," she said. It wasn't a question. The whole city had heard the criers. *By decree of His Majesty: all persons harboring the taint of unsanctioned gift shall present themselves for registration within nine days. Those who conceal themselves shall be judged apostates.* Apostates get pyres. Registered gifted get something slower and called mercy.

"I've heard them," I said.

"Then you know I can't stay in Brasse." She wrapped her hands around the cup I gave her but didn't drink. "There's a way out. South, past the flats. Odile — the hedge-witch, you remember Odile — she knows about a Door. A real passage out of the country. It needs consecrated salt to open, and she knows where some is."

My tea went cold in my mouth. "Consecrated salt."

"You know what that means."

I knew what that meant. Consecrated salt hadn't been made since the Silence. There were perhaps a dozen jars of it left in the world, relics hoarded by the kind of people who hoard relics, and every one of them was worth more than my guildhall, and possessing one was now — as of nine days ago — a hanging offense twice over.

"Marguerite." I set down the cup. "What are you asking me?"

"Nothing yet. I'm telling you because you're the only man alive who knew me before, and because—" she paused, and I saw her choose her words the way she used to choose footing in a bog, "—because if I don't come back from this, someone should know where I went. Someone who remembers."

*Someone who remembers.* As if memory were a service I provided. As if I hadn't spent twenty years building a life specifically engineered to forget.

* * *

I want to be honest about what happened inside me in that kitchen, because honesty is the only penance I have left, and I intend to spend all of it.

Two voices spoke in me, and both of them sounded like love.

The first voice said: *Help her.* It said it in the old cadence, the shield-wall cadence, the voice that had carried me through Varenne and the drowned year after. *She stood up first when the god died. She got you moving when kneeling would have been easier. You owe her the debt of your own spine.*

The second voice also said: *Help her.* But it said it differently. It said: *Help her by getting her out of this. She cannot outrun the Inquisition. You've seen what Cassel's people do to runners — they don't even bother with trials anymore, they burn them at the crossroads as examples. She is one woman with a jar and a legend about a Door. If she runs, she dies badly, publicly, and everyone she's touched burns with her. The only way to save her life is to take her choice away before the Inquisition takes it anyway.*

That second voice is the one I listened to. I want that recorded plainly. I did not betray her out of fear, or greed, or ambition, though God — though Veyl, though whatever listens now — knows I had plenty of all three available. I betrayed her because I convinced myself, with the full machinery of a mind that has spent two decades rationalizing, that surrender was the last shelter left to offer her.

Registration means a collar and a work-gang and a life. Flight means fire. I chose the collar for her. I never asked if she wanted it chosen.

That is the whole of it. That is the shape of my sin, and it is such a respectable little sin, isn't it? So reasonable. So well-lit. I have met men who betrayed for gold and I confess I envied them their simplicity.

* * *

She stayed until the bells rang compline. We talked about nothing for most of it — the price of salt, the new breakwater, whether Perrin the smuggler was still alive (she asked; I said I thought so; I should have wondered why she asked). Twice she almost smiled at something I said, and both times I felt it like warmth on a scar.

At the door she stopped. "Tomas. If it comes to it — if they come for me — you keep your head down. Promise me. You've built something here. Don't let them take it for an old comrade."

And I promised her. I stood in my own doorway and lied to her face while she thanked me for it, and then she pulled her scarf up and became a fishwife again and dissolved into the evening, and I stood there until the lamp in the hall guttered, listening to the sound of my own heart doing arithmetic.

Nine days on the proclamation. She'd need maybe two to prepare. Which gave me seven days to decide.

It took me one night. That is the part I cannot forgive. Not the decision — decisions are just weather passing through a man. The speed of it. Twenty-two years of respectability, and it turned out the respectable part of me had been waiting the whole time, fully armed, for permission to do exactly this.

* * *

Inquisitor Cassel keeps offices in the old customs house, which amuses him, I think — the crown taxing faith now instead of goods. I went at morning, in my guildmaster's coat, with my guildmaster's seal, and I told the clerk I had information regarding a fugitive of the purge, and I was shown upstairs within the hour because information about Marguerite Joubert was, unknown to me, already the most valuable commodity in Brasse-les-Salines.

Cassel was not what I expected. I had imagined a butcher. What sat behind the desk was a lean, grey-templed man with a soldier's posture and a clerk's patience, and when he looked at me I had the sudden vertiginous feeling of being assessed by someone who had once held the same oath I had. Later I learned it was true — he'd served in the marsh campaigns, different company, same mud, same silence. He knew exactly what I had been. That knowledge sat between us the whole interview like a third man.

"Master Ferreol." He gestured to a chair. "The guildmaster of the Saltwrights comes to the Inquisition personally. This must be serious."

"It concerns a woman named Marguerite Joubert."

Something moved behind his eyes — not surprise. Recognition. Confirmation. "Go on."

"She came to me last night. She intends to flee south across the flats. She spoke of a Door, and of consecrated salt in her possession or nearly so." My voice held steady. I'm proud of that and ashamed of it in equal measure; steadiness under these circumstances is not virtue, it is training, and my training never asked whether the orders were good ones.

Cassel listened without interrupting, which unnerved me more than shouting would have. When I finished he was quiet for a long moment, fingers laced on the desk.

"Do you know what she was?" he asked.

"A shield-sister. Eleven years. The best soldier I ever served beside."

"And after Varenne?"

"After Varenne she walked away."

"Yes," Cassel said softly. "They mostly did. The strong ones walked away." He rose and went to the window, looking down at the market square where the criers were already at work, their decree bouncing off the salt-white walls. "Do you know why the god went silent, Master Ferreol?"

"No one knows why the god went silent."

"The Church says it was a test. A trial of faith — he withdrew his voice to see who would remain faithful without reward." He turned back. "I believed that for a long time. I believe a version of it still. But I'll tell you what I've learned in fifteen years of hunting the gifted, and you may make of it what you will." He came around the desk and stood close, and his voice dropped to something almost gentle. "Every gifted soul I have taken — every single one — wept with relief when it was done. Do you understand? They are tired, Master Ferreol. Carrying a dead god's spark is heavy, and they have been carrying it alone, in hiding, for twenty years. Your Marguerite is tired too. Whatever she told you in your kitchen, some part of her wants to put it down."

"That's not why I came," I said, and hated how weak it sounded.

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "You came because you love her, and you have concluded that love requires her capture. It's the oldest story in my profession. Half my informants are former comrades." He extended his hand. "You have my word as an officer of the Inquisition: if she is taken alive and unresisting, she will face registration, not the pyre. I give that word rarely, and I keep it absolutely."

I shook his hand. His grip was dry and warm and human, and that was the worst thing about the entire morning.

* * *

They took her the next evening.

Not at my house — Cassel was scrupulous about that, another courtesy that curdles in my stomach. They took her at Odile's warehouse in the salt quarter, and I know this because I made it my business to know, standing three streets away in the crowd of gawkers while smoke went up and someone screamed, and the scream had Marguerite's timbre even if it couldn't have been Marguerite, because Marguerite doesn't scream, Marguerite *moves*, and by all accounts move she did, out a loading door with a jar under her arm while the warehouse burned behind her and old Odile — they say — stood in the flames holding the soldiers off with hexes that shouldn't have worked and did.

A jar under her arm. Consecrated salt. Which meant everything I had given Cassel had purchased nothing except a dead hedge-witch, a burned warehouse, and a fugitive now harder to find than ever, running south with the one relic in the country that opens the Door.

I have replayed that evening ten thousand times since. Here is the version where I warn her instead: she laughs at me, kindly, the way she laughed at my fear before Varenne, and goes anyway, and Odile lives, and the soldiers catch her on the flats within the week because Cassel's outriders ride the coast road, and she burns at a crossroads as an example. Here is the version where I go with her: we both die, and my wife, and my guild, and nothing changes. Here is the version where I never tell Cassel at all: she reaches the Door alone, or doesn't, and either way I sleep at night.

I have examined every version the way a merchant examines coin, biting each one, and every single one of them is false metal except the one that happened. That is the trick of betrayal wrapped in love — it presents itself as the only sound currency in a market of counterfeits. Every other choice looks like sentiment. Only surrender looks like sense.

Sense. Twenty years of sense. I could wallpaper my house with it.

* * *

They say she's heading for the border flats, and that half the refugees in the province are drifting the same direction like iron filings toward a magnet nobody can see. They say Cassel rides out tomorrow with two companies. They say the Inquisitor gave orders that the fugitive Joubert is to be taken alive.

He kept his word to me. I want that noted by whatever court eventually convenes over my soul. He kept his word, and it is the cruelest thing anyone has ever done in my name.

Tonight I took the luck-knot down from over my desk. Twenty years it hung there, her knots, her fingers, her patient deniable magic, and tonight I finally looked at it properly — really looked, the way I hadn't let myself since the market stall — and I saw what I should have seen the day I bought it.

The knot isn't a luck charm. It's an oath-knot. The old pattern, the shield-wall pattern, the one we tied around each other's sword-hilts at dawn before battle: *your death is mine to prevent.* She sold them in the market for coppers for twenty years, and every frightened fisherman who bought one walked away carrying a soldier's vow without knowing it, and the vow worked, because it was hers, because she never stopped keeping it, not for one day, not for one soul, not even while hiding.

She kept her oath to strangers. I broke mine to her in a single night, and called it salvation.

Tomorrow the Inquisition rides south. Tomorrow I open my ledgers and balance my columns and sign my contracts and be Master Ferreol, respectable, prosperous, whole. And somewhere on the white flats, a woman with iron in her hair is walking toward a Door with a dead god's salt in her hands, carrying my oath for me, the way she carried everything else.

There is one thing left I can do. One payment against a debt that has no bottom.

I am going to find the smuggler Perrin.
