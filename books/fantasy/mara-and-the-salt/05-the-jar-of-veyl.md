## 5. The Jar of Veyl

Odile had told me once that the best hiding place in Brasse-les-Salines was inside the thing everyone was afraid of. She lived by it. The salt warehouses on the Quai des Morts were condemned, half-collapsed, and rumored to be haunted by the ghosts of men who'd died shoveling brine for a kingdom that no longer existed. Nobody went there. Nobody except an old hedge-witch who thought fear made excellent insulation.

I should have known better. Fear doesn't insulate. It accumulates. And when the crown finally came looking, they came to the place where all the frightened things had been quietly stored.

I heard them before I saw them—the particular rhythm of boots that isn't marching and isn't casual, the rhythm of men trying to be quiet in numbers. Twenty pairs at least. I was on the third floor of the warehouse, in Odile's workroom among the hanging bundles of dried herbs and the jars labeled in her crabbed hand—*for sleep*, *for grief*, *for women who ask too many questions*—when the first torchlight bloomed orange against the salt-crusted windows.

"Marguerite." Odile's voice came up through the floorboards, low and unhurried, as if she were calling me down for tea instead of telling me to run. "The back stair. Take the jar."

"The jar—" I had seen it only once, two nights ago, when she'd pulled it from beneath her bed: a squat clay vessel sealed with black wax, unremarkable except that my hands had shaken when I touched it, the way they hadn't shaken in twenty years.

"You know which one. Don't argue with an old woman, we haven't the time."

I argued anyway. It took four seconds, which was three more than we had. "Come with me."

She appeared at the foot of the back stair, small and bent and utterly calm, wearing the same gray shawl she wore to market, as though this were any ordinary evening and the light climbing the walls outside was just sunset arriving early. "Child," she said, "I am seventy-one years old, my knees are worse than the city's roads, and I have been waiting twenty years for someone to come take this thing off my hands. This is not my night. It's yours."

Below us, the front doors splintered.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about running: your body does it perfectly well without you. My legs knew stairs I'd never descended. My hands found the jar where Odile had pressed it into my satchel, wrapped in oilcloth, heavier than salt had any right to be—heavier, I would think later, than guilt, though at the time I wouldn't have believed anything could outweigh that.

The back stair let out into the alley behind the warehouses, the one that ran along the canal where the water was too brine-thick to freeze even in winter. Behind me, the front of the building filled with shouting. I heard Odile's voice, clear and conversational, saying something I couldn't make out, and then a soldier's voice, much less conversational, and then—

Then nothing. Which was worse.

I made it perhaps forty paces before my legs staged their mutiny and stopped working altogether. I stood in the dark between two dead warehouses, clutching a satchel like a widow clutches jewelry, listening to the silence where an old woman's voice used to be.

Go back, said every part of me that had ever sworn an oath. Go back and die usefully beside her, the way soldiers do, the way we were trained to believe was noble.

Stay alive, said the older part, the part that had survived the mud at Vaulx-en-Brume while ten thousand better soldiers knelt and prayed and died. Stay alive and carry it.

I hated both parts of myself equally, which is, I've since learned, the closest thing to balance most of us achieve.

The first smoke reached me before the flames did—that sweetish, chemical reek of burning salt-saturated timber, a smell I knew from childhood, from the year they burned the temple district, from the year everything ended. I turned once, at the mouth of the alley, and looked back.

The third-floor window of Odile's workroom glowed like a lantern. Her herbs, her jars, her careful labels—all of it going up in a bloom of orange, and somewhere inside it, either dead or dying or standing very straight while soldiers did what soldiers are paid to do.

I did not see her die. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger the silent god keeps, if he keeps one: I did not see it. But I heard the roof beam go, that long groaning crack like a tree falling in a forest where no one is allowed to pray, and I knew, the way you know things about people you love, and I ran.

* * *

I ran until the Quai des Morts was behind me and the fish market was around me and the pre-dawn gulls were screaming over the stalls, and then I stopped running because stopping was the last thing anyone would expect of a fugitive. That's a trick Tomas taught me, back when Tomas taught me things instead of selling me. Hide in plain motion. A woman walking briskly through a market at dawn is invisible; a woman sprinting is a spectacle.

So I walked. Past the fishmongers laying out the night's catch on beds of ice. Past the bread ovens exhaling their first warm breath of the day. Past a knot of people already gathered around a crier, though I didn't need to hear him—I'd heard the proclamation yesterday, registration or the pyre, the crown's generous offer—and past a girl of maybe ten who looked at me with the flat, assessing stare of a child who has already learned that adults can't be trusted.

My hands were steady. That was the obscene thing. Twenty years of practice, and my hands had learned steadiness so thoroughly that they could hold a dead woman's relic through a burning city without so much as a tremor.

The jar sat against my hip like a second heartbeat.

I bought a roll from a baker who didn't look at me, because buying bread is the most innocent act in the world, and I ate it standing in the shadow of the customs house, and I tasted nothing at all.

* * *

By full light I had put the river between myself and the warehouse district and found the ruined chapel of Saint Ferreol-the-Lesser—not named for my traitor, mercifully, some other Ferreol, some older and presumably better one—where the roof had fallen in decades ago and the salt had crept up the walls like frost given ambition. I sat in the corner where the altar used to be and unwrapped the jar.

Up close, in honest daylight, it was almost comically ordinary. Clay, fired cheaply, glazed a dull brown. The black wax seal was cracked along one edge, ancient, repaired once with newer wax that had been blessed by someone with a surer hand. No markings. No inscription. If I set it on a market stall between the luck-knots and the fever-charms, no one would give it a second glance.

But when I laid my palm flat against it, the salt inside shifted. Not poured, not settled—*shifted*, deliberately, the way a sleeping animal turns toward warmth. And through my palm came a feeling I had spent twenty years learning to live without: the sensation of being *known*. Not judged. Not forgiven. Just known, completely, the way the sea knows a drowned man.

"Oh," I said aloud, to the empty chapel, to the god who wasn't there. "Oh, you're still in there."

The salt said nothing. Salt is famously reticent. But it leaned into my hand, and I sat in a burned church holding the last consecrated thing in a country that had outlawed consecration, and I understood—for the first time, truly understood—what Odile had handed me in those last minutes.

Not a key. Keys open doors and then you walk through and the door closes behind you and the room you left stays a room. This was not that. This was a debt. Somewhere out there was a Door, and this salt was its price, and the price had been kept safe for twenty years by a woman with bad knees who had never once complained, and now it was mine, and so was everything that came with it.

Every soldier who would follow me. Every checkpoint. Every name on Cassel's list—and mine would be at the top now, underlined, because Odile had died buying me minutes and the Inquisition did not forgive minutes.

"I didn't ask for this," I told the jar.

It leaned into my hand again, patient, and I got the distinct impression—which I recognize now as the first whisper of something far larger, though then it felt like nothing but my own conscience wearing salt's face—that it had heard that argument before, from better people, and remained unmoved.

* * *

I buried Odile's memory the only way I could: I stayed in the chapel until dusk and I spoke her name out loud, which is more than the crown will ever do for her, and I promised her I would carry it well. I have broken most of the promises of my life. I intend to keep that one, or die in the attempt, and given the current state of affairs, the odds are roughly even.

Toward evening I heard boots on the chapel steps—two men, maybe three, moving with the unhurried confidence of hunters checking a trapline—and I went out through the gap where the eastern wall had fallen, soft-footed across the salt crust, and did not look back at the second building I'd abandoned in one day.

From the ridge above the fish gate, I could see the smoke still rising from the Quai des Morts, a thin gray column standing straight up in the windless air, like a candle lit for the dead. Like a marker on a map. *Here. Here is where she started.*

They would find tracks eventually, or witnesses, or simply follow the logic: the witch is dead, therefore the thief lives, therefore the thief runs for the border, because everyone runs for the border. They would send riders to the coast road. They would send Cassel.

And Cassel knew me. Knew how I moved, how I thought, how I hid. Twenty years ago he'd been the one who found *other* people's hiding places, back when finding them was our job, back when we both served something we believed in.

I tightened the satchel strap across my chest and felt the jar press against my ribs, warm now, or perhaps that was just me.

"Well," I said to it, to the country, to the smoke, to whatever remained of Odile Joubert's stubborn spirit. "Let's see what you're worth."

Below me, the fish gate opened for the dawn carts, and beyond it the coast road ran white and empty toward the flats, toward the drowned villages, toward a Door I had learned of three days ago and would spend the rest of my shortening life trying to reach.

Somewhere behind me, a rider's horn sounded—once, twice, the signal for *sighted, moving north*—and I stepped off the ridge and began to run.
