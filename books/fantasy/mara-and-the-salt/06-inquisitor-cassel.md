## 6. Inquisitor Cassel

The confession room in the Bastide had once been a counting house, back when the Inquisition was poor and honest about it. I knew because I'd signed the requisition for its conversion eleven years ago, and because the old tally marks were still scratched into the doorframe where no amount of whitewash could kill them. Someone had counted something here for decades—barrels, coins, days. Now we counted other things.

I counted, that morning: one table, two chairs, one window with salt-frosted glass, one guildmaster of the Coopers' and Bonded Freight Guild sitting across from me with his hands folded like a man at his own funeral who has decided to be gracious about the flowers.

Tomas Ferreol. We had never met before this week, and yet I had known him for twenty years, the way you know a scar you can't see. He was the friend. The shield-brother. The name Marguerite Joubert said in her sleep, if the informants were to be believed—and they were, because informants lie about everything except the things that break people.

"You've been here since dawn," he said. Not a complaint. An observation, delivered in the tone of a man who had spent the night cataloguing his own failures and found the list shorter than expected.

"I have." I set down the file. It was thin. That was the thing nobody understood about my work—the guilty ones always came with thick files, and the ones who mattered came with almost nothing. "You reported her yesterday afternoon. By evening she was gone. I'd like to know what happened in between."

"She left."

"People don't leave, Master Ferreol. They flee, or they're taken, or they die on the road and are eaten by something with too many opinions. *Left* is a word for ships and husbands." I turned the file around so he could read it upside down, which is a cruelty I reserve for men who think themselves clever. "Your statement says she came to you asking for shelter. Your statement says you refused. Your statement does not say why your hands were shaking when you gave it."

He looked at his hands as though seeing them for the first time. Big hands, cooper's hands, scarred across the knuckles in a pattern I recognized, because mine matched. Sword calluses under the barrel-calluses. You can sand a man down into a merchant, but the grain remembers.

"They shook," he admitted, "because I love her."

There it is. Twenty years in this trade and they still hand it to you like that, wrapped in ribbon, expecting you to be gentle with it. I have been called many things—cassel the Cruel, cassel the Leash, once, memorably, *the Salt Priest's dog* by a woman whose tongue I did not take because she was right—but no one has ever accused me of enjoying this part. The part where the love comes out. It always comes out. Heresy is just love that took a wrong turn at the border of the self and kept walking.

"Tell me about her," I said, and leaned back, and let him talk.

* * *

Here is what the pamphlets say about me, because every office needs its pamphlets: that Cassel believes the silence is a punishment; that Cassel burns the gifted out of hatred; that Cassel has never laughed, or slept, or eaten anything but ash and duty. The pamphlets are written by people who need their monsters simple.

The truth is worse and simpler both. I believe the god is listening. That is the whole of my creed, and everything else—the pyres, the registries, the long gray machinery of the purge—is only what follows if you believe it honestly.

Veyl went silent twenty-two years ago. Mid-prayer, mid-battle, mid-miracle. Ten thousand sworn soldiers kneeling in the mud at Vaurmont with their oaths still warm in their mouths, and heaven closed like a shopkeeper shuttering early. The kingdom broke within the year. The gifted went from saints to suspects in a generation, which is not hypocrisy—it is arithmetic. A miracle you can verify is a covenant. A miracle you cannot is a debt someone else will eventually try to collect.

But I was there. At Vaurmont. I knelt in that mud, and I felt the oath go out of me like heat from an open door, and I have spent every day since doing the only arithmetic that matters: either the god abandoned us, or the god is testing us. There is no third door. Abandonment means nothing we do matters, and a life where nothing matters is a life I decline to live. So: testing. And a test implies a standard. And a standard implies that some of us are failing it.

The gifted fail it loudest. That's all the purge is. Not cruelty. Pruning.

I know how that sounds. I have heard how it sounds. But consider the alternative reading—that we should keep the gifted among us, unregistered, unwatched, carrying embers of a fire that already burned one kingdom down—and tell me which position requires more faith in luck.

* * *

"She was the best of us," Tomas was saying. He had been talking for some minutes and I had let him, because a man telling you the truth about someone else is building the scaffold for the truth about himself. "In the company, before. You'd want her at your shield side in any bad hour ever made. She—" He stopped. Started again. "She used to do small things. Charms. Luck-knots for the townsfolk when we wintered somewhere, little warmth-spells in gloves, nothing that would raise a eyebrow now. And Veyl never minded. That's what people forget. He *never minded*. The small gifts were his favorite ones, she said. He liked magic that fit in a pocket."

"And then the silence came."

"And then the silence came." His jaw worked. "She took it harder than any of us. Harder than the commander. She'd been sworn since she was fourteen. When the rest of us drifted—married, mustered out, found other gods or no gods—she kept the disciplines. Kept the fasts. I've watched her stand vigil on the anniversary every year, alone, in the rain, like a madwoman. Like the last soldier of an army that doesn't exist."

"Yes," I said quietly. "I know."

Because I had read the surveillance reports, yes, but also because I had done versions of the same thing, in different weather. There are perhaps four hundred people in this country keeping vigils for a silent god, and half of them are on my lists, and the other half are on my lists under wrong names. Grief keeps better records than any chancery.

"So when she came to my door yesterday," Tomas said, "asking me to hide her—I want you to understand that every part of me that ever stood in a shield wall said yes before she finished the sentence."

"But you didn't say yes."

"No." He looked up, and his eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady, and I want it recorded, whoever reads this file, wherever it ends up in whatever archive survives us: Tomas Ferreol betrayed his shield-sister with his eyes open. "I didn't say yes. Because hiding her would have killed her slower. You'd have found her. You always find them. And when you found her, she'd have run, and running gifted women get burned at the roadside without trial, and I—" his voice cracked and he hated it, I watched him hate it, "—I couldn't be the reason she died badly. So I made her die cleanly instead. Registered. Trial. Mercy of the court. That's what I told myself. Tell me if I'm lying, Inquisitor. You people are supposed to know."

I let the silence sit between us for a while. Salt wind worried at the frosted window. Somewhere below, the market was opening, and I thought—not for the first time, but with new weight—of luck-knots.

"You're not lying," I said finally. "That's what makes you useful to me, and dangerous to yourself. You surrendered her out of love, and you'll sleep tonight believing it was mercy, and you may even be right. But answer me this, and answer carefully, because it's the only question that matters." I leaned forward. "When she asked you for shelter—did she say where she was going?"

His face did something complicated. Grief and guilt fighting over the same ground, and underneath both, briefly, a flash of the old soldier's calculation: *how much can I give away and still live with myself.*

"She didn't trust me enough to say."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the truest one I have." He spread his hands. "She looked at me like I was already a stranger. Like she'd known, somehow, before she ever knocked. Twenty years of friendship and she knocked on my door already saying goodbye to me. What does that tell you, Inquisitor? About what she's carrying? About what she thinks she has to do?"

It told me she had the jar. I did not say so. Odile the hedge-witch's warehouse had been on my lists for months—a node, a relay, one of those quiet houses where the gifted passed words and relics along like a bucket chain fighting a fire. Two days ago the bucket chain had started moving fast. Yesterday Marguerite Joubert surfaced after twenty years of perfect invisibility, asked her oldest friend for help, and vanished again within hours. Things move quickly when they are frightened, and more quickly still when they are *needed*.

And there was only one relic in this country worth moving quickly for. Every child in Brasse-les-Salines knows the story the way they know the tide-tables: the Door in the salt, the last way out of a country whose name we agreed to stop writing down. And every child is told it's a story. I had spent eleven years confirming it wasn't.

"Master Ferreol," I said, rising, gathering the thin file, "you're free to go. Your cooperation is noted. The Guild will hear that you assisted the Inquisition fully."

He didn't move. "Will she burn? If you catch her."

"If she resists, yes. If she submits, she'll be registered and released. The crown prefers registration. Fire is expensive and the smoke annoys the neighbors." I said it flatly, because it was true, and because kindness delivered as fact hurts less than kindness delivered as comfort. "You did what your conscience required. Go home. Sleep. If she contacts you again—"

"She won't."

"—if she does, you'll send word. Not for your sake. For hers. Clean deaths are rarer than you think, and I am the only man in this city offering them."

He walked to the door and stopped there, one hand on the frame, under the old tally marks. He didn't turn around.

"When the god comes back," Tomas said, "what do you think he'll say about all this? The lists. The fires. The *pruning*." He said the word back to me gently, the way you return a knife handle-first. "Have you ever actually prayed about it, Cassel? Or only about everyone else?"

Then he was gone, and I sat alone in the counting house with the salt wind and the tally marks, and I did the thing I do every night and admit to no one.

I prayed.

Not for guidance—I stopped asking for guidance around year six, when the silence had answered so many questions with its beautiful terrible nothing that I understood the silence *was* the answer. No. I prayed the way a soldier stands watch. Not because the watch changes anything, but because someone must hold the wall until morning, and morning is coming, and I would rather be found at my post than anywhere else.

*Veyl,* I said, to the frost on the glass, to the salt in the air, to the country with no name. *They say you're dead. They say we ate you. I say you're waiting, and that you are watching how we wait. I am keeping your faithful clean. I am keeping the count.*

*Show me where she is.*

* * *

The answer came within the hour, which I choose to read as diligence rather than miracle.

A runner from the harbor watch, breathless, hatless: fire at the salt warehouses on the Quai d'Avoine. Odile Marchand's row. The witch herself confirmed inside—dead or dying, the watch couldn't say, the flames were through the roof. And a witness, a fisherman, half-hysterical, insisting he'd seen a woman come out of the burning building at a dead run with something white clutched to her chest, heading for the Fish Gate, heading for the coast road.

Something white. Clutched to her chest.

I was moving before the runner finished, calling for horses, for the leash-men, for the road warrants. My lieutenant—a good boy, devout, the kind of believer who thinks conviction is a mood rather than a discipline—fell into step beside me and asked, breathless, whether we'd catch her.

"At the Fish Gate or past it," I said. "She has a day's start at most and a burden she cannot put down. Whatever she's carrying, Lieutenant, she believes it's worth more than her life." I swung into the saddle, and the salt flats opened ahead of us, gray and endless and patient as scripture. "Which means she'll make mistakes. People always do, carrying that much weight."

We rode. Behind us Brasse-les-Salines smoked against the morning, and ahead of us the drowned villages waited in their shallow graves, and somewhere on the coast road a woman ran with a jar of consecrated salt against her heart, running toward the one door I had sworn my whole gray life to keep shut.

I did not hate her. Understand that. I have never hated her.

I loved her once, at Vaurmont, kneeling in the mud beside ten thousand others, before the silence fell and taught us all what our faith was actually made of. And love, in my experience, is the only thing worth pursuing across a salt flat.

Even when it runs the wrong way.
