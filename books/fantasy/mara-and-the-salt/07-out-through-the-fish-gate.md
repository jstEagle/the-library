## 7. Out Through the Fish Gate

**Marguerite**

The smuggler's name was Perrin, and he looked like a man who had been assembled from spare parts of other, better men. A coat that had been fine once and was now merely warm. Hands stained with fish scales and tar. Eyes the color of shallow water over sand—eyes that went everywhere and settled nowhere, which in Brasse-les-Salines was a survival trait I recognized the way you recognize your own handwriting.

I found him where Odile's note said I would find him: behind the Fish Gate, in the tannery row, mending nets he clearly had no intention of finishing.

"You're the one who wants the coast road," he said without looking up. "The one who can't pay."

"Word travels."

"Word travels when it's paid to travel. Yours walked." He bit through a knot with his teeth and spat the end into the mud. "So. You've got no coin. What have you got?"

"A jar."

He laughed. It wasn't a kind laugh, but it wasn't unkind either; it was the laugh of a man who priced things for a living and found my inventory amusing. "A jar. Wonderful. Is there wine in it? Wine I could sell."

"There's salt in it."

That stopped him. Not much—just a hitch, like a cart wheel finding a rut—but I'd spent twenty years reading people for a living before I'd spent them selling luck-knots, and I knew what that hitch meant. He knew what salt meant. Consecrated salt, specifically, though I hadn't said so, and he was too careful to ask.

"Salt," he repeated. "In this city. Which is built on it. Which everyone has."

"Not like this."

He set down the net at last and looked at me properly, and I watched him do the arithmetic behind his eyes—the same arithmetic every desperate person in this country was doing since the criers came through. Registration or the pyre. The pyre or the road. The road cost money, and money had become the rarest substance in Brasse-les-Salines, rarer than mercy, rarer than magic.

"The checkpoints are doubled," he said. "Fish Gate, Salt Gate, the bridge at Vaudrey. They're not even checking papers anymore. They're checking *faces*. Looking for the look." He tapped the corner of his own eye. "You've got the look, friend. Whatever your name is."

"Liora," I said, because lying about my name was as natural as breathing and twice as necessary.

"Liora." He tried it out like a bad coin between his teeth. "And Liora has a jar of special salt and no money and needs to be on the coast road by dawn, which is—" he squinted at the sky, gray as dishwater, "—four hours away. And what exactly does Liora imagine she's paying me with?"

"I'll pay you when we're clear. There are people past the wall who owe me."

"People past the wall." He said it the way you'd say *dragons past the wall*. "There's nothing past the wall. That's rather the point of the wall."

"There's a Door."

For a moment—just a moment—I thought I'd made a mistake. His face did something complicated, quick as a card trick, and then it was just a smuggler's face again, bored and slightly amused. But I'd seen the first face. Under all that professional boredom, Perrin believed in the Door. Believed in it the way you believe in a wound: privately, constantly, against your will.

"Everyone's heard of the Door," he said lightly. "Door's a story for people who can't afford boats."

"So put me on a boat."

"Boats are being burned. Did you miss that? The Inquisitor's men went down the harbor two nights ago with lantern oil. Every hull bigger than a skiff." He shrugged. "Cassel doesn't want anyone leaving. Cassel wants everyone accounted for. There's a difference between a shepherd and a jailer, but Cassel hasn't worked it out yet."

I knew Cassel. That was the thing I couldn't say, standing in the tannery stink with a dead woman's jar wrapped in sacking against my ribs. I'd known Cassel when he was a boy who shared his bread ration and prayed with his eyes open because he didn't trust the dark. I knew exactly how thorough he was being about the harbor, and the gates, and the faces.

"Four hours," I said. "Dawn. The Fish Gate. How much?"

"For you? Given your assets?" He smiled, showing teeth that had seen better decades. "Everything in the jar."

"No."

"No?"

"It's not mine to spend. It's a debt I'm carrying for someone who died buying me four hours, and I don't intend to insult her by paying her off in passage east." I heard my own voice go flat and hard, the mercenary's voice, the one I hadn't used in twenty years. It fit worse than it used to. Or maybe it fit better, and that was the problem.

Perrin studied me for a long moment. Somewhere behind us a dog started up, and a whistle answered it—one short, one long—and I saw him cataloguing that sound the way I catalogued exits.

"Half," he said finally. "Half the jar now, half when we're clear of the last checkpoint. And if you're lying about any part of this, Liora-who-isn't-Liora, I'll leave you in a ditch somewhere with your throat open, and I'll feel bad about it for almost a whole day."

"That's the most honest offer I've had in weeks."

"Business has been slow." He stood, joints cracking like green wood, and kicked dirt over his unfinished net. "Come on, then. We go the long way through the curing sheds, and you keep your mouth shut and your face stupid. Can you do stupid?"

"I've been doing it for twenty years."

**Perrin**

Here's what nobody tells you about smuggling: it's ninety percent waiting and ten percent lying, and the lying is mostly to yourself.

I'd been telling myself for three years that I was done with the big jobs. Small runs only. Tobacco, untaxed brandy, letters from people who missed each other across lines that hadn't used to exist. A man my age doesn't need glory; a man my age needs his knees and his neck to keep agreeing with each other. And then a woman walks into tannery row smelling of woodsmoke and old blood, carrying a jar that hums against my senses like a struck bell, asking after the one road out of this country that officially does not exist, and I hear myself saying yes.

Not because of the salt. All right—partly because of the salt. Half a jar of consecrated salt is a fortune, and fortunes buy boats, and boats buy distance, and distance buys mornings where nobody checks your face.

But also—gods forgive me—because she said *the Door*, and something under my ribs leaned toward it like a plant toward a window.

We moved through the curing sheds in the blue hour before dawn, when the whole city smells of brine and rot and the gulls are arguing about whose corpse-day it is. She walked well. That surprised me. Most of the people who come to me are soft—merchants' sons, failed clerks, women who've never been farther from their own beds than the market square. They walk like they're apologizing to the ground. This one walked like the ground should apologize to her, heel to toe, weight low, eyes doing the same sweep mine were doing. Checking corners. Counting soldiers.

"You've done this before," I murmured.

"Done what?" she said, with a face of such perfect blank stupidity that I nearly laughed.

"That. Exactly that."

"Twenty years of practice."

At the mouth of the sheds, lamplight. Two of the crown's men, halberds leaning, and between them a table with a brazier on it, and I felt my stomach drop, because braziers at checkpoints meant one thing: they were burning things. Papers, mostly. Sometimes other things.

"New procedure," I breathed. "They'll want names. Give them a name that isn't yours, obviously, but give them one easily. Liars trip on complicated lies."

"What's yours?"

"Perrin Ferreol, curer, third shed down. Boring as unsalted bread."

Her head turned, very slightly. "Ferreol."

"Cousin to the guildmaster, if you must know. Family's everywhere in this town like mold. Why?"

"Nothing," she said, and her face did the trick again, the blankness sliding down like a shutter. But I'd been lying professionally for thirty years, and I know what a person looks like when a name lands wrong. I filed it away. Filing things away was most of my trade.

We joined the line of dawn workers—fishwives, shed hands, a boy hauling baskets of eels that writhed like the city's conscience. The soldier at the table was young. Young ones were dangerous; they still thought the job meant something.

"Name."

"Perrin Ferreol. Curing shed three."

He checked a list. My name was on it, because I paid a clerk in the harbormaster's office to make sure it always was. The soldier waved me through and turned to her.

"Name."

"Liora Vasse," she said. "Shed hand. New."

"New." He looked up, and I saw him do the thing they'd trained into them, the thing the criers had promised: he looked for the look. The fear. The flinch. And she gave him—nothing. She gave him the dull, resentful patience of a woman who'd been standing in lines her whole life and expected nothing better from men with tables. It was beautiful. It was the best lying I'd ever seen, and it consisted entirely of telling the truth badly on purpose.

"Open your basket."

She opened the basket. Bread, a knife for gutting, a bundle of dried herbs. And the jar, wrapped in sacking, sitting right there among the eel-smell and the dill like it belonged, and I confess my heart performed a small acrobatic maneuver, because I'd told her to carry it in her coat and she'd decided the basket was smarter—a search pats the body, but who digs through fish guts?

The soldier prodded the bundle with his halberd's butt. "What's this?"

"Herbs. For smoking the catch. You want to smell my hands instead?" She held them out, palms up, cracked and reeking. "Twenty years of herring, sergent. It doesn't wash out."

He recoiled slightly. Young ones always did. "Go on."

We walked. We didn't hurry—that was the trick, hurrying was a confession—and only when we'd rounded the seawall and the checkpoint was a smudge of lamplight behind us did she exhale, once, controlled, like a woman letting air out of a sail.

"Ferreol," she said again.

"My family name. Don't wear it out."

"Tomas Ferreol. Guildmaster Tomas Ferreol."

My turn to walk a few steps in silence. "My cousin. My mother's sister's boy. Why does a shed hand named Liora Vasse know my cousin's full name?"

"Because until three days ago, I knew him by another name," she said quietly. "Shield-brother. Before the silence. Before any of this."

I stopped walking. The gulls screamed. Somewhere out on the flats the tide was going out and taking the light with it, and here was this woman telling me, in the middle of the one morning I needed everything to be simple, that she was one of them. One of the sworn. One of the ten thousand who'd knelt in the mud when the god stopped answering, back when I was a boy stealing fish off the harbor carts and listening to the grown-ups whisper about the day heaven went quiet.

"You're gifted," I said.

"I was. Twenty years ago. Now I'm a woman with a jar and a talent for looking boring." Her eyes came around to me, and for the first time they weren't performing anything at all, and what was underneath was tired in a way I recognized from funerals. "Are you going to turn me in? There's a reward. There's always a reward."

"I'm going to get you to the coast road," I said, "because you've paid me half a fortune in salt, and because turning you in would mean talking to the Inquisition, and I'd rather be flayed." I started walking again. "Also because you carried that jar in a fish basket past a boy with a halberd, and I want to see what else you do. Call it professional curiosity."

"How generous."

"Don't mistake it for kindness. Kindness is expensive. Curiosity is free."

**Marguerite**

We walked the coast road as the sun came up the color of an old bruise, and I learned three things about Perrin Ferreol in the first hour.

One: he talked constantly, and none of it meant anything. He had opinions on the price of eels, the morals of gulls, the architectural failings of the watchtowers, and the tragic decline of the city's bread, and every word of it was a curtain drawn in front of a window. I'd known men like him in the company. The best scouts talked the most.

Two: he was afraid. Not of the soldiers—we'd passed a second checkpoint wedged in a cart of kelp, and he'd chatted the driver through it like they were old friends—but of something else. Every time the road bent and I could see the open water, his jaw tightened. I'd seen that look before too. On men who'd left a country and couldn't stop looking back at it.

Three: he kept glancing at the sacking-wrapped jar in my basket the way a starving man glances at bread. Not greedy. Hungrier than greedy. Like it was something he'd lost.

"Ask," I said finally, when the road straightened and the walls of Brasse-les-Salines had shrunk to a gray line behind us.

"Ask what?"

"Whatever you've been chewing on since the tannery. You'll give yourself indigestion."

He was quiet for a dozen steps. The sea muttered against the rocks below. "Does it work?" he said at last. "The Door. Do you actually know, or are you chasing a story like the rest of them?"

"I know it exists. I don't know what's on the other side."

"And the salt opens it."

"So I'm told."

"By whom?"

"By a woman who died last night so I could carry it." I said it flatly, because dressing it up would have hurt more. "Odile. Hedge-witch. She lived in the salt warehouses forty years. The soldiers burned the warehouse with her in it."

Perrin walked a while without talking, which for him was like a river running backward. "I sold her tobacco," he said finally. "Twice a year, twenty years. She paid in charms. Little ones. Weather charms, mostly—kept the damp out of my boat." He rubbed his face with a tar-stained hand. "Was it quick?"

"No."

He nodded, accepting the honesty the way you accept a blow you asked for. Then: "Why you? Forty years she keeps that jar, and she gives it to a stranger who can't pay?"

"Because I'm not a stranger. Because I swore an oath to the god that jar belongs to, back when oaths to him meant something, and she said—" I stopped. The words caught like a fishhook. "She said the salt remembers its keepers. And that it was time one of them stopped hiding."

"The salt remembers," he repeated. "That's a priest's line. My grandmother used to say it. *The salt remembers what you bury in it.*" He glanced at me sideways. "You believe that? Truly? Because I've noticed that people who believe things truly tend to die of it, and I've developed a strong preference for people who believe things loosely."

"I believe the salt remembers," I said. "I've touched it. I know."

Something in my voice must have reached him, because he didn't joke. We walked. The sun climbed and found no warmth in it. Ahead, the coast road ran white and empty toward the drowned country, and behind us, faint as smoke, I could have sworn I heard bells—from the city, or from somewhere deeper than the city, ringing in a register that had no business existing.

Perrin heard it too. He stopped, head cocked, and his face—for once—had no curtain in front of it at all.

"That's not the cathedral," he said. "The cathedral's behind us."

"I know."

"Bells don't carry over open water. Everyone knows that."

"I know," I said again, and shifted the basket, and under the sacking the jar was warm—warm as a living thing, warm as a hand—and far down the white road, where the blight began and the world stopped growing, I saw dust rising. Riders. Three, maybe four, moving fast, moving the way men move when they're not following a road but following a smell.

Perrin saw them a heartbeat later. "Friends of yours?"

"Inquisitor's outriders."

"Then we're about to find out," he said, already moving, already pulling me off the road toward a fold in the dunes, "how loosely you believe things, Liora-who-isn't-Liora. Run."
