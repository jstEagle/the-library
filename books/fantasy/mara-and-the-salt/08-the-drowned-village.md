## 8. The Drowned Village

Perrin smelled it before I did.

"Salt water," he said, pulling up short on the levee road with the particular disgust of a man who has spent his life avoiding honest work. "You smell that? That's the sea getting ideas."

The sea had more than ideas. It had Sainte-Avoie.

We came over the last rise at dusk and there it was below us: a village drowned to its second stories, rooftops breaking the surface like the backs of patient animals, a church spire leaning out of the water at an angle that suggested it had given up on verticality sometime around the damming of the flats. The water was flat and gray-green and utterly still, which was the first wrong thing. The second wrong thing was the lamplight.

There shouldn't have been lamplight.

"Tell me that's a reflection," Perrin said.

"Of what? There's no sun left to reflect."

"Then tell me something comforting, because I'm a smuggler, not a ghost story, and I'd very much like to keep it that way."

I looked at the lights bobbing in the drowned windows—warm, ordinary, domestic—and felt something in my chest I hadn't felt in twenty years, which was close to homesickness, aimed at a place I'd never lived. "It's Sainte-Avoie," I said. "They flooded it when the crown dammed the flats. Moved everyone out. Or meant to."

"And the ones they didn't move?"

I nudged my horse down the levee slope. "They're keeping their routines, apparently."

* * *

We left the horses tied above the waterline and waded in to our thighs. The water was warmer than it had any right to be, and it had a texture to it, faintly gritty, faintly sweet—salt and something under the salt, like tears that had been sitting too long in a cup.

The streets were still streets. That was the thing about drowned places: water doesn't erase a street, it just makes it a different kind of corridor. We walked down the middle of what had been a market lane, past shopfronts with their shutters rotted to lace, past a well that now served the entire sea. Doorways gaped on either side of us, black and patient.

In one of them, an old woman was sweeping.

She stood in water to her waist, in a doorway that was mostly water, and she swept the water with a broom whose bristles had long since dissolved, moving it back and forth across a floor that was no longer there, and every stroke made a small sound like a sigh. She did not look at us. She had been sweeping, I understood, for thirty years, and she intended to finish.

"Don't stare," I murmured to Perrin.

"I'm not staring. I'm assessing. Professionally." He was staring. "Is she going to do that all night?"

"Probably."

"Gods." He said it the way people said it now—casually, the way you'd say *weather*, a word for a thing that had stopped showing up to work. Then, quieter: "This is the kind of place you don't come back from, you know. Places like this. They keep a piece of you."

"I'm aware."

"Are you? Because you're walking toward the church like you've got an appointment."

I did have an appointment, in the sense that the jar at my breastbone had grown warmer with every step into the water, and warmth in consecrated salt meant company. It had been warm since the warehouse burned. It had been warmer since the levee. Now it was nearly singing, a low hum against my ribs like a struck bell that refused to finish its note.

The church of Sainte-Avoie stood in what had been the village square, sunk to its shoulders. Its doors were open. They had been open, I suspected, for thirty years, waiting for a congregation that had been evacuated out from under them.

I waded up the steps and into the dark.

* * *

The nave was full of water and, less surprisingly, full of the dead.

They sat in the pews. That was the detail that undid me—not the translucence of them, not the way candlelight passed through their shoulders, but the arrangement. Families together. The widow in the front row where she'd always sat, I supposed, and the boys in the back where boys sit, and a young couple in the middle who kept leaning their heads together though their heads had nothing left to lean. They were not worshipping. They were not even present, exactly. They were *attending*, the way you attend a service in a language you've forgotten, out of habit, out of love, out of the simple animal need to be in a warm room with other people.

The water in the nave was waist-deep and perfectly calm, and the candles burned under the surface without hissing, their flames bending gently, like weeds.

"Veyl's mercy," Perrin breathed behind me. "It's a whole congregation."

"It's a drowned village," I said. "This is what's left when you move the living out and forget to move the rest."

At the altar, a figure turned.

He had been a priest once—you could see it in the way he stood, the way his hands folded, the way his vestments clung to a body that was more memory than matter. He was younger than the sweeping woman, or had died younger, and he had the particular stillness of a man who has been waiting a long time for someone specific and is not going to waste the moment being startled.

"You're carrying it under your coat," he said. His voice came through the water the way light does, bent and dimmed but arriving. "Left side. Against the heart, which is either devotion or poor planning."

"Both," I said.

He came down the altar steps, and where he passed, the candles leaned toward him. "I am Father Anselme. I was priest here for nineteen years, and then I was priest here for thirty more, which is the sort of career trajectory the crown's engineers never consider." He stopped an arm's length away. His eyes were the pale gray of weather coming. "That jar is consecrated salt. Altar-grade. Older than me, and I am very old. Where did you get it?"

"From a woman named Odile," I said. "In Brasse. The Inquisition burned her warehouse last week."

Something moved through the congregation then—not the dead themselves, but the water, a ripple passing pew to pew, as if the news had been poured in and was finding its level. The old woman in the front row turned her head, just slightly, toward the door she hadn't looked at in thirty years.

"Odile Marchetti," Father Anselme said. "She was altar girl here, before the dam. Before everything." He closed his eyes, or the space where eyes had been. "Then the salt has come home. And it has come home to *you*, which interests me more."

"Why me?"

"Because consecrated salt doesn't travel in a smuggler's coat by accident." He looked past me at Perrin, then back. "Because it's warm. I can see it from here—it's glowing through the wool like a coal. Salt only does that for two reasons. It's near the Door. Or it's near someone it remembers."

The hum against my ribs changed key. I put my hand flat over my heart, over the jar, like a child with a fever.

"It remembers me?"

"Salt remembers everything it has blessed." He said it simply, the way you'd tell a woman the tide schedule. "Every oath sworn on it. Every hand anointed. Every soldier marked before battle. It was on the altars of the old kingdom for six hundred years, Marguerite Joubert. It has been touched by more prayers than the god ever answered. And it keeps them. All of them. It's not a jar of salt. It's an archive."

Behind me, Perrin made a small noise, the noise of a man recalculating a fare. "An archive," he repeated. "The thing we've been hauling across three checkpoints is a *library*."

"Shut up, Perrin."

"I'm just saying, if I'd known—"

"You'd have charged me more. I know. Shut up."

Father Anselme watched us with something that in a living face would have been wry affection. "You were sworn," he said to me. Not a question.

"I was battle-sworn to Veyl. Twenty-two years ago. Before the silence."

"Then you should understand what I'm about to tell you, and I want you to hear it before you sleep, because you're going to sleep here—you have no choice, the water rises at night on the levee road—and the salt is going to talk to you, and it will be easier if someone has told you first what it is."

The candles all leaned at once. In the pews, the drowned congregation sat in their patient rows, and I thought: *they come here every night. This is what they do. This is all they do.* And I thought: *I have spent twenty years doing the same thing in a different building.*

"Say it," I said.

"Consecrated salt was on the altar the day you took your oath." He drifted closer, and up close he smelled—not smelled, *suggested*—incense. "It was in the oil they marked you with, or the water, or pressed into your palm when you swore. It heard every word. It has been holding your oath for twenty-two years, the way the rest of us hold our dead. And now you've brought it close, and it's warm, and you're tired, and tired people sleep, and sleeping people—" he spread his translucent hands, an entire homily in the gesture, "—dream."

"Dream what?"

"Everything it heard. Everything it blessed. The day you knelt. The day ten thousand of you knelt." His voice gentled, which was somehow worse. "The day the god stopped answering mid-prayer. You were there. It was there. It remembers there better than you do, because you've spent two decades trying to forget, and salt has never once tried anything of the kind."

The hum under my hand had become a pulse. My own pulse, or the jar's—I genuinely could not have told you which, and that frightened me more than the drowned congregation, more than the burning warehouse, more than Cassel's name on the wind.

Perrin, to his credit, had gone quiet. He stood in the aisle with water to his ribs, a professional liar in a church full of people who had run out of things to lie about, and he said, very quietly: "Is she going to be all right?"

"No," said Father Anselme, with the honesty of the dead. "But she's going to be *true*, which is rarer, and costs more."

* * *

We sheltered in the bell tower, which stood above the waterline, barely. Perrin got a fire going from driftwood and complained about it the entire time, on the principle, he explained, that complaining was the only prayer he knew. I sat with my back against the bell and the jar in my lap and did not open it, because I was suddenly, deeply afraid of what opening it would feel like.

The dead below us kept their vigil. Through the tower window I could see the sweeping woman, still at it, her dissolved broom sighing across a floor that thirty years of water had not managed to make her stop believing in.

"Your priest friend," Perrin said. "He knew your name."

"He read it off the jar, probably. Or the salt told him."

"Uh-huh. And did the salt also tell him you'd be coming, or was that a coincidence?"

"Perrin."

"I'm just establishing the terms of my employment. If I'm ferrying an archive of divine memory across a police state, I'd like it noted in the ledger." He poked the fire. "What did you swear? That day. The oath."

I almost didn't answer. Twenty years of practice said *nothing, nothing, I'm nobody, I sell charms.* But the jar was warm in my lap, and the drowned were singing below us—wordlessly, a sound like wind through rigging—and there is only so long a person can stand in a church before the truth comes out of her sideways.

"I swore to hold the line," I said. "That was the whole of it. Not to win, not to survive. *To hold the line while Veyl holds us.* Ten thousand of us, kneeling in the mud at Vaucourt, saying it together. And he was answering. You could feel it, the way you feel heat from a hearth. And then—" I turned the jar over in my hands. "Mid-prayer. Mid-*word*. Like a man struck dumb at his own altar. And ten thousand sworn soldiers stayed kneeling in the mud because no one had told us what to do instead. We didn't have instructions for a god going silent. Nobody had ever written that drill."

Perrin said nothing for a while. The fire cracked. Below, the congregation sighed.

"My mother drowned in this village," he said at last, conversationally, the way he said everything. "Not in the flooding. Before. In the harbor, when there was a harbor. I was six." He nodded at the water below, at the lights, at the woman still sweeping. "Sometimes I think if I waded down there I'd find her in a pew. And I think that's why I never come here, and also why I came the moment you said the name." He shrugged. "People keep their routines. Even the dead. Especially the dead."

"Especially the dead," I agreed.

"And you? What's your routine? Twenty years of luck-knots and small lies. That's a routine. That's a woman sweeping a floor that isn't there anymore."

I looked at him. He looked at the fire, insufferably calm, a man who had just compared me to a ghost and was waiting to see if I'd argue.

The jar pulsed against my palms, warm as a sleeping animal, warm as a hearth, warm as the answer that had stopped mid-word twenty-two years ago. And I was so tired. I had been tired for twenty years, and tired was a routine too.

"Sleep," I said. "I'll take first watch."

"You're lying."

"Obviously. But you look worse than I do."

He grinned and lay down on the bell tower floor, and within minutes he was asleep, the professional liar, trusting me completely, which was the most dishonest thing either of us had done all day.

I sat with the jar. The candles below leaned. The congregation sang its wordless hymn, and the sweeping woman swept, and somewhere out on the black water a bell that had no rope rang once, softly, as if remembering how.

I put my hand flat on the jar's lid. Just resting it there. Just holding the line.

The salt remembered.

And it began, gently, patiently, in the voice of ten thousand kneeling soldiers and one god going quiet mid-word, to tell me everything I had spent twenty years refusing to know.
