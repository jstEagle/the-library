## 9. What the Salt Remembers

I want to say I touched the jar on purpose.

That's the version I'd tell across a market stall, if anyone ever asked: *I knew what I was doing, I braced myself, I went in like a soldier.* But the truth is that I woke in the dark with my hand already wrapped around it, the way you wake with your hand around a knife when something's been circling the camp, and I couldn't remember reaching for it. My fingers had decided for me. Twenty years of discipline, of never touching anything consecrated, of keeping my gift folded up small as a moth in a matchbox—and my own hand had gone and broken the rule while I slept, because hands are traitors, because they remember oaths the mind has buried under two decades of luck-knots and small change.

The jar was warm. Not fire-warm. Skin-warm. The warmth of something that had been waiting to be held.

"Oh no," I said, out loud, to an empty boathouse in a drowned village, "no, absolutely not—"

And then the salt took me.

* * *

You should understand what it's like, being pulled into memory by consecrated salt. It is not like dreaming. Dreams have the decency to be vague at the edges, to let you float. This was the opposite of floating. This was being poured into a mold that fit too well.

Mud first. I was kneeling in mud before I understood I had knees, and the mud was the famous mud of the Veyrine campaign—the kind that sucked your boots off if you stood still too long, the kind we joked about because joking was cheaper than thinking about what we were there to do. Rain came down in ropes. Ten thousand of us knelt in it, rank upon rank upon rank, because Commander Aurel Vaissac had given the order to kneel, and you did not disobey Aurel Vaissac, and more importantly you did not interrupt him.

He was praying.

I could see him from where I knelt—third rank, left wing, standard-bearer's position, which meant I got the best view of every disaster we ever walked into. He stood on the low rise with his arms open and his face turned up into the rain, and he was praying the Long Intercession, the one we only said before battles we weren't sure we'd walk away from. His voice carried the way it always did, that strange carrying quality that made a whisper reach the back ranks, and ten thousand soldiers murmured along with him, *Veyl who walks in salt, Veyl who keeps the tally, Veyl who remembers our names*—

And I felt it happen.

I don't know how else to describe it. It wasn't a sound stopping. It was like standing next to a river all your life and then, between one heartbeat and the next, the river simply isn't there—not dried up, not dammed, just *gone*, as though it had never been a river at all, as though you'd been kneeling beside a groove in the earth and calling it water. The prayer kept going. Vaissac's voice kept going. But the thing the prayer had been *landing on*—the vast patient attention we'd all been speaking into since we were children, the presence at the other end of every oath—wasn't there anymore.

Ten thousand people noticed at once. You could feel it move through the ranks like wind through wheat, that terrible collective flinch. Somewhere behind me a man started screaming the prayer instead of saying it, louder and louder, as if volume could bridge the gap. Vaissac's arms stayed open. His face stayed turned up. And I watched—I will always be watching—the exact moment he understood, because his mouth kept moving through three more lines of the Intercession before it stopped, and in those three lines his whole face aged thirty years.

Then the screaming started in earnest, and the kneeling broke, and the battle we'd been about to fight arrived early, and everything after that is the part I've spent twenty years not remembering.

The salt remembered it for me anyway. All of it. The mud. The boy from the fourth rank who knelt back down in the middle of the rout and wouldn't get up, just knelt there praying to nothing with arrows coming down like weather. Vaissac walking untouched through the melee afterward, not fighting, not fleeing, just walking, looking at faces, as if he were counting. As if he were taking attendance for a god who no longer kept the register.

I tried to pull out. That's the humiliating part—you can struggle inside your own memory and lose. I clawed at the edges of it the way you claw at a nightmare, and the salt held me gently, firmly, the way you hold someone having a seizure so they don't crack their head on the floor. *No,* said the salt, without words, without anything so crude as words. *You put this in me. You swore this into me. Look at it.*

So I looked.

* * *

There was more. Of course there was more. The salt didn't stop at the battle; it kept unwinding backward, showing me things I'd forgotten I'd forgotten. My swearing-in at fourteen, the salt pressed to my tongue, cold as a coin from a well. My mother's hands tying the first knot of my luck-charm, her fingers moving in a pattern I now realized—with a lurch that had nothing to do with the vision—was older than any rite of Veyl's church. Older. The salt showed me that. Showed me hands doing that same knot-work in kitchens and birthing rooms and field hospitals for generations before anyone thought to build a god on top of it.

*We were here first,* the salt said, or didn't say, but meant, the way stone means. *Your family. We were keepers before we were soldiers.*

I didn't want to know that. I want to be honest about that too, since I'm apparently being honest tonight. There is a particular horror in learning that the thing you hid for twenty years wasn't even yours—that you were guarding an heirloom you never agreed to inherit, that your whole careful small life was a kind of unpaid custodianship. I had built a philosophy out of smallness. Small magic, small risks, small deniable charms sold to frightened fishermen. And the salt sat there in my hands like a deed to property I'd never seen, signed by grandmothers whose names I didn't know, and it said: *this was never about hiding. This was about keeping.*

*Keeping what?* I asked it, or screamed it, I genuinely couldn't tell you which.

The answer was the silence itself. It showed me the silence from the inside—which sounds impossible, but salt was there when it happened, salt was in the ground and in the wounds and in the tears of ten thousand kneeling soldiers, and salt does not forget what it has witnessed. And what I saw, or felt, or whatever verb applies to being shown the death of a god by his own grave-goods, was this:

It hadn't been abandonment. It hadn't been heaven closing a door. It had been consumption. Slow, pious, generations-deep consumption. Every prayer a mouthful. Every oath a swallow. The church had fed Veyl their devotions the way you feed anything you love, faithfully, daily, without ever asking whether the thing being fed wanted to be a meal—and the god had grown vast and hollow and finally, quietly, had been eaten down to the walls. What went silent mid-prayer wasn't a god turning away. It was a god running out.

I came back to myself the way a drowning person comes back to the surface—all at once, violently, with no dignity whatsoever.

* * *

I woke sobbing. Not crying—sobbing, the ugly full-body kind, the kind you can't perform or suppress, hunched over the jar with my arms around it like it was a child I'd failed. The boathouse was dark except for one shuttered lantern and the grey seam of pre-dawn under the door. My face was soaked. My throat hurt. Somewhere in the last however-many-hours I had apparently slid off the pallet onto the floor, and my knee had a bruise forming to show for it.

"Marguerite."

Perrin. He was sitting against the wall by the door, cross-legged, a knife loose in his lap in the way he holds knives when he's relaxed, which is to say never entirely. He must have heard everything. The sobbing, certainly. Possibly the parts where I talked—because I had talked, I could feel the rawness in my throat, I'd been narrating the vision aloud like a madwoman giving testimony to an empty court.

"How long," I managed.

"You went under around midnight." He tilted his head toward the grey line under the door. "It's near dawn. Four hours, maybe five. I counted your breathing for the first hour, then stopped, because it stopped being breathing and started being something else, and I decided I didn't want to know."

"Something else," I repeated. My voice sounded like gravel dragged through a swamp.

"You were talking in a voice that wasn't yours." He said it flatly, the way he reports the price of contraband. No drama. Perrin treats drama like spoilage—he won't carry it. "Deeper. Slower. Old-fashioned, like a priest at a funeral. I've heard smugglers fake possession to raise their rates. This wasn't that." A pause. "Was it?"

I looked down at the jar. In the lantern light the salt inside had settled into patterns I didn't remember it having—lines, whorls, almost like script, almost like the knot-patterns my mother's hands used to make. I made myself look away from it.

"No," I said. "It wasn't that."

"Do you want to tell me?"

"Do you want to hear it?"

"No," Perrin said honestly. "But I'm going to be traveling with you and that thing, and the Inquisition wants both of you badly enough to burn warehouses for it, and I find I lie better to people when I know what I'm lying about. So yes. Tell me."

So I told him. Not all of it—I kept my mother's hands and the knot-work to myself, some inheritance is private—but I told him about the battle, the silence, the feeling of the river that had never been a river. And I told him the worst part, the part I'd have paid good money not to believe: that the god hadn't left. That we'd eaten him. That every hymn had been a bite.

Perrin listened the way he does everything—without wasting motion. When I finished he was quiet for a long moment, turning his knife over once, twice.

"My grandmother," he said finally, "used to leave a bowl of salt on the windowsill every night. Never said why. When I asked, she cuffed me and said it wasn't for me." He shrugged. "I thought it was a superstition. Fisherman's nonsense."

"It might still be."

"No." He shook his head slowly. "Fisherman's nonsense doesn't make a woman talk in a dead priest's voice for four hours. Whatever's in that jar, it's not nonsense, and it's not finished with you." He stood, joints cracking, and began kicking dirt over the lantern coals. "Which means we're leaving. Now. Before daylight. Because whatever you saw in there, the people hunting you saw the outside of it, and I promise you they're asking the drowned villagers questions this morning, and drowned villagers answer questions."

He was right, which was annoying, because I'd been hoping to sit on the floor and fall apart for another hour or two. Instead I got up, washed my face from the rain barrel, and rewrapped the jar in its oilcloth with hands that only shook a little.

At the door, Perrin paused. "One question," he said. "In there. Whatever spoke through you. Did it have a name?"

I thought about lying to him. We were, after all, two professionals.

"It had mine," I said.

* * *

We went out through the back of the boathouse, down the flooded lane where the ghost-lights of Sainte-Avoie burned low and green above the waterline, the dead keeping their old routines—someone's laundry line strung between two drowned chimneys, sheets that weren't quite sheets stirring in air that wasn't quite air. They watched us pass. They always watch. But this morning they watched differently, and when I looked back, the old priest was standing on the surface of the flooded square, and he raised one translucent hand—not in blessing, I realized. In warning.

Behind him, far off across the flats toward Brasse-les-Salines, the sky had begun to glow. Not sunrise. Sunrise comes from the east, and this glow was west, city-colored, orange and flickering.

They were burning something. Or someone.

And the salt against my chest pulsed once, warm as a heartbeat, as if to say: *faster.*
