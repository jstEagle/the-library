## 10. The Blight Road

I have smuggled a lot of things in my life. Brandy, gunpowder, a bishop's mistress, once an entire choir of contraband hymnals sewn into salt hides. I have never before smuggled hope, and let me tell you: it is the worst cargo there is. It's heavy. It leaks. It gets into everything.

Marguerite Joubert carried hers like she carried everything else—close to the body, where you couldn't see it unless you knew to look.

We'd been on the blight road two days when the first of them appeared ahead of us on the horizon. A figure, then three figures, then a smear of figures that resolved, as we crested the long dead rise above what had once been the Vasseur wheat country, into a line of people walking north. Dozens of them. Then, over the next hour, hundreds.

"Well," I said. "Either the Door is real, or somebody's selling something very good."

"Both can be true," Marguerite said.

"That's the most honest thing anyone's said to me all week."

She didn't laugh. She never laughed at my best material, which I had decided was either a character flaw or a survival strategy, and knowing her, both.

The road itself deserves description, because the road is the whole argument of this chapter of my life, if my life turns out to have chapters. The blight didn't look like fire. Fire at least leaves you something to mourn in recognizable shapes—a beam, a wall, a child's shoe. The blight left nothing. The fields were white going gray going a color I don't have a name for, the color of old teeth, of things that have given up being things. Fence posts stood like gallows with nothing to hang. A farm cart sat in a field forty years from any road, its wheels sunk to the hubs in crusted salt, and no farmer anywhere near it, and I found myself inventing stories about that cart for the better part of a morning because the alternative was thinking about the fact that nothing grew here, not weeds, not lichen, not the little pale mushrooms that even a crypt will grow if you leave it alone long enough.

"Salt-blight," Marguerite said, when I asked her about it. "When the god went silent, the consecration went out of the land. Salt remembers what it blessed. When the blessing left—" She made a small gesture, opening her hand, letting something invisible go.

"And this is what, twenty years of that?"

"Twenty years."

"And nobody fixed it."

"Nobody knew how."

I looked out at the gray country. "Somebody should have tried."

She gave me one of her looks—the kind that makes you feel like a child who has just suggested the army could be defeated by asking nicely. "You think people haven't tried?"

"I think," I said, "that I've spent twenty years assuming the world outside Brasse was fine, actually, and this is making me feel like a fool."

"You're not a fool, Perrin."

"No," I agreed. "I'm a professional. There's a difference, but it's smaller than I used to think."

* * *

Here is what I knew about Marguerite Joubert when I took her contract: she paid half up front, she walked like a soldier pretending to be a widow, and she wanted to go somewhere that officially did not exist, along a route that officially did not exist, toward a Door that officially did not exist. In my trade, "officially does not exist" usually means "exists, but the paperwork would embarrass someone." I priced accordingly. She paid without haggling, which should have been my second warning. People who haggle plan to survive long enough to regret the price. People who pay full freight are spending money they don't expect to need.

My third warning came the night we sheltered in the drowned village, though I didn't understand it until later. I woke in the dark and she was sitting up, holding a clay jar against her chest like a child, and she was crying—not loudly, not dramatically, just crying the way rain comes through a bad roof, steadily, in the place you least prepared for it. I pretended to sleep. It seemed polite. Also I had learned by then that Marguerite Joubert pretending to be fine was like the sea pretending to be flat: technically possible, briefly, in certain weather.

So when the refugees started appearing on the blight road, I watched her watch them, and I understood that whatever she was carrying in that jar, these people were carrying it too—in their chests, in their stories, in the rumors they fed each other like starving men feeding each other pictures of bread.

* * *

We fell in with the column on the second day, because there was no longer any way around it. The road funneled everyone the same direction, north and west, toward the border wall and the flats beyond, and the refugees moved with the particular grim patience of people who have already lost everything except the walking.

Their name for it was the same everywhere: the Door. Some said it was in the old quarry at Cernay. Some said under the wall itself, a seam in the stones that opened only for the innocent. An old man told me his grandmother had seen it as a girl, black glass standing alone in the salt like a doorframe with no house, and that her father had touched it and come back changed, quieter, kinder, and had died within the year smiling. A woman with twin boys on her hips said the crown knew about it and that was why the purge had started—to keep the gifted from leaving, because a country that loses its magic stops being a country and becomes just ground.

"They're not wrong about that last part," Marguerite murmured.

"Don't encourage them," I murmured back. "Hope is expensive. I know the market."

But I talked to them anyway, because talking to people is what I do instead of praying, and I heard the same story wearing forty different coats: the Inquisition had come to their town, or was coming, or had come to the next town over, and someone had been registered, or burned, or had simply vanished between one evening mass and the next, and so they had gathered the children and whatever wouldn't burn and started walking toward the one rumor left in the country that hadn't been taxed, licensed, or outlawed yet.

A girl of maybe fifteen walked beside me for an hour. She had a charm around her neck—a luck-knot, badly tied, the work of some amateur—and when I asked where she got it she said her mother made them, before. Before what, I didn't ask. There's a whole dictionary of meaning in "before" now, and everyone speaks it fluently.

"Is it true?" she asked me. "The Door. Is it true?"

Now. Here is a professional question. I have lied to customs officers, husbands, magistrates, and one memorable archdeacon, and I lie well, because lying is mostly listening—you find out what a person needs to hear and you sell it to them at a fair markup. And this girl needed to hear yes. Her feet needed yes. Her mother's charm needed yes.

"It's true there's something out there," I said, which was true, because I could feel it against Marguerite's back every time she shifted on the march—that jar, that weight, that cargo of hers. "Whether it's a door or something else, I couldn't say. But there's something."

She nodded, satisfied, and dropped back to her family, and I felt like a man who has sold a drowning stranger a rope and told him it's a ladder.

"Perrin." Marguerite had come up beside me without sound, which she did; twenty years of hiding teaches you to walk like an apology. "That was almost honest."

"Don't get used to it."

"What happens to them?" she said quietly, looking down the column—hundreds of heads, thousands of feet, the whole sad procession of a country that had eaten itself. "If the Door opens for one. If it opens for none. What happens to all of them?"

"Ah," I said. "See, this is why I charge extra for hope. You've got to account for the returns."

She didn't answer. She was doing arithmetic behind her eyes—I saw it, the same cold tallying look soldiers get before a battle, counting mouths and measures—and I thought, with the particular clarity of a man watching a storm front come in over flat water: she knows how much is in that jar, and she knows how many people are on this road, and those two numbers are not friends.

I decided not to think about it. I'm excellent at deciding not to think about things. It's most of my personality.

* * *

The camp was at a crossroads called, according to a leaning signpost, Saint-Rémy-sous-Salin, though there was no saint visible and precious little anything else. Maybe four hundred people had stopped there—stopped, not settled, because settling implies a future—tents of sailcloth and salt-hide, cook fires burning the last scrubby peat cut from the ditches, children running between the fires in a game whose rules I couldn't follow but which involved a great deal of shrieking, bless them.

We didn't mean to stop. Marguerite wanted to move through, keep distance, stay a shadow among shadows. But the column had slowed to a crawl, and dusk came down like a lid, and there is a limit to what even the guilty will ask of their feet.

"We're two days out," she said, crouched by our own small fire at the camp's edge. "Maybe less. If we walk before dawn—"

"If we walk before dawn we'll be walking past Cassel's outriders in the dark," I said. "Smoke travels far out here. Sound farther. Every rider between here and the wall knows four hundred people are camping at Saint-Rémy like it's a festival."

"They won't burn a refugee camp."

I looked at her. She looked back, and I watched her arrive, in real time, at the same place I'd already arrived, which is that of course they would. Of course they would. I've watched crowns do arithmetic before. Four hundred people who believe in a magic door are not four hundred citizens; they are four hundred rumors with legs, and rumors are the one cargo a frightened government always burns.

"We should warn them," she said.

"With what? 'Good evening, friends, the Inquisition may ride through tonight, do consider dispersing into the featureless salt desert'?" I shook my head. "They know. Everyone on this road knows. They're walking anyway. That's the whole point of them."

"That's the whole tragedy of them, you mean."

"I don't say tragedies," I said. "Bad for business. Tragedies don't tip."

She almost smiled. It was the closest thing to warmth either of us had produced in days, and I was still savoring it when the dogs started barking.

* * *

You hear riders before you see them at night—the ground carries it, a tremor in your back teeth. Then the first torches crested the southern rise, and the camp went quiet in that terrible way, the silence of animals, mothers reaching for children mid-shriek, the game ending all at once across four hundred yards of scrubland.

There weren't many of them. Eight riders, maybe ten, torches on lances, and at their head a banner I knew from twenty years ago and had hoped never to see again: the sun-and-key of the Inquisition, stitched in gold thread that probably cost more than the horses.

"Outriders," Marguerite breathed. "Screening force. Where's the main—"

And then the first torch hit the tents.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left of that night. They didn't charge. They didn't cut anyone down. They rode the camp's perimeter, unhurried, and threw fire into the sailcloth, and the wind off the salt did the rest. That was the horror of it—the leisure of it. A sergeant's voice calling out, cheerful as a market crier: *"Disperse! Disperse in the king's name! Any person bearing charms or relics will be taken!"* And the camp coming apart like a kicked anthill, families scattering into the dark with bundles and children and that awful low sound a crowd makes when it isn't screaming yet but knows it's about to.

A woman ran past us with one boy by the hand and no second boy, and she was turning in circles, turning, calling a name into the smoke. Two old men carried a third between them, and the third was laughing, actually laughing, high and wrong, the laugh of a mind that had found the exit ahead of the body. The girl with the luck-knot—I saw her, I swear on every god including the dead one, I saw her—she stood rooted near the center of the camp, staring at the burning tents, and her hand was closed around her mother's charm, and she wasn't running.

Marguerite was moving before I finished saying her name.

I'll give the professional's account, since the other account is not fit for narration. She went low, through the smoke, the way soldiers move, and she reached the girl, and she didn't argue with her—there's no arguing with that kind of paralysis—she simply took her wrist and pulled, and the girl's legs remembered their job, and the two of them came out of the smoke on the north side while a rider wheeled past close enough that I could see the torchlight on his visor. And Marguerite put the girl's hand into her mother's hands—no, into another woman's arms, her mother was elsewhere, I don't know where, that's part of what I carry now—and turned back toward the fire line, and I grabbed her arm and said, "There's more of them, we can't save all of them," and she looked at me with an expression I will see when I close my eyes for the rest of my life, whenever that turns out to be.

"I know," she said. "I know that. Help me anyway."

So I did. I'm not going to dress it up as heroism. I hauled a family out of a collapsing tent and pointed three hundred people north and screamed at strangers until my voice went, and somewhere in the middle of it I stopped being a smuggler watching cargo and became a man in a burning camp, and I hated it, I hated every minute, because cynicism was armor and I could feel it melting off me like wax.

By midnight the camp was a red stain on the dark and the outriders were gone, having done their work in under an hour, tidy as tax collectors. We walked north with the survivors strung out behind us, a mile of scattered humanity under the stars, and nobody spoke. What was there to say? *The crown burns its own faithful now. Walk faster.*

* * *

Near dawn we stopped on a low rise and looked back. The glow was still visible, a bruise on the southern horizon. Somewhere behind us, the main column of the Inquisition was coming up the road we'd have to share tomorrow.

Marguerite stood with the jar strapped against her chest, watching the glow, and I watched her, and I finally said the thing I'd been not-thinking for two days.

"How much salt is in that jar?"

She didn't turn. "Enough."

"Enough for what? Say the word, Marguerite. Enough for what?"

The pause lasted long enough for the eastern sky to start going gray. Behind us, the ragged line of survivors stretched away into the dark, hundreds of them, thousands by now, everyone in the country who had heard the rumor and bet their feet on it.

"Enough," she said at last, "for what it was made for."

And that, dear reader, whoever you are, wherever the maps put you now—that was the moment I understood I wasn't smuggling a woman to a door. I was smuggling a lit fuse toward a powder store, and the only question left was how many of us were standing inside the blast radius when it went.

The glow faded. The column behind us grew. We walked north.
