## 12. Cassel Closes In

*They tell you to write a confession before the Inquisition comes, so that your sins are sorted and your soul is tidy. This is mine. I am writing it in my head, because they took my pencil, and I am telling it crooked, because that is how I know it.*

*My name is Perrin Vasse. I am a smuggler, which is a priest of a different order, and everything I say should be weighed accordingly.*

* * *

The first thing you learn on the coast road is that checkpoints are not walls. They are questions, and every question has a price. I have paid that price in coin, in flattery, in one memorable case in a crate of pickled eels, and I have crossed the Fish Gate at dawn more times than I have attended any service to any god, silent or otherwise. So when I saw the lanterns strung across the estuary road at an hour when honest men are still dreaming, I did what any professional does.

I turned around.

Slowly. A man who turns around quickly at a checkpoint is a man with something to hide. A man who turns around slowly is a man remembering he left his stove unlit. There is a whole grammar to it, and I am fluent.

Except the lanterns were not at the checkpoint. They were behind me too. They had been behind me for some time, and I had been so busy admiring my own cleverness—slipping Marguerite Joubert past two garrisons and a toll bridge, pointing her down the salt road toward a Door I still half believed was a story old women tell—that I had failed to notice I was being herded like a fat goose toward Michaelmas.

I put my hand on the mule's neck. Bessie, bless her, was the only creature on that road with any sense, and she had gone stiff-eared and still, the way animals do when they smell something older than fear.

"Evening," said a voice out of the dark, conversational as a neighbor borrowing sugar. "You'll be Perrin Vasse."

Now, there are two responses to hearing your full name spoken by a stranger in the dark. The first is to deny it. The second is to deny it convincingly. I chose neither, because between the lanterns I could see the gray cloaks, and the gray cloaks were not wearing the livery of the crown. They wore no livery at all, which is the Inquisition's way of saying their authority comes from somewhere higher than a king, namely from a god who hasn't spoken in twenty years, which struck me then, as it strikes me now, as a curious sort of credential.

"I might be," I said. "Depends who's asking."

A man stepped forward into the light. He was not what I expected. I had built Inquisitor Cassel out of campfire stories: tall, gaunt, eyes like nails. What stood in front of me was broad, weathered, ordinary-looking, with the face of a man who had once carried heavy things for a living and now carried heavier ones for free. His hands were wrong, though. Soldiers' hands rest. His never did. They hung at his sides like they were waiting for orders.

"You're asking yourself why we stopped you," he said, "and not the other travelers on this road."

"It hadn't escaped my notice."

"Because the others are going somewhere." He said it without malice, which somehow made it worse. "You're coming back. Nobody rides toward the coast at night unless they've already delivered what they were carrying."

I want it noted, in whatever ledger they keep, that I admired him in that moment. It takes a particular kind of mind to read a man's direction of travel and call it a confession.

"I'm a salt merchant," I said. "I go where the salt is."

"In the dark. Alone. With a mule carrying no salt." He glanced at Bessie's empty panniers, and Bessie, traitor that she is, looked away. "Inquisitor Cassel," he said, though I hadn't asked. "And you're going to help me find my friend."

* * *

They took me to a farmhouse that had been requisitioned, which is a word the crown uses when it means stolen, and sat me at a table that had been scrubbed so hard the grain stood up in ridges like scar tissue. Two soldiers by the door. Cassel across from me. On the table between us: a lamp, a jug of water, two cups, and nothing else. No instruments. No irons. I have seen enough interrogations—mostly from the outside, thank all forgotten gods—to know that the absence of tools is itself a tool.

He poured water into both cups. Pushed one across.

"You're not under arrest," he said.

"No?"

"Not yet. Arrest implies charges. You haven't done anything yet." He folded his hands. "You helped a fugitive cross two garrisons. That's not a crime yet either. It becomes one the moment I decide it is, and I'd rather decide it isn't."

"That's generous."

"It's practical." He drank. Set the cup down with the care of a man who treats even cups as things that can be lost. "Perrin Vasse. Smuggler. Eleven years on the coast road. No family, no guild, no loyalties anyone can name—which is its own kind of loyalty, I suppose, loyalty to the open door." He looked up. "I'm not here to threaten you. Threats make men stubborn. I'm here to make you a trade."

"Water for water?"

"Her location. And in exchange—" he spread his hands, and for a moment the soldier's stillness dropped away and I saw something underneath, something tired and enormous, like a man standing in front of a landslide he believed he could argue with— "you walk away. Free passage. Your mule. Your route. Nobody remembers your name. You go back to being nobody, which I understand is a profession you've practiced longer than smuggling."

I laughed. I couldn't help it. It came out wrong, high and thin, the laugh of a man whose professional composure has just been professionally dismantled.

"And if I don't?"

"Then I arrest you, and the process begins, and the process is not cruel, Master Vasse, but it is thorough, and thoroughness is worse." He said it the way you'd describe weather. "I don't want that. Genuinely. I have spent twenty years learning to distinguish between the guilty and the frightened, and you are not guilty. You're a man who was paid, or will be paid, or hoped to be paid, and there is no sin in that. There's barely even a choice in it. Men like you survive. That's what men like you are *for*."

And there it was. The hook, baited with my own self-image, lowered gently into the water. Because he was right. That's the filthy truth of it—he was right. I have survived eleven years precisely by knowing which way the wind blows and being gone before it changes. I sold charms in a city where charms were illegal; I moved people past checkpoints where moving people was treason; I have lied so long and so fluently that lying is not something I do but something I am made of, the way Bessie is made of hair and bad temper.

Marguerite Joubert is a woman I have known for four days.

Four days. One shared fire. One conversation about the price of silence in which she overpaid for my discretion and I undercharged for my company, and both of us knew it, and neither of us said it. She called me a liar to my face and meant it as a compliment. She slept sitting up with her back against a wall and her hand on a knife, and in the morning she thanked me for the watch as if the watch had been a kindness rather than a transaction.

She is not my friend. She is cargo. Expensive, difficult, haunted cargo, and the sensible thing—the thing I have done a hundred times—is to sell her location and walk away with my skin and my route intact.

So I told him where she was going.

* * *

Here is where I ask whoever reads this—Cassel, presumably, or whatever gray-cloaked clerk inherits his papers—to pay attention, because this is the part that matters, and I will only say it once, and I will say it plainly, without the usual varnish.

I told him she was heading for the fen crossings at Marest. East. Toward the old toll road, the drowned causeway, the route every fleeing gifted soul has taken since the purges began, because it is the route that makes sense, and sense is the best lie there is. I gave him details. I gave her a head start measured in hours, a boatman's name I invented on the spot—a real boatman, mind, but a dead one, drowned three winters back, which means Cassel's men will spend a day discovering that Guillaume Tessier cannot ferry anyone anywhere—and I gave it all with the steady eyes and easy breathing of a man telling the truth.

Eleven years of practice. Every lie I ever told was rehearsal for that table.

Cassel listened. He didn't interrupt. When I finished, he was quiet for a long moment, looking at the water in his cup, and I thought: he knows. Of course he knows. He read my direction of travel off a road; he'll read the lie off my face.

But he didn't. Or—if he did—he set it aside, and I have thought about that a great deal since, sitting here in this room with the grain of the table under my fingers, and I believe I finally understand why.

He wanted it to be true. Not because it served him. Because if I had sold her honestly, cheaply, the way men like me are *for*, then his world stayed assembled. The world where faith is a debt and obedience is payment and everyone, eventually, chooses survival—that world held together as long as someone like me confirmed it. My lie wasn't a failure of his interrogation. It was a mercy I did him without meaning to. I let him keep believing that people are what he needs them to be.

He stood. Offered his hand across the table, and I shook it, because refusing would have cost more than shaking.

"Marest," he said. "The fen crossings. You understand that if this proves false, I'll find you again, and the next conversation won't have water in it."

"I understand."

"For what it's worth—" and here his voice did something I didn't expect, dropped into something almost gentle, almost grieving— "she was the best of us. Before. Whatever she's told you about those days, halve the glory and double the grief, and you'll be close." He paused at the door. "Pray for her, Master Vasse. Even if you don't believe anyone's listening. Especially then."

And he left me alone with two cups of water and the growing understanding of what I had actually done—not sold her out, not saved myself, but bought something with the only coin I had left that was worth anything: two days. Maybe three, if the ghost of Guillaume Tessier holds his grave a while longer. Two days for a woman with a jar of salt and a story about a Door, to reach a Door, while the most patient man in the country walks east into a fen.

Two days. I have smuggled contraband, fugitives, and once, God forgive me, a bishop. But I have never smuggled time before. It turns out it's the one cargo that gets lighter the further you carry it, until you arrive at the far end holding nothing at all.

* * *

They locked me in the farmhouse's back room, which has a window barred with wood so old I could probably chew through it given a week and sufficient motivation. I have a blanket, a bucket, and a view of the yard where the gray cloaks are saddling up even now, torchlight jumping on their buckles, Cassel among them checking a map by lamplight, east, always east, toward the fens and the lie I fed him.

I should feel afraid. I am, a little. But mostly I feel something I don't have a word for, and I am a man with a very large vocabulary of disreputable words. It's the feeling of having finally, after forty-one years, told the truth—and the truth being that I care what happens to her. Four days, one fire, and I have gone and done the one thing my entire life was architected to prevent: I have made myself matter to an outcome.

There's a charm I once sold a fisherman—back in Brasse, in the market, in another life—a luck-knot, three strands braided against drowning. I told him it worked. I told every customer it worked. Here is the confession beneath the confession: I never once believed it. Magic was outlawed, gods went quiet, and a luck-knot is just string with good marketing.

But tonight I took the spare cord from my boot—I kept one cord, always, the way a priest keeps a relic—and I braided three strands together in the dark, badly, with cold fingers, and I tied it around my wrist, and I said the words I used to say to customers, the little blessing, the nonsense syllables, the theater of it.

And I swear on every god I've ever robbed that the cord felt warm.

Maybe it's the fever coming on. Maybe it's the room. Maybe it's a silent god reaching through twenty years of quiet to pat a smuggler on the wrist for a lie well told. I don't know. I've stopped needing to know. That's what she taught me in four days, without trying, probably without noticing: that belief isn't a wager you place when the odds are good. It's what's left when you've already lost everything you were protecting, and you braid the cord anyway.

They'll come back for me when the fen crossing proves false. I give it two days, maybe less if Cassel's patience is shorter than his reputation. And when he comes, he'll ask me again, and this time there won't be a lie ready, because I've spent the only one that mattered.

So let the record show: Perrin Vasse, smuggler, liar, coward by profession, chose wrong. Chose the woman over the exit. Chose the fire he shared over the road he kept. If there's a pyre in my future, let them use good wood—I've earned at least that much vanity—and let someone, someday, tell her what it cost.

Not because she owes me. Because I'd like the arithmetic to come out even, just once, in a country where nothing has balanced in twenty years.

The torches are moving out the gate now, east along the estuary road, a string of small fires going away into the dark. Two days. Hold that thought, Marguerite. Walk fast, sleep little, trust the salt.

And if you ever learn what I paid—

Spend it well.
