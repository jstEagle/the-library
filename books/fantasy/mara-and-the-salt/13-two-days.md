## 13. Two Days

Perrin had bought me two days with a lie, which was the most honest thing anyone had done for me in twenty years.

I counted them the way you count anything that's running out: in blisters and in light. The first day began grey and ended white, because out on the flats there is no dusk to speak of—just the sun going down behind the wall of haze like a coin dropped into milk. I walked. The jar rode against my sternum under my coat, wrapped in Odile's shawl, warm as a sleeping bird or a small fever. I tried not to think about how it was warm. Jars of salt are not supposed to be warm.

The coordinates were scratched into my forearm in Odile's hand, done with her last strength and a splinter of char: *three days north-northeast past the Blight Road, where the glass shows black.* She'd pressed so hard she'd broken skin, and the letters had scabbed into me like a brand. I suppose if you're going to die for someone, you may as well leave them directions.

"North-northeast," I told the empty salt. "Past the drowned church, past where the road gives up pretending."

The salt said nothing, which was, as I would learn, only its way of clearing its throat.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being alone after years of being careful: the carefulness doesn't stop. It just turns inward. Twenty years in Brasse-les-Salines I had made myself small enough to fit through the eye of an ordinary life—a widow's stall, a widow's rooms, a widow's hands that sold luck-knots and nothing else, madame, certainly nothing else. And now that every eye in the country wanted those hands on a pyre, the habit of hiding remained, ticking away like a clock in an empty house.

So when I heard footsteps behind me on the first afternoon, I did not turn around. I drifted left toward a ridge of crusted brine the color of old bone, put it between us, and walked faster without appearing to walk faster. This is a mercenary skill. You would be amazed how much of soldiering is just lying with your legs.

The footsteps kept pace.

I stopped at a sinkhole rimmed with dead reeds and pretended to adjust my bootlaces, and in the warped mirror of the brine pool I saw him.

Commander Aurel Vasseur. Dead eleven years. Walking forty paces back with his helmet under his arm like a gentleman caller.

Now. A reasonable woman would have said: grief, heat, hunger, the beginning of a very bad afternoon. A reasonable woman would have drunk from her waterskin, said something rude to the sky, and kept walking. But I had spent the night before touching a jar that remembered everything it had ever blessed, including me, and I knew—I knew the way you know a scar is yours—that this was not madness. Or not only madness. Madness was in the mix, certainly; madness adds body and depth like good stock. But underneath it was something older, looking out through my dead commander's face the way you look out through a window you've found unlocked.

"You're not him," I said, without turning around.

"No," said the thing wearing Vasseur pleasantly. "But he's here. They're all here. That's rather the point."

I walked faster. He kept pace. We went on like that for half a league, a woman fleeing a conversation and a dead man having it anyway, until the light failed and I made camp in the lee of a salt hummock with my back to a stone that had once, according to the maps that no longer existed, been part of a chapel wall.

He sat down across the fire I didn't light. Fires draw eyes; eyes draw Cassel. So we shared an unlit fire in the dark, the ghost and I, which felt fitting. Most of my prayers had gone unanswered in better company.

"You knelt," he said. "At Redmarch. Ten thousand of us, and you knelt fastest."

"I knelt first," I corrected. "There's a difference. First is faith. Fastest is fear."

"And which was it?"

"Both." I pulled my coat tighter. "It was both. It was always both. That's what they don't understand—the Inquisitors, the ones who burn us. Faith isn't pure. It never was. It's fear with somewhere to go."

Vasseur smiled with someone else's patience. "And now it has nowhere to go. So it walks north-northeast."

I didn't answer. Out on the flats, the wind moved the crust with a sound like sand through an hourglass, like rice poured over a coffin, like all the small dry voices of a country that had forgotten its own name. Somewhere under that sound, I thought I could hear the rest of them. Not just Vasseur. All of them. The ten thousand kneeling in the mud at Redmarch, still waiting for their god to finish the sentence he'd started.

* * *

I slept badly, which is to say I slept the way soldiers sleep: in shallow coins, each one stamped with something I'd rather not carry. When I woke the second morning, my mouth tasted of consecrated wine, though I hadn't touched any in twenty years, and my hands smelled of incense I hadn't burned.

That was new.

I held my palms up to the flat white light and turned them over. Old scars, new blisters, and beneath the skin—or beneath the idea of the skin, which out here amounts to the same thing—a warmth that was not mine. It had been leaking into me since Sainte-Avoie, I think. Since the ghost-priest put his translucent finger an inch from the jar and told me the salt remembers everything it has blessed. I had thought he meant memories. Pictures. Little hauntings, souvenirs.

I had not understood that remembering is not something the salt does. Remembering is something the salt *is*. And it had been waiting twenty years for a sworn hand to hold it, and now it was remembering itself into me, one liturgy at a time.

"Fine," I told my hands. "But I'm driving."

My hands said nothing. Everything out here says nothing. It's very persuasive.

* * *

By midday of the second day the Blight Road had given up even the pretense of being a road, and I was navigating by the coordinates on my arm and by the growing conviction that the ground itself was leaning me northward. The salt crust had begun to change. Where the blight had taken hold deepest, the flats weren't white anymore but grey-glassed, fused, and in the hollows between ridges I started seeing things caught mid-fall in the vitreous surface: a hoe. A doorframe. A child's shoe. Whole fields flash-frozen into stone the moment the god stopped holding the land together, as if the silence hadn't just broken a kingdom but dropped it.

And the visions came properly then. I say visions because *hallucinations* implies they were mine, and by then I was no longer sure whose property anything was.

They came in layers. The top layer was the present: white light, cracked glass, my boots, my breath. Underneath ran Redmarch, always Redmarch—the rain, the mud, the prayer rising off ten thousand throats like steam—and Vasseur at the front with his sword raised, mid-benediction, saying *and Veyl, who walks in salt, who keeps the—*

And stopping.

You cannot imagine a silence like that unless you've stood inside one. It wasn't absence. Absence is quiet. This was a *presence* of quiet, a thing with weight and appetite, arriving exactly where the god had been, filling the shape of him the way floodwater fills a cellar—completely, without malice, and forever. Ten thousand men and women kneeling in the rain, mouths open around the ends of prayers that would never be finished, while something vast and patient settled into the vacancy and began, very slowly, to eat.

Because that was the truth the cantor had sung in the ruined chapel, and the salt confirmed it now in the only voice I had left to hear it in: Veyl had not abandoned us. Veyl had been *consumed*. Devoured by his own faithful, prayer by prayer, decade by decade, a god fed on worship until worship was all that was left of him and then the worshippers ate that too, and what remained wore his face the way a glove wears a hand.

And the glove had found me.

"You keep calling it a glove," said Vasseur, walking beside me now, close enough that I could see the rain still beaded on his ghost-shoulders, eleven years undried. "It doesn't care for the word."

"It can lodge its complaints with the management," I said. "Oh, wait."

"You're angry."

"I'm *walking*, Commander. Anger is what walking looks like from the inside."

He laughed, and it was Vasseur's laugh, exactly, warm and rough, and that was the cruelest thing yet—that the thing that ate my god had eaten my commander too, and served him back to me in pieces, seasoned with affection. Because it had learned affection from us. That was the horror at the bottom of the well: the devourer wasn't some alien hunger from beyond the Door. It was made of us. Of our prayers, our oaths, our kneeling. Every scrap of devotion ever poured into the silence had gone into building it, and so of course it loved us. We were its ingredients.

"The Door," I said. "When I open it. What comes through?"

"What has always been trying to come through." Vasseur looked north, and for a moment—half a heartbeat—something else looked out, something with no face at all, and my knees nearly went. Then the commander's face returned, apologetic as a clerk. "The salt opens it. The salt feeds it. You've been carrying dinner across a country, Marguerite."

I stopped walking.

The flats stretched away in every direction, white and grey and silent, and somewhere ahead—two days' walk, one day now, less than a day—the black glass seam waited with hundreds of refugees already gathering at it like gulls over a gutting table. And behind me, somewhere south, Cassel rode with soldiers who loved the same dead god I did, and between us all lay the arithmetic Odile had died without finishing: one jar, one opening, and a mouth on the other side.

"If I pour it," I said slowly, working it through the way you work a blade free of a wound, "it eats. If I don't pour it, the Door stays shut, and everyone at it stays here with your Inquisitors and their pyres." I looked at the ghost. "Those are the options? Eat or burn?"

"There's a third option," Vasseur said gently.

"There's always a third option. That's how gods get people killed."

"This one's simpler than most." He smiled, and beneath the smile, faint as a watermark, I saw—not the devourer, not Vasseur, but something small and scattered and patient, something that had been hiding inside the silence for twenty years the way I'd been hiding inside Brasse-les-Salines. Hiding the way I taught it, maybe. Hiding the way its faithful hide. "The salt remembers everything it blessed. Including the oath. Including *you*. What was the oath, Marguerite?"

I knew it. I'd known it since Sainte-Avoie, known it in my marrow, and I had been refusing to know it the way you refuse a diagnosis. I recited it anyway, there alone on the glass, my voice cracking on the old formal words:

"My life for the gate. My blood for the threshold. My self for the keeping."

"Not escape," Vasseur said. "Never escape. The Door was never an exit. It was a *post*. And the salt doesn't open it for you to walk through." His ghost-hand rose, and touched my forehead, cold as consecrated ground, and the whole grey world rang like a struck bell. "It opens it for you to *hold*."

* * *

I came back to myself face-down on the salt with the sun low and red through the haze, blood in my mouth where I'd bitten my cheek, and the jar against my sternum beating—there is no other word—beating like a second heart, fast, excited, a dog that has finally made its master understand.

Two days, Perrin had bought me. One was spent. The sun going down meant maybe fourteen hours of dark to cross the last of the flats, and I intended to use every hour, because I finally understood what I was carrying and what it cost and who was waiting at the end of the coordinates, and understanding, in my long experience, is a thing best acted on before it talks you out of itself.

I got up. I checked the water I didn't have, the food I'd finished yesterday, the knife I did. North-northeast. Where the glass shows black.

And then, far off across the white, at the exact edge of sight where the haze begins, I saw lights. Not campfires—they moved wrong, strung out in a line, a procession of lanterns walking north with the patient, terrible orderliness of the devout. Hundreds of them. Maybe thousands. The refugees, all the lost and burned-out faithful of a nameless country, converging on the Door like iron filings to a magnet none of them could see.

And at the head of the line, riding a grey horse with a lantern raised, a figure in Inquisitorial black who was not following the pilgrims.

He was walking among them. Unarmed. And every few steps, he knelt.

"Oh," I said to the empty salt, to the jar, to whatever was listening in the silence between stars. "Oh, you have got to be joking."

The jar beat twice against my heart, which I chose to interpret as agreement, and I ran north into the dark.
