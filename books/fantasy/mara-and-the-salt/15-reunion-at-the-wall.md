## 15. Reunion at the Wall

I should have known the wall would be where it ended. Walls always are. They're the only honest thing a country ever builds—everything else is promises, but a wall just stands there and says *no* in stone.

The border wall of the old kingdom ran along the northern edge of the flats, thirty feet of black basalt that had outlasted the kingdom itself, outlasted its name, outlasted, apparently, my luck. I came over the last rise at dusk with two days of Cassel's false trail burning behind me like a spent candle, and there it was: the wall, running to the horizon in both directions, and leaning against it with the patience of a man who has never once been late for anything, Inquisitor Cassel.

No soldiers. That was the first wrong thing. The second was that he'd built a small fire, and there was a kettle on it.

"Marguerite," he said, without standing. "You look terrible."

"You look worse," I said, which was true, which was also the kind of thing you say to a man instead of *why aren't your men flanking me right now*. Twenty years ago we'd have been checking each other's blind sides by now. Instead he poured tea into a tin cup and held it out, and I took it, because I am a coward in small ways even when I'm brave in large ones, and because it smelled like real leaf, not salt-weed.

"You gave Perrin up," I said.

"He gave himself up. There's a difference." He watched me over his own cup. "He lied beautifully, by the way. Sent us toward the Meridian ruins. Two days of marching through nothing." A pause. "He knew what it cost him when he did it. You could see him doing the arithmetic while he talked. Choosing the number that wasn't himself."

My hands tightened on the cup. "Where is he?"

"Cold cell in the garrison at Vaux. Fed, unharmed, and very much alive as of yesterday morning." He said it flatly, a fact offered like a coin. "Whether he stays alive depends largely on you."

"There it is," I said. "I was wondering how long the tea would last before the invoice arrived."

"It's not an invoice." And here was the third wrong thing, the one that made the hair on my arms stand up under twenty years of scar tissue: he sounded tired. Not interrogator-tired, not cat-and-mouse tired. Tired the way Odile had sounded, the night before she died. "Drink your tea, Marguerite. Then I'd like you to pray with me."

I laughed. It came out ugly. "You didn't march two hundred miles across the blight to arrest me. You marched it to *convert* me."

"I marched it because I've been walking toward this wall for twenty years," Cassel said, "and you're the first person who might understand what I'm going to say when I get here."

* * *

You want to know about the day the god went silent? Everyone asks eventually, once they learn what I was. They ask it the way people ask about battles—as if silence were an event with a shape, a charge, something you could draw on a map with little arrows.

It wasn't. It was a Tuesday.

We were at Rive-Salée, the last field action of the war nobody won, ten thousand of us sworn to Veyl kneeling in the mud for the morning office, and Commander Aubry—I've told you about Aubry, or someone has; everyone who was there tells you about Aubry—was leading the canticle. His voice was the best voice in the company, a voice like a door opening onto somewhere warm. He got four lines in. On the fifth line, mid-word, mid-*breath*, he stopped.

Not faltered. Stopped. Like a man whose throat had been quietly removed.

And then ten thousand voices kept going without him, and then nine thousand, and then five hundred, and then it was just me, kneeling in the mud, singing alone into a silence so complete I could hear my own heart misfiring, and I understood—I understood with the whole surface of my skin—that no one else could hear me either. That the words were falling out of my mouth and landing nowhere. That the line between me and my god hadn't gone quiet.

It had been *cut*.

I sang the rest of the office alone. All of it. Because if I stopped, it would be real, and if it was real, then everything I was had already ended, and I was nineteen years old and did not yet know how to be anything except his.

Cassel knelt beside me the whole time. I remember that. I remember thinking, *good, he hears it too, he'll sing with me.* He never opened his mouth. When it was over he helped me up out of the mud and said, "It's a test," and his voice was steady, and I hated him a little for it, and I have hated him a little for it every day since, the way you hate the person who was calm during the shipwreck.

That was the day I lost my gift. Not gradually. Not fading. Gone, between one syllable and the next, like a tooth knocked out—you keep touching the gap for years.

What people don't understand about losing magic is that it isn't like losing a limb. A limb doesn't itch. What I lost was more like hearing. For years afterward I'd catch myself turning toward sounds that weren't there—the way a deaf man turns toward music—and finding nothing, and the nothing had a texture, and the texture was *mockery*.

So yes. I became an Inquisitor. Everyone thinks that's a contradiction. It's the opposite. If the god went silent, then either he died—which is unbearable—or he is testing us, winnowing the faithful down to those who can hold the forms without the reward, obedience without sweetness, prayer without answer. And if it's a test, then someone must enforce the terms. Someone must keep the vessel pure for his return. Because he will return. He has to. The alternative is a universe in which I sang alone in the mud for nothing, and I have built an entire life on the foundation that this is not true.

You can see the architecture of it, can't you? I could see it too, some nights. Faith is a house, and if you never open the curtains you can live in it very comfortably.

Then they put a jar of consecrated salt in my custody reports, and a woman I loved on the run from my own pyres, and I started walking north, and somewhere around the drowned village I began to suspect the curtains were going to open whether I wanted them to or not.

* * *

"Pray with me," he said again, by the fire, and set down his cup, and folded his hands, and waited.

"This is a trap with better manners than most," I told him.

"Everything is a trap with better manners than most. That's what a country is." He didn't smile. "Please, Marguerite."

And God forgive me—God, or Veyl, or whatever is left wearing either name—I knelt. Not because I believed. Because I remembered kneeling, and the body wants what it remembers, the way an amputee's hand closes on nothing.

We said the morning office. At dusk, backwards, which any hedge-priest will tell you is either blasphemy or honesty depending on the hour. His voice was exactly as I remembered it, careful and level, each word placed like a stone in a wall. Mine cracked twice. The old responses rose up out of me whole—I hadn't spoken them aloud in twenty years and they lived in my mouth like the language of a country I'd emigrated from, which is to say, exactly.

And when we reached the fifth line—the line where Aubry's voice had stopped, the line I had sung alone into the cut—

Nothing.

Of course nothing. Silence, wind off the flats, the kettle ticking as it cooled. Cassel's eyes were closed and his face was wet and he kept saying the line, over and over, quieter each time, the way you call a name down a well hoping the echo will change.

"I hear it," he whispered finally. "Every time. Every single time, I hear it just before it comes—there's a moment, right at the edge, where I can feel it gathering, like a voice drawing breath—" He opened his eyes. "Do you feel that?"

I did. God help me, I did. Ever since Sainte-Avoie, ever since the jar, there had been something at the edge of my hearing like breath drawn and held. I'd been telling myself it was the salt talking, the ghosts, exhaustion, guilt. Anything.

"Yes," I said.

His face changed. I watched hope move through it like weather, and I watched something behind the hope that frightened me much more, because it looked like a man watching a door he'd nailed shut from the inside.

"The jar," he said. "The consecrated salt. Where is it?"

"Why do you think I feel it near the jar, Cassel?"

"Because it's *consecrated*." He stood, and the firelight made him tall again, made him the Inquisitor again, and for one merciful second I thought we were back on solid ground, back in the old argument where I knew all the moves. "Because holiness draws holiness. Because if the god's presence gathers anywhere in this dead country, it gathers at the relic of his own rite—"

"And if it doesn't?" I stayed kneeling. It seemed wise. "If what gathers at that jar isn't Veyl at all? The priest at Sainte-Avoie told me something, Cassel. Something the church burned people for saying. He said Veyl wasn't silent because he left. He said Veyl was *eaten*. Swallowed by his own faithful, fed on prayers until there was nothing left but the hunger, wearing the shape of what it ate." I looked up at him. "You've felt it gathering too. Ask yourself why it feels like breath drawn and never released. Ask yourself what kind of thing holds its breath."

For a long moment he said nothing. The wind moved along the top of the wall with a sound like a vast slow page turning.

"You're asking me to believe that everything I've done for twenty years—the purges, the pyres, the registrations, the children I sent to the towers—" His voice didn't break. That was the terrible part. It stayed level, and underneath the levelness I heard the sound of a building deciding whether to fall. "You're asking me to believe I wasn't keeping the faith pure. I was *feeding* it."

"I'm not asking you to believe anything," I said. "I'm telling you what the dead told me, and the dead have no reason to lie. We're the ones with careers invested in it."

That almost got a laugh out of him. Almost. "The Door," he said instead. "You're going to the Door. With the jar. To leave."

"To open it," I said, which was true, and left out the rest, which was also true, and he heard the leaving-out—he always could, damn him, twenty years and a war and a schism and he still read me like a dispatch.

"Open it for whom?" he asked softly. "There's one measure of salt in that jar, Marguerite. One passage. And there are hundreds of them coming behind you—I've seen the roads. Refugees walking toward a rumor, all of them believing the Door opens wide enough for everyone." He crouched down to my level, and his eyes were wet again, and furious, and underneath the fury something so nakedly afraid that I recognized it the way you recognize your own face in dark glass. "Tell me what you're going to do."

And I opened my mouth to lie to him—one clean professional lie, the kind Perrin would have admired—and what came out instead was:

"I don't know."

The words hung there between us, and I realized I'd never said them to another living soul in twenty years of hiding. *I don't know.* It felt like taking off armor in winter.

Cassel stared at me. Then he sat down in the dirt beside me, the Inquisitor of the Silent Throne, keeper of the pure vessel, and put his face in his hands, and his shoulders shook once, twice, and stopped, because he was the sort of man who permitted himself exactly that much.

"When I was twelve," he said, into his hands, "my gift came in. Small thing. I could make bread rise. Just bread. My mother cried for a week—said the god had marked our family, said I'd be sworn by seventeen." He lowered his hands. "The day he went silent, I was baking. The loaf collapsed. Flat as a coin. I've baked a thousand loaves since, trying to make one rise. A thousand. I still bake one every morning before I interrogate anyone. Do you know why?"

"No," I said, though I suspected I did.

"Because if I stop, it means I've accepted the silence. And if I accept the silence, then the test theory is dead, and then—" He breathed out. "Then I'm just a man who burned people for a god who wasn't listening. Or worse. A man who burned them for a god who was *hungry*."

The fire had burned down to coals. Somewhere far off along the wall, a sentry horn sounded—not for us, some other watch, some other fear. Behind us, south, the flats lay dark and enormous, and somewhere out in that darkness, hundreds of exhausted pilgrims were walking toward a seam of black glass, carrying their children, carrying their dead, carrying the same rumor I was carrying.

And inside my pack, wrapped in Odile's shawl, the jar sat warm against my back like a sleeping animal. Like a held breath. Like a mouth.

"Come with me," I heard myself say.

Cassel looked up sharply.

"Not as my captor. Not as my convert. As—" I searched for the word and couldn't find a dignified one. "As a witness. You've spent twenty years enforcing a theory. Come and see the data."

"That's heresy," he said automatically.

"Probably," I agreed. "Most true things are, in my experience."

He was quiet for a long time. The coals ticked. And I watched him do the arithmetic Perrin had done in that interrogation room—watched him weigh the numbers, self against truth, the life he'd built against the thing waiting behind the curtain—and I saw the exact moment the sum came out wrong for him, the moment he understood that whichever way he chose, he'd already paid the price years ago in the mud at Rive-Salée.

He stood. He kicked dirt over the fire. And he picked up his staff—not pointed at me, pointed south, toward the flats, toward the Door.

"If you're wrong," he said, "I'll arrest you at the threshold. That's the offer. Take it or walk ahead of me and take your chances."

"Those are the same offer," I said.

"No," said Cassel, and for the first time in twenty years, he smiled, and it was awful and human and young. "One of them means I still get to pray with you on the way."

We walked south together, two broken believers and a jar full of held breath, toward the place where the country ended and the arithmetic began.
