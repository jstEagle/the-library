## 16. The Door in the Salt

I had been walking for two days when I noticed I was no longer alone.

That's not quite true. I had been noticing them for a while—the shapes on the horizon, the dust of many feet on the blight road—but I'd told myself they were mirages. The salt does that. It throws up cities where there are none, herds of white cattle that dissolve when you blink, and once, memorably, my mother standing at the edge of a dry canal with her arms folded, waiting for me to explain myself. Mirages are the flats' idea of hospitality.

But these did not dissolve. These resolved, the closer I came, into people. Dozens of them. Then hundreds.

They were walking the way I was walking: badly, stubbornly, with the particular gait of people who have stopped expecting anything from their legs and are simply using them anyway. A woman carried a child who looked too old to be carried and too hollowed out to complain about it. An old man led a goat on a rope made of knotted rags. There were fishermen with rope-burned hands, farmers with salt-cracked palms, a boy of maybe twelve wearing an Inquisition surcoat he'd clearly looted off someone smaller than himself. Nobody spoke much. When they did speak, it was the same word, passed mouth to mouth like a coal:

*The Door.*

I fell in among them because it was easier than explaining why I shouldn't. They took me for what I appeared to be—a tired woman in a dead man's coat with a jar wrapped in oilcloth against her chest—and I let them. There is a strange comfort in being anonymous inside a crowd of the desperate. We were all running from the same fire. Nobody asked whose spark had started it.

By the second evening I understood something that made the back of my neck prickle: they weren't following me. They couldn't have been; half of them had set out before I ever left Brasse-les-Salines. They were following the rumor, the same rumor Odile had died for, the same coordinates scratched into salt-bleached paper and whispered across three provinces. And yet the procession bent around me like water around a stone. People glanced at the bundle under my coat and then quickly away, the way you glance at a lit lamp in a dark room—not because you want to look, but because your eyes insist.

They knew. Not what I carried, exactly. But *that* I carried. Word travels faster than feet, even across ground where nothing grows.

* * *

On the third morning, the land changed.

I don't know how else to say it. The blight road had been flat as a griddle for days—white crust, cracked mud, the occasional skeleton of a fence post leaning at angles geometry would disown—and then, all at once, the salt began to *sing*. Not aloud. Underfoot. A faint ringing, like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass, so low I felt it in my teeth before I heard it in my ears. The woman with the child stopped. The old man's goat refused to move another step and had to be dragged, complaining, its hooves skidding on crust that had gone suddenly glassy.

And ahead—maybe a mile, maybe less—a seam of black cut across all that whiteness like a stroke of ink on a wedding letter.

I had imagined the Door a hundred times since Odile first described it, and every imagining had been wrong. I'd pictured a gate, an arch, something built, something with hinges and intent. What lay ahead was nothing so cooperative. It was a fissure in the world itself: a line of black glass perhaps forty paces long, running straight as a surveyor's rule through the salt, wide enough that I could not have jumped it if I'd wanted to, deep enough that when I finally stood at its edge and looked down, I saw no bottom. Only darkness that returned my gaze with interest.

The crowd gathered along it the way mourners gather at a graveside—respectful, scattered, afraid to stand too close. No one touched it. I got the sense that touching it hadn't occurred to anyone, the way jumping off a cliff doesn't occur to most people. The black glass radiated a cold I could feel from ten paces, a cold with texture to it, almost a personality. It didn't feel like absence. It felt like attention.

"Well," said the old man with the goat, beside me, to nobody, to everybody. "It's real."

That was the moment awe curdled, right on schedule, into arithmetic.

Because here is what Odile had told me, in her warehouse that smelled of brine and candle smoke, in the last conversation we would ever have: the Door opens for consecrated salt. One measure poured along the seam, one opening. And here is what I knew, holding the jar against my sternum, feeling its weight the way you feel a debt: the jar held enough for one passage. Maybe. If I was careful. If the salt was generous. If the god it had been blessed to—if the thing it had been blessed *to*—was feeling merciful.

One measure. One opening. And behind me, stretching back down the blight road as far as I could turn my head, hundreds of people who had walked out of burned villages and salted fields on the promise of exactly one miracle.

I did the sum the way you do sums at three in the morning, when you're too tired to lie to yourself. One jar. Hundreds of souls. The remainder was not a number I could make come out kind.

* * *

They began to arrive in earnest by midday, and by late afternoon the crowd had organized itself into something between a pilgrimage and a queue. That was the part that undid me, honestly. Not the hunger or the fear—I'd seen plenty of both, had worn both like old boots—but the *order* of it. Someone had found a stick and drawn lines in the salt, and people stood behind the lines. Families clustered together. The woman with the child had been given the front place by unanimous, unspoken consent, and she stood there holding the boy's hand and staring at the black seam with an expression I recognized because I'd seen it in mirrors for twenty years: hope held very carefully, the way you hold a full cup crossing a rough floor.

Nobody had told them. Of course nobody had told them. The rumor that moved faster than feet had carried everything except the fine print.

A man near me—a fisherman, by his hands, with the permanent squint of someone who has spent decades arguing with weather—struck up conversation the way people do when silence has become heavier than talk.

"You came from the city?" he asked.

"Brasse-les-Salines," I said. "A while ago."

"My sister's there. Or was." He rubbed his palms together, working at nothing. "They say the Inquisitor Cassel emptied whole streets. Registration or the pyre, they say." He glanced at my coat—at the burn mark on the sleeve I hadn't been able to scrub out—and something in his face suggested he was doing his own quiet arithmetic about me and deciding not to share the result. "You think it's true? What they say about the Door?"

"It's real," I said. "I'm looking at it."

"And past it?"

"I don't know."

He nodded slowly, accepting this the way the desperate accept everything: completely, and without it helping. "Past is past," he said. "My grandmother used to say the old kingdom had a name. Before the maps lost it. She said when she was a girl her father pointed north and called it by name, and the word tasted like rain." He smiled at the black seam. "Maybe past it, things have their names again."

I didn't tell him that names were the least of what might be waiting. I had spent two days with fragments of Veyl whispering through the salt in my skull, wearing my dead commander's face, saying things I couldn't repeat without screaming. Whatever waited beyond the seam, I suspected it was not a country with a name. But the fisherman's grandmother had given him a word that tasted like rain, and I was not going to be the one to spit on it.

The sun went down red over the flats, and the black glass drank the color and gave nothing back, and still the people came.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat apart from the fires—there were fires now, little huddles of flame and song, refugees singing the way soldiers sing, badly and loudly, to prove they're still alive—and I unwrapped the jar just far enough to see it.

Consecrated salt doesn't look like anything special. That's the joke of it. It looks like salt. Coarse, grey-white, faintly damp, packed into a clay jar sealed with wax that Odile had pressed her thumbprint into. Twenty years ago I would have known it on sight, would have felt it hum against my gift like a struck tuning fork. Now I felt only cold, and weight, and the memory of a ghost-priest in a drowned village telling me: *the salt remembers everything it has blessed—including your oath.*

I put my palm flat against the clay.

The flats went quiet. Not silent—quiet, the way a room goes quiet when someone important enters. And then, beneath my hand, the memory rose.

Not the battle this time. Something smaller. A chapel, incense, a young priest with flour still on his sleeves from the morning's bread, blessing a jar of salt with hands that shook—not from fear, from reverence. *Bless this salt, Veyl, keeper of the threshold, walker between.* His voice was steady until the last line, and then it wasn't: *and keep it for the day the door must open.* He pressed his thumbprint into the wax. He looked directly at me—or at whoever would carry this, twenty years downstream of that morning—and he said, in a voice like a key turning: *One measure opens. Remember. One.*

I came back to myself with my heart slamming and the fisherman watching me with concern.

"Bad memories?" he asked.

"The worst kind," I said. "The accurate ones."

I wrapped the jar again. Around me the fires burned, the songs stumbled on, the child slept against his mother's shoulder, and the black seam glittered under the stars like a wound in the world that had never quite decided whether to heal or widen.

One measure. One opening.

I turned the problem over and over, looking for a side of it that wasn't cruelty. Give the passage to the woman and the child—they had the most years ahead of them, the most to lose. But then the fisherman's sister in Brasse-les-Salines, waiting for a brother who would never come. The boy in the stolen surcoat. The old man and his ridiculous goat. Every solution was just a different way of choosing who to abandon, and I had spent twenty years practicing smallness precisely so I would never again have to choose who lived. The god I'd sworn to had done the choosing for us, once, mid-prayer, and ten thousand of us had knelt in the mud and learned what silence costs.

Now the choice was mine, and it fit my hand like an old sword.

Unless.

There was a thought at the edge of this, the way the Door had been at the edge of the horizon—dark, straight, refusing to resolve. The thought was this: Odile had said the Door opened for consecrated salt. She had never said what it opened *onto*. The ghost-priest had warned me the salt remembers. The visions had shown me a devouring god wearing familiar faces. And somewhere underneath all of it, underneath the arithmetic and the awe, a colder voice kept asking a question I didn't want to hear:

*What if the salt isn't a key?*

*What if it's an offering?*

I pushed the thought down. I was good at pushing thoughts down; I'd had practice. I watched the fires, and the stars wheeled over the black seam, and sometime before dawn I must have slept after all, because I dreamed of my mother pointing north and saying a word I couldn't quite catch, a word that tasted like rain.

* * *

I woke to shouting.

For one lurching heartbeat I thought: Cassel. The Inquisition had found us, the fires would scatter, it would end here in blood on the salt. My hand went to the jar and my other hand went to the knife at my hip, and I came up out of my bedroll ready for—

But no. The crowd was facing east, all of them, faces lit gold by the rising sun, and the shouting wasn't panic. It was the sound people make when a rumor stops being a rumor.

Down the blight road, catching the dawn light, came more of them. Hundreds more. A column of the displaced stretching back beyond sight, walking toward the Door with the grim patience of people who have already survived the worst thing and are merely negotiating the terms of the rest. And at the head of the column, unmistakable even at distance because he walked like a man leading a parade he'd rather be fleeing, rode a figure in grey.

Cassel.

My stomach dropped through the salt, through the black glass, into whatever waited below. Behind him rode outriders—two dozen, maybe more—but here was the thing, the thing that made the hair rise on my arms: they weren't riding down the pilgrims. They were riding *with* them. Flanking them. And the soldiers' weapons were slung, and the pilgrims' pace didn't falter, and the two streams of humanity flowed together toward the seam like rivers meeting, and I could not read it at all.

Had he come to burn us? To arrest me in front of everyone? Or—

The fisherman grabbed my arm. "Is that him? The Inquisitor?"

"That's him."

"Then what—" He swallowed. "Why aren't they stopping us?"

I had no answer. I had less than no answer, because a worse question was forming underneath it, the kind of question that arrives with cold hands: if Cassel had wanted me dead, I would be dead already. Two days alone on the flats, sleeping in the open, dreaming loud enough to wake the salt. He'd had me. Instead he was here, at the Door, at dawn, with an army that wouldn't fire and a face I couldn't see yet but could already imagine—that terrible gentle certainty, the conviction of a man who thinks grief is a form of obedience.

The crowd parted as the riders approached, and the pilgrims did something I will remember until I die, which given my current prospects may not be long: they didn't run. They stood their ground, hundreds of them, unarmed, and watched the Inquisition ride into their midst, and the silence that fell over the salt was so complete I could hear the black glass ringing.

Cassel dismounted thirty paces from me. Of course he saw me immediately; I was the still point the whole crowd bent around. Our eyes met across the heads of the faithful, across the seam of night in the daylit salt, and he did not reach for a weapon or call for chains.

He reached for the clasp of his cloak instead, and threw it back, and beneath it he wore no armor at all.

"Marguerite," he called, and his voice carried the way voices do over salt, clean and terribly far. "We need to talk before you open it."

Behind me, the woman with the child tightened her grip on her son's hand. Ahead of me, the black seam waited, patient as a held breath. And in the jar against my heart, the consecrated salt shifted—just slightly, just once—like something turning over in its sleep.
