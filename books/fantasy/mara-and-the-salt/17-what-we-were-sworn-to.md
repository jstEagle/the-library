## 17. What We Were Sworn To

*Cassel*

I had commanded men through three wars and one heresy, and I had learned that the hardest order to give is the one your soldiers can already see is wrong.

They stood in a line behind me on the salt, forty of them, pikes grounded, faces gray in the flat white light of morning. Before us, spread across the flats like something spilled from heaven's pocket, were the pilgrims. Four hundred. Maybe five. Old women carrying children who were not theirs. Farmers with salt-blighted hands. A mute congregation from some ruined chapel, walking in silence so complete it made my ears ache. And at the front of all of them, alone on the glass-black seam in the earth, Marguerite Joubert with a jar in her hands.

"Loose on my command," I said.

No one moved. That was answer enough.

Sergeant Aubry—twenty years in my service, a man who had once held a bridge for six hours with a broken arm because I told him to hold it—looked at me sideways. Not mutinous. Worse than mutinous. *Pitying.*

"Inquisitor," he said quietly, "they're not armed."

"They are armed." My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "She carries a relic of the old faith. She carries an oath older than the crown. You've seen what the gifted do when cornered."

"I've seen what they did," Aubry said. "Twenty years ago. I haven't seen one do anything since but hide."

Behind him, in the ranks, someone shifted their weight. Someone else looked away from me entirely, out at the pilgrims, at the black seam in the salt, at the horizon where the drowned country used to be. I had spent eleven years building the Inquisition on the certainty that faith was a discipline, that doubt was rot, that obedience was the last prayer left to us. I had built it well enough to know exactly what I was looking at now: rot, spreading down the line like water through chalk.

And the terrible thing—the thing I would carry to whatever grave the god allowed me—was that I could not swear they were wrong.

* * *

*Marguerite*

The jar was warm against my palms, which should have frightened me more than it did. Salt isn't supposed to be warm. Salt is supposed to be patient, indifferent, the thing you scatter on ice and wounds alike. But this salt had been remembering things since Sainte-Avoie, and lately it remembered them at me, and I had stopped pretending I didn't hear.

Four hundred people stood behind me because I hadn't told them the truth yet. The truth being: the Door opens once. The jar holds one measure. And every soul on these flats was doing arithmetic with their lives, hoping the god's mercy would be divisible.

Cassel came across the salt the way he'd done everything in his life—at a walk, unhurried, as if the world would wait for him because waiting was simply correct. He'd changed. Twenty years had put silver in his beard and hollows under his eyes, but he still carried himself like a man holding a door shut from the inside.

He stopped ten paces off. Close enough to talk. Far enough that we both pretended the distance meant something.

"Marguerite."

"Cassian." His name still fit in my mouth, which annoyed me. "You brought forty men to arrest one woman."

"I brought forty men to arrest a heretic carrying stolen consecrated property." He glanced past me at the crowd, and something flickered across his face—not fear, exactly. Recognition. "Though it appears the property has been walking toward me on its own feet for days."

"They're not my army. They're just tired."

"Everyone's tired, Marguerite. Tiredness isn't innocence." He looked at the jar. "Give it to me. I'll open the Door myself. Let them go through—all of them—and then come back and face judgment with a clean conscience. That's mercy. That's the order of things."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I order my men to fire, and we learn together whether they obey."

We both turned, almost in unison, and looked at the line. Forty men who had not moved. Sergeant Aubry standing there with his pike planted like a fence post, wearing the expression of a man watching his commander walk into deep water.

"You already know," I said.

"I know what I see." Cassel's jaw tightened. "What I see is discipline fraying because the moment is hard. Discipline exists *for* the hard moments. If we only obeyed when obedience was comfortable, it wouldn't be worth anything at all."

"That's not why they're wavering, and you know it. They're wavering because they've spent twenty years burning hedge-witches and dragging fishwives to registration, and none of it has brought him back. They've done everything right, Cassian. Everything. And the sky has said nothing." I lifted the jar between us. "Maybe the problem isn't our obedience."

* * *

*Cassel*

There it was. The question underneath every question I had buried for two decades, and she dug it up in front of four hundred witnesses and forty of my own men, and set it on the salt between us like a stone.

"The problem," I said, "is that the faithful devoured themselves with sin, and the silence is the wound. Veyl withdrew because we became unworthy. The purge is not cruelty—it's surgery. Cut away the corruption, keep the pure, and when the last impurity burns, he returns. That is the doctrine. That is the *test*."

"I know the doctrine. I helped write half of it, back when we were young and stupid and thought the god owed us an explanation." Her voice didn't rise. That was the worst part about arguing with Marguerite Joubert; she never raised her voice, she just opened doors you'd nailed shut and stood aside politely while everything you'd locked in walked out. "Tell me, Cassian. When did you last pray?"

"What?"

"When did you last pray, and have him hear you? Not the liturgy. Not the forms. When did you last speak to him and feel—" she touched her own chest, briefly, "—anything back?"

I did not answer. Which was an answer.

"I lost it the day he went silent," I said finally. "We all did. The gift went out of the world like a lamp going out. That's not abandonment, Marguerite. That's a father locking the door until his children stop killing each other. And here you are, twenty years later, picking the lock."

"No." She shook her head slowly. "Here I am, twenty years later, learning what actually happened to him. In Sainte-Avoie, a dead priest told me the salt remembers. In the chapel of ash, a blind cantor told me the rest. Do you want to know where your god went, Cassian? Because I know. And the knowing is worse than the silence, and I would still rather have it."

"Don't." For the first time my voice cracked, and I hated it. "Don't stand there and offer me a lie dressed as revelation. Whatever the cantor told you—"

"He was eaten, Cassian." Quietly. Gently, even, which was somehow crueler. "His own church fed him their prayers, decade after decade, generation after generation, until there was nothing left of Veyl but appetite wearing his shape. The silence wasn't punishment. It was mercy. It was the last unselfish act of a dying god—going quiet so we couldn't feed ourselves to him anymore. And the purge, your surgery, your test—" she spread her hand at the flats, at the burned camps behind us, at the registration lists and the pyres, "—you're not purifying the faithful. You're fattening the herd."

* * *

*Marguerite*

I watched the sentence land in him the way frost lands on a window—slowly, in patterns, from the edges inward.

He wanted to call it heresy. I could see him reaching for the word the way a drowning man reaches for rope. But forty men were listening, and four hundred pilgrims were listening, and somewhere in the last mile of salt between the chapel and this place, the argument had stopped being ours. It belonged to everyone standing in the white light now.

"You have no proof," he said.

"I have a jar that's warm. I have a Door that opens onto hunger—I've seen what waits beyond it, Cassian, or the beginning of it, in dreams the salt gives me. I have ten thousand sworn soldiers' last memory of kneeling in mud, praying to a god who had already stopped existing." I held his eyes. "And I have you. You lost your gift the day he went silent. So did I. Ask yourself—if he were merely withdrawn, testing us, why would the gift be gone? A father who locks the door still lives behind it. Something that's dead doesn't answer. Something that's *eaten* doesn't answer either. Only one of those explains why the land itself has been starving ever since."

His hand went to the medallion at his throat—an old habit, the sign of Veyl worn by every soldier who'd ever sworn. He'd kept it polished all these years. I noticed that. Of course I noticed; I'd known him since we were nineteen and full of fire.

"If I believe you," he said, very low, "then everything I have done—every pyre, every name on every list, every man I sent to die on the blight road—was not faith. It was feeding. And I don't—" His voice failed and rebuilt itself. "I cannot carry that, Marguerite. I would rather be damned than believe I was never loyal at all."

"I know." And I did know; that was the whole tragedy of him, the whole tragedy of us. "But loyalty to a thing that no longer exists isn't loyalty, Cassian. It's grief with a uniform on. I wore it for twenty years too. Hiding my gift in market stalls, selling luck-knots to fishermen, making myself small enough that the silence couldn't find me. I called it survival. It was mourning. There's a difference, and I only found it because they came to burn me."

"Then what would you have us do?" He gestured at the crowd, the soldiers, the endless salt. "Kneel? Abandon the order? Believe a ghost and a blind man and a fugitive with stolen property?"

"Believe the land," I said. "Look at it. Look at the blight. A withdrawn god doesn't poison the fields. A dead one doesn't either. But a *devoured* one leaves the world hungry, because the thing that ate him is still hungry, and it eats through us. Every prayer we send into the silence goes somewhere, Cassian. It always did. We just stopped asking where."

Behind him, Sergeant Aubry had turned fully around to face the pilgrims. Several other soldiers had too. Cassel saw it happen—he saw his command dissolve not into mutiny but into attention, which is far harder to punish—and I watched him understand that the battle was already over, and had been for years, and he had simply been the last man on the field.

* * *

*Cassel*

I had prepared for this day for eleven years. I had rehearsed the arrest, the tribunal, the dignified condemnation. I had imagined her defiant, or broken, or laughing at me. I had not imagined her *right*, and I had certainly not imagined that being right would look like this: a woman with gray in her hair holding a jar of salt, surrounded by the poorest people in a country that no longer existed, offering me nothing but the truth.

"Stand down," I heard myself say.

Aubry blinked. "Inquisitor?"

"Stand down. Ground arms. All of you." My voice sounded strange to me—lighter, somehow, as though saying it removed weight instead of adding it. "There will be no firing on pilgrims. There will be no arrests today. If the crown wants my head for it, the crown knows where I stand. I'm standing here."

They grounded their pikes. All forty, in a long rolling clatter that echoed off the flats like the sound of a door closing on a house nobody lived in anymore. Some of the men looked relieved. Some looked terrified. Most looked like men waking from a long fever, blinking at a world that had continued without their permission.

Marguerite watched me with an expression I remembered from another life—from the night after our first battle, when I had vomited behind a wall and she had sat with me without speaking until I could stand again. Pity without contempt. It was unbearable. It was also, I understood suddenly, the only honest thing left between us.

"It doesn't end here," I said. "Whatever's behind that Door—whatever ate him—you mean to face it."

"I mean to face it alone. One measure, one passage. That's the arithmetic."

"I never could abide your arithmetic." I stepped forward, over the seam's edge, until the black glass hummed faintly under my boots. "You'll have witnesses. If you're right, the country deserves to see it. And if you're wrong—" I almost smiled. "If you're wrong, I want to be close enough to catch you when you fall. Old habit."

Behind us, the first measure of light was already gathering along the seam, thin as a blade, and the jar in her hands had begun—very softly, very patiently—to glow.

She looked down at it. Then at me. And I saw in her face what I had refused to see for twenty years: not hope. Readiness.

"It's opening," she said. "Cassian—whatever comes through, don't pray to it. No matter whose face it wears."

The salt under our feet began, gently, to sing.
