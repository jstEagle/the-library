## 18. The Devourer Wakes

I have poured salt on wounds before. I have poured it on graves, on thresholds, on the heads of screaming recruits who thought consecration was a form of hazing—which, to be fair, it often was. I know the weight of a measure of salt in my palm the way other women know the weight of a sleeping child.

So when I tipped the jar and let the first measure fall into the seam of black glass, I expected—what? A click. A shudder. The groan of an old lock giving way.

What I got was silence.

Not the ordinary silence of a crowd holding its breath, though there was that too—three hundred pilgrims behind me going quiet as a held note. This was a silence with texture. A silence like the inside of a bell before it's struck. It came up out of the Door the way cold comes up out of a cellar, and every hair on my arms stood up and pointed at the black glass as if to say *there, that's where it's coming from, run.*

The salt didn't scatter when it hit the seam. It lay there in a thin white line, and then it sank. Not slid—*sank*, the way a stone sinks into still water, without disturbing anything, and the glass drank it down and waited for more.

"Well," said the old woman beside me, whose name I never learned and now never will. "That's not ominous at all."

I liked her instantly. I would have told her so, if the Door hadn't chosen that moment to open.

* * *

It did not open the way doors open. There was no swing, no parting. The seam simply stopped being a seam and became an absence—a vertical darkness in the salt flats, tall as a gallows and twice as narrow, and the light around its edges bent inward like water finding a drain. Behind me, three hundred people made three hundred small sounds of awe, and every one of those sounds bent toward the dark too. I felt them bend. I felt my own breath lean forward into that absence like a drunk toward a bottle.

And then something came through.

I want to tell you it crawled. That's the word I reached for at the time and it's the word I'll use now, because language is a rope bridge over a chasm and you use what holds. But what came through wore Veyl's shape, and Veyl's shape had been the most beautiful thing I ever knelt to: a tall figure like a man drawn by someone who had only ever seen men from very far away, robed in grey, faceless except where the face should be there was a stillness, a calm, a *listening*. Ten thousand soldiers had died happy looking at that listening.

This thing had the shape. It had the grey robe. It even had the listening—but the listening was wrong the way a portrait is wrong when the eyes follow you around the room. Its stillness was the stillness of a trap already sprung. And where the god's face had been empty and serene, this thing's emptiness was *hungry*. You could feel it tasting the air. Tasting us.

The pilgrims nearest the Door began to weep. I understood why. It looked like Him. After twenty years of silence, after twenty years of praying into a well that never once echoed back, here was a figure in His shape standing in a doorway made of night, and every starving faithful heart in that crowd lunged toward it like a dog toward a hand that might feed or might hold a knife.

"Stop," I said. My voice came out small. Salt wind took it.

The thing turned its non-face toward me.

And in the space where a voice would live, it spoke—not in words, not in sound, but in memory. It reached into me the way Cassel reached into Tomas's guilt, the way Odile reached into her jars, and it showed me myself kneeling in the mud at Kessel Ford, ten thousand strong, mid-prayer, mid-*breath*, and then nothing. Then the silence. Then twenty years of it.

*I did that,* it said, without saying. *That was mine. Wasn't it glorious?*

"No," I said aloud, because saying things aloud is how I keep from drowning, and because some part of me—the part that had spent two decades selling luck-knots to frightened fishermen, small deniable magic, small deniable courage—knew exactly what this was.

The ghost-priest of Sainte-Avoie had told me the salt remembers everything it has blessed. He'd said it gently, the way you tell someone their father is dying. And the cantor in the chapel of ash had told me the rest, blind eyes turned toward a ceiling that wasn't there anymore: *He wasn't abandoned, child. He was eaten. We fed him our prayers until there was no him left, and something else learned to answer.*

I had thought she meant it as history. A wound explained. Something to grieve and carry.

Standing in front of the Door, watching the thing wear my god like a coat, I finally understood she'd meant it as a warning label.

*Come,* it said to the crowd, in the voice of every prayer they'd ever whispered into the dark. *You've been so faithful. You've kept yourselves pure. Come home.*

And the first pilgrim stepped forward.

* * *

She was young. Of course she was young—they're always young, the ones who go first. Maybe nineteen, maybe twenty, born long after the silence, raised on stories of a god who loved His people so much He'd gone quiet rather than watch them suffer. She had salt-white burn scars on her hands from working the pans, and she held them out toward the Door the way you'd reach for a mother.

"Wait—" I grabbed her arm.

She looked at me with such pity. That's the detail I can't shake, the one that wakes me now when I do sleep. Not fear. Pity. As if *I* were the one lost, the poor bitter woman standing between her and everything she'd ever wanted.

"He's calling us," she said.

"He is not." I heard my own voice crack like river ice. "Listen to me. Twenty years ago I knelt in the mud at Kessel Ford and prayed until my knees bled and He never answered, and I have hated Him for it, and I have missed Him for it, and I would give anything—*anything*—to hear His voice again. And this is not His voice. This is the thing that ate Him wearing His clothes."

"How do you know?" she asked, and it wasn't defiance. It was honest. It was the question I'd been asking myself since the chapel, since the drowned village, since the jar first warmed in my hands like a living thing.

How did I know?

Because I remembered what Veyl felt like. Not the sermons, not the doctrine—Cassel could quote you doctrine until the sun burned out. I mean the feel of Him. When Veyl had been present in the world, in the war-camps, in the small hours, His presence had been like a hand on the back of your neck: heavy, steadying, asking nothing. He had never once called anyone forward. That was the whole scandal of Him, the reason the old church had starved Him out in the end. Other gods demanded. Veyl waited. He let you come to Him or not, and His silence was not absence—it was courtesy. It was room. It was a god who understood that love compelled is not love.

This thing in the doorway was all compulsion. It pulled at the crowd like a riptide. It *wanted*, and its wanting had weight, and three hundred people were leaning into it like grass into wind.

"I know," I told the girl, "because the god I served never asked anyone to walk toward Him. He left the door open and trusted you to choose. Whatever this is, it doesn't trust you. It's hungry, and hunger always calls itself love."

For one heartbeat—one—her eyes cleared. She wavered, half-turned, and I thought, *there, that's the crack, that's where the light gets in—*

Then the thing spoke again, directly into her, and whatever it showed her was sweeter than anything I could offer, because she smiled, and stepped past me, and walked into the dark.

The dark closed around her like water around a dropped coin.

No scream. That was the worst of it. No scream, no struggle, just a soft intake of breath from the crowd, and then—worse than the silence of the god—the silence of a girl who had been there and wasn't.

And the crowd, God forgive them, the crowd *cheered*.

* * *

They cheered because they thought she'd gone home. They cheered because grief needs a miracle the way a wound needs a bandage, and any bandage will do if you press hard enough. I stood in the middle of it with salt grit under my fingernails and the jar heavy against my hip, and I did the arithmetic I'd been doing since the flats, the arithmetic that had brought me here with three hundred witnesses instead of alone:

One opening. One passage. The jar held enough consecrated salt for one crossing, maybe two if the Door was merciful, and the Door had shown no sign of mercy since the kingdom fell.

Except the Door wasn't a door. That was the clarity arriving now, cold and complete, the way Cassel's blade had arrived at my throat outside the wall—no anger in it, just geometry. The Door was a mouth. The salt wasn't a key. The salt was the first course. Every prayer the faithful had fed into the silence for twenty years had been seasoning; every soul that walked through was the meal; and somewhere beyond the black glass, the thing that had eaten my god was finishing its appetizer and finding the crowd at the threshold exactly to its taste.

Odile had died for this jar. She'd stood in the burning warehouse doorway with her hedge-witch knives and her terrible crooked smile and bought me thirty seconds with her life, and her last word had been *run*, and I had run, and I had carried her death across the blight road and the drowned country and the ash chapel, and I had believed—I had *needed* to believe—that it was for a door. For an exit. For the small selfish hope that survival was a place you could walk to.

It was never an exit. It was a lure. The whole country's hope, bottled and hung over the deepest water.

Behind me, the second pilgrim was already moving. Then a third. The thing in the doorway had begun, very softly, to hum—and the hum was the litany, the actual litany, the words we'd chanted at dawn prayers, rendered in a voice like a wet finger on glass, and I watched grown men and women weep and shuffle forward, and I understood that I had perhaps minutes before the arithmetic resolved itself into a massacre conducted entirely by volunteers.

I looked down at the jar.

Grey clay, unremarkable, warm. Inside it: the last consecrated salt in the country, blessed by a god who no longer existed, guarded by a woman who no longer believed in escape. Odile's voice in my memory, dry as her namesake herb: *Everything has a price, girl. The trick is finding out what the thing you're holding actually costs before you spend it.*

I'd spent twenty years learning what things cost. Luck-knots: two coppers. Silence: everything. Loyalty to a dead god: a life lived small in a city built on salt.

And this?

I hefted the jar. The thing in the doorway turned its wrongness toward me again, and this time it didn't show me Kessel Ford. It showed me Perrin—Perrin alive, Perrin laughing at his own lies, Perrin walking free of whatever cell Cassel had put him in, because the thing could see I wanted that, the way a fisherman sees where the current carries the bait. *Give me the jar,* it offered, without words. *And everyone lives. Everyone goes home. Isn't that what you've wanted all along? To stop running?*

And the horrible thing, the thing I'll confess because dead women owe the truth: for a moment, I wanted to say yes. Not because I believed it. Because I was tired. Because three hundred people were going to die in the next hour no matter what I chose, and the version of me that dies tired and deceived is at least a version that gets to put the weight down.

Then I thought of the girl with the burned hands, and how she'd wavered. How for one heartbeat she'd *heard* me.

One heartbeat. That's what Veyl had ever asked of anyone. Not worship. Not purity. Just the pause before the choice—the courtesy of a door left open, and the faith that you'd walk through it or not, freely.

The thing in the doorway had taken even that from these people. It had eaten the pause. It had eaten the choice. It had eaten Him, and now it was eating everything He'd ever protected, and it was doing it with His face, and it would keep doing it—tonight, tomorrow, in every country with a name—as long as the jar fed it and the faithful came.

Unless the meal was poisoned.

I looked at the salt in the jar, and I thought about what salt does. What it has always done. It preserves. It draws the poison out of a wound. It remembers everything it has blessed—including, the ghost-priest said, my oath. My oath, sworn at Kessel Ford with ten thousand voices: *to stand between the faithful and what harms them.* I'd assumed the oath died with the god. But the salt remembered. The salt had been carrying it across the whole cursed country like a letter waiting for delivery.

The creature in Veyl's shape extended its grey sleeve toward the crowd, hospitable, patient, and the third pilgrim began to walk, and the fourth, and I tipped the jar up and drank the measure of my own choice like a woman taking medicine.

"Not a door," I said. "A mouth. And I know what feeds mouths."

I raised the jar over the black glass, and the thing's stillness—for the first time—flickered.

It knew. In the last instant before I moved, it knew, and its hunger turned on me with the full weight of everything it had swallowed, every prayer, every god, every faithful fool who had ever walked willingly into the dark—and I found, to my considerable surprise, that I wasn't afraid.

Twenty years I'd hidden. Small magic, small life, small courage, measured out in coppers and luck-knots. But I had been battle-sworn once, and the oath was still in the salt, and the salt was still in my hands, and some inheritances you don't get to refuse—you only get to decide whether you pass them on or pay them off.

"Veyl," I said—to the god, not the thing wearing him—"this one's for you."

I turned the jar over.

* * *

The salt did not fall. It *went*—all of it at once, a white stream like a struck bell's sound made visible, pouring into the dark throat of the Door faster than gravity had any right to allow, and the black glass screamed. Not the crowd. The glass. The Door itself shrieked in a register I felt in my teeth, in the fillings of my bones, and the absence in the salt flats convulsed like a throat around something too large to swallow.

The thing in Veyl's shape came apart at the edges. Grey robe fraying into smoke, the listening stillness cracking like lake ice, and beneath it—for one blazing, unbearable moment—I saw what it had eaten. Faces. Thousands of faces pressed against the inside of its skin like drowners under ice, and among them, unmistakable, the calm empty serenity I had knelt to, my god's true face, still there, still waiting, buried twenty years deep in the belly of his own church.

*No,* the devourer said, in every voice it had ever stolen, including mine, including Odile's, including, horribly, the girl with the burned hands. *No, you don't—*

The Door began to close. Not gently. It snapped shut like a jaw with a hook in it, and the hook was me, because I had done the only thing left to do, the thing the whole journey had been walking me toward since Odile's warehouse burned: I stepped into the seam with the jar, and I poured the last of the salt—not into the Door.

Into myself.

Because the salt remembers everything it blesses. And I was going to be the blessing it couldn't swallow.

The last thing I saw, as the black glass sealed and the white light came up out of the ground like a tide returning, was the crowd—three hundred faces lit silver, falling to their knees, not in compulsion this time but in wonder—and beyond them, riding hard across the flats with his black coat snapping, a familiar silhouette I had last seen at the border wall, coming too late, or exactly on time, depending on what happened next.

"Cassel," I said, or meant to say.

But by then I was already opening.
