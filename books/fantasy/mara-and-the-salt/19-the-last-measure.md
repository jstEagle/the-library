## 19. The Last Measure

The thing wearing my god's face smiled at me, and I finally understood the joke.

It had taken me twenty years and one jar of salt to get there, which by any honest accounting was slow. But then, I'd had help being slow. The whole country had. We'd spent two decades calling the silence a mystery when it was a menu, and we'd been on it.

The Door stood open behind the creature—a seam of black glass in the salt, wide as a cathedral door now, drinking the light. The pilgrims behind me had stopped singing. Five hundred people who had walked across blighted flats on blistered feet to reach salvation were watching salvation stand up out of the ground and look hungry.

"Marguerite." It said my name in Veyl's voice, and that was the cruelest part. Not some monster's growl. His voice. The voice that had given the battle-oath at Corvane Ford, that had said *hold the line* to ten thousand kneeling soldiers and meant *I'm sorry* without knowing how to say it. "You brought it. You brought me home."

"You're not him," I said.

"I am every piece of him you didn't eat."

I'll give it this: it didn't lie. That's more than I can say for most of the faithful I've known, myself included.

The jar was warm against my chest. Consecrated salt, blessed by hands that had believed they were blessing a god, and here was the arithmetic Odile had died without finishing for me, the sum the ghost-priest of Sainte-Avoie had tried to hand me across his drowned altar: the Door doesn't open for consecrated salt. The Door opens *because* of it. Salt is an offering. A threshold is just a mouth that hasn't been fed yet.

The creature took a step toward the crowd, and five hundred people leaned back like wheat before wind.

"No," I said.

It turned its head—Veyl's head, gray-stubbled jaw, the scar through the left eyebrow I'd watched him earn at the ford—and regarded me with something almost like patience. "You want to bargain. They always want to bargain. Your Inquisitor prayed to me for twenty years. Do you know what he offered? Everything. Obedience, purity, the blood of everyone he caught. And none of it filled me, because he gave it *to* me, and I am the hole things are given to."

"Cassel," I said. "Where is he?"

"Behind you. Kneeling. They do that." The mouth that had been my commander's curved. "You could run, Marguerite Joubert. The jar holds enough for one passage. One. That was always the design—one keeper, kept safe, carrying the seed of the faith out into some other country where it could be planted and grown and—" it gestured with Veyl's hand at the black seam, at itself, at the whole groaning architecture of hunger, "—harvested. Again. Somewhere new. Somewhere fresh."

The pilgrims. The children. Perrin, wherever Cassel's soldiers had left him, if they'd left him anything at all. The whole procession of the desperate stretched back along the flats like a wound that wouldn't close.

One passage. Me or them.

Twenty years ago I would have done the math differently. Twenty years ago I'd have told you survival was the whole of virtue—that staying alive was the last loyalty, because dead soldiers keep no oaths. I'd built a life on that arithmetic. Small charms, small lies, a room above a chandler's shop where nobody knew my name was worth anything. I'd made peace with smallness, and I'd called the peace wisdom.

But peace, it turns out, is just a word for the quiet between feedings.

"You ate him," I said. "Your own church ate him. Prayers, tithes, obedience—all of it poured into a god until there was no god left, just the pouring. And when he was empty, you kept pouring, and what was left when the pouring stopped was *you*."

"We loved him," it said, and Veyl's face did something terrible and true, something grieving. "That's all devotion is. Love with the exits bricked up."

Behind me, someone started to sing. An old woman's voice, cracked and off-key, one of the processional hymns—the ones the Choir of Ash had taught us, sung without words because words were what got eaten first. Others joined. No lyrics. Just sound, rising over the salt, and the creature flinched from it the way a man flinches from a debt collector.

They weren't feeding it. That was the thing the Choir had understood and the rest of us hadn't. Wordless song gives nothing. It asks nothing. It's love with the doors left open.

I looked down at the jar.

Odile's hands had wrapped it in oilcloth, and her blood was still on the cloth, brown now, dried into the weave. She'd died so I could carry this. Tomas had betrayed me out of love; Cassel had hunted me out of love; Odile had burned out of love. Everyone in this story loved somebody, and nearly all of it had come out wrong, because we'd all been loving toward a mouth instead of a man.

The salt remembered everything it had blessed. My oath among them. *I hold the line.* I'd sworn it at nineteen with mud to my knees and Veyl's hand heavy on my shoulder, and I'd spent twenty years pretending the oath had died with the god.

It hadn't. Oaths don't die. They just wait for you to understand them.

"Hold the line," I said aloud.

The creature went very still.

"That's what it meant," I said. "All along. Not *survive*. Not *flee*. Hold. You hold a line so that what's behind you doesn't have to see what's in front of you." I looked back once—at the old woman singing, at the children, at the whole ragged human tide of us—and then I faced the Door. "You wanted a keeper. Fine. I keep."

I pulled the stopper.

What came out of the jar wasn't salt anymore. It had been salt when Odile sealed it, but consecration is a direction, not a state, and twenty years in the dark with a desperate woman's prayers had turned it into something else—light, maybe, or memory, or just the part of Veyl that had never been swallowed, hiding in crystal the way I'd hidden in Brasse-les-Salines. It rose around my hands like cold fire.

The creature lunged.

Of course it did. I'd have been insulted otherwise.

It crossed the salt faster than anything that size should move, wearing my dead commander's body like a stolen coat, and I did the only thing a shield-sister ever really does, the thing under all the drills and the doctrine and the theology:

I stepped forward to meet it.

We collided at the threshold. Its hands closed on my arms—Veyl's hands, callused, strong, hands I'd trusted with my life a hundred times—and its mouth opened wider than a mouth should, and I saw down into it, past the teeth, past the dark, into the place where ten thousand swallowed prayers churned like fish in a net. I saw pieces of him in there. A laugh. A scar. The way he'd hummed when he sharpened his blade, badly, always off-key. Everything the faithful had ever poured into him, packed down into the belly of the thing that had drunk him.

And I poured the salt in after it.

Not a measure. Not a portion. All of it. Every grain, straight down the throat of the devourer, and I held its head while it drank because someone should have to witness this, even if the witness was me.

It screamed. Not with Veyl's voice—with a hundred voices, a thousand, the whole congregation of the eaten crying out at once as the consecrated salt found them and *remembered*. That was the trick of it, the beautiful, horrible trick: the salt remembers everything it has blessed, and it had blessed a god. Every fragment of Veyl inside that hunger woke up and recognized itself. And a thing made of holes cannot survive being full.

The black seam shuddered. The Door—the real Door, the mouth—began to close, and it closed the way a wound closes, reluctant, grinding, the glass screaming as it knitted shut around a throat that was learning, grain by grain, what it felt like to be a god again instead of an appetite.

Its grip on my arms loosened. Changed. Became, at the last, just a man holding onto a woman.

"Marguerite." Veyl's voice, and this time it was only his. Tired. Small. Human-sized at last. "You held."

"Somebody had to," I said. "You were busy."

He laughed—or the piece of him that was a laugh did—and it came out of him and out of me and out of the closing seam and into the ground, into the salt, into the air, everywhere at once, and I understood that this was what unbound felt like. Not a commandment. Not a voice from heaven. Just presence, quiet as weather, going home through every crack in the world.

The light took the flats. It took the blight and the drowned villages and the burned camps and the chapel of ash. It took my hands, which had gone translucent, which had stopped hurting, which had stopped being entirely mine.

Funny. I'd spent twenty years hiding my gift, and now that I was spending the last of it, I couldn't remember why I'd bothered. It had never been mine to hide. It was the family's, the salt's, the god's—passed down hand to hand like a jar, and every keeper before me had made the same mistake I had: we'd thought inheritance meant keeping it safe.

It meant passing it on.

The last thing I saw clearly was the pilgrims. The old woman had stopped singing. They were all looking at me—or past me, or through me, I honestly couldn't tell you where I ended—and their faces weren't afraid anymore. That mattered. I don't know why it mattered so much, but it did. I'd spent my whole life making people afraid of the wrong things, including myself, and it seemed important, at the end, to be the reason for a different expression entirely.

The salt rose around me like a tide coming in.

Somewhere very far away, or very close—I'd lost the ability to distinguish—Cassel was shouting my name. Of course he was. Twenty years of hunting me and he still couldn't manage to arrive on time.

Hold the line, I thought, and let go.

* * *

Dying, it turns out, is mostly listening.

The noise of the world came back slowly, in pieces, the way hearing returns after a blast: first pressure, then murmur, then voices sorting themselves out of the murmur. Wind. Gulls, absurdly, though we were a hundred miles from any living sea. And underneath it all, a low sustained note that wasn't a sound so much as a *presence*—the god, unbound, humming through the salt the way Veyl used to hum through a whetstone. Off-key. Still off-key. Some things survive anything.

I was lying on my back on the flats. I knew because I could feel the crystals against my shoulders, sharp and specific, which seemed like poor service for a woman who'd just saved the world. Above me the sky was doing something extravagant—clouds breaking apart over the place where the Door had been, letting down columns of light like the heavens were taking credit.

Hands were under me. Lifting. Careful hands, soldier's hands, moving with the economy of a man who has carried the wounded before and expects to carry them again.

"Don't," Cassel was saying—to me, to himself, to the sky, hard to say. "Don't you dare. You don't get to make me a witness and then leave. That's not how testimony works. You stay and you *say it*, Marguerite, you say it so I don't have to spend the rest of my life being the only one who—"

His voice broke. I'd never heard it break. Twenty years of knowing the man, through war and purge and heresy, and I'd have sworn his voice was load-bearing.

I tried to tell him I was still here. Mostly. Sort of. The vocabulary for my current condition hadn't been invented yet.

The salt shifted beneath me, and I felt it answer him—not in words, nothing so crude—but the wind changed, and the light moved, and somewhere in the shifting air there was a warmth against his cheek like a hand, or a pardon, or just the acknowledgment that grief witnessed is grief halved.

He went still. Then, slowly, Inquisitor Cassel—hunter of the gifted, burner of warehouses, the most obedient man I have ever known—knelt down in the salt with my body in his arms, and bowed his head, and for the first time in twenty years, listened.

Above us, the clouds kept opening. Behind us, five hundred pilgrims began, very quietly, to walk—not away from the place where the Door had been, but around it, around *me*, in a slow orbit, the way water moves around a stone it has decided to keep.

And far off across the flats, at the edge of the blight, in soil that had been dead for thirty years—

something green caught the light.
