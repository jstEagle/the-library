## 22. Charms for the Faithful

The market of Brasse-les-Salines smelled different now. That was the thing nobody warned you about after the end of the world — not the banners, not the new maps, not even the Inquisition dissolving itself like sugar in warm water. It was the smell. Twenty years of fear had a stink to it, sour and close, like a room where someone had been crying with the windows shut. And now the windows were open, and the salt wind came through off the flats carrying something that wasn't quite a smell at all. More like a memory of a smell. Rain on stone that hadn't seen rain in a generation.

I set up my stall an hour before dawn because old habits die hard and mine had been buried twice already.

The stall was the same one I'd kept before — or near enough. Pine boards, a canvas awning patched in three places, a folding table that wobbled on its left leg unless you wedged it with a flat stone, which I did, every morning, with the same stone, which I had named. Don't ask me why. A man who has sold false luck to frightened people for two decades develops relationships with inanimate objects. It's cheaper than developing them with people.

On the table I laid out my wares: luck-knots braided from tarred cord, salt-charms in little glass vials, knuckle-bones painted with sigils that meant nothing and everything depending on how much you'd paid, and — new this season, and legal, gods help me, *legal* — small blessings. Actual ones. Consecrated by actual hedge-priests who had come out of hiding like mushrooms after rain, blinking in the daylight, astonished to find themselves not only unpunished but *wanted*.

I still didn't know if what I sold worked. That was the joke of my life, and I intended to keep telling it.

* * *

You should understand something about me, since apparently I'm going to be remembered now — there's talk of putting me in a book, which is obscene, but more on that later. You should understand that I am not a good man. I'm a survivor with a merchant's instincts and a liar's conscience, which is to say: a conscience that works exactly as hard as it needs to and no harder.

I gave Marguerite Joubert's name to Cassel's men.

Not her real location. A false trail, two days stale, pointing south toward the Meridian road when she'd gone west across the blight. I sat in a cell that smelled of my own sweat and told a lie with such conviction that I briefly believed it myself, which is the only way to lie well, and then they took me out and I prepared to die, and I want to be honest about this part because everyone keeps asking: I was not brave about it. I bargained. I wept. I offered them my supplier contacts, my mother's silver, a map to a smuggler's cache that I made up on the spot with such detail that I nearly believed in it too. I was ready to sell anything except the one thing I'd already refused to sell, and when they marched me into the yard where the pyres were stacked, I thought: *well, this is what honesty costs, and it turns out I couldn't afford it after all.*

They didn't burn me. They put me back in the cell. For three days I didn't know why, and those three days were worse than the yard, because hope is a cruelty the body inflicts on itself without any help from the authorities.

Then the walls shook. Then the sky over the western flats turned the color of a bruise healing. Then the guards simply walked away, all at once, mid-shift, like men remembering an appointment they'd forgotten for twenty years. Then a junior Inquisitor — a boy, barely shaving — unlocked my cell and said, "Go home," in the voice of someone reciting words he'd heard somewhere and found, to his own surprise, that he meant them.

So I went home. And here I am. Selling charms. Legal ones. Blessed ones. To customers who don't have to look over their shoulders while they buy.

It should feel like victory. Mostly it feels like the wobbling table: functional, familiar, and in need of a wedge underneath it somewhere.

* * *

My first customer of the morning, as always, was Marta the fishwife, who bought a charm against storms she didn't need anymore — the storms having calmed along the whole coast since the Door opened, though the fishermen refuse to say so out loud because calm seas mean smaller catches, and a fisherman would rather drown than admit the weather owes him nothing.

"Same as usual, Perrin?" she asked.

"Same as ever, Marta. The sea doesn't change, only our opinions of it."

"The sea changed." She said it flatly, counting out her coins. "Everyone knows it changed. My husband says the water tastes sweet past the second shoal."

"Your husband also says he once wrestled a seal."

"He did wrestle a seal."

"And lost."

"He *disputes* that account." She took her charm and paused, and her voice dropped into the register women use when they're about to say something true. "You knew her. The Joubert woman. Everyone says so."

I busied my hands with the cord. "I moved her along the coast road. That's all. A job."

"They say she opened the Door."

"They say a lot of things."

"They say she poured herself into it." Marta looked at me with those flat grey eyes that had watched a thousand tides and been impressed by none of them. "Is that true?"

And here is where I could have given the answer I'd been giving for weeks — the shrug, the deflection, the professional's smile. *Who can say? Stories grow in the telling.* But the salt air was doing that thing it does now, that soft answering hum against the skin, like the world listening in on its own conversations, and lying felt suddenly like spitting into a well I drank from.

"It's true," I said. "I wasn't there. But it's true. You can feel it, can't you? When the wind comes off the flats. Like being remembered by something enormous and gentle."

Marta nodded slowly. "Like my grandmother," she said, and left, and I stood there holding coins that trembled slightly in my hand, because I had never once in my life said a true thing to a customer and found it *easier* than the lie.

* * *

The girl came at midmorning.

I noticed her the way you notice anything in a market — peripherally first, a small shape drifting between the stalls, pretending to browse, which is the universal posture of someone who wants something they can't afford or isn't sure they're allowed to want. She was maybe eleven. Thin in the way of children who've been hungry recently rather than chronically; the refugee camps had emptied over the summer, families trickling back to the blight's edge where, everyone whispered, things actually grew now. Green shoots along the drainage ditches. Real grass, stubborn and astonished, pushing up through crusted salt.

She wore a man's coat cut down badly and shoes that had belonged to two previous owners at minimum, and she stopped at my stall the way pilgrims stop at shrines — with that terrible hopeful hunger that used to make my living and now just made my chest hurt.

"You're the charm-seller," she said. Not a question. Children don't bother with questions when they've already decided.

"I'm a merchant of small protections, yes. Luck-knots, salt-charms, blessings. What ails you?"

"Nothing ails me." Her chin came up. "I want a luck-knot. For my mother. She's planting at the edge of the blight and the ground is—" she searched for the word, "—*stubborn*."

"Ground usually is. It learned it from us." I pulled a knot from the rack — good braid, tarred cord, tight work — and held it out, and she reached for it, and that's when I saw it.

Her fingers brushed the knot and the salt in the air *answered*.

I don't know how else to put it, and believe me, I've tried, because half the city asks me now, as if I'm some kind of authority, which is the crueler joke. There was a sound — not a sound, a pressure, a sweetness — like a struck glass singing just below hearing. The morning light bent very slightly toward her hands. The knot in her fingers glimmered with frost-light that wasn't cold, and the girl flinched, snatched her hand back, and looked at me with an expression I knew down to the bone.

Terror. The old terror. The *hide it, bury it, deny it, they'll come for you* terror, worn by a child who was maybe four years old when the purge ended and shouldn't know that expression at all, except that fear outlives the reasons for it the way scars outlive the wound.

"It does that sometimes," I said quietly.

"It's not supposed to do that." Her voice cracked. "I didn't — I wasn't trying to —"

"I know."

"They said it's allowed now." She said it like a child reporting a rule she doesn't trust, because adults have lied to her generation before, or lied to her parents' generation, which amounts to the same inheritance. "They said the gifted don't have to hide. But I'm not gifted. It's just — sometimes things happen. Small things. Knots hold tighter. Bread rises better. It's *nothing*."

Small magic. Deniable magic. The magic of a girl who had already learned, from someone, somewhere, that survival meant making your gift small enough to fit inside a lie.

I had spent twenty years selling charms to people exactly like her. Frightened people. People who needed a luck-knot not because the knot did anything but because holding something in your hand is easier than holding nothing, and hope needs a handle. I had told myself for twenty years that my wares were worthless and my trade was honest precisely because it was worthless — that I sold comfort, not miracles, and comfort was no crime.

Now the salt in the air sang against a child's fingers, and the knot in her hand glowed like a held breath, and the law said it was fine, and the priests said it was blessed, and the world said *come out, come out*, and she stood there anyway, in her dead man's coat, apologizing for existing.

"Here's what you should know about that knot," I said.

She waited, suspicious. Good. Suspicion was a survival trait and I wouldn't talk her out of it.

"That braid is tarred cord. Three strands. Nothing special. I've tied ten thousand of them." I turned it over in my own hands, careful not to touch where she'd touched. "For twenty years, none of them did anything. I'm fairly sure of that. Fairly. Some mornings I wasn't, and those mornings I charged double." Her mouth twitched — almost a smile, strangled young. "But this one answered you. Which means either I've finally gotten good at my job, or the world has gotten good at listening. And I'll tell you which one I'd bet on, but I never bet against myself, it's bad for business."

"So it's... broken?"

"It's *yours*," I said. "That's what it is. It answered you because there's something in you that answers. And I'm going to tell you the thing I wish someone had told a woman I knew, thirty years ago, before she spent her whole life folding herself small enough to hide in a market crowd."

The girl's eyes were wide. Around us the market went on — Marta haggling, cart wheels grinding, somebody's baby screaming about a pear — ordinary noise, the noise of a world that had decided to keep going.

"She hid it," I said. "Her whole gift. Made it small, made it deniable, made it nothing. And it kept her alive, I won't pretend otherwise — hiding saved her life for twenty years, and I helped, and I'd help again. But the day came when hiding wasn't enough, and on that day she had to spend everything she'd been saving, all at once, for strangers." I pressed the knot back into her hands, gently, avoiding the place where the light lived. "Don't save it up like that. Don't fold yourself small. Use it for your mother's garden. Let the bread rise. Let the knots hold. Be *obviously* lucky, in front of everyone, until obvious stops being dangerous. That's the whole sermon. I charge extra for it normally, but today it's free, because you're my first customer of the day and I'm feeling generous, and also because I'm a terrible businessman and always have been."

She looked down at the knot. The frost-light pulsed once, softly, like something breathing in its sleep.

"How much?" she asked.

"One copper."

"It glowed. Shouldn't it cost more?"

"It glowed because of *you*, not me. I'm charging for the cord." I took her copper and bit it, ceremonially, because tradition matters. "There. Now it's a fair transaction between a merchant and a gardener's daughter, and no one can say otherwise."

She tucked the knot inside her coat, against her chest, where you keep things you intend to survive for. Then she hesitated, in the doorway of leaving, the way people do when they have one more question and are deciding whether it's safe.

"Monsieur? Did you know her? The one who opened the Door?"

Everyone asked that. Everyone wanted the story, the epic, the martyr with her arms full of light. And I had a whole answer prepared, the one I gave at the taverns when the wine was paid for: the Door, the creature wearing Veyl's face, the last measure poured, the god coming home through a dying woman like rain coming home to a dry field. I'd told it well enough that a novelist — an actual novelist, a man named Crane with ink-stained fingers and an appetite for other people's griefs — had sought me out to write it all down, which remains the most absurd sentence I have ever been a participant in.

But looking at this girl, with her stolen coat and her hidden light and her mother's stubborn garden waiting at the edge of the blight, I found I didn't want to give her the epic. Epics are for people who are safely finished dying.

"She was a customer of mine," I said. "A long time ago. First year I had the stall. She bought a luck-knot and paid with a coin she'd clearly bitten to check, same as I bite yours, and she laughed when I caught her at it. She was scared, that day. Scared of the wrong things, mostly. She spent years being scared of the wrong things." I shrugged. "She got it right in the end. That's more than most of us manage."

"Did the knot work?"

I smiled. "Ask the country," I said. "It's got its name back."

* * *

She went off toward the west gate, small and straight-backed, carrying her copper's worth of miracle against her heart, and I watched her go longer than a businessman should watch a paying customer leave.

The salt wind came up off the flats, right on schedule, and did the thing it does now — that soft pressure against the skin, that below-hearing hum, the enormous gentle attention of something that had been eaten and was slowly, patiently, becoming the land again instead of ruling it. It touched the awning. It touched the table. It touched, I swear on whatever's left to swear on, the flat stone wedged under the wobbly leg, which has never once slipped since the day the Door opened, and I have decided to take this personally, as a compliment.

I looked down at my wares. Luck-knots. Salt-charms. Blessings from hedge-priests whose names I was learning, whose hands shook when they consecrated things, because they'd spent twenty years practicing their faith in cellars and hadn't yet gotten used to doing it in daylight.

For twenty years I'd sold hope with a disclaimer. *No refunds, no guarantees, the management accepts no responsibility for outcomes, divine or otherwise.*

I picked up a fresh knot, plain cord, three strands, and held it in my open palm, and waited.

The wind moved through the market. Somewhere behind me, Marta was shouting about prices. Somewhere west, a girl was walking toward a stubborn garden with a glowing knot in her coat. Somewhere under everything, patient as salt, the god we had eaten and mourned and finally let go of was letting go of us right back — which is, I've come to believe, the only kind of blessing there is.

The knot in my palm warmed. Just slightly. Just enough.

"Well," I said to no one, to the wind, to her. "About time."

And I hung it on the rack at the front of the stall, unpriced, where anyone who needed it could reach.

It sold before noon. They all did.
