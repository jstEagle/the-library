## 1. The Ledger Room

The rain came in off the harbor at dusk, and Theo Eastwick counted it the way they counted everything: in columns, in sums, in the small cold arithmetic of what was owed.

They sat alone in the counting house of the Guild of Impossible Things, three floors above a chandler's shop that smelled perpetually of tallow and salt. The room was long and narrow, lit by forty candles because Master Corvane believed electric light distorted the truth of numbers — *a lie told under bright light is still a lie, but a lie told by candlelight confesses itself* — and so the whole ledger room flickered like something underwater. Shadows pooled in the corners and moved when you weren't watching them, which, in this guild, was not a metaphor.

Theo dipped the pen and drew a line through the evening's first entry.

**One (1) year, traded. Seller: widow of the Salt Road, aged sixty-one. Purchased by: unnamed buyer, paid in grief remitted. Condition on receipt: slightly worn at the edges, as all years are.**

The ink dried brown in the candlelight. Theo had stopped finding this horrifying around their third month. Now it was just work. You balanced the accounts, you initialed the margins, you locked the drawer, and you went home to the rented room where you did not sleep well and did not ask why.

Below the year entry came the shadows.

**Two (2) shadows, borrowed against future repayment. Borrower: a playwright who wished to be someone else onstage. Interest accruing at one hour of self per week. Note: borrower has begun returning letters unopened.**

Theo made a small mark beside that one. In six weeks the guild would send an enforcer to collect, and the playwright would either pay or become a very convincing stranger for the rest of his life. Not Theo's problem. That was the phrase they repeated like a prayer. *Not my problem.* It worked about as well as prayers usually did.

Third column: luck.

**Eleven (11) vials bottled luck, received from the mill at Graywater. Three cracked in transit. Contents escaped into the shipment of ordinary flour. Consequences: unknown, possibly delightful.**

Almost despite themselves, Theo smiled. Then the smile faded, because the next page was the one they'd been avoiding since noon — the quarterly reconciliation of the vault, the souls held as collateral, each one entered in a hand so fine it looked like embroidery. Theo turned past it quickly, the way you'd step over a body in the street without looking down.

Outside, the storm was building properly now. Thunder rolled up from the sea in long gray swells, and the candle flames leaned all at once, forty little soldiers flinching from a wind that hadn't yet found the shutters. Theo pressed their left wrist flat against the desk, beneath the oak, beneath the ledgers, and felt it answer.

The brand sat on the inside of their forearm, old scar tissue shaped like a branching bolt, and every time a storm came near it woke up. Not pain, exactly. More like a hand closing around their pulse from the inside. Like being remembered by something enormous.

*Storm-caller,* the guild had called them once, back before the exile, back when the word was spoken with respect instead of spat through teeth. They had called lightning down on a fleet — on whose orders, whose war, whose fault, depended entirely on which ledger you read — and afterward the guild had sheltered them, hidden the brand under binding charms and long sleeves, given them a desk and a pen and a life measured out in entries. Shelter with a price. There was always a price. Theo knew that better than anyone; they wrote the prices down for a living.

They pulled the sleeve down over the wrist and went back to work.

* * *

The reconciliation could not be avoided forever. At half past nine, Theo unlocked the vault drawer and laid the soul-ledger open under its own dedicated candle, because Corvane insisted souls be read by flame alone — *they keep better under firelight; they know what fire is* — and began the tedious business of confirming that everything stolen was still accounted for.

That was how Theo thought of it, privately, in the privacy of their own skull where no enforcer could hear. Not *held*. Not *collateralized*. *Stolen*, filed neatly, insured against loss.

Page after page. A farmer's soul taken against a debt for seed he never received. A child's, God help everyone, pledged by a father who'd gambled away everything else. Each entry bore the guild's seal in red wax, each wax seal pressed with the same sigil: a key inside a closed eye. *We see nothing. We open everything.*

Theo was three pages from the end when the door below slammed open hard enough to rattle the candles.

They froze, pen lifted. The counting house kept no hours for visitors; the street door was supposed to be warded, and the wards were supposed to sing a warning down the speaking-tube before anyone reached the stairs. No song came. Just boots on the steps, uneven, dragging, and then the door at the top of the stairs burst inward and a woman fell through it.

She was soaked through, streaming seawater and rain and something darker that Theo's professional eye identified before their human eye wanted to: blood, diluted but plentiful, tracking from her hairline down her jaw, soaking the shoulder of her coat. She was maybe thirty, sharp-faced, dark-haired, with the particular pallor of someone who had been running for a long time and had finally run out of road.

Her eyes found Theo across the candlelit room. Found the ledgers. Found the seal on the spine of the open book, and something in her face went from desperation to a terrible, focused certainty.

"You're them," she said. Her voice was wrecked, gravel and salt. "You're the ones who keep the books."

"The guild is closed," Theo said, rising, keeping the desk between them, one hand drifting toward the bell-pull that would bring enforcers up from the lower floor. "If you have a matter to—"

"I don't have a matter." She took another step and her knee buckled; she caught herself on the edge of the desk, and the whole great oak groaned. "I *am* the matter." She laughed, wet and awful. "I'm in one of your books. I need to see which one."

Thunder hit close enough to shiver the windowpanes. Under Theo's sleeve, the brand burned like a struck match, and they pressed their arm harder against the desk and did not let their face change. Fifteen years of practice. *A lie told by candlelight confesses itself.* So did a face, if you let it.

"Sit down before you fall down," Theo said, and heard their own voice go flat and clerical, the voice they used for grieving customers. "You're bleeding."

"Bleeding's the least of—" She got the words out and then the rest of her strength ran out between one syllable and the next, the way a candle goes when the wick's spent. Her legs simply quit. She went down against the desk, caught its corner with her hip, and slid, and Theo lunged too late to do more than break part of the fall. She ended up half-collapsed against the leg of the desk, one hand still knotted in the front of Theo's shirt like she was afraid the floor would take her if she let go.

"Listen," she said. Up close her eyes were extraordinary, gray-green and fever-bright, fixed on Theo with the ferocity of someone spending her last coin. "Listen to me. They sold mine while I slept. I never signed anything. I never— my brother—" A cough racked her, blood-flecked. "There's a court. Under the mountain. Under the *old* mountain, the one the maps pretend isn't there. My name is Mara. Find my name. It'll be in your books. It'll be there, because I can feel it gone, I can feel the shape of where it used to—"

"Stop talking," Theo said. "Save your breath."

"No. No time. They know I've run." Her grip tightened. With her free hand she pulled something from inside her coat and pressed it against Theo's chest, hard, insistent, leaving a smear of red on the guild-gray cloth. It was a map — folded small, oilcloth-wrapped, stained dark at one corner. Old paper. Older ink. "Take it. Take it *inside* yourself, do you understand? Don't put it in a drawer. Drawers are theirs. Everything in a drawer is theirs."

"Mara—"

"That's my name. Remember it. Names are the only thing they can't forge." Her eyes were losing focus even as she said it, sliding toward the candleflames. "The map only opens for two. I don't know why. The ferryman said— the ferryman said..." Her head tipped back against the desk leg. "...said the debt wants witnesses..."

And she was out. Not dead — Theo checked, fingers to her throat, pulse quick and shallow as a bird's — but gone somewhere deep, her weight suddenly all real, all at once, dragging at Theo's arm.

For a long moment Theo knelt on the floor of the counting house, holding a bleeding stranger, listening to the storm lean on the roof.

Then, gently, they pried the map free of her grip and set it on the desk, and looked at it, and looked at the open soul-ledger beside it, at the fine embroidered handwriting and the red seals, key-in-eye, key-in-eye, dozens upon dozens of them marching down the page like a procession of small closed eyes that saw everything and admitted nothing.

Under Theo's sleeve, the brand pulsed with the thunder, patient as tide.

Somewhere below, a ward finally sang its late warning, and boots began on the stairs — heavy ones, several pairs, the tread of men asking questions they already knew the answers to.

Theo closed the ledger, tucked the map into their shirt, and stood up wearing the face they wore for auditors.

*Not my problem,* they thought, and for the first time in fifteen years, the prayer didn't take.
