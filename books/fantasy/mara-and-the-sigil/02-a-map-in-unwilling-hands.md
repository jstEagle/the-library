## 2. A Map in Unwilling Hands

The woman was still bleeding on the counting house floor when the storm found its voice again.

Theo had done what could be done without magic—stripped off their coat, wadded it against the gash along her ribs, propped her head on a rolled ledger of borrowed shadows. The wound was ugly but not mortal, the kind of cut that meant someone had wanted to slow her down rather than stop her. That distinction mattered. Theo had spent six years learning to read wounds the way other people read weather, and this one said *pursuit*.

Outside, rain hammered the shutters like a creditor's fist.

"Hey." Theo pressed two fingers beneath her jaw. Pulse quick and shallow, but there. "You're in the Vellum & Crow. Counting house of the Guild of Improbable Exchange. You're safe, more or less."

Her eyes opened. They were gray, the color of slate before it decides to storm, and they fixed on Theo with an urgency that outran her body entirely.

"You're guild," she rasped.

"I keep the books."

"Then you can read." Her hand shot out and closed around Theo's wrist with a strength that had no business living in her arms. "Then you can *read*."

She dragged something from inside her ruined jacket—a roll of oilcloth, tied with waxed cord, stained dark at one end where blood had soaked through. She pushed it against Theo's chest hard enough to bruise.

"Take it."

"I don't take things from dying women. Bad for the ledgers."

"I'm not dying." A wet laugh cost her something; she winced around it. "I'm *owed*. There's a difference. Ask your masters—they'll know. They brokered it."

Theo's hands went still on the pressure bandage. The candle flame between them leaned sideways in a draft that shouldn't have existed indoors.

"What did they broker?"

But she wasn't listening anymore. She was watching the door, ears straining against the storm, and whatever she heard there made her grab Theo's collar and pull them close enough that Theo could count the exhaustion in her face, layer under layer like sediment.

"The court beneath the mountain," she whispered. "That's where they took it. That's where it's kept. The map shows the way in—but it only opens for—" Her breath hitched. "It needs two. It only opens for two who hold it together. Remember that. Whatever else you forget, remember *that*."

"Hold what together? Hold it how?"

She didn't answer. Her eyes rolled back and she sagged against the desk leg, and the map sat in Theo's hands like something alive, warm from her body, heavier than oilcloth and paper had any right to be.

* * *

Theo did not open it.

That was the first discipline of the ledger room: you do not read what is not entered in your charge. Every parcel that crossed the counter got weighed, sealed, logged—not examined. Curiosity was a liability the guild taxed heavily.

So Theo set the roll on the desk, beside the inkwell, in the exact center of the blotter, and went back to pressing the stranger's wound closed with needle and gut thread from the mending drawer, because whatever else she was, she was now a liability lying unconscious on guild property, and liabilities were Theo's problem until someone senior decided otherwise.

The storm eased toward midnight. In the quiet after it, Theo heard the horses.

Not hooves on the street—the Vellum & Crow stood at the end of a cobbled lane where the city gave up pretending to be respectable. Hooves on the *lane*, at half past midnight, three sets, riding unhurried. Enforcers never hurried. Hurrying suggested urgency, and urgency suggested the guild had been caught off guard, and the guild was never caught off guard. That was the whole point of the guild.

Theo blew out the candles except one, dragged a blanket over the stranger, and stood behind the counter with their sleeves pulled down over the brand on their forearm just as the door swung open without a knock.

Three of them. Rain steaming off black oilskins. The one in front was Brannoc Hale, senior collector, a man built like a strongbox and about as easy to open. His eyes went to the blanket on the floor, then to Theo, and stayed there.

"Eastwick." He said the name the way he'd read an entry: flat, complete, filed. "Storm brought anything in tonight?"

"Rain," Theo said. "And a leak over the eastern stacks. I've flagged it for repair."

Hale walked forward. He didn't look at the blanket again, which was worse than looking. He looked at the desk—at the blotter—at the oilcloth roll sitting dead center where any fool could see it, because Theo had put it there, because hiding it would have been a decision and Theo had not yet decided anything.

"Whose is that?"

"A delivery. Unclaimed. I'll log it in the morning."

"Unclaimed deliveries come through the door, Eastwick. Not through storms." He picked up the roll. Turned it over once. Theo watched his thumb find the bloodstain and rest there, reading it the way Theo read entries. "There's a woman missing out of Harrowgate. Sister of a man who does business with the guild. Family's worried."

"People go missing all the time. We trade in it."

Something flickered across Hale's face—not quite a smile. "We do. So if she came through here, bleeding, carrying guild property—" he set the roll back down, precisely where it had been, which told Theo he'd already known exactly what it was— "that would be an entry requiring my attention."

"She didn't come through here."

"Where did she come through, then?"

"The storm," Theo said. "Doors were open. I found her on the step."

Hale studied them for a long moment. Rain ticked against the shutters. Behind the counter, under the blanket, the stranger breathed slow and even—too even, Theo realized. She was awake. She was listening. And she was holding very, very still, the way people do who have learned that stillness is the only armor left.

"Master Corvane will want a full accounting," Hale said at last. "Of the night's entries. All of them." He tapped the blotter once with one thick finger, an inch from the map. "You know how Corvane feels about unlogged items."

"They upset the balance," Theo said.

"They do." He turned for the door, then paused, half into the rain-smell of the night. "You've been reliable, Eastwick. Six years, no discrepancies. That's rare in a place like this. Rare things get noticed." He pulled his hood up. "Both kinds of noticed."

The door closed behind them. Hooves faded up the lane. The candle flame straightened.

* * *

Theo stood in the dark for a long time before moving.

Then they crossed to the blanket and crouched, and the stranger's eyes opened—gray, alert, afraid in the specific way of someone doing arithmetic with bad numbers.

"You lied to them," she whispered.

"Yes."

"Why?"

Theo didn't have an answer. Six years of clean entries, of being the person whose numbers always balanced, of being *safe*—and one bleeding woman on the floor, and Theo had lied with the ease of long practice, which was the frightening part. Which lie had been the practice?

"Don't answer that," she said, reading their face. "I don't want to owe you gratitude. Gratitude's a debt, and I'm done with debts." She tried to sit up, hissed, pressed her hand to the stitched wound. "They'll come back."

"At dawn. With Corvane's authority. They'll search the stacks."

"Then we have until dawn." She nodded at the roll on the desk. "Open it. Please. I need to know you've seen it, or none of the rest of this works."

Theo crossed the room, untied the waxed cord, and unrolled the oilcloth on the blotter.

It was a map—not drawn, *pressed*, as if someone had taken a sheet of paper and held it against the mountain itself until the mountain confessed. Lines descended in spirals through strata of ink: gates, tolls, a long descending road marked in a handwriting too elegant to trust. At the bottom, beneath everything, a chamber drawn as a circle of small squares, each square labeled in script so fine Theo had to lean close—

Glass drawers. Dozens of them. Each bearing a name.

And at the center of the spiral, written in the same elegant hand, a seal Theo knew better than their own heartbeat. The Vellum & Crow. The guild's mark, pressed into the deepest level of the court beneath the mountain, over and over, like a signature on a confession.

"That's where mine is," Mara said quietly behind them. "Bottom row. Third drawer from the left. Go on. Look for the name."

Theo looked.

*Mara Vess. One soul, whole. Held as collateral.*

Filed under a debt number Theo recognized instantly, because Theo had balanced that account a hundred times—an old entry, a large one, signed and sealed by the guild's own master.

The candle guttered. Somewhere above the counting house, in the good part of the building where the ledgers smelled of cedar instead of blood, Master Corvane was asleep behind a locked door, and his name was on this page, and his collectors would be back by dawn.

"Who signed for you?" Theo asked, though the knowing was already settling into their chest like cold water.

"My brother," Mara said. "In my name. While I slept." She watched Theo's face with those slate-gray eyes. "Now you've seen it. Now you understand why I need someone who reads."

Theo's hands were shaking. They pressed them flat against the desk, against the map, feeling the pressed lines of the mountain under their palms like a pulse.

Dawn was four hours away. Four hours to decide whether to log this night honestly—and hand a bleeding woman to Brannoc Hale—or to commit the first discrepancy of a spotless six years, and become the kind of rare thing that gets noticed.

Theo reached for a fresh ledger page, dipped the pen, and wrote:

*One map, provenance disputed. One soul, status contested. One keeper, complicit pending review.*

Then, after a moment, in the margin, small enough that only a reader who knew to look would ever find it:

*Two who hold it together.*
