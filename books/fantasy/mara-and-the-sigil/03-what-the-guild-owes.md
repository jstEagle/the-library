## 3. What the Guild Owes

The ledgers did not sleep, and neither, it seemed, did Theo.

They sat in the counting house long after the enforcers had carried the stranger's bloodstained map away in a wax-sealed satchel — no. That was what should have happened. What had actually happened was simpler and far more dangerous: Theo had lied.

The memory of it still sat strangely in their chest, like a coin swallowed wrong. Enforcer Vask, rain streaming off his oilskin, his voice flat as a slate: *Where is the map?* And Theo, without thinking, without deciding, hearing their own mouth say: *She never had one. She was raving. Delirium and blood loss.*

Vask had looked at them for three full seconds. Three seconds was a long time in the counting house, where everything was weighed and nothing was free. Then he'd nodded and gone back out into the storm, and Theo had stood there with their heart hammering like a debtor at the door, wondering when exactly they had become the kind of person who lied to Vask.

*She pressed it into my hands,* Mara had said, before the dark took her. *It only works if—* and then nothing, just the wet rasp of her breathing and the storm screaming against the shutters.

Theo had hidden her in the old records cellar beneath the counting house, among the dead accounts no one audited anymore — debts of clients forty years deceased, trades so old even Master Corvane had forgotten them. A woman bleeding out could lie there unnoticed as easily as a cancelled contract. It wasn't mercy, Theo told themselves. It was procedure. Unregistered assets were a liability. You didn't hand a liability to Vask; you secured it, catalogued it, understood it first.

That was the story they told themselves, anyway, somewhere around the fourth candle.

Now they pulled ledgers off the shelves by candlelight, one after another, following the thread of a name. *Mara.* It was a common enough name. It would be nothing. It would be a fishwife in Harrowmere or a midwife who'd borrowed luck against a difficult birth. It would be routine, and then Theo could stop feeling this tight wire of dread strung from their throat to their stomach.

The guild's indexing system was Corvane's own design, elegant and merciless. Every entry cross-referenced. Every soul, every shadow, every traded year filed under name, origin, and collateral class. Theo worked backward from the night's date, through the recent volumes, into older ones whose spines crackled like kindling.

Volume 112. Volume 113. The ink went from brown to black to that strange violet the guild used for high-value entries, the ink made from ground nightglass that cost more per ounce than a house in the merchant quarter.

And there, in Volume 114, entry 4471, in violet ink, in a clerk's immaculate hand:

*Mara Vess, of Dunmark Hollow. Asset class: SOUL, WHOLE & UNENCUMBERED. Received as collateral against debt instrument 4402. Signed: Mara Vess.*

Theo stared at the signature.

They had spent eleven years reading signatures. It was the first thing they taught you in the counting house — before arithmetic, before the law of instruments, before anything. *A signature is a person compressed into ink,* old Clerk Fenner used to say. *Learn to read people in ink and you will never be cheated.*

This was not Mara Vess's signature.

Oh, it said *Mara Vess*, letter for letter. But the hand was wrong. The M was squared where a natural writer's M curves. The descender on the final s hooked left, a left-hander's hook, and the pressure of the strokes was even throughout — no hesitation, no fatigue, none of the small tremors that living hands leave in ink like fingerprints in wax. This was a signature copied, not written. Practiced. Forged by someone who had studied the original and reproduced its shape while missing entirely its soul.

And Theo knew, because they had seen the original only hours ago, when Mara Vess had gripped their wrist with a bloody hand and pressed a map into it, and her handwriting — scrawled, desperate, alive — had been loose and slanting and utterly unlike the neat forgery on the debt instrument.

Someone had signed her soul away in her name.

Theo turned to debt instrument 4402. The principal was staggering — enough to buy a district, not a farmwife. The creditor column bore no name, only the guild's seal and a reference code Theo didn't recognize: *C-7*. The date was fourteen months ago. The guarantor of record, the party who had brokered the transaction, initialed in the margin in a hand Theo knew better than their own—

Corvane.

Master Corvane had brokered the sale of Mara Vess's soul.

Theo sat very still. The candle flame leaned in a draft from under the door and righted itself. Somewhere above, the storm was moving off eastward, and the counting house settled into the particular silence of a building full of paper — that dry, patient silence, as though the ledgers themselves were waiting to see what Theo would do.

There were explanations. There were always explanations; the guild dealt in impossible things, and impossible things required paperwork that looked strange to untrained eyes. Perhaps C-7 was a standard code. Perhaps the signature had been witnessed properly. Perhaps Mara Vess had signed, and illness or injury had changed her hand. People changed. Ink endured. That was the whole point of ink.

But the guarantor's initials. The forged hand. The violet ink reserved for assets the guild expected to *move* — and souls only moved when someone wanted them, and who wanted the soul of a woman from Dunmark Hollow badly enough to forge her name?

Theo copied the entry number onto a scrap of paper, then caught themselves doing it and stopped, pen hovering. Copying was what you did when you intended to report. Reporting was what a loyal factor did. Eleven years ago the guild had taken them in — a branded child, storm-marked, exiled from two towns and one family, hands that called lightning when they should have been learning sums. The guild hadn't cared about the brand. Corvane had looked at Theo's scarred forearms and said only, *Can you count?* And when Theo said yes, he'd said, *Then you have a home until your arithmetic fails you.*

Their arithmetic had never failed them. Not once.

So why were their hands shaking now?

Footsteps on the stair. Theo closed Volume 114 and slid it back into place with a smoothness born of long practice, and by the time the door opened they were deep in an unrelated audit, pen moving, face arranged into the mild concentration of a factor at work.

Master Corvane stood in the doorway. He was not a large man, but rooms rearranged themselves around him the way water rearranges around a keel. He wore his age the way he wore everything — deliberately, expensively. His eyes went to the candles, to the open shelf, to Theo.

"Burning midnight oil," he said. "Or midnight something."

"The Harrowmere account," Theo said. "The interest calculations won't reconcile. Someone's been shaving years off the deposits."

"Mm." Corvane came further into the room. He never hurried; hurrying was for people who could be made to wait. "The woman. The one Vask found traces of at your desk. Blood on the floorboards, Theo. You had her here and let her go?"

"She ran," Theo said. "I couldn't stop her. She was—" *Delirious. Bleeding. Afraid.* All true things, arranged into a lie. "Confused. I don't think she knew where she was."

"Did she give a name?"

"No."

"Did she give you anything?"

The question hung there, delicate as a blade balanced on its point. Theo kept their eyes on the ledger page, on numbers that meant nothing. *She gave me a map. She gave me her name. She gave me a reason to pull Volume 114 off the shelf at midnight.* 

"No, Master."

Corvane studied them. Theo felt the study like weather — a change in pressure, a stillness before something. Eleven years, and they had never once lied to this man. They wondered if he could hear it, the way he claimed he could hear a false entry across a room.

"The court beneath the mountain has opened negotiations," Corvane said at last, conversationally, turning a small glass weight over in his fingers. "First time in a generation. When it opens, the guild will be asked to present certain... holdings. Certain assets due for transfer." He set the weight down on Theo's desk, precisely centered. "If anything unregistered passes through this house, Theo — anything at all — it jeopardizes negotiations that keep us all fed and free. You understand."

"I understand, Master."

"Good." At the door he paused, and did not turn around. "You've been a faithful factor. Faithfulness is the rarest commodity we trade. Don't devalue yours."

His footsteps faded up the stairs. The front door opened and closed. Rain again, lighter now, the storm's tail end.

Theo sat in the dark with the glass weight gleaming on their desk and understood it perfectly: it was not a gift. It was a marker. *I know something passed through your hands. I am watching. Prove your faithfulness.*

They waited until the last carriage sound died in the street below. Then they took the lamp down to the records cellar, past the dead accounts, to the far corner behind the shelving where a woman lay wrapped in a factor's coat, feverish, breathing shallow but steady, alive because Theo had lied twice now and was beginning to suspect they would lie a great deal more.

Mara Vess stirred at the lamplight. Her eyes opened — grey, fever-bright, and sharp underneath the sickness, sharper than any dying woman's had a right to be.

"You're still here," she rasped.

"I work here," Theo said.

"Then you read the books." Her hand crept out from under the coat and caught Theo's sleeve, weak but insistent. "Then tell me what I already know. Tell me what my brother sold."

Theo looked down at her, and at their own sleeve, and at the entry number burning behind their eyes — *Volume 114, entry 4471, signed in a hand that was not hers, guaranteed by initials that were his* — and heard themselves say:

"It wasn't your brother who signed alone. The guild countersigned. My master brokered it."

Mara Vess smiled, and it was the worst thing Theo had seen all night, worse than the blood, worse than the lie.

"Then you already know," she whispered, "why the map only works in your hands too."
