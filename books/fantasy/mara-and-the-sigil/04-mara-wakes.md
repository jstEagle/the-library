## 4. Mara Wakes

She woke to the smell of tallow and old rope, and for a long moment she did not know where the ceiling had come from.

It was low, slanted, patched with tar paper gone soft as cloth. A single round window let in gray light and the sound of gulls — gulls meant water, water meant the coast, and the coast was a hundred miles from anywhere she'd been standing when her legs gave out. She lay still and took inventory the way her mother had taught her: fingers, toes, ribs. Everything answered, most of it complaining. Her side throbbed where something had torn, but the wound beneath her borrowed shirt was bound in clean linen that smelled faintly of salt and spirits. Someone had stitched it. Someone with careful hands and no conversation whatsoever.

"You snore," said a voice from the corner.

Mara turned her head. The ledger clerk from the counting house sat on an upturned crate, knees drawn up, a stub of candle burning beside them. In the gray light their face was younger than it had seemed by candlelight, and more tired. Their sleeves were rolled to the elbow now, and she noticed — filed away without deciding what it meant — that they wore bandages wrapped around both forearms, neat as bookbinding.

"I don't snore," Mara said. Her voice came out like gravel dragged over gravel.

"Like a landslide." The clerk — Eastwick, someone downstairs had called them, Theo Eastwick — rose and offered a tin cup. Water, with something bitter steeped in it. "Drink. You've been out two days. The fever broke this morning."

Mara drank because her body overruled her pride, then looked around properly. An attic above some kind of chandlery or boatwright's shop; coils of hemp, a broken dinghy, crates stenciled with names that were obviously not the names of what was inside them. Smugglers' storage. Which meant the guild kept safehouses even here, at the ragged edge of its maps, which meant nowhere was far enough.

"Where are we?"

"Saltmere. Fishing town, smuggling town, town that asks no questions because questions cost money." Eastwick sat back down on the crate. "You're welcome, by the way."

"For what?"

"The stitching. The fever tea. Not turning you over to the men who came asking about you with very polite voices and very heavy boots."

Mara set down the cup slowly. "You didn't tell them I was here?"

"They asked if a bleeding woman had come through the counting house during the storm. I said no." Something flickered across their face — surprise at themselves, maybe, or regret. "First lie I've told in eleven years of ledgers. It went easier than I expected. That worries me more than the enforcers do."

"Then you're smarter than you look," Mara said, "and you look like a man who alphabetizes his regrets."

"I'm not a man."

"Fine. A person who alphabetizes their regrets."

"That's better, actually." But there was no humor behind it. Eastwick leaned forward, elbows on knees, and studied her with the flat assessing look of someone reading a column of figures. "Here's what happens now. You're going to tell me who you are, who cut you, and why you pressed a bloodstained map into my hands and said *the court beneath the mountain* like it was a prayer and a curse at once. And then I'm going to decide whether I made the worst mistake of my life two nights ago."

"And if I don't feel like telling you anything?"

"Then I walk downstairs, and the next people through that door won't be me."

It wasn't a threat, exactly. It was arithmetic. Mara recognized the type — she'd grown up around merchants' clerks, people who believed the world balanced if you just kept the columns straight. She almost pitied them. Almost.

* * *

She told it plainly, because dressing it up would only make it harder to say.

"My name is Mara Vess. I was born in Callow's Ford, upriver, a market town nobody's heard of because nothing worth stealing ever happened there. My father kept a wool factor's business. When he died it passed to me and my brother Aldous — half to each, per the will. Aldous never liked halves."

She pulled the blanket tighter around her shoulders. The attic was cold despite the season; cold came off the sea here like a grudge.

"Aldous borrowed money. Everyone knew it, he didn't hide it well, he never hid anything well. What I didn't know was who he'd borrowed from. Not a bank. Not a lender. The Guild of the Weighted Scale — your guild — the ones who trade in years and shadows and bottled luck. He signed notes against his half of the business until there was nothing left, and then he needed collateral, and his half was gone, so—"

"So he used yours," Eastwick said quietly.

"He used *me*." The word came out sharper than she intended, and she let it stay sharp. "There's a form. I've seen it since, in a copy I stole from their archive before I ran. *Deed of Collateral, Personate.* It requires the signature of the party pledged. My name is on it. My signature is on it. I have never in my life written those letters in that order in that hand."

"You're certain."

"I taught penmanship in the Callow's Ford parish school for four years, clerk. I know my own hand the way you know your own teeth. He forged it while I slept — that part I only pieced together after. I'd been ill that winter. Feverish, sleeping whole days. Convenient, isn't it? A sister too sick to sign her own life away awake."

Eastwick said nothing, but their jaw tightened, and Mara thought: *good. Be angry. Anger is the one honest thing your guild has ever produced.*

"The debt was called due last month. All of it, at once, which no legitimate creditor does unless they want the collateral more than the coin. Men came to the house. Not bailiffs — bailiffs carry writs. These carried a brass box, and inside the box was a receipt, and the receipt said my soul had been sold at auction to satisfy the balance." She laughed, short and ugly. "I didn't believe it either. Then I started dreaming of a mountain. Every night, the same mountain, and under it a hall full of drawers, and every night I woke further from myself. Colors went first. I stopped being able to remember the taste of plums. My mother's voice went thin, like a song played through a wall. That's what it's like, being sold. Nobody tells you that. You don't vanish. You *fade*, in installments."

"And the map?"

"I found it in our father's things. He was a factor; factors know things. There's a road into the court beneath the mountain — a real road, with a tollgate and a ferryman and doors that open for the living as well as the dead, because the court likes visitors, it likes *customers*. The map shows the way. But—" she hesitated, hating the next part, hating that it was true, "—it doesn't work alone. I tried. For three weeks I tried. Ink swims, lines rearrange, the road goes somewhere else entirely. It's keyed. Blood-keyed, soul-keyed, I don't know. It only holds true when two hands hold it. Two people who both have reason to go down."

"Why yours and mine?"

"Yours I can't explain yet. Mine is obvious." She met their eyes. "I'm the collateral walking around loose. The court wants its property delivered. The map knows it."

The candle guttered. Somewhere below, a door opened and closed, and they both went silent until footsteps faded toward the street.

* * *

Eastwick fetched the map after dark.

They brought it wrapped in oilcloth, unwrapped it on the floor between them with the reverence of a priest handling relics, and Mara saw again the brown stains along its edges — her own blood, given freely in the counting house because she'd been afraid the clerk would burn it or surrender it, and blood freely given was the only ink the thing respected.

"Hold one corner," Eastwick said.

"Why?"

"Because you said it takes two, and because I want to see it work before I decide how much of my life I'm spending on it."

Mara took the western edge. Eastwick took the eastern. For a moment nothing happened, and she felt the old humiliation rise — *maybe I imagined it, maybe the fever, maybe I'm just a woman dying slowly in an attic* —

Then the ink moved.

Lines crawled like insects waking. The coastline of the map shivered and re-drew itself, and roads that hadn't been there surfaced through the parchment like bones through skin, and at the center of it all a mountain rose in strokes so fine they might have been engraved, with a spiral descending beneath it, turn after turn after turn, down to a small precise symbol at the bottom that looked like a drawer seen edge-on.

Eastwick made a sound low in their throat. Their hands were shaking. Mara watched the bandaged forearms tremble and thought: *they've seen something like this before. They've felt something like this before.*

"It's showing the tollgate," Eastwick said. "And the ferryman. And—" they stopped.

"What?"

"There's a second route. Marked in different ink. Newer ink." They looked up, and in the candlelight their face had gone the color of the tallow. "It's marked in guild cipher. This map has been used by the guild before. Recently. And the newer route doesn't lead to the court's front door. It leads to the vault."

"The vault."

"Where they keep what they sell." Eastwick began rolling the map up with hands that weren't quite steady. "Eleven years I've balanced accounts for these people. Years. Shadows. Luck. I told myself it was all metaphor. Ledger language. Nobody actually *held* any of it in a drawer."

"Now you know."

"Now I know." They stood, walked to the window, stood with their back to her for a long time. When they spoke again their voice was carefully level, the voice of someone reading figures aloud. "Corvane came to me the morning after you collapsed. Asked for the map. Said the woman was a fraud, a debtor running from a lawful claim. I told him I'd lost it in the storm." A pause. "Second lie. They're getting easier. That should frighten me."

"Does it?"

"Yes." They turned around. "But not as much as the vault does. So here's my terms, Vess. We go down together — your road, my knowledge of how the guild works, because Corvane will send hunters and I know their procedures better than they do. We find whatever record holds your claim. And we void it. After that, we owe each other nothing."

Mara studied them for a long moment. The careful hands. The bandaged arms they hadn't explained. The lies accumulating like snowfall on a branch that would eventually break.

"You could still sell me," she said. "Right now. Walk downstairs, make one call, and Corvane clears your debts and pats your head. Why haven't you?"

Eastwick looked at the rolled map in their hands as if it were a live coal.

"Because I found your entry in the ledger," they said. "*Deed of Collateral, Personate,* witnessed and sealed. And because the seal beside your brother's forged signature — the broker's seal, the one that made the sale lawful —" they swallowed, "— was Corvane's own."

Outside, far off across the water, thunder rolled though the sky was clear, and Mara saw the clerk flinch as if the sound had spoken their name.

"We leave at dawn," Eastwick said. "Before they realize I lied twice."
