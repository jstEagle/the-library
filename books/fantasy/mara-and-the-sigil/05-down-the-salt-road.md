## 5. Down the Salt Road

The salt flats began where the road gave up pretending.

Theo had walked the salt road once before, years ago, in the company of guild wagons and guild guards, and had thought it the ugliest country they'd ever seen: a white crust stretching to the horizon, cracked into hexagonal plates like the skin of something ancient and scalded. Nothing grew. Nothing lived, except the things that fed on travelers. The guild ran caravans across it twice a year because the mountain court paid well for goods that couldn't be traced, and because salt drowned scent, and sound, and — if you believed the old stories — prayer.

Now Theo crossed it on foot with a woman who could barely stand, three days ahead of the hunters, and found it beautiful. Ugly things looked different when they were the only cover you had.

Mara's wound had closed but not healed. She walked with her arm pressed to her side and refused, every time Theo offered, to lean on them. She saved her breath for walking. At night she slept sitting up against whatever stone or dead post the flats offered, one hand always on the map, which she'd taken back without apology and kept inside her coat, against her skin.

"It works better warm," she'd said, as if that explained anything. Maybe it did. Everything about the map was wrong. It showed roads that hadn't existed in a century and omitted the ones that had, and when Theo held one corner of it alone, the ink went gray and meaningless, like a ledger entry struck through.

They made eight miles the first day. Six the second. On the third day, Theo saw the riders.

* * *

There was no high ground on the flats, so Theo used the next best thing: a dead village.

It crouched at the edge of the white like a row of pulled teeth — nine houses, maybe ten, their timber bleached the same color as the salt, doors gone, windows gone, everything that could rot long since rotted. A well in the center with a rotted crossbeam. Theo had passed through places like it before, on guild business, and had learned not to ask what emptied them. Some debts were collected from whole towns at once.

"Inside," Theo said. "The far house. Don't light anything."

Mara looked at the sky, then at Theo. "Riders?"

"Three. Maybe four. They're not riding a line, they're riding a search pattern." Theo had spent eleven years reading ledgers; patterns were the same skill wearing different clothes. "Guild. They're quartering the flats."

"How can you tell?"

"Because nobody else searches this carefully for people who aren't worth finding." Theo said it lightly and hated how much it cost. Three days ago they'd have said it with pride.

They took the last house in the row, the one whose back wall had half-collapsed into a drift of salt, giving them a view of the flats behind. Mara lowered herself onto the floorboards with a hiss and set the map on her knees. Theo crouched at the broken wall and watched the horizon through the gap between two fallen beams.

The riders came into view an hour past noon. Four of them, dark against the white, moving in the patient grid of professionals. Even at distance Theo recognized the cut of their coats. Guild gray. And leading them, riding slightly ahead the way seniority rode, a figure Theo knew by posture alone: Vess Halloway, who had taught Theo to double-entry a soul-conveyance and who had once, drunk at midwinter, called Theo *the best hand I ever trained and the worst thing I ever helped hide.*

"They'll check the village," Mara said. Not a question.

"They'll check the village."

"Then we run now, while they're still—"

"And they see two figures crossing open salt for six miles." Theo shook their head. "We're faster than they think and slower than we need to be. You especially."

Mara's jaw tightened. She knew it was true. "Then what?"

Theo didn't answer, because the answer had been sitting in their chest for three days, heavy as swallowed lead, and saying it aloud would make it real.

The hunters reached the near end of the village. Theo heard boots on salt-crusted boards. Heard Vess's voice, flat and carrying: "Check the wells. Check under floors. If they came this way, they stopped somewhere."

* * *

Later, Theo would try to remember making the decision and find nothing there — no moment of choice, just a gap, the way there was a gap in every storm before the first strike.

What Theo remembered was standing up. Walking out the front door of the dead house with their hands empty and their sleeves down. Standing in the salt street while four riders reined in around them, and Vess Halloway looked down with an expression that was equal parts relief and grief.

"Eastwick." Vess dismounted slowly. "Thank the tall gods. I told Corvane you'd been coerced. Sick woman, blood loss, bad map — anyone would shelter her. Come home. Hand over the map, bring her in for questioning, and this all goes away. Your room's still made up. I checked."

"You checked," Theo repeated.

"I argued for you." Vess stepped closer, voice dropping. "That has to count. Eleven years, Eastwick. The guild took you in when no one else would touch a branded—"

"Don't." The word came out harder than Theo intended. Behind them, in the doorway, he heard Mara shift her weight.

Vess's eyes flicked past Theo's shoulder. "The woman. Step out where I can see you. No one's going to hurt you — we just need your statement. There's a matter of a debt—"

"She doesn't owe a debt," Theo said. "You know that. I read the entry, Vess. Signed in her name by another hand. The guild brokered the sale anyway. That's not collection. That's theft with paperwork."

Something moved behind Vess's face — Theo had seen it before, in the counting house, in the faces of clerks who found an entry they wished they hadn't. The other three riders shifted in their saddles. One of them, young, rested his hand on his coil of binding-cord.

"The Master says the map comes back," Vess said. "And the woman comes back. Those are the terms. I'd hoped to offer them gently."

"Gently." Theo almost laughed. "You crossed the salt flats with binding cord to offer something gently."

"Eastwick—" Vess sighed, and the grief drained out of his face, leaving the professional underneath. "Don't make me do this the way Corvane expects."

And there it was. The gap before the strike.

Theo had sworn, at nineteen, bleeding in a guild infirmary with a brand seared into their forearm and their name struck from every registry in three provinces, never to call again. The guild had found them half-dead after their first and only storm — a storm that had killed two people and unroofed a chapel and written Theo's name in every book of banishment from the coast to the mountains. The guild had hidden the brand under guild gray sleeves. Had given them ledgers instead of lightning. Had said: *this is your penance, and your protection, and your cage, and you will love all three.*

The guild had also taught them exactly how storms worked.

"I'm sorry, Vess," Theo said, and meant it, and raised their arms.

* * *

Storm-calling wasn't shouting at the sky. That was what ballad-singers thought. It was accounting.

Every storm was a balance: pressure owed here, pressure held there, heat borrowed from the ground and payable to the clouds. A caller didn't command the weather. A caller *found the imbalance and offered it a shape.* And the salt flats in high summer were the most unbalanced ledger Theo had ever stood inside — dry air stacked on hot ground, pressure mounting hour by hour with nowhere to go, a debt accumulating all day with no collector.

Theo had been reading it since dawn.

They dropped to one knee, pressed both bare palms flat to the salt, and opened the account.

The world tilted. It always did — the sensation of every weight in the sky suddenly noticing you. Heat poured up through Theo's arms, through the old brand, which woke along its whole length like a scar remembering fire. Theo breathed the way they'd been taught by no one, the way that had gotten them exiled: in through the ground, out through the skin, holding the shape of the imbalance in their mind the way you held a column of numbers.

Above the dead village, the clear blue sky bruised.

"No," Vess said. He actually stepped back. "Eastwick, don't — you know what happens when you—"

Thunder answered him. Not overhead — everywhere, rolling along the flat of the world like a barrel across a floor. The four horses screamed and bolted in four directions. The young rider hit the salt hard and scrambled backward on his elbows. Clouds assembled out of nothing, black-stacked, wrong for a sky that had been empty five minutes ago, and the air pressure dropped so fast Theo's ears popped and Mara cried out behind them.

Theo stood, arms still raised, and let the storm see them.

This was the part the stories got right, at least. The part where the caller and the storm regarded each other. It was not a negotiation. It was a debtor recognizing a creditor, and both knowing the account could only grow.

*One strike,* Theo told it. *Small. Close enough to scatter them. Then you go home.*

The storm considered. Storms always considered. Somewhere in its black architecture it weighed what Theo asked against what it wanted, and Theo felt, as always, the terrible arithmetic of it — the sense that it would grant the request and keep the change.

Lightning fell a hundred feet from Vess Halloway's horse and turned the salt to glass in a forked white star. The thunderclap knocked every rider off their feet. The rain came down all at once, a single drowning sheet, and then — as promised, as bargained — the whole storm tore itself eastward over the flats, dragging its thunder with it, chasing nothing, going home.

Silence. Steam rising off the salt. Four guild hunters on their hands and knees in a dead village, soaked and deafened and utterly undone.

Theo swayed. Their nose was bleeding. Their sleeve had torn back to the elbow, and the brand showed — the coiled spiral with its barbed tail, the mark of a storm-caller condemned, the reason three provinces had closed their registries to the name Eastwick.

Mara stood in the doorway, staring at it.

Vess got to his feet slowly. Rain dripped off his chin. He looked at Theo with something worse than anger — with recognition, with the awful tenderness of a teacher watching a student fail exactly as predicted.

"There it is," he said quietly. "Eleven years keeping that sleeve down. For what? For her?" He jerked his head at Mara. "You know what she is, Eastwick? Collateral. That's all. A walking entry. And you've burned us both for a walking entry."

"She's a person," Theo said. Blood dripped off their chin too. They wiped it with the back of their wrist, leaving a red smear beside the brand. "That's the difference between us, Vess. You see entries. I've started seeing people. It's a terrible habit. I recommend it."

Vess mounted his spooked horse with difficulty. He didn't look at Theo again. "Corvane will send more than four, next time. He'll send the Kindly Ones." He gathered his reins. "I'm sorry, Eastwick. I truly am. I liked who you were in the counting house."

He rode east, toward the storm, because it was the only direction left.

* * *

They walked until dark, putting miles on the hunters' humiliation, and made camp in the lee of a salt ridge with a fire small enough to cup in two hands. Theo's arms shook. Calling always cost — the tremors lasted hours, sometimes days, and the headaches were their own special tax. But the shaking stopped eventually, and then there was nothing left between them and the fire and each other except the things neither of them had said.

Mara spoke first.

"The brand." Her voice was careful. "The story they tell about storm-callers in the lowlands — that they're cursed, that they kill for pleasure—"

"I killed two people." Theo fed a stick to the fire. "First storm. I was nineteen and I didn't know what I was doing, and I found an imbalance over a market town and I shaped it badly. A man and his daughter died when the roof came down. So the stories are true, mostly. We just leave out the part where I was a child playing with a force I didn't understand, and the part where the guild arrived and said, essentially, *we can kill you, or we can own you.*" Theo turned their arm in the firelight, letting Mara see the spiral plainly. "They hid this. Fed me. Taught me numbers. Gave me a life so quiet I could almost forget what I was. Eleven years of quiet. And I told myself the quiet was worth it."

"And now?"

"Now I've seen their vaults, Mara. I've read their ledgers. Quiet bought with other people's souls isn't quiet. It's just a very well-kept grave." Theo looked at her across the fire — this stubborn, half-healed stranger who had bled into their life and refused to leave it. "Why are you still following me? You saw what I am tonight. What I did. You should be running the other way."

Mara was quiet a long time. When she answered, she pulled the map from her coat and laid it on the salt between them, and in the firelight the ink had begun to move — faint lines crawling, the mountain drawing itself closer.

"Because when you called that storm," she said, "you knelt down and put your hands on the ground like you were asking permission. Like you were apologizing to it." She met Theo's eyes. "My brother sold my soul while I slept, and everyone who touched the sale — every clerk, every broker, every master — handled me like an entry. You're the first one who apologized. To the sky, even. To anything." She pushed the map toward the middle, to the space between them. "Besides. It only works when we hold it together. I told you that. I'm starting to think whoever made it knew what they were doing."

Theo picked up one corner. The lines brightened. And there, crawling up from the southern edge, new ink was surfacing — a second route to the mountain, shorter, meaner, marked with a symbol Theo had only ever seen stamped on the guild's oldest documents.

A tollgate. And beneath it, in letters that faded almost as soon as they appeared:

*PAYMENT REQUIRED OF EACH TRAVELER. THE ROAD REMEMBERS WHAT YOU FORGET.*

Mara read it upside down and went pale. "Memories," she whispered. "It wants memories."

Theo stared at the words, and at the road ahead, and understood that the mountain had already begun collecting from them both.

"We camp here," Theo said quietly. "Tomorrow we pay."
