## 6. The Tollgate of Small Mercies

The mountain did not look like a place where souls were kept. It looked like a place where weather went to die.

Mara stood at the edge of the salt flats and watched the clouds break against its shoulders, shredding into rags that never quite made it over the peak. Below her feet, the salt stretched white to the horizon, cracked into hexagons like the floor of some abandoned temple. Three days they had walked it. Her boots had gone stiff with crusted brine. Her lips tasted of it even when she wasn't licking them.

Behind them, somewhere past the horizon, the guild hunters had stopped following. That should have been a comfort. It wasn't. Things that stopped chasing you on the salt road were usually waiting at the destination.

"You're limping," Theo said.

"I'm walking with character."

Theo didn't smile. They hadn't smiled much since the ambush — since Mara had watched them raise their hand and pull lightning out of a sky that had no business obeying anyone, least of all an exiled clerk with ink-stained fingers. The scars on their forearms had caught the light of that storm, pale ridges of old burns in the shape of chains, and Mara had understood then that exile wasn't a punishment the guild had given Theo. It was a wound they'd both agreed never to discuss.

She was starting to understand a lot of things she'd rather not.

"The ferryman's toll," she said. "You knew about this."

Theo's jaw worked. "I suspected. The ledgers mention threshold payments. They don't itemize what's paid." A pause. "I should have told you sooner."

"Yes," Mara said. "You should have."

* * *

The tollgate was not a gate. It was a boat.

It sat at the mouth of a river that poured out of the mountain's base — black water, unnaturally still, running without sound as if the mountain had swallowed every noise it made. The boat was long and low, tarred black, and the man poling it wore gray robes that might once have been fine. His face was smooth in the way of things that had been worn down rather than born: no wrinkles, no scars, no particular age. His eyes were the color of the water.

He looked at them the way a clerk looks at debtors.

"Two passengers," he said. His voice came from everywhere and nowhere, the way sound behaves underground. "One crossing. The toll is memory. Freely given, fairly weighed. Refuse, and walk around." He gestured with his pole at the mountain, which rose sheer for two thousand feet of unbroken stone. "Many choose to walk around."

"How much?" Mara asked.

"That depends on what you carry." The ferryman's gaze settled on her, and she felt it the way you feel a hand on the back of your neck. "You carry grief. Grief is heavy. Heavy memories weigh less each — fewer are needed." Something flickered across the smooth face; it might have been appetite. "For you, three. For your companion—" the eyes slid to Theo, "—two. You've already given so much away. There's little left worth taking."

Mara's stomach dropped. She thought of the vault below, the glass drawers, the souls filed like correspondence. Of course the court would want more than passage. Of course it would want to hollow them out before they arrived, so they'd be easier to deal with.

"What happens to what you take?" she asked.

"It goes into the water." The ferryman said it the way other men said *into the till*. "The mountain keeps what the mountain is owed."

"And if we pay, do we remember losing it?"

"No one remembers what they've lost. That is the mercy." He almost smiled. "It is why the gate is called what it's called."

*The Tollgate of Small Mercies.* Mara turned the name over and found it obscene.

"Three days," she said quietly, when they'd stepped back from the bank. "Three days I've been deciding what I'd give up if it came to this. I thought it would be coin. Blood. Maybe years."

Theo said nothing. Their hands were buried in their sleeves, the way they always kept them, hiding the brand, hiding the scars, hiding everything.

"My wedding day," Mara said.

Theo looked at her.

"I was married." She heard her own voice go flat, clerical, the voice she'd learned from watching Theo read ledgers aloud. "His name was Aldric. He died four winters ago — fever, nothing dramatic, nothing that makes a good story. But the day itself. The morning light through the chapel windows. My brother walking me down the aisle because our father was dead. Aldric crying before I even reached him, trying to pretend he wasn't." Her throat tightened. "That day is the last thing I own that isn't about the debt. Everything else — my name, my house, my future — it all got signed away by someone else's hand. But that day was mine. Nobody forged it. Nobody sold it."

"If you give it up, you won't know you gave it up," Theo said. "You won't know there's a hole."

"That's what frightens me." She met their eyes. "I'll be standing inside my own life and not notice the shape of what's missing. Like a tooth. Everyone says you stop tonguing the gap eventually."

"Mara—"

"Don't argue me out of it. You need your memories more than I need mine. Yours are the only map of who you were before the guild — and if we get my soul back, I'll need someone who remembers how to be angry about it. I can't afford to lose my anger. Anger is load-bearing."

Something moved behind Theo's eyes — something that looked, briefly, like it wanted to argue anyway, and decided against it. Later, Mara would turn that moment over and over. Later, it would matter enormously.

"All right," Theo said finally. "All right."

* * *

The ferryman took Theo first.

Mara stood on the bank and watched, and this was the part she would remember longest afterward — though not, she would come to realize, all of it. Theo waded to the boat without ceremony, sat on the wet planks, and held out their hands palm-up like a penitent. The ferryman bent close, and where his face touched theirs, the air shimmered faintly, heat-haze over cold water.

Twice he drew back with something cupped invisibly in his grip, and twice he let it fall into the river, and twice the black water accepted it without a ripple.

When Theo stepped off the boat, they paused on the stones, frowning slightly, the way a person frowns at a room where furniture has been moved. Then the frown smoothed away, and they looked at Mara and said, "Your turn," and their voice was steady and their eyes were clear and something in Mara screamed that she had just watched them lose something important and could not say what.

Then it was her turn, and there was no more time to think.

She sat on the planks. The wood was colder than it should have been, cold like the vault drawers must be. The ferryman leaned in, and his breath smelled of deep places, of stone and standing water.

"Choose," he said.

"The day I was married," Mara said. "All of it. Morning to night. Keep the whole thing — I don't want fragments haunting me."

He considered her. "Grief-weighted. Fairly priced. Two remain after this one." He raised his hands—

—and stopped.

His head tilted, the way a clerk's tilts when a column doesn't sum. He looked at Mara. He looked at the water. He looked at something in the water that Mara couldn't see.

"There has already been a payment," he said slowly, "made against your account."

"What? No. I haven't paid anything."

"Not you." The smooth face contracted, working through something that might have been confusion, or might have been the closest thing a creature like him could come to respect. "Your companion. While you slept on the salt road, two nights past. A payment tendered against your toll, without witness, without acknowledgment. It was... irregular." His eyes drifted to Theo, standing on the bank with their hands hidden in their sleeves. "Highly irregular. Payments must be made by the debtor."

"Apparently not," Mara said, and her voice shook.

"One of your three is satisfied." The ferryman straightened. "You owe two. Choose again."

Mara stood up so fast the boat rocked. "What did they give?" she demanded, turning to the bank. "Theo. *What did you give me?*"

Theo's face did something complicated and closed. "It doesn't matter."

"It matters *enormously*—"

"It doesn't matter because I don't remember," Theo said, and there it was — the crack in the steady voice, the thing underneath. "Whatever it was, it's in the water now. I chose it knowing that. I chose it while you were asleep so you couldn't refuse, because you would have refused, because you're the kind of person who'd rather drown than owe someone." Their jaw tightened. "So don't ask me what it was. I can't tell you. No one can. That's the mercy, remember?"

Mara stared at them. This person she'd known for barely two weeks — this exiled clerk who lied to their master for her, who burned their own skin calling storms to save her, who had just quietly amputated some piece of their own past and fed it to a river so that she could keep one more day of hers.

"You absolute fool," she whispered.

"Yes," Theo agreed.

"I'm going to find out what it was."

"I'd rather you didn't."

"I'm going to find out, and then I'm going to spend the rest of my life making it up to you."

Theo opened their mouth — to argue, probably, to deflect, to do any of the ten thousand small evasions that clerks were trained in —

And the ferryman cleared his throat.

"Two remain," he said. "The water grows impatient. And you should know—" the smooth face turned toward the mountain, toward whatever waited beneath it, "—you are not the only travelers expected today. Others crossed at dawn. Gray cloaks. Guild seals. They paid their tolls in memories of obedience, which the water found very nourishing indeed." He extended his pole toward the boat. "Choose quickly, debtor. Your hunters are already inside."

Mara looked at the black water. At the mountain swallowing the sky. Somewhere under all that stone was a drawer with her name on it, and somewhere between here and there were men who had followed her across a desert to make sure she arrived as merchandise.

She sat back down on the cold planks.

"The day I was married," she said again, steadier now. "Take it. And hurry."

The ferryman leaned in, and the world tilted, and Mara felt something lift out of her — gently, the way you'd lift a sleeping child — and she was left reaching for a name that had been warm, that had cried before she reached him, that had—

Had—

There was a gap. She could feel the gap, smooth-edged, painless, exactly as promised. A missing tooth she'd never tongue.

She stepped off the boat onto the far bank, where Theo waited, and she looked at this person who had paid her debts in secret, and she found — to her surprise — that she trusted them completely, and could not for the life of her say when that had happened.

"Which way?" she asked.

Theo pointed to a fissure in the mountainside, breathing cold air, lit from far below by a light that was not firelight and not daylight either.

"Down," Theo said. "And Mara — we're not alone down there. The hunters crossed ahead of us. Corvane knows exactly where we're going."

"Good," Mara said, and found she meant it. "Let him watch what happens next."
