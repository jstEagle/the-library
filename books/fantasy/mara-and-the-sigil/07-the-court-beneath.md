## 7. The Court Beneath

The mountain did not have a door. It had a *toll*, and past the toll it had a throat.

Theo had expected a cave, something crude and dripping, the kind of hole smugglers crawled through with lanterns clenched in their teeth. What they got instead was a stairway cut so smooth and so deep that the walls gave back their footsteps three seconds late, as if the stone were considering whether to repeat them. The steps went down farther than any stair had a right to go. Theo counted four hundred before giving up, and Mara counted nothing at all, because she had stopped speaking an hour ago and hadn't started again.

The dark down here wasn't empty. It was *furnished*. Veins of pale light ran through the rock like frost through a windowpane, brightening as they descended, until Theo could see the fine grain of the stairs and the way Mara's knuckles whitened around the strap of her pack. The map had gone quiet in Theo's coat — no warmth, no pull — which meant one thing only: they had arrived. Maps, like debts, stopped mattering once you reached the place they pointed to.

"Theo." Mara's voice came out scraped thin. "What is that smell?"

Theo had been refusing to name it for two hundred steps. Sweet, underneath. Like honey left too long in a sealed jar. And under the sweetness, something older and worse — the smell of a counting house at the end of a long fiscal year, ink and tallow and cold metal, the smell of value being extracted from things that used to be alive.

"Ledger oil," Theo said. "They burn it down here. I've seen the barrels upstairs."

"Upstairs," Mara repeated, with a small broken laugh. "You mean the world."

* * *

The stair ended at an archway carved from a single piece of black stone, and beyond the archway was the court.

Theo had grown up on stories of it, the way children grow up on stories of wolves. The court beneath the mountain, where souls were weighed and sold and held in trust; where the old powers kept their accounts; where a debt, once entered, outlived the debtor, the creditor, and sometimes the civilization that signed it. Every guild clerk learned the court's liturgy before learning to write: *All entries final. All balances sacred. All sales irreversible.*

Nobody ever said what the court *looked* like. Now Theo understood why. There was nothing to compare it to.

It was a cavern the size of a cathedral district, vaulted so high the ceiling vanished into haze, and it was filled — wall to wall, floor to impossible height — with drawers. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Little glass-fronted drawers set into frames of black iron that climbed the cavern walls like ivy made by an accountant, each drawer exactly the same size, each one glowing faintly from within with a light that was not firelight and not moonlight but something warmer and more personal, the color of a lamp seen through a window from a cold street.

And inside the drawers —

Theo stepped closer to the nearest frame, close enough to fog the glass with their breath, and looked.

A drawer held a soul the way a ledger line holds a debt: compressed, itemized, legible. This one glowed a soft amber, and within the glow Theo could see — not a shape, not a face, but a *coherence*, a pattern of light that moved the way a person moves when they think no one is watching. A small brass plate below the glass read:

**ENTRY 44,117 — FIRMIRIN OF THE ASH VALE — HELD AS COLLATERAL — INTEREST ACCRUING**

Firmirin of the Ash Vale. Theo didn't know the name. That was the horror of it — the name meant nothing. Somewhere above, someone had woken up hollow and hadn't known why, or hadn't woken up at all, and here was all that remained of them, filed under a number, accruing interest.

Mara had come up beside Theo without making a sound. She stood with both hands pressed flat against the iron frame, staring into the amber light, and her breath was coming fast and shallow.

"They're *awake*," she whispered. "Theo, they're awake in there."

"I know."

"How long? How long do they stay like this?"

Theo thought of the guild's training manuals, the dry procedural language, the chapter on collateral management that every apprentice skimmed and none of them discussed. *Held souls require no maintenance. Held souls do not age. Held souls may be transferred, pledged, or liquidated at the holder's discretion.*

"Indefinitely," Theo said. "As far as I know. As far as anyone wrote down."

Mara pulled her hands off the frame like the iron had burned her. She turned away, pressed the back of her wrist to her mouth, and for a moment Theo thought she would be sick. Then she straightened, and her face did something Theo had seen once before, on the salt flats, in the moment before she'd told Theo about her brother: it closed like a door.

"Find mine," she said. "Find my entry. I want to see what he sold me for."

* * *

There was a system. Of course there was a system. Theo had spent eleven years keeping ledgers, and the first thing you learned was that every archive is a map of its keeper's mind, and this keeper's mind was vast, patient, and organized by *creditor*.

The frames were grouped. Theo walked the aisles reading brass plates, and the names on the grouping-plaques were names Theo knew — some of them the way you know a scar. Merchant houses. Moneylenders. Two minor temples that had diversified. And then, on a plaque larger than most, polished brighter than most:

**THE HONORABLE GUILD OF THE UNCOMMON BARGAIN — ENTRIES 40,001 THROUGH 47,880**

Seven thousand eight hundred and eighty entries.

Theo stopped walking.

Eleven years Theo had worked for the guild. Eleven years balancing books that recorded traded years, borrowed shadows, bottled luck — the strange end of the trade, the end clients whispered about. And in eleven years, Theo had never once seen the collateral wing of the archives, never been given a key, never asked. You don't ask to see the cellar when you're paid to mind the shop. You tell yourself the cellar is where the wine is.

Seven thousand eight hundred and eighty souls. Theo walked the rows and the numbers climbed and the little lights burned in their drawers, amber and blue and one whole section of green, and Theo found themselves doing the arithmetic they were trained to do, the terrible fluent arithmetic: at standard brokerage rates, seven thousand entries represented roughly forty years of steady business. Forty years. Theo had drunk tea in the guild kitchen with clerks who'd been there forty years. Corvane himself had held his seat for thirty-one.

Every one of these had passed through a desk like Theo's. Had been entered in a hand like Theo's hand. Had been stamped with the seal Theo had pressed ten thousand times without ever asking what, precisely, it certified.

Theo reached out and touched one of the plates. **ENTRY 41,902 — SOLD, NOT HELD — BUYER WITHHELD.** The light inside that drawer was dimmer than the others. Dimmer, and slower, moving the way a person moves when they are very tired and have decided to stop hoping.

"Don't," Mara said quietly. She had come back to stand beside Theo, and her voice had lost its edge. "Don't touch them. Not yet. We can't open anything until we know how the vault works, and we can't learn how it works while its clerks are watching."

"Clerks?"

"You haven't seen them?" Mara nodded down the aisle, toward the far end of the cavern, where the frost-light gathered thickest. Figures moved between the frames there — tall, gray-robed, unhurried, carrying slates. They moved the way Theo moved through the counting house at night: with the total absorption of someone doing work they had done so long it had replaced them. "I saw them in my dream. Every night since the sale. They come and they *check the seals*."

Theo looked at the figures a long moment. Then, because the alternative was to scream, Theo did what Theo always did with dread: turned it into procedure.

"All right. We find your entry, we confirm the terms, we find the original instrument — there has to be an original, nothing voids without one — and then we get out and we plan. Agreed?"

Mara nodded. But she was looking past Theo now, at the guild's section, and her expression had changed again — sharpened, the way it did when the map warmed between their joined hands.

"Theo," she said. "Your master's seal. Show me what it looks like."

* * *

Corvane's personal seal was a thing of ugly beauty: a coiled serpent swallowing its own tail, struck in black wax, ringed with the guild's motto in letters so worn most clients assumed they were decorative. *Omnia Tenet.* It holds all. Theo had pressed it into soft wax more times than they could count — on bills of sale, on transfer orders, on the quarterly reconciliations that went into the deep archive and were never seen again.

So when Theo found the drawer — third row, eye level, in the subsection reserved for private holdings rather than guild business — there was no mistaking it. The brass plate bore the serpent and the motto, and beneath them, in the court's own austere script:

**ENTRY 43,556 — HELD IN TRUST FOR M. CORVANE — SOURCE: PRIVATE ACQUISITION — TERMS SEALED**

And beside the plate, struck into the iron itself, a second mark. Older. Different. A sigil Theo did not recognize — a circle broken by a vertical stroke — but whose *style* Theo recognized instantly, because Theo had spent a lifetime hiding a brand of the same make beneath long sleeves.

Storm-caller work. Someone had bound that drawer with storm-caller craft, and done it recently, the lines still sharp.

"Mara," Theo said. Their voice came out wrong. "This isn't guild business. This is Corvane's *private* holding. He has a soul of his own down here, held in trust, terms sealed —"

"That's not what I'm looking at." Mara was very still. "Look at the date plate."

Below the entry number, as on every drawer, was the date of acquisition. Theo read it, and the counting-house part of their mind — the part that never slept, that caught the misplaced decimal, the transposed digit — laid it against everything else they knew with a click like a lock turning.

Entry 43,556 had been acquired nineteen years ago.

Nineteen years ago, Corvane had been a junior partner. Nineteen years ago, the guild had taken in a disgraced storm-caller with nowhere else to go, given them ledgers instead of chains, and taught them that the trade was regrettable but necessary, that the court was a bank and not a butcher, that the best thing a person could do was keep clean books in a dirty world.

Nineteen years ago — Theo did the subtraction twice, three times, hoping to be wrong — was the year of the Purge of the Storm-Callers. The year the guild had claimed, in writing, in the histories, in the story it told itself every founding-day feast, that it had *refused* to participate. That it had sheltered the branded ones at cost to itself.

The drawer beside Corvane's held a soul that glowed a fierce, restless blue. Its plate read: **ENTRY 43,557 — HELD IN TRUST FOR M. CORVANE — SOURCE: PRIVATE ACQUISITION.** And its binding sigil was the same broken circle, and its date was the same year, and the blue light inside it moved the way a storm moves — the way *Theo's* storms moved, the way the light under Theo's own skin moved when they let it.

"Oh," Theo said. Very quietly. To the drawer, to the blue light, to no one. "Oh, you *liar*."

"Theo?" Mara's hand found their arm. "Theo, what is it? What's wrong?"

Down the aisle, one of the gray clerks had stopped checking seals. It stood motionless between the frames, slate in hand, its hood tilted toward them with the attentive patience of a machine that has detected an error in the books.

And in the blue-lit drawer, the storm-soul pressed itself against the glass — once, twice — like a fist knocking on a door from the inside.

Theo knew, with the absolute certainty of a clerk who has finally read the whole page, that they were looking at the price of their own shelter. The guild had not saved the storm-callers out of mercy. It had *bought* them. And somewhere in these seven thousand drawers was the entry that proved it — maybe under Theo's own name, maybe already spoken for.

The clerk began to walk toward them, unhurried, slate raised.

"Mara," Theo said. "We need to find your page. *Now.*"
