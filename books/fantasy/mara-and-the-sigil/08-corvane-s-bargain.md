## 8. Corvane's Bargain

The court beneath the mountain did not smell like death. That was the first thing Mara noticed, and the thing she would remember longest. It smelled like a counting house — ink and candlewax and cold stone — as if someone had taken everything terrible about the world and filed it neatly.

They had been walking for hours through corridors of black glass, past clerks who were not entirely people anymore, their fingers stained to the second knuckle with ledger-ink that never washed out. Theo kept close to her side, one hand always near hers, where the map lay folded against her ribs. Even now it tugged faintly, like a fish on a line, pulling them deeper.

"You're limping," Theo said quietly.

"I'm walking."

"You're limping *and* walking."

"The toll took my wedding day," Mara said. "It didn't take my stubbornness. There wasn't room in the jar."

Theo almost smiled. Then the corridor opened, and neither of them smiled again.

* * *

The vault was vast — a cavern dressed up as an office, if an office could be carved by something that hated geometry. Glass drawers lined the walls from floor to unseen ceiling, thousands upon thousands of them, each no bigger than a bread box, each glowing faintly from within with a light that had no color Mara could name. She felt them before she understood them. A pressure behind her sternum. A homesickness for a place she'd never been.

Her soul was in here somewhere. In a drawer. Filed under a debt she never owed.

"Third gallery," Theo murmured, reading the brass plaques by the light of their own pale brand — the storm-mark on their forearm, uncovered since the salt flats, since Mara had seen what the guild's long sleeves had hidden all those years. "Souls, collateral class. Alphabetical by debtor."

"And if the name isn't mine?"

"It'll be yours." Theo's voice was flat. "I've read enough ledgers to know how they make you into a number before they make you into nothing."

They found the drawer at the end of a row: NYSTROM is not there — wait, no, that's her surname? No. The plaque read **VANE, M.** — no. It read simply:

**MARA — SOUL, WHOLE. COLLATERAL. DEBT OF RECORD: 4,000 CROWNS. HOLDER: THE HONORABLE GUILD OF IMPOSSIBLE THINGS.**

Mara put her palm flat against the glass. Something inside pressed back. Not warmth, exactly. Recognition. Like a dog hearing its name after years.

"There you are," she whispered.

"That's when he came," she told Theo later, though later never quite arrived down there; time moved like syrup in the vault. "You hear footsteps in a place like that, and every clerk stops writing at once. That's how you know it's someone who matters. Or someone who's about to matter very much, briefly."

* * *

Master Corvane walked into the vault the way a man walks into his own parlor.

He was smaller than Mara had imagined. She had built him enormous in her head — the guild's master, the broker of souls, the man whose seal sat on her sale like a signature on a death warrant. But he was just a gray-bearded man in a good coat, with tired eyes and ink on his fingers, and that was somehow worse. Monsters should be larger than life. Corvane was exactly life-sized, which meant he could fit through any door, including hers.

"Mara," he said, as if they'd met at market. "And Theo Eastwick. My wayward bookkeeper." His gaze went to Theo's bare forearm, to the storm-brand, and something flickered across his face — not anger. Grief, maybe. Or its well-practiced imitation. "You've been showing that off in public. Do you know what I paid to bury that?"

"I know what *I* paid," Theo said.

"Yes," Corvane said softly. "We all pay. That's rather the point of the establishment." He turned to Mara, and his voice gentled into the tone merchants use on customers they've already won. "You want your soul back. I understand. I do. But you must see the position. This entry is an asset. Assets have holders. Holders have obligations. I am not a cruel man, Mara. I am a *solvent* one. There is a difference, and the difference keeps the lights on."

"What crime are you buying your way out of?" Mara asked.

Corvane blinked. For one unguarded moment he looked genuinely startled — and then, terribly, pleased. "Theo," he said, "you talk in your sleep, apparently. Or you read my correspondence. Either way." He sighed, and adjusted his cuffs. "Twenty-three years ago I sold something I shouldn't have. Someone. A person who trusted this guild, who signed nothing, agreed to nothing, and woke up lighter by one soul because a buyer paid handsomely and the ledger needed balancing. Does that sound familiar?"

Mara's hand found the glass drawer again. Pressed harder.

"It should," Corvane said. "Because I learned the trade from the best, and then I improved upon it. The court beneath the mountain holds the original record of my crime. One word from me, one payment, and that record burns. Clean slate. Pardon. And the price—" he gestured, almost apologetically, at the drawer holding her, "—is you. Your soul, whole, as tendered. The court finds it acceptable. They find you *acceptable*, which is more than most can say."

"You're feeding me to them," Mara said, "to erase the fact that you fed someone else to them."

"Now you understand commerce." He said it without malice. That was the horror of him. He said it the way a man says *it's raining*.

* * *

Then he turned to Theo, and Mara watched the trap close with both its jaws.

"Theo Eastwick." Corvane's voice changed — warmer, lower, the voice he must have used when he'd found a half-dead storm-caller years ago and offered shelter instead of a pyre. "Exiled for calling lightning into a guildhall full of witnesses. Branded. Hunted. Name struck from every honest register in three kingdoms. I hid you. I fed you. I gave you a desk and a candle and work worth doing when no one else would give you so much as a glance." He stepped closer. "Hand her over. Stand aside while the clerks do their work. And I will tear up your exile with my own hands. Reinstatement. Your name restored. Your brand struck from the hunt-lists. You could walk into any city in the world with your head up. You could call a storm in broad daylight and be *thanked* for it."

The vault was silent except for the faint hum of ten thousand drawers.

Mara didn't look at Theo. She couldn't. She looked at the glass instead, at the dim shape pressing back against her palm, and she thought: *this is the moment. This is where everyone leaves.* Her brother had left for less. The ferryman had taken her wedding day and hadn't even looked sorry. Every person she had ever trusted had eventually presented her with a bill.

"Theo," Corvane prompted gently. "You've balanced my books for six years. You know I keep my word. It's the only currency I've never debased."

"I know," Theo said.

Mara closed her eyes.

"I know you keep your word," Theo said again, and now their voice had something underneath it, something low and building, like the first pressure-drop before weather. "That's what makes this so simple, Master Corvane. Because I've read your ledgers. All of them. Six years, every page. And I know exactly what your word is worth, because I know what you wrote about *her*."

A pause. "Careful."

"Entry four-one-one-nine," Theo said. "'Soul received in good order. Debtor signature witnessed.' Except she never signed. Her brother signed. You knew — it's in your own marginalia, your own hand, *'signature irregular — proceed'* — you *knew*, and you proceeded, because the court pays double for souls surrendered clean and triple for souls surrendered *willingly*, and you have never once in your life declined a profit." Theo's voice cracked like ice on a pond. "You're not buying a pardon. You're repeating the crime. Word for word. Victim for victim. And you want me to hold the door."

"Theo—"

"You want me to be the witness this time. That's the part you really need, isn't it? The court requires a witness of standing. A guild member in good order. That's why you came yourself. That's why you're offering me the world." Theo laughed, and it was not a kind sound. "You don't want my loyalty. You want my *signature*."

Corvane's face had gone very still. When he spoke again, all the warmth was gone, packed away like winter clothes in spring. "Everything I built, I built to keep people like you safe. The guild is a wall between the impossible and the innocent. You would tear it down over one soul. One. Out of thousands."

"No," Mara said.

Both men turned. She opened her eyes.

"Not one out of thousands." She lifted her palm from the drawer, and where her skin had touched the glass, frost bloomed in the shape of her handprint — though the vault was warm, though nothing down here had ever been cold. "One out of one. Me. And then the next one. And the next. That's how it works, Corvane. That's how it always works. Somebody has to be the one, or nobody ever is."

The frost spread. Down the row, drawer after drawer, a white rime crept along the glass, and inside each one, something stirred.

Corvane took a step back. "Clerks," he said quietly. "Witnesses. Now."

From the shadows between the galleries, the ink-stained clerks began to move. And Theo rolled up their sleeve past the elbow, baring the storm-brand entire, which was burning now, blue-white, like a sky deciding.

"Mara," Theo said. "Get behind me. And whatever happens — tell me the oldest law again. The one from the founding charter. Tell me you remember it."

Mara remembered. She had made Theo recite it every night on the salt road, a superstition, a prayer, a joke between two people with nothing else to carry.

"*Any debt,*" she said, loud enough for the whole vault to hear, "*may be voided by destroying its record in the presence of the debtor.*"

Corvane's eyes went wide. "There is no charter down here—"

"No," Theo said, and the first thunder rolled somewhere above the mountain, impossibly far and impossibly near. "But there's a ledger. And I'm the bookkeeper."

The lights in ten thousand drawers flickered — and went out.
