## 9. The Torn Page

The vault of glass drawers stretched away from us in rows, each one lit from within by a light that had no source and no mercy in it. Somewhere behind those drawers, Mara's soul sat filed like a receipt.

Corvane's voice still hung in the air where he'd left it, that patient, reasonable tone he used when he was about to do something unforgivable. *Think of the guild, Eastwick. Think of what you were.*

I thought of it. I thought of the counting house and the candle-wax smell and the ledgers with their careful columns, and I thought of a woman bleeding out against my desk because the guild had sold her name to pay for a crime she never committed.

Then I did something no clerk of the Guild of Impossible Things had done in four hundred years.

I reached into my coat and pulled out the oldest law.

Not a book. Not a page. A law doesn't need paper — it needs only to be spoken in the right place, by the right person, at the right time. And there is no righter place than the vault itself, where every debt the guild ever brokered slept in its glass coffin.

"Article One," I said, and my voice cracked across the vault like the first drop before a flood. "The Founding Statute. *Any debt may be voided by the destruction of its record, witnessed in the presence of the debtor.*"

The nearest clerks — gray-robed things with pens for fingers and ink for blood — went very still. Stillness, for them, was terror.

"You don't have standing," Corvane said. He hadn't drawn a weapon. He never needed one; his weapon was procedure. "You're exiled. Your mark was struck from the rolls."

"My mark," I said, and pulled back my sleeve.

The storm-brand flared on my forearm, blue-white and furious, the sigil they'd burned into me at sixteen and then spent twenty years pretending didn't exist. The glass drawers nearest me fogged with condensation. Somewhere far above us, through a mile of mountain, thunder answered.

"I was never unmarked," I said. "Only unwritten. And Article One doesn't require a clerk in good standing. It requires a witness." I looked at Mara. She stood beside me, pale as the salt road, her hand pressed flat against the drawer where her own name was written in a hand too fine to be human. "And a debtor. She's both."

"Kill them," Corvane said quietly.

* * *

The clerks came on like weather — not fast, but total, a gray tide of them pouring down the aisles between the drawers, pens extended, ink hissing where it struck the stone. They didn't want to kill us, I realized. They wanted to *amend* us. To write us out of the moment, the way the guild had written Mara out of her own life.

Mara moved first. She'd been a smuggler's daughter and a debtor and a ghost for three years, and none of those people waited politely. She wrenched a glass drawer from its housing — it came free with a sound like a scream held underwater — and swung it into the first clerk's chest. The thing burst into a spill of ink and dried paper and was gone.

"That one owed me money," she said. Her voice shook. Her hands didn't.

I stopped thinking like a clerk and started thinking like a storm.

The brand woke all the way up. It had been sleeping under my sleeve for twenty years, fed on small denials and long sleeves and ledgers full of other people's stolen lives, and now it opened like an eye. Lightning isn't summoned. It's *invited*, and everything underground is desperate for the sky.

The first bolt came down through the vault's high dark ceiling and split the central aisle end to end. The second came off my knuckles sideways and took three clerks apart mid-stride. The third I aimed at the great iron chain that locked the vault doors open — Corvane's insurance, so no one could seal him in with his purchase — and the chain fell in a red-hot heap, ringing like a funeral bell.

"Eastwick." Corvane stood his ground amid the smoke. Ink spotted his fine coat. He looked, for the first time since I'd known him, old. "You're destroying four centuries of order."

"No," I said. "Four centuries of theft. Order would have been nice."

He lunged for me then — not with magic, not with guards, but with a clerk's weapon: he grabbed for my ledger arm, for the brand itself, and his grip burned cold as a creditor's handshake. "Your name," he hissed, "your pardon, your *home* — I am offering you everything they took—"

"You're offering me a share of the take."

I broke his grip and drove my shoulder into his chest, and we went down together among the fallen drawers, and for one bad moment his hands were at my throat and the world narrowed to his face above mine, calm even now, even bleeding, because Corvane had never once in his life believed the rules applied to him.

Then Mara hit him with a filing cabinet.

It was, I would think later, the most honest act of accounting I had ever witnessed.

* * *

We found her drawer in the seventh row, under the seal of the Guild of Impossible Things, countersigned in Corvane's own hand. MARA VELL. COLLATERAL HELD AGAINST DEBT OF 3,000 CROWNS, CONTRACTED BY T. VELL (BROTHER), BROKERED BY GUILD MASTER R. CORVANE FOR CONSIDERATION RENDERED UNDISCLOSED.

Undisclosed. That was the word they'd used for her life.

Mara stood over it, breathing hard, ink-streaked, alive in a way the vault was built to make impossible. "Do it," she said.

"Wait." My hands were shaking, and not from the lightning. "Article One requires the destruction witnessed in the debtor's presence. It doesn't say who destroys it. If I tear it, some future court could call it void for improper execution. But if *you* tear it—" I met her eyes. "If you tear it, it's yours. Your page. Your hand. No one can ever say it wasn't her choice."

She understood immediately. Of course she did. She'd been property for three years; she knew exactly what ownership meant.

Mara Vell put both hands on the drawer containing her own soul, and she tore the record in half.

The sound was small. That was the terrible part — after everything, after the salt road and the ferryman and the storm underground, the sound of a soul going free was no louder than tearing a letter you regret sending. The glass drawer went dark. The fine handwriting faded like breath off a window. And something rose out of the ruined page — not visible, exactly, but *present*, a warmth that passed through the vault like sunrise passing through a prison, touching every drawer it crossed.

Every soul in the vault felt it. I heard them. Hundreds of drawers, thousands, humming like struck glass, like a congregation saying *amen.*

Then the clerks remembered themselves, and the tide came back.

* * *

We ran. There was no dignity in it and no strategy — just two people sprinting down aisles of stolen souls while gray shapes poured in from every archway, pens first. Mara smashed drawers as she went, dozens of them, dozens of torn pages, and each freed soul added its warmth to the air until the vault was less a vault than a bonfire of the unowned.

At the great doors, Corvane blocked our way. He was on his feet somehow, coat ruined, one eye swelling shut where the filing cabinet had educated him, and in his hand he held the one thing I hadn't accounted for: the master ledger. The original. Every contract the guild had ever signed, bound in leather and law.

"Tear this," he said, holding it out, almost gentle, "and you tear every debt. Every sale. Every pardon — including mine. Including *yours*, Eastwick. You'll never come home. You'll never be written anywhere again. You'll be nothing, forever, in a world that keeps records."

I looked at the ledger. God help me, I looked at it. Twenty years of exile weighed exactly as much as it had always weighed, and home weighed more.

Then I thought about what home had cost Mara. What it cost everyone in those drawers. What it had always cost, and who had always paid.

"The guild taught me one true thing," I said. "Debts must be honored."

I called the storm.

Not a bolt this time. All of it. Everything the mountain had been holding back, everything my brand had been strangling for two decades, everything the sky above the peak was begging to give — I opened my arms and let it come down through me and out, and the vault filled with white light and roaring air, and the clerks scattered like ash, and Corvane was thrown back against the doors with the master ledger tumbling from his hands.

I caught it before it hit the ground.

Mara and I walked out of the Court Beneath the Mountain side by side, through falling water and singing glass, past drawers that glowed now instead of gleamed, their occupants awake and waiting for hands brave enough to tear them free. Behind us, Corvane screamed something about consequences, about tribunals, about names.

Ahead of us, the long stair climbed toward a night sky actually raining, actual rain, falling on the actual world.

In my arms I carried the master ledger — every debt the guild had ever brokered, every soul it had ever filed.

"Mara," I said, as the first clean wind in years hit my face. "How do you feel about bookkeeping?"

She looked at the ledger. Then at me. Then she smiled like a storm coming in off the salt flats.

"Teach me everything."
