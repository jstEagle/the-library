## 10. Exile, Twice Over

The sun was the first thing that tried to kill her.

Mara had spent weeks beneath the mountain, in air that tasted of ink and cold stone and other people's lives, and when they finally climbed the last stair into daylight it hit her like a slap — warm, gold, indifferent. She stood at the mouth of the cave with her face tipped up and her eyes shut and let it hurt.

"You're crying," Theo said beside her.

"No I'm not."

"Your soul's back in your body for six hours. You're allowed."

"I'm not crying," she said, and cried a little more.

Her chest felt strange in a way she couldn't name. Not empty anymore — that was the thing she kept reaching for and finding wrong. For months there had been a hollowness under her ribs, a draft blowing through a room she couldn't see, and she'd told herself it was grief, or hunger, or the road. It hadn't been any of those. It had been absence, precise as a pulled tooth. Now the tooth was back and the jaw ached around it, and every breath felt like a payment received rather than a payment owed.

She flexed her hands. They were hers. She said her own name out loud, quietly, just to hear something answer.

"Mara Vess," Theo said, watching her do it. "In case you'd forgotten."

"I hadn't." She had, a little. "Say it again anyway."

"Mara Vess."

The mountain stood behind them, gray and patient, its court sealed now behind rubble and storm-scatter and whatever Theo's lightning had done to the clerks' beautiful glass drawers. Somewhere down in the dark, forty years of sold souls were either free or shattered, and Mara found she could not make herself care which. Corvane's ledger was ash. Corvane himself had not come up after them. Whatever pardon he'd been buying, he'd paid for it in full and gotten nothing, and the thought of him sitting alone in that vault with his blank pages was the only mercy the whole rotten business had produced.

* * *

They walked down out of the high country for two days before the guild found them.

Not hunters this time. Mara saw them first from the ridge above the salt road: three figures in gray traveling cloaks, no sigils, no drawn steel, standing in the middle of the track like men waiting for a coach. One of them held a lantern even though it was midday. Guild habit. Light for reading by.

Theo stopped walking so abruptly that Mara nearly went past them.

"That's Halloway," Theo said. "Left of the lantern. He taught me double-entry."

"Friends of yours?"

"Everyone is friends of yours until you're a line item." But Theo's voice did something complicated on *friends*, and Mara understood that this was worse than hunters. Hunters you could run from. This was the guild coming to say goodbye.

Halloway raised a hand when they were twenty paces off. "Eastwick."

"Halloway."

"The master's seal is broken. You understand what that means."

"I understand what everything means," Theo said. "I kept the books."

Mara watched the exchange like a woman watching weather. Halloway was older than she'd expected, soft-faced, tired-eyed, the kind of man who looked like he apologized to furniture. The other two hung back with the stillness of people who had done violence before and hoped not to again today.

"There are those on the council who argued for pursuit," Halloway said. "Corvane's faction is scattered but not gone. I argued otherwise. I said the guild has lost enough to storms." His gaze flicked, briefly, to Mara's throat, where the brand of ownership had faded to a pale ghost since the page tore. "I'm here to tell you it won't be pursued. And to tell you it won't be forgiven, either. There's no third column for what you did."

"I know how ledgers work," Theo said.

"Do you?" Halloway smiled without warmth. "You tore one up. I'd have called that the opposite."

"It's the only entry I ever made that balanced."

For a moment nobody moved. Wind came off the flats and pushed at their cloaks, carrying salt and the smell of the dead villages further south. Then Halloway reached into his coat, and both Mara and Theo tensed, but what he brought out was small and wrapped in oilcloth. He set it on a flat stone between them and stepped back.

"Your old brand-kit. Your real name papers, unregistered. A purse." He paused. "And a letter of credit that doesn't exist, drawn on an account that never was, spendable nowhere. Burn it if you like. Some of us thought you should have the option of being nobody properly."

Theo didn't move. "Why?"

"Because thirty years ago a storm-caller burned half a province and the guild took them in anyway, and some of us remember why we did." Halloway's voice stayed level, but something moved underneath it, deep water under ice. "Because the master sold a woman's soul to save his own skin and we all signed the quarterly reports that made it possible, and a debt is a debt even when no one will collect it." He turned. "You were the best of us, Eastwick. That's the tragedy. If you'd been worse, none of this would hurt."

He walked away. The other two followed. None of them looked back, and Mara understood that too — that looking back was a luxury for people who still had somewhere to return to.

When they were gone, Theo picked up the oilcloth bundle and weighed it in their hand for a long time.

"Well," Mara said. "That was almost kind."

"The guild doesn't do kind. It does accounting." Theo unwrapped it far enough to see the papers, then wrapped it again. "But yes. Almost."

* * *

They burned the map that night.

It seemed right to do it properly, so they built a fire on the shore of a lake whose name neither of them knew, and Mara took the bloodstained thing out of her coat one last time. It had carried them through salt flats and tollgates and the long dark under the stone, and now it was just paper again, creased white along its folds, her brother's forged signature still visible in the corner where the cartographer had left room for a buyer's mark.

She almost kept it. That surprised her. It was the last piece of Tam she had — not the brother who'd sold her, but the boy who'd taught her to read this kind of chart, who'd traced roads in the dust and promised her the world was full of towns where nobody knew your business. She hated him and missed him in the same breath, and she suspected she would for the rest of her life, and she suspected that was normal, and she hated that too.

"Burn it or keep it," Theo said. "No wrong answer."

"There's only wrong answers. That's what I've learned." She fed it to the fire corner-first. "That's the whole lesson, really. Everything costs. The trick is choosing what you can afford to lose."

The map caught, curled, and was gone in less time than it deserved. Sparks went up toward the stars and became indistinguishable from them.

"What did you pay at the ferryman's gate?" she asked, because she'd wanted to ask since the vault, since the tearing, since the climb into the sun. "My toll was my wedding day. Yours was supposed to be — what? What did they take?"

Theo poked the fire. Took their time about it.

"They didn't take anything," they said finally. "I paid forward. Part of your toll, out of my own account. A memory of yours that I'd seen in the ledger and thought you shouldn't lose." A pause. "The morning your mother taught you to swim. You were seven. The water was freezing and you screamed at her and then you wouldn't get out for hours."

Mara stared at them. "How do you know that?"

"Because I've got it now." Theo's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "It's a good memory, Mara Vess. Cold water and your mother laughing. I've been carrying it for days. I hope you don't mind."

She should have minded. It was theft, technically, or charity, or something with no clean name, and she spent a long moment searching herself for outrage and finding instead a strange, unbearable tenderness — the knowledge that somewhere inside another person, her mother was laughing on a riverbank, and would go on laughing there as long as Theo lived.

"You could have let me lose it," she said. "One day out of thousands. No one would have known."

"I'd have known. I keep accounts." Theo looked at her across the fire, and the light did things to their face that made her chest ache in the new, crowded way. "Besides. Someone should hold a memory of your family that isn't a betrayal."

Mara laughed — really laughed, the first time since before the mountain — and it came out wet and cracked and she didn't care. She leaned over and put her hand over Theo's where it rested on the stones, and Theo, who flinched from touch the way exiles flinch from doors, did not pull away.

"So what now?" she asked. "We're nobodies. Properly, thanks to Halloway. No guild, no names, no debts." She turned her hand over and laced her fingers through theirs, deliberate, watching for the flinch that didn't come. "Where does a nobody go?"

"There's a town three days east. Millbrook, on the maps. Small enough that nobody checks papers. Big enough for a counting house that needs a clerk who can actually add." Theo hesitated. "And a woman there owes me a favor from before, if she's still alive, and if she'll extend it to someone with no history at all."

"A fresh ledger," Mara said.

"The freshest. Blank pages. Nothing owed, nothing owned." Theo looked at the fire, then at her, and their voice went quiet in a way she was learning meant honesty. "I've never had one. I don't entirely know what a person does with it."

"We'll figure it out," Mara said. "Between us we've got one good memory of my mother, a purse of guild money, and a letter of credit that spends nowhere. That's more than most people start with."

They sat until the fire burned down to coals and the lake went silver with moonrise, and neither of them spoke, because there was nothing left that needed saying and everything ahead that would need doing. Somewhere out in the dark a bird called once and gave up. The mountain was a black shape far behind them, already shrinking into the ordinary landscape of the world, already becoming a story they might someday tell badly, over wine, to strangers.

In the morning they would walk east. In three days they would reach a town where no one knew their names, and Mara Vess would introduce herself to someone for the first time as exactly who she was — no debts, no brands, no brother's signature dragging behind her like a chain. She fell asleep to that thought, her shoulder against Theo's shoulder, her soul humming in her chest like a struck bell still ringing.

And if, in the last hour before dawn, she dreamed of cold river water and her mother's laugh — well. That memory belonged to someone else now too. She decided she didn't mind sharing.

The sun came up. They broke camp. They walked.
