## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The poison was in the wine.

Petra knew it the way she knew rain coming — not by seeing it but by some older sense in her mouth, a prickle along the tongue like the first static of a storm. She stood at Magistrate Corven's left shoulder with the silver cup balanced on her palm, and she let him lift it, let him admire the color of it against the candlelight, let him bring it to his lips.

Then she said, "Before you drink, my lord."

Corven paused. He was a heavy man with a magistrate's patience and a merchant's suspicion, and he had hired her for exactly this moment, though he always hoped she'd be wrong. "You taste something?"

"I'd like to." She took the cup from his hand before he could decide whether to be offended, and drank.

The hall went quiet. Forty guests of the Harvest Assizes watched her swallow, and Petra watched them back over the rim of the cup, cataloguing faces out of habit: the steward whose eyes flicked toward the door, the vintner sweating through his good coat, the young clerk who looked genuinely alarmed and therefore was probably innocent. Somewhere behind her, a woman gasped.

The poison bloomed on her tongue like a plum gone wrong — bitter almond under something greener, hemlock cut with foxglove, if she had to guess, and she usually did have to guess because poisons were rarely labeled. It spread down her throat and settled into her chest like a swallowed coal, and then, as it always did, it simply *stopped*. Her body considered it, found it uninteresting, and set it aside. The fever-flush came and went in the space of three heartbeats. By the fourth, she felt nothing at all except the ordinary ache of hunger she carried everywhere these days.

She lowered the cup. "Hemlock," she said. "Cut with foxglove to mask the smell. It was in the bottle, not the decanter, so whoever did it had access before the table was set." She turned the cup so the magistrate could see the faint oily ring inside the lip. "I'd start with your vintner's cellar keys, my lord. And I wouldn't eat the pheasant either, just on principle."

The vintner ran. That was answer enough for the hall; two guards went after him, boots hammering on flagstone, and the Harvest Assizes dissolved into the particular chaos of rich people discovering they were mortal.

Magistrate Corven gripped Petra's arm hard enough to bruise. "You're certain?"

"I'm certain you'd be dead." She eased free of his grip. "The rest is deduction. You can hang the right man or the wrong one, but you'll hang someone either way — that part isn't mine to carry."

"It should frighten you," Corven said, staring at her with the expression she'd come to know too well, the one that wasn't gratitude at all. "Drinking that. It should kill you."

"Yes," Petra said. "Everyone says."

* * *

She collected her coin at the servants' door, because magistrates paid tasters the way they paid latrine-diggers — promptly, and where no one could see. Nine coppers and a clipped silver bit that Corven clearly hoped she wouldn't notice was clipped. She noticed. She said nothing. Nine coppers bought bread for four days if she ate thin, and the silver would buy lamp oil, and wanting more than that in the Valley of Stillness was like wanting the moon delivered to your door.

Outside, evening had come down blue and cold. The long winter was starting early this year — everyone said so, the way people say things they hope will make a bad thing feel fated instead of merely bad. Frost furred the pump handles. Smoke stood up straight from every chimney in Tarn Mill, too straight, as if even the wind had been told to hold its tongue.

That was the thing about the valley that visitors never understood, back when there were visitors. People called it peaceful. Peaceful was the wrong word. A held breath was peaceful only until you remembered it was being held. The Valley of Stillness sat between mountains that leaned in like conspirators, and the air in it never quite moved the way air was supposed to move elsewhere. Songs said magic couldn't cross the boundary stones. Songs said a lot of things about what lay beyond — Vessia, the kingdom of glass and aurora-light, the Spire that sang. Petra had grown up on those songs the way other children grew up on milk, and she had never once been able to decide whether they were history or lullaby.

Her mother had believed them. Her mother, who was eleven years dead now, had hummed them while mending nets and told Petra that their family came from beyond the stones, from somewhere the songs still remembered. Everyone in Tarn Mill had a story like that. Nobody ever tried to leave to check.

Petra walked home through streets that smelled of woodsmoke and turnips and the mineral cold coming off the millrace. Her body still hummed faintly with the poison, the way a bell hums after it stops ringing. That was the other thing nobody understood. Immunity sounded like a gift. In practice it meant she made her living swallowing death for men who flinched when she reached for the bread basket afterward. It meant sitting alone at feasts, at the edge of conversations, at the exact distance where courtesy required them to nod at her and cowardice kept them from asking questions. *How does it work? What does it feel like? Are you not afraid?*

It feels like nothing, she wanted to tell them. That's the horror of it. Everything that should touch me doesn't. Poison, fever, the red rash that took half the village when she was nine — all of it arrived at her skin and turned politely away, like petitioners refused at a gate. She had spent her whole life on the far side of her own front door.

At the corner by the chandler's, old Maretta was hauling a basket of tallow ends up her steps, and she flagged Petra down with the imperiousness of the very old. "Girl. You working the Assizes tonight?"

"Finished. The vintner poisoned the magistrate."

"Again?" Maretta clucked. "Third one this year. You'd think they'd learn to drink water." She pressed a stub of candle into Petra's hand before Petra could refuse it, which was Maretta's way of charity — fast, so shame couldn't catch up. "Your mother would've hated seeing you do that work."

"My mother hated seeing me do everything except stay exactly where she put me."

"Well," Maretta said, "she wasn't wrong to want that." And there was something in the old woman's face then, a flicker, gone too quick to read — grief, or guilt, or just the wind that never blew here — before she shut her door on the cold.

Petra stood a moment longer than she needed to, turning the candle stub over in her fingers.

* * *

Her rooms were above the cooper's shed, one room and a lean-to, rent owed three weeks deep. She lit Maretta's candle and counted her coins onto the table out of habit, though counting changed nothing: nine coppers, one clipped bit, and a button she kept because it was brass and pretty and lying about being money. Supper was bread end and the last of the pickled onions, eaten standing up, because sitting down made poverty feel official.

Through the wall, the cooper's family argued cheerfully about a daughter's dowry. Downstairs, a dog dreamed audibly. The ordinary sounds of a valley closing itself for the night.

Petra washed the cup she'd brought home from the Assizes — hers now, confiscated wages, silver with a maker's mark she didn't recognize: a tower, slender, struck through with a line like a needle. She polished it while she thought, which was also habit. Thinking went easier when her hands were busy, and tonight her thoughts were restless as flies.

Nine coppers. Four days of bread. Then the Assizes season ended and the roads iced over and the magistrates went home to their manors, and tasters starved politely until spring. Every winter was the same ledger. Every spring, the same arithmetic. She was twenty-three years old and she could recite her own future the way the priest recited the litany, and the future had no surprises in it anywhere, and she had begun to understand that this — not the hunger, not the poison, not the loneliness — this was the thing that would eventually kill her. Not danger. The absence of it. The held breath that never, ever let out.

She caught herself humming. One of the songs, the one about the spire on the ridge drinking light. She stopped, annoyed at herself, and then — alone, candle low, no one to see — she let herself finish the verse.

*I walked beyond the boundary stone,*
*where stillness ends and sky is known,*
*and what I lost upon the way*
*was worth the price I had to pay.*

What price, the song never said. Songs were cagey that way. They promised doors and kingdoms and skies that *knew* things, and then they trailed off into la-la-las precisely at the interesting part.

Petra set the silver cup on the sill where the last grey light could find its mark, and that was when she heard the dogs.

Not the cooper's dog. Not any of the yard dogs, who were already barking in that ragged, uncertain way dogs bark when something is wrong in a direction they can't name. These were farther off, up-valley, near the boundary stones — a sound she had never heard before and recognized anyway, the way you recognize a word from a language you don't speak. Deep, rhythmic, wrong. Like hounds bred for something other than hunting foxes.

The hair rose on her arms. The candle flame bent, though there was no draft.

She went to the window. The street below was empty. The smoke still stood straight up from the chimneys, unnaturally straight, and the mountains had gone the color of iron, and somewhere past them, past the stones, past the edge of everything the valley permitted itself to know, those voices went on calling — patient, methodical, like searchers who had finally picked up a scent after twenty years of looking.

Petra stood with her hand flat against the cold glass and told herself it was wolves. Wolves happened. Wolves came down hard winters early.

But the sound wasn't wolves, and some animal part of her, older than sense, older than the songs, knew it — knew it the way her tongue knew poison before her mind caught up. Something had crossed the line the valley was drawn around. Something was coming down out of the dark toward Tarn Mill, and it was coming for a reason, and the reason had teeth.

She blew out the candle and stood in the dark, listening, her heart going hard and strange, and thought: *finally.*

Then she thought: *God forgive me. Finally.*
