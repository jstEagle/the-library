## 2. A Stranger at the Millrace

The millrace was frozen in a way that made the whole valley look like it had stopped mid-sentence. Petra walked the ice shelf along its edge every morning on her way to the magistrate's house, and every morning she thought the same thing: *the water is still moving under there.* You could hear it if you knelt and pressed your ear to the frost — a low, patient mutter, like someone talking in their sleep.

She knelt there now, breath fogging, and listened.

"Still going," she told it.

The water didn't answer. Nothing in the Valley of Stillness ever did. That was rather the point of it.

She stood, brushed the frost from her knees, and took inventory of her morning the way she took inventory of everything: two coins left from Saturday's tasting, one heel of black bread, a blister on her right thumb from the mortar she'd ground the magistrate's breakfast into paste with. The poison had been in the honey. It was always in the honey — sweet things were where careless poisoners hid their work, because they assumed sweetness disarmed suspicion. Petra had learned to taste sweetness as a kind of question. *What are you hiding?*

The answer this morning had been monkshood, three drops' worth, enough to stop a man's heart before his second cup. Enough to make her tongue go numb to the wrist and her vision swim gray for an hour while she sat very still in the magistrate's kitchen and let her body do what her body had always done, which was refuse to die.

The magistrate's wife had watched her sway and said, not unkindly, "You should eat more, girl."

"I ate," Petra said. "That's rather the problem."

Two coins for that. Two coins and the walk home along the millrace, past the boundary stones with their worn faces, past the shrine where old women tied ribbons for sons who'd never left the valley and therefore couldn't be lost anywhere but to time. The sky was the color of pewter. The air had that quality it got in late autumn, dense and expectant, like the valley was holding its breath between one year and the next.

Petra had lived twenty-two years inside that held breath. She knew every stone of it the way you know the inside of your own mouth — intimately, uselessly, without ever having chosen it.

She was halfway home when she saw the man.

* * *

He was lying at the outflow of the race where the water pooled before spilling into the shallows, half on the ice and half off, and at first she took him for a scarecrow some wind had knocked down. Then the scarecrow coughed.

Petra had seen dying before. The valley didn't spare anyone that education — fevers, falls, the slow arithmetic of age. But she had never seen anyone die *arriving*, which is what this looked like: a man who had spent everything he had simply getting here, and now had nothing left to spend on staying.

He was dressed wrong. That was the first thing her mind snagged on, the way her tongue snagged on poison. His coat was heavy wool dyed a deep blue that no weaver in the valley made, and it was cut close to the body in a style she'd only ever seen in the woodcut illustrations of the songbooks — Vessian dress, or what people imagined Vessian dress to be. His boots were ruined. Frostbite had gotten into them somewhere far back; the leather was split and his feet inside were gray-black to the ankle. There was blood on his sleeve that had frozen and thawed and frozen again, layered like tree rings. He had been bleeding a long time, and walking anyway.

She knelt beside him. "You're on my millrace."

His eyes opened. They were pale, almost colorless, and they went through her face once, quickly, like a man checking a ledger against a name.

"Not yours," he rasped. "Nothing here is—" A cough folded him nearly in half. When it passed he lay panting, staring up at the pewter sky as if reading something written there. "Stillness. Gods. You can feel it. Like swimming in oil."

"You're frostbitten. I need to get you—"

"No." His hand shot out and caught her wrist with a strength that startled her, fingers hard as the ice beneath them. "No fire. No house. No warden sees me. Do you understand? If the wardens come, I'm dead, and then so are you, and then—" He stopped. Something moved behind his eyes, some recalculation, some grief arriving late. "Then it doesn't matter, does it. Then nothing matters."

"You're raving."

"I'm *dying*," he said, with the flat precision of a man correcting a small error. "Raving is what comes after. Listen to me. What's your name?"

"Petra Villalobos."

Something happened to his face when she said it. She would think about that moment for the rest of her life, though she wouldn't know until much later what she'd been looking at: relief and horror arriving together, twins, holding hands. His grip on her wrist loosened. His colorless eyes filled.

"Villalobos," he whispered. "Oh. Oh, you're real. All this way, and you're *real*."

"Sir—"

"My name is Corvus Aldane." He said it like a man laying down a card he'd been holding all game. "I'm a cartographer. Was. And I've come a very long way to lose a map, and I find I'd rather lose it to you than to the cold."

"I don't want anything of yours. I want to get you warm."

"You can't." He smiled, and it was a terrible smile, cracked at the corners like old paint. "There's no warming what's already given away. I gave my warmth to the road, girl. It's spent. But the map isn't, and the map is worth more than every life in this valley, including yours, especially yours—" Another cough, worse, wet-sounding now. Blood showed at the corner of his mouth, bright against the frost. "Help me sit up. Please. I can't press it into your hands lying down. It has to be hands. It has to be willing."

Against every sensible instinct she had, Petra helped him sit up. He weighed almost nothing, as though the road had eaten him from the inside out. She braced his shoulders against her arm and felt the tremor running through him, deep as the millrace's muttering under the ice.

From inside his coat he drew a roll of leather, stiff with cold, bound with a cord knotted in a pattern too deliberate to be accidental. Even frozen she could feel the weight of it, the density of good hide worked by a master hand. He pushed it against her chest with both fists, the way you'd give bread to a starving stranger — urgently, personally, as if the giving mattered more than the thing given.

"What is it?" she asked.

"A door." His breath was failing; the words came in shorter pieces now, hauled up from somewhere deep. "Out. Out of Stillness. Out of the winter. Your valley is a lock, Petra Villalobos, and you are—" He stopped himself, jaw working, and she understood dimly that he was editing his last words, spending them carefully, a dying man doing arithmetic. "Your grandmother sealed it. To keep you safe. It kept everyone safe. For a while."

"My grandmother was a goat-herd from Tarn Hollow."

"Yes," he said gently. "And before that?"

Before that was nothing. Before that was a blank her mother had always waved away — *we came from up-valley, child, we've always been here* — and Petra had never pushed, because the valley taught you early that some silences were load-bearing.

"The Spire knows," Corvus said. "It's known for twenty years. It's patient the way winter is patient. It will send wardens over those stones, and they'll read your blood like a page, and they'll take you apart memory by memory until there's nothing left but a girl-shaped jar." His hands tightened on hers around the map. "Don't let them. Whatever they offer you — and they will offer you beautiful things, they offered my queen beautiful things — don't let them."

"Corvus." She used his name deliberately, the way you'd touch a frightened animal. "Who is my grandmother?"

But he was looking past her now, up the white length of the valley, and his expression had gone strange and soft. "Ilian," he said. "My brother. My sworn brother. If he comes after me — he swore to burn it, don't let him burn it, tell him—" The sentence collapsed into coughing, and the coughing into shivering, and the shivering into stillness, and Petra held him against the cold the only way she could, with her own body heat, uselessly, the way you'd bail a sinking boat with your cupped hands.

His eyes came back to her. They were clearing, oddly, the way dying men's sometimes did, focused and calm.

"Petra Villalobos," he said. Full name. Both names, spoken slowly, like a key turning. "Daughter of Sera. Granddaughter of Mara. Last heir of the house that built the door." He smiled again, smaller this time, almost private. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry it's you. I'm sorry it's anyone."

Then he said her name one more time — just *Petra*, just the first part, softer, as if shortening it were a kindness, as if she'd earned the familiarity in the space of a morning — and died with the frost coming up over his boots and the water still muttering under the ice, patient, unhurried, going about its business the way it had gone about it for a hundred years and would go about it for a hundred more.

* * *

She sat with him for a long time.

Long enough for the cold to work into her knees and her spine and the spaces between her thoughts. Long enough for the light to shift from pewter to the beginnings of true gray, snow-light gathering somewhere beyond the ridge. She held the leather roll against her chest and did not open it. Opening it felt like it would make everything true, and she was spending these quiet minutes in the country of *not yet*, where the man was only sleeping and the words he'd said were only fever-talk and her name was only her name, two syllables and a surname, belonging to a taster who tasted poisons for coins and wanted, quietly, stupidly, for the world to be bigger than a valley.

Dread arrived the way weather arrives. Not all at once. First a pressure behind the ears. Then a change in the smell of the air. Then, when you finally looked up, the sky was already full of it, and there was nowhere to stand where it wasn't falling.

She looked up.

On the far side of the millrace, up the slope where the pines began, something was watching her. Not a person. Not an animal. A shape like both — tall, robed in a gray that was almost exactly the gray of the sky, standing motionless among the trees with the stillness of something that did not need to breathe. As she stared, it tilted its head. The gesture was curious, almost polite. Almost human.

Then it stepped backward into the pines and was gone, and the forest closed behind it like water closing over a dropped stone.

Petra's heart was going hard and fast, and her body — her strange, stubborn, poison-proof body — had broken out in gooseflesh from scalp to sole, every hair standing, every nerve singing a single note. Not fear exactly. Recognition. The way her tongue recognized monkshood before her mind named it.

*They'll read your blood like a page.*

She looked down at Corvus Aldane, cartographer, dead at her millrace with her name on his lips. Snow had begun to fall, fine and dry, settling on his open eyes without melting.

She should fetch the reeve. She should fetch anyone. A man was dead on the ice and there were laws about it, procedures, the small machinery of a small place doing what small places do. Instead she found herself on her feet with the map thrust inside her coat, against her skin, hidden, and her legs carrying her toward home with the particular speed of someone who has not yet decided to run and is already running.

Behind her the millrace muttered under its ice. Ahead, the valley lay white and silent and holding its breath, and for the first time in twenty-two years, Petra Villalobos understood that the breath it was holding was not the valley's.

It was hers. It had always been hers. And something out there in the falling snow had finally come to collect it.

She was a quarter mile from her door when she heard the dogs begin — far off, high and thin, coming down from the ridge in voices that did not sound like any hounds bred in the Valley of Stillness — and she ran the rest of the way without letting herself think about why.
