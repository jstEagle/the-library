## 5. Winter's First Teeth

The high road out of the valley was a lie told in switchbacks. It looked like a road. It behaved like a dare.

Ilian had walked it twice before — once with Sefen on the way south, once alone on the way back to bury what was left of him — and both times the wind had come off the peaks with the same attitude: *you don't belong up here, and I intend to prove it.* This time it had Petra with him, and Petra walked like the mountain owed her an apology.

She'd been quiet since the boundary stones. Not frightened-quiet. Calculating-quiet. He'd seen that look before, on his brother's face, the night Sefen decided they were going back into Vessia instead of away from it. The look of a person doing arithmetic with their own life.

"You're limping," she said, without turning around.

"I'm favoring my left knee. There's a difference."

"Is there?"

"It's a matter of pride."

She snorted, breath steaming, and kept walking. Three days out from the valley and she'd already learned the most important survival skill on the high road: never stop moving while the light lasts.

The snow came at dusk, fat and slow, the first real snow of the long winter — not valley snow, which fell politely and melted by morning, but mountain snow, snow with intent. Within an hour the road was a rumor. Within two, Ilian's hands were numb through his gloves and Petra had stopped talking entirely, conserving heat the way she conserved everything else, and he knew they were in trouble, because he'd been pretending not to notice the smoke.

It rose off the eastern slope, thin and gray, where no fire should be. Travelers this far north didn't light fires unless they were either desperate or dangerous, and on the high road those were usually the same people.

"We shelter," Petra said.

"We could walk past."

"In this?" She turned, and snow had gathered white along her dark brows, and her eyes were doing that thing again — the arithmetic. "We could also freeze standing up. At least freezing near a fire is warmer."

He couldn't argue with the logic. He hated that. It was becoming a pattern.

* * *

The caravan sat in a bowl below the ridgeline, six wagons ringed around a central fire like a fist closing. They were painted wagons, or had been once — the paint flaked now, but you could still see the old bright colors underneath, suns and rivers and birds, the kind of decoration meant for markets and festivals, not for a frozen hollow in the middle of nowhere. Mules stamped in their traces. A woman on the nearest wagon bench watched them come down the slope with the unhurried attention of someone who had already counted their weapons.

"Smugglers," Ilian said quietly.

"How can you tell?"

"The wagons are too cheerful. Nobody honest paints a wagon that color."

The woman stood as they reached the firelight. She was sixty if she was a day, wrapped in furs that had been mended so many times the original fur was mostly theory, and she looked at Ilian the way a butcher looks at a cut of meat — assessing quality, estimating price.

"Travelers," she said. "In winter. On the high road." Each word landed like a coin on a counter. "Either lost or running."

"Running," Petra said.

Honesty. Ilian shot her a look. She ignored it.

The old woman smiled, and it transformed her face into something almost kind. "Good. Lost people ask questions. Running people pay for silence." She jerked her chin toward the fire. "Warm yourselves. Food costs. Shelter costs more. And whatever you do—" her eyes flicked to the wagons, to the shapes moving behind their curtained doors, "—don't touch the glass."

* * *

They ate stew that tasted mostly of salt and turnip and sat close enough to the fire to feel their fingers again. Around them the caravan went about its evening business, and Ilian watched, because watching was the one skill he'd never let rust.

The smugglers were memory-runners. He should have known from the wagons — the painted ones, the river-and-sun ones, belonged to the caravans that worked the borderlands between what Vessia had been and what the Spire had made of everything else. They moved bottled recollections out of the Spire's territory, past its wardens, into the free towns where rich people paid obscene prices to feel something that had never happened to them.

He'd seen the trade before, in the port city of Callow's Reach. Glass vials the size of a thumb, each one holding a stranger's afternoon. A first kiss. A mother's voice. The smell of a house that burned down twenty years ago. The wealthy bought them the way they bought anything — carelessly, by the dozen — uncorked them over a candle, breathed deep, and wore other lives for an hour before returning to their own.

Sefen had called it grave-robbing with extra steps. Sefen had also, Ilian remembered with a twist in his chest, sold three memories himself to pay for the map. He'd never said which three. Ilian had never asked, because some questions have answers you can't unknow.

"They're staring at us," Petra murmured.

"Everyone stares at strangers."

"No. They stare at me." Her voice stayed level, but her hand found the strap of her pack — the one with the map rolled inside it — and held on. "Like they're pricing me."

The old woman reappeared, lowering herself onto the bench across the fire with a grunt. "You've got a good eye, girl. We are pricing you. Everyone gets priced. That's not cruelty, that's just the road." She produced a clay pipe, packed it, lit it off a stick from the fire. "Where are you running to?"

"Nowhere," Ilian said.

"Everyone's running somewhere. Nowhere is just somewhere with worse weather." She drew on the pipe, exhaled blue smoke. "You came out of the Stillness Valley. Don't bother denying it — nobody else walks that direction in winter, and the girl has the look. Pale as paper. Unmarked." Her eyes narrowed slightly. "No tattoos, no charms, no warding knots. Out there, everyone wears something against the magic. She wears nothing."

"It doesn't work on her," Ilian said, before he could stop himself.

The old woman went very still. Then, slowly, she leaned forward, and the firelight moved in her eyes like something swimming. "Doesn't it," she said. "Well. That's worth knowing."

Petra's shoulder pressed against his under the shared blanket — deliberate, a warning. *Say less.* He'd known her four days and she was already better at this than he was.

"We're passing through," Petra said. "That's all. We'll be gone at first light."

"Of course you will." The old woman smiled again, and this time the kindness didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "First light. The road will be glad of it." She stood, joints cracking like green wood. "Sleep well. And girl — truly — don't touch the glass."

* * *

He woke because the cold changed.

Not warmed — changed. The fire had burned down to orange bones, the camp was silent except for the mules and the wind worrying the wagon canvas, and the air had taken on a texture he didn't have a name for. Thick. Sweetish. Like the air inside a room where flowers have been left too long in water.

He rolled over to check on Petra and found her bedroll empty.

Panic is a fast animal. It had him on his feet and halfway across the camp before thought caught up, and then thought did catch up, and stopped him dead behind the second wagon — because Petra wasn't being dragged anywhere. She was standing at the open door of the third wagon, swaying gently, a small glass vial uncorked in her bare hand.

The smell was coming from the vial.

"Petra." He kept his voice low. In a smugglers' camp, waking the wrong sleeper got you knifed. "Petra, put it down."

She didn't hear him. Her eyes were open but pointed at nothing, fixed on some middle distance that existed only for her, and her face —

Her face was the problem. He'd watched her for four days now, catalogued her expressions the way he catalogued exits and weapon racks, and every one of them had been guarded, wry, armored. This was none of those things. This was a face with all the doors open. She was smiling. She was crying, silently, without any change in the smile. She looked like someone hearing music from a house they'd forgotten they lived in.

He took the vial from her hand. She didn't resist; her fingers were loose as a sleeping child's. He stoppered it, and the sweet thickness drained out of the air, and Petra gasped like a surfacing swimmer and grabbed his arm hard enough to bruise.

"Ilian." Her voice shook. "Ilian, I was — there was a wedding—"

* * *

They sat by the rebuilt fire, wrapped in blankets, while the camp slept on around them and the snow came down soft as ash. She told it in pieces, hands moving, eyes too wide.

"It was mine. I mean — it wasn't mine, I know it wasn't, but it *felt*—" She pressed her palms flat against her knees. "A courtyard. Strings of lanterns, little colored ones, hundreds of them. Music from somewhere, pipes and drums. Everyone laughing. And I was wearing blue, this blue dress with silver at the collar, and I could feel it, the weight of the fabric, the cold air on my arms, and I was so *happy*, Ilian, I have never in my life been that happy, and I knew everyone there. Not their names, but — I knew them. The way you know your own street. And there was a man turning me in a circle under the lanterns, and I could feel his hands, and I knew—" her voice cracked, steadied, "—I knew he was going to die. I knew it the way you know rain is coming. And I danced anyway. I danced anyway because it was the wedding and you dance at weddings, and I loved him so much it was like a wound, and then—"

She stopped.

"And then?" Ilian asked quietly.

"Then it ended. Like a door closing. And I was here, and my hands were empty, and I was grieving a man I never met." She looked at him, and her eyes were wet and furious. "Whose wedding was that?"

He turned the vial over in his gloved fingers. Cheap glass, cork sealed with wax, a scrap of parchment glued to the base bearing a number and nothing else. No name. The runners never wrote names — names were evidence, and evidence got you hanged in the Spire's territories.

"A dead woman's," he said. "Probably. Most of what they carry comes out of the harvesting wards. When the Spire drains someone all the way down, the memories don't vanish. They get decanted. Bottled. Sold." He set the vial down between them on the stone, where the firelight made it glow faintly amber. "Somebody danced at that wedding. Somebody loved that man. And now she's an empty room somewhere in the Spire, and her happiest day is merchandise."

Petra stared at the vial for a long time.

"She had chapped hands," she said finally. "In the memory. My hands were hers, and they were chapped, work-worn, and there was a scar on the left thumb. She wasn't nobility. She was a working woman who married somebody she loved under colored lanterns." Her jaw tightened. "And some warden pulled her apart like a knot and now a merchant's wife in some free town puts on her wedding for an hour after dinner."

"That's the trade."

"I know that's the trade. I'm allowed to hate it anyway."

"Yes," Ilian said. "You are."

She picked up the vial, weighed it in her palm, and for one alarming moment he thought she was going to drink the rest of it — go back under, find the lanterns again, finish the dance. He saw the want move across her face like weather. He knew that want. Sefen had worn it too, near the end, when the memories started going and he'd begun visiting the runners' stalls just to stand near the bottles.

But Petra closed her fist around the glass and set it down, deliberately, out of her own reach.

"If that's what the Spire does," she said, "to ordinary people, for fuel — for *lamp oil*—" She looked up at him, and the fury in her had cooled into something much more dangerous. Resolve. "Then we need to walk faster."

* * *

He didn't sleep again. He sat by the fire turning the vial, thinking about Sefen, about the three memories his brother had sold and never named, wondering if one of them was a wedding, wondering if somewhere in this caravan's inventory was a bottle of his own childhood that he'd walk past tomorrow without knowing.

Near dawn, the old woman appeared again, silent as snowfall, and lowered herself onto the bench. She looked at the vial between them, then at Petra, who was asleep sitting up, chin on chest, exhausted past dreaming.

"She drank from an unsealed bottle," the old woman said. "Lucky it was a gentle one. Some of what we carry would strip her mind to the studs." She tapped ash from her pipe. "You'll want to watch that. Someone with no wards, no marks, nothing between her and the magic — she doesn't sip, friend. She *drinks*. Whatever's in the glass pours all the way in."

"I know."

"Do you?" The old woman's eyes were sharp as awls in the gray light. "Because I've been in this trade thirty years, and I've heard stories about blood like hers. Blood that takes everything and keeps it. Blood the Spire has been hunting since before she was born." She leaned forward, and the firelight found her smile, thin as a blade. "So here's my price for the night's shelter, since you're poor in coin. Tell me why the wardens' hounds have been howling along the ridge for two nights straight. Tell me what you're carrying in that girl's pack. Or tell me to mind my own business — and pay full rate for the mule you'll need, because the pass ahead is closed to walkers, and the only way through is with my caravan."

Behind her, at the edge of the firelight, the wind carried a sound down off the ridge. Faint. Distant. Rhythmic.

Howling.

Petra's eyes opened.
