## 6. The Warden's Ledger

They caught her at the well in Harrow's Bend, three days out from the smugglers' camp, and they caught her because she was thirsty.

That was the thing about wardens Petra would learn, though she didn't know it yet: they didn't chase. They waited. They understood thirst the way she understood poison — as a certainty you could build a whole strategy around. The village had one working well, its rope stiff with frost, and by midmorning of their third day on the road there was nowhere else to drink.

Ilian had gone ahead to barter for bread. She'd told him she'd fill the skins and meet him at the east fence. Simple. Sensible. The kind of errand that gets a person taken.

The warden came up behind her while the bucket was still descending, and he did it without sound, which should have been impossible for a man in a grey wool coat with iron buckles down the front. But the snow took his footsteps, and the wind took his smell, and when Petra turned with the dripping bucket in both hands he was simply *there*, close enough that she could see the fine stitching at his collar and the pale, patient eyes above it.

"Petra Villalobos," he said.

Her name in a stranger's mouth. Not her name the way the dying cartographer had said it — reverent, spent, like a coin pressed into her palm. This was different. This was her name read aloud from a list.

"I don't know what you mean," she said, which was stupid, because he had just said it perfectly.

"You're not in any village record," the warden said. "No tithe paid since birth. No baptism. No marriage banns, no death notice, no debt. A girl who exists in no ledger at all." He smiled, and it was a small smile, almost kind, the way a trap is kind right up until the jaw closes. "Do you know how interesting that makes you?"

"I'm a taster," she said. "I taste for magistrates. I have no business being in anyone's book."

"Everyone is in someone's book." He gestured, courteous, toward the lane. "Walk with me."

She considered running. She actually considered it, standing there with the bucket going heavy in her arms, water slopping over her knuckles and freezing in the wind. But two more figures in grey had appeared at the far end of the lane, unhurried, hands loose at their sides, and beyond them Ilian was nowhere in sight, and Petra had spent her whole life learning the difference between a fight she could win and a fight that would only cost more later.

So she walked.

* * *

They kept her in the tithe-house at the edge of the village, a stone room with a stove and a table and a window too narrow to fit anything but light. They took her knife, her map — her heart stopped at that, a full three beats — and her boots, which struck her as either very thorough or very strange, until she realized the boots were the only thing she owned that might survive a long run across frozen ground.

They were keeping her for travel, then. Not for questioning. Wardens didn't question; they collected.

The warden who had taken her — his name, when another guard addressed him, was Corvess — sat across the table from her that evening and opened a book.

It was not a large book. That was the first shock. She had imagined, without knowing it, something vast: a cathedral volume with brass corners, chained to a desk, guarded. Instead it was a plain ledger, calf-bound, the leather worn soft at the edges like a hymnal carried to church every week for forty years. Corvess handled it the way a priest handles such a book. With love, or the shape of love.

"Do you know what we do?" he asked.

"Ilian says you eat memories."

"Eat." He turned the word over and found it wanting. "We keep. Everything the Spire takes is kept. Nothing is lost, Petra Villalobos. That's the whole point. Out there—" he nodded at the window, at the falling dark, "—memory rots. People forget their mothers' voices within a decade of the grave. Whole villages forget why they were founded. It all goes to rot and rain. We are the only archive in the world that doesn't decay." He laid his hand flat on the cover. "Every harvest is recorded. Every donor. Every memory, dated, described, catalogued. When your grandmother dies, and thirty years after that her daughter follows, and thirty years after that *you* follow — everything they were will still be here. Readable. Alive." His pale eyes came up. "Isn't that a mercy?"

"It's theft," Petra said.

"It's *harvest*." He said it gently, correcting a child's arithmetic. "And it's voluntary. Mostly. Donors give willingly, in exchange for consideration. Coin. Safety. Longer winters made shorter. And they sign for it." He opened the ledger.

She hadn't meant to look. She looked anyway, because she was her mother's daughter and her mother had taught her that the dangerous things in a room are always the ones you're pretending not to see.

Columns. Names in a fine clerk's hand, dates, sums. And descriptions — short, spare, devastating. *The smell of bread at her sister's wedding. Her son's face at age four. Twenty years of summers at the millpond.* Each entry a life reduced to inventory. Each one signed off at the bottom in the donor's own hand, because even the Spire wanted its thefts to look like gifts.

Petra's eyes went down the page the way they go down a page of names read at a funeral — dutiful, distant, already sliding toward the next — and then stopped.

Halfway down, between a farmer from somewhere called Vael's Crossing and a ferryman's widow, was a signature she knew better than her own.

She knew it in the way you know handwriting that has written your name ten thousand times. On meal notes left under the door when she slept past dawn. On the medicine labels she'd copied out as a child, sounding the letters. On the single letter that had ever come to her, folded into a parcel of dried pears, saying nothing of importance, meaning everything.

Her mother's hand. Slightly sloped. The crossbar of the T dragged long, impatient. The L of her surname looped too wide, always had, and there it was, looped too wide, in a warden's ledger, dated nineteen years ago.

*Donor:* the name Petra had never been allowed to speak aloud in the valley, the name she'd only ever whispered into her pillow like a prayer she was ashamed of. *Memory given:* and beneath it, in the clerk's spare script: *All recollections pertaining to the child. Complete.*

Complete.

Corvess watched her read it. She knew he did, because he said nothing, and men like Corvess filled silence the way other men filled air.

"She sold me," Petra said. Her voice came out flat and strange, like someone else's.

"No," Corvess said. "Read the date again. Read what came *after*."

She read. Below her mother's entry, in the same clerk's hand, a second notation, added later, the ink a shade darker:

*Donor deceased. Harvest incomplete — subject's memories of the child retained against instruction. Note: donor resisted extraction at point of collection. Unprecedented.*

Below that, a third note, older still, in different ink entirely — a heavy, formal hand, the kind that signs decrees:

*The bloodline remains unaccounted for. All stations: continue inquiry. The Spire does not forget what it is owed.*

Twenty years. The ink had faded twenty years' worth. The Spire had been looking for her since before she could walk.

"They told me she died of fever," Petra said. The words felt borrowed. "In the valley. Where magic can't follow. That's why we lived there. That's why she never—" She stopped. Started again. "She hid me."

"Your mother fled here with an infant and a false name," Corvess agreed, pleasantly, terribly. "And when we found her, she bargained. Her memories of Vessia, of her family, of her queen-mother — all of it, freely given, in exchange for our leaving the valley alone. Our reaching no further." He tilted his head. "But she kept you. Kept every memory of you, against the terms. Hid you inside her own head where we couldn't touch, and died before we could collect." Something moved behind the pale eyes — not grief, nothing so warm. Interest. The interest of a man reading a very old letter that has finally grown relevant. "Sentiment. It complicates every ledger."

Petra looked at her mother's handwriting and understood, all at once, the shape of her whole life. The poverty that kept them moving. The refusal to speak of the past. The way her mother had flinched at songs about Vessia, had gone white at the word *Spire*, had checked the boundary stones twice on festival nights. Not a woman fleeing grief. A woman running a twenty-year concealment with a child as the cargo, dying mid-run, leaving the cargo half-hidden and the hunt still open.

Every kindness had been a wall. Every silence, a lock.

And the map in her chest pocket — the map they had taken with her boots — was the key to a door her grandmother had built, and the Spire had been hunting the key's bearer since before she could walk.

"The cartographer," Petra said slowly. "The man who gave me the map. You've been following him too."

"For eleven years," Corvess said. "He was thorough. Grief usually is." He closed the ledger, softly, and laced his fingers over it. "Here is what happens now, Petra Villalobos. You will be taken to the Spire. You will be treated *well* — I want that understood. Your blood is valuable; damaged goods are not. And in time, perhaps, you'll come to see the ledger as I do. As a mercy. As the only way anything loved survives its ending."

"And if I don't come to see it?"

"Then you'll see it anyway," he said, "from inside."

He rose, tucked the ledger under his arm like a psalter, and paused at the door.

"For what it's worth," he said, "your mother fought us at the end. It took three harvesters to hold her, and she broke one's wrist. In nineteen years of service, I have seen donors weep, beg, bargain, and go quiet. I have seen exactly one fight." He looked back at her, and for one unguarded moment the small kind smile was gone and something almost like respect sat in its place. "I expect you'll be the second."

The door shut. The bolt fell.

* * *

They had taken her boots but not her stockings, and they had taken her knife but not her hairpins, and they had taken the map but not the six words of it she had made herself memorize the first night, lying awake beside a sleeping Ilian, because her mother had raised her to believe that anything carried can be lost and only what's remembered is safe.

*The door knows its own,* the cartographer had written. Six words. She held them now like a coal cupped in bare hands.

The window was narrow, but the tithe-house was old, and old buildings in poor villages are held together by habit more than mortar. The lintel above the window had cracked years ago and been mended with a wooden peg gone soft with rot. Petra worked it loose with a hairpin through the long dark hours, slow as water wearing stone, and thought about her mother fighting three harvesters with her memories of her daughter in her fists.

Near midnight the guard outside began to snore. Near midnight, also, there came a low whistle from the lane — two notes, rising, the exact call Ilian had used at the smugglers' camp to bring her back to the fire.

She put her eye to the gap where the peg had been.

He stood in the shadow of the woodpile with her boots in one hand and her knife in the other, and his face, when the moonlight caught it, was the face of a man deciding — again, still, always — exactly what she was worth.

"How," she breathed through the crack.

"Your warden drinks," Ilian said. "Badly. And brags worse. I've been listening since sundown." He tossed the boots beneath the window. "There's a horse tied behind the tithe-house and a road north that nobody watches, because everyone believes this village is watched enough. Which it was." A pause. "Until tonight."

She dropped the last foot into the frost-silenced lane, took the knife, took the boots, and turned to run —

— and stopped, because Ilian hadn't moved, and because his voice, when it came, was quieter than the snow.

"The ledger," he said. "What was in it. Tell me while we run."

So she told him — her mother's name, her mother's hand, the words *complete* and *unprecedented* and *the Spire does not forget what it is owed* — as they ran through the sleeping village and out along the north road with the horse blowing steam behind them, and she watched the knowledge land on him like weather, changing the shape of his face.

Because she saw him understand it a heartbeat before she said it, and say it anyway, to make it real:

"Twenty years they've hunted your bloodline. And my brother spent his last strength putting the map into your hands." He shook his head slowly. "Petra — you were never carrying the map to the door."

Behind them, far off, a hound began to howl.

"You *are* the door."
