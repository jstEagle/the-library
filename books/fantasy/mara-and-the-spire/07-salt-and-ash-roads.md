## 7. Salt and Ash Roads

The salt flats began where the road gave up pretending.

Ilian felt the change in his boots first — the packed earth going soft, then white, then crunching underfoot like old bone. By midmorning there was nothing left of the world but white. Salt to the horizon, salt to the sky, a glare so total it erased distance and made every step feel like walking on the inside of his own skull. The caravan's tracks had vanished behind them within an hour, smothered by wind-driven crust, and ahead there was only the faint dark seam of the ash roads, where the old Vessian engineers had once laid cinder over the flats so their couriers could cross at any season.

Two days of it. Maybe three, if the weather held, which it wouldn't.

He walked three paces behind Petra Villalobos and watched her the way he'd watched her for six days now: constantly, carefully, and without ever letting her catch him doing it.

She was tougher than she looked. That was the problem. He'd expected the valley girl to complain by now — about the cold, the blisters, the food, the endless flat nothing — and instead she'd said maybe forty words since dawn, all of them useful. She'd learned to walk with her chin down against the glare. She'd learned to sleep sitting up, back against her pack, hands loose near the knife he'd given her. She'd learned too fast, the way people learn when they understand that the world they've walked into is trying to kill them.

She'd also learned to watch him back.

That was the other problem.

* * *

They made camp at dusk in the lee of a waymarker — one of Vessia's old boundary stones, taller than a man, its carved face worn to a suggestion of features. Whoever the figure had been, the salt had taken its eyes first. Ilian got a fire going from hoarded scrub-wood, small and mean, because anything bigger would be visible for miles, and out here visibility was death. Wardens didn't need hounds on the flats. They needed only height, and patience.

Petra sat across the flames, working grease into her cracked hands, and looked at him over the fire with those level gray eyes.

"You've been deciding something," she said. "For six days."

Ilian kept his face still. "I've been navigating."

"You've been navigating," she agreed. "And deciding. You look at me like a man doing sums." She turned her hands over, examined her palms. "I grew up in a taster's house. I know what it looks like when someone's weighing whether you're worth the dose."

The fire popped. Somewhere far off across the flats, ice in the salt crust settled with a sound like a sigh.

"I could be wrong," she added, almost gently.

"You're not," Ilian said.

* * *

He hadn't meant to say it. He heard himself say it anyway, and once it was out there in the cold air between them, hanging over the little fire, there was nothing to do but keep going.

"His name was Corren," Ilian said. "The man at the millrace. You called him a stranger. Everyone did. He wasn't mine by blood, but he was my brother all the same — sworn brother. We took oath together when we were nineteen, on the ash roads, in weather worse than this. Salt-oath. You don't break it. There isn't anywhere to break it *to*."

Petra had gone very still. The grease pot sat forgotten in her lap.

"He carried the map because he was the better runner and I was the better fighter, and we thought — we were young, we thought that mattered forever." Ilian fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "We were carrying it south, away from Vessia. Away from the Spire. Our job wasn't to deliver it. Our job was to make sure it never arrived anywhere. Burn it, scatter the ashes, swear silence, live out our lives as two more nameless men in a country that had forgotten us. That was the whole glorious mission."

"And then?"

"And then the wardens caught up with us at the river crossings, and Corren took the map and ran north instead of south, alone, in the falling winter, and I spent four years assuming he was dead." His voice came out flat. He let it. Flat was safer than the alternative. "Then I found his trail again last autumn. Fresh. Heading for the Valley of Stillness. And I followed him, because a salt-oath doesn't end just because your brother decides to be an idiot."

"He wasn't being an idiot," Petra said quietly.

"No?"

"He was dying. Frostbite, fever — you saw what was left of him. He knew he wasn't going to make it back out. So he delivered it anyway. To me." She lifted her chin. "Why me? Why does a man crawl out of Vessia, half-dead, to put a map in the hands of a valley girl who tastes poison for coin?"

Ilian looked at her across the fire and understood that the moment had arrived, the one he'd been dreading since the millrace, since before the millrace — since the night he'd stood over her sleeping form outside the boundary stones with his knife half-drawn and his oath screaming at him from both directions at once.

"Because of who your grandmother was," he said.

The wind moved across the flats. Petra didn't move at all.

"Say it," she said.

"Your grandmother was Queen Sarelle of Vessia. Last queen. The Spire ate her kingdom's memory twenty-two years ago, and when it fell, she ran — not with soldiers, not with gold, but with an infant. She crossed the boundary stones into the valley where magic couldn't follow, and she left the child there to grow up ignorant and safe, and she went back to die buying time." He swallowed. "Corren knew. He was the queen's cartographer's son; he'd grown up on these roads. When the Spire started hunting the bloodline — and it has been hunting, Petra, for twenty years, patiently, everywhere — Corren decided the map shouldn't be burned after all. It should go to the heir. Because the door out of this country needs royal blood to open, and hers is the last there is."

The silence stretched long enough that Ilian heard his own heartbeat.

"The door," Petra said at last. Her voice was careful, the way voices get when they're holding something heavy. "The door you told me about. Out of Vessia. The one that matters to the wardens."

"It matters to more than wardens. It matters to the Spire itself. It's the one way magic can leave this country — or be let in from outside. In the wrong hands—"

"In whose hands is it supposed to be?" she cut in. "Yours? Your dead brother's? Some smuggler's? Whose hands make it safe, Ilian?"

"Nobody's," he said honestly. "That's why the oath said burn it."

* * *

She stood up. Not fast, not angry — just up, the way a person stands when sitting has become unbearable. She walked a few paces out past the firelight, onto the salt, and stood looking at nothing, arms wrapped around herself, and Ilian stayed where he was because following her right then would have been the worst possible thing.

When she came back, her face had been rearranged into something calmer, which frightened him more than shouting would have.

"My whole life," she said, "I thought I was nobody. A taster's orphan. Good tongue, bad prospects. And it turns out I'm a *delivery*. A package someone hid in the valley twenty-two years ago, and everyone who's died on these roads died moving me around like cargo." She held up a hand before he could speak. "No. Don't soften it. I want it true. Was I cargo to you too? Six days ago, when you found me — what was the plan, Ilian? What were you deciding?"

He could have lied. He considered it, briefly, the way a drowning man considers air.

"I was deciding whether to kill you," he said.

The words landed on the salt flats and lay there.

"Not because I wanted to. Because Corren was dead and the map was in your hands and the oath said no living person should carry it further. If I killed you and burned the map, the bloodline hunt ends. The Spire loses the scent. Some version of Vessia stays lost but *safe*, and I keep my oath, and I go home and drink myself quiet about it." He met her eyes. "That was the arithmetic. For six days. Every time you slept, I did the sums again."

"And what changed the answer?"

"You did," he said. "Every day. You walked into a country that eats memory and you didn't flinch. You tasted a dead woman's wedding out of a bottle and grieved for her like she was family. You saw the warden's ledger with your mother's handwriting in it and you didn't shatter — you got *angry*, which is the correct response." He shook his head slowly. "A package doesn't do that. Cargo doesn't do that. I kept waiting for the heir, the symbol, the weapon — and instead there was just Petra, who swallows poison for strangers' coins and walks home through the dark singing songs about a country she's never seen."

Her breath caught, very slightly, at *singing*. He hadn't meant to reveal he'd heard that. Too late.

"So the sums stopped coming out the same," he finished. "And I hate that. I want you to know I hate it. My oath says burn it all. Everything in me that loved my brother says burn it all. But—" He spread his hands. "But here we are. On the ash roads. Walking toward the thing I swore to destroy, with the person I swore to protect the world from it."

Petra sat down across from him again. For a while she said nothing, turning the knife he'd given her over in her fingers, watching the firelight run along the blade.

"If you'd decided the other way," she said finally, "I'd never have known. Would I?"

"No."

"That's the part I can't forgive yet. Not the deciding — the deciding was honest, given what you knew. It's that I'd have just been gone. Another body on the flats, another name the salt takes." She looked up. "So here are my terms. One: you don't decide things about me anymore. You tell me, and we decide together, or I walk. Two: when we reach whatever's left of Vessia, I see everything. No protecting me from truths because they're heavy. Three—" she paused, and something flickered through the gray of her eyes that might have been the ghost of a smile, "—three, you teach me to fight properly. This knife work you've been tolerating is insulting to us both."

Ilian let out a breath he felt he'd been holding since the millrace.

"Those are terrible terms," he said. "I accept."

"Good." She sheathed the knife. Then, quieter, not quite looking at him: "Your brother. Corren. Did he know? About me — did he know what opening the door costs?"

Ilian went still. "Costs?"

"The door needs willing blood and a freely given memory. You told me that much, back at the library ruins, before the wardens came." Her voice stayed level, but her hands had gone tight in her lap. "Blood I have. But I've been thinking about the memory part, Ilian. Whose memory? Given how?"

The fire crackled. Far off, the waymarker stood against the last gray light like a headstone for a country.

"He knew," Ilian said slowly, remembering — Corren feverish at the ferry crossing, four years ago, saying things Ilian had filed away as delirium. *The door doesn't take payment, little brother. It takes love. There's a difference, and it will matter.* "He knew something. He never said it plain."

"Then we'll find out plain," Petra said, "when we get there."

She rolled into her bedroll with her back to the fire, and Ilian took first watch, and the salt flats held their whiteness around them like a held breath.

It was near midnight, the cold at its deepest, when he saw it: a light on the southern horizon. Not a star. Stars didn't move in a straight line, low and steady, tracking along the edge of the flats exactly where the ash road ran.

Not a star. Lanterns. Many of them, spaced with military precision, sweeping the white ground in slow arcs.

Wardens. Behind them, and closing.

Ilian reached over and put his hand on Petra's shoulder, and she woke instantly, silently, the way she'd learned to in six hard days — already reaching for her knife, already reading his face.

"They found us," he whispered, nodding toward the light. "Time to see how fast you really learned to run."
