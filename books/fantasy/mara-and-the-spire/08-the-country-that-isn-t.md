## 8. The Country That Isn't

The border was not a line. It was a smell.

Petra noticed it first as a change in the air — the salt-and-iron tang of the flats giving way to something older, sweeter, like rain on stone that hadn't fallen in years. Then the light changed too, going thin and gold in a way that made her think of late afternoons she had never actually lived.

"Stop fidgeting," Ilian said behind her. He'd been quiet for two days since the confession about his brother, and his voice came out rusted from disuse.

"I'm not fidgeting."

"You've checked the map four times since the last cairn."

She had. She folded it away now and kept walking. The road under their boots was still cobbled — actual fitted stones, swept clean, which was wrong in a way her mind kept sliding off of. Roads out here should have been ruts and gravel and old bones of cart traffic. This looked maintained. This looked *tended*.

They crested a low rise just past midday, and there it was: the first village.

It sat in a shallow bowl of green hills, maybe forty houses with slate roofs and whitewashed walls, a church spire — no, not a church, she corrected herself, a bell tower, Vessia had no churches, only bell towers where they rang the hours and the names — rising neat and dark against the sky. Smoke curled from three chimneys. Laundry hung on lines between two houses, shirts and sheets lifting gently in the wind.

"People," Ilian breathed beside her.

But even as he said it, Petra's stomach dropped, because the laundry had been hanging long enough that the sheets had faded to the same color as the sky, and the smoke from those chimneys rose perfectly straight, perfectly slow, like it wasn't burning anything at all. Like it was remembering how to rise rather than actually doing it.

They walked down into the village anyway. There was nowhere else to go, and besides, Petra wanted to be wrong. She wanted it badly enough that she made herself quicken her pace, made herself look for faces at windows, children in lanes, an old man splitting wood.

The lane was empty. The woodpile stood split and stacked by a door, axe still buried in the chopping block, a mitten left draped over its handle.

"Ilian."

"I see it." His hand had found his knife without him seeming to notice. "Wardens could have—"

"Wardens don't leave mittens." She touched the mitten. It was stiff with age, brittle as autumn leaf, but it had been knitted carefully, in stripes, sized for a child of maybe six. "Look at the smoke. Look at the sheets. Everything's *mid-moment*. It's like—" She searched for the words and hated the ones that came. "Like everyone walked out of a story and the story kept going without them."

Ilian said nothing. He was looking at a window across the lane, and when Petra followed his gaze she saw why: inside, a table set for supper. Plates, cups, a loaf of bread gone to gray dust under a cloth. Chairs pushed back as if the family had risen all at once, mid-meal, called away by something urgent.

Or taken.

*Gone from memory,* the smuggler woman had said back on the high road, when Petra asked what happened to Vessia's people. Not dead. Dead leaves bodies. The Spire's harvesters took the memory of you out of the world, and the world let you go the way a dream lets you go when you wake — completely, instantly, without even the courtesy of a trace.

Except the traces were here. The traces were everywhere. The Spire had eaten the people and choked on their leavings.

* * *

They made camp that night in a house at the village edge because sleeping outdoors felt obscene when there were beds right here, empty and waiting. Ilian picked the one furthest from the bell tower. He didn't explain why and Petra didn't ask.

She lay awake on a stranger's bed under a stranger's quilt, listening to the wind move through streets built for voices, and understood for the first time what homesickness meant when it had no home attached to it. It was grief with the photograph missing. It was crying at a funeral for someone whose face you couldn't recall.

Somewhere down the lane, a shutter banged. Once. Twice. Rhythmically, almost.

Almost like a knock.

She slept anyway, eventually, and dreamed of a woman singing in a language she somehow knew every word of and could not name.

* * *

In the morning they found the schoolhouse.

It stood at the center of the village, as if everything else had been arranged around it — low, wide, built of the same white stone, with tall windows and a yard where hopscotch squares were scratched into flagstones worn smooth by decades of small feet. A bell hung in its little cupola, green with age.

And over the door, carved deep into the lintel where generations of hands had smoothed it, ran a line of names.

It was custom, Ilian said quietly. Vessian schools carved the founding families' children into the doorways, a blessing and a claim at once: *you belong to this place, and this place belongs to you.* He'd seen them in other villages on the way in. He'd stopped letting Petra walk ahead of him after the second one.

She walked ahead of him now.

The names were cut in the old Vessian script, the one with the doubled letters and the little crowns over certain vowels, and Petra had never learned it, had never seen it before this week, and yet her eyes moved along the lintel reading it the way you read your own handwriting — without effort, without translation, the meaning arriving whole.

*Alenna Sova. Brek Andal. Maren Ostrev. Cael Duvane.*

Her breath went strange.

Because there, third from the end, cut shallower than the others — newer, or more treasured — was a name she knew better than her own reflection.

*Mara Villalobos.*

Not Petra. Mara. But *Villalobos*, her mother's name, the name her mother had carried out of nowhere and never explained, the name Petra had swallowed poison under for eleven years while magistrates praised her steady tongue and never once asked where a valley taster had gotten a foreign surname.

Her fingers rose to touch it. The stone was cold and smooth as water.

"That's not possible," Ilian said. He'd come up beside her and gone very still, the way he did when he was deciding whether to draw his blade. "That name died out. The royal line ended with the queen. Everyone knows—"

"Everyone knows," Petra repeated softly, "because everyone remembers it that way." She traced the M, the a, the r, the a. Under her fingertip the groove felt warm. That was impossible too. She catalogued the impossibility and kept touching it anyway. "The Spire edits memory, you told me. Whole villages, whole years. So who carved this? Who remembered me?"

"Nobody remembers you. You're from the valley. You said—"

"I know what I said." Her voice came out sharper than she intended. She took a breath. The air tasted of that old sweet rain-on-stone again, and underneath it, faintly, of woodsmoke and chalk dust and something like bread baking, none of which existed within twenty miles. "Ilian, I have never been here. I grew up four hundred miles south of this. I couldn't have carved my own name into a schoolhouse door in a country that fell before I was born."

"No," he agreed slowly. "But somebody who loved you could have."

The words landed in her chest and stayed there, heavy and glowing, like a coal she couldn't spit out.

They went inside.

* * *

The schoolroom was exactly what a schoolroom should be, which was the most horrifying thing about it. Rows of small desks. A slate board still bearing the ghost-pressures of the last lesson, chalk dust settled into the grooves of numbers she couldn't quite resolve into sense. A shelf of books with spines cracked from use. On the wall, a chart of the seasons illustrated with cheerful painted figures — a girl planting, a boy harvesting, both of them smiling with the fixed bright smiles of things that had been looked at ten thousand times and then abandoned mid-glance.

On one desk near the window, someone had left a half-finished composition. The ink had faded to brown. Petra leaned over it.

*My grandmother says the aurora is the Spire drinking the sky,* it read, in a child's careful hand, *and that someday it will drink so much there will be nothing left to remember us with. I asked her what we should do and she said someone is already doing it. She wouldn't say who. She smiled when she said it, which means it is either very good news or very bad news.*

There was no signature. There was a blot where the pen had rested too long, and a smudge where a small thumb had dragged through wet ink, hurrying.

Petra stood in the empty schoolroom of a country that wasn't, holding a dead child's homework, and wept.

She didn't decide to. It arrived like weather, the way dread had arrived at the millrace, the way magic had touched her skin at the boundary stones — something moving through her that she was only the landscape of. She cried for the child who wrote about the aurora. She cried for the mittens and the supper tables and the sheets fading into the sky. She cried because the room smelled like chalk and woodsmoke and, God help her, like *home*, like the specific home of a life she had never lived, and some locked room in her chest recognized it and rattled its door and would not stop rattling.

Ilian didn't touch her. He stood by the door with his back to her, keeping watch on the empty street, and let her cry in private, which was the kindest thing anyone had done for her in weeks, and possibly the kindest thing he knew how to do.

When it passed, she wiped her face on her sleeve and said, "My mother used to hum. Songs in a language I didn't know. When I asked, she said they were work songs, from her side of the mountains." She laughed, wet and awful. "There are no mountains near the valley, Ilian. I never even questioned it. Isn't that strange? Twenty-two years and I never once questioned it."

"The best hiding places are the ones the hidden build themselves," Ilian said. "My brother taught me that." Something crossed his face — the brother thing, always the brother thing now — and he turned back to the street. "We should keep moving. We're visible from the ridge."

"We're visible from the ridge," she echoed, and followed him out, and paused at the door with her hand on the lintel one last time, over the carved name.

Mara Villalobos.

*You belong to this place, and this place belongs to you.*

"Not yet," she whispered to it, to the country, to whoever had held the chisel. "But I'm coming back."

* * *

They walked north through the afternoon, and the villages kept coming, each one intact, each one empty, each one a wound dressed as a painting. A mill with grain still in the hopper, gone to stone. A tavern with tankards on the tables and a game of dice frozen mid-throw, the dice themselves lost somewhere in the dust of the floor, as if even they had been forgotten out of existence. A wedding arch of woven branches over one cottage door, ribbons bleached colorless, and Petra thought of the bottled wedding she'd tasted in the smugglers' caravan — the dead woman, the dancing, the joy spent like coin — and had to stop walking for a moment until her throat worked again.

Toward dusk they reached a crossroads with a signpost, and this was where the day finally broke something open in earnest.

The signpost had four arms. Three pointed to towns Ilian named under his breath — Corvess, Aldenmere, the Salt Gate — and the fourth arm pointed north, up the road toward a black shape on the horizon that Petra had been pretending not to see since noon.

The Spire. Even at this distance it looked less like a building than like a hole in the world, a needle-thin absence that the sunset bent around.

Someone had carved words into that fourth arm, deep, recent enough that the wood hadn't weathered. Recent enough to mean someone had stood here, alive, within the last few years, and left a message for whoever came after.

Petra read it aloud because she couldn't not.

*"If you can read this, you're the one she was waiting for. Don't go to the Spire. Go under it. — E."*

Ilian read it twice more, lips moving. "E," he said. "Your grandmother was Queen Elenna. Elenna of the House of Villalobos." He looked at her, and for the first time since the millrace there was something in his face that wasn't suspicion or grief or arithmetic. It was awe, and it frightened her worse than any of the rest of it. "Petra. Someone has been leaving messages for you across a dead kingdom for twenty years."

The wind came down the north road, off the flats of the falling winter, and it carried that smell again — rain on old stone, bread baking, chalk dust — the smell of a home she'd never had, calling her the way a bell calls, the way a name calls.

Above the horizon, the aurora began to wake, green and slow, and Petra could have sworn — though she would not say it aloud, not yet, not to him — that it bent toward the Spire like a mouth opening.

"Under it, then," she said, and turned north.
