## 9. Doors That Remember

The library had been a cathedral once, or the other way around—Ilian couldn't tell, and after three days in old Vessia he'd stopped trying to make sense of what the country remembered about itself.

The building stood at the crook of a dead river, its spine of arches still holding even though half the roof had folded inward like a lung giving up. Snow came through the gaps in slow, deliberate spirals, settling on shelves that rose four stories into the gloom. Books everywhere. Not burned, not looted—just left. That was the thing about Vessia that kept catching in his throat. In the valley, when something was abandoned, it was picked clean within a season. Here the people had simply stopped existing from memory, and their possessions had stayed behind like shed skins, too sacred to touch and too forgotten to matter.

Petra stood three shelves away with a book open in her hands, and she wasn't reading it so much as listening to it, the way she did everything now. She'd changed since the salt flats. Since his confession. She watched him the way you watch weather that might turn.

He deserved that. He'd told her he'd sworn to burn the map, and then he hadn't explained the rest—that some nights on the road he still reached for his knife out of habit, and had to remind himself which brother he was mourning and which woman he was walking beside.

"Here." Her voice echoed wrong in all that stone, flattened instead of multiplied. "Ilian. This one's not a book."

She was right. The spine was leather, but the pages inside were vellum stretched over boards, and the writing on them wasn't ink. It was pressed into the surface, grooves cut by a stylus, and the letters were Vessian—the flowing script he'd learned to read off his mother's knee before the Spire's wardens made literacy a hanging offense in the occupied territories.

*Catalogue of Doors,* the first page said. *Being a true account of the ways out, kept against the day of need.*

His mouth went dry. "Petra. Put it down gently. Like it's asleep."

"I know how to handle things that can kill me." But she set it down on the reading table between them, careful as a taster handling a cup for someone else's lips, and they both leaned over it the way pilgrims lean over relics.

The catalogue listed eleven doors. Most were crossed out—not struck through but *unwritten*, the letters smoothed to blankness, as if the words themselves had been eaten. Two remained. One was marked with a symbol Ilian recognized from his brother's map: an arch with a keyhole where the keystone should be.

And beneath it, in a different hand, newer, harder-pressed:

*Requirements of the Door at the River's Root:*

*First: the blood of the heir, given willingly. Coerced blood opens nothing. The door knows the difference.*

*Second: a memory, freely surrendered, of equal weight to what passes through. The door does not take. It receives. There is a difference, and the door knows that too.*

*Third: the door opens outward only. What is saved may leave. What is lost may not return.*

Ilian read it twice. Then a third time, because the second reading had gone through him like cold water and he needed to confirm the shape of the thing.

"A memory," Petra said quietly. "Of equal weight."

"It's a toll," Ilian said. "Everything in this country is a toll. The ferryman. The smugglers. Now the door itself."

"No." She traced the groove of the letters with one fingertip, not touching, hovering. "A toll you pay without meaning to. This says *freely*. It says the door *receives*, doesn't take." She looked up at him, and in the grey light falling through the broken roof her eyes were the color of the valley's winter sky right before snow. "Someone wrote this down knowing it would cost them. You don't carve your own grief into vellum unless you want whoever comes after to understand it was worth it."

He wanted to argue. He wanted to say *we don't know whose grief, we don't know if it's true, we don't know anything except that wardens are two days behind us and closing.* Instead he said, "Equal weight to what passes through. What passes through is the valley. Every soul in it."

"That's not—" She stopped. He watched her do the same arithmetic he'd been doing since the third line, watched her arrive at the sum and flinch from it. "That can't be what it means. No single memory weighs that much."

"Maybe not one." His voice came out rougher than he intended. "But the catalogue doesn't say one."

They stood there in the falling snow, in the silence of a country that had forgotten itself, and let the mathematics sit between them like a blade on a table.

It was Petra who broke it, because Petra always broke things open rather than letting them close on her. "Then we find another way. There were eleven doors. Nine are unwritten, but unwritten isn't destroyed. Maybe—"

"Petra."

"—maybe the Spire didn't eat them, maybe they just went dormant, maybe—"

"*Petra.*" He caught her wrist, gentle, the way you hold someone back from a ledge. Her pulse hammered under his fingers, fast as a bird's. "Look at the last line again."

*What is saved may leave. What is lost may not return.*

Her jaw set. He knew that jaw. He'd seen it on the millrace morning, on the salt flats, on every road since. It was the jaw of a woman deciding that the world could go to hell in any direction it liked, as long as it went there after she'd finished what she came to do.

"The valley seals in weeks," she said. "The boundary stones. My grandmother built them to keep magic out, and everything outside them is dying because the Spire drinks the world like a wound drinks blood. If the valley seals before the door opens—"

"Then everyone inside lives small and safe forever, and everything outside goes dark." He released her wrist. "I know. I've known since the ferry. I've known since before the ferry, if I'm honest."

"Then say it plainly, Ilian. Whatever you're circling. Say it."

He should have lied. Some part of him, the part that had loved his brother and sworn oaths over his brother's body, said *lie to her, buy her one more night of not knowing.* But he'd spent enough of this road lying by omission, and it had nearly gotten them both killed on the stair road, and besides—she was looking at him like she'd already read the catalogue herself, somewhere behind her eyes, and just needed him to confirm the text.

"The door needs a memory of equal weight," he said. "There might be one memory in the world heavy enough. Yours. Everything you are. And the price isn't dying, Petra. Dying would be simpler. The price is being *unmade from remembering*. Everyone who ever knew you forgets you ever existed. You walk out of the door and into a world that has no room in it for you at all."

The snow fell. Somewhere high in the stacks, a shelf groaned and settled, shedding a fine dust of paper-mold into the light.

"That's why my grandmother hid me in the valley," Petra said at last, very quietly. "Not to protect me *from* the door. To protect me *for* it. She hid me past magic's reach so I'd grow up whole, with a full life to give. A life nobody could harvest first." She laughed, one short note with no humor in it at all. "I'm not the heir. I'm the *payment*. Twenty years of keeping me safe, and I was never meant to survive this. I was meant to be spent."

"No." He said it hard enough that it echoed. "No. The catalogue says *willingly*. Your grandmother could have raised you to this like a lamb to slaughter. Instead she hid you so well that even the Spire lost the scent for twenty years. That's not a woman fattening a sacrifice. That's a woman buying her granddaughter time to choose."

"And if I choose no?"

"Then the valley seals, and the world outside ends, and you live." He held her gaze. "I won't pretend that's nothing. I'd take it. Gods help me, I'd take it and sleep at night."

Something moved across her face—too fast to name—and then the moment shattered, because the dogs started.

***

He heard them before he saw anything: a high, thin belling through the broken windows, carried on wind that had turned without warning. North wind. The wardens had been tracking them south along the river. Now they were north. They'd doubled back, or they'd never left, or—

"The smugglers' route," Ilian breathed. "We came in on the smugglers' route. They know it. They've always known it; they let it run because it flushes out people like us."

Petra already had the catalogue wrapped in cloth and shoved inside her coat. "How long?"

"Minutes. Maybe less, if the wind stays at their backs." He was moving as he spoke, sweeping the table clear with his forearm, killing the stub of candle they'd allowed themselves. "Back door?"

"There's no back door. There's a window on the river side, three stories up, above the flood line—it's how the librarians got books in, there'll be a hoist—"

"You've been reading the building."

"I've been reading everything since the schoolhouse." She was already climbing, quick and sure-footed up the collapsed shelving that formed a stair none of the original architects intended. "Come on. And Ilian—"

"Yeah."

"If they take the catalogue—"

"They won't." He followed her up, palms burning on old wood. Below them, faint but getting less faint, boots on stone. Many boots. The particular cadence of men who had done this a hundred times and enjoyed it.

The window was exactly where she'd promised: a square of white sky framed in rotted casing, with the rusted arm of a book-hoist jutting out over the frozen river. Beyond it, the ice stretched away flat and blue-white toward the far bank and the black line of forest.

Too far to jump. Too thin to run. And the dogs were in the nave below now—he could hear them snuffling, hear the handlers' low voices, hear the wet click of claws on marble.

Ilian looked at the hoist. The hoist looked back at him, insolent with rust.

"Rope?" Petra asked.

"Rope." He had thirty feet of it, coiled at his hip, because he was not a man who traveled without rope anymore, not since the stair road, not since he'd watched the smugglers die buying him seconds. He knotted it to the hoist arm, tested the rusted iron with his whole weight, felt it hold the way old things sometimes hold—out of stubbornness rather than strength.

Below, a dog bayed directly beneath them, and a warden's voice said, clearly, "Up there. The dust is disturbed. Someone's been sleeping in my library."

*My* library. As if the Spire owned even forgetting.

Ilian went down the rope first, hand over hand, boots scraping stone, and dropped the last ten feet onto the river ice with a crack that sounded, in the dead air, like the end of the world. Petra came after him, faster, and landed rolling, and was up and running before he'd found his balance.

Behind them, above, the window filled with shapes. A whistle blew. The dogs began to sing.

They ran. The ice held them the way it holds everything in winter—reluctantly, temporarily, with malice aforethought. Ahead the far bank grew slowly, insultingly slowly, and Ilian's lungs filled with knives, and beside him Petra ran the way she did everything, with her whole self, spending herself like coin she didn't expect to get change for.

And then the trees ahead of them opened, and men stepped out of them.

Ilian's knife was in his hand before his mind caught up. Six figures, hooded, bundled, faces half-lost behind scarves—and then the foremost pulled the scarf down and grinned through a beard furred with frost, and Ilian almost sobbed with relief.

"Sable," he gasped. "You're supposed to be dead."

"Everyone's supposed to be dead. It's the great convenience of my profession." Sable jerked his head and his five companions melted into the treeline, covering the river behind them. "Wardens on the ice. Move. We'll argue about my funeral later."

They ran together into the trees, and behind them the dogs reached the riverbank and stopped, howling at the waterline, unwilling—for now—to cross.

***

The smugglers' camp was a hollow beneath a fallen watchtower, screened by pines and warmed by a fire dug into a pit so its light wouldn't show. There was food—actual food, dried meat and a hard cheese and something like beer—and Ilian ate like a man making up for a week of hunger while Sable crouched across the flames and studied Petra with the frank appraisal of a man pricing a horse.

"So," Sable said. "You found the catalogue."

Petra stiffened. "How do you know that?"

"Because you're alive. Anyone finds that library without the catalogue, they come out with a head full of poetry and no direction. Anyone finds it *with* the catalogue—" he spread his hands, "—they come out looking like the two of you look. Like people who've just learned the price of the ticket." He leaned forward. Firelight moved in his eyes. "Blood of the heir, freely given. Memory of equal weight, freely surrendered. Am I close?"

"How do you know the terms?" Ilian said. "Sable. How do you *know*?"

"Because my grandmother helped write them." Sable said it lightly, the way he said everything, but his eyes didn't. "The smugglers aren't just smugglers, friend. We're what's left of the Vessian royal household. The ones who slipped out when the Spire fell on the palace. We've been running memory out of the country for twenty years—bottling it, hiding it, keeping it from the Spire's jars—one bottle at a time. Waiting for the heir to come looking for a door." He smiled, and it was the saddest smile Ilian had ever seen on a living face. "We got tired of waiting. Started thinking maybe there wasn't an heir. And then word comes down the wire about a valley-touched girl and a man carrying a dead cartographer's map, and here you are."

"You said you'd help us," Ilian said slowly. "On the stair road. You died helping us."

"I said I'd help you get to the library. Contract fulfilled, tragically." Sable shrugged. "Getting you to the *door* is a different contract. Harder roads. More wardens. And a price."

"There's always a price," Petra said.

"Now she's learning." Sable nodded at her with something like respect. "The price is this: when the door opens, we take our due. The household has waited twenty years in the cold for the way out to work. When it works, we go through first—all of us, our families, our bottled memories, everything we've saved. Before the valley. Before anyone else."

Ilian frowned. "The catalogue says the door opens outward once. What is saved may leave—"

"What is saved," Sable agreed. "So the question becomes: what counts as saved? Who decides? The heir decides." He looked at Petra, and for the first time the levity dropped away entirely, and underneath it Ilian saw the thing that had kept six smugglers alive twenty years in a hunted country: iron. "That's the price, girl. Not coin. Not blood—we'll spill plenty of that before the root of the river. The price is that you carry the choosing. Every soul you rank behind us is a soul you weighed and found lighter. Can you do that? Because if you can't, say so now, and we'll walk away, and you can meet the wardens on your own."

The fire cracked. Somewhere out in the dark, a wolf—or a dog, out here it hardly mattered—gave one long cry and got no answer.

Petra looked into the fire for a long time. Ilian watched her and thought: *she's tasting it. The way she tastes everything. Testing for poison.* And he understood suddenly that the poison in this cup was not the choosing. It was that some part of her wanted to say yes, wanted the weight of it, the way she'd always wanted a world larger than the valley—and the world kept answering by handing her larger and larger weights to carry.

"Yes," Petra said. "I can do that."

"Then we have a contract." Sable spat in his palm and held it out, and after a heartbeat Petra spat in hers and took it, and the deal was struck the old way, the Vessian way, witnessed by fire and iron and the five silent shadows around the pit.

"One more thing," Sable said, settling back. "You should know what's coming. The door at the river's root isn't unguarded. The Spire has wanted it sealed—or worse, wanted it *theirs*—since the fall. There's a garrison at the root. Wardens, hounds, and worse than wardens." He fed a stick to the fire. "Three days' march. We'll get you to the tree line. After that, you're on your own until the last hundred yards."

"And the memory?" Ilian asked. "The payment. Equal weight. Has anyone ever actually—"

"No one's ever opened it." Sable's grin came back, thinner. "But the old queen left instructions. Sealed orders, to be opened by the heir at the door itself. My family's kept them twenty years." He reached into his coat and drew out a slim packet wrapped in oilcloth, tied with a cord the color of dried blood, and set it on the ground between them. "Whatever's in there, girl, it's waited longer than you've been alive. Sleep on it tonight. Open it tomorrow."

Petra reached for the packet and stopped, her fingers an inch from the oilcloth, and Ilian saw her hand trembling—just barely, just enough that only a taster trained to notice small betrayals would catch it.

Above the hollow, through the pine branches, the northern sky had begun to move. Green light, rippling, bending—as if something very tall and very far away were drinking it.

The Spire, feeding.

"Three days," Sable said softly, watching the same sky. "Best sleep fast."
