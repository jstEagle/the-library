## 10. The Spire's Shadow

They climbed for two days to reach the ridge, and the whole time the aurora grew.

In the valley, Petra had seen the northern lights only in songs — *the sky's green rivers*, her mother used to sing, *where the dead go walking with their lanterns lit*. Here they were not a song. They were weather. They poured across the dark like spilled oil catching fire, green and violet and a bruised red at the edges, and they moved. That was the worst part. They moved like something alive, like something feeding.

"It's brighter here," she said. "Than anywhere else on the road."

Ilian didn't answer at first. He was ahead of her on the switchback, one hand on the rock, his breath smoking out of him in short white flags. He had been quieter since the salt flats, quieter even than his usual quiet, which was already a kind of architecture — walls built carefully around whatever he carried. Since he'd told her about his brother, about the oath to burn the map, he'd been watching her the way a man watches a fire he started.

"Because it's drinking," he said finally.

"Drinking what?"

He tipped his chin up at the sky. "What do you think the lights are, Petra? Pretty weather? It's memory. All of it. Everything the Spire has ever taken out of anyone's head goes up through that needle and burns off into the dark. Vessia's whole country, still burning. Twenty years of it." He said it flatly, the way you'd report the price of grain. But his jaw was tight. "My brother used to say you could taste it on the wind this close. Sweet, like overripe fruit."

Petra tasted the wind. It tasted like cold stone and her own blood where her cracked lips had split. But she didn't tell him that. Some part of her had learned, over these weeks, when not to hand Ilian more reasons to look at her like that.

The ridge came up to meet them in the last hour before dark — a spine of black rock furred with old snow, and beyond it nothing but air and the falling long winter and, far off across a frozen plain, the Spire.

She had imagined it a hundred times since the library, since the smugglers' maps with their careful inked needle. She had imagined a tower. What stood on the plain was not a tower. It was a wound the world had never bothered to close — a needle of black glass so tall its top vanished into the aurora itself, and the aurora bent toward it. Bent. Curved down along its length like water finding a drain, all those rivers of light pouring inward, spiraling, swallowed. The green went in at the top and did not come out.

The plain below it was not empty. Even at this distance, even in the failing light, Petra could see them: the harvested villages, laid out in perfect concentric rings around the Spire's root like ripples in a pond. Intact roofs. Straight streets. No smoke from any chimney. No light in any window. A country arranged as an offering.

"That's where the names go," she said. She hadn't meant to say it aloud.

"The jars go inside. The people stay where they were standing when it took them." Ilian crouched at the ridgeline, pulling his cloak tight. "We camp here tonight. Tomorrow we drop north, cross the river ice, come at it from the ferry road. You don't approach the Spire straight on. The wardens' sight-lines are—"

"Ilian."

"—designed so that anything moving toward the root gets seen three miles—"

"Ilian. Look at it."

He looked. And she watched his face do what hers was doing: the thing where awe and horror arrive at exactly the same moment and can't be told apart.

The Spire pulsed. Slowly, like a heartbeat measured in minutes rather than seconds, the black glass brightened from within — a deep red glow climbing its length, and where the glow passed, the aurora above it flared and thickened, fed, *grateful*. Then the glow sank again and the needle went dark and the sky dimmed by a degree, and somewhere down in those ring-villages, Petra understood, someone's last remembered birthday burned off into nothing.

"It's eating," she said quietly. "Right now. We're watching it eat."

"We're watching it digest." Ilian's voice had gone rough. "There's a difference, and my brother spent his last year explaining it to wardens who didn't care." He turned away from it deliberately, the way you turn your back on a fire you can't put out. "Come on. Shelter behind the rocks. Cold comes off that plain like a blade."

But Petra stayed one moment longer at the edge.

And the Spire — she would swear this later, swear it on anything, though there was no one left who'd remember her well enough to doubt her — the Spire seemed to notice her looking.

Not with eyes. With attention. A pressure against her mind like a thumb testing fruit at market, gentle, appraising, patient. The valley had taught her what held breath felt like; this was the opposite. This was an exhale twenty years long, washing over her, and in it, faint as a word spoken in another room, something said:

*Petra.*

She stumbled back from the ridgeline. Her heart slammed. Behind her, Ilian was arranging their bedrolls among the rocks, oblivious, and the aurora wheeled on overhead, indifferent, magnificent, and the pressure was gone as if it had never been.

Just the wind, she told herself. Just the cold getting into her skull.

But her hands were shaking, and it wasn't from the cold, and she did not sleep easily.

* * *

The dream came without permission, the way everything true does.

She was standing on the salt flats, except the flats were wrong — they were silver, mirror-bright, and the sky above them was the aurora stretched down to touch the ground, so that she walked inside the light. Every direction looked the same. There was no Ilian. There was no map. There was no valley, no millrace, no magistrate's poisoned cup, no mother's grave under the rowan tree. There was only the light and her own footprints appearing behind her and vanishing ahead of her, as if she had always been walking and would always be walking, and none of it needed remembering because none of it would ever end.

"You're difficult to reach," said a voice.

It came from everywhere, the way the aurora had bent from everywhere. It was not cruel. That surprised her. She had expected the voice of the thing that ate countries to be cruel, or at least vast and impersonal, thunder in a jar. This voice was warm. Reasonable. Almost kind. It sounded, horribly, like someone she might have trusted.

"You're difficult to reach," it said again, "because you were hidden well. Your grandmother was thorough. I'll grant her that." A pause, and then, almost companionably: "I've been looking for you for twenty years, Petra Villalobos. Do you know how rare you are? Do you know what you are?"

"A door," Petra said. In the dream her voice didn't shake. "That's all I am. A way out."

"A door, yes. A key. An heir." The light around her rippled, and shapes formed in it — faces, hundreds of them, thousands, drifting past her like fish under ice. She recognized none of them and grieved for all of them. "But you keep thinking small. Heir of Vessia. Key of the door. Blood for the lock. Your grandmother thought in locks too. It's why she lost."

"She didn't lose. She saved me."

"And what did saving get her?" The voice softened further, and now it was gentle the way a hand on a fevered forehead is gentle. "A granddaughter raised on poison-tasting coin in a valley of amnesiacs. A life spent swallowing other people's deaths. Petra. I have watched you since the millrace. I watched you carry a dead man's map through winter. I watched you learn what memory costs — the ferryman's price, the jars in the warden's ledger, your own mother's handwriting among the donors. You understand the economy better than any heir before you. So let me make you an honest offer, one ruler to a future ruler."

The light parted.

Before her stood the Spire — but small, human-scaled, a model of itself rising from the silver floor, and beside it rose something else: Vessia. Whole. Alive. She knew it instantly, the way you know your own face, though she had never seen it. White-walled cities with banners going up in the morning wind. Orchards in blossom. A harbor full of ships. People — thousands of people, laughing, arguing, singing, *remembering* — filling streets that in the waking world stood empty, tables set for no one.

"Give me your blood willingly," the voice said, "and I will give you this. Not a memory of Vessia. Vessia. Restored. Every name in my jars poured back into every mouth. Every table filled. Every song sung by its singer. You would be queen of a country that remembers itself — and Petra, you would hold the ledger yourself. Power over memory. Over what is kept and what is given up. Your mother's face, alive whenever you wish it. Your valley's love, guaranteed forever — they would never forget you, because forgetting would be yours to forbid."

The dream-Petra reached toward the blossoming orchard. Her fingers passed into it and it was warm. It smelled like her childhood, which had been cold and poor and smelled like tallow and turnips, and it smelled like that anyway, because memory is generous to the young.

"No one would ever forget me," she repeated.

"No one would ever forget you," the Spire agreed. "You would never have to open the door. You would never have to pay. Everyone pays, Petra — that is the first law of my country. The door demands a memory freely given, and the only memories worth giving are the ones you cannot bear to lose. I am offering you the one bargain in existence where you lose nothing. Where *everyone* loses nothing." The warmth gathered close around her like a coat being laid on her shoulders. "The girl who swallows poison for strangers deserves better than to be swallowed herself. Take instead of give. Rule instead of unlock. Say yes."

And here was the terrible thing, the thing she would carry up out of the dream like a splinter under the skin: she wanted to say yes.

Not weakly. Not in some fog the Spire had spun around her. She wanted it with her whole stubborn, hungry, valley-raised heart. She was tired. She was so tired — of cold roads and half-rations and a man beside her who kept deciding whether to trust her, of being a key, a door, a bloodline, a burden arranged by a dead queen. The offer was not a trap made of lies. That was what made it a trap. Every word of it might be true, and wanting it was not foolishness, it was the most sane thing she had felt since the millrace.

Her mouth opened.

And in the dream, distantly, she heard dogs.

Not real dogs — the memory of them, the shape of them, hounds baying behind two figures slipping past boundary stones at night, the sound that had chased her out of the only home she'd ever known. And threaded through the baying, another sound: Ilian's voice on the salt flats, flat and rough. *My brother used to say you could taste it on the wind.*

His brother. Who had died pressing a map into her hands with her full name on his lips. Who had sworn Ilian to burn that map rather than deliver it — and Ilian hadn't burned it. Whatever else he was deciding, whatever walls he built, he had carried it to her instead.

Everyone pays. Yes. But some people chose what they paid, and chose it for each other, and that was the whole difference between the Spire and everything it had eaten.

Petra closed her hand around the dream-orchard's warmth and let it go.

"No," she said.

The light stilled. For the first time the voice lost its warmth, and underneath it she heard something enormous shifting its weight, the way a mountain might shift, the way a debt comes due.

"No?" 

"I'm a taster," Petra said. "It's my whole trade. I know poison when it's offered kindly." She stepped back from the little Vessia, from the little Spire, from the smiling harbor full of returned souls. "You showed me everything I want. That's how I know you'll never give it. Things freely offered by things that eat—" She shook her head. "Find another key."

The silver flats cracked like ice on a pond.

The last thing the voice said, as the dream tore apart around her, was not angry after all. It was worse. It was patient, and amused, and quiet as a ledger closing:

*"The Spire collects either way, little queen. Ask the ferryman."*

* * *

She woke gasping, hand pressed flat against the frozen rock, heart going like a fist on a door.

Grey dawn. Wind. The smell of snow coming down off the high passes. Ilian was already up, kicking dirt over their small fire's ashes, and he glanced at her once — that quick, assessing glance she'd learned to read, the one that catalogued whether she was wounded, frostbitten, or lying.

"Dream?" he asked.

"How could you possibly—"

"You said a name in your sleep. Not mine." He shouldered his pack. His face was turned away, doing its wall-building work. "You said *no* very loudly, and then you said *Vessia*, like a curse."

Petra sat up slowly. Her palm ached, and when she looked at it, confused, there was nothing there — no cut, no mark — but the ache sat in it anyway, exactly where the door's lock would one day take her blood. She flexed her fingers and made herself say it plainly, because she was done letting things grow in the dark between them.

"It offered me everything, Ilian. Vessia restored. My mother alive. Power over memory itself. All of it, if I gave my blood to the Spire instead of the door." She watched him go still. "It nearly worked. That's what I need you to understand. It didn't lie to me. It just showed me what I want and waited."

For a long moment Ilian said nothing. The wind moved his hair; the aurora's last pale ghosts faded in the brightening east. When he finally spoke, his voice was low and strange.

"My brother dreamed it too. Three nights before he died. He wouldn't tell me what it said — only that he'd stopped sleeping facing north." He looked at her then, fully, and for once there were no walls in it at all, only a raw and frightened honesty. "He also stopped trying to burn the map after that dream. Said burning it wouldn't matter anymore. Said *it knows where she is now.*"

The words landed in Petra's chest like stones dropped down a well.

"So it wasn't a dream," she said. "It was a visit."

"It was an opening bid." Ilian glanced north, toward the ridge, toward the black needle drinking the sky, and his hand found his knife hilt without seeming to decide to. "Which means we're out of time. The river crossing is a day's hard walk and the ice won't hold past the next snowfall. There's a ferryman on the far bank who takes payment no one wants to give." He held out his hand to pull her up. "Get your pack, Petra. The Spire doesn't knock twice."

She took his hand and rose, and together they turned their backs on the ridge and started down toward the frozen river — while behind them, high above the empty villages, the aurora flared once, brief and red, like something licking its lips.
