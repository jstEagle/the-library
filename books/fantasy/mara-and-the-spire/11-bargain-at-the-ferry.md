## 11. Bargain at the Ferry

The river announced itself before we saw it — a long pale seam in the salt flats, frozen so hard it rang when the wind crossed it. Ilian heard it first and stopped walking, one hand up, because in Vessia you learned early that a sound like that could be ice singing or ice breaking, and the difference was the difference between a road and a grave.

"The Verrow," I said.

Petra came up beside me, breath steaming, cheeks scoured red by three days of salt wind. She'd stopped complaining about the cold somewhere on the second day, not because she'd made peace with it but because she'd decided complaining was a debt she couldn't afford. That was Petra. She counted everything.

"It doesn't look like much," she said.

"Nothing out here does." I squinted north along the white seam. "That's the point of it."

In the old days — my brother's stories, mostly, and he'd gotten half of them from smugglers who got theirs from drunks — the Verrow had been a living river, brown and slow, thick with barges carrying grain down from the hill country to the cities of the plain. Then the Spire rose, and the Spire wanted cold, wanted stillness, wanted everything held exactly where it was. The river froze in a single winter and never thawed again. Now there was only the ferryman, and the ferryman's boat, which did not sail but slid, drawn by something no one had ever seen clearly enough to describe twice.

"You're making that face," Petra said.

"What face?"

"The one where you know something and you're deciding how much of it to tell me."

I almost laughed. Three weeks ago I'd put a knife to this woman's throat in a ditch outside Stillwater, and now she read me like her own handwriting. "There's a toll," I said. "For the crossing."

"Coin?"

"No."

She waited. She was good at waiting. It was one of the things about her that had unsettled me most, back at the beginning — everyone else in my life filled silence like a hole in a roof, hammering at it until something gave way. Petra just stood in it and let it stand around her.

"Memories," I said finally. "One each. Given freely. That's what the stories say, anyway."

"That's absurd."

"Yes."

"I don't even know how you'd *give* one. Or how he'd take it. Or—"

"Petra." I turned to face her. The wind pulled at her hood, at the frayed edge of the map-case strap across her chest. "Everything between here and the Spire is going to be absurd. We've walked through a country where every table is still set for dinner and nobody ever came home. A river that froze the year magic died is not going to be the place where the world starts making sense again."

She looked at me for a long moment, and then she nodded once, sharp, and started walking again, and I followed, and neither of us said what we were both thinking: *what would you even choose to lose?*

* * *

We found him at midday, or he found us — it was hard to say which, with him. One moment the riverbank was empty white nothing, and the next there was a hut crouched against the ice like a barnacle, black timber furred with frost, smoke rising from its chimney in a column so straight it looked drawn on with ink. And beside the hut, moored to a post driven into the frozen bank, lay the boat: long, low, iron-banded, its hull scarred with old burns.

The ferryman sat on an overturned crate outside the hut, mending a net that had no business existing on a frozen river. He was old in a way that didn't add up — his hands were young, quick and unspotted, but his eyes had gone the color of weathered bone, and when he looked up at us I felt the look go through me and keep going, all the way back down the salt flats to Stillwater, to the millrace, to every step of the road behind us.

"Two travelers," he said. His voice was mild, unhurried, the voice of a man who had nowhere to be for a hundred years. "South to north. You'll want the crossing."

"We will," I said.

"Mm." He went back to his net. His fingers moved without him, knotting and slipping, knotting and slipping. "Sit, if you like. There's tea. It tastes like nothing. Everything here does, eventually."

We sat. Petra took the crate across from him; I stood, because standing let me keep the boat in view and I trusted the boat less than I trusted the man, which was saying something. The tea, when he poured it from a dented kettle, was gray and smelled faintly of snow about to fall. Petra drank hers without flinching. I'd stopped being surprised by that.

"Name's Corvess," he said. "Was. Names wear thin out here. You can call me ferryman."

"Ilian."

"And the lady?"

"She doesn't give her name to strangers," I said.

Something moved behind the ferryman's bone-colored eyes — not quite a smile, more the memory of where a smile used to live. "Sensible. Names are currency too, though fewer people know it." He set down his cup. "You've heard the price."

"One memory each," Petra said. "Given freely."

"Given freely," he agreed. "That part matters. I can't take. Taking is the Spire's trade, and I want nothing of its methods on my river." He flexed those young hands. "A memory has to be handed over the way you'd hand over a coin. Open palm. Willing. And once it's mine, it's gone from you — not sleeping, not folded away. Gone. You won't miss it, because missing requires remembering that there was something to miss. That's the mercy of it. Or the horror. Depends on the day."

"And what do you do with them?" Petra asked.

He looked at her for a while. "I keep the river frozen," he said simply. "The Spire wants the Verrow dead. I have other opinions. Every memory I hold is a coal in the stove, and the stove keeps the water under the ice moving, just enough, just where no one can see. Your memories cross my river warm, traveler. That's the whole arrangement. That's the whole country, if you look at it straight. Somebody's past is always paying for somebody's future."

The wind came off the ice with a sound like a struck bell. In the hut, something ticked — a clock, maybe, or a jar settling.

"Which memory?" I asked. "Do we choose?"

"You choose. That's the other half of freely." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment the youth of his hands and the age of his eyes seemed to meet in the middle of his face, and I saw what he must have been once, before the river and the cold and whatever bargain had made him this. "Choose carefully. Not your worst memory — everyone thinks they should pay with their worst, as if pain were the only honest coin. It isn't. Pay with something you love. That's what makes it a sacrifice instead of a disposal. The Spire takes your worst and calls it cleansing. I ask for your best and call it fare. You can tell a lot about a power by what it asks for."

* * *

We walked apart after that, down the bank, out of earshot of the hut, though I doubted distance mattered to a man like that. The ice stretched away north, featureless, and somewhere far along it the far shore waited, invisible.

"He's lying," Petra said. "Or tricking us. Some third thing."

"Maybe. But the stories agree with him, and the stories come from smugglers who'd cheat their own mothers. When men like that repeat a rumor without embellishing it, it's usually because the truth is worse than anything they could invent."

"So we pay. Fine." Her jaw was set. "You pick something. I'll pick something. We cross, we move on."

"Petra." I kept my voice level. "Think about what you're carrying. Think about what's in your head right now that the Spire wants. If you hand this man a memory — any memory — and he looks into it, even for a second—"

"I've thought about it."

"Then think harder. You're the last heir of Vessia. Every memory you own is a room with treasure in it. You'd be handing a stranger the keys and asking him not to look around."

"And what are you handing over?" she shot back. "You've got twenty years of running and hiding and—"

"My brother," I said.

It came out before I'd decided to say it, the way true things do. Petra went still.

"Davin," I said. "The man who died at the millrace. Everyone calls him the stranger, the cartographer, the poor frozen fool. He was my brother. Older by two years. Taught me to read maps before I could read words. Swore the same oath I did — we were both sworn to the old cartographers' guild, before the guild was ash." I looked north, at the blank ice, because looking at Petra while I said this was more than I could manage. "When he ran, he carried the map. When I caught up to him — three years chasing him across two countries — I told him I'd burn it. I meant it. The map gets people killed, and it got him killed, and I wasn't going to let it do the same to some taster girl from a valley that magic forgot."

"But you didn't burn it."

"I couldn't. He'd sewn it into his coat lining and I found it after—" My throat closed. I pushed through it. "After. And by then I'd spent three weeks watching you walk toward a thing that wants to eat you alive, and I understood why Davin wouldn't burn it. Some doors you don't get to close. You only get to decide who walks through."

Petra said nothing. The wind filled the silence. When I finally turned to her, her eyes were wet, but her voice, when it came, was steady.

"Pay with him," she said quietly. "If you have to pay. Give the ferryman a memory of Davin. Not your best one — one you can survive losing. But make it count."

"That's the problem," I said, and laughed, and it came out wrong. "They all count. That's what nobody tells you about memories. There's no small ones. There's just the ones you've practiced losing and the ones you haven't."

* * *

I chose at dusk, sitting alone on the ice with my back against the hull of the boat, because the boat was iron and iron felt honest.

Davin filled my head the way he always did when I let him: nineteen and insufferable, teaching me constellations on the roof of the guildhouse, insisting the stars were a map too, just one nobody had finished surveying. There was a night — one night, out of thousands — that I kept returning to. The night before he ran. We'd sat in the guildhouse cellar among the rolled charts of dead Vessia, and he'd shown me the door on the old survey, the door out, and he'd said, *if it comes to it, Lian, remember the map isn't the country. The country's the people. Save the people.* And then he'd stolen the map and vanished, and I'd hated him for six years, and then I'd held him while he died with a girl's name in his mouth.

I could give the ferryman the cellar. I could give him the stars on the roof. I could not give him Davin dying, because that memory was the whetstone everything else in me had been sharpened against, and without it I didn't know what kind of blade I'd be.

But the cellar — the cellar I could lose. Barely. Almost.

I walked back to the fire. The ferryman was waiting, hands folded, patient as stone. Petra sat apart, arms wrapped around her knees, watching me with an expression I couldn't read and didn't try to.

"Well?" the ferryman said.

"I have one." My mouth was dry. "How does it work?"

"Open palm. Say it aloud — what you're giving. Speak it into your hand like you're setting a bird down. Then close your fingers, and open them empty."

"That's it? No ritual? No—"

"Ritual is for people who need to pretend the thing is bigger than them." He extended his own hand, palm up, and I swear the air above it warmed. "Whenever you're ready, traveler. The river doesn't hurry and neither do I."

I thought of Davin in the cellar, lamplight on the old vellum, his voice saying *save the people.*

I opened my hand.

"I give the night my brother showed me the door," I said. "Willingly. As fare."

For a heartbeat nothing happened. Then something left me — there's no honest way to describe it except as weight, a specific weight, the exact weight of lamplight and dust and my brother's voice, lifting out through my arm and into my palm, and I closed my fist on it and felt it press back, warm, alive, *his*, and then I opened my fingers and my hand was empty and the cellar was gone.

Not dimmed. Gone. I reached for it and found smooth wall where the door had been. I knew — abstractly, the way you know a fact from a ledger — that there had been a night, that Davin had shown me something important in it. The shape of the knowledge remained. The night itself was a hole with edges.

"There," the ferryman said softly, and closed his hand over the air above my palm, and drew something in — I saw it for half a second, a curl of gold light like a thread off a loom — and pressed it to his chest, and it sank into him and was gone. "Paid in full. Warm fare, traveler. Your brother sounds like he was worth the crossing."

I couldn't answer. I stood there testing the hole where the memory had been, the way a tongue tests a missing tooth, and Petra rose and came to stand beside me, and her shoulder touched mine, and that was the only reason I stayed upright.

"Now you," the ferryman said, turning to her, palm open, patient.

"No."

The word dropped into the fire-crackle silence like a stone into a well. The ferryman's bone-colored eyes sharpened, just slightly.

"No?" he said.

"I'm not paying." Petra lifted her chin. "I'll find another way across. Walk the ice. Go around. Whatever it takes."

"The ice is forty miles of nothing and the cold out there doesn't kill you, it *keeps* you," the ferryman said mildly. "And there is no around. The Verrow runs bank to bank across the whole width of the world. Men have walked upstream for a season and found me waiting for them on the other side of their own detour. I'm not a man, precisely. I'm more of a fact."

"Then I'll swim under the ice."

"You couldn't hold your breath long enough to insult me properly."

"Then—"

"Child." He said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "Look at me. Look at what I am. I was a king's harbormaster once, on this very river, when it ran brown and loud. I refused a toll too — refused to let the Spire's collectors board my barges. So the Spire made me the toll. Do you understand? It didn't kill me. It *employed* me. That is what refusal costs, here, in the end." He leaned forward. "You want to refuse me. Refuse the Spire instead. It's coming for your memories regardless — it's been coming for twenty years, since the day you were born behind walls it couldn't see through. Every step north, you've been spending them. The valley you grew up in, the mother whose handwriting you found in a warden's ledger, the taste of poison on your tongue and the taste of a stranger's kiss — the Spire keeps accounts, and yours is already open. Sooner or later it collects."

The fire popped. Somewhere under our feet, deep beneath forty feet of black ice, the river moved, and I felt it move, faint and endless, like a pulse.

"Pay me one memory, given freely, and it's *yours* to spend," the ferryman said. "Warm. Willing. On your terms. Refuse, and you cross on credit — and the Spire's interest, little queen, is ruinous."

"I'm not a queen," Petra said, but her voice had gone quiet, and her hand had drifted to the map-case at her chest, and I saw her fingers pressing flat against the leather over her heart, the way you press a wound.

"No," the ferryman agreed, settling back onto his crate, picking up his impossible net. "Not yet. But you will be, or you'll be fuel, and either way—" he nodded north, at the darkening ice, at the horizon where a smudge of black stood against the aurora like a needle through cloth "—you'll want to reach the far shore before the last snows seal the valley. The boat leaves at first light. Both of you, or one. The river doesn't care. The river's dead. Only the fare cares."

He mended his net. We stood there. And under the ice, faint as a heartbeat, the river kept moving, kept warm, paid for — I understood now — by every soul who had ever sat where we sat and opened their hand.

Petra stared north for a long time. Then she looked at me, and in the firelight her face was the face of someone doing arithmetic she hated.

"Ilian," she said. "Tell me again exactly what you gave him. Every detail you can still remember."

Because — I realized, with a cold that had nothing to do with the river — she'd heard it too. What the ferryman had called me, when he took my brother's night into his chest.

*Your brother sounds like he was worth the crossing.*

Except I had never said my brother's name aloud.
