## 12. Halfway House

The inn appeared out of the snow like something remembered rather than seen.

One moment there was only the white road and the wind worrying at their hoods, and the next there was a dark shape against the grey — a squat stone building with a sagging porch, its signboard hanging by one chain. Ilian climbed the steps first, knife already drawn, because that was how he did everything now. He'd been drawing that knife for three days straight, ever since the ferryman took his brother's memory and Ilian had stood on the ice looking like a man who had set down something heavy and couldn't remember what it was.

"Clear," he said through the door.

Petra followed him into a common room that smelled of cold ash and old lavender. A hearth big enough to stand in. Tables pushed neatly against the walls, as if someone had tidied up before leaving. Behind the bar, rows of bottles caught the light from the frost-furred windows — but when she pulled one down, it held nothing but dust and a smell like rain on hot stone.

"No drink," she said.

"No guests either." Ilian was standing very still in the middle of the room, turning slowly, the way he did in every new space. Cataloguing exits. Counting shadows. "Whoever ran this place left in a hurry."

She looked around again. The tables weren't shoved aside in panic; they were stacked with care. Someone had swept before they went. There were cups upside down on their shelf, bowls nested inside each other, chairs balanced seat-to-seat in a tower like sleeping birds.

"They left in an orderly hurry," Petra said. "There's a difference."

He glanced at her then, and something moved across his face that wasn't vigilance. It might have been the beginning of a smile. "You'd know. You're the most orderly person I've ever fled for my life with."

"I'm not orderly. I'm careful. There's a difference."

"There it is again." And this time it *was* a smile, small and crooked, gone almost before it arrived — but Petra felt it land somewhere under her ribs and stay warm.

They had not laughed since the millrace. She realized it as she watched him kneel at the hearth, striking flint, sparks leaping against his knuckles. Since the night a stranger died with her name in his mouth, there had been no laughter between them, only marching and rationing and the low constant hum of being hunted. Even at the ferry, even after the ferryman's terrible price, there had been no laughter. Grief didn't leave room for it. Fear didn't either.

But the fire caught, and the room filled with orange light, and outside the wind changed its mind about killing them, and Petra thought: *one evening*. Just one. The wardens were a day behind them if they were a day behind them at all. The stair road waited tomorrow. Tonight could be nothing but a fire and four walls and two people who were, for a few hours, not running.

* * *

The cellar gave them salt pork gone white with age, a jar of pickled onions still sealed, and — buried under sacking like treasure — half a wheel of hard cheese that smelled sharp enough to make her eyes water and tasted, once you committed to it, like the best thing she had ever eaten.

"This is theft," Ilian said, cutting slices with his belt knife.

"It's salvage. There's a difference."

"You keep saying that."

"I keep meaning it." She took the plate from him and carried it to the table nearest the fire, dragging two chairs down from the tower. They sat across from each other, knees almost touching, and ate like wolves pretending to be civilized.

Halfway through the cheese, Ilian said, "Tell me something true."

It was such an abrupt request that she stopped chewing. "What?"

"A game my brother—" He paused. She watched him hit the wall where his brother used to be. She had watched him hit it every few hours since the ferry: reaching for a memory and finding smooth blankness, like a tongue probing a missing tooth. He recovered the way he always recovered, by going flat and practical. "A game we played. When one of us was lying about something. You ask for something true, and the other has to give it. No strategy. No useful information. Just — true."

"What counts as useful?"

"Anything a warden would want."

Petra considered. The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs a shutter knocked against its frame in a rhythm almost like speech.

"All right," she said. "Something true. When I was nine, I swallowed a whole vial of widow's-bane to prove to my mother I could, and she beat me for it — not because I'd nearly died, but because I'd wasted good poison. That's true."

Ilian winced. "That's a grim kind of true."

"You asked for true, not pleasant. There's a difference."

"That's the third time. I'm starting to think it's your whole philosophy."

"It is my whole philosophy." She pointed the knife at him. "Your turn. Something true."

He turned his cup in his hands. For a long moment she thought he wouldn't answer, and she braced herself for another wall, another flat recovery. But then he said, quietly:

"I don't remember my brother's face anymore."

The words came out level, which somehow made them worse. "I know I had one. I know his name was Corvan and he was older and he swore oaths too easily and he died carrying a map he made me promise to burn. I know all of that the way you know a story someone told you. But his face—" His jaw worked. "The ferryman took one memory. One. And it turns out a face isn't one memory. It's a hundred. It's every time you ever looked at him, and he took the thread they were all strung on, and now when I reach for Corvan I get a name and a shape and nothing else. Nothing else, Petra. I paid for a river crossing with my own brother and I can't even—"

He stopped. Set the cup down with exaggerated care, the way you do when your hands want to do something else entirely.

"That's true," he finished. "That's mine. Your turn."

But Petra found she couldn't play anymore. Her throat had closed around everything she might have said — and underneath the grief for him was a colder thing, a thing she had been carrying since chapter upon chapter of map and miles, since the ruined library where the old books told her the door's price: *an heir's willing blood, and a memory given freely.*

She knew what she would have to pay. She had known for days. And she had not told him, because telling him would make it real, and because — she admitted this to herself now, sitting in firelight with a man who had just handed her his worst wound like a coin — because some small cowardly part of her hoped the door might change its rules before she reached it.

"Ilian," she started.

"Don't." He said it gently. "Whatever it is — I can see it weighing on you, and I'm not asking. Not tonight. Tonight we're just two people eating stolen cheese in a haunted inn, and tomorrow we go back to being dead men walking. Let tonight be tonight."

*Let tonight be tonight.* As if he'd read the exact shape of her wanting and handed it back to her wrapped in kindness. She looked at him across the little table — this sworn companion of a stranger, this man who had spent weeks deciding whether to kill her and had instead taught her card games on the salt flats and given away his brother's face for a river — and felt something take root in her that had no business taking root in a woman carrying a death sentence in her blood.

"All right," she said. "Tonight is tonight."

* * *

Afterward they explored, because neither of them could sit still for long. Upstairs: six bedrooms with beds stripped bare, except one, where the blankets were still rumpled and a child's wooden horse lay on its side on the floor, mid-gallop forever. Petra picked it up and set it upright facing the window, and couldn't have said why, only that it seemed rude to leave it fallen.

In the landlord's study they found a ledger, and Ilian read the last entry aloud by candle stub: *Snow early this year. Guests thin. Marta says the valley roads will close by month's end. We'll go south to her sister's while the pass holds. Leave the key under the loose stone for whoever comes next.*

"Whoever comes next," Petra repeated.

"Us." Ilian crouched, worked a floorstone loose with his knife, and came up holding an iron key green with age. He weighed it in his palm. "Well. We're legitimate squatters now."

And Petra laughed.

It startled her out of her own mouth — a real laugh, unguarded, the first since the millrace, maybe the first in years if she was honest, because tasters don't laugh much and orphans laugh less. It bounced off the study walls and came back sounding younger than she was. Ilian stared at her as if she'd done magic, and then he was laughing too, both of them helplessly, stupidly, over an iron key and a dead landlord's hospitality, laughing until she had to hold the desk, until the sound wore itself out and left them breathless and grinning in the candlelight like conspirators.

"We should sleep," he said finally, in a voice that meant the opposite of nothing at all.

"In shifts," she agreed, in a voice that agreed to nothing of the kind.

* * *

They took the room with the hearth. He built the fire up; she spread the one intact blanket and both their cloaks. Then the geometry problem presented itself: one bed, two people, and a whole evening of almosts stretching ahead of them.

Ilian solved it by lying down on top of the covers, fully clothed, arms crossed, staring at the ceiling like a corpse at his own wake. "I'll take first watch."

"The watch is watching the door, not the ceiling."

"I'm watching the ceiling *and* the door. I'm very talented."

Petra sat on the edge of the bed. The firelight moved on his face, on the scar through his eyebrow she'd never asked about, on the hollows the road had carved under his eyes. She thought about the ledger downstairs. *Leave the key for whoever comes next.* People had lived here. People had eaten at those tables and argued over that cheese and lain in this bed, and then one day they'd folded their chairs and gone south, and the snow had kept their rooms like pressed flowers.

Everything ended. Everything got left behind. Which was exactly why—

"Ilian."

"Mm."

"Look at me."

He turned his head. And whatever he saw in her face made him sit up slowly, the vigilance draining out of him by degrees, until he was just a tired man looking at a woman in firelight.

She reached out and touched his jaw. Rough with three days of beard. Warm. Real. Her hand was steady, which surprised her; taster's hands, trained never to shake, never to spill, never to give the game away.

"If I tell you something true," she said, "right now, no game attached — will you let me stop halfway if I need to?"

"Yes," he said.

So she kissed him.

It was not graceful. Their noses bumped and his hand came up too fast and knocked against her shoulder, and none of it mattered, because the second it settled it was like setting down a weight she'd forgotten she carried. He kissed her back carefully at first, then less carefully, his fingers sliding into her hair, and Petra thought: *oh.* All those songs in the valley about Vessia and its wonders, and not one of them mentioned this — that a person could be a country too, and you could cross into them, and feel the boundary stones fall away.

When they broke apart, they stayed close, foreheads touching, breathing the same air.

"That was true," she whispered.

"I noticed." His voice was rough. His thumb traced her cheekbone like he was memorizing it, and something in her chest twisted, because that was exactly the wrong thing for him to be doing, exactly the thing she couldn't allow, and she opened her mouth to say so —

*I have to pay a memory. Freely given. And I don't know yet whose memory the door will choose to take, or whether I get to choose, and if it takes you — if it takes every memory anyone holds of me —*

"—Petra?" he said softly. "You went somewhere."

She closed her mouth. Opened it. The confession sat right there behind her teeth, one push from freedom.

Tomorrow, she thought. Tomorrow on the stair road, when it couldn't be unsaid but also couldn't ruin this. Tomorrow, when duty put its hand back on her shoulder. *Let tonight be tonight.*

"Ask me something true instead," she said.

He studied her for a long moment. He was not a fool; she could see him cataloguing the retreat, filing it away. But he let it go, the way he'd been letting things go since the millrace, and pulled her down beside him instead.

"What's your favorite color," he said, "and don't say you don't have one, everyone has one."

"Green," she said into his collarbone. "Like the valley in thaw. Like the world supposedly is, outside winter."

"Mine's blue. Like the ferryman's bottles." A pause. "That's a joke. Mostly."

She laughed again — quieter this time, private, a laugh that belonged only to this room — and they lay tangled together while the fire burned down, talking in true things: her first taste of honey after a fever, his terror of deep water, the song her mother used to hum that Petra could still sing note for note and had never told a living soul. Small treasures, laid out one by one, like coins counted in the dark.

Outside, the snow fell soft and endless. Somewhere far to the north, the Spire drank its aurora. Somewhere behind them, wardens moved through the dark with their ledgers and their hounds. But here, in a dead inn with a key under a loose stone, two people who were not allowed futures held onto one evening with both hands.

Petra fell asleep last. In the grey hour before dawn she woke once and found Ilian watching her, firelight in his eyes, an expression on his face she'd never seen him wear — open, undefended, undone.

"What?" she murmured.

"Nothing," he said. "Just learning your face. In case."

*In case.* The words slid into her like a splinter of ice, and she was still turning them over, still trying to understand what he'd meant — when the shutter below banged hard, twice, three times, and Ilian was on his feet with his knife drawn, and from somewhere down the valley road came a sound that did not belong to any weather:

Hounds.
