## 13. Ambush on the Stair Road

The stair road began where the salt ended, cut into the mountain's flank like a scar that had never healed. Two thousand steps, the smugglers said, carved by masons of old Vessia so that pilgrims could climb to the Spire and beg it for mercy. Nobody begged anymore. The Spire didn't take requests. It took payment.

Ilian counted steps because counting kept his hands from shaking.

Behind him, Petra climbed with her hood up and her breath steaming in short white bursts. She hadn't spoken since the ferry. Since she'd refused to pay the ferryman's price and watched Ilian hand over a memory of his brother — watched his own face go briefly blank as the ferryman drew it out of him like a splinter. He remembered that Vess had existed. He remembered loving him. He could no longer remember what Vess's laugh sounded like, or whether he'd been tall, or which of them had been afraid of dogs.

He'd traded a piece of his brother to cross a frozen river, and the worst part — the part that sat in his chest like swallowed ice — was that he couldn't even grieve properly. Grief needed details. The Spire's toll had taken the details and left only the shape of the hole.

"You're limping," Petra said, catching up to him on a landing where the mountain opened westward and the whole grey world fell away below them.

"I'm conserving."

"You're limping *and* lying." Her eyes were sharp over the edge of her hood. Taster's eyes. Eyes trained to notice what was wrong with a thing before anyone else did. "How bad?"

"Bad enough that if we're attacked, you run and don't argue."

"We've discussed this."

"We have. My answer hasn't changed."

"Neither has mine." She said it lightly, but her hand found his sleeve and held it for three steps before letting go, and Ilian understood that this was how they'd learned to say things too large for words: in touches, in small stubborn repetitions, in the way she always walked on his left side now, between him and the drop.

Ahead, the smugglers moved like the professionals they were. There were six left of the nine who'd sheltered them in chapter five's caravan — no, he corrected himself grimly, who'd sheltered them at the winter camp. Sefa led them, a broad woman with frost-scars on her knuckles and a voice like gravel in honey. Her brother Doss walked point. Behind them came the twins, Yara and Mott, and old Hesk with his bottled memories clinking softly in their padded crates, each one a life someone had sold or stolen, each one worth more than the mountain they were climbing.

They were being paid, Sefa had said, in memories themselves — a year of Ilian's, two of Petra's, drawn out painless at journey's end. A fortune by smuggler reckoning. Ilian had wondered then, privately, why the price seemed low. Now, watching Sefa keep checking the sky, watching Doss walk with his axe already loose in its loop, he understood.

They all knew something was coming up this road.

* * *

The ambush came at step eleven hundred, where the stair narrowed through a cleft in the rock and the switchback above hung directly overhead, close enough that a man could drop a stone onto your head.

Ilian felt it before he saw it — a wrongness in the air, the same held-breath quality the valley had, except inverted, as though the mountain itself were listening. Then Hesk stopped walking, and Hesk had survived forty years in the memory trade, and men like Hesk didn't stop without reason.

"Down," Hesk breathed.

The first bolt took the twin Mott through the throat.

It happened fast after that, the way violence always does — too fast for the mind, slow enough for every detail to brand itself permanently into memory Ilian would later wish he could sell. Wardens rose from the rocks above in their grey lacquered armor, faceless behind mirrored visors, crossbows braced on the switchback rail. Six bolts. Eight. Yara screamed her brother's name and went over the outer edge rather than stand still, and Ilian heard her fall for a long time, longer than seemed possible, the sound thinning until the wind ate it.

"Scatter!" Sefa roared, and hurled something — not a weapon, one of Hesk's bottles, a whole memory shattered against the rock above them, and for one surreal moment the wardens staggered as a dead woman's wedding flooded the cleft: music, candlelight, a bride laughing, ghost-light spilling across the stones. The smugglers' contraband, spent like a grenade. Somewhere in that golden wash Ilian grabbed Petra's wrist and hauled her against the inner wall, into the lee of a boulder older than kingdoms.

"They're not here for us," Petra gasped. "Look—"

She was right. The wardens weren't firing at random. They were converging — two dropping down from above, three advancing from below, and every visor turned toward the space where Ilian and Petra crouched. Toward *her*. The smugglers were in the way, nothing more. Obstacles to be cleared.

Sefa understood it too. Ilian saw the understanding land on her face like a blow, saw her look back once at the two of them pressed into the rock — the cargo she'd contracted to carry, suddenly revealed as the shipment everyone wanted.

"Well," Sefa said, and laughed, a short ugly bark. "Should've charged more."

Then she went up the stairs at the wardens with her axe singing, and Doss followed her, and old Hesk — gentle Hesk, who'd shown Petra how to hold a bottled memory without bruising it — pulled the stopper on his entire crate.

Ilian would carry that image for the rest of his life, however long the Spire allowed it to be his. Forty years of harvested lives released at once into the narrow dark of the cleft: a hundred weddings, a hundred childhoods, funerals and harvest festivals and first kisses and last words, all of it detonating together in a storm of other people's light. The wardens reeled. One tore off his visor and stood weeping inside his armor, drowning in a stranger's mother's lullaby. Hesk stood in the middle of it with his arms spread like a conductor, mouth moving — Ilian couldn't hear the words over the roar of borrowed joy — and then a bolt took him, and he folded down among the ruins of everything he'd carried, and looked, absurdly, at peace.

"Go," Sefa snarled at them through the chaos, blood on her teeth, backing up the stairs with her axe held high. "GO, you stupid beautiful children, RUN—"

Petra ran. Ilian ran. That was the arrangement; that had always been the arrangement; he'd made her swear it on the ferry, made her promise that when it broke, she wouldn't try to fix it.

He should have known better than to trust either of them to keep promises.

* * *

They gained four switchbacks before the pursuit caught up — not the main body of wardens but a single one, faster than the rest, moving with the terrible economy of something built for exactly this. Ilian heard him coming the way you hear weather change: a rhythm in the stone, boots finding the same worn hollows the pilgrims had used, centuries of footsteps compressed into one hunter's stride.

At the next landing the stair split — one branch continuing upward toward the pass, one branching east along a ledge toward nothing Ilian could see. The map, burned into his memory along with everything else about Vess, showed the eastern ledge as a dead end. A shrine, maybe. A lookout. It bought time and gave back nothing.

"The ledge," he said.

"That's a dead end."

"It's a *delay*. Take it. When they follow you onto it, double back past me and climb."

"And what will you be doing while I'm doubling back?"

What he'd be doing was standing in the narrows with a smuggler's axe and half a plan, holding the stair against men in lacquered armor. They both knew it. Her taster's hands could slip poison into wine; they could not swing an axe. His could. It was the only arithmetic the moment offered, and it was ugly, and he hated it, and he stepped into the narrows anyway.

"Ilian—"

"*Run.*" He turned and put his back to the mountain and his face to the hunter coming up out of the noise below, and the last thing he saw of her was Petra Villalobos standing on the landing with tears freezing on her cheeks, hating him, choosing to live.

Then the warden arrived, and there was no more room in the world for anything but the fight.

The warden fought like the ledger he served: precise, patient, indifferent. Ilian took a slash across the forearm in the first exchange and barely felt it. He got the warden's crossbow away and threw it over the edge. He drove the man back two steps, three, felt for one bright instant the wild impossible hope that he might actually do this —

Then the second warden came up from below, quiet as snowfall, and the arithmetic changed.

He lasted long enough. That was the only victory available and he took it. Long enough to hear, faintly above, the scrape of Petra's boots going up and over, gone, free. Long enough to put his axe into the first warden's knee joint, the seam in the lacquer, and feel something crack that wasn't stone. Long enough to turn, bleeding, breathing in wet red bubbles, and face the second one with his empty hands raised and his heart doing the strangest thing:

Not fearing. Hoping.

Because captured meant alive, and alive meant the Spire would keep him, and kept things can be found. Let them take him up the black needle. Let them open his head and pour him out jar by jar. Every memory they harvested from him was a memory they weren't taking from her — and somewhere in those jars, someday, someone might find the map of a dying cartographer's brother, and read it, and understand.

The warden's gauntlet closed on his collar. The mountain tilted. Far below, the salt flats gleamed like a spilled sky, and far above, past the clouds, the Spire waited with its mouth open.

"Hold on, Petra," Ilian said, or meant to say. What came out was mostly blood.

The world went grey, then black, then — for a moment, mercifully — it was summer again, and Vess was laughing at something, and Ilian couldn't remember what, and loved him anyway.

* * *

Petra climbed until her legs failed, and then climbed more, because stopping was dying and she had buried enough people today to fill a graveyard.

Six. Six smugglers who owed her nothing, who'd fed her stew and taught her card games and shown her how to hold a stranger's memory gently, dead on a staircase in the mountains because she had a certain kind of blood. Sefa's axe lay somewhere below in the snow. Hesk's crates were scattered shards and drifting light. And Ilian—

She didn't let herself finish the thought. Finishing it would make it true, and she couldn't afford true, not yet, not on this stair, not with the wardens' voices still echoing up the switchbacks behind her.

At the top of the pass the wind hit her like a wall, and beyond it the land fell away into the high country of old Vessia, and far off — impossibly far, impossibly near — the Spire stood against the darkening sky, a black needle drinking the first stars.

They had Ilian. They were taking him there.

Petra stood alone on the roof of the world with a dead woman's wedding still ghosting in her eyes, and made the only vow left to make.

*I'm coming,* she told the Spire. *And you taught me yourself what a taster's patience is worth.*

She pulled her hood up and started down the far side, alone for the first time in her life, toward the country that wasn't and the tower that ate memories and the man she refused — *refused* — to mourn.
