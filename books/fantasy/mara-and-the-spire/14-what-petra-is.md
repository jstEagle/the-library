## 14. What Petra Is

The smugglers' camp had a rule about grief: you were allowed one night of it, and then you carried wood like everyone else. Petra took her night in the lee of a fallen watchtower two hundred paces from the fires, where the wind off the salt flats couldn't find her and no one would see what her face did when it finally broke.

She hadn't cried at the ambush. She hadn't cried watching them drag Ilian up the stair road with a hood over his head, his boots leaving twin furrows in the snow like something being plowed under. She hadn't cried when the last of the smugglers — a girl called Vetch with burn-scarred hands and a laugh like gravel — pressed a satchel into her arms and said, *Go. He bought you the hours; don't waste them weeping.*

But alone, with the dark coming down and the mountain silent in the way that mountains are only silent after violence, Petra sat with her back against cold stone and shook until her teeth chattered.

Then she stopped. Because shaking was a luxury, and she had been raised by a woman who believed luxuries were debts.

*The map,* she thought. *He bought you the hours. Spend them.*

She unrolled it by the light of a stub of tallow she'd found in the tower's ruins, weighting the corners with four stones the size of her fist. The leather was soft as skin now, worn to translucence along its creases from all the times she'd handled it without understanding. She had traced its roads a hundred times since the millrace. She knew the river crossing, the salt flats, the stair road where Ilian had been taken. She knew the door at the end, drawn as a simple arch with a keyhole shaped like nothing she'd ever seen — a spiral, or a folded wing.

What she had never understood was the writing underneath everything. Not the ferryman's script, not the cartographer's neat labels for towns that no longer existed. A third hand, fainter than either, pressed so lightly into the leather that it only appeared when the light raked across it at an angle. She'd noticed it once on the road and told herself it was water damage.

Tonight, holding the candle low and tilting the map toward it, she saw the whole of it at once.

Not water damage. A letter. Hidden the way her grandmother must have hidden everything else — in plain sight, beneath the work of men, legible only to someone patient enough to tilt the world and look again.

Petra read it three times before she let herself believe it.

---

*My daughter's daughter, if you are reading this, then every wall I built has failed, and I am sorry, and I am not sorry, because the walls held for twenty years and that is twenty years the Spire did not have you.*

*You will want to know why your mother never spoke of me. It is because I asked her not to. A name is a hook. The Spire fishes by name. So I cut us out of every record, paid the cartographer to forget he drew this, and sent my daughter east with a new surname and instructions never to look back. She obeyed better than I deserved.*

*You will want to know what you are. You are the blood of Vessia's last queen, which is to say: you are the lock. The door out of this country was made from our line, keyed to our line, and it will open for no other hand. The Spire has spent twenty years searching for an heir because an heir who opens the door ends them, and an heir who gives her blood willingly to the Spire makes them eternal. There is no third option they will offer you. Do not listen for a third option. They are very good at making lies sound like mercy.*

*You will want to know why the valley. Because magic cannot follow you there. I chose it the way one chooses a grave — for its stillness. If you are reading this, the stillness has failed too, and I can only tell you what I told your mother: run toward the door, not away from it. The Spire cannot be outrun. It can only be ended.*

*I loved you before I ever met you. That is the one thing they cannot harvest, because I gave it away freely, and freely-given things do not belong to them.*

*— Sera Villalobos, Queen of Vessia, who was nobody's grandmother but yours*

---

Petra sat for a long time with the letter glowing faintly gold in the candlelight, and the first thing she felt was not wonder. It was anger — hot and total and strangely clarifying, like swallowing the bitter draughts she'd been paid to taste.

Her whole life rearranged itself around the letter's shape.

The poverty. She had always assumed her mother was simply poor, the way the valley was poor, the way everyone scraped. But no — they had been poor *on purpose*, poor the way fugitives are poor, because wealth leaves records and records leave trails. The rented rooms they never furnished. The way her mother flinched whenever a stranger asked where the family came from. The way she'd taught Petra to answer questions slowly, vaguely, to give her name only when law required it and never a syllable more.

The taster's trade. Her mother had pushed her toward it when she was fourteen, had paid the guild fee out of money they didn't have, and Petra had always thought it was pragmatism — a steady wage for a girl with no dowry. But a taster learned poisons. A taster learned what killed and how fast and what countered it. A taster's body grew accustomed to small doses of everything, and somewhere in the valley there had been a woman who knew that her daughter might one day need a body that could survive what the world fed it.

Even the valley itself. Even the flat grey air that had always felt to Petra like held breath. Her grandmother had chosen it *because* it was suffocating. Stillness wasn't a flaw in the place. It was the entire design.

"I was hidden," Petra said aloud, just to hear it in her own voice. "Like a coin sewn into a hem."

The wind moved through the broken tower above her, sighing through arrow slits, and for a moment she let herself feel the other thing underneath the anger — the terrible, tender weight of it. Twenty years. A queen had dismantled her own country's memory of herself, sent her daughter into exile, arranged a life of deliberate smallness, all to keep one child beyond the reach of a black needle on the horizon. Every hardship Petra had ever cursed had been a wall. Every hunger, every humiliation at the magistrate's table, every winter coat patched past patching — someone had chosen those for her, the way you choose armor. Heavy. Ugly. Meant to stop arrows.

And now the armor was failing, because the wardens had found her anyway, and Ilian was paying for it in a harvesting ward somewhere inside the Spire.

Petra wiped her face with the heel of her hand and got practical. Grief was a luxury. Anger was fuel. Her mother had taught her that too, though probably not on purpose.

* * *

She worked through the rest of the map by candlelight while the camp behind her slept.

With the third hand revealed, details she'd taken for decoration resolved into instruction. The spiral at the door's heart wasn't ornament; it was a diagram of the lock itself, and beside it, in letters smaller than any others, the price was written out plainly, the way her grandmother wrote everything — no comfort spared, no cruelty added:

*The door asks two things. Blood of the line, given willingly. And a memory, given freely, as toll. The Spire takes memories by force and calls it harvest. The door will accept only what is offered. This is the difference between them, and it is the only difference, and you must never forget it.*

*A memory offered freely is not lost. It is spent. Understand the distinction, child. What the Spire steals is gone. What the door receives becomes part of the opening — the door remembers for you. Whatever you give it, you will walk through carrying still, changed into stone and iron and light. Nothing freely given is ever truly gone. It has only changed shape.*

Petra read that paragraph four times. On the fourth reading her eyes snagged on the last line and refused to move.

*Nothing freely given is ever truly gone.*

She thought of Ilian then — not the way she'd been trying not to think of him all evening, in the raw and useless way, but the way a tactician thinks. He had known the rules before she did. He'd grown up beside a brother who carried this map, who died pressing it into her hands. Which meant Ilian had known, since the millrace or near it, what the door would cost whoever opened it. He had marched beside her for weeks knowing that the woman next to him was walking toward a price, and he had never once said *turn back*, and he had never once said *I'll pay it for you* — because he couldn't. No one could. The blood was hers alone.

But the memory. The memory anyone could give, if they loved her enough to volunteer.

"No," she said to the empty tower, and her voice cracked on it. "No, absolutely not."

Because she could see it already, the way she could see a poison working in a magistrate's face before he felt it. Ilian standing at that door in some final hour, stepping forward with that stubborn set to his jaw, offering everything he had of her — the millrace, the inn, the kiss, all of it — so that she wouldn't have to spend a memory of her own. So that she'd walk out of Vessia whole while he walked out hollow.

She would have to get to the door first. Or get to him first. Or find some third option her grandmother hadn't imagined.

*Do not listen for a third option,* the letter warned. *They are very good at making lies sound like mercy.*

"That's for the Spire," Petra muttered. "I'm allowed my own third options."

* * *

Near midnight, Vetch found her. The smuggler came up the slope without a sound, which was a professional habit, and sat down against the fallen stone without asking, which was a personal one. She looked at the map, the candle, the tear tracks Petra hadn't managed to fully hide.

"Found something?"

"My grandmother," Petra said. It was the first time she'd said the word in her life. It tasted strange, like a spice from a country she'd never visited. "She was the last queen of Vessia."

Vetch absorbed this with the flat calm of a woman who trafficked in bottled dead women's weddings for a living. "That explains the wardens."

"It explains everything." Petra turned the map so the firelight caught the hidden letters. "Look. The whole route — it's not a path to the door. It's a gauntlet. Every stop, every contact, every danger on this road was placed here by her. The smugglers, the ferryman, the library. She built a road out of people she trusted, twenty years ago, and left it waiting."

"And the Spire knows the road too."

"The Spire knows the destination. It's been hunting my bloodline since before I was born." Petra's hands had gone steady, which surprised her. Taster's hands. Hands that did their best work under pressure. "They didn't find me despite my mother's hiding. They found me because the hiding is failing. The boundary of the valley, the wardens crossing it, the hounds — the stillness is thinning. My grandmother's walls are coming down all at once, and the Spire can finally smell what's behind them."

Vetch was quiet a moment. Then: "Your man. The one they took up the stair road."

"He's not my—" Petra started, and stopped, because it was a lie and Vetch would know it and, worse, because saying it out loud cost her something she wasn't willing to pay anymore. "They'll be harvesting him. Emptying him out to power the Spire. Every day he's in there, there's less of him to save."

"So you're going in after him."

It wasn't a question. Petra looked at her — at the burn-scarred hands, the gravel voice, the face of a woman whose whole profession was moving other people's memories through dangerous country — and made the ask she'd been building toward all night.

"You said the smugglers owe him. For the ambush."

"We owe him more than that. He stood in the gap so three of my people could run." Vetch spat, ceremonially, toward the dark. "What do you need?"

"I need to get inside the Spire." Petra heard herself say it and felt the strangest sensation — not fear, exactly, but the click of a lock turning. "They take servants. Laborers for the harvesting wards. Every fortress eats, and someone has to carry the food." She flexed her fingers. "And I've spent ten years tasting poison for powerful men who never once looked at my face. I know how to be invisible in a rich house. I know how to stand at a wall with my eyes down and miss nothing. It's the only trade I have."

"It's a good trade," Vetch said. "Terrible life expectancy."

"So I've heard."

The smuggler studied her a long moment, and whatever she saw seemed to settle something. "There's a supply caravan runs the salt road to the Spire's foot every month. Wardens don't inspect labor drafts closely — they figure anyone desperate enough to sign on is no threat." A grim half-smile. "They're usually right."

"When does it leave?"

"Two days. There's a woman in the caravan, Osla, who owes me. She can get you a place in the draft, a name that isn't yours, papers that won't survive scrutiny but won't face any." Vetch paused. "You understand what you're walking into. Those corridors are lined with jars. Names in jars. People go in and come out smiling at nothing."

"I've seen it."

"And your man may not know you when you find him. Half-emptied, you said. The early harvest takes the edges first — recent things, sharp things. Faces. Names." Vetch said it gently, which somehow made it worse. "He might look at you like a stranger."

Petra looked down at the map, at her grandmother's hidden hand, at the line she'd read a dozen times tonight: *freely-given things do not belong to them.*

"Then I'll introduce myself," she said. "Again. As many times as it takes."

Vetch snorted softly. "Queens," she said, half to herself, and got up to go.

"Vetch." Petra caught her sleeve. "One more thing. When I'm inside — if I don't come out — the map goes back to the smugglers' roads. Someone keeps it. Someone remembers there was a door."

"Nobody will remember you," Vetch said quietly. "That's how it works in there. You'll be a jar on a shelf."

"Then put the map somewhere a stranger will find it," Petra said. "Strangers are the only ones who ever find anything."

* * *

Alone again, she rolled the map closed and held it against her chest, and for the first time since the millrace she prayed — not to any god the valley permitted, which was none, but to her grandmother, wherever queens went when their countries ended.

*You hid me well. It wasn't enough. So I'm done being hidden.*

She thought of the letter's last line, the one written in a hand gone suddenly less steady, as if the queen had paused there a long time before pressing quill to leather:

*Whatever they told you you were — a poor man's daughter, a taster, a nobody from a nowhere valley — they were wrong, and so, I suspect, was I. You are not what we made you. You are what you choose when the choosing gets hard. Choose well, child. The door is waiting, and so is the morning.*

Petra banked the candle, shouldered the satchel, and looked east, where beyond the black shoulder of the mountain the Spire stood drinking the aurora, a needle of dark threaded with stolen light.

Two days to the caravan. Then the longest walk of her life, straight into the throat of the thing that had eaten a kingdom.

She went down the slope toward the fires to learn what a labor draft looked like on paper, and behind her the wind filled the ruined tower with a sound almost like a woman's voice, saying a name.

Hers.
