## 15. Inside the Spire

The Spire took servants the way a leech takes blood: without asking, and without ever quite letting go.

Petra learned this on her third day in the queue outside the service gate, standing among a hundred hollow-eyed souls who had come, like her, to trade their labor for the Spire's gruel and its terrible shelter. The gate wardens moved down the line with a ledger and a long silver needle, pricking each thumb, reading something in the bead of blood that rose. Most they waved through. A few they turned away with a word — *spent*, or *marked*, or nothing at all.

When the needle touched Petra's thumb, the warden frowned at it. Frowned at her blood as if it had said something rude.

"Name?"

"Marren," she said. "I was a taster. In the valley country."

The frown deepened. "Taster."

"Poison-taster. For magistrates." She let her shoulders slump into the shape of a woman who had survived too many winters by being useful. "I can tell you what's in your lord's wine before he swallows it. I can tell you which of his cooks hates him."

The needle came back to her thumb, hovered, withdrew. Whatever her blood said to the silver, the warden didn't share it. He wrote *Marren* in the ledger, and the ink seemed to sink into the page rather than sit upon it, and Petra understood that she had just been written into a book that ate names.

"East stair. Third landing. The kitchens will find you work."

She climbed.

* * *

The corridors above the third landing were lined with jars.

She had expected cold. She had expected the hush of stone and the drone of wardens' boots. She had not expected the jars — thousands of them, floor to ceiling, wall to wall, receding down every hallway like the ribs of some enormous swallowed animal. Each held a curl of pale light suspended in dark glass, turning slowly, the way smoke turns, the way milk turns when it's about to spoil.

Each jar had a label. Not a name — she looked for names first, because after the ledger she would never stop looking for names — but a date, and a place, and a few words in a clerk's cramped hand. *First snow, the orchard road.* *Her hands, kneading bread.* *The dog, before.*

The lights inside them were memories. She knew it the way you know a thing you've always known and only now been told: instantly, completely, with a sick drop beneath the breastbone. And because she was who she was — because her blood read blank to silver needles and her mind kept its doors shut against everything — the jars did not reach for her. They reached for everyone else. She watched a scullery boy pass a shelf and flinch, one hand rising toward his own temple as though something had plucked a hair from his scalp. He didn't even notice he'd done it. It was simply the tax of walking these halls: the Spire sipping from everyone, all the time, a vast slow drinking that no one thought to call theft because no one could remember anything better.

Petra walked with her eyes forward and her hands folded and her heart going like a trapped bird.

The kitchens found her work. Of course they did; tasters were rare, and the Spire's warden-lord was famously careful about his plate. By the second evening she stood behind his chair in a high cold hall, tasting from a silver spoon while he ate without looking at her, and by the fourth evening she had learned three things.

One: the warden-lord drank a tea of bitterleaf and ironroot that dulled his own memory-drinkers' reach — he trusted no one, not even his jars.

Two: the harvesting wards were below, not above. Down past the kitchens, down past the cisterns, down where the air went warm and wrong, like breath on the back of your neck.

Three: Ilian was alive. She knew it the way she knew the poison in wine — not by proof but by the body's own chemistry, the way hope sat bitter and electric under her tongue whenever a servant mentioned the prisoner taken on the Stair Road. *The one who fought. The one they're emptying slow, because he keeps filling back up.*

He keeps filling back up. She carried those words down through her days like a coal cupped in both hands.

* * *

On the sixth night she went down.

Not bravely — she wanted to be brave, but what she actually felt was closer to nausea, a climbing dread that made her glad of the slop-bucket she carried as her excuse. Servants went down to the cisterns. Servants were invisible. That was the whole architecture of her plan, if a plan could be built out of being overlooked.

The stairs wound past the last lit lamp and into a warmth that had no source. The walls here sweated. The jars down here were bigger, and older, and their labels were written in a different hand — an elegant, patient script that Petra recognized with a lurch before she could stop herself from recognizing it.

Her mother's handwriting.

No. Not her mother's. Her mother's mother's. The queen's hand, the one from the map's margins, the looping Vessian letters that had named the door. These oldest jars had been labeled by a queen, which meant the Spire had been drinking since before the fall, which meant—

Later. She put it away. Later, when there was air to breathe.

The harvesting wards opened off a gallery where the ceiling arched like the inside of a bell. Rows of chairs, iron-framed, reclined like barber's chairs, and in them lay people. Some slept. Some did not sleep but only stared, their eyes open and tracking nothing, their mouths slightly slack, wearing the expression of someone listening to a beautiful story told by someone very far away. Above each chair hung a single jar, unlabeled, filling by degrees with light.

And in the chair nearest the door, half-lit by the glow of his own draining memory, was Ilian.

She nearly said his name. She caught it behind her teeth so hard she tasted blood.

They had emptied him partway. She could see it the way you see a candle burned below its halfway mark — something missing from him, some structural absence, a man with rooms knocked out of his interior. His face was thinner than it had been on the salt flats. There was a bruise along his jaw gone greenish-yellow with age, six days old, seven. His hands lay open on the chair's arms, palms up, the hands of a man who had stopped fighting, and that frightened her more than wounds would have. Ilian's hands had never once been still in all the miles she'd known him. They had always been moving — checking a blade, tracing a map, finding her shoulder in the dark.

Now they were quiet. Now they were empty.

A warden sat at a desk near the far wall, scratching entries into a ledger, not even watching the chairs. The harvest ran itself. The Spire had built a machine so patient it needed no cruelty in the room.

Petra set down the slop-bucket, knelt, and began scrubbing a stain from the flagstones that may or may not have existed. Scrubbing was invisibility. Scrubbing was a servant's cloak of mist. She worked her way across the floor by inches, one kneeling shuffle at a time, close enough now to hear the sound the chair made — a low hum, almost gentle, the sound of a lullaby played backward.

Ilian's eyes moved. Found her. Did not know her.

That was the moment her chest actually broke — not loudly, not visibly, but somewhere in her a load-bearing wall gave way. Because he looked at her the way he'd look at weather. Mild attention. Nothing more. Six days ago on the Stair Road he had turned back for her with arrows in the air, and now she knelt two feet from him scrubbing stones and he saw a woman cleaning a floor.

"Water," he said. His voice was gravel and rust. "Please."

The warden at the desk didn't look up. Prisoners were allowed water; it kept the harvest sweet, or so the theory went. Petra fetched the tin cup from the stand by the desk, dipped it at the cistern tap, and carried it to him with a servant's downcast face and a traitor's hammering heart.

He drank. She held the cup steady. Their fingers did not touch, because she couldn't risk it, because if she touched him she would shatter right there on the flagstones among the jars.

"You're new," he said. Conversational. Empty. "I don't—" A small frown crossed his face, the ghost of a search that found nothing. "I feel like I should know you."

The words went into her like a knife going into bread. Clean, and then all at once not clean at all.

"Do you?" she managed.

"No." He shook his head slowly, and then, with a flicker of the old stubbornness, the old dry humor rising out of the ruin like grass through ash: "But you scrub floors like my brother argued. Like you're trying to win."

She laughed. It came out broken, half sob, and she disguised it as a cough, and the warden at the desk glanced over, and Petra bent lower over her bucket until the moment passed.

"I'll come back," she whispered, so low it was barely breath, barely sound, a message sent across a distance wider than the salt flats. "Do you hear me? I'm coming back for you."

Ilian looked at her for a long moment. Something moved behind his eyes — not memory, not quite, but the shape where memory had been, the negative space of him, the groove worn deep enough that even emptied, even drained, some part of him leaned toward her the way a plant leans toward light it cannot name.

"I believe you," he said slowly. Wonderingly, as if the belief had arrived from somewhere outside himself. "I don't know why. But I believe you."

Above his chair, the jar filled another degree. The hum of the machine went on, patient as winter.

Petra rose, took up her bucket, and walked out of the harvesting wards with her spine straight and her vision blurred and her mind already moving — past the cisterns, past the kitchens, up through the jar-lined halls to the high cold room where the warden-lord took his supper, where a taster with a silver spoon and a blood that read blank to silver needles was invited, nightly, to stand one step behind his chair.

She had spent her whole life swallowing poison meant for other men.

It was time, she thought, climbing, to serve some of her own.
