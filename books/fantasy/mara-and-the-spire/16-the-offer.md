## 16. The Offer

They kept Ilian in a room with no corners.

That was the first thing he understood about the Spire: it did not imprison the way ordinary places imprisoned. There were no bars, no locks he could test with his shoulder. The walls curved away from him in every direction, pale as bone, and wherever he looked the surface held a faint sheen, like the inside of a shell. When he pressed his palm against it, it was warm. Warm and faintly pulsing, as if the whole room were an organ and he had been swallowed into its softest chamber.

He had been awake for what felt like two days. Or one. Or none. The light never changed — a sourceless glow that came from everywhere and cast no shadows — and without shadows there was no way to count time, and without time there was no way to hold on to yourself. He understood that this was the point. He sat with his back against the warm wall and recited things to keep the edges on.

His name was Ilian Vess. His brother's name was Corren. Corren had died at the millrace with frost in his beard and a map pressed into a stranger's hands, and Ilian had sworn over his body to burn that map, and then he had looked into Petra Villalobos's eyes and sworn something else instead, something he'd never said aloud but had carried every mile since salt and ash roads and frozen rivers and the ambush on the stair road where the smugglers died buying him thirty seconds and he'd spent those thirty seconds getting captured like a fool.

Her name was Petra. She cut her palm open once, testing her own blood against a boundary stone, and hadn't flinched. She laughed with her whole chest when something was actually funny, which was rare enough to be worth waiting for. She slept on her left side with her knife under her right hand even in safe houses, especially in safe houses.

He recited these things the way other men prayed.

Then the wall opened, and the warden-lord came in, and Ilian stopped being able to pray.

* * *

The man who entered wore no armor. That was somehow worse than armor. He was tall and spare, dressed in grey so fine it moved like water, and his face was the kind of face you forgot while looking at it — pleasant, unlined, arranged. Only his eyes stayed with you: pale grey, almost colorless, with a stillness in them like deep water under ice.

Two wardens flanked him, hooded, their faces lost in shadow that didn't behave like shadow should. They took up positions by the wall and became furniture.

"Master Ilian." The warden-lord's voice was mild. Almost kind. "You've been difficult."

"I've been asleep," Ilian said. "On a floor. In a room made of meat."

"A holding cell. Crude, I admit. We usually reserve them for memories too violent to shelve." The man folded himself onto a chair that seemed to rise out of the floor to meet him, and gestured, and a second chair rose for Ilian, who ignored it and stayed standing. "You are wondering about your companion."

Ilian kept his face flat. Twenty years of card games with caravan cheats and smugglers and one very memorable winter with a professional liar named Osk had taught him that the first tell was always interest. "Should I be?"

"She is being spoken with. Not harmed." A pause, delicate as a fingertip. "Yet."

"You people don't harm anyone," Ilian said. "You just empty them out and put them on shelves. My brother told me about the harvesting wards before he died. Half of him told me. The half you'd already taken."

Something moved behind the warden-lord's eyes — not guilt; Ilian would have bet everything he had left that it wasn't guilt. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man hearing an old song.

"Corren," the warden-lord said softly. "Yes. He came through our hands twice. A remarkable mind. He remembered everything we took from him — reconstructed it, invented around the gaps, built whole new rooms where we'd stripped out the furniture. Do you know how rare that is? Most minds collapse when you harvest them. His grew back like grass." He tilted his head. "You have the same stubbornness. I can see it. It's in the jaw."

"My brother is dead because of your wardens."

"My brother is dead because of your brother's choices," the warden-lord said, and there it was, the first crack in the mildness, a hairline fracture of something hot underneath. "We offered him a place here. A real place. He chose the map instead. He chose a dying country and a dead queen's bloodline over—" He stopped. Smoothed himself flat again, water over stone. "But you didn't come here to discuss Corren."

"No," Ilian agreed. "I came here to be emptied onto a shelf. Whenever you're ready."

The warden-lord smiled. It was a good smile. Practiced, warm at the edges, and completely empty in the middle, like the smile of a portrait. "No," he said. "I don't think we'll do that at all. You see, Master Ilian, you're worth more to us whole. Every memory intact. Because in a few hours, you're going to want to remember all of this very clearly."

* * *

They walked.

The corridors of the Spire were lined with jars.

Ilian had imagined this since Corren's death — had built it in his head a hundred times on the long roads, the way a man worries a wound. But imagining was nothing. Nothing prepared him for the sheer *quantity*. The corridor stretched ahead of them, curving gently upward, and both walls were shelved floor to ceiling with glass vessels, thousands upon thousands of them, each one glowing faintly with a light that shifted color as they passed. Amber. Rose. A green like shallow sea water. And inside each jar, if you looked — and he couldn't stop looking — movement. Shapes. A woman dancing. A boy running through wheat. Hands braiding hair. Snow falling on a roof, seen from below, forever.

Names were etched into the glass. Some of the etchings were fresh and bright. Most were old, worn nearly smooth, handled so often they'd been loved down to ghosts of themselves.

"They're not suffering," the warden-lord said, watching him watch. "I want to be clear about that, because your kind always imagines screaming. There is no screaming. A memory doesn't feel anything. It's a recording. The person who lived it is elsewhere — living new days, making new memories, or dead and past all feeling. What we take costs them nothing but a little fog. What we gain—"

He gestured upward, and Ilian followed the gesture, and for the first time truly understood what the Spire was. The corridor wasn't a corridor. It was a vein. The shelves ran up and up and up, spiraling into darkness overhead, and far above, visible through some vast ocular window, the aurora poured down the black needle of the tower like wine into a throat. All those jars. All those lights. Feeding it.

"The kingdom fell," Ilian said quietly, "and you built a larder."

"The kingdom fell," the warden-lord corrected, "because it refused to feed itself. Vessia starved — magic starves without memory, everyone knew that, it's the oldest law we have — and the last queens decided it was more noble to die than to pay. Noble. Yes. And twenty years of winter, and a valley full of children freezing behind their little boundary stones, and a country going hollow from the borders inward." His voice gained heat, and for a moment the pleasant face slipped and Ilian saw what was under it: exhaustion, vast and old, the fatigue of a man who has been carrying a kingdom on his shoulders and has decided the shoulders are justified. "We ended the famine. That's all. We found a way to make the rich soil of other people's lives feed the whole field. Someone was always going to do it. We simply had the courage to be first."

"And Petra?"

The name landed between them, and the warden-lord went very still.

"Ah," he said. "So you'll say it plainly. Good. I prefer plain men; they're easier to buy." He resumed walking, slower now, and the jar-light played across his colorless eyes. "Petra Villalobos. Last daughter of the line of Maren. Granddaughter of the queen who sealed the door and hid the heir beyond magic's reach, in a valley where our hounds go blind and our ledgers show blank pages. Do you know how long we've looked for her? Twenty years. Two decades of searching every refugee, every smuggler, every child born near the boundary stones. And where was she all along?" He laughed — a short, genuine sound, and somehow that was the most frightening thing yet. "Serving us poison to taste. Walking past our informants every market day. Her blood singing so loudly that any warden within ten miles should have heard it, muffled by that cursed valley until the day she stepped outside it."

"You could have taken her at the boundary stones," Ilian said. "Your hounds. Your wardens on the stair road. You had her twice, and both times she walked away. Why?"

"Because taking is not the same as having." The warden-lord stopped before a door of black wood, older than everything around it, carved with spirals. "Blood given willingly opens doors. Blood taken by force opens nothing — the old queens saw to that, the clever, spiteful women. The door out of Vessia will only answer an heir who chooses. And so we cannot take Petra Villalobos, Master Ilian. We can only ask her." He turned, and his face was grave and reasonable and terrible. "And I intend to ask her very well indeed."

* * *

The room beyond the black door was round and small and held a single chair, and in the chair sat a woman.

She was perhaps forty, with dark hair going silver at the temples and ink stains on the fingers of her folded hands, and she was writing — no, she had been writing; she sat now with a pen lifted above a page, caught mid-thought, looking toward the window of whatever room she occupied in whatever year she occupied it. Light fell across her face. She was smiling slightly, the private smile of someone about to write a sentence she likes.

Ilian's breath stopped.

Because he knew that face. Not from life — from a bottle. From a smuggler's caravan on a night of sleet, when Petra had knocked a crate and a vial had rolled to her boot and cracked, and a stranger's wedding had poured out into the cold air around them: candles, fiddles, a bride laughing. And standing at the edge of that stolen scene, in the background, a guest in a blue dress, turning toward the camera of someone's remembering—

"That's her mother," he said. His voice came out wrong. "That's Lira Villalobos. She's in the ledger. Petra saw the handwriting."

"Lira wrote beautifully," the warden-lord said. "One of the finest donors we ever processed. She came to us voluntarily, you know — did Petra mention that? No, of course not; Petra doesn't know. Lira Villalobos walked into a warden station nineteen years ago with her daughter's location locked behind her teeth and her own memories held out in her open hands, and she traded them one by one, year after year, purchase after purchase, to buy the girl another season of silence. Another season of the wardens looking elsewhere." He stepped closer to the seated woman, who did not see him, who would never see anything again except the sentence she was about to write. "This is her final payment. The last of her. She gave it knowing what it bought. Knowing it might not be enough."

"She's alive," Ilian said. "The ledger said donor, not—"

"Oh, she's alive. In a village two hundred miles south, raising chickens, smiling at neighbors, with a hole in her heart the shape of everything she sold. She doesn't remember why she cries at harvest festivals. She doesn't remember a daughter at all." The warden-lord touched the edge of the vision, and it rippled like pond water. "But the memory remembers her. Everything she was, everything she thought, every hour she spent loving that child — it's all in here. Perfect. Whole. Eternal." He looked back at Ilian, and now his eyes were burning, and Ilian understood with a lurch that this man loved something. That was the horror of him. He wasn't a monster in the simple way. He was a believer. "Do you understand what we can do, Master Ilian? With enough stored memory, with the Spire fed for another century, we can pour these vessels back. We can rebuild a mind the way you'd rebuild a burned house, beam by beam, from the original plans. We can give Lira Villalobos back every memory she sold. We can make her *whole*. And not only her. We can restore every harvested soul in this tower. We can raise Vessia from the grave entire — its people re-membered, its fields un-wintered, its queen on her throne."

"At a price."

"Everything has a price. The only question is who pays." The warden-lord spread his hands. "The door wants her blood and a memory freely given, and what does it give back? An open valley. A sealed border. One small country saved, and the rest of the world's magic still starving, and the Spire still hungry, and in fifty years some other desperate architect building some other tower. Petra can be a footnote. Or—" and here his voice dropped, went intimate, went gentle, the voice of a man offering a frightened child his hand on a dark road, "—she can be the queen. Restored Vessia, crowned and living, with the Spire's whole treasury of memory at her command. Her mother made whole and sitting at her right hand. A kingdom that worships the girl who saved it. All she has to do is give her blood — willingly, gladly — to *me*, to the Spire, instead of to a dead queen's door. One vessel, poured into another. The difference between a key and a crown, Master Ilian, is only a question of what you want to unlock."

"And if she says no?"

"Then we are back to taking," the warden-lord said simply, "and taking has never once worked, and I am tired, and the winter is ending, and I would very much rather not." He straightened his grey sleeves. "Which brings me to you."

"To me."

"You love her." It wasn't a question, and Ilian didn't insult them both by denying it. "It's written on you the way her blood is written in her. Which makes you the single most persuasive object in this building. Because here is what Petra will ask herself tonight, alone in her cell, staring at the ceiling: *what happens to Ilian if I refuse?* And here is what I need her to believe." The warden-lord smiled again, and this smile had no warmth in it at all. "Tell her the truth, Master Ilian. Tell her that if she refuses, we will harvest you to the last hour. Every memory of her, every memory of your brother, every kindness anyone ever showed you — poured out, jarred, and fed to the aurora while you watch yourself forget. Tell her that. Mean it. And then, when she gives me her blood to save you — thank her. That's all I ask. Thank her nicely."

The wall opened. The wardens stirred. And Ilian, standing in the vein of a tower built on ten thousand stolen lives, with the ghost of Petra's mother smiling at a page she'd never finish, found that his hands were shaking — and that beneath the shaking, cold and clear as well-water, was the only thought that mattered:

*She won't trade herself for me. She'll try something else. She always tries something else.*

He just had to live long enough to find out what.

The warden-lord paused at the threshold, glancing back, almost as an afterthought.

"One more thing. The map your brother died carrying — the original. We have it. We've had it since the stair road." He let that settle, watching Ilian's face with clinical interest. "Which means Petra's copy is the only one in the world, and she trusts it, and she shouldn't. Ask yourself, Master Ilian, why a man who wanted the door closed would draw such careful maps to it. Ask yourself whose hand guided Corren's." The smile, one last time, brief and terrible. "Sleep well. Tomorrow, you'll meet her again — and everything either of you says will be a move on a board neither of you can see the edges of."

The wall closed behind him like water closing over a stone.

In the silence, in the shell-warm dark, Ilian slid down against the pulsing wall and began, in a whisper, to recite.

Her name was Petra. She cut her palm open once and didn't flinch. She laughed with her whole chest. She slept with her knife under her right hand.

And somewhere above him, the aurora drank, and drank, and drank.
