## 17. The Long Winter Falls

The warden-lord's offer hung in the air between them like smoke that refused to rise.

Petra stood in the heart of the Spire's offering chamber, where the walls were veined with pale light and the floor hummed beneath her boots like a struck bell that never finished ringing. Before her, the warden-lord unfolded his vision again — her mother, alive, laughing, turning toward her with flour on her hands and a question on her lips. *Petra, love, where have you been?*

It was beautiful. It was a lie stitched from stolen hours.

"No," Petra said.

The word came out small. She made herself say it again, louder, the way she'd learned to speak over the roar of the millrace. "No. You don't get to sell me my own mother back to me. She gave everything so I could stand here and say no."

The vision flickered. The warden-lord's face did not change — it was a smooth, patient thing, like ice over deep water — but the light in the chamber dimmed by a degree, as if the Spire itself had exhaled.

"You would refuse restoration," he said. "A kingdom. A mother. A name written in every book of record from here to the salt flats."

"I'd refuse *you*." Petra lifted her chin. "You eat memory because you can't make anything of your own. You've had twenty years to find my bloodline and all you've built is a bigger appetite." She thought of the ledger, her mother's handwriting marching down its columns like soldiers going to their deaths. "My grandmother hid me beyond magic's reach. My mother paid for it in pieces of herself. And you think I'll hand you the last of it because you dressed it up in her face?"

The warden-lord studied her for a long moment. Somewhere above them, through floors and floors of stone, the Spire drank the aurora with a sound like slow swallowing.

"The valley will seal," he said at last. "The boundary stones will close within three days. Whatever door stands at my root will be buried under winter until spring — and I will have grown strong enough by then to dig it out myself. You are not saving anyone, taster. You are only choosing when you lose."

"Then I choose now," Petra said, "while I still remember why."

She moved before he could answer — not toward him, never toward him — but toward the narrow stair she'd memorized during her days carrying trays through these corridors, the servants' passage down into the harvesting wards. The warden-lord did not shout. That was the worst part. He simply watched her go, and the watching felt like a hand laid flat against her spine.

The wards were colder than the rest of the Spire, cold enough that her breath hung in the air like small ghosts. Rows of alcoves stretched away into the dark, each one cradling a body kept in the gray half-sleep of the harvested. She found Ilian in the seventh row, exactly where she'd left him two nights ago when she'd bribed, flattered, and finally threatened her way past the ward-keeper.

He was worse. His skin had gone the color of old candle wax, and when she touched his shoulder his eyes opened slowly, searching her face with an effort that seemed almost physical.

"They took more," he rasped.

"I know." Her voice cracked and she hated it. "I know. Can you walk?"

"I can walk." He tried to prove it and failed, listing sideways until she caught him under the arm. He weighed nothing. That frightened her more than the wardens had. "Did you — the offer—"

"I said no."

Something moved behind his hollowed eyes. Relief, maybe. Or grief for what the refusal would cost them both. "Of course you did," he murmured. "Stubborn valley girl."

"Taster," she corrected. "Get it right if you're going to die on my behalf."

They went up through the servants' stairs, past jars of names stacked to the ceiling — she made herself look at none of them — and out onto a landing where the mountain wind hit them like a wall. Below, the world was already whitening. The first of the final snows came down in fat, deliberate flakes, each one landing with the weight of a decision.

"There," Ilian breathed.

She followed his gaze down the mountainside to where the Spire's root met the earth: a black bulge of ancient stone half-buried in the slope, and set into it, faintly glowing even at this distance, a seam shaped exactly like a door.

Between them and it stood the wall of names.

* * *

They descended through snow that thickened as they went, until the world shrank to a circle ten paces wide. Petra kept Ilian upright by main force, his arm across her shoulders, his breath coming in shallow pulls. Twice they heard horns on the ridge above — the wardens mustering — and twice they pressed themselves into the lee of fallen stones and waited for the sound to pass.

"They'll seal the boundary first," Ilian said against her ear. "Cut off your retreat. Then they'll come for us at the door. They won't risk damaging the root, though. They need it intact."

"How comforting."

"It means we fight in a narrow space." He managed something like a smile. "You're good in narrow spaces. You spent your whole life in one."

The wall of names rose out of the white ahead of them sooner than she wanted — a rampart of black stone perhaps man-high, curving in a wide arc before the buried door, and carved edge to edge with writing. As she drew closer Petra saw that the letters moved. Slowly, like insects under bark, the names crawled and rearranged themselves, old ones fading as new ones surfaced. Every person the Spire had ever harvested, she understood. Every memory eaten, recorded here in the only monument its victims would ever get.

Her legs nearly gave out then, and not from the climb.

"Petra." Ilian's grip tightened on her arm. "Look at me. Whatever happens — whatever you see in there — the door needs two things. Your blood, willing. And a memory, given freely. Nothing else will open it. Not force, not trickery. That's what the library said. That's what we came all this way to learn."

"I remember."

"Do you?" His voice was very quiet. "Because I've watched you decide things this whole journey, and you always decide alone. You swallowed poison alone. You crossed the boundary alone in your heart long before your feet did. So I'm telling you now, before the fighting starts, before either of us can be clever about it—" He stopped. Started again. "Whatever the price is, we pay it together or not at all."

She looked at him — this hollowed-out stranger who had once been full of stories about his brother and the sea roads, who had given up memories to a ferryman without blinking, who had held off three wardens on the stair road so a girl he barely trusted could run. Snow gathered in his hair. He looked, suddenly, unbearably mortal.

"Together," she said.

The horns sounded again, closer now. Down the slope, dark shapes began to pick their way through the falling snow — six of them, then eight, warden-blades catching the aurora's light like chips of frozen sky. And at their center walked the warden-lord, unhurried, his smooth face tilted up toward the snowfall as if it were a gift.

Petra drew the knife she'd taken from the ward-keeper's stores and set her back against the wall of names. The stone hummed against her spine. Somewhere inside it, she was certain, her mother's handwriting was crawling among the letters.

"Here we go," she said.

* * *

The first warden reached them fast, too fast for the snow, moving with that wrong fluidity the harvested sometimes kept. Petra met him low, the way she'd been taught to meet a charging boar — knife up, weight forward, letting his momentum do the work. He folded around her blade and slid past her into the drifts, and she was already turning, already looking for the next.

There were always more.

Ilian fought beside her like a man spending coins he didn't have, each movement precise and economical, buying seconds at ruinous cost. She saw him take a warden's blade across the forearm and barely react, saw him drive his elbow into another's throat, saw him fall to one knee and rise again. The snow ran pink around their feet.

"Door!" he shouted. "Go! I'll hold them!"

"Not without—"

"*Go!*"

But she couldn't. That was the truth of it, the plain stupid truth she'd been circling since the millrace: she could not open that door while he bled behind her, and she could not reach it while the wardens came in waves, and the warden-lord was walking toward them through the storm untouched, unhurried, snowflakes melting an inch above his shoulders.

So Petra did the only thing left. She turned to the wall of names, pressed both palms flat against the crawling letters, and shouted into the stone:

"I'm Petra Villalobos! Granddaughter of the last queen of Vessia! These are my people's names and I'm claiming them!"

The wall blazed.

Light poured up through the carved letters like dawn through frost, and every warden within ten paces staggered, clawing at their heads, as the harvested names roared back through them — fragments of stolen lives flooding home to bodies that no longer knew how to hold them. Two dropped their blades entirely. One sat down in the snow and began, horribly, to laugh.

The warden-lord alone did not falter. He looked at the burning wall, and then at Petra, and for the first time his smooth face showed something underneath.

"Clever," he said. "And useless. The door still requires payment, little queen. Blood and memory, freely given. Will you cut yourself for a door that leads nowhere? Will you give a memory you cannot spare?" He spread his arms, and the snow bent around him. "Or will you let me make you an empress instead?"

"I told you," Petra said, drawing the knife across her left palm in one clean line — the taster's cut, quick and certain, the same motion she'd used a hundred times to test a magistrate's wine. "I choose now."

Blood ran down her fingers and dripped onto the threshold stone beneath the wall, and the seam in the black rock began, slowly, to glow.

The warden-lord lunged.

Ilian hit him from the side like a falling tree.

They went down together into the drifts, and Petra screamed — she'd admit that later, screaming, kneeling at the threshold with her bleeding hand pressed to warm stone — because Ilian had no strength left to spend and they both knew it. He lasted four seconds. Five. Long enough for the door's glow to strengthen, long enough for the seam to widen into a crack, long enough for him to look up at her through the snow with an expression she'd seen once before, on a ferry deck, when he'd handed over his brother's memory without hesitation.

Long enough for him to mouth one word.

*Together.*

And then the warden-lord threw him aside like a broken doll and rose, and turned, and smiled at Petra with the patience of winter itself.

"Now," he said softly, "let us discuss what you cannot spare."

Behind him, the crack in the door widened. It needed only one thing more.

Petra looked at Ilian, sprawled and breathing, and understood at last what the payment would have to be — and who would have to give it.

The snow fell harder, sealing the world, sealing the valley, sealing every road but one.
