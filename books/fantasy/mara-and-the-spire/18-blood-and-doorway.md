## 18. Blood and Doorway

*Petra*

The wall of names was not a wall.

She had thought it would be stone. She had imagined the door behind it like a door in any village of old Vessia — oak and iron, a latch worn smooth by generations of hands. But when they came down into the Spire's root, past the last of the harvesting wards with their jars glowing like drowned lanterns, what waited for them was a curtain of light made of words.

Names. Thousands upon thousands of them, drifting in slow suspension, silver script turning over itself like fish under ice. Every name the Spire had ever taken. Every name it had ever eaten. They hung in the air between Petra and the door, and the door was there, all right — a seam in the world, tall as three men, thin as a knife's edge, humming at a pitch she felt in her back teeth.

"It's beautiful," she said, and hated herself for saying it.

"Don't look at it too long." Ilian stood at her shoulder, sword drawn, his breath ragged from the stairs. He had been half-emptied in the wards; she had seen the hollows where memories used to live behind his eyes. But he had walked out on his own feet, and he had not let go of her hand since. "It's how they get you. The Spire doesn't chase. It *invites*."

Behind them, somewhere up through the black throat of the tower, boots were coming down. Many boots. The warden-lord did not run; he descended, which was worse, because it meant he had never once doubted he would arrive.

Petra pressed her palm against the curtain of names.

They parted around her skin like water around a stone. Not resisting. *Recognizing.* And beneath the names, close now, she could see the lock: a rose of iron set into nothing at all, its petals folded shut, waiting for blood that knew what it was.

"Petra." Ilian turned, putting himself between her and the stairwell. "Whatever happens — cut first. Talk never."

"I'm a taster," she said. "Talking is the whole job."

"Then lie beautifully."

The warden-lord came down the last flight like winter arriving in a room.

He was not large. She had expected large. He was narrow and gray and precise, and his coat was stitched with names — actual names, embroidered in thread so fine it might have been hair, running down the sleeves and across the chest until he looked like a man wearing a ledger. His eyes were the color of the aurora the Spire drank: green shot through with dying silver.

"The heir," he said. His voice was soft. It was the softness of a man reading aloud to children. "Twenty years I have kept your name in my mouth like a seed. Do you know how patient one must be, to keep a name that long without tasting it?"

"You could have just asked," Petra said. Her voice held. She was proud of that. "I would have said no, but you could have asked."

"And the valley?" He tilted his head. Behind him, wardens fanned out along the walls, their hooks and blades catching the light of the name-curtain. "Your mother's people. Your grandmother's people. The Stillness keeps them safe the way a tomb keeps a body safe. When the boundary seals this winter — and it will seal, child, the snows have already begun — the valley dies of forgetting. No trade. No letters. No new memory in or out. A country of held breath, suffocating politely. You know this."

"I know why I'm here."

"Yes." He smiled, and it was almost kind, and that was the most frightening thing yet. "You are here to give. That is what heirs do. So give — but give to me. Blood willingly offered to the Spire, and I can hold the door open from this side forever. Vessia restored. Your valley fed and remembered. A queen on a throne that has been empty twenty years, and every soul who ever forgot anything given back their dead." He spread his narrow hands. "I am not asking you to be cruel, Petra Villalobos. I am asking you to be *generous* on a scale you have only just learned to imagine."

And God help her, some part of her — the part that had swallowed poison for coin and walked home hungry past lit windows — leaned toward it like a plant toward sun.

That was the moment Ilian moved.

Not toward the warden-lord. Toward *her*. He stepped in front of her, sword low, and said, quietly, in the voice of a man stating a fact about weather: "She's already decided. You're just late."

* * *

*Ilian*

He had fought men before. He had fought wardens before, on salt flats and stair roads, and he knew exactly how much of him they could break in the time it took a girl to open a door.

What he had not planned for was the ledger-coat.

The warden-lord did not draw a weapon. He drew a *name*. He spoke it — a single word, a man's name, spoken with terrible precision — and the air where the word landed went wrong, and Ilian's left arm simply stopped answering him, as if someone had reached into his past and taken the years he'd spent learning to use it.

"That was a swordsman," the warden-lord said conversationally, walking forward while his wardens closed ranks behind him. "A man I harvested in the border wars. I keep my favorites close. Shall I tell you what else I keep?"

Ilian swung anyway, one-armed, ugly, effective enough to buy a step. Steel met steel further down the line — the wardens coming on now, hooks flashing — and he gave ground the way you give ground in a doorway fight: slowly, sideways, always keeping his body between the blades and the girl at the curtain of names.

He heard her gasp. He heard the wet sound of a palm opening on iron.

Good girl. Cut first. Talk never.

The pain hit him next — not his own. Hers, somehow, carried along the hum of the door, a bright wire of it, and the lock-petals unfolding petal by petal with a sound like a book being opened after a hundred years. Light came off the seam in the world. Real light, warm, the color of the valley in late summer, and the wardens flinched from it like men flinching from dawn.

"No," the warden-lord said, and for the first time his voice lost its softness. "Blood alone opens nothing. Blood is the *key*. It still requires payment. A memory, freely given—" and here he laughed, short and sharp, a man who has caught a flaw in a contract, "—and who among you has a memory to spare? The boy is already half-harvested. The heir—"

"I do," Ilian said.

Everything stopped. Even the fighting, for one heartbeat, even the wardens, even the hum of the door, as if the whole root of the Spire were leaning in to listen.

He looked at Petra. She was looking back at him, her cut hand pressed to the iron rose, her eyes going wide, going *no*, going everywhere a person's eyes go when they understand a thing one second too late to stop it.

Every memory he had of her. The millrace, the frostbitten stranger dying with her name on his lips. The map. The boundary stones and the hounds. The smugglers' fire and the bottled wedding. The ferryman. The inn, the laugh, the kiss, the evening they were only two people. The ambush. The stair road. Her face above him in the harvesting ward, furious and weeping and refusing to leave.

All of it. Freely given. Which meant freely *lost*, and he knew — he had watched his brother come apart jar by jar — he knew exactly what the losing felt like.

"Dorian swore to burn the map," he said to her, because if he was going to do this he wanted her to hear it clean. "I swore to help him. Then he died holding your name like it was worth more than everything, and I read his notes, and I understood. Some doors are worth walking through even if you can't remember why." He smiled. His eyes were burning. "Tell me later. You'll have to tell me everything. Start with the millrace."

"Ilian—"

"Freely," he said, to the door, to the Spire, to the whole listening dark. "Given. All of it. Take it."

* * *

*Petra*

She felt him leave her.

There was no other way to say it. It was not like watching a man walk away; it was like watching a room empty. One moment she was full of him — full of shared winters and salt roads and a kiss in an abandoned inn — and the next moment those rooms in her heart stood open and bare, and the furniture of him was gone, carried off through the seam in the world as payment, as fuel, as the price of a door.

The lock bloomed.

The iron rose unfolded all the way, and the seam became a doorway, and through it she saw — not Vessia, not the ruins, but *through*, down to the roots of things, down to the Stillness and the boundary stones and the falling snow that would seal her valley into a tomb. And the door asked her, without words, the way a river asks a dam: *where?*

"Through," Petra said. "To the valley. Open the winter."

She poured everything through.

Her blood on the iron. Her will behind it. Twenty years of a grandmother's cunning and a mother's handwriting in a warden's ledger and a taster's stubborn, poison-proof life — all of it went through the door in a flood, and on the far side, impossibly, she felt snow *stop*. Felt the boundary stones exhale. Felt the held breath of the Valley of Stillness finally, finally released, magic and memory and spring-air pouring in through a wound in the world that she had opened with her own hand.

The wardens fled from it. The lesser ones simply ran. The ones who stayed — the ones whose coats were stitched with too many names — came apart as the door's light passed over them, unraveling into dust that glittered, dust that *remembered*, each grain a stolen recollection going home to whoever it had been taken from.

The warden-lord did not run.

He stood in the flood of returning memory and let it unmake him stitch by stitch, name by name peeling off his coat and flying home through the light, and the last thing he did, the very last thing, was look at Petra with something that was almost relief and say, "Ah. There it is," as if he had spent twenty years waiting to be forgiven.

Then he was dust, and the dust was gone, and the door stood open between the root of the Spire and everything.

And Ilian stood beside her, sword lowered, looking at her with polite, total strangeness.

"Did we win?" he asked. His voice was careful, the voice of a man testing ice. "I feel like we won. I don't — forgive me. Have we met?"

* * *

*Petra*

The door would not stay open long. She could feel it closing, the way you feel a fever breaking — slowly, and then all at once. Through it, faintly, she could hear the valley: dogs barking, bells, the millrace running loud and free for the first time in living memory. People would find the boundary stones cold and ordinary, mere rocks. People would never know what had sealed around them all their lives, or what it had cost to open.

No one would know. That had been the price of her blood, written into her before she was born. The door saved everyone who passed its light — and took from every mind the one who opened it.

Everyone's. Except, apparently, the man standing in front of her who had already paid everything he had.

"You saved us," she said. Her voice cracked in the middle. "Both of us. The valley, me, the whole — you gave—"

"I did?" He looked at his own hands as if checking. "I feel lighter. Is that strange? Everything feels—" He frowned, searching, and she watched him search, watched him come up empty, watched politeness settle over his face like frost. "I'm sorry. I appear to have arrived in the middle of something."

The door began to close. Beyond it, the Spire groaned, its harvest interrupted, its hunger suddenly starving. Somewhere far above, glass jars were shattering as the memories inside them tore free and flew home through the narrowing seam.

Petra took Ilian's hand. He let her, courteous, puzzled, a stranger being kind.

"My name is Petra," she said.

"Petra," he repeated. Testing it. Finding nothing underneath. "It's — hm. It feels like it should mean something."

"It does," she said. "It will. Come on. We have to go home."

She pulled him through the light as the door shut behind them with a sound like a book closing — and ahead of them, at the top of the long stair, the first true sunrise of the unsealed world was coming up gold over the ruins of Vessia, and neither of them knew the way, and only one of them knew why that didn't matter.

"Home," Ilian said slowly, as if tasting the word for poison. "Where's that?"

Petra looked east, toward the valley, toward the grave and the feast and the empty chair, and began to walk.

"You'll see," she said. "I'll show you everything. Twice, if I have to."
