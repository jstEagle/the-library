## 20. Homecoming, Unmet

The valley took her back the way a river takes back a leaf — without ceremony, without comment, as if she had never left.

Petra came down from the boundary stones at dusk on the last day of winter, and the snow was already going soft at the edges, already forgetting itself into mud. Behind her, far to the north, the sky over where the Spire had stood was clean and empty for the first time in twenty years. No black needle. No aurora drinking downward. Just stars, ordinary stars, doing what stars do.

She had walked three days from the door. Her boots were ruined. Her palms were scarred — one cut across the left, the taster's old habit of testing edges with bare skin, and one deeper mark that no poison had ever made. She smelled of woodsmoke and old wool and someone else's horse. She was, by every measure she could count, victorious.

No one looked up when she passed the first farmhouse.

That was correct, she reminded herself. That was the price. She had known the price before she paid it, had held it in her mouth like a coin before swallowing, and she would not insult her own sacrifice by being surprised at the taste.

Still. When the miller's boy ran past her on the road, chasing a dog, and did not so much as glance sideways — when he should have said *there goes Petra Villalobos, who swallows poison for coin and once threw a magistrate's entire supper out a window* — something in her chest folded up small and stayed folded.

The millrace was running loud with meltwater. She stopped there because she had to, because this was where everything began, because a frostbitten stranger had died on these stones with her name in his mouth and a map against her heart. The wheel turned. The water went white and green. A woman Petra had known all her life — the baker's widow, who used to trade her day-old loaves for taster's gossip — walked by with a basket and nodded the way you nod at any stranger on a road.

*Any stranger.*

"Good evening," the widow said politely.

"Evening," Petra said, and her voice worked, which felt like a small miracle, and then it didn't feel like anything at all.

* * *

Her mother's grave was on the hill behind the meetinghouse, third row, under the crooked birch. Petra climbed to it in the blue hour, when the valley below was lighting its windows one by one and the air smelled like thaw and woodsmoke and the particular sweetness of the season's first open earth.

The stone was still there. Of course it was. Stones don't remember people; they only remember names, and her mother's name had been carved deep enough to outlast even the Spire's hunger.

*MAERWEN VILLALOBOS*, it said. And beneath, in smaller letters: *SHE KEPT WHAT WAS HERS.*

Petra sat down in the wet grass and laughed, once, a sound with nothing in it.

"You hid me," she told the stone. "Twenty years. You let them carve a lie on your grave so I'd stay small and safe and poor and alive." She touched the letters. The granite was cold and patient. "It almost worked."

A queen's daughter. A queen's granddaughter. The last heir of Vessia, raised in a hovel above the tanner's row, taught to hold poison on her tongue and keep her face still while it burned. Every hardship of her childhood rearranged itself in her memory now, clicking into place like the tumblers of a lock: the lessons disguised as games, the insistence that she never speak of dreams, the way her mother had gone white whenever anyone mentioned the north. It hadn't been poverty. It had been armor. It had been love wearing poverty as a disguise.

"I opened it," she said. "The door. You'd have wanted to know that."

The birch moved in the wind. That was all the answer there was, and it was enough, and it wasn't.

She stayed until her legs went numb. Below, the meetinghouse windows glowed gold, and she could hear — faintly, carried up the hill on the softening air — the sound of a feast being prepared. Voices. Laughter. Someone tuning a fiddle.

Of course. The valley would feast tonight. They always marked the winter's turning, but this year the celebration would be wilder than any in living memory, because this year the long winter had *broken*. Twenty years of cold that no one could explain, frost in high summer, the boundary stones weeping ice — and then, three days ago, warmth. Sun. Snowmelt roaring down every channel. The oldest people in the valley had wept and called it providence.

They would feast tonight and call it providence too. They would never know it had a name, and that the name was hers, and that she had paid for their spring with every memory of herself they possessed.

Petra stood, brushed off her skirts, and went down the hill toward the light.

* * *

The meetinghouse hall was full to bursting. Long tables, tallow candles, the good dishes brought out of chests. Roast whatever-they-had-been-able-to-spare, which turned out to be two pigs and an astonishing number of chickens. Barley soup. The dark winter beer. Children underfoot, dogs under the tables, an old man already asleep in the corner with his cup tipped against his chest.

Petra stood in the doorway with snowmelt drying on her boots and watched them.

She knew every face. That was the cruelty of it — the memories hadn't been taken from *her*. She remembered the widow's laugh and the miller's boy's missing tooth and the way the magistrate pursed his lips before tasting anything suspicious. She remembered being seven years old and falling into the millrace and being pulled out half-drowned by a neighbor who had scolded her and then secretly cried. She remembered all of it, alone, completely, a whole valley's worth of shared history preserved in a single head that no one else could enter.

They looked through her the way you look through weather.

"Room by the fire, miss?" offered the reeve's wife, kind and blank. "You've come far, by the look of you. Sit, sit. There's soup yet."

"Thank you," Petra said, and sat, at the end of the farthest table, among strangers who had once been her people.

The feast roared around her. Toasts were drunk — to the spring, to providence, to the ancestors. The fiddle struck up and feet found the boards. Someone retold the story of the great frost of twenty years past, and how the old folk said a curse had come down from the mountains, and everyone agreed the curse had lifted, though none could say why, and they shivered pleasantly at the mystery of it and drank again.

*The curse had a name,* Petra thought, holding her soup bowl in both hands for the warmth. *You're drinking toasts to my grandmother's grief. You're dancing on the grave of a country that died so yours could live.*

She ate. It was good soup. That was somehow the worst part — that everything here continued, warm and unbroken, needing nothing from her now, not even her absence. She had imagined, on the long walk home, some moment of recognition. Not worship. Nothing so vain. Just — one face turning. One old woman squinting across the room and saying, slowly, *do I know you?* One thread of herself left snagged in the world.

But the door had taken it all, cleanly, exactly as promised. Every memory anyone held of Petra Villalobos, gone. The taster's tongue, the magistrate's table, the girl who fell in the millrace — erased so thoroughly that even the shape of the hole had healed over. She was not missed. She was not mourned. She was a stranger with road-worn boots eating soup at the edge of the light.

Her chair — the one that had always been hers at the winter feasts, third bench from the fire, because her mother had claimed it years ago and Petra had inherited the claim like everything else — was occupied. A young family sat there, parents and a baby, laughing at something. The baby banged a spoon. Nobody had saved the seat. Nobody had ever saved the seat, because nobody had ever been saving it *for* anyone.

Petra finished her soup and set down the bowl very carefully, the way her mother had taught her to set down anything that might be the last of its kind.

* * *

Later, when the dancing was at its height and the candles had burned down to stubs, she slipped out the side door into the cold to breathe. The night was clear. Far north, over the ridge, the sky was just sky.

That was where she heard the footsteps behind her — hesitant, wrong-footed, the steps of someone who had started forward twice and stopped twice.

She turned.

He stood in the doorway's spill of candlelight, and her heart did something complicated and painful, because she knew him instantly and he did not know her at all. He was thinner than he had been. There was a new scar along his jaw that she hadn't given him time to earn. His hands hung at his sides like hands that had forgotten what they were for. But his eyes — his eyes were the same, dark and careful and kind, and they were fixed on her with an expression she recognized from mirrors: the look of a person trying to remember something that has been taken from them.

"You," he said. Then he stopped, visibly embarrassed. "Forgive me. I don't— I saw you inside. At the far table. And I couldn't stop looking, and I don't know why, and that's—" He pressed his hand over his face briefly. "I'm not usually this man. I'm sorry."

"It's all right," Petra said. Her voice came out steady. Inside her chest, everything was shaking.

"Ilian," he said, offering his hand, the way you introduce yourself to a stranger. "I came through the pass a few days ago. I don't entirely know why I stayed." He laughed, short and lost. "I woke up three days ago on the north road with a map in my coat I don't remember buying, and half my memories feeling like a house with the furniture gone, and I walked south because my feet seemed to know the way. And then I saw you sitting there, and I thought—" He shook his head. "I don't know what I thought. Forgive me. I'll leave you be."

*Every memory he has of me.* He had stepped forward in the dark beneath the mountain and given it all — willingly, freely, the only payment the door would accept — and now he stood in front of her introducing himself, and the map was still in his coat, and his feet had walked him three days south to the exact valley where she lived, because some part of him deeper than memory had refused to let go.

"Don't," Petra said.

He paused.

"Don't leave me be," she said. "Please. Everyone in there is kind to me and none of them know me, and I have spent three days walking home to people who will feed me and house me and never once say my name right, because they can't, because I paid for their spring with—" She stopped. Breathed. The taster's discipline, the still face. "I'm Petra," she said. "I'm pleased to meet you, Ilian. I think you may be the only person in the world who doesn't know me, and I find that I would rather talk to you than to anyone breathing."

Something moved across his face — not recognition, nothing so generous, but a kind of gravitational pull, the sense of a thing falling toward the place it had always fallen.

"Petra," he repeated, trying the name like a word in a language he'd almost learned once. "Why does that feel like the beginning of something?"

"Because it is," she said.

Above them the last stars of winter wheeled north, and somewhere beyond the ridge, in a country that no longer existed, a door stood open in the roots of a dead spire, and spring was coming down the mountain toward them both — slow, certain, and starting over.
