## 21. The Second First Word

The dreams came every night now, and they were all the same dream, which was strange, because Ilian could not remember ever dreaming the same thing twice.

He stood on a ridge of black stone. Wind came off it like a blade being drawn. Below him a needle rose out of the earth, taller than anything had a right to be, drinking light out of the sky — pulling the aurora down in long green ribbons the way a man drinks from a stream. And there was a woman on the ridge beside him. He never saw her face. He only knew two things about her, the way you know things in dreams without being told: that her name mattered more than any name in the world, and that he was standing next to her because he had chosen to.

He always woke before she turned around.

He woke this morning with his heart going hard and the taste of snow in his mouth, though the window of his rented room showed a valley wrapped in late winter sun, the frost steaming off the fields, the millrace loud and bright below. The Valley of Stillness. He'd been here three weeks. He knew that because the innkeeper's ledger said so, in the innkeeper's careful hand: *Ilian Vess. Arrived at the thaw's edge. Paid through the month.*

He did not know how he'd arrived. He did not know where he'd come from. He did not know why the name *Vess* sat in his chest like a stone dropped down a well — familiar, heavy, and landing somewhere he couldn't see.

The healers in the valley had a word for him. They said it gently, the way people say words to a man they think might break: *hollowed.* Some travelers came through the boundary stones that way, they told him. The crossing took something out of them. Memory, usually. Sometimes more.

"You're lucky," the old herbwoman had said, pressing a cup of willowbark tea into his hands. "Whatever you lost, you lost clean. No gaps, no ghosts. Just an empty road behind you."

She meant it kindly. He had thanked her. He had not told her about the ridge, or the needle, or the woman whose face he couldn't see but whose absence felt like a wound that hadn't decided whether to bleed.

He dressed, went down, ate bread and honey he didn't taste, and walked out into the valley because staying still made the hollow feeling worse.

* * *

He found her at the millrace again.

That was the other thing. He kept finding her.

Petra Villalobos. He knew her name the way everyone in the valley knew it — she was the taster who had swallowed poison for Magistrate Corren last autumn, the girl with no family and no history who had somehow talked her way into the good graces of half the village and the suspicion of the other half. That much was common knowledge, free for the asking.

What wasn't common knowledge was why his feet turned toward her like water finding its level.

Three weeks. Twenty-one mornings. And twenty-one times, some pull under his ribs had walked him past the tannery row, past the shrine with its unlit candle, down to the millrace where she sat on the flat stone with her boots off and her ankles in the freezing water, watching the wheel turn as if it were telling her something.

Today she heard his steps on the gravel and didn't turn around.

"You walk loudly," she said, "for someone who pretends he isn't following me."

"I'm not following you." He sat down on the stone beside her, because apparently he was going to do that too, the way he'd done it every day, and neither of them had ever once remarked on it. "I'm walking by the river."

"The river goes north."

"I like going north."

"The river also goes past my house, my work, and the shrine where you stand every evening for ten minutes looking at nothing."

He opened his mouth. Closed it. She finally turned and looked at him, and there it was again — that look she gave him, half wariness and half something else, something almost like grief, as if she were mourning a man who was sitting right in front of her alive and breathing.

"Why do you look at me like that?" he asked. It came out rougher than he meant.

"Like what?"

"Like I owe you money."

A laugh escaped her — short, startled, quickly smothered, but real. It did something to his chest that he decided not to examine. "You don't owe me money," she said, quieter now. "I don't think you owe anyone anything anymore."

"What does that mean?"

"It means—" She stopped. Pulled her feet out of the water and started drying them on her hem, buying time with small motions. "It means you're new here. New everywhere, maybe. And some people find that sad."

"Do you find it sad?"

She looked at him straight on then, and whatever was in her face she let him see all of it, which was more than most people would have dared. "I find it the saddest thing in this valley," she said. "And this valley has a lot of practice at sad things."

Then she stood, shook out her skirt, and left him sitting on the stone with the wheel turning and the cold coming up through his bones, wondering what kind of man loses everything except the ache.

* * *

He went back to his room because his coat was bothering him.

That was the whole reason, and it sounded absurd even inside his own head. The brown wool coat he'd arrived in — patched at the elbows, too big across the shoulders, smelling faintly of woodsmoke and something else, something older, like a room long shut. He'd worn it every day since he woke up in it. But today the left side hung wrong, dragging at his hip like a hand in his pocket, and when he finally shrugged it off and turned it over, he found the seam.

The lining seam, under the left arm. Stitched closed with black thread in stitches so fine and so deliberate they could only belong to someone hiding something from someone determined to find it.

His hands weren't steady when he picked them open.

Inside the lining, folded into a square no bigger than a hymnal, was leather. Old leather, cracked white along the folds, worked with a design he recognized the way you recognize your own handwriting — instantly, totally, and with no idea how. A spire. A needle rising out of a circle of mountains, inked in silver that had gone grey with age.

He carried it to the window. Unfolded it.

A map. Or the ghost of one — roads that faded into blank vellum halfway across, mountains drawn in a hand confident enough to name them and then, strangely, refusing to. Rivers marked. A border drawn twice, once firm and once wavering, as if the cartographer had argued with himself about whether the line was real. In the corner, a title in a fine slanted script:

*A Door Out of Vessia.*

Vessia. The word hit him somewhere below thought. Not the valley's name for the outside world — everyone here called the beyond Vessia, the way you'd call the sea *the sea*, a story more than a place. But this wasn't a story. This was a working document. This was a map drawn by someone who had stood on those roads.

And under the mountains, small enough that he nearly missed it, a second inscription. Not the cartographer's hand. A rougher hand. Hurried. Pressed hard enough to score the leather:

*Her name is Petra. Start again.*

He read it four times. His own eyes, he realized — he knew that hand. Knew it the way you know the shape of your fingers. Whatever else the crossing had taken from him, it had left him his own handwriting, and his own handwriting was telling him a woman's name and giving him an order.

*Start again.*

Start what again?

He sat down on the edge of the bed because his legs had opinions about standing. The map lay open across his knees. Outside, the valley went on being beautiful and quiet, the millwheel turning, children shouting in the lane, the held-breath hush that lived over this place like weather.

*Her name is Petra.*

There was only one Petra. There was only one woman in this valley whose sadness looked at him like that, whose presence pulled at him like tide, who appeared in his dreams every night on a black ridge with her face just out of reach.

He had been carrying her name against his ribs for three weeks. Stitched there by his own hands, hidden from — from whom? From whoever had searched him? From himself?

He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. Fragments moved behind them, not memories exactly, more like the shadows memories leave on a wall after the fire's gone out. A frozen river. A ferryman's voice. Jars on shelves, hundreds of jars, each one labeled with a name. A stair road and screaming. A door.

*A door out of Vessia.*

And a woman stepping through it while he stood on the wrong side, and the moment she passed the threshold, the world going soft and white around the place where she had been—

He was on his feet without deciding to stand. The chair went over backward. His breath was coming fast and shallow and his hands were shaking, and none of it made sense, none of it was a memory, it couldn't be a memory because the herbwoman had told him he'd lost everything clean, no gaps, no ghosts —

But the ghosts didn't care what she'd told him.

*Start again,* his own hand had written. As if he'd known. As if some version of him — the version with the memories, the version who had stitched this map into his coat with shaking fingers on the far side of a door — had looked ahead at exactly this moment, this empty man in this rented room, and left him one sentence of instructions.

Not *remember*. You can't order a man to remember.

*Start again.* Start from nothing. Start from the name.

He folded the map along its old creases, careful, the way you handle a thing that has already survived more than it should have. Then he stopped, unfolded it once more, and looked at the corner inscription. *A Door Out of Vessia.* And beneath the title, so faint he'd missed it twice, a date. A year.

Twenty years ago. Older than the fall of whatever Vessia had been. Older, maybe, than the stranger who had carried it — because he was certain now, certain in his bones, that the coat had belonged to someone before it belonged to him. Someone who had died getting it here. Someone whose face he almost, almost had.

His brother, whispered something in his chest that was not quite a memory. The word arrived whole and aching, attached to nothing, and he gripped the windowsill until it passed.

* * *

Evening came down gold and cold. He stood at the shrine for his ten minutes, looking at nothing, because that was the ritual now, though he no longer knew what he was waiting for the nothing to say.

Then he went to find her.

She was drawing water at the village well, and she saw him coming from thirty paces — saw the coat, saw his face, and set the bucket down slowly, the way you set down something fragile when you think the ground might move.

"You found it," she said.

Not *what is it*. Not *why are you looking at me like that*. *You found it.* Said flat and quiet, with the weariness of a woman who has been waiting three weeks for a door to open and dreading what comes through it.

"How did you know?" His voice came out strange. Far away. "How did you know I'd find it?"

"Because I put it there."

The world tilted gently on its axis. "You—"

"After. After the door." She glanced around — the lane was empty, the well rope dripping, the first stars pricking through above the eastern ridge — and stepped closer, close enough that he could see her hands were trembling. "You were on the wrong side of it. You gave up everything you had of me, freely, and the door took it, and you looked at me like I was a stranger. Because you were one. We both were." Her jaw tightened. "So I hid the map in your coat. Your brother's map. With the note you wrote yourself, the night before, when you still knew what was coming. I figured — I hoped — someday you'd find it. Someday the hollow would itch enough."

"*Her name is Petra,*" he said. "*Start again.*"

"That's right."

"And the ridge. The spire. The woman I can't see." He was talking fast now, the fragments tumbling loose like stones from a breaking wall. "The ferryman. The jars. The stair road. Those aren't dreams, are they. They're yours. They're ours."

Something broke open in her face — relief and grief in the same instant, rain and sunlight falling together. "They're ours," she whispered.

"Why can't I remember you?" It came out raw, almost angry, and he didn't know who the anger was for. "Why did you let me—"

"Because you chose it." She reached out and took his hand, and her grip was cold from the well water and steady as bedrock. "You heard the price and you paid it anyway. Nobody made you. That's the whole point of you, Ilian. That's the thing I can't forget even if everyone else did." Her voice caught. "You loved me knowing you wouldn't know why."

He looked down at their hands. At the map-shaped weight gone from his coat and the new weight in his chest, heavier and warmer. Three weeks of walking to the millrace for no reason. Three weeks of a name itching under his skin. It hadn't been nothing. It had never been nothing. Something in him had known her the whole time, some part too deep for the door to reach, patient as a seed under snow.

"Tell me," he said. "All of it. From the beginning."

Her breath went out of her in a rush, like she'd been holding it for twenty years instead of three weeks. Above the eastern ridge, where the boundary stones stood in their silent ring, the last light was fading — and with spring coming on, with the long winter finally breaking, the valley felt less like held breath and more like an exhale about to begin.

"Where do you want me to start?" she asked.

He looked at her — really looked, the way you look at a country you're seeing for the first time and already know you'll never finish learning — and smiled.

"Start with the spire," he said. "And don't leave anything out."
