## 1. The Weight of Unpaid Things

The hall was built for forty Keepers. It held one.

Ada Iversen counted the empty desks the way she counted everything—twice, and then once more to be sure. Forty-one desks, actually, because old Keeper Maren had died eleven years ago and no one had ever moved hers. Her inkwell still sat there with a skin of black dust on it like a second lid. Ada dusted it every month. She told herself this was inventory maintenance. She knew better. It was the closest thing the guild had to a graveyard, and she was its only mourner.

She set her lamp on the master table, unrolled the audit scroll, and opened the first ledger.

*Inventory, Autumn Reckoning,* she wrote at the top in her small, exact hand. *Conducted by A. Iversen, apprentice third class.* There were no other classes anymore. There was Vess, who was senior Keeper and mostly elsewhere, and there was Ada, who was everything else: auditor, archivist, floor-sweeper, key-keeper, and the last person alive who could read the guild's shorthand without flinching.

The Ledgerkeepers' Guild traded in impossible things. That was not a boast; it was the letterhead. *Bottled Echoes, Borrowed Years, and Honest Weights for What Cannot Be Weighed.* The sign outside had faded so that HONEST now read as ONES, which Ada privately thought was an improvement.

She began, as always, with the echoes.

"Bottled echo, minor," she read aloud, because the silence in a room that size needed breaking or it would press down on you like water. "Vendor: the widow Anselm, east quarter. Contents: one afternoon of rain on a tin roof, duration four minutes." She checked the bottle against the shelf. It sat where it should, green glass, stopper waxed twice, the rain inside it going *tish-tish-tish* faintly when she tilted it toward the light. Four minutes of a dead woman's favorite sound. The widow had sold it to pay for a headstone and had come back three times since just to stand near the shelf and listen through the glass. Ada always let her. This was against policy. Policy said assets did not receive visitors. Ada had decided, somewhere around her sixteenth year, that policy could be quietly wrong about things.

"Condition: full," she wrote. "Note: do not sell."

That note appeared forty times through the ledger by now. Do not sell. Do not sell. She was, she understood, running the world's smallest act of sabotage—one line at a time, in a hand too neat to be accused of carelessness.

* * *

By the second hour she reached the borrowed years.

These were kept in the long cabinet at the north wall, drawers labeled in decades. A borrowed year looked like nothing—a sliver of pale light pressed between two panes of glass, humming very slightly, the way a struck tuning fork hums after you can no longer hear it. People sold their years here the way farmers sold grain: bad harvests, sick children, debts with teeth. The guild bought low and sold high, and what the buyers did with a stranger's year Ada tried not to imagine. She had seen one buyer's receipt once. *For the purpose of reliving.* She had decided not to know more.

She counted them out drawer by drawer. Thirty-one years total. One of them, in the bottom drawer under 1840s, was warm to the touch, which years were not supposed to be. She made a note: *Drawer 7, item 3, anomalous warmth. Investigate.* She had made that same note for six consecutive reckonings. No one had ever investigated. Investigation required a quorum of two Keepers, and the guild had not had a quorum since before her voice changed.

That was the arithmetic of the place, really. Not the ledgers—the absence. Forty desks, two people, one of them always leaving. Vess came and went like weather, arriving with road dust on her hem and sealed instructions from nobody Ada ever saw, staying just long enough to sign what needed signing and remind Ada that the vault below stayed locked, that some entries were above her grade, that curiosity was a debt and debts collected themselves.

"You count what's there, girl," Vess had told her the first winter. "Never what isn't. That way lies ruin."

Ada had spent nine years counting what wasn't there anyway. It was, she'd found, a much longer list.

* * *

Midday came and went without ceremony. She ate bread and hard cheese at the master table, careful to keep crumbs off the ledger pages, and worked through the afternoon with the lamp lit against the early dark. Autumn came early to the guildhall, or perhaps the guildhall simply agreed with autumn about how things ought to be: dim, orderly, slightly cold.

The sealed grief lived on the top shelf, alone, in a lead-lined box the size of a prayer book.

There was exactly one. In two hundred years of operation, the guild had taken in precisely one grief, and no record remained of who had sold it or why, because the entry itself was written in the founder's cipher and the cipher key had been lost—or burned, depending on which of Vess's moods you caught her in. The box was cold. Not cool—cold, the specific cold of a cellar in January, radiating outward like the opposite of a hearth. When Ada audited it, she had to hold her hands near it for a full minute before they stopped wanting to shake.

She didn't open it. She never opened it. But she had learned, over the years, to put her palm flat against the lead and wait, and something on the other side waited too. Not malevolent. Just present. Grief, even sealed, even sold, even owned outright by a guild that filed it between the bottled lullabies and the confiscated promises—it still wanted, somewhere in its cold weight, to be felt by someone.

"Still here," Ada told it, the way you'd tell an old dog. "Still counted."

Then she wrote: *Sealed grief, one (1). Condition: intact. Weight: refuses all scales.*

It was true. They had tried. Three generations of Keepers had tried, with counterweights and water displacement and one memorable attempt involving a priest and a very large balance beam. The grief weighed nothing and everything, depending on who was asking, and eventually everyone had agreed to write *refuses all scales* in the margin and stop trying. Ada liked that line. It was the most honest sentence in two hundred years of bookkeeping.

* * *

The light failed properly around five. She trimmed the lamp, moved to the window desk because the window desk had the last of the day, and began the part of the audit she both dreaded and loved beyond reason: reconciling the margins.

Every ledger kept secrets in its margins. Little annotations, centuries of them, in forty different hands. *Paid in kind. Paid in regret. Paid forward, see vol. IX.* And once, in handwriting so fine it might have been embroidered:

*Nothing is free. Everything is owed. Count carefully, little sister.*

No signature. No date. Ada had found it her first year, in the flyleaf of volume one, and had built half her private mythology around it. Little sister. As if the ledger itself had spoken. As if the guild, in its bones, in its paper and wax and dust, had noticed her—the orphan the Keepers took in as a charity case and then forgot to notice again—and had said: *you, then. You count.*

She loved the ledger. She had made peace with that a long time ago, the way you make peace with loving a person who cannot love you back. The ledgers were patient. The ledgers remembered everything, which was more than any living Keeper could claim. When she ran her fingers down a column of entries two hundred years deep, she was touching every hand that had ever written here, and every desperate soul who had walked through that door with something impossible to sell, and the ledger held them all equally, without judgment, in its beautiful merciless columns.

The guild ignored her. The ledger did not. It was not a difficult choice, loving one over the other. It was barely a choice at all.

* * *

She was halfway through volume fourteen—confiscated promises, a sad thin section, mostly broken oaths bought off drunk men—when she heard the sound.

Not a knock. Not yet. First, the lamps.

All of them, at once, guttered—not out, but *down*, flames crouching like animals hearing a distant hound. The fire in the great hearth, banked to coals since morning, exhaled a thread of smoke that smelled wrong. Not woodsmoke. Older. Like the smell of a book burning in a house you haven't visited yet.

Ada sat very still. In nine years the guildhall had creaked and settled and moaned in the wind, but it had never done *this*. The building had moods, sure—all old buildings did—but its moods were groans and drafts, not a held breath.

Then the ledgers moved.

Not dramatically. Nothing flew, nothing rattled. Volume fourteen simply shifted under her hand, a fraction of an inch, the way a sleeping animal shifts toward warmth. And beneath her palm she felt it—felt it clearly, unmistakably, the way you feel a heartbeat through a wrist—

the pages breathing.

In. Out. Slow and vast, the whole stack of them, volumes one through twenty-two on their shelves, rising and falling together like the chest of something enormous asleep in the walls.

"Hello?" she said, and hated how small it sounded in a hall built for forty.

The ledgers breathed. The lamps crouched. Somewhere far below—below the cellar, below the vault Vess kept triple-locked, below whatever the guildhall stood on instead of ordinary ground—something turned over in its sleep, and the floorboards carried the motion up through Ada's boots and into her teeth.

And then, faint but getting closer, up the long road from the village: footsteps. Small ones. Uneven ones. The steps of someone very young walking a very long way, and behind them, riding on the dusk like smoke on a chimney, a sound Ada had no framework for—a voice, a child's voice, singing words in a language that made the ledger's breathing quicken.

Volume one opened itself on its shelf. She heard it clearly: the soft *whump* of covers parting, pages turning of their own accord, faster and faster, until they stopped.

Ada stood. Her chair scraped back loud as a gunshot in the silence. Every instinct trained into her by nine years of Vess's rules said *sit down, count what's there, never what isn't.* But the ledger had opened itself, and the ledgers did not lie, and the small uneven footsteps were almost at the door.

She picked up the lamp and went to answer a knock in a language older than kingdoms.
