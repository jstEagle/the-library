## 2. A Knock in a Dead Language

The knock came at dusk, and it was wrong.

Not the rhythm—three raps, polite enough, spaced like a merchant's greeting. It was the sound. Every knock I'd ever heard landed on wood and stopped there. This one went *through* the door, through the entry hall, through the floor, down into the foundations of the guildhall, and something down there answered. I felt it in my teeth. A hum, low and brief, like a struck bell heard from another room.

I set down my pen. In the great hall, forty desks stood empty around me, their inkwells dry, their ledgers squared away by hands that hadn't touched them in years. Mine was the only lamp still lit. The guild had been built for a chorus and now held one voice, and that voice was mine, and mostly what it said all day was *two bottles of echo, condition fair, provenance disputed.*

I went to the door because nobody else would. That was the whole of my job description, some days.

The hinges groaned. Evening stood on the step, blue-gray and cold, and in the middle of it stood a child.

Perhaps seven years old. Small for seven, even. He wore a coat that had been mended so many times it was less a garment than an argument between patches, and his boots were two sizes too large, stuffed at the toes with what looked like straw. His hair was dark and uncombed and full of road dust. He looked up at me with eyes that were an ordinary brown, ordinary as bread, and he said something.

It was not any language I knew.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the first thing they teach you here: *a ledger is only as honest as its entries.* I know four tongues well enough to bargain in them and six more well enough to swear in them. The guild's library holds grammars for languages that died before the mountain had a name, and I have read most of them out of boredom, which is its own kind of scholarship. What the child said was none of these. It was none of anything. The sounds were simple—a handful of syllables, rising and falling like a question asked twice—and yet my mind slid off them the way a thumb slides off wet glass. I heard every sound and could not hold a single one.

He said it again, patiently, the way you speak to someone who is slow rather than deaf.

"Come inside," I said, in Trade-Tongue, then in Low Cant, then, absurdly, in Old Vellish, which no one under sixty speaks. "It's cold."

He considered me. Then he stepped over the threshold, and the moment his boot crossed it, the bell-hum sounded again, deeper this time, and every lamp in the entry hall flared green for half a heartbeat before settling back to gold.

That should have been my first warning. Lamps in the guildhall burn on rendered candlefat like anywhere else. They do not flare. They do not change color. I told myself it was a draft.

* * *

Here is what you must understand about the Ledgerkeepers, since I will keep saying *we* and *us* until I can't anymore: we trade in impossible things, but we are not magicians. We are clerks. The magic, such as it is, lives entirely in the ledger—the great master ledger chained to its lectern in the counting room, the one that has recorded every transaction since the founding. Bottled echoes. Borrowed years. A widow's grief, sealed in wax and sold to a collector who wanted to know what mourning felt like without doing any of it. The ledger records these things, authenticates them, fixes their terms. It does not, in my experience, do anything else.

It has never once opened itself. In three hundred years of guild history—which I have read, all of it, including the boring parts—it has never once turned its own pages.

I brought the boy into the counting room because it was warm and because I didn't know what else to do with him. He walked in slowly, turning his head, reading the shelves of inventory the way other children read picture books. When he passed the shelf holding the sealed grief, he stopped. He put his small hand flat against the wax seal, and he made a sound—not the sliding language, just a sound, soft and sad—and moved on.

My skin prickled. I told myself he was a lost child, hungry, probably, and that lost children say strange things, and that lamps flicker.

Then the ledger opened itself.

I had my back to it. I heard the chain slacken—that chain weighs more than I do; it takes both hands and a lever to lift—and then the whisper of vellum, page after page after page, turning fast, like wind through wheat. By the time I turned around, the book lay open near its own beginning, at a page I had read a hundred times and never been able to read at all.

Page nine. The founders' section. The ink on page nine was dense and old and written in a script that matched nothing in our grammars, and every Keeper for three centuries had catalogued it the same way: *illegible, possibly decorative, possibly a cipher, see attached notes.* There were eleven attached notes, each one a different Keeper's failed attempt at translation. I had added the eleventh myself, two years ago, at nineteen, very proud of my failure.

The boy walked straight to the lectern. He did not hesitate. He did not search. He went to page nine the way water goes downhill, and he looked at the writing, and he began to read aloud.

The language came off his tongue like it lived there. And this time—I don't know how to explain this except that the room changed—this time I could almost hear the shape beneath the sounds. Not the words. The *grammar of the meaning*, if that makes any sense, the way you can stand outside a house in the rain and know how many rooms it has by the light in the windows. The words were old. Older than Old Vellish. Older than the mountain. And they were not decorative, and they were not a cipher.

They were a contract.

I know contracts the way priests know prayers. I could feel the structure of it even untranslated: parties named, consideration stated, terms fixed, penalty specified. The boy's voice rose and fell through clauses. Somewhere in the middle he touched one line with his fingertip, gently, the way you'd touch a scar, and repeated it three times, and each time the lamps leaned toward him like plants toward sun.

"Stop," I said. My voice came out thin. "Stop, please."

He stopped. He looked up at me, and his face was a child's face, open and puzzled, and behind the puzzlement was something else, something patient and vast and terribly tired, and I have thought about that look every day since, and I am thinking about it now.

"Who are you?" I asked.

He answered in the sliding language. All of it slid off me except one word—one word that snagged, that caught in my ear like a hook, and I want to write it down here exactly as my memory keeps it, though letters were never made for it: *Vess-ka-lun.* No. Closer to *Veskalune.* It sounded like a name, or like the space where a name should be, and when he said it he put his hand flat on his own chest.

"You don't have a name," I said stupidly.

He tilted his head. Then he pointed—at the ledger, at page nine, at the line he had touched—and then at his chest again.

The book knew him. That was the thought that arrived then, cold and complete, arriving the way ice arrives, all at once: *the book knows him.* Three hundred years of transactions, and somewhere in that dense old ink was a line about this child, this ragged, straw-booted, nameless child who had knocked on our door at dusk and been let in by the last apprentice of a dying guild.

Boots on the stairs. Heavy ones, deliberate ones, with the particular drag of a woman who has carried authority so long it has become a physical weight.

Keeper Vess.

I did not think. Thinking is for afterward. I did what the oldest instinct in the guildhall told me to do, the instinct older than the founders, older than the ledger itself, the instinct of anyone who has ever kept accounts: I protected the asset. I shut the ledger—the chain relocked itself with a sound like a swallowed word—and I took the boy by the hand and pulled him into the alcove behind the inventory shelves where we store the bottled echoes, and I pressed my finger to my lips, and he, understanding perfectly, which was itself terrifying, went silent as a closed book.

Vess filled the doorway. She is not a large woman but she occupies space the way a magistrate does. Her eyes went over the counting room: the lamp, the cold hearth, the lectern, the ledger sitting exactly as it always sits.

"Audit going well?" she asked.

"Two discrepancies," I said. My voice held. I was almost proud of it. "Both minor."

"Mm." She stood there a moment longer than she needed to. Vess always stands a moment longer than she needs to; it's how she counts things without touching them. "You left the front door unlocked."

"I was expecting no one."

"No one knocks at dusk," she said, "unless they want something. Remember that, apprentice. Wanting things is how debts start." She turned to go, then paused on the threshold. "The lamps burned green earlier. Did you notice?"

"I noticed a draft," I said.

Something moved across her face—too fast to name, too slow to miss. "A draft," she repeated. "Yes. Lock the door behind me, Ada. And Ada—" she did not turn around, "—if anything comes to this door speaking a dead tongue, you will bring it to me. Immediately. Do you understand?"

The alcove was utterly silent behind me. The boy might not have existed.

"I understand," I said.

She left. I listened to her door close at the far end of the hall, and then I listened to the guildhall settle into its nighttime creaks, and then, only then, I drew back the curtain of echoes.

The boy was standing very close to the shelf, looking up at the nearest bottle. Inside it, faint as breath on glass, an echo swirled—the recorded laughter of a man who had sold it to pay a gambling debt, thirty years back. The boy watched it turn. Then he looked at me and said, in that sliding, unholdable language, one short sentence.

And though I could not translate a syllable of it, I understood it completely, the way you understand thunder:

*The book knows me. Do you?*

I locked the front door. I brought him bread and the good blanket from my own cot, and I sat up all night watching him sleep in the counting room chair, and I watched the ledger, chained and shut and quiet, and I did not sleep at all.

Because here is what I kept returning to, the thought that curdled the wonder in me drop by drop until morning: Vess had known. Not suspected—*known*. *If anything comes to this door speaking a dead tongue, bring it to me.* You do not prepare instructions for miracles. You prepare instructions for deliveries.

The boy was not a visitor.

He was an arrival. Something the guild had been waiting for, or owed, or owed *by*—and page nine, the illegible page, the page every Keeper had failed to read for three hundred years, had opened for him like a door.

At dawn he woke, and ate, and watched me watch him, and then he climbed onto the lectern stool, laid his small hand flat on the ledger's cover, and looked at me with those ordinary brown eyes until I lifted the chain.

The book fell open to page nine on its own.

And this time, when he began to read, I did not stop him.
