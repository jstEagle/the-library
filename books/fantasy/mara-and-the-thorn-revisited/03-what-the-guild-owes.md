## 3. What the Guild Owes

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width by the time I admitted I wasn't going to sleep.

The boy was asleep instead—curled on the reading chaise in the east alcove with his knees drawn up and one hand still gripping the hem of my sleeve, as if letting go of it might let go of him. He'd eaten half a wheel of cheese and all of the dried plums and then simply stopped mid-chew, sat down where he was, and gone under like a stone in a well. Children do that, I supposed. I wouldn't know. I'd been raised by ledgers.

I should have carried him somewhere softer than a chaise. I didn't, because moving him meant unwinding his fist from my sleeve, and some small cowardly part of me wanted to stay anchored.

So I worked.

That was the honest truth of me, anyway: when the world went strange, I reached for a tally. It was what the guild had made of me, or what I'd made of myself out of the materials they'd left lying around—a girl with no family and an aptitude for arithmetic, useful in the way a good lock is useful. Nobody loves a lock. Everybody leans on it.

The page was still open.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have: ledgers do not open themselves. In two hundred years of recorded guild history—and I had read every word of those records twice—the Ledgerkeepers' master ledger had never once turned its own pages. Books are objects. Objects obey hands. That was the first law of the trade, older than any contract in the vault: *a ledger records; it does not act.*

And yet at dusk, when the knock came in that dead language, the book had opened itself to a page written in the same dead language, as though it had been waiting by the door with its coat already on.

The script was beautiful in the way old things are beautiful—dense, unhurried, each character built like it expected to stand for a thousand years. I knew perhaps forty words of Old Ledger-Tongue, the ceremonial dialect we used for binding contracts, and most of those were numbers. The page before me was not that dialect. It was older. It was the language the dialect had been *borrowed from*, the way a coin is borrowed from a treasury that no longer exists.

But here is the thing about ledgers, the thing they teach you in your first year and you spend your whole life unlearning: the format survives translation. A contract is a contract in any tongue. The architecture doesn't change. There will be parties. There will be terms. There will be consideration given and held and owed. There will be signatures at the bottom, because there are always signatures at the bottom, because someone always wants the last word to be theirs.

I spread my reference folios across the table—the Concordance of Binding Forms, Aldous Vane's *Comparative Contract Structures*, three generations of my own marginalia—and I began the work of my life.

* * *

The boy slept through the first hour. Somewhere in the second hour he turned over and murmured something in that language, soft as water over stones, and the candle flame leaned toward him like a listener. I told myself I hadn't seen that. I was getting very good at telling myself things.

By the third hour I had the structure mapped.

The document was a collateral instrument. That much came fast, because collateral instruments have a shape you could recognize blindfolded: an asset named, a debt described, conditions of forfeiture laid out in the grim litany every Keeper memorizes before they're allowed to touch a client's goods. *In the event of default. In the event of insolvency. In the event of*—

There it was. The fourth condition. I read it eleven times, cross-referencing each character against the concordance, checking and rechecking, because what it said was too large to fit through the door of my understanding on the first pass.

*Payable when the fire dims.*

Not *if*. When. The drafters had never entertained doubt. The fire would dim—it was only a question of accounting for when—and on that day, the asset would transfer.

The asset.

I put down my pen. I picked it back up. This is the discipline of the trade: you do not stop when the numbers frighten you. You stop when they're finished.

The asset was listed the way the guild lists everything, in the cold grammar of inventory. Not a description. A designation. A single word in the old tongue, repeated throughout the document wherever a name should be, the way you'd write *the property* or *the holding* or *the lot.*

Except it wasn't a word for property.

I found it in the deepest stratum of the concordance, in an appendix Vane had titled "Pre-Imperial Roots (Speculative)"—forty pages of guesses by a man who admitted, in his preface, that he was reconstructing a language from its wreckage. The word appeared exactly once in his tables. His gloss was tentative. His gloss was four words long.

*The one who is owed.*

I looked at the sleeping child. Seven years old, maybe. Cheek pressed against the chaise cushion. Fist still knotted in my sleeve. Breathing in the slow, deep rhythm of someone who has walked a very long way and finally been allowed to stop.

*The one who is owed.*

"No," I said aloud, to the empty hall, to the candle, to nobody. Because there was another reading. There had to be. Words drift; meanings slide; Vane himself had flagged the root as speculative. Maybe it meant debtor. Maybe it meant witness. Maybe it meant nothing at all and the true name of the asset was recorded elsewhere, in a supplement, in a sealed annex, in some drawer I hadn't opened yet.

Ledgers do not lie. That was the second law. I had built my entire self on the second law. But ledgers can tell the truth in a voice so flat it sounds like a lie, and this page was telling the truth in a voice like a frozen lake: smooth, silent, and bearing weight I couldn't see the bottom of.

I went back to the beginning of the document and forced myself through the opening formula, the part I'd skimmed because openings are boilerplate. Parties. Terms. Consideration.

The parties were listed first, as parties always are, and the first party was not a person.

It was a guild.

My guild. Named—not by the name on our sign, but by its founding name, the one carved into the lintel above the vault door that everyone assumed was decorative. *The Keepers of the Unpaid.* I'd read that carving ten thousand times and never once thought about what it said. We called ourselves Ledgerkeepers now. Someone, somewhere in our history, had sanded the edges off the original title, the way you sand the edge off a table that drew blood.

The counterparty was named below it, and I recognized that name too, though I'd only ever seen it in the oldest folios, always abbreviated, always with a respectful space left after it the way you leave a space after the name of the dead.

*The Court Beneath the Mountain.*

I sat very still. The candle burned. The boy breathed. Somewhere in the walls of the guildhall, the building settled into night with small wooden complaints, the sounds a house makes when it thinks no one is listening.

The consideration—the thing given, the thing received—was laid out in a schedule. And schedules, thank every god watching, schedules are just lists, and lists translate even when language fails. Item after item after item, each entry a single line, each line structured identically, and it took me until the twentieth line to understand what I was counting because my mind kept refusing the sum the way a hand refuses a hot iron.

One soul. Another soul. Another soul. Another soul.

Paid. Paid. Paid. Paid.

To the Court Beneath. On account. Against the debt. Payable when the fire dims.

I counted the lines. I am very good at counting lines; it is the one talent I have never doubted. Four hundred and six entries. Four hundred and six souls, sold down the mountain one at a time across two centuries, each payment logged with the meticulous care my guild brought to everything, each entry initialed in the margin by a senior Keeper whose initials I knew. I had catalogued those initials. I had admired the consistency of them. Master Oren's hand, steady as a plumb line. Master Cett's, sharp and slanted. Master Ilse's, round and patient, the hand that had taught my own teacher.

Masters I had worshipped. Masters who were dust now, honored in portraits in the west corridor, their names spoken at convocation with the reverence owed to founders.

Founders. The signatures at the bottom of the page belonged to them—all nine, in ink that had gone brown with age but had not faded, because binding ink does not fade, binding ink holds, that was the whole point of it, that was the pride of the craft.

They had signed this. They had stood in this hall—or whatever this hall had been, two hundred years ago—and they had signed away a human being as security on a loan, and then they had signed away four hundred more, one per generation, quietly, in flawless bookkeeping, while the guild above grew rich trading bottled echoes and borrowed years and sealed griefs to clients who never asked where the Keepers got the capital for their own side of the trades.

We weren't traders. I understood it then, sitting in the dark with a dead language bleeding across my table. We were middlemen. We took impossible things from people who couldn't afford to keep them, and we paid our suppliers down the mountain, and our supplier dealt in the only commodity that never loses value.

People.

* * *

The candle guttered and I replaced it without standing, from the box we kept under the table, and the mechanical smallness of that act—new wick, new light, same darkness—nearly broke something in me. I gripped the table edge and waited it out. Grief is just a debt you didn't know you'd taken on. You pay it in installments. I paid mine in the currency I had: I kept working.

Because there was one more section. Collateral instruments end with a custody clause—who holds the asset, where, under what conditions—and this one was no different. The final paragraph specified the manner in which the asset was to be maintained pending transfer.

Maintained. The word choice turned my stomach even before I translated the rest. You maintain a furnace. You maintain a hedge. You do not *maintain* a child.

But the clause was specific, and specificity, in the old tongue, was a form of mercy I could read: the asset was to be kept ignorant of its status. The asset was to be delivered unmarked. The asset was to remember nothing of its origin, because memory, the clause noted in a subordinate line I had to parse three times, *complicates valuation.*

They had erased him. Before he could walk, before he could speak, someone had reached into whatever he was and taken his name out, the way you'd take a coin out of circulation, and the reason—the stated, documented, initialed reason—was that a named soul appraises higher, and an unnamed one stays flexible.

Flexible. The word sat in my chest like swallowed glass.

He stirred on the chaise. For one horrible moment I thought he'd heard me thinking, which is nonsense, but guilt makes mystics of us all. He didn't wake. He turned his face toward the candlelight and sighed, a small sound, entirely ordinary, the sound of any child anywhere dreaming about anything.

And the ledger, open on my table, turned a page.

I did not touch it. I want that on the record, in whatever court eventually reads these words: I did not touch it. The page lifted at its corner, hung a moment as though considering, and settled flat again—and the new page was blank except for a single line of the old script, fresh, the ink still holding a faint shine, as though it had been written seconds ago by a hand I couldn't see.

I translated it slowly, because my hands were shaking and slow work steadies them.

It was a due date.

Not a date—a *term*. The instrument, the whole four-hundred-and-six-soul edifice of quiet horror, contained a maturity provision I had missed, buried in the schedule's final column, and the ledger had turned to it now with the patience of a creditor who knows the debtor has finally read the contract.

*Balance payable in full upon the dimming of the fire,* the line read. *Remaining collateral: one (1).*

One. After two hundred years of payments, after four hundred and six souls fed down the mountain to keep some fire burning beneath the earth, the account stood nearly settled, and what remained—what the entire ancient machine of my beloved guild existed to deliver—was a seven-year-old boy asleep on a chaise, holding my sleeve.

The fire dims. I didn't know what fire. I didn't know where the mountain was, or whether the Court Beneath was a place or a metaphor, and some traitorous part of me hoped, briefly, desperately, for metaphor.

But I knew what happened at maturity. Every Keeper knows. At maturity, the collateral transfers. At maturity, you don't get to renegotiate, because the contract was sacred, because the contract was the guild, because the guild was the only home I had ever—

I stopped that thought. I set it down carefully, the way you set down something breakable, and I looked at it.

The guild was not my home. The guild was a counterparty. And I had just spent one night discovering that my counterparty had been selling people since before my grandmother was born—selling them with beautiful penmanship, selling them with initials in the margins, selling them so cleanly that two centuries of Keepers had polished the practice into tradition and the tradition into doctrine and the doctrine into the quiet pride of a job well done.

I had loved this ledger more than the guild that ignored me. I saw that now with terrible clarity. I had loved it because it was honest, because it never lied, because in a hall built for forty where I ate alone every night, the ledger was the only thing that told me the truth.

Well. It was telling me the truth now.

The boy's grip tightened on my sleeve in his sleep. Small fingers. Chapped knuckles. Feet that had walked a very long way to arrive at the one door in the world where his name was written down—even if it was written down as an asset, even if the writing was a sentence.

Somewhere beneath the mountain, a fire was dimming. Somewhere in this building, a senior Keeper would arrive at dawn and find a strange child in the east alcove and a page open in the master ledger, and I had perhaps five hours to decide what I was going to do about both.

I blew out the candle, because some things you can't read by daylight, and some things you shouldn't read by any light at all—and because in the dark, with my hand resting on the cover of the book that had betrayed us both, I heard the boy say a word in his sleep.

One word. In the old tongue.

I knew it by then. I'd spent all night learning it.

He said *owed*, and rolled over, and slept on, and downstairs in the sealed vault, faint through three floors of stone, something that might have been a book turned a page.
