## 4. Keeper Vess Explains the Arithmetic

Vess had always found that the best lies were the ones you told yourself first, slowly, over decades, until they wore smooth as river stone and you could carry them in your pocket without cutting your hand.

She climbed the stairs from her study with a lamp in one hand and a cup of cold tea in the other, and she did not hurry, because hurrying was a confession. The guildhall at night was her favorite version of it: the great ledger hall dark and breathing, forty empty desks where forty Keepers had once worked, the shelves of bottled echoes glinting like a harbor at dusk. Everything in its place. Everything accounted for.

Except the girl.

Ada Iversen was in the reading alcove with a lamp turned low and the guild's oldest ledger open across her knees, and the boy was asleep against her shoulder with the boneless trust of a child who had decided, somewhere in the last two days, that this stranger was his. Vess stopped in the shadow of the archway and watched them for a moment the way an auditor watches a column of numbers that has begun, quietly, to refuse to add up.

She had known this conversation was coming. She had been rehearsing it, in one form or another, for thirty years.

"Put the book down, Ada," she said.

The girl flinched—Vess noted the flinch with the detached satisfaction of a professional, the way you note a signature on a contract—and closed the ledger over her thumb. "Keeper Vess. I didn't hear you."

"No. You never do." She came into the light and set her cup on the reading desk, deliberately, so the girl would watch her do it. Deliberateness was half of authority; the other half was not blinking. "You've been in this alcove since dusk. The boy hasn't eaten since noon. And you've been reading the Founders' Folio, which is not on any apprentice's approved list."

Ada's chin came up. She was twenty-two and had the stubbornness of someone raised by an institution that never once told her she belonged in it. "The page was open. It opened itself."

"Books do that here," Vess said. "It's one of the first things you should have been taught, if anyone had bothered to teach you anything. Sit down."

"I am sitting."

"Then we understand each other." Vess drew the second chair out and sat, arranging her skirts with the small economy of a woman who had spent her life making sure no motion was wasted. Up close the boy smelled of woodsmoke and something older, something like the air in the vault, and she did not let herself look at him longer than a second. Looking too long was how Keepers started asking questions, and she had finished asking questions in the winter of her fortieth year.

"Tell me what you've read," she said.

"I've read a contract." Ada's voice was steady. Good. Anger was easier to work with than tears. "Signed by nine founders. Dated one hundred and ninety years ago. It lists one item of collateral." She looked down at the sleeping child. "It lists him."

"It lists an asset," Vess corrected. "The asset happens to be embodied. That's not unusual in our trade. We've held bottled echoes that had faces. We've held a grief so complete it had to be sealed in lead. Collateral takes whatever shape the debt requires."

"It's a *child*."

"It's a *balance*," Vess said, and heard, as she always heard, how reasonable the word sounded. That was the trick of it. That had always been the trick of it. "Ada. I'm going to tell you something I've told no apprentice, because no apprentice has ever earned it. You've earned it by reading what you shouldn't have. Consider that a lesson in consequences."

She folded her hands. She let the silence do some of the work first. Silence was a tool; the guild had taught her that, back when the guild had still been teaching anyone anything.

"You know what dragon-fire is," she began. "Or you know the version we put in the primer. Eternal flame, the hearth of the world, the light that never gutters. Poetry. Apprentices like poetry." She paused. "Here is the accounting version. The world has borders. Not the borders on maps—those are drawings. I mean the real ones. The edge of the valley that is the valley and not the sea. The reason a road remembers to arrive where it's going. The reason your mother's village is still where you left it, and not somewhere else, or nowhere else. Those borders are not a wall. They are a *fire*. One fire, under the mountain, and every border in the world is a shadow it casts."

Ada was very still. "And if it goes out?"

"Then the shadows go with it. The valley forgets it isn't the sea. The road forgets where it was going and takes everything on it along. The village forgets it was ever anywhere." Vess let that sit. "The fire has been burning since before the guild. It burned on its own for an age, the way a forest burns on rain and sunlight. But fires end. All of them. And about two hundred years ago, the nine founders discovered that the fire could burn on something else. Something that holds a great deal of heat."

She watched Ada arrive at it. She watched the girl's face do the arithmetic before her mouth did.

"Souls," Ada said.

"Remembered souls. A soul that is held—recorded, catalogued, *kept*—burns slowly and beautifully, the way a well-kept memory burns. The court beneath the mountain keeps the fire. We supply it." Vess spread her hands, the gesture of a merchant displaying honest wares. "One soul, properly kept, buys the world roughly a century of continuing to be the world. That is the exchange rate. It has never varied. It is, in my professional opinion, the only honest price in existence."

The boy stirred in his sleep and made a small sound, and both of them went quiet until he settled again. Vess used the pause. She was good with pauses.

"You're going to tell me it's murder," she said. "I want you to know that I understand. I stood exactly where you're sitting, thirty-one years ago, and I said the word *murder* to Keeper Halden, and he did what I'm about to do. He asked me a question."

Ada's jaw was tight. "Ask it, then."

"Name one person who has died because of what we do."

Silence.

"Go on," Vess said softly. "Think. The souls we sell are not taken. They are *held*. Their owners live out their whole lives, marry, bury, sing, forget, die of old age in their beds—and afterward, instead of scattering the way souls are supposed to scatter, instead of thinning into nothing the way everything does, they are kept. Warm. Complete. Every memory they ever made, still burning. The world does not lose them, Ada. The world *keeps* them, which is more than the world has ever done for anyone. And in exchange, the borders hold. The valley stays a valley. Your grandmother's village stayed put long enough for your grandmother to be born in it." She let her voice drop, gentle as a ledger line. "Name the victim."

Ada's eyes were wet and furious. "Him."

"He hasn't lost anything either. Not yet. He is collateral, not payment. He's been walking the world for seven years, unowned, unrecorded, and the moment he came to our door, the moment the ledger opened to his page, he came *home* to where his value is kept safe." Vess shook her head. "You think a nameless child with a dead language in his mouth has a better life waiting somewhere? We are the only institution in the world that knows what he is. We are the only ones who will feed him."

"And when the fire dims again? When the century runs out?"

"Then the guild pays again. That is the whole of our purpose. That has always been the whole of our purpose." And here Vess leaned forward, because this was the part that mattered, the part she had carried for thirty years like a stone in her chest, and she wanted—needed—the girl to feel its weight. "Do you know what we were, before? Nine clerks with a talent for impossible inventory, renting out borrowed years and bottled echoes to duchies and heartbroken widows. A curiosity. A footnote. And then the founders found the fire dying, and found the price of the world, and they *paid it*. Every one of them. They signed, and they spent the rest of their lives finding souls to keep the light on, and they died one by one with the world still standing, and not one peasant in one village ever knew, or thanked them, or would have understood. That is what a Keeper is, Ada. Not a merchant. A *wick*. We burn so the map doesn't."

She sat back. Her tea had gone fully cold. She drank it anyway.

"The fire is dimming now," she said. "You've seen the signs, even if you don't know them as signs. Roads misbehaving. Places gone soft at the edges. The court sent word last month: the current reserve buys us years, not decades. And the ledger has opened to one page, one asset, one name—" she nodded at the boy— "because the debt is coming due, and the guild pays its debts. That is the one thing, the *only* thing, that has kept this world coherent for two hundred years. We pay. We have never once defaulted."

"And if I take him and run?"

"Then the fire dims faster, the borders fray, and somewhere a village full of people who never heard your name will stop having ever existed. And the court will send for him anyway. They are very patient, and they are very thorough, and they have never lost an asset." Vess said it without cruelty, which was what made it land. "You cannot steal from the arithmetic, child. You can only choose who does the counting."

She rose, took up her lamp, and paused at the edge of the alcove's light.

"You hate me now," she said. "Good. Hatred keeps the mind sharp. But remember this: I am not the villain of this story. There is no villain. There is only the fire, and the bill, and the people willing to pay it. Sleep on that. In the morning, we'll prepare the transfer."

She was three steps into the dark when Ada's voice stopped her—quiet, level, and nothing like a child's.

"The vault book," Ada said. "The true ledger. It's downstairs, isn't it. And my grandmother's name is in it."

Vess did not turn around.

The lamp flame leaned, as if listening.

* * *

She stood there long enough for the silence to become its own kind of answer, and she hated herself for the hesitation, because hesitation was a confession too.

"Who told you about the vault?" she asked.

"Nobody. The Founders' Folio referenced a *primary record*. And you just did." Ada's voice didn't waver. "You didn't say no."

Vess closed her eyes briefly. Thirty-one years of discipline, and one exhausted girl with a stolen book had found the seam in it in a single evening. Perhaps that was what happened when you let the guildhall fall to one Keeper and one apprentice. Perhaps that was the real cost of the fire—paid not in souls but in staff, in the slow emptying of forty desks until the last arithmetic sat alone with its conscience in a hall built for a guild.

"Your grandmother sold her sorrow to this guild when your mother was six," Vess said, turning at last. "A standard transaction. She came here weeping and asked us to take it, and we did, and she lived thirty more years lighter than any woman I ever met. When she died, her soul came to us whole, as all our clients' souls do, and it was entered in the reserve, and it has been keeping the borders warm ever since. She is not suffering, Ada. She is *remembered*. Perfectly. Forever. More than I will ever be."

"Is she in the fire now?"

"She is in the vault, on the shelf, awaiting transfer." Vess lifted the lamp. "And if you go down there tonight—if you open that book and find her name and decide, in your grief, to do something reckless—you should know that the vault door answers to me, and the court's patience does not answer to anyone."

"Then open it," Ada said. She stood, and the boy woke and clung to her skirts, blinking at Vess with those eyes that were too old by several centuries. "Open it yourself. If the arithmetic is so honest, you won't mind me checking your figures."

For a moment—just a moment—Vess felt something she had not felt in thirty years. Not fear. Something adjacent to fear. The feeling of a number that has been checked and found to be correct, when you had been counting on it being wrong.

"Very well," she said, because refusing would have been the greater confession. "But you go down as an apprentice, with your Keeper, and you come back up having learned the last lesson this guild has to teach."

She led them to the stair behind the ledger hall, the one that went down farther than the guildhall had floors, and as her lamp touched the first step she heard the true ledger breathing below—slow, warm, patient, like a fire that had learned to read.

And beneath the breathing, faint as a signature, she could have sworn she heard a second sound: a child's voice, humming in a dead language, keeping time.

The boy, at her shoulder, had not opened his mouth.
