## 5. The Boy Who Remembers Forward

He slept in the reading alcove, on a pallet of ledger-cloth that still smelled of ink and dust, and Ada sat across from him with a lamp turned low, telling herself she was only keeping watch.

That was the lie she'd settled on. The truth was simpler and stranger: she didn't want to be in the same building as the page without someone else in the room. The contract written in the dead language had stayed open all night, refusing to close, its letters shifting like fish under ice whenever she looked away and back again. She'd covered it with a blotter. She could still feel it through the wood of the desk, a faint warmth, like a hand pressed flat against the other side.

The child slept the way children sleep—utterly, bonelessly, one arm flung above his head—and Ada almost believed he was only what he appeared to be. Seven years old, maybe eight. A smudge of soot on his cheekbone. Bare feet that had walked through three days of frost without leaving prints, though she hadn't understood what that meant until later, standing at the door at dawn and looking back down the steps at nothing.

Then he began to dream out loud.

Not words, at first. Sounds. A creak of hinges rendered perfectly in a child's throat—the particular groan of the guild's front door, which Ada knew down to the pitch of it because she oiled it herself every month and no one else ever did. Then footsteps. Then a voice, low and formal, reciting something with the cadence of an oath.

Ada set down her pen.

The voice coming out of the boy was not his own. It was older, rougher, worn at the edges like a coin passed through ten thousand hands. And she recognized it—not the speaker, but the *room*. The acoustics of it. She had spent six years alone in this hall; she knew how sound behaved here, how it pooled in the corners and came back slightly delayed off the stone. The boy's dreaming mouth reproduced it exactly: the founding hall, before the shelves were rearranged, before the east windows were bricked over. He was speaking the room as it had been two hundred years ago.

"Founding night," she whispered, and felt foolish, and kept whispering anyway. "You're dreaming founding night."

There were no records of founding night. That was the first thing every apprentice learned: the guild began with its ledgers, and the ledgers began mid-entry, as if the guild had simply always been there, keeping accounts of things that shouldn't exist. Bottled echoes. Borrowed years. The sealed grief on the third shelf that hummed when it rained. No founder's name. no charter, no first page. The guild had no birthday. It had only a balance.

But the boy had one.

He talked in his sleep for two hours, and Ada wrote down everything, her hand moving faster than her doubt. He described the room she knew and the room she didn't: forty chairs, each with a name burned into the backrest in a script she couldn't read but somehow recognized the shape of, the way you recognize a face from a country you've never visited. He described the smell—beeswax and hot iron and, underneath it, something sweet and wrong, like fruit going bad in a locked cellar. He described the fire.

"The fire was small," he said, in the old man's voice, in the old language, and Ada understood him the way you understand a song—you don't translate it, you just know. "They carried it up from below in a lantern with no handle. It wanted to go home. They would not let it."

Her pen stopped. Ink pooled into a blot shaped like a closed eye.

*They stole it,* she thought, and immediately hated herself for thinking it, because it was absurd, because the guild was many things—cold, indifferent, a building that had never once learned her name—but it wasn't a thief. It was a keeper. That was the whole word. Keeper.

The boy rolled onto his side. In his sleep his face changed—not aged, exactly, but *occupied*, the way a house is occupied, and you can tell when the person inside has lived there a very long time. His breathing went slow and deliberate. And then, in the flat tone of someone reading aloud from a document, he said:

"They asked whose soul would light it."

Ada's mouth went dry.

"There was a woman at the table," the dream-voice continued. "She had signed first. Her signature is the anchor of the whole contract; every line after it bends toward her name. She stood up and said, 'We will not give our own. We will hold one in trust. A debt deferred is a debt designed.' And they brought in a man who was already sleeping—he slept through all of it, he never woke—and they opened him the way you open a book to the middle, and they took the page that was burning, and they put it in the fire, and the fire grew, and everyone agreed it was a fair price because no one in the room had paid it."

The lamp guttered. Outside, the frost ticked against the shutters.

The boy sighed, a long exhale that seemed to come from somewhere beneath his ribs, some cellar deeper than a child should have, and murmured a string of syllables in the dead language—soft ones, this time, almost gentle. Then he was quiet.

Ada sat very still for a long time. Then she looked down at her notes and saw that her hand, without her permission, had transcribed the oath he'd spoken at the beginning—the hinge-creak, the footsteps, the formal voice—in characters she had never learned. They sat on the page in her own handwriting, neat and correct, and she understood them completely, and she could not have told you why.

The first line read: *Let what we take be held, not spent.*

The second line read: *And let the holding be forever.*

He woke at noon with no memory of any of it. He woke hungry, cheerful, and utterly ordinary, demanding bread and asking questions about everything—the pulley system for the upper shelves, the purpose of the sealed grief ("it's sad," Ada told him, and he nodded gravely, satisfied), whether the bottled echoes could talk to each other if you put them on the same shelf. She let him arrange a row of them while she worked, and the echoes murmured to one another in voices two decades dead, and the boy giggled, and Ada thought: *this is fine. This can be fine. I'll find another answer. There's always another entry.*

Then, in the late afternoon, while she was rebinding a water-damaged inventory of forgotten birthdays, he climbed onto the stool beside her, watched her work for a while, and said:

"You hold your pen the way she did."

Ada didn't look up. "Who?"

"The one who isn't here." He said it without sadness, the way children say true things. "Your hands are hers. When you're counting, your lips move. Hers moved too."

"My grandmother died before I was born," Ada said. "I never met her."

"I know," the boy said, kindly, as if correcting a small error in a ledger. "But she met you. She used to come here and sit where you sit and audit the impossible things, and she always saved the sealed grief for last because it made her cry and she liked to end the day having cried already." He swung his feet. "She talked about you. About the granddaughter she'd have. She said you'd have her hands."

The shop went very quiet. Somewhere upstairs a pipe knocked twice and went silent, as pipes do, as if embarrassed.

"That's not possible," Ada said finally. Her voice came out level, which she counted as a triumph. "You're seven years old."

"I'm not," he said, agreeably. "But I'm also this." He tapped his own chest. "It's like being a book with a lot of pages. Right now you're reading the early ones. But the pages remember the whole story even when you're only on chapter five." He frowned suddenly, concentrating. "Is this chapter five? It feels like chapter five."

"How do you know my grandmother?" Ada asked. "How do you know *any* of this?"

He considered the question with the seriousness of a magistrate. "I was there," he said. "For the founding. For the first soul. For all of them, after." He said it the way you'd admit to being tired, or hungry—a fact about himself, neither proud nor ashamed. "I'm the part that gets remembered. Everything that happens near me, I keep. Even things that haven't happened yet. Especially those."

"Remember forward," Ada repeated. "You said that. The first night."

"It's the same as remembering backward," he said. "Only lonelier."

She should have sent for Vess then. She knew that. Every rule in the guild—every rule she'd sworn to uphold, every procedure drilled into her during those first terrible months when the senior Keepers still bothered to notice she existed—said that an anomaly of this magnitude went upstairs immediately, that the ledger decided, that the ledger always decided. Instead she closed the damaged inventory, took the boy's cold hand in hers, and asked him the question she'd been carrying since dawn:

"What's my grandmother's name?"

He looked at her with those eyes that were too old by several centuries and said, gently:

"You already know it. You've been writing it in the margins of your notes since you were twelve. You think nobody checks the margins." He smiled. "She checked the margins. That's how she found the first discrepancy."

Ada's breath caught. Because it was true. Since childhood she'd had a habit—doodling, she'd called it, a nervous tic—of writing a certain name in the margins of everything, over and over, a name that belonged to a woman in a single fading portrait in the guild's west stairwell, a woman whose face Ada had loved without reason her whole life. She'd assumed it was grief for someone she'd never met. An orphan's invented ancestor.

The boy watched her understand it.

"She left something for you," he said. "In the vault. Under the floor of the third aisle. She couldn't bring it up—the others were watching, even then—but she marked it. You'll know the mark. You've been drawing it in the margins too, next to the name. You thought it was a decoration."

"It *is* a decoration," Ada said automatically, and then heard how young she sounded, how much she wanted it to be untrue, because if it was untrue then the world was still the world, the guild was still a guild, and the child beside her was only a lost boy and not whatever he actually was.

"It's a map," the boy said.

Outside, the light was failing. Dusk came early now; Ada had noticed it in the ledgers without understanding—days shortening in the records themselves, appointments logged at hours that no longer existed, borrowed years coming due faster than they were lent. The guild's whole inventory was drifting out of alignment, like a ship whose ballast had shifted, and she had been too busy tallying to ask why the horizon kept moving.

The boy slid off the stool and stood at the dark window, looking north, toward the mountain that wasn't visible from anywhere in the city but that everyone knew was there anyway, the way you know about a bruise through clothing.

"He's getting hungry," the boy said quietly.

"Who is?"

"The fire." He didn't turn around. "It eats memory. That's all fire is—it's memory that decided to keep going. And the mountain fire is very old and very hungry and it's starting to reach for things farther away." He touched the glass with one finger, and for a moment—Ada would swear to this for the rest of her life—for a moment the frost on the window rearranged itself into rows of names, thousands of them, tiny and precise, before melting back into ordinary ice. "When it gets hungry enough, it stops caring whose memory it eats. Yours. Mine. The city's. It'll start forgetting the borders between things, and then the things will stop bothering to be separate."

He turned and looked up at her, and his face was a child's face, and his eyes were not.

"You should get the thing from under the floor," he said. "Before the others remember it's there."

*The others.* Ada crossed to the window and looked north with him, at nothing, at the place where the mountain waited. Behind her, on the desk, the contract page turned itself over with a soft papery sigh, as if settling in for a long stay.

Down in the vault, under the floor of the third aisle, something her grandmother had hidden was waiting. And somewhere above her, boots on the upper gallery—Vess, or one of the others, making their evening rounds, checking on assets.

Ada took the boy's hand.

"We go tonight," she said.
