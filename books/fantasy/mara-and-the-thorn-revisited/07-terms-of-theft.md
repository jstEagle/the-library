## 7. Terms of Theft

The vault book did not sleep.

Ada had assumed it would—had hoped, in some cowardly corner of herself, that when she climbed back down the spiral stair with a candle and a plan, the thing bound in something warm would have gone still, its pages slack, its attention elsewhere. Books slept. Ledgers especially. A ledger was a record, and records were the past, and the past had no eyes.

But when she set her candle on the reading stand beside it, the true ledger turned its own page to meet her.

*There you are,* said the movement of it. Not words. The book didn't speak; it *inclined*, the way a person inclines their head when you enter a room they've been waiting in. Ada had grown up around ledgers that behaved like this—the guild's ordinary books rustled when lied to, warmed when balanced—but this one was different. This one was not a record of transactions. This one was a transaction, still ongoing, still warm, still *owing*.

"Good morning," she said, and immediately hated how small it sounded. She was alone in a vault beneath a guildhall built for forty Keepers, addressing a book made of stolen souls, and she had opened with pleasantries.

The page before her showed the same heading it had shown the night before, in letters that were not ink but something older and redder, like light through a closed eyelid:

**PAID IN FULL TO THE COURT BENEATH**

And below it, in columns as neat as anything her masters had ever taught her to write: names. Thousands of them. Bottled echoes with names attached. Borrowed years with names attached. And souls—actual, whole, unrepeatable souls—each one entered with a date of sale, a price paid, and a small notation in the margin that Ada had spent the whole night above, pacing her narrow room, trying not to think about.

The notation was a single word, repeated down every column, patient as water wearing stone:

*satisfied.*

She found her grandmother's name again. She had known she would look for it. She had told herself she wouldn't, that she'd stay disciplined, work the ledger top to bottom like a proper audit, and then she had opened the book and gone straight to the I's like a girl checking a letter had come.

**IVERSEN, MAREN — soul, whole — sold 61 years past — satisfied.**

Sixty-one years. Her grandmother had died two winters ago at ninety-one, sharp-tongued and warm-handed, teaching Ada to double-entry her first impossible inventory in this very hall. All those years, and somewhere beneath the mountain a flame had been burning with the better part of her, and Ada had never once felt the draft.

"I'm going to steal them back," she said to the book.

The pages went very still. Stillness, from this book, was not neutral. It was the stillness of an accountant hearing a number that doesn't add up.

"You heard me." Ada pulled the reading stool closer and sat, the way she sat for difficult reconciliations, spine straight, hands flat on the desk. "Every entry. Every name. I don't care what it takes or how long. This guild has been selling people to keep a fire lit, and I am going to un-sell every single one of them, starting with—" her finger hovered over the entry, and she made herself touch it, made herself feel the warmth of the page under her grandmother's name, which was either her imagination or the worst kindness the book had yet done her—"*starting with her.*"

The book turned a page.

It did this gently, which somehow made it worse. It turned the page the way Vess used to turn the ledger toward a client who had just proposed something foolish, presenting the relevant clause without comment, letting arithmetic do the cruelty so no one had to be cruel.

The new page was headed, in the same red-under-eyelid script:

**TERMS OF RECOVERY**

Below it, the book began to write. Not print—write, in a hand that shifted as she watched, cycling through scripts she recognized from the guild's historical folios: the angular hand of the founding generation, the looped hand of the expansion years, the cramped modern hand of the last century of decline. As if the book had been written by everyone who had ever kept it, and could borrow any of their pens.

*The Guild asks: can a sold thing be unsold?*

"No," Ada said flatly. "That's the wrong question. Can a stolen thing be returned? Yes. That's basic."

*The Guild taught you well,* the page wrote, and the hand it used for the sentence was her grandmother's.

Ada's breath went out of her all at once. She gripped the edge of the reading stand until the wood creaked. "Don't do that."

*You asked me to start with her,* the book wrote, in the same hand, softer now. *I am only showing you what she wrote. Article Nine of the Founding Charter, in her copy. She annotated it. Would you like to see her annotations?*

Ada wanted to say no. She wanted to close the book, climb the stairs, and go on wanting things she couldn't have. Instead she said, "Show me," because she was a Ledgerkeeper, and Ledgerkeepers read the contract.

The page filled with a facsimile—she understood, distantly, that the book was reproducing it from memory, from sixty years of holding the original against its own binding—and there it was: Article Nine, the recovery clause, drafted by the founders themselves in the guild's first optimistic decade, when they still believed everything they took could be given back.

She read it three times. Each time, the last sentence got heavier.

*A debt recorded may be recovered by repayment, by forfeiture, or by transfer. It cannot be erased. What the Court has bought, the Court owns until the account is settled; and an account is settled only by payment in kind, by payment in equivalent, or by the assumption of the debt by a party of sufficient standing.*

"Transfer," Ada said aloud, testing the word the way you test ice.

*Transfer,* the book agreed. *A debt unpaid can be moved. Never removed. You cannot burn an obligation out of existence. You can only sign your own name where another name was.*

"That's—" She stopped. Started again. "That's not theft, then. That's substitution. I steal the entries back and the court isn't robbed, it's *reassigned*. Someone still owes them. Someone always owes them."

*Now you are doing arithmetic,* the book wrote, and if a book could sound sad, it sounded sad. *This is why the founders wrote Article Nine last, and in small letters, and hoped no one would read it. They knew what they had built. A machine that does not destroy debts. Only moves them.*

Ada sat with that. The candle burned. Somewhere far above, through layers of stone and floorboards, the guildhall creaked in the wind, empty except for her and a sleeping child who spoke a language older than kingdoms and whose name was listed, in this very book, as an asset.

"The boy," she said. "If his soul is sold—if the court collects him—does the fire get a century?"

*The Court's price list is not mine to quote,* the book wrote. *But yes. A whole soul, young and unspent, buys more than any flame now on their shelves. He is the finest collateral this guild has produced in four hundred years. That is why they are patient. That is why the fire dims slowly enough for them to wait.*

"And if instead of him—" Her voice did something she didn't authorize. It stayed level, but it went somewhere else, somewhere colder and higher, like a room in winter. "If instead of him, someone else's name went into the ledger as surety. Someone of sufficient standing. Someone the court would accept as equivalent."

The book did not answer immediately. When it did, it wrote in the founding hand, formal as a tombstone:

*The Court accepts surety from any party who understands the terms fully and signs freely. This is the oldest rule they keep. Even they honor it.*

"Then I understand the terms."

*You understand the words,* the book wrote. *Understanding comes later, and it is worse.*

"Don't." Ada stood up. The stool scraped back across the stone. "Don't do the wise old book routine. Don't tell me I don't know what I'm asking. I know exactly what I'm asking. I'm asking whether I can put my name where his is, and you're telling me yes, and that's the only part of this I need tonight."

*It is not the only part,* the book wrote, and now the hand was hers—her own handwriting, lifted off a hundred practice exercises, a hundred audits, the margins of her apprenticeship. *Here is the rest. If you transfer yourself, the boy goes free, and the fire burns another lifetime on you, and the guild keeps its ledgers, and in eighty years some other apprentice finds this book and reads these names and sits on this stool and weeps. Nothing ends. The machine runs on volunteers now. Is that theft? Or is it maintenance?*

"I'll steal the others too," she said. "All of them. Every entry. I'll take the whole ledger back and give the court nothing but ash."

*With what hands?* the book asked. *You are one person. Your guild is three people and a building. The Court Beneath has been collecting since before your language had a word for *no*.*

"Then I'll find hands." She was pacing now, three steps and turn, three steps and turn, the way she paced when a reconciliation wouldn't balance. "I owe someone a call-in. Bram Ferro. He's disgraced, he's brilliant, and he owes me a life-debt from the Vellum job, and he's exactly dishonest enough to be useful stealing from honest thieves. And there's a woman at the edge of the map who sold her sorrow and might remember the road down. And there's—" she stopped pacing. "And there's whatever the boy is. Because the book opened itself to him, didn't it? The night he knocked. You knew him before I brought him in."

The pages trembled. Actually trembled, a shiver running through the vellum like a horse feeling a storm come.

*He is not in my ledger as a debtor,* the book wrote, very slowly, each character forming with the care of someone handling something breakable. *Look again at his entry.*

Ada flipped back. Found the page. The boy's entry sat near the front of the current century's section, where high-value assets were kept:

**[NAME WITHHELD PENDING COLLECTION] — soul, whole, unspent — held as collateral against the Debt of Founding — payable when the fire dims.**

She had read it a dozen times since the knock at the door. But now, with the recovery clause fresh in her mind, she read the marginal notation. On every sold soul, the margin said *satisfied*. On the boy's entry, the margin said something else, in the red-under-eyelid script, in letters so old they predated the guild's founding hand:

*owed.*

Not satisfied. Not even pending. *Owed*—as though the direction of the debt ran the wrong way, as though somewhere in the arithmetic of the founding night a sign had been flipped and nobody, in four hundred years of impeccable bookkeeping, had caught the error.

"What does that mean?" she whispered. "*Owed.* Owed by whom?"

The book turned its own page before she could touch it. It wrote one line, in no hand she recognized—a hand older than the founding, older than the charter, a hand like cracks in glacier ice:

*Ask him what the first word of his language means. Then ask yourself why a court that owns everything would leave its most valuable asset walking free at a guild's door for seven years, waiting to be collected.*

Ada stared at the line until the candle guttered and steadied.

"You're telling me to go down there anyway."

*I am telling you the terms,* the book wrote, returning to her own handwriting, tired now, human now. *You cannot erase the debt. You can transfer it. You can contest it. Or—you can find out what happens when the creditor calls it due. But hear me, Keeper's-last-apprentice: the Court honors contracts absolutely. Which means if you walk through their gate offering yourself as surety, they will take you. There is no cleverness that survives a signature given freely. So decide what you are signing before you sign it. Decide whether you are their collateral—or their auditor.*

"The difference being?"

*An auditor arrives uninvited, reads everything, and leaves with the truth. Collateral stays.*

Ada looked at her grandmother's name one more time. **Satisfied**, said the margin. Sixty years of satisfaction, and the woman had died anyway with half of herself underground, and no one had ever told her, and no one had ever asked her permission, and that—that more than the fire, more than the court, more than the whole rotten elegant machine of it—was the thing Ada could not reconcile. It didn't balance. It had never balanced. And she was the last apprentice of the Ledgerkeepers, and balancing the unbalanceable was the entire job.

"Then here are my terms," she said to the book. "Write them down. You're good at writing things down."

The pages turned to a fresh leaf and waited.

"One: I contest every entry in this ledger. Two: I offer myself as surety of last resort—not first resort, *last*—and only if every other road fails. Three: nobody's soul gets sold while I'm working, including mine, including his, and if the guild tries, I will burn this building down around the ledgers before I let another signature happen. Four—" her voice caught, and she let it, because a term entered honestly should carry its cost—"*four: my grandmother comes home. First. Before me, before the boy, before anyone. Whatever the order of operations says. She goes first.*"

The book wrote it all down. When it finished, it added, unprompted, in her grandmother's hand:

*She would have hated this plan. She would also have been proud. Those are compatible. Sign here.*

A blank line appeared at the bottom of the page, and beneath it, the words:

**SURETY OF LAST RESORT — offered freely, understood poorly, meant completely.**

Ada laughed—one short, cracked note that echoed off the vault stones—and picked up the candle, and touched the flame to her own fingertip, and pressed it to the line. The wax stung. The book drank the mark in like rain on dry ground.

*Witnessed,* it wrote. *Filed. Now go up, Apprentice. The boy is dreaming again, and in his dream, someone is calling your name—an old one, a wrong one—and you should be awake to hear it, because the dead only lend us their names for so long.*

Ada climbed the stairs with the candle shaking in her hand, and did not let herself think about what she had just signed, because thinking could wait and the boy could not.

Above her, faint through the floorboards, a child's voice was saying a name in the dark.

It was not *Ada.*

* * *

By dawn she had a list.

She wrote it on the back of an inventory sheet, in pencil, because pencil could be erased and ink couldn't and she was done making permanent marks for one night. At the top: **BRAM FERRO — life-debt, callable.** Below that: **the inn-woman, if she exists, if the road holds.** Below that: **the boy — ask him the first word.** And at the bottom, after a long pause, in letters pressed hard enough to groove the paper: **ME — last resort. Not first. Last.**

She read it over. It was a bad plan. It was the kind of plan that got apprentices expelled from guilds less corrupt than hers, assuming there were guilds less corrupt than hers. It required a disgraced thief, a half-faded stranger, a child who remembered forward, and a signature she'd already given and couldn't take back, all pointed at a court that honored contracts with the devotion other institutions reserved for gods.

It was, she thought, the only honest ledger she'd ever kept. Four lines, no hidden clauses, and every debt on it meant to be broken.

She folded the list into her pocket, woke the boy gently, and asked him, kneeling so their eyes were level, the question the vault book had sent up the stairs with her.

"What's the first word of your language?"

The child looked at her for a long moment with eyes that had seen the founding night. Then he smiled—not a child's smile, not quite—and answered in the dead tongue, one word, round and old and heavy as a cornerstone.

And Ada, who had spent her whole life learning languages of numbers, felt the meaning arrive in her chest before her mind could translate it, cold and enormous, like a door opening onto winter:

*the one who is owed.*

She stood up very slowly. Outside, the mountain stood against the sunrise, patient as a creditor.

"Oh," she said quietly. "Oh, you're not the collateral at all, are you?"

The boy tilted his head, waiting, the way creditors wait.

"We're going to need a thief," Ada said.
