## 8. The Last Honest Thief

Bram Tolliver was three cups into a bad morning when the knock came.

He knew it was her before he opened the door. Nobody else knocked like that—twice, precise, like a signature on a document. Like she was filing something. He'd spent six years trying to forget that knock, and there it was, patient as interest accruing on his door.

He considered not answering. He considered the window, which was one story up and over a gutter he'd have to trust with his life, and he had learned the hard way what trusting things got you. Then he considered that Ada Iversen had once pulled him out of a flooded counting-house by the collar of his coat while the water rose around ledgers that screamed when they got wet—he still didn't like thinking about that—and that a woman who did a thing like that tended to keep doing things like that until you paid her back or died.

He opened the door.

"You look terrible," Ada said.

"You look like you haven't slept in a week," Bram said. "Which for you is an improvement."

She didn't smile. That was the first wrong thing. The second was that she wasn't wearing guild colors. Six years since they'd thrown him out, and he could still see the gray-and-gold in his sleep—the way the tailors folded it, the way the senior Keepers wore their sashes like they were holding their own spines straight. Ada stood in the doorway in plain wool, hood back, hair escaping its braid, and behind her on the street a child of about seven held her hand and looked at Bram's door with the expression of someone reading a sign in a language only he couldn't read.

"Can I come in?" Ada said. "It's not safe to talk on the street."

"That's what everyone says right before it becomes my problem."

"Bram."

He stepped aside. Because of course he did.

* * *

His rooms were above a chandler's shop that smelled permanently of tallow and regret. He cleared a chair of laundry, then of knives, then gave up and set the knives on the floor where they could watch. The boy sat very still on the bench by the window, hands folded, watching everything—the knives especially—with an attention no seven-year-old should have. Not fear. Inventory.

Ada sat across from Bram and put a wrapped bundle on the table between them. She didn't unwrap it.

"Do you remember the Vellum Street job?" she said.

"I remember you carrying me out of it." He kept his voice flat. "I remember the water. I remember that I owed the guild a confession and you took the blame instead, and they believed you because you were the last apprentice and I was already half-disgraced, and they broke you down to scullery work and me out the door entirely." He turned his cup. "I've done the arithmetic every day for six years, Ada. It doesn't come out even. You saved my life twice and ruined your standing once, and I never paid any of it back."

"I know," she said. "That's why I'm here."

"To collect?"

"To hire."

He laughed. It came out ugly. "Hire me. The guild's last apprentice walks into the house of the guild's most famous failure and offers him work. What is it? Retrieval? Someone's stolen a bottled echo and the seniors are too dignified to chase it themselves?"

"No."

"Then what?"

Ada looked at the boy. The boy looked at Ada, and nodded, once, like a colleague.

"We're going to steal something back," she said. "From the court beneath the mountain."

Bram put his cup down very carefully, because his hand had started to disagree with him about steady. "There is no court beneath the mountain. That's a story we tell apprentices to keep them from wandering off the ledger roads. There's no such thing as—"

"There is," the boy said.

Bram stopped.

The voice was small and clear and wrong—not wrong like a child lying, wrong like a bell rung in a room built for silence. And the language wasn't Common. It wasn't anything. It was older than anything, syllables that seemed to arrive slightly before he heard them, and yet he understood it the way you understand a fall before you hit the ground.

"He does that," Ada said quietly. "You get used to it. You don't, actually, but people say you do."

"What did he say?"

"He said there is. He'd know." She unwrapped the bundle at last.

Bram had seen a lot of books in his life. He'd stolen books, fenced books, burned one book he still felt bad about, and been beaten with several more. He knew what old books looked like: cracked leather, foxed pages, the smell of dust and other people's patience.

The book on his table was none of those things. It was bound in something warm-colored and faintly moving, and as he leaned closer he realized the cover was breathing. Slowly. Patiently. The way a sleeping animal breathes.

"Don't touch it," Ada said. "Just look."

He looked. The page facing up was written in ink that gleamed like it was still wet after—who knew how long. At the top, in letters neat as a magistrate's hand:

**PAID IN FULL TO THE COURT BENEATH**

And below it, names. Columns of them. Hundreds. Thousands. Each with a date, a sum, and a small notation in the margin that Bram's eye, trained on contracts and confessions, parsed before his stomach did.

*Received. Received. Received.*

"This is a joke," he said. "This is some kind of test. They sent you to see if I'd gone soft, if I'd believe any—"

"It's the guild's true ledger," Ada said. "Not the one in the hall. The real one. Every soul the Ledgerkeepers ever sold to keep the dragon-fire lit. Founders' signatures on the original contract. My grandmother's name is in there, Bram. Page forty-one."

The room was very quiet. Downstairs, the chandler was singing to himself, something cheerful and tuneless, and the ordinariness of it made everything worse.

"The fire under the mountain," Bram said slowly. "The one the stories say holds the borders of the world in place. The one the whole map hangs on."

"Sells souls as fuel," Ada said. "Has for generations. Quietly. Bookkept beautifully. And now it's nearly out, and the guild wants to feed it the boy."

Bram looked at the child. The child looked back, unblinking, and Bram had the sudden vertiginous certainty that those eyes had watched this exact conversation happen before, in some dream, some memory running the wrong direction.

"And the guild sent you to stop this?" he said. "Or did you steal the book?"

"I stole the book," Ada said. "And the guild doesn't know I'm here yet. But it will. Keeper Vess knows I know, and Vess doesn't let go of assets." Her jaw tightened. "I need a thief, Bram. The best one the guild ever threw away. Because I'm going to take every name out of that book and give them back, and I can't do it alone, and you're the only person I trust who has reason to hate them as much as I do."

Bram sat back. He thought about saying no. He ran through all the excellent reasons—the danger, the impossibility, the fact that the court beneath the mountain was a bedtime story and bedtime stories ate people—and he heard himself say, instead:

"Why me? You could hire anyone. Half the free companies in the city would do it for the notoriety."

"Because they'd do it for the notoriety," Ada said. "And because you owe me a life-debt, and life-debts are the one currency the guild never figured out how to ledger." She almost smiled. Almost. "Because when the water was rising in the counting-house, you went back for the junior clerk's ledger instead of the strongbox. Everyone thought you were mad. I knew what you were."

"Honest," Bram said bitterly.

"The last honest thief," Ada said. "That's why it has to be you."

He looked at the book again. It breathed. Somewhere in those columns, page forty-one, a woman named Maren Iversen sat in ink, sold and received and filed, and Bram thought about his own mother, whose face he could barely hold onto anymore, and wondered if somewhere there was a line with her on it too, and whether anyone had ever gotten a receipt.

"If I say yes," he said, "what's the job, exactly? Steal the book back from the mountain?"

"Worse," Ada said. "Steal every entry in it. One soul at a time if we have to. From a court that considers contracts sacred and has been collecting for four hundred years."

Bram laughed again, but it came out different this time—thinner, and underneath it, unwillingly, bright. It was the laugh he used to get before good jobs, back when he was young and stupid and the guild still pretended to value him. The laugh of a man about to do something unforgivable for reasons that were finally, finally clean.

"They'll hang us," he said.

"Probably."

"The Magistrate—if it exists—will be polite about it, which is worse."

"Almost certainly."

"And the world might come apart at the seams if we pull the wrong thread."

"That," Ada said, "is still being determined."

The boy spoke again in the dead language, and this time Bram caught enough of the shape of it to feel the meaning land in his chest like a stone dropped down a well: *the one who is owed will walk with us.* Or maybe he made that up. Maybe understanding the boy was just deciding to.

Bram stood up. He went to the corner where a floorboard had been loose since before he rented the room, pried it up, and took out the only thing he'd kept from his guild years: a set of picks forged from a broken ledger-clasp, gray steel with a thread of gold in it. He weighed them in his palm.

"Terms," he said. "One: nobody calls this a rescue mission. Rescues go wrong. This is a retrieval, and retrievals have structure. Two: I plan the entries, you plan the ethics, and when those conflict, we argue fast and decide faster. Three—" he looked at the boy, who was watching the picks with that same terrible inventory-attention— "nobody uses the child as collateral. Not the guild, not the court, not us. Whatever else happens."

"Agreed," Ada said, without hesitation, and the relief in her voice told him she'd been braced to bargain for that one and had prepared to lose.

"Then I'm in," Bram said. "Against my considerable better judgment, which I want noted for the record."

"Noted," Ada said. "I'll write it down."

"Don't you dare write anything down. That's how they get you."

For a moment—one brief, unguarded moment—they both laughed, two discarded instruments of a dying guild, and the sound surprised them both, and the boy smiled like he'd been waiting years to hear it.

Then the book on the table turned its own page.

They went silent. The breathing cover stilled. New ink surfaced on the fresh leaf, writing itself in that wet gleam, and Bram read it upside down and felt the morning go cold.

**ONE ENTRY HAS ALREADY BEGUN TO MOVE. THE COURT EXPECTS DELIVERY OF THE CHILD WITHIN THE MONTH. A KEEPER RIDES BEHIND YOU.**

Ada's face went white. "Vess," she whispered. "She's already coming."

Bram picked up his coat and his picks and his knives off the floor, one by one, like a man arming for a war he'd sworn he'd retired from.

"Then we ride faster," he said.
