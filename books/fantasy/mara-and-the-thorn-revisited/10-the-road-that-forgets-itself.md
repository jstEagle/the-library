## 10. The Road That Forgets Itself

Bram had stolen from kings, cheated a bishop at dice, and once carried a sleeping countess out of her own burning manor on the strength of a promise he'd made while drunk. He considered himself a man who knew roads.

The road to the mountain did not know itself.

They'd been walking three days when he first noticed it: a milestone they'd passed yesterday, standing on the wrong side of the track, its carved numeral worn smooth as if the stone had forgotten what it was counting. He stopped, squinted at it, and said nothing, because saying things aloud in strange country was how you got laughed at by girls and children alike, and Bram Vetch had a reputation to maintain, even with an audience of two.

By the fourth day he couldn't pretend anymore.

"Bram." Ada's voice came from ahead, flat and careful, the voice she used when the ledger-sense in her was doing something she didn't like. "The bridge."

There was no bridge. There had been a bridge yesterday—he remembered it clearly, a humpbacked thing of grey stone with moss on the northern parapet and a beggar's cup wedged between two cobbles, empty, which had struck him as a bad omen for their whole enterprise. In its place stood a ford, shallow and brown, with stepping stones that looked like they'd been there since the world was young.

"That's not possible," Bram said.

"Nothing about this is possible," Ada said. "That's rather the point of us."

Behind them, the child hummed.

He'd been humming since dawn—no, singing, though the sounds weren't words Bram knew. They were rounder than words, older, the kind of sound you felt in your back teeth. The boy walked with his eyes half-closed, one hand trailing along the air as if it were a wall only he could feel, and where his hand passed, Bram would have sworn the light bent slightly, the way heat bends off summer stones.

"Ask him," Bram said. "Ask him which way."

"He doesn't need asking," Ada said. "Listen."

And Bram listened, properly, for the first time. The song wasn't random. It rose and fell like something following a line—a thread through a maze—and every time the boy reached a bend in the melody, within a hundred paces the land itself obliged him with a bend in the path.

"He's not following the road," Bram said slowly. "The road's following him."

Ada didn't answer, but her jaw did that thing it did when she was filing a fact away in some internal ledger of her own, and Bram thought, not for the first time, that if anyone ever stole *her*, the guild would want her back more than any soul in their books.

* * *

They camped that night in the lee of a hill that hadn't been there that morning. Bram built the fire because building fires was the one domestic art he possessed, and Ada fed the boy dried plums and pretended not to watch him the way you pretend not to watch a candle guttering in a room full of papers.

"Tell me again why fewer souls means fewer roads," Bram said. "I keep turning it over and it keeps coming out nonsense."

"Because places are held in place by being remembered." Ada poked the fire. "That's what Vess told me, before we left—or what I dragged out of her, which amounts to the same thing. A road exists because thousands of people remember walking it. Their memories are like... stakes. Tent pegs. Pin the road down so it can't wander. But the court beneath has been buying memories for centuries. Every soul sold is a thousand small rememberings gone with it. The pegs come up. The tent moves."

"So the country's coming loose."

"The country's coming loose," Ada agreed. "Slowly. From the edges inward. The inn-woman's village—we saw how far gone that was. Out here it's subtler. Roads first. Then landmarks. Then—" she gestured vaguely at the dark beyond the firelight, "—then whatever wants to stop existing does."

"And the mountain?"

"The mountain's the last thing standing. The fire holds everything else up. Which is why they're feeding it."

Bram chewed on this, and on a strip of salt mutton, and watched the boy. The child sat cross-legged closest to the flames, hands open on his knees, and the firelight did something odd around him—it leaned toward him, subtly, the way plants lean toward sun. When he sang his low evening song, the flames kept time.

"You know what he is, don't you," Bram said quietly. "You've known since the vault."

"I know what the book says he is."

"Which is?"

"A debt." She said it simply, without flinching, and Bram admired that even as it chilled him. "Or maybe the opposite of a debt. I don't think the old language distinguishes them the way ours does. Owed and owing might be the same word."

"That's no language," Bram said. "That's a marriage."

For the first time in days, Ada laughed—a short, surprised bark, quickly smothered, but real. Bram counted it a victory and banked the fire.

* * *

On the sixth day the road gave up entirely.

They crested a rise at midmorning and looked down into a valley that should have held a market town—Bram knew it should, because he'd fenced goods through that town twice in his misspent youth, and had fond memories of a pie shop near the well. Instead there was a smear. That was the only word for it. A grey-green blur in the shape of a town, like wet ink someone had tried to blot up. You could see the suggestion of rooftops. You could almost hear, if you strained, the ghost of market noise, thin as wind through reeds.

The boy stopped singing.

It was the first time Bram had heard silence come off him, and it landed on all three of them like a dropped weight. The child stared down at the smear with an expression no seven-year-old face should have been able to make—an expression of recognition, and grief, and something older than both, something like *accounting*.

"What is it?" Ada knelt beside him. "What do you see?"

He spoke then—not the dead language but plain trade-tongue, which he'd been learning from them word by word, hoarding vocabulary like a magpie. His accent was strange, each word placed with the care of a man laying stones.

"They are not gone," he said. "They are *unpaid*. There is a difference. Gone is finished. Unpaid is waiting." He touched his own chest, over the heart. "Like me. Before your door."

Ada went very still. Bram watched her hand drift toward the satchel where she kept the copy of the vault book's pages—the pages she'd transcribed by lamplight, name after name after name under that heading that still turned his stomach: PAID IN FULL TO THE COURT BENEATH.

"How many are in that valley?" she asked gently.

The boy frowned, working at the question, and finally answered in the dead language—one soft syllable—and then, seeing their faces, tried again. "More than songs," he said. "Fewer than stars."

"We could go around," Bram offered. "Cut east, through the pass."

"There is no east," the boy said, matter-of-fact. "Not today. Today the east is remembering itself somewhere else. We go through. They would like to be sung past. It is polite."

So they went through.

Walking into the smear was like wading into water that had opinions. The air thickened; sounds arrived late; Bram's own footsteps seemed to reach his ears a half-beat after his boots struck ground. And everywhere, at the edge of hearing, the murmur—that market-ghost, that unfinished noise of a place holding its breath between existence and otherwise.

The boy sang the whole way. Low, steady, unhurried, the same phrase repeated like a hand laid on a frightened animal. And as they passed through the heart of the un-town, Bram saw them: flickers. Figures. A woman at a stall that was mostly suggestion, arranging fruit that was mostly color. A dog, briefly, gloriously solid, chasing a ball that never quite resolved. Children's laughter, one bright note, then gone.

Each flicker turned toward the boy as he passed. Not toward Ada, not toward Bram. Toward him. And each one, for just a moment, grew sharper—more *there*—as if his song were a lamp and they were moths deciding whether to live or die.

He was giving them something, Bram realized. With every note. He was spending himself.

"Boy." Bram caught his shoulder as they neared the far edge of the valley. "Stop spending. We need you whole."

The child looked up at him with those too-old eyes and said, in his careful, placed words: "Spending is what I am *for*. But—" and here something almost like mischief crossed his face, the first Bram had seen, and it made him look seven years old for exactly one heartbeat, "—I will spend slowly. For you. Because you carry me over rivers sometimes."

"I do not—"

"You do. On the second day. You complained the entire time."

Ada made the laughing-bark sound again. Bram scowled at them both and adjusted his pack, and if he walked a little closer to the boy for the rest of the afternoon, nobody remarked on it.

* * *

That night the road rearranged itself around their campfire while they slept, and Bram woke to find them facing a direction none of them had chosen.

He came awake the way he always did—knife already in hand, breath shallow, cataloguing exits—and found the landscape transformed. The pass they'd aimed for was now a day's walk north. The river they'd have followed had wandered westward overnight, leaving a bed of pale stones like a scar. Even the stars had shifted, subtly, the constellations pulled a finger's width off true, and that more than anything put ice down Bram's spine, because a man could cope with moving roads but the sky was supposed to hold still.

Only the fire hadn't moved. Only the boy hadn't moved. He sat exactly where he'd lain down, though Bram could have sworn he'd curled up asleep, and he was singing the dawn-song with his eyes closed, and around the little camp, in a perfect circle perhaps thirty feet across, the grass was green and firm and utterly ordinary.

Beyond the circle, the world blurred like wet ink.

"It holds when he sings," Ada said softly. She was awake, had perhaps never slept, watching the boy with an expression Bram was learning to read as her version of terror. "While he sings, we're pinned down. Real. The moment he stops—"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to. Bram looked at the blurred horizon, at the wandering riverbed, at the shy, apologetic stars, and understood what she couldn't say: that the child wasn't their compass. He was their anchor. Everything they were trying to free—the remembered souls, the paid-in-full names, the whole stolen inventory of the guild's long sin—all of it lived in that song somehow. He was made of the same stuff the missing pieces of the world were made of, and while he sang, the pieces remembered where they went.

"He can't sing forever," Bram said.

"No."

"And when he stops—"

"Then we'd better be inside the mountain," Ada said, "before the outside stops being anywhere at all."

* * *

They made nine miles that day, which was eight more than the map allowed, and camped in the ruins of a shrine whose god's name had worn off the altar stone—as if the stone itself had been asked to forget, and had obeyed. The boy liked the shrine. He pressed his palm flat against the blank altar and hummed a questioning note, and for a moment Bram would have sworn the stone warmed under his hand, straining toward a name it could no longer hold.

"Who was it?" Ada asked.

The boy shook his head. "Unpaid," he said. "Waiting."

Everything came back to that. Waiting. Bram lay back against his pack and stared at the shy stars and let himself do the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since the vault: thousands of names in the ledger, thousands of flames on shelves below the mountain, thousands of unpaid things waiting. And one small boy, spending himself note by note to keep three travelers stitched into a fraying world.

"You know what I can't get out of my head?" Bram said to the darkness, because Ada was asleep or pretending to be. "The Magistrate. Whatever's down there. Courteous, she said. Polite. Contracts sacred." He turned the phrase over. "A man like that doesn't lose an argument. He waits for you to break your word. And everyone breaks their word eventually. Everyone's got a price."

From across the dead fire, the boy's voice came softly, in his placed and careful words:

"He has a price," the child said. "Everyone says it is very large. It is not. It is very *old*."

Bram sat up. "What's that supposed to mean?"

But the boy had turned away, curling into sleep, and the last thing Bram heard before his own eyes shut was the low beginning of the night-song, holding the circle, holding the world, one patient note at a time.

In the morning, the road to the mountain appeared for the first time since they'd set out—straight, sure, and freshly remembered, as if something far ahead had finally decided to let them come.

Ada stood at the head of it for a long moment, satchel clutched to her chest, and Bram saw her lips move around two words he was fairly sure he wasn't meant to hear:

*"We're expected."*
