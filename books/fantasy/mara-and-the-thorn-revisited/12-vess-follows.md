## 12. Vess Follows

Keeper Vess had not traveled in thirty years, and her body reminded her of it within the first hour.

The road east of the guildhall was old cart-track gone soft with moss, the kind of road that existed only because no one had bothered to unmake it. She walked it with a staff she'd taken from the hall's umbrella stand—she refused to call it a walking stick; it was an instrument of posture—and with a satchel containing three things: the guild seal, a copy of the vault ledger's index, and a jar of preserved plums she would later be embarrassed about.

She was seventy-one years old. She had spent her life among numbers that did not behave like numbers, and she had learned this: when the arithmetic turns against you, you do not panic. You go to where the error is being made and you correct it at the source.

The error, in this case, was Ada Iversen.

* * *

Vess had watched the girl leave. That was the part no one would believe afterward—that she had simply watched. She had stood at the tall window of the counting room with her tea going cold and observed Ada slip out the postern gate with a stolen child, a disgraced thief, and, if Vess's reading of the empty shelf-cases was right, a hand-copy of the true ledger pressed flat between boards.

She could have raised the alarm. There were still six Keepers in the hall, and though five of them were ceremonial—a word Vess used internally to mean *useless*—the sixth, old Halloway, still had hands strong enough to break a door.

She did not raise the alarm.

Instead she finished her tea, washed the cup, dried it, set it back on its hook, and wrote a note for Halloway that said only: *Gone to audit. Back when accounts are settled.* Then she took the umbrella-stand staff and followed.

Because here was the thing about Keeper Vess, the thing the younger Keepers whispered about and got wrong: they thought she loved the ledger. She did not love the ledger. She loved what the ledger *did*. Every entry in those books, every impossible trade—the bottled echoes, the borrowed years, the sealed griefs—every one of them was a wall holding up a piece of the world. The borders between kingdoms held because someone remembered where they ran. The seasons turned because something beneath the mountain kept the count. And the fire that kept the count was fed, year after year, century after century, by souls the guild sold down into the dark.

Vess had signed off on forty-one such sales in her tenure. She knew each name. She kept them in a private memorandum book, in her own cipher, and once a year, on the longest night, she read the list aloud to herself in the empty hall, standing exactly where the founders had stood when they made the first bargain.

It was not penance. Penance implied she believed she'd done wrong.

It was *bookkeeping*. You did not let an asset pass without acknowledgment. Not even this kind of asset. Especially not this kind.

And now Ada Iversen—last apprentice, half-trained, sentimental, twenty-three years old and armed with nothing but grief and a hand-copied ledger—was walking toward the Court Beneath with the one soul whose recovery could unravel everything.

Vess calculated as she walked. It steadied her. Miles per day: fourteen, given her knees. Days to the mountain by the child's route, assuming the roads behaved: nine. Days remaining before the fire guttered past the point of negotiation: she did not know precisely, but the last reading from the deep gauge—taken eleven months ago, because no one else would go down to take it—had shown a flame the height of a thumb.

Weeks. Not years. Weeks.

So she walked faster than her knees approved of, and she ate the plums, and she did not think about the memorandum book sitting locked in her desk drawer with its forty-one names, because thinking about it produced a sensation in her chest that she had classified, long ago, as *inefficiency*, and Vess did not permit inefficiencies.

* * *

She caught them on the eighth day, at a place where the road crossed itself.

Not a crossroads—Vess had seen enough of those lately to know the difference. This was one road lying across its own path, the way a snake lies across itself, running north-south and also east-west along the same stretch of dirt. Bram stood in the middle of it scratching his head. The child sat on a milestone that read two different distances to the same town. And Ada was arguing with the landscape in the particular tone of a person who has been arguing with it for some time.

Vess stepped out from behind a leaning birch and said, "Your left boot is coming apart."

Ada spun. Bram's hand went to his knife and then, recognizing her, went instead to his hair, which he smoothed in a gesture of pure reflexive guilt. The child looked up at Vess with eyes that were wrong for a seven-year-old—old water in a young well—and said nothing, because he never said anything in any tongue she could use.

"You," Ada said. "You followed us."

"I walked behind you. Following implies concealment." Vess planted the staff. "I've been a day back on your trail since the ford. You camped with the fire downwind of your bedroll. Sloppy."

"We didn't ask for company."

"No one ever does. Company arrives anyway. It's one of the great injustices of accounting." She looked at the self-crossing road, then at the sky, doing sums. "How far has this pushed you off course?"

"Half a day," Bram admitted. "Maybe more. Roads don't hold anymore. You feel it?"

"I feel it." She felt it in her teeth, actually—a faint loosening, like a tooth deciding whether to stay in the jaw. The world forgetting its own seams. "Which is why I'm here. Sit down. All three of you. I have a proposal, and I'd rather deliver it standing, but my legs have opinions."

They sat. Ada sat farthest away, arms folded, and Vess noted the distance and did not comment on it. There would be time. Negotiation was mostly patience wearing a polite coat.

"Here is the state of things," Vess began, "as the guild understands it, which is more completely than you understand it, since you're working from a copied index while we hold the original marginalia. The fire beneath the mountain is dying. You know this. What you don't know—" she watched their faces, and gave them the number slowly, letting it land, "—is how close. My last reading put the flame at under a hand's height. At current rate of consumption, it gutters out in approximately five weeks. Perhaps six, if the court rations itself, which they will not, because the court does not ration. The court *waits*. Waiting is their entire culture."

"That's not possible," Ada said. "The ledgers say—"

"The ledgers say what the ledgers have always said. One soul buys a century. That was true when the founders struck the bargain, and it remained true for four hundred years, because the fire was large and fat and well-fed. But fire is not a bank, girl. It's a stomach. And a stomach that's been fed the same diet for four centuries begins to burn through it faster. We've known for sixty years that the exchange rate was failing. We've been quietly increasing the volume to compensate." She said it flatly, without apology, because apology was a currency and she was not spending it here. "Forty-one souls in my tenure alone. More under my predecessors. And now the fire is small enough that even that isn't enough. Which brings me to my proposal."

Bram shifted. "I already don't like it."

"You haven't heard it."

"Don't need to. Proposals from guildmasters are like gifts from tax collectors."

"Then hear it anyway, for the pleasure of disliking it accurately." Vess drew a breath. "Return the child to the guild. In exchange, the guild commits—in writing, witnessed, bound by every oath we still have power to swear—to buying back the souls in the vault ledger. All of them. Slowly. Over however many centuries it takes. We will negotiate with the court directly. We will pay in echoes, in years, in griefs, in anything they'll accept besides people. We will begin with the oldest entries and work forward. Your grandmother first, Ada. I give you my word on that specifically."

The silence after that had texture. Vess let it sit. She had learned long ago that the first response to any offer was silence, and that whoever spoke second conceded ground.

It was Ada who broke it, which told Vess the girl was greener than she'd hoped.

"Why?" Ada said. "Why buy them back now? You've had four hundred years. Why does it start the moment I walk out the door with a copied ledger?"

"Because you're going to do something catastrophic," Vess said, "and I am trying to prevent it in the least damaging way available."

"You mean the theft."

"I mean the *calling-in*." Vess turned to look directly at the child, and the child looked back, unblinking, and for a moment something moved behind Vess's composure—not fear, exactly. Recognition. The look of an accountant finding a figure in a column that should not exist. "You've read the vault book. You know what he is listed as. Collateral. An asset held against debt. But you haven't asked the obvious question, so I'll ask it for you: collateral against *whose* debt? A pledge requires a pledgor. Someone in this arrangement owes something. And if you steal the collateral—if you simply take him and run—the debt doesn't vanish. Debts never vanish. They transfer. They come due. And when this particular debt comes due, it comes due all at once, with interest accrued over four hundred years, payable by the party the contract names as guarantor."

"Which is the guild," Bram said slowly.

"Which is the guild," Vess agreed. "And the guild's assets are, at present, a drafty building, six ceremonial Keepers, and a cellar of bottled echoes worth perhaps a decade of fire. When the court calls in our default, they will not stop at the guild. Contracts of this age have clauses. The founders were thorough men and careless ones, both at once—they wrote protections for themselves and none for anyone downstream. If the debt defaults, the court is entitled to collect from the guarantor's *estate*." She paused. "The estate, in the old drafting, means everything the guarantor's world touches. Borders. Rivers. Names. The map itself."

"That's a threat," Ada said quietly.

"It's a forecast," Vess said. "Threats are optional. Forecasts are not. I am not threatening you, Ada Iversen. I am telling you what the arithmetic does if you pull the thread you're pulling. Return the boy, and we buy everyone back over time, gently, without collapse. Refuse, and in five weeks the fire dies anyway, and everything after that is not negotiable by anyone."

* * *

The child spoke then.

He had not spoken in their presence before—not in words. He had sung, on the road, in the dead language, and the roads had bent themselves straight for him. But now he stood up from the milestone, small and deliberate, and he said something in that older-than-kingdoms tongue, three or four syllables, addressed directly to Vess.

And Vess—who had spent her life among languages of contract, who could read the founders' hand in the dark, who had once parsed a clause written in frost on a window—understood him.

Not the words. The *meaning*. It arrived in her the way arithmetic arrives: whole, undeniable, cold.

*You are counting the wrong column.*

She stood very still.

"What did he say?" Ada demanded, watching her face.

"He said—" Vess stopped. Recalibrated. "He said I'm counting the wrong column."

"And is he?"

Vess looked at the child. The child looked at her with the patience of something that had waited four hundred years inside a single line of a ledger, and she felt, for the first time in her professional life, the sensation of a number looking back at her.

"The fire is weeks from death," she said, but her voice came out less certain than she had budgeted for. "That is not a matter of opinion. I read the gauge myself."

"Then we have five weeks," Ada said, rising, "to find a better answer than selling him back to the thing that owns him. You can come with us or you can go home, Keeper. But if you come, you carry your own pack, and you don't give orders."

Vess considered. Her knees hurt. The plum jar was empty. Somewhere behind her, thirty years of immaculate bookkeeping sat in a locked drawer with forty-one names in it, and somewhere ahead of her, a flame the height of a thumb was burning through the last of what it had been fed.

She picked up her staff.

"I'll come," she said. "Someone has to keep the accounts."

But that night, unable to sleep, she took out her memorandum book by the light of Bram's careful fire and read the forty-one names again—and found, for the first time, that the list would not sit still in her mind as assets. It sat as something else. Something with a column heading she had never dared write.

And at dawn, the child touched her hand and sang one syllable of the dead language, and the road ahead—which had spent three days wandering in circles—straightened suddenly toward the mountain, as if it too had finally decided where it was going.
