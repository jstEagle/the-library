## 13. The Gate of Paid Debts

The mountain announced itself three days before we saw it.

It did this through the boy. He woke in the grey hour before dawn, sat up in his bedroll with his eyes still closed, and began to hum—a low, looping phrase that made my teeth ache the way a struck tuning fork aches in your jaw. By the second day he was singing it outright, and by the third, the road itself seemed to be keeping time.

"The gate," he said, when I asked him what the song was for. "It likes to be sung to. Everything old does."

Bram spat into the scrub grass and adjusted the pack straps that had been eating his shoulders since the inn. "Everything old wants something, is what it wants. Songs are just how it asks."

He'd been sourer since the inn. We all had. The woman who couldn't remember why she cried had waved us off from her porch with both hands raised like a blessing, and none of us had spoken for the first mile, because what was there to say? That we were going to do for thousands what we could not undo for one? That grief, once sold, stayed sold unless someone went down into the dark and argued with the arithmetic?

So we walked, and the boy sang, and on the morning of the fourth day the mountain came over the horizon all at once, the way mountains do—no warning, just stone where sky had been.

It was wrong, though. I want to be precise about that, because everything else about it looked ordinary enough: a black massif shouldering up out of the moorland, snow at its crown, cloud snagged on its shoulders like laundry left out too long. But the light behaved strangely near it. It bent. Not dramatically—the way water bends around a stone—but enough that when I held my hand up at arm's length, the mountain's edge wavered behind my fingers as if seen through heat shimmer, or through tears.

"Fewer remembered souls hold fewer places in place," I said, quoting Vess without meaning to.

"Stop quoting the woman who tried to buy the child back from us," Bram said.

"She explained the world better than anyone else has."

"That's not a defense of her. That's an indictment of the world."

I didn't answer, because he was right, and because the boy had stopped singing.

He stood very still on the road ahead of us, small hands loose at his sides, face tilted up toward the mountain the way you'd tilt your face toward rain. Then he turned and looked at me, and his expression was one I hadn't seen on him before—not wonder, not dread, but recognition. The look of someone arriving at a house they'd lived in long ago.

"It knows me," he said. "The gate."

* * *

We reached it by late afternoon: a wall of fitted basalt rising forty feet out of the scree, running in both directions until it vanished into haze, with no visible end and no visible purpose except to stand between the world and the mountain. And set into its center, a gate.

I have spent my life among objects that hold more than their shape. Bottled echoes. Borrowed years. A sealed grief in a lead-lined drawer. I thought I understood what it meant for a thing to be crowded.

The gate put me right.

Every inch of it was carved—and not decorated, *inscribed*. Contracts. Thousands upon thousands of them, rendered in script so fine it might have been done with a needle, tiered upon tiered, older forms sinking beneath newer ones the way geological strata sink beneath each other. I saw guildhands I recognized: the angular Ledgerkeeper formal of two centuries past, the flowing merchant-cipher of the coastal houses, marks from traditions whose names survive only in footnotes. Clauses coiled around clauses. Signatures overlapped signatures. Somewhere in that stone, I knew, was the founding contract of my own guild, signed by men whose portraits hung in the hall I'd grown up dusting.

And every single contract on that gate had been honored.

That was the horror of it. I ran my fingers across the carvings and felt no trickery in them, no coercion, no forgery. Each line had been struck through in the old way—*paid in full*—and the striking-through had been done with such care, such terrible conscientiousness, that the stone itself seemed proud of it. This was not a monument to theft. It was a monument to *keeping accounts*. Every soul ever traded below had gone down these terms, agreed or inherited, witnessed and sealed. The court beneath had never once cheated.

It had never once needed to.

"How does it open?" Bram asked. He'd gone quiet in the way he went quiet before a job, which meant he was afraid and converting the fear into competence.

The boy pressed his palm flat against the stone. Nothing happened. He pressed harder, frowning, and then stepped back and shook his head slowly.

"I'm owed," he said. "But the door doesn't open for creditors. Creditors don't come *in*. They send letters." His voice caught on something between a laugh and a sob. "You have to be *in the book*. You have to be part of what's been paid."

We stood with that for a while. Wind came down off the moor and moved nothing on the gate—the carvings were too dense for wind to find purchase—and I watched my breath drift across four hundred years of honored debts and dissolve.

Then I took out the copy of the vault ledger.

Vess had given it to me before we left—or rather, she had failed to stop me taking it, which from Vess amounted to the same thing. A transcription, ink on calfskin, of the true living book downstairs where the books breathe. Thousands of names under the heading PAID IN FULL TO THE COURT BENEATH. I had read it every night by firelight, not because I needed to memorize it but because reading it was a kind of promise. My grandmother's name was on page ninety-one. I had traced the letters so often they were going soft.

I unrolled it now against the gate, weighting the corners with stones, and the wind, finding no grip on the carving, found plenty on the skin.

"Bram," I said. "Hold this flat."

He did, without asking why, which told me he already knew.

I found my own name where I'd known it would be—not yet, not as a debt paid, but as a line I had written myself, weeks ago, in the vault, in the moment the living book had warned me that a debt unpaid can be transferred but never erased. *Someone must go beneath the mountain.* I had dipped my pen and written, in clean apprentice-hand:

**Ada Iversen — offered as surety — pending.**

Pending. That word had felt like a loophole when I wrote it. Standing in front of the gate, it felt like what it was: a signature waiting for its counterparty.

"The book lists me," I said. "Not as paid. As offered."

Bram's hands stopped moving on the calfskin. "Ada."

"It opens for someone listed. He can't get in—he's the creditor, they'd sooner let the mountain fall than admit him. You're not listed anywhere, thank every god there is. So it's me, or we turn around, and if we turn around the fire goes out and the borders go with it and the inn-woman's whole village blinks out of memory while she's still standing in it." I kept my voice level. I had practiced being level about this for weeks, alone, at night, and the practice held, mostly. "I'm not asking permission. I'm telling you what I wrote before either of you could argue."

"You wrote yourself into a debtors' prison," Bram said, "and you're calling it a door."

"I'm calling it the only honest thing the guild ever taught me. An entry is a commitment. I committed."

The boy tugged my sleeve. When I knelt to his height, he put both hands on either side of my face, the way my grandmother used to when she wanted me to actually hear something, and I wondered—not for the first time, but with new sharpness—where he had learned that gesture, and from whom.

"When you go in," he said, "they will be very polite to you. That's the worst part. Be polite back, but don't mistake it for kindness. Politeness is how they count."

"Where did you learn that?"

His eyes went distant, old, seven years old and centuries deep. "From everyone they counted."

* * *

Bram argued for another hour. I'll give him that—he argued like a man doing honest work, thoroughly, from every angle, and when he ran out of angles he argued from anger, and when he ran out of anger he sat down on a rock and said, very quietly, "Your grandmother would skin me for letting you do this."

"My grandmother is on page ninety-one of that book," I said. "She'd skin me for *not* doing it."

He laughed then, one short bark with no humor in it, and rubbed his face with both hands, and when he lowered them he was the disgraced thief again, the professional, the man who owed me a life-debt from a job gone wrong and had decided, somewhere on the forgetting road, that this was how he'd pay it.

"Fine. Fine. Then we do it properly." He stood and started pulling rope, flint, the little iron pry-bar he'd carried since I'd known him. "You go through, I camp here. If you're not out in three days, I start looking for another way in, and I don't care what the stone says about who's listed."

"There is no other way in."

"Then I'll spend three days proving you wrong, and after that I'll spend forever." He said it flatly, a fact about himself, like his eye color. "Don't make me do forever, Ada. Come out."

The boy said nothing at all. He sat cross-legged in the shadow of the gate and sang softly to it, the looping phrase from the road, and I noticed—for the first time—that the song had words now, or almost-words, syllables in the dead language surfacing like fish rising in a pond. The gate listened. I could feel it listening, the way you feel a sleeping animal attend to a familiar voice.

Dusk came. In the guildhall, dusk had meant lamplighting and the smell of ink and the senior Keepers' footsteps overhead, sounds I had loved more than the people making them. Here dusk meant the carvings began to glow.

Faintly—no more than foxfire, no more than the last coal of a banked fire—but unmistakably, the paid-in-full lines kindled along their lengths, strike-through by strike-through, until the whole gate was a lattice of dim gold and the mountain behind it breathed once, slow and vast, and the air coming off the stone was warm.

It smelled like the vault. Like the living book. Like something bound in something warm.

"Now," the boy said, and stopped singing.

I rolled the ledger copy, tied it down, slung my pack—lighter than it should have been, because everything I owned suddenly looked like it belonged to someone else's life. I kissed the top of the boy's head. I let Bram grip my forearm, thief-fashion, hard enough to leave marks, neither of us saying anything because we had already said everything and it hadn't helped.

Then I walked to the gate and laid my palm against the stone, over the strata of honored debts, and spoke the way I'd been taught to speak entries aloud, clear and unhurried, in the voice the guild had drilled into me for exactly occasions like this and never once imagined I'd use like this:

"Ada Iversen. Listed. Offered as surety. Pending."

The gold lines flickered.

Somewhere inside the stone, I felt—there is no other word for it—a *reading*. Something turned my name over, weighed it, checked it against clauses older than kingdoms. The warmth of the stone increased under my hand. The carvings shifted—I swear this, though Bram will tell you he saw nothing—the tiers of contracts sliding like the leaves of a book opening to its most recent page, and there, glowing fresh-cut among signatures centuries dead, my own line surfaced:

**Ada Iversen — surety — accepted.**

Accepted. Before it was demanded. Before I had crossed any threshold or spoken any plea. The court beneath had simply taken my offer the way a patient merchant takes coin laid on the counter—without surprise, without gratitude, without hurry. Of course it had. What else was I for? I was inventory now. I had made myself inventory, and the mountain had filed me accordingly.

The gate swung inward on hinges that made no sound at all.

Beyond it: a stair, descending into warm dark, wide enough for six abreast, cut smooth by feet or by centuries. Air rose up it carrying the smell of hot metal and old paper and, underneath both, something sweet and fading that I recognized from my grandmother's kitchen and could not name without my throat closing.

"Three days," Bram said behind me.

"Come out," the boy said, and it wasn't a request, and it wasn't quite a prediction. It sounded like a term in a contract only he could read.

I stepped through.

The gate closed behind me with the soft finality of a ledger being shut, and far below, at the bottom of a stair that had already swallowed my lantern-light, something stirred—a sound like ten thousand pages turning at once, patient and courteous and utterly immovable, beginning, I understood, to read *me*.
