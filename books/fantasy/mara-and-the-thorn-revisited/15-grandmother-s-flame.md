## 15. Grandmother's Flame

The shelves went on longer than the mountain should have allowed.

Ada had stopped counting rows somewhere past the two hundredth. Each shelf held flames—not candles, not lamps, but small standing fires the size of a cupped hand, each one burning without fuel, without smoke, without any of the ordinary appetites of fire. They gave off light the way a sleeping person gives off breath: steadily, unconsciously, alive.

She walked between them with the vault book pressed to her chest and felt each flame notice her passing. Not heat. Attention. Like walking through a crowd where everyone had their eyes closed and could still feel you looking.

*Don't think about it,* she told herself. *Find her first. Then you're allowed to fall apart.*

Bram was somewhere behind her, keeping the child close, keeping his voice low in that way he thought passed for reassurance. The court had let them descend—had *escorted* them, with the unhurried courtesy of hosts who knew exactly how long their guests could stay and saw no reason to mention it. The Magistrate had even provided a guide: a tall figure with a face like smoothed wax who walked ahead of them and said nothing and needed to eat nothing and never once turned its head all the way around.

"Row three hundred and eleven," the guide had said at the top of the stairs, in a voice like a ledger closing. "Family names are filed under debt origin, not bloodline. You will find yours under *Iversen, founding obligation.*"

*Iversen.* Her own name, spoken by something that didn't breathe. Filed under an obligation she'd never signed.

Now she walked the row, reading the small brass plates beneath each flame. Not names, mostly. Contract numbers. Dates of sale. Some plates bore only a single word: *PAID*, which meant the soul inside had been consumed down to the last ember, spent keeping the dragon-fire lit, keeping the borders of the world standing, keeping the map from forgetting itself.

Some of those flames were very small now. Some were almost out.

Ada's throat tightened. She kept walking.

*Founding obligation.* The founders' debts would be the oldest entries, the deepest shelves, closest to the heart of the mountain where the air tasted faintly of iron and old decisions. She passed flames that flickered when she looked at them—souls that remembered being looked at, maybe. Souls that had been waiting so long for a visitor that attention itself was a kind of meal.

And then, on a shelf set slightly apart, in a niche carved with more care than the others, she saw it.

A flame the color of late afternoon through kitchen windows. Gold going amber going rose. It burned in a slow, patient rhythm, and Ada knew the rhythm before she knew why, the way you know a lullaby from the first three notes even if you can't name the song.

The brass plate read:

**IVERSEN, MARGIT — HELD AS SURETY — ORIGINAL PRINCIPAL UNPAID**

Her grandmother's name.

Ada stopped. The vault book against her chest grew warm, then warmer, as if it recognized what she'd found and wanted credit for it. She made herself look at the plate again. *Original principal unpaid.* Not sold. Not spent. Held. For sixty years her grandmother's soul had sat in this niche, not because the court had taken it but because the guild had *pledged* it—the way a farmer pledges land, the way a king pledges a crown—and had never once, in six decades, made a payment against the principal.

They'd been paying interest. In other people.

"Oh," Ada said aloud, and her voice cracked in the middle of the word like thin ice. "Oh, Gran."

The flame leaned toward her.

Not much. A degree or two of tilt, the way a flower leans after sun. But Ada felt it the way you feel someone turn toward you in a dark room—a change in the quality of the silence, a sudden specificity to the dark.

She knelt. The stone floor was warm here, warmer than it had any right to be, and she understood dimly that everything in this place was warmed by the same fire, fed by the same souls, running on the same terrible arithmetic. She knelt anyway. Her knees didn't care about arithmetic.

"I don't know if you can hear me," she said. "I don't know if you're *in* there, or if it's just—" She stopped. Started again. "I'm Ada. Margit's granddaughter. Ada Iversen. I keep the ledgers now. Well. I *kept* them. I've recently developed some strong opinions about keeping them."

The flame did not laugh. But it brightened, just slightly, at the edges—the way her grandmother's eyes used to crinkle when Ada said something too honest for company.

"You're really there," Ada whispered. "You're really in there."

*Little spark.*

The voice came not through her ears but through the warmth—rising off the flame like heat shimmer given grammar. It was her grandmother's voice. Not the memory of it, not an impression. The voice itself, dry and amused and unsentimental, the voice that had taught her to count by weighing flour, the voice that had said *a debt is just a promise wearing armor* while kneading bread on a scarred oak table.

"Gran." Ada's hands hovered near the flame, afraid to touch, afraid not to. "I came to get you out."

*Mm.* The sound was exactly the noncommittal hum her grandmother had deployed against every one of Ada's childhood schemes. *And how is my girl planning to do that? Steal me?*

"If I have to."

*From whom?* The warmth sharpened. *Child, look at the plate. Read it properly. Who holds me?*

"The court," Ada said. "The guild pledged you and the court holds you, and I'm going to—"

*Read it again.*

Ada read it again. And this time, kneeling this close, with the vault book humming against her ribs like a second heartbeat, she saw what she'd missed. The plate didn't say the court held Margit Iversen as surety.

It said the *fire* did.

**IVERSEN, MARGIT — HELD AS SURETY BY THE FIRST FLAME — ORIGINAL PRINCIPAL UNPAID**

"The first flame," Ada said slowly. "The dragon-fire."

*Now you're counting,* her grandmother said, and there was pride in it, and grief, and something underneath both that felt terribly like apology. *Sit down properly, little spark. You'll want to be sitting.*

Ada sat. The stone accepted her like an old chair.

*Your masters taught you the story their masters taught them,* her grandmother said. *That the fire is dying and souls feed it and the guild pays tribute to keep the world whole. Yes?*

"Yes."

*A tidy story. Tidy stories are how thieves sleep at night.* The flame guttered once—not failing, Ada realized, but gathering itself, the way a speaker draws breath. *Here is the untidy one. The fire was never theirs to feed. It was never anyone's to feed. It burned before the guild existed. It burned before the court existed. It burned because the world burned, once, at the beginning, and this is the last coal of that beginning, and it asked for nothing. Do you understand? Nothing. Fire doesn't want. Fire only is.*

"Then why does it need souls?"

*It doesn't.* The word landed like a dropped coin in an empty room. *It never did. Your founders stole from it.*

The vault book went still against Ada's chest. Completely still, the way a person goes still listening at a door.

*Three hundred years ago,* her grandmother said, *four clever people climbed this mountain with a contract they believed was very fine work. They had discovered the first flame—the last coal of the world's first burning—and they saw what everyone sees when they find something ancient and generous: they saw an asset. They took a spark. They carried it down and built your guildhall around it, and every impossible thing the Ledgerkeepers ever traded—every bottled echo, every borrowed year, every sealed grief—they could trade because they were working by stolen light. The spark made the impossible possible. That was the theft. Not the taking of a thing, little spark. The taking of the *condition* that made all things possible.*

Ada's mouth was dry. "And the court?"

*There was no court yet. There was only the wound.* The flame dimmed, and Ada had the horrible sense of her grandmother flinching. *When you steal fire from the first fire, the fire remembers. It began to reach for what was taken—to call its spark home—and the reaching is what your founders mistook for hunger. They thought the mountain was demanding payment. So they paid. First gold, which meant nothing. Then years, which meant little. Then they offered a soul, because a soul is memory, and memory is the only currency the first fire has ever recognized—it burns on remembering, child, it always has, that's what fire *is*, the world remembering how to be lit—*

"And it worked," Ada said hollowly. "Feeding it souls made the reaching stop."

*For a while. Because a soul freely given looks, from a distance, like a spark returning home.* Her grandmother's voice gentled into something Ada had never heard in it before: shame. *But it isn't homecoming. It's a hostage dressed in Sunday clothes. The fire takes the memory, gets a moment of relief, realizes it's been handed a stranger, and reaches again. Harder. Every payment bought less time. Every soul made the hunger sharper, because the fire isn't hungry for souls—it's hungry for its *spark*, and your guild has been feeding it everything except the one thing it actually lost.*

Ada looked up the endless rows of shelves. Thousands of flames. Thousands of memories, poured into a wound that couldn't drink them.

"They built an entire economy," she said, "on misreading a symptom."

*They built a religion on it. Then a guild. Then a habit.* The ember-warm humor returned, brittle at the edges. *Your Keeper Vess believes she's saving the world. I watched her kind come down here for sixty years, little spark. Sincere as sunrise, every one. That's the horror of good bookkeeping—it lets you mistake the balance for the truth.*

Ada's eyes stung. "Why didn't anyone say? Sixty years, Gran—you've been down here sixty years watching them pay interest in people—"

*On what evidence? A dead woman's word against four founders' contracts?* A pause. The flame steadied. *And because I signed, Ada. Don't make me cleaner than I was. When your grandfather died and the guild offered to hold my surety against the family's founding obligation—offered to *protect* us—I said yes. I thought I understood the terms. I thought a pledge was a promise wearing armor, and promises could be unmade.* The flame flickered. *I was half right. Here's what I learned in sixty years of burning, and you write it on the inside of your skull where no ledger can reach it: a debt you didn't incur is not your debt. An inheritance is only a debt if you accept the delivery.*

The words went through Ada like a blade finding the seam in armor.

Because she had been carrying it since the vault—since she'd seen PAID IN FULL TO THE COURT BENEATH and understood that her whole life had been built on top of other people's extinguishing. She had assumed the weight was hers now. That inheriting the guild meant inheriting its arithmetic, its obligations, its three-hundred-year installment plan. That the only question was how much of herself she'd have to put on the scale.

*You're doing it right now,* her grandmother said, with the terrifying accuracy of women who had raised you. *I can feel you adding it up. Your share, Bram's share, the child's share, Vess's share. Stop. Listen to me. The principal was never paid because the principal was never owed. The founders owed a *return*—the spark, given back—not a tribute. Three centuries of souls weren't payments. They were interest on a theft, collected by people who forgot there was a difference between settling a debt and feeding a mistake.*

"So I refuse the delivery," Ada said slowly.

*You refuse the whole shipment.* Warmth flooded the niche, fierce as a struck match. *The spark is still in your guildhall. Under the hall, in the old hearth, behind the founder's seal—your masters think it's decorative. It's the only real asset the Ledgerkeepers ever held, and they've been renting it out for three hundred years without knowing they owned it. Return the spark to the first flame, and the reaching stops. The hunger stops. Every soul in this mountain becomes not payment but *prisoner*, and prisoners can be freed.*

Ada stood. Her legs shook. Somewhere far above, muffled by miles of patient stone, she could almost hear the dragon-fire guttering—weeks, Vess had said. Weeks, not years.

"There's a problem," she said. "The Magistrate. Contracts are sacred down here. The moment we move, the court will—"

*The court,* her grandmother said, and something in the flame's rhythm shifted—something that sounded, impossibly, like caution, like a warning being passed hand to hand across sixty years of burning. *The court enforces the contracts, yes. Faithfully. Patiently. Ask yourself why a court that polite exists at all, little spark. Ask yourself who the contracts are actually *for*. The Magistrate will tell you the ledger must be honored. But I have burned beside these flames for sixty years, and I have heard things in the dark between the shelves, and I am telling you: the court is not the creditor. The court is the collector. Someone else holds the paper.*

Cold walked up Ada's spine. "Who?"

*That,* said her grandmother, *is the wrong question. The right one is: who lent the court the authority to collect? What did the court itself borrow, and from whom, and what happens when *that* debt comes due?* The flame leaned toward her, urgent now, gold bleeding toward white. *Because I have seen the oldest shelf, Ada. The one below the founding obligations. It holds one entry, and the entry is blank, and the blankness is the most frightening thing in this mountain. Something is owed to someone whose name cannot be written. And your child—the nameless one, the one who sings the dead language—he walks these shelves and the flames *lean toward him*, the way mine leans toward you. He hasn't noticed. Or he's pretending not to.*

Above them, faint through the stone, came a sound that stopped Ada's heart: the child's voice, rising in the old tongue—but not the wandering song he used for navigation. This was shorter. Sharper. A question, repeated, growing louder as if he were asking it of someone.

Of something.

*Go,* her grandmother said. *Go to him. And Ada—* the flame caught her back for one last second, warm as a kitchen, warm as a hand on her hair— *when you take me out of here, don't carry me. Let me walk. I've been sitting for sixty years, and I intend to be insufferable about the view.*

Ada laughed, and it broke in the middle, and she pressed her palm to the warm stone beside the niche because she could not touch the flame, not yet, not until the arithmetic was undone.

"I'm coming back for you," she said. "I'm refusing the delivery. All of it."

*Good girl,* said Margit Iversen, last honest creditor of a dishonest guild. *Now run. I think your boy just remembered something the whole mountain has been trying to forget.*

Ada ran—up the endless rows, past thousands of leaning flames, toward the sound of the child's voice asking its question in a language older than kingdoms, older than courts, older than debt itself.

And the vault book, pressed against her chest, had opened itself to a page she hadn't turned.

One line, in ink that gleamed wet and fresh:

**THE CREDITOR APPROACHES. ALL ACCOUNTS PENDING REVIEW.**
