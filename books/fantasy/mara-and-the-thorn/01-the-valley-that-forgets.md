## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The dead of Thornmere were a polite people.

That was the first thing Freya Hartwell had learned about them, forty years ago, when she was seven and her grandmother first pressed a whittling knife into her small hands and said, *Listen before you cut.* The dead of the valley did not wail or claw at the walls between worlds. They lingered the way lamplight lingers in a room after the flame is pinched out—faintly, courteously, asking nothing except to be remembered in their own words.

The living of Thornmere were another matter entirely.

Freya rose before dawn on the last morning of harvest month, as she did every morning, and built her fire from birch and dried gorse because birch cracked softly and gorse smelled like the moorland where she'd gathered it, and both things mattered more than anyone would ever guess. A bone-carver's workshop should smell of nothing but clean dust and linseed oil. If it smelled of anything stranger—if it hummed, if it whispered—the neighbors would notice. The neighbors noticed everything in Thornmere. Noticing was the valley's chief industry after sheep.

She boiled water for barley tea and stood at her window while it steeped, watching the fog peel itself off the River Mere in slow gray ribbons. The village lay below her cottage in a huddle of slate roofs, chimneys just beginning to breathe. Beyond it the terraced fields climbed the valley walls in pale green steps, and beyond those the cliffs rose sheer and dark, crowned with thorn trees that grew nowhere else in the world—or so the valley claimed—and which no one, in living memory, had ever been able to fell.

Thornmere. Named twice over: once for the mere, once for the thorns that ringed it like a wall of black teeth. The old folk said the thorns kept something out. The older folk, the ones Freya heard through their bones rather than through their mouths, said the thorns kept something *in*. She had never decided which version frightened her more, and so, as with most uncomfortable questions, she had simply stopped asking.

Her tea was too hot. She drank it anyway, scalding her tongue, a small penance she performed every morning without quite admitting it was a penance. Then she tied back her hair, gray-streaked now though she was only nine-and-forty, put on her leather apron, and went into the workshop.

* * *

The workshop had been her grandmother's before her, and her great-grandmother's before that, though officially it had always been a *carpenter's shed*, and officially Freya Hartwell was a carpenter who made an odd specialty of small carved keepsakes. The bench ran the length of the north wall beneath a window frosted with salt-spray from the mere. Along the south wall hung her tools: gouges and veiners and rifflers, each handle worn to the shape of her grip, each blade honed to a whisper. And along the east wall, wrapped in linen and laid in cedar boxes like sleeping children, waited the work.

Three commissions this week. She took down the first box and unwrapped it with the care of a woman uncovering a face.

A rib bone, human, cured and cleaned and pale as winter moonlight. It had come to her nine days ago from Tam Ellery, the cooper, whose wife Maren had died at the turn of summer with her hands still full of bread dough. Tam had not known what to ask for. He had stood in Freya's doorway holding the little bundle like something stolen, his eyes red-rimmed and ashamed, and said only, "They say you can make a thing. From her. Something I could hold."

Freya had said yes, because she always said yes, and because the alternative was to let a grieving man carry an uncarved bone home and feel its silence pressing against him like a locked door.

Now she set the rib on her bench, clamped it gently in padded jaws, and picked up her finest veiner.

And she listened.

This was the part no one knew. This was the part that would see her drowned in the mere or burned in the barley fields if the neighbors ever learned of it, and so this was the part she performed in absolute stillness, door barred, shutters drawn, breath held—not literally held, for she needed breath, but held in some deeper way, the way a deer holds itself at the forest's edge.

The dead spoke through their bones. Not all bones, and not always, and never in words exactly—it was more like heat rising off stone, more like the shape of a song remembered by someone who has forgotten the tune. But when Freya's blade touched bone that had lived, something came up through the steel and into her wrist and settled behind her eyes, and she *knew*.

Maren Ellery had loved mornings. That came first, always the strongest thing first: mornings, the smell of yeast, the blue hour before her husband woke. Maren had been afraid at the end—not of dying, Freya understood, but of being forgotten mid-motion, of the whole village remembering her only as the woman who collapsed, the dough spoiling on the table, the ordinary interrupted. Her last thought had not been a prayer. It had been *the loaves*, and beneath that, threaded through everything like a silver wire: *Tam. Tam. Tell Tam the third shelf sticks and he must lift it with his knee, he always forgets, tell him—*

Freya exhaled slowly and began to carve.

She carved what the bone asked for, not what Tam had requested, because Tam hadn't known enough to request anything true. The rib became a woman's hands cradling a round of bread, the fingers rendered in curling relief, the loaf scored with the cross Maren had cut into every loaf of her married life. It took four hours. When she finished, she held it to the window light and listened one last time, and from deep in the carved grain came something almost like a sigh, almost like *yes*, and then the presence faded the way warmth fades from a bathed body, leaving only bone.

Freya wrapped it in linen. Her eyes stung. They always stung at this moment, and she always told herself it was the dust.

* * *

Midmorning brought a knock: two raps, hesitant, the rhythm of someone who hoped she wasn't home.

It was Old Sorrel from the mill, eighty if she was a day, carrying a cloth bundle and wearing the expression of a woman about to confess to theft. "Mistress Hartwell. I don't know if you—I heard you do carvings. Keepsakes. From—" She faltered.

"From bone," Freya said mildly. "Yes. Come in, Sorrel. Mind the step."

Inside, over tea that Sorrel was too nervous to drink, the story came out in pieces. Sorrel's brother, dead these thirty years, drowned in the mere when they were young, and Sorrel had kept one of his finger bones hidden in a prayer box all this time because she couldn't bear to bury the last of him, and now she was dying herself—she didn't say this outright, but Freya heard it in the wet rattle under her voice, in the way her own knees had begun speaking to her of endings—and she wanted, before she went, to give him back something. To have him made into something beautiful. To apologize, though she wouldn't say for what.

"I know it's a strange request," Sorrel finished. "I know folk say it's not natural, carving the dead. My mother always said—"

"Your mother said a great many things," Freya said, "and half of them were wrong." She held out her hand for the bundle. "Leave it with me. Come back at the new moon."

Sorrel left lighter than she'd arrived, and Freya stood in her doorway watching her go, and felt the familiar ache settle into her chest like a stone dropping into a well.

Because here was the truth of her life, the truth she had arranged so carefully around herself that sometimes she forgot it was there, the way you forget the furniture in a dark room: Sorrel liked her. Sorrel trusted her. Sorrel would defend her at the market if anyone spoke ill. And Sorrel would be the first to hold her down at the water's edge if she ever learned what Freya actually did with those bones.

Not out of malice. Out of fear. Thornmere feared magic the way a scarred dog fears raised hands—reflexively, totally, without memory of why. There had been burnings once; the stones of the old chapel yard remembered them even if the villagers didn't. Somewhere in the valley's past, someone with power had done something terrible enough that three generations later the word *witch* still emptied a room. So the valley had learned to forget, and forgetting was its armor, and Freya had learned to be forgettable right along with it.

She was good at it now. That was the bitter joke she never told anyone: she had become a master craftsman of invisibility. She paid her tithes early. She traded fairly and complained about prices exactly as much as everyone else. She attended chapel every seventh day and sang slightly off-key. She kept her hair plain, her dress patched, her opinions dull. When the reeve's wife mentioned the bad lambing, Freya agreed it was the bad lambing. When the smith complained about his shoulder, Freya agreed shoulders were treacherous. She had spent forty years agreeing, and the agreement had built her a life as smooth and featureless as river stone.

And underneath it, alone in her workshop with the shutters drawn, she talked with the dead, and they were better company than any living soul in the valley, and she could not have explained to a single one of her neighbors why that was, or how much it cost her.

She looked at Sorrel's little bundle on her bench and thought: *thirty years in a prayer box.* The brother's presence was faint, worn thin as old cloth, but it was there—a young man's restlessness, water still in his lungs in some ghostly way, and beneath it a sister's name repeated like a heartbeat. *Sorrel. Sorrel.* He had been calling for her when he drowned.

Freya sat down heavily and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes.

"Contentment," she said aloud to the empty workshop, testing the word the way she tested a blade's edge. It did not cut true. It never had. But it was the word she showed the world, and the world believed it, and most days she could almost believe it herself, and that, she supposed, was the most any hidden thing could hope for.

* * *

The afternoon she spent on Tam Ellery's second commission—he'd returned two days after the first, weeping with gratitude, asking shyly if a piece might be made for his daughter, who was too young to remember her mother otherwise—and then walked down into the village as the light went long and gold across the fields.

She delivered the bread-hands carving to Tam personally, watched him turn it over in his big calloused fingers, watched his face do something complicated and private when his thumb found the score-mark on the loaf. "It's her," he said hoarsely. "How did you—it's *her*, mistress. How did you know about the cross?"

"Lucky guess," Freya said. "Bread wants a cross. Everyone knows that."

He pressed coins on her, too many, and she took only the fair amount, and walked home through the gathering dusk feeling the particular loneliness that comes after doing a kindness no one can fully thank you for. The thorn trees on the clifftops stood black against a bruising sky. The wind had changed since morning; it came off the moors now instead of the sea, and it carried a smell she couldn't place—dust, maybe, or rain on far-off stone, or something older than either.

At her gate she paused. The geese were restless in their pen, necks low, making their trouble-sound. The dog on the Hollis farm, half a mile off, was barking—not the idle bark of evening, but the flat, continuous alarm-bark that meant *something is wrong and I will say so until someone listens.*

Freya stood in her garden and listened past the dog, past the wind, the way she listened to bones—with her whole self quiet.

Far away, at the very edge of what her gift could reach, at the border of the valley where the thorns grew thickest, something was coming. She couldn't have said what. There were no bones in it yet, no death she could hear. Only a wrongness, small and moving, like a splinter working its way toward the heart of a hand.

Behind her, inside the cottage, on the bench where three cured ribs and a finger bone lay waiting in their cedar boxes, the dead stirred. Not speaking. Not yet.

Listening too.

The first drops of rain struck the garden path as the storm rolled in over the moors, and Freya Hartwell, bone-carver, invisible woman, went inside and barred her door against a night that suddenly felt much longer than usual.
