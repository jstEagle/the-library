## 2. A Knock at Dusk

The storm came down off the western crags like something with intent.

Freya Hartwell had watched weather her whole life—carvers learned to read the sky the way farmers read soil—and this was not an ordinary autumn gale. The clouds had a bruised, greenish underbelly, and the wind moved in long shuddering gusts that made the shutters of her workshop knock and knock and knock, as though someone impatient stood outside asking to be let in.

She tried not to think of it as knocking. She failed.

The fire in her hearth was small; she kept it small on principle. A bone-carver's hands needed warmth but not heat, for heat swelled the material and lied about its grain. She sat at her workbench with a lamp turned low, turning a femur in her fingers—a fine one, from old Marta Ellery's cow, though the family swore it was Marta herself they wanted remembered, and who was Freya to argue? Grief chose strange vessels. That was the whole of her trade.

She set her burin against the bone and began the first line of the border pattern.

And the bone spoke.

Not words—not yet. It did what all bones did when she cut them: it hummed up through her fingertips, a vibration more felt than heard, the way a plucked string is felt in the teeth. But there was something wrong in the hum tonight. Old Marta's cow had died contented in its pasture, and its marrow should have carried nothing but grass and sunlight and the dumb peace of beasts. Instead the note that rose out of the femur was dark and syllabic, almost speech, almost—

Almost a language.

Freya lifted the burin away. The hum died. She sat very still in the lamplight, listening to the storm batter the valley, and told herself she was tired. She had spent the morning with the Ashcombe family, taking the last words of their drowned son through a knucklebone he'd carried since boyhood, and his final word—*mother*, of course, it was always *mother* or *forgive me* or sometimes both—had lodged under her ribs like a splinter. Grief was contagious. That was the other half of her trade.

She set the burin down again.

The hum started without her.

It came faintly from the shelf above the hearth, where the finished pieces waited in their rows of wool and linen: a rib carved with lilies for a stillborn child, a vertebra strung on leather for a widow who wore it at her throat, a skull-cap polished smooth for the Ashcombes' memorial niche. Each one held its dead. Each one, tonight, was murmuring.

Freya rose so fast her stool went over backward. She crossed the workshop and put her palm flat against the shelf, and the murmur gathered beneath her hand like bees under a hive-lid, dozens of small voices, all of them saying the same thing in the same tongue she did not know—long vowels, hard consonants, a rhythm like water over stones, like a litany, like a name being repeated and repeated and repeated by mouths that had been closed for years.

*Wake. Wake. Wake.* Or perhaps *come*. Or perhaps *run*. The dead were never clear. They were feeling given sound, and feeling translated badly.

"Quiet," she whispered. "Be quiet, be quiet."

They did not quiet. In eleven years of hearing the dead through the things she carved, they had never once disobeyed the shape she gave them. Bones spoke only when cut; that was the law of her gift, the law of Thornmere itself, where magic pooled thin as winter milk and nothing crossed the borders except weather and travelers and trouble. Inside the valley, the dead slept unless she woke them. Outside—she'd been told, by the one teacher she'd ever had, dead now herself—the world beyond the thorn-hedges roared with voices day and night, a flood no living mind could stand in for long.

So why were they waking here? Why now?

The wind screamed down the chimney. The lamp guttered. And beneath the storm, beneath the muttering shelf, Freya heard something else: footsteps in the mud. Small ones. Unsteady ones. Coming up her path.

She knew every footfall in Thornmere the way she knew every voice. Tam Weller walked heel-first. The Hedley twins ran everywhere, even indoors. Old Garrick shuffled with a stick he refused to admit he needed. These steps belonged to none of them—they were too light, too arrhythmic, the steps of someone falling and rising, falling and rising, forcing a body forward past its own exhaustion.

Freya blew out the lamp. Darkness took the workshop except for the hearth's red seam. She stood with her back against the shelf of murmuring bones and watched the door, and the door shook under the wind, and the footsteps stopped just outside it.

A pause. Long enough for her heart to beat six times.

Then three knocks. Small. Polite. Almost apologetic.

For a moment she considered not answering. She was, by careful design, a woman nobody thought about—a carver of mementos, a little odd, a little plain, useful at funerals and invisible everywhere else. Strangers did not come to her door after dark. Strangers did not come to Thornmere at all, really; the valley lay two days off any honest road, wrapped in hedges and superstition, and the people here liked it that way. They buried their dead quick and deep and they did not speak of magic, because magic had been burned out of the lowlands a generation ago and the burning had come close enough to Thornmere that some families still had scorch marks in their histories.

But the knocking came again, weaker, and beneath it she heard a small body slide down the door onto her step, and whatever else Freya Hartwell was—coward, ghost-listener, keeper of silences—she had never yet let anyone freeze on her threshold while she stood warm on the other side of four inches of oak.

She opened the door.

The storm shoved in first, rain and cold and a smell of torn earth. Then the child.

It was hard to say how old—eight, perhaps ten, small for either. Mud caked it from scalp to shoe, thick as bark, so that at first glance it seemed less a child than something the flood had coughed up: a knot of roots and river-silt with two eyes. The eyes were the worst part. They were open, wide and unblinking, fixed on Freya with an attention no child should own—an old attention, patient, weighing. Rain ran off the child's chin in ropes. Its lips were blue. Its bare feet were cut and gray with cold.

Behind Freya, the shelf of bones went silent. Every piece, every dead voice, all at once—as if the dead themselves had leaned close to look.

"Child," Freya said, and her voice came out rougher than she intended. "Whose are you?"

The child opened its mouth. What came out was not weeping, though the shape of it was weeping—it was language, that same language from the bones, rolling and ancient, full of sounds the valley tongue had lost a thousand years before Freya was born. Vowels held long past comfort. A consonant like a stone dropped in a well. The child's ruined voice cracked on the syllables, and still it spoke, and still it spoke, mud-streaked face tilted up, addressing her in the tongue of a kingdom that existed only in grave-goods and the roots of place-names.

"I don't understand you." Freya's breath fogged. "I don't—child, can you walk? Can you stand?"

She reached out. The child flinched from her hand—or rather, flinched from the burin she'd forgotten she was holding, tucked behind her ear where carvers keep their tools. Its eyes fixed on the little knife of white horn, and something moved through its face like a shadow moving under ice: recognition. Terror. Relief. All three, in the space of a heartbeat, too fast and too old for a child's face.

Then its knees gave.

Freya caught it—caught all of it, the shocking weight of a soaked, freezing body, the mud smearing across her apron, the head lolling against her shoulder. Up close the child smelled of river and woodsmoke and something else underneath, something mineral and wrong, like the air inside a cave that has been sealed a long time. Its skin burned hot with fever even as its feet were ice.

She should have left it on the step. Some part of her, the part that had spent eleven years making herself invisible, said so plainly: *This is not your trouble. This is exactly the kind of trouble that gets women like you asked questions you cannot answer. Set it down. Bar the door. In the morning it will be someone else's problem.*

The child's fist had closed around her collar with the desperate strength of the drowning.

"Oh, hush," Freya told it, though it hadn't spoken. She hauled the door shut against the storm with her hip and stood a moment in the sudden relative calm, holding a stranger's child in the dark, listening to the rain hammer the roof like a thousand small fists.

* * *

She got the mud off in stages, and each stage frightened her more.

Warm water and rags took the first layer, and revealed a face that was neither boy nor girl at first glance—sharp-boned, sunburnt peeling to pale, hair hacked short with a knife rather than cut with scissors. The second layer revealed old scars, thin and deliberate, running along both forearms in patterns too regular to be accident. Carved patterns. Someone had marked this child the way Freya marked bone.

The third layer she could not remove, because it was not mud. It was a mark at the base of the throat, dark as ink, shaped like a branching thing—a thorn, or a crown of thorns, drawn into the skin as if grown there. When her rag touched it, the child seized, back arching, and spoke again in that terrible old tongue—one word this time, repeated, urgent, the same syllables the bones on the shelf had been chanting.

Freya wrapped the child in her spare blanket and set it by the hearth, and then, because she could not help herself—because the question had been gnawing her since the doorstep—she went to the shelf and took down the smallest finished piece: a finger-bone charm, carved for a hedgewitch who had died the summer before, a woman who in life had known three languages and in death, Freya hoped, might know more.

She had never used her gift on herself like this. She carved for the grieving; she did not carve for her own curiosity. Curiosity was how you ended up asked questions.

Her hands were steady. Her breath was not.

She set the tip of her burin against the charm and cut a single fresh line, a question-mark of a groove, and opened herself the way she always opened herself—that slight loosening behind the sternum, the letting-down of the gate she kept barred—and the dead woman's voice poured through the bone into Freya's mind, thin and dry as winter grass:

*cold, so cold, where is my cat, I left the kettle—*

"Hush," Freya breathed. "Mistress Sorrel. Listen. There's a child at my fire speaking the old tongue—the true old tongue, the tongue before kingdoms. What is it saying?"

Silence. Then the dead woman's presence gathered itself, sharpened, and Freya felt her attention swing toward the hearth like a compass needle finding north. When Sorrel spoke again, her voice had changed. It had gone flat and careful, the voice of a woman looking at something she did not want to see.

*That is not the old tongue, carver. That is the older tongue. The tongue of the binding-oaths. Where did that child come from?*

"The river road. The storm."

*No.* The dead do not lie; that was the one mercy and the one horror of Freya's gift. *Nothing walks that road in this weather. Nothing walks that road at all anymore. Child, listen to me—*

But the charm had gone quiet in her hand, the dead woman recoiling from something at the edge of her perception, and in the hearth-light the child was watching Freya with those old, old eyes. Its lips moved. No sound came. Then sound came, one word, worn smooth as a river pebble from repetition, and this time—whether because the fever had broken something loose or because the word was older than language itself—Freya understood it.

Not in her ears. Deeper. The way she understood the dead.

"*Karath*," the child said. "*Karath. Karath.*"

And on the shelf above the hearth, every bone Freya had ever carved began to answer.

The sound filled the workshop like a struck bell that would not stop fading—dozens of dead voices braided into one, speaking in the oath-tongue, and though Freya could not translate the words, she could feel their shape the way she felt grief in a knucklebone: urgency, warning, and underneath it all a terror so vast and so old that it predated everyone in the room, living and dead alike.

The child curled tighter under its blanket, watching the shelf, watching her, and said the word once more—softer now, pleading.

"*Karath.*"

Freya stood in her small warm workshop with the storm sealing the valley shut around her, and understood, with the certainty of the dead behind her, that the invisibility she had built her whole life upon had just been knocked on, three times, politely, at dusk.

Whatever the child was carrying, it had walked days through flood-water to bring it here—to the one house in Thornmere with ears.

She banked the fire. She fetched broth and bread and sat beside the child while it ate like a small starving animal, and she did not sleep, and near dawn, when the fever peaked and the child thrashed and spoke whole rivers of the oath-tongue, one phrase surfaced again and again through the delirium—and this time Freya caught it whole, felt its meaning land in her chest like a stone dropped down a well:

*Aldric wakes. Aldric wakes. The crown is failing.*

Outside, the storm broke apart against the mountains, and far to the west, past the hedges and the borders and the safe forgetting of Thornmere, something that had been bound for twenty years shifted in its sleep—and smiled.
