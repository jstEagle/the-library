## 3. What the Bones Remember

The storm had spent itself by morning, but the river had not. It ran brown and swollen past the bottom of Freya's garden, dragging branches and a fence post and something pale that she chose not to look at too closely. The valley dripped. Every eave in Thornmere ticked like a slow clock, and the smell of wet earth and woodsmoke lay over everything the way a blanket lies over a sleeping child—gently, but with weight.

The child had not slept.

Freya had given it her bed and taken the settle by the hearth herself, and through the thin wall she had listened to the small body turn and turn again, restless as a hooked fish. Twice in the night the murmuring had started—that language like water running over old stones—and twice it had stopped when Freya spoke aloud, nonsense words, soothing words, *hush now, nothing will take you while I'm here*, until the sound sank back into breath.

Now the child sat at her worktable in the grey light, wrapped in Freya's spare wool shift, which fit like a tent, watching her hands.

That was the thing Freya could not stop noticing. Everyone who came to her workshop watched her hands eventually—it was natural; the carving was the whole point of a visit—but they watched them the way people watch anything: loosely, glancingly, their eyes wandering to the finished pieces on the shelves, to the fire, to the window. This child watched her hands the way a starving person watches bread. As though the movement of the knife along the bone were the only real thing left in the world.

"You'll want broth," Freya said, mostly to fill the silence. "And I'll want you to tell me your name, if you have one."

The child's mouth moved. What came out was the river-language again, three syllables that slid off the ear like oil, and then—the child's face crumpling with effort—a word that was almost a word, almost Thornmere's own tongue, worn down to a nub: "*Frrr-ya.*"

Her name. In a mouth that had never heard it before yesterday.

Freya set down her knife very carefully, because her hands had begun to shake, and shaking hands near a carver's blade was how people lost fingers.

* * *

She tried, through the morning, to be practical about it.

Practical meant porridge, which the child ate with the desperate efficiency of someone who had gone hungry longer than one night. Practical meant checking the small body for injury—bruised feet, cracked lips, a healing cut on one palm that looked days old—and finding nothing worse than exhaustion and a strange, faint scarring across the collarbone, like the ghost of a chain laid on skin. Practical meant walking out into the lane under the pretense of fetching milk from Marta Ostler and listening, instead, for what the neighbors were saying.

They were saying little. That was the mercy of Thornmere: after a storm, everyone was busy with their own soaked lives. But Widow Crane, passing with her goat, gave Freya a look that lingered half a heartbeat too long, and called out, "Heard voices in your house last night, carver. Odd-sounding ones."

"Praying," Freya said. "For my mother. Yesterday was her year-mind."

It was a good lie. It was even nearly true—yesterday *had* been her mother's year-mind, and Freya had spoken to her, in the way she always did, holding the little rib-carved bird she'd made of her mother's finger bone and hearing, faint and fond and unbearably far away, *mind the damp, love, and mind yourself.*

Widow Crane nodded and moved on, satisfied, and Freya stood in the lane with the milk jug cold against her ribs and understood that her invisibility had developed a crack in it. Voices in the night. A stranger seen by someone, somewhere, coming over the pass. A child that no one in the valley had birthed or fostered or lost. Thornmere forgot many things, but it did not forget a mystery, and mysteries in Thornmere had historically ended with pyres.

By the time she got home, she had stopped being practical and started being afraid.

* * *

The workshop smelled of linseed and dust and, beneath that, the particular dry sweetness of old bone. Freya lit two lamps against the gloom, though it was barely midday, because what she intended to do felt like a thing that should be done in light.

The bones had been talking all night. She had known it even before the child arrived—had felt it the way you feel weather coming in an old wound. Her shelves held forty years of work: memorial pieces mostly, the little carved relics grieving families commissioned so that a last word might be kept close. A sparrow for a drowned boy, carved from his shin. A lark for a grandmother. A wolf for a hunter who had died grinning at it. Each one held its dead the way a bell holds a ringing—not loudly, not always, but ready.

Since the child had crossed her threshold, every bell in the house had been ringing at once.

Not words yet. Not sense. A pressure, a crowding, like mourners pressing at a church door. And underneath it, threading through all the rest, one voice louder than the others—one voice that was not murmuring but *insisting*, in the old formal cadence of someone used to being obeyed:

*Carver. Carver. Set your tools aside and listen.*

"I'm listening," Freya said aloud, and felt foolish, and did it anyway.

She went to the shelf where she kept the oldest pieces, the ones inherited from her mother's own workshop, and took down the reliquary: a palm-sized figure of a woman in robes, carved from a femur so dense and yellowed it had to predate the valley's founding. Her mother had called it the Sister and said it had come north with the first families, and that no one living knew whose bone it was. It had never once spoken to Freya. Some relics were simply objects; the gift didn't catch in everything, any more than every field catches fire from one spark.

Tonight the Sister was warm. Warm as a living hand.

Freya sat down across the table from the child, set the reliquary between them, and waited.

It did not make her wait long.

The lamplight guttered though there was no draft, and the voice that came through the carved woman was nothing like the murmurs of the valley dead. Those were echoes, fading things, the last warmth leaving a room. This voice was *present*. It filled the workshop the way incense fills a chapel, and it was tired in a way that made Freya's chest ache, the tiredness of someone who has been shouting into wind for a very long time.

"So," the Sister said, in a woman's voice, low and worn smooth as a stair-stone. "It found you after all. Or you found it. With your kind it is usually both."

The child went rigid. Its eyes fixed on the little carved figure and widened, and it began to tremble, and it said—softly, in the river-language—one word that sounded, unmistakably, like a name.

"Yes," the voice agreed gently. "I know you. Be still, little vessel. I am Sera Vael. I was priestess at the Court of Candles, in the capital, in the reign of Aldric son of Aldmar." A pause, and something that might have been a dry laugh moved through the bones. "I say *was*. I have been other things since. Dead, chiefly."

Freya's mouth was dry. "You're the king's—" She stopped, recalculated forty years of history. "Aldric has reigned thirty years. He was crowned before I was born."

"He was crowned twice," Sera Vael said. "Once by the realm, and once by the thing that eats him. You know the story as the valleys keep it. Tell me the shape of it, carver, so I know how deep the rot goes."

"The Thorn," Freya said slowly. The words came up out of childhood, out of winter firesides and grandmothers' warnings. "When King Aldric was young, a spirit came out of the deep forest—a devouring thing, older than the kingdoms. It ate the old royal line, all of them, and it would have eaten him, but the priests bound it. Bound it into his crown, so it would feed on him alone and be caged forever, and the realm would be safe. And the price was—" she frowned, reaching for the ending every version agreed on— "the price was his voice. The binding took his true voice as its anchor. He has ruled thirty years without speaking above a whisper. They say it's penance. They say he chose silence in exchange for the realm's safety."

"They say," Sera Vael repeated, "because the truth was carved out of every record and every throat that remembered it. Sit down, carver. This is long, and you will want to be sitting."

Freya sat. Outside, the swollen river muttered over its stones. The child reached out one small hand and rested it, very lightly, on the warm bone of the reliquary, and closed its eyes.

* * *

"There was no binding," Sera Vael said. "There was a surrender dressed in vestments. Hear me.

"Aldric was twenty-six when the Thorn came south. It had already eaten the old dynasty—yes, eaten, that part is true, though not as beasts eat; it eats *names*, carver, it eats the self, and wears what remains. By the time it reached the palace it had learned to wear kings. We could not kill it. We could not drive it out. Every rite we knew failed, and the court died around us by inches—first their courage, then their judgment, then their faces, which began to look alike, all of them, like masks cut from the same pale wood.

"And so we did the only thing left. The Thorn wanted a crown; it wanted to *be* the realm. We let it have the crown—but we bound the binding, and this is the part that was burned from every book: a binding must anchor to something the spirit cannot reach. Not gold, not stone, not holy ground. It anchored to the king's true voice. His name-self. The word that he is. While that voice exists apart from him, the Thorn cannot fully merge with its crown, cannot spread past the palace walls, cannot finish its meal. It is a man chained to a corpse of his own office, and the chain is his own stolen soul.

"We hid the voice. Not in a vault—the Thorn would have found a vault. We hid it the only place it could not look." The priestess's voice gentled. "In blood. In a line of vessels, each one carrying it forward, each one born knowing nothing, raised knowing nothing, passing it on unknowing at marriage or at death. Generations of ordinary people, farmers and fishwives, carrying a king's soul inside them like a coal wrapped in wool.

"For thirty years it worked. For thirty years the Thorn gnawed at its cage and the realm slept safe and the king whispered his way through audiences, and everyone called it piety." The voice hardened. "But bindings are not stone, carver. They are rope. They fray. And ours was tied to mortal flesh, and flesh ends, and the line thinned as lines do—until now there is one vessel left. One child. Walking mud roads in the rain, alone, because the last keeper died and the voice *ran*, the way a frightened animal runs, looking for shelter it remembers from a story."

Freya looked at the child. The child looked back, and its eyes were wrong—not wrong in color or shape, but wrong in *depth*, like windows into a room much larger than the house that held them. And in those depths, for just a moment, Freya saw something ancient and exhausted and desperately polite, something that inclined its head to her the way a king inclines to a subject, and then the moment passed and there was only a tired, muddy, hungry child again.

"It speaks through it?" Freya asked. "The king's voice?"

"When it can. When the child sleeps, mostly, or when the vessel weakens. The child fights it—bravely, poorly. It does not understand what it carries. It knows only that the voice is loud and the road was long and everyone it ever loved told it to run north and find the bone-witch." A pause. "That would be you, carver. The last keepers' lore named you. The valley that forgets magic keeps one witch, like a coin sewn into a coat lining."

"I'm not a witch," Freya said automatically, and heard how it sounded, and hated it.

"No," Sera Vael said, and there was something terribly kind in it. "You are worse. You are useful."

* * *

"Tell me the rest," Freya said. "Tell me why it came *now*."

The lamp flames leaned, though the air was still.

"Because the binding fails at a coronation," the priestess said. "This is the last law of it, and the cruelest. The binding was sworn on succession—on the continuity of the crown. While Aldric wears it, the Thorn is caged. But a coronation is a door. The moment the crown passes to another head, the terms of the binding complete. The debt is paid. The voice-anchor is released back to the crown—to the Thorn—and the cage opens.

"Prince Corin takes the crown in nineteen days."

The number landed in Freya's chest like a stone dropped down a well. Nineteen days. She thought of the proclamations nailed to the meeting-house door each autumn, the ones nobody read: *By order of the Crown, the heir apparent shall be crowned at the turning of the season.* She had never once wondered what happened in the capital at such times. Why would she? Nothing that happened in the capital had ever touched Thornmere. That was the entire architecture of her life: the valley kept things out, and distance kept her safe, and the world beyond the pass was a story told by travelers.

"Nineteen days," she repeated. "And then?"

"And then the Thorn finishes eating its king, and wears him out, and turns to the realm. It has been patient for thirty years, carver. It will not be patient after. Within a season the court will be masks. Within five years, the provinces. It eats names, remember. It eats the self. Your neighbors will still walk and talk and plant their barley, and there will be no one home behind their eyes, and no one will notice, because the masks are very good." The voice dropped lower. "And it will come for the vessels first. There is one vessel. It is sitting at your table eating the last of your bread."

Freya's hands were flat on the table. She noticed distantly that they had stopped shaking. Fear, past a certain depth, went very quiet.

"How do we unbind it? Take the voice back to the king—surely that breaks—"

"You do not understand what you are asking. The voice cannot simply be *returned*. The binding must be unwoven at the source—at the binding-stone beneath the palace, where we swore it, where the anchor was first cut free. And the unwinding requires a vessel of living bone willing to hold the Thorn as it comes loose. Willing, carver. That word is load-bearing. The whole rite rests on it. A coerced vessel shatters, and the Thorn escapes loose into the world with no crown to contain its hunger at all." The priestess sighed, a sound like wind through a ribcage. "There is more. There is always more. But first—"

"First," Freya said, "you'll tell me why the child is dying."

The silence that followed had texture. The child opened its eyes.

"Look at its hands," Sera Vael said quietly. "Under the nails. Look at its gums, when next it sleeps. A vessel carrying a king's voice for this long, this far, with no training, no rites, no rest—the friction of it burns. The voice is too large for the house it hides in. It is burning the house down around itself. Three weeks, perhaps four, even without the coronation. The child was sent to you to be saved, carver, and it arrived already halfway to being a relic." A pause, and when the voice resumed it was stripped of all its formality, just a dead woman's grief moving through old bone. "I am sorry. I guarded this secret for my whole life and it cost everything, and I would pay it again, and I am sorry. The realm was worth it. I believed that. Most days I still believe it. But the bill has come due, and it has come due in the shape of a child at a bone-carver's table, and I do not know how to make that right. I only know that you are the last hand it can fall into."

Freya sat for a long moment. The lamps burned steadily now. On the shelf behind her, forty years of the valley's dead murmured in their sleep, sparrows and larks and wolves, all of them safe, all of them small, all of them hers.

She thought: *the valley keeps things out.* She thought: *that was the story.* And beneath the thought, rising like groundwater, the colder knowledge she had been refusing since the child first knocked: that Thornmere did not keep magic out. Thornmere kept magic *asleep*. That the silence she had built her life inside was not peace but anesthesia, and that somewhere beyond the pass a devouring thing had been wearing a kingdom for thirty years while she carved sparrows for grieving mothers and called it a life.

The child tugged her sleeve. When she turned, it pointed one small finger at the reliquary, then at its own chest, then out the window—north, toward the pass, toward the capital it had crossed mountains to reach—and it said, in the river-language, in a voice far too old for its body, a single sentence.

"She says you know what it means," Sera Vael translated softly. "And she asks—little one, forgive me—" the priestess's voice caught, "—she asks if you will help. She asks it the way children ask, carver. As though refusal were a thing that happens to other people."

Outside, far down the valley road, a dog began to bark—not the idle barking of dogs at shadows, but the flat, continuous alarm of a dog facing something it did not understand. Then another joined it. Then a man's voice, shouting, muffled by rain-heavy air.

Freya rose, blew out one lamp, and went to the window.

On the far side of the flooded river, at the bend where the north road came down off the pass, she could see riders. Four of them, maybe five, in colors she had never seen in Thornmere in her life: the black and silver of the Crown's justiciars, moving slow through the mud, reading the valley the way hunters read a trail.

They had not come for the coronation. Justiciars did not ride circuit for pageantry.

They had come for a witch and a changeling, and the storm had washed their quarry straight to her door.

Behind her, in the darkened workshop, the child began—in a whisper, in a dead king's stolen voice, in perfect courtly High Tongue—to pray.
