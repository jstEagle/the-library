## 4. The Justiciar's Road

The rain had followed Ser Dain Voss for three days, and he had begun to think of it as a companion—unwelcome, persistent, and impossible to argue with.

He rode at the head of six men through the hill country north of the Ashwater, where the road narrowed to a ribbon of mud and the pines pressed close enough to drip cold water down the back of his collar. Behind him, his justiciars muttered and hunched in their saddles. They were good men, most of them. Brannick with his grey beard and his habit of humming when he was nervous. Young Tam, who still apologized to horses. Ollard, silent as a fence post, who had once carried Dain two miles on his back after the ambush at Redford Bridge and never once mentioned it afterward.

They were good men, and they were tired, and they did not understand why they were riding into the teeth of a storm to hunt a bone-carver and a child.

"The warrant's thin," Brannick said that evening, as they made camp beneath an overhang of rock and the rain drummed its endless fingers on the leather of their cloaks. He said it carefully, the way he said everything that might be mistaken for complaint. "A rumor out of a shepherd's mouth. A woman who talks to bones. A child that shouldn't exist. That's not evidence, ser. That's a fireside story."

Dain fed a branch to the fire and watched it catch. "The order came from the Lord Justiciar himself."

"The Lord Justiciar signs a hundred orders a week."

"This one came sealed in black wax." He turned the memory over again, as he had every night since leaving the capital. Black wax was reserved for matters touching the crown's own safety. The seal had been pressed deep and clean, the royal thorn-and-crown stamped so hard it had nearly gone through the parchment. Someone wanted this done quickly. Someone wanted it done quietly. "And the reward posted at Waymark Cross was drawn on the privy purse. Not provincial funds. The crown is paying for this out of its own hand, Brannick. That means the crown considers it important."

Brannick grunted and said nothing more, but his silence had a shape to it, and Dain knew the shape well. It was the silence of a man who thought his commander was wrong and was too loyal to say so twice.

Dain lay back against his saddle and let the rain sing him toward sleep.

He had been a justiciar for nineteen years. He had taken the oath at seventeen, kneeling on cold flagstones before a magistrate with breath like sour wine, and the words had gone into him like iron into green wood: *To seek truth without fear. To deal justice without favor. To serve the crown as the crown serves the law.* He had believed every word then. He believed them now. That was the whole of him, really—the belief, worn smooth as a river stone by two decades of use. When men in the villages saw his white cloak coming up the road, some of them hid their daughters and some of them ran for the priest, but a surprising number of them simply waited, because Ser Dain Voss listened before he judged, and once in a great while he even changed his mind.

There was the matter of Hollowmere.

He did not let himself think of Hollowmere often, but the rain always brought it back. Eleven years ago. A royal order, black wax again, commanding the burning of a village that had sheltered a tax rebel. Dain had ridden there ahead of the soldiers, alone, and found nothing but farmers and a lame priest and a granary already half-empty from a bad harvest. He had sat in the headman's house all night drinking barley tea while the man's children slept behind a curtain, and in the morning he had written his report: *No rebel found. No shelter given. Village innocent.* He had lied in an official report, his first and only lie in nineteen years of service, and the village had stood, and the rebel had been caught three months later forty leagues away, and no one had ever known what Dain Voss had done in the rain outside Hollowmere except Dain Voss.

He thought about that sometimes. About how easy the lie had been, once he'd decided to tell it. How clean his hand had felt afterward.

*Truth without fear,* he reminded himself now, staring at the fire. *This time you'll find the truth first. This time there will be no need for lies.*

* * *

They reached the last waystation before Thornmere on the fifth day, a squat tollhouse wedged between the road and a river the color of old pewter. The tollkeeper was a broad woman with a face like a closed door, and she took their coins and answered their questions with the flat economy of someone who had learned that information was also a toll.

"A carver?" She shrugged. "Thornmere's full of carvers. Wood, mostly. Some stone."

"A bone-carver," Dain said. "A woman. Freya Hartwell."

Something moved behind the woman's eyes—quick, quickly hidden. Dain had spent nineteen years learning to read faces, and he read hers now the way he'd read a ledger: there was an entry there, and someone had tried to scratch it out.

"Never heard the name," she said.

"You have heard it." He kept his voice mild. Mildness worked better than iron, nine times out of ten. "I'm not here to hurt her. If she's done nothing wrong, she has nothing to fear from me. I only want to ask questions."

The tollkeeper looked at him for a long moment. Rain ran off the eaves between them in a silver curtain. "Everyone says that," she said finally. "The ones who mean it and the ones who don't. You all sound the same when you say it." She counted his coins back onto the board between them, one by one, deliberate as a funeral bell. "Road past here forks at the standing stone. Left goes down into the valley. Right goes around, adds two days. Valley folk don't love visitors, justiciar. They especially don't love yours."

"Why mine?"

"Because you wear the crown on your back." She nodded at his cloak, at the stitched sigil of the thorn-and-crown. "That mark used to mean something else, before the old king died. Used to be people saw it and felt safer. Now—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening, as if she'd said a coin too many. "Two coppers for the bridge. Move along."

Dain moved along. But he turned the woman's words over all afternoon as they rode the high road around the valley's rim, and by evening they had grown sharp edges.

*That mark used to mean something else.*

He knew the history; every justiciar did. King Aldric, crowned twenty-six years ago, the young king with the bright reputation, the reformer, the one who had sworn to break the barons' private wars and open the courts to common folk. And then, eight years into his reign, the change. The histories called it *the Quiet Years*—as if the king had merely grown thoughtful, contemplative. But Dain's father had been a soldier under Aldric's early commanders, and his father had said otherwise, late at night, with ale in him: *The king didn't grow quiet, boy. The king went quiet. There's a difference. Something got into him, or something got out of him, and after that the laws started coming down like winter.*

The witch-laws had come in the Quiet Years. Dain had enforced them. He had arrested hedge-healers and charm-sellers and one poor madwoman in Lowmarsh who claimed she could smell storms coming, and he had done it because the law was the law, and the law came from the crown, and the crown—

The crown served the law. The law served the crown. He had never examined which way the wheel actually turned. There had never seemed to be a reason to.

Until now. Until a black-wax warrant for a bone-carver and a child, with a reward from the privy purse, and a tollkeeper looking at his cloak like it was something that crawled.

* * *

On the seventh day they took the left fork, down into Thornmere.

The descent was long and steep, the road switchbacking through pine shadow, and somewhere along it the quality of the air changed. Dain noticed it before he could name it—a lightness, almost, a feeling like walking out of a room where someone had been weeping. His horse lifted its head. Beside him, Tam straightened in the saddle and took a deep breath and looked embarrassed about it.

"It's the valley," Brannick said quietly. "My grandmother was born near here. She used to say Thornmere was the one place the world kept its mouth shut."

"What does that mean?"

"No idea. Grandmothers say things." But Brannick crossed himself with two fingers, an old soldier's superstition, and would not meet Dain's eye.

The village appeared at dusk: grey stone, slate roofs, smoke rising straight in the windless cold. Fields lay fallow and neat. Goats watched them pass with flat yellow indifference. And the people—Dain marked them the way he marked everything—came out of their doors and stood in the lanes and watched the white cloaks come down the street, and their faces held nothing he could use. No guilt. No fear. Only a kind of patient, settled watchfulness, like sheep observing dogs from the far side of a fence they trusted.

At the inn, the innkeeper told him yes, there was a bone-carver, everyone knew Freya Hartwell, she lived at the end of Mill Lane, she carved keepsakes for funerals, lovely work, very respected. No, she'd caused no trouble. No, no one had seen any child. Yes, they'd be happy to answer any other questions, and would the justiciars like supper?

It was all true. Dain could feel that it was all true, the way he could feel a false coin by weight. And that was what troubled him as he climbed Mill Lane in the blue last light, because the warrant described a fugitive, and the village described a neighbor, and both could not be right unless the village was lying—and no one lied that smoothly, that unanimously, without practice.

Unless, said a small voice at the base of his skull, the warrant was the thing that was wrong.

He put the thought away. He was good at putting thoughts away. Nineteen years of practice.

The house at the end of Mill Lane was dark. He knocked anyway, twice, and heard nothing but the tick of cooling timber, and when he tried the latch the door swung open on a workshop that smelled of tallow and dust and something older underneath, something mineral and sweet, like a crypt after rain.

Bones hung from the rafters on fine cords, dozens of them, each carved with work so delicate it made his chest ache—wings, leaves, faces, letters in scripts he didn't know. On the bench lay a half-finished piece: a sparrow, wings folded, carved from what looked like the knucklebone of an ox. And beside the sparrow, out of place among the shavings, a child's wooden whistle. Chewed at one end.

Dain stood in the doorway a long time. He did not touch anything. That was the rule, and he kept the rules; it was the only thing he had ever been entirely sure of.

"Warrant says witch," Brannick murmured behind him. "Looks like a craftsman to me."

"Craftsmen don't flee their villages the night before soldiers arrive." Except she hadn't fled—not yet. The hearth ash was soft. The bed, visible through the inner door, had been recently slept in. They had missed her by hours, perhaps less. A woman and a child, moving fast, in country she knew and he did not.

Dain walked the workshop slowly, reading it the way he read rooms, and it was near the window that he found the thing that stopped him: a carving pinned to the wall among the others, a small roundel of bone no bigger than his palm, and cut into it a face. A man's face, gaunt and proud, with a crown rendered in lines so fine they might have been spider silk. Beneath it, in careful lettering: *ALDRIC, BY THE GRACE OF THE THORN, KING.*

By the grace of the Thorn. Not *by the grace of the gods*. Not *by right and blood*. Every official document in the kingdom bore the formula *by the grace of the gods and the blood of kings*, and had for three hundred years. But this—this was the other version, the old version, the one Dain had seen exactly once before, carved into the lintel of the palace chapel where no one ever looked, worn nearly smooth by centuries of hands.

*By the grace of the Thorn.*

His skin prickled. Outside, the rain had stopped, and the silence it left behind was enormous, and in that silence Dain became aware of a sound he could not account for: faint, rhythmic, coming from the bones hanging in the rafters. Cords creaking. Bones knocking together, gently, gently, though there was no wind.

Though there was no wind at all.

"Ser?" Tam, in the doorway, white-faced. "Did you hear—"

"I heard." Dain took the roundel down and turned it in his fingers. The little crowned face seemed to look at him with an expression he could not parse—pity, maybe. Or warning. "Pack nothing. Touch nothing. We ride for the border town tonight. She'll be making for the capital, and we'll be ahead of her by morning if we push."

"And the report, ser? The Lord Justiciar expects word."

Dain looked at the roundel a moment longer. Then he set it back on its nail, precisely where it had hung, and squared it with his thumb.

"Tell them the trail leads south," he said. "Tell them we're following it."

It was not a lie. Not quite. He filed the difference away in the same drawer where Hollowmere lived, and told himself the drawer was not getting full.

Behind him, unnoticed, the bones in the rafters had gone still—all but one. A small wing-bone, turning slowly on its cord, pointing south. Pointing, though no man in the room marked it, not toward the capital at all.

Toward the fens.
