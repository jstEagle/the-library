## 5. Leaving Thornmere

They left before dawn, while the mist still owned the valley floor and the village slept behind its shuttered windows, dreaming the small, safe dreams of people who had never been asked to leave.

Freya had packed in the dark, by touch rather than sight, because she could not bear to see her workshop by lamplight one last time. Seeing it would have made it a farewell, and farewells were things she had spent forty years learning to avoid. She took her carver's knives wrapped in oilcloth—the good set, the ones her mother had ground from cart-spring steel—and a satchel of finished relics she could not explain even to herself. A grandmother's knuckle-bone that still hummed with lullabies. A soldier's tooth that whispered of a river he'd never reached. Things that belonged to no one living in Thornmere, and so were hers to carry.

The child stood in the doorway while she worked, watching her with those eyes that were too old for the small face around them. Wren—that was what Freya had begun calling the child privately, though she hadn't dared say it aloud yet—wore the coat Freya had cut down from one of her own, sleeves rolled three times at the wrists, and carried nothing. The dead needed nothing. That was the trouble with them.

"We go east," Freya said, for perhaps the tenth time since the priestess's bones had told her what she must do. "Over the Shepherd's Stair, past the boundary stones. You remember the way I showed you on the map?"

The child tilted its head—a gesture Freya was learning to read as *yes*, or as close to yes as the ancient tongue allowed. Then it raised one hand and touched its own throat, and spoke a single syllable in that language older than kingdoms, a sound like water moving under ice.

"I know," Freya said. "I know you're dying. I'm coming."

She banked the hearth. She left the door unlocked—not because she expected to return, but because locking it would have been a lie, and she had grown suddenly, violently tired of lies, even the small kind a person tells herself about doors.

* * *

The path up the Shepherd's Stair was a goat track when it was anything at all, switchbacking up through gorse and scree until Thornmere became a silver thread below them—the river, not the village; the village was already lost in morning shadow—and then not even that. Freya climbed with her breath sawing in her chest and the child trotting ahead of her, sure-footed as something born on cliffs, pausing every hundred paces to look back and wait with an patience that felt less like childhood and more like courtesy.

By midmorning they reached the saddle where the valley's rim dipped low enough to cross, and there stood the boundary stones.

There were seven of them, though most maps showed five. They were older than any carving Freya had ever made—older than the church in Thornmere, older than the king's grandfather, older, she suspected now, than the kingdom whose border they marked. Granite gone black with lichen, each taller than a man, sunk into the earth like teeth into gum. Shepherds called them the Seven Sisters and swore you shouldn't count them twice, because sometimes there were eight.

Freya had lived her whole life within sight of them and never once crossed them. No one in Thornmere did. It wasn't law. It was something deeper than law—a bone-deep understanding, passed from mother to child with the milk, that the valley kept what it kept for a reason. Magic couldn't cross the stones. Neither, the old stories said, should anyone who valued their soul.

She stopped ten paces short and stood there, wind pulling at her hair, and understood that she was afraid in a way she had never been afraid in her life. Not of what waited beyond. Of what waited within herself.

Because she knew—I had always known, some quiet cellar-room of her mind had always known—that the silence of Thornmere was not the world's natural state. It was a held breath. And she was about to walk out of it.

"Stay behind me," she told the child, uselessly, in the tone people use for prayers.

She stepped between the stones.

* * *

Later, she would try to describe it to herself, lying sleepless beside a cold fire, and fail every time. But this is what it was:

It was deafness ending.

Imagine a woman born deaf who has learned, over forty years, to make a life of silence—to read the world through vibration, through the shiver of floorboards, through the careful grammar of hands. Imagine that woman walking through a doorway and having hearing poured into her all at once: not birdsong first, not gentle things, but every sound in the world arriving together, unsorted, screaming.

That was what the dead were like beyond the stones.

They came at her like weather. Like a tide. Like standing in a river of voices with her mouth open.

Freya went to her knees in the wet grass on the far side of the boundary and pressed both palms against her ears, which helped not at all, because the dead did not speak through air. They spoke through bone. Her own bones rang with them, her skull a struck bell, her ribs humming like fence wire in a gale.

In Thornmere, the dead she heard were few and quiet—generations of valley folk who had died in their beds, mostly, their last words worn smooth as river pebbles by the time they reached her knife. *Tell my daughter the blue dress.* *The dog knows where I hid it.* *I'm not afraid, tell them I wasn't afraid.* She had built her whole life on those whispers. She had thought she understood grief.

She had understood nothing.

Beyond the stones, the dead were a nation. They were multitudes. They were wars and famines and plagues and shipwrecks, they were the drowned and the burned and the buried wrong, they were thousands upon thousands upon thousands, and none of them were at peace, because peace required someone to listen, and almost no one listened anymore.

*—cold, it's so cold, why did they put me in the north field—*

*—the toll, I never paid the toll, tell my wife I never paid—*

*—burning, we were burning, the justiciars said it was lawful—*

*—my baby, where is my baby, I put her down and when I woke—*

Freya screamed. She was distantly aware of screaming, aware of the child crouched beside her with its small hand pressed flat against her back, and aware that through that hand something flowed—a steadiness, a dam against the flood, as if the ancient tongue itself could hold back lesser voices. The child spoke then, one long low phrase in that language like water under ice, and the roar dimmed. Not silenced. Dimmed. Held at arm's length, the way a strong man might hold back a crowd.

Slowly, shaking, Freya lowered her hands from her ears.

The world was beautiful. That was the obscenity of it. The world beyond the stones was green and gold and ruthlessly alive—rolling moorland falling away eastward in waves of bracken, hawks turning in a sky so wide it made Thornmere's little bowl of heaven seem like a lid on a pot—and threaded through all of it, invisible, insistent, the dead. Voices in the stones of the boundary markers themselves. Voices in the road. Voices in a cairn on the far hill, in a dry well, in the ribs of a sheep long dead bleaching in the grass. Every place humans had ever been, they had left their echoes, and the echoes were starving.

"How," she said aloud, and her voice cracked in the middle of the word, "how does anyone bear this?"

The child looked at her with pity. Actual pity, ancient and unhurried, and said nothing, because there was nothing to say. No one bore it. That was why the gift had fled into the valleys, into the borders, into the forgotten places where magic pooled thin and quiet. The bone-speakers of the old world had either gone silent or gone mad, and the world had decided, in its practical way, to forget them.

Freya knelt in the grass and wept—not elegantly, not the way she wept for other people's dead, but the ugly animal weeping of a woman who has just understood the size of her own cage. Because here was the truth the flood had shown her, the truth she would carry now like a stone in her chest:

She had thought she was hiding from the neighbors. From fear, from fire, from the word *witch*. She had told herself that for forty years. But the valley had also hidden *this* from her. All her life she had believed she served the dead, and the dead of Thornmere had loved her for it, and that love had been real—but it had been a garden's worth of service in a world of wilderness. There were dead here who had waited two hundred years for a single listener. There were dead here who had never once been heard, whose last words were still caught in their teeth, and every year of her comfortable, careful, invisible life in Thornmere had been a year she had not spent finding them.

She had traded the world for safety and called it contentment.

The child waited while she wept, and when she was done it offered her a strip of dried apple from its pocket, solemnly, the way you'd offer bread to a stranger at a funeral, and Freya laughed—a broken, wet sound—and took it.

* * *

They walked east through the morning, and Freya learned to build walls.

Not real walls—there was no shutting the dead out entirely, she understood that now; the gift didn't work like a door but like skin, and skin could only be thickened, never removed. But she found, hour by hour, a discipline. She let the voices become a surf rather than a drowning. She learned to sort them by nearness, by urgency, by the texture of their need. The freshly dead shouted. The long dead murmured. The ones who had died violently never stopped moving, their words circling like birds over a battlefield.

And she learned, to her sorrow, how much of it was the crown's doing.

Every few miles the road grew more traveled, and the travelers' relics—horseshoes, buttons, a rusted soldier's buckle dropped in the mud—spoke to her whether she wanted them to or not, and the story they told was the same story told a hundred ways. Taxes that ate whole harvests. Sons taken for the king's roads and the king's wars. A neighbor denounced and dragged away, and the neighbors who watched saying nothing, saying nothing, saying nothing. And underneath it all, everywhere, like rot under paint: obedience. A sweetness in people's surrender that frightened her more than cruelty would have. In Thornmere, folk obeyed the reeve because he was the reeve and their fathers had obeyed his father. Out here, people obeyed the way puppets obeyed strings—with a slackness, a willingness, that made the hair rise on her arms.

"The Thorn," she murmured, and a dead woman's voice rose from a milestone to answer her, thin and furious: *it drinks through the crown, carver, it drinks through the crown, it has been drinking for thirty years.*

"I know," Freya whispered. "I'm going to stop it."

The voice laughed. It was not a kind laugh, but it was not cruel either—it was the laugh of someone who had heard promises before, from the ground, for a very long time. Then it faded, and Freya walked on with the child's warm hand in hers, and the moor opened before them, and the dead of the wide world pressed close around her like a congregation that had finally, after centuries, been given a priest.

Near dusk they crested a long ridge and saw the road below: a proper road now, gravelled and crowned, running straight as a rule toward a smudge of woodsmoke on the horizon. A town. The first town. And along the road, at intervals, posts had been planted, and on the posts hung notices, white paper pale in the failing light, and even at this distance Freya could see the royal seal stamped in black wax at the bottom of each one.

The child stopped walking. Its grip on her hand tightened until the small bones ground against her own.

Freya squinted. She could not read the notices from here. But she didn't need to, because the dead on the nearest post—a post that had, she realized with a lurch, been grown rather than planted, its wood twisted into shapes like grasping fingers—whispered up to her on the evening wind, in a chorus of a dozen stolen voices, all saying the same thing:

*A witch. A changeling. Seen on the eastern moors. The king's justice follows.*

And beneath the printed words, in a voice that did not belong to any of the dead but rose up through the post itself, from whatever dark root fed it, something else said—softly, patiently, with the satisfaction of a trap feeling weight settle into it:

*Come, little listener. We have been waiting to hear you.*
