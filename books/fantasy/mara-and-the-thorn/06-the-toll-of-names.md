## 6. The Toll of Names

Three days out of Thornmere, Freya learned that the road had a smell.

She had never known this before. In the valley, the air had been one thing only—pine and cold stone and the mineral breath of the river—and she had assumed the world beyond was simply more of the same, stretched thinner. But the road smelled of everything at once: dung and woodsmoke, crushed nettle, the sweet rot of fallen apples in an orchard they passed at dusk, the iron tang of a stream running red through peat. It was too much. It was wonderful. She hated how much it was.

The child walked beside her and did not seem to notice any of it.

That was the thing Freya could not stop watching for—the not-noticing. The child's eyes moved over the world the way eyes move over wallpaper. When a hawk screamed overhead on the second morning, the child did not flinch. When they passed a shrine to some border-god whose name Freya didn't know, its little stone face worn smooth by weather and offerings, the child stopped, stared, and said something in that old tongue—three syllables, low and level, like a stone dropped into a deep well—and walked on.

Freya was beginning to be able to hear the shape of the language even when she couldn't parse it. That frightened her more than anything else on the road. Languages were supposed to stay foreign unless you learned them. This one seemed to be learning *her*.

*You're listening to it in your sleep,* she told herself that night, wrapped in her cloak beneath a hawthorn hedge while the child slept like a felled tree beside her. *That's all. The mind fills empty rooms with whatever noise is nearest.*

But she dreamed in three syllables, low and level, and woke with her jaw aching from clenching.

* * *

On the fourth day they came down out of the hills into a country of long wet meadows, and Freya decided, with the particular stubbornness of a woman who has run out of patience with herself, that she would learn the child's name or die trying.

It wasn't sentiment. She told herself it wasn't sentiment. A name was a tool. Her whole trade was built on names—carve the name into the bone and the dead could hold onto themselves a little longer, like fingers gripping a ledge. *Maren, who kept bees. Tomas, who feared water and drowned anyway.* Without their names, the dead slipped. She had seen it happen, carved a hundred relics for people who arrived at her door unable to say what their mother had been called, and those relics always came out wrong somehow, blurred, the voice behind them muffled as if speaking through wool.

And this child was slipping. She could see it happening day by day, the way you see a candle burning down. The periods when the child was merely a child—stubborn, hungry, fond of walking along the tops of walls when there were walls to walk along—were growing shorter. More and more often Freya looked over and found that ancient stillness looking back out of the small face, patient and vast and terribly old, wearing the child like a borrowed coat.

So: the name. If a name could hold a dead man to his bone, perhaps it could hold a living child to herself.

She tried the obvious approaches first. She pointed at herself and said, "Freya." The child watched her mouth with polite attention and repeated, in the old tongue, a word that meant—or so Freya guessed—*the one who shapes*, because the child then pointed at Freya's knife, at her hands, at the little half-finished hare-bone whistle she'd been whittling at night, and said the same word each time.

"Not what I do," Freya said. "*Me*. My name."

The child tilted its head. And then, in accented but unmistakable Common, said: "Names are doors."

Freya sat down hard on a milestone.

It was the first sentence the child had spoken in anything resembling her own language since the storm-night at her door. The voice was thin, rusty, a hinge unswung for years—but it was a child's voice, high and slightly hoarse, and there was something underneath it that made Freya's chest hurt: fear. The child was afraid of its own words.

"What do you mean, doors?" Freya asked carefully, the way you'd speak to a bird you wanted to land on your hand.

The child's mouth worked. Its small fists clenched. For a moment Freya watched a struggle play itself out across the young face like weather crossing a valley—something pushing up from below, something holding down from above—and then the stillness won, the old patience settled back over the features like frost, and the child turned away and began, with maddening calm, to gather firewood.

"Names are doors," Freya muttered to the milestone. "Wonderful. Doors for whom?"

* * *

The answer came that night, and it came from the bones, because everything in Freya's life eventually came from the bones.

They had sheltered in the ruin of a shepherd's cot, its roof half-collapsed, its walls thick enough to break the wind. Freya had taken out her traveling kit—the few relics she'd carried from Thornmere, wrapped in wool—and done what she did every night now, what had become as necessary as sleep: she'd listened.

Mostly what she heard was grief. The wider world's dead were loud, angry, numerous; she'd learned that much crossing the border, learned it so painfully she'd vomited the first hour outside the valley, her skull ringing with voices like a church of broken bells. But she'd also learned to sort them, the way a woman who lives near a river learns to sleep under its sound. The recently dead wailed. The long dead murmured. And every so often, if she held a relic just so and went very quiet inside, one voice would come forward out of the crowd and speak plainly, as if leaning close to a window.

That night it was an old man's voice, dry as seed-husks, coming through a knucklebone she'd carved for a farmer's widow two winters past.

*"Ask the small one its name,"* the old man said. *"Not the name it carries. The name it was given."*

Freya's hands went cold around the bone. "How do you know about—" she began aloud, and stopped, feeling foolish, feeling the dark cot full of listening air.

*"We all know,"* the old man said, and there was something in his tone she had almost never heard from the dead toward the living: gentleness. *"The ones it passes through—we feel it go by. Like a cold draft in a warm house. Ask it its name, carver. Every vessel keeps one. They hide it deep, deeper than the king's voice, because the name is the last thing the old hunger cannot digest. A name is the hook the soul hangs on. Take away a soul's name and there is nothing left to pull free."*

The relic went quiet. Outside, wind worried the ruined thatch. Across the dead fire, the child slept curled like a comma, and in its sleep its face was finally, wholly young.

A name is the hook the soul hangs on.

Freya lay back and stared at the broken rafters and understood, with a slow cold spreading through her chest, what *names are doors* had meant. Not doors for the child. Doors *through* the child. Every time someone spoke to the vessel, spoke *at* it, the old thing surfaced to answer. But a true name—a hidden name, kept secret even from the king's voice—might be a door that opened the other way.

* * *

In the morning she tried again, and this time she did not point at herself. She built a fire instead, and made porridge, and let the child eat, and waited until the bowl was scraped clean and the child was sitting in the early light picking burrs out of its sleeve—an ordinary child doing an ordinary chore, and Freya's heart turned over at how ordinary it looked, how desperately it must want to look that way always.

"When I was small," Freya said, conversationally, to the fire, "my grandmother called me Lark. Not Freya—Lark. Because I sang badly and constantly." She fed another stick to the flames. "Nobody else ever used it. She died when I was nine, and after that nobody in the world knew that name but me. I've never said it out loud since. Saying it felt like letting her go."

The child had gone very still. Burrs forgotten between its fingers.

"I'm not asking you to tell me yours," Freya said. "I'm telling you why a person might keep one. So it stays hers. So it stays *safe*."

Silence. The fire cracked. Somewhere in the wet meadows a curlew cried its bubbling note.

Then the child spoke, and its voice was the thin, rusty, frightened child-voice again, and this time it did not lose the struggle: "If I give it to you, the other one will hear it."

"It might," Freya admitted. "I won't lie to you. But here's what an old dead farmer taught me last night—" and she heard herself say it, *an old dead farmer taught me*, as if that were a normal sentence, as if her whole life were normal, "—a name isn't just a door. It's a hook. It's the one thing the old hunger can't swallow. Your name might be the only rope you have back to yourself. And a rope does no good coiled up in a chest. Someone has to hold the end of it."

The child looked at her for a long time. Long enough that Freya counted her own heartbeats and lost count and started again. There was a war on behind those eyes, and she understood suddenly that it had been running for years, maybe generations, maybe longer than that—somebody's daughter trained to hide the one word that mattered, handed down like contraband, like a coal carried in cupped hands through a long dark country.

"Wren," the child said.

Just that. One syllable, soft as a wingbeat.

And the world changed.

It was not dramatic. That was the terrible, beautiful thing—it was barely anything at all. The ancient stillness drained out of the small face like water out of a basin, and what rushed in behind it was *color*: wariness, exhaustion, a flicker of humor at the corners of the mouth, and underneath all of it a bottomless, staggering relief, the look of a drowning person whose foot has found bottom. Wren blinked. Looked down at her own hands as though confirming ownership. Then looked up at Freya, and her eyes filled, and she said, in that rusty Common, in a rush, as if the door might slam:

"You said it. You said it and I'm—I'm *here*. I'm always under it, do you understand? Underneath. Listening to it use my mouth. Years. I don't know how many years. And you said my name and it's like—like a window opening in a house where all the shutters have been nailed shut since before I can remember—"

She ran out of breath. Or courage. She pressed both fists against her mouth.

Freya found she couldn't speak at all. She did the only thing her body knew how to do: she reached across the dead fire and took one of the small grimy hands and held it, the way she'd held the hands of a hundred grieving families, the way she'd held the hands of the dead themselves sometimes, arranging them for burial. Warm. Alive. A pulse going fast as a bird's against her palm.

"Wren," she said again, deliberately, tasting it, hanging her own weight on the rope. "I'm holding the end. You climb."

Wren laughed. It was a wrecked, wet, astonishing sound, and then she was crying, and then laughing again, and Freya pulled her across the ashes into an embrace that the child resisted for exactly one heartbeat before clinging with the desperate strength of the starved.

* * *

They walked that day through country Freya barely saw, because she was busy cataloguing miracles.

Wren talked. That was the miracle, the plainest one: the child talked, in fragments, in bursts, hoarse and out of practice, but talking—about the cot they'd slept in (*the shepherd who lived there beat his dogs, I remember his boots*), about food (*why does everyone out here put fat on everything, I love it, I hate it*), about Freya herself (*you talk in your sleep, you said a name last night, it wasn't Lark*). Each sentence was a small resurrection. Freya learned that Wren was eight, maybe nine; that she had walked a very long way alone, though when Freya asked from where, the old stillness flickered at the edges of her face and Wren visibly, deliberately stepped back from the question, and Freya let it go. There would be time. There had to be time.

She also learned the rules, and the rules were written in cost.

Speaking the name brought Wren up—but the old thing noticed. By midday Freya could read the signs: after twenty minutes, half an hour of being herself, Wren's speech would slow, gaps opening between words like ice floes drifting apart; her eyes would glaze mid-sentence; and then the ancient cadence would slide back into place beneath her voice like a knife sliding under a door, and the vast patient presence would be looking out again, courteous, watchful, saying things in the old tongue that made the hairs on Freya's arms rise.

"How long?" Freya asked, the third time it happened. "When you surface. How long can you stay?"

Wren's face worked. "Longer each time," she whispered. "Since you started saying it. Before—before, on the road, I got minutes. Sometimes I didn't come up at all for days." She hugged her knees, small in the grey afternoon light. "It hates the name. It tries to bury it. But it can't dig it out, because—" and here she recited, in a sing-song that was clearly memorized, clearly handed down through generations of hidden children, "—*a name is the hook the soul hangs on.*"

"Who taught you that?"

"My mother." Wren looked at the horizon. "Her mother taught her. And hers." A pause, and then, flatly, with a child's brutal clarity: "We're a line of doors, my family. Built and bred for it. I'm the last one."

Freya's blood went cold, and colder still when she remembered the priestess's words through the carved relic, the binding failing, the coronation, the moon turning. *The last one.* Bred for a moment that was coming whether Freya walked fast or slow.

But that was tomorrow's terror. She was learning, out here on the road, to ration her terrors—one per day, like bread in a famine.

Instead she said, "Wren. Say it back to yourself. Right now. Out loud."

Wren frowned. Then, obediently, puzzled: "Wren."

Something happened in the air between them—Freya felt it pass over her skin like the pressure change before a storm, a faint wrongness, a turning of attention from somewhere very far away and very far *down*. Wren gasped. Grabbed Freya's arm.

"It heard that," she breathed. "From wherever it lives. It heard me say my own name and it *hates* it. Did you feel—"

"I felt it." Freya's mouth was dry, but she was smiling, and she couldn't seem to stop. "Good. Let it hate it. We'll say it every morning and every night, like prayers. Wren. Wren. Until it's worn smooth as a stair."

And Wren laughed—that wrecked, wet, astonishing laugh—and for one hour more the road was only a road, and the meadows were only meadows golding into evening, and the two of them were only a woman and a child walking together, arguing amiably about whether fat belonged on everything.

* * *

It was nearly full dark when Freya realized they were being followed.

She didn't hear it—she felt it, in the bones she carried, in the kit wrapped in wool against her back. All at once, without warning, every relic she owned went silent. Not quiet. *Silent*—the silence of held breath, of birds gone still before a hawk crosses the sun. She had known the dead her whole life. She had never once known them to be afraid.

She stopped. Wren stopped beside her, and Freya saw the child's head tilt, listening to something with those other senses, and saw the color leave her face.

Behind them, back along the road they'd traveled, in the blue dark between the hedgerows, a light appeared. Then another. Lanterns, moving steadily, unhurried, in the manner of people who have all the time in the world and know exactly where they are going.

And threading up the road on the cold evening air, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of hoofbeats—and beneath the hoofbeats, so low Freya might have imagined it, a voice chanting in the old tongue.

Wren's hand found Freya's and gripped it hard.

"That's not the king's voice," she whispered. "That's the other thing. It's singing through *them*."

The lanterns came on through the dark, patient as stars falling.
