## 7. Ash Market

The town was called Cinder's Rest, though no one living remembered the fire that had named it, and Freya thought that was its own kind of warning—a place that had forgotten even its own wounds.

She saw it first from the ridge road, Wren's small hand tight in hers, and her stomach turned over with a feeling she could not at once name. It took her a long moment to understand what was wrong: nothing was wrong. That was what was wrong. The town lay in the valley of the Ashwater like any other town, smoke rising straight from its chimneys, laundry strung between crooked houses, geese arguing in a muddy yard—and yet every line of it, every roof and fencepost and leaning wall, sat perfectly, terribly still. Not quiet. Still. The way a held breath is still.

"The dead are loud here," Wren said.

Freya looked down at the child. Since leaving Thornmere, Wren spoke more often—but never in the voice Freya had begun to hope for. The words came out in that old tongue, syllables like river-stones worn smooth, and only sometimes did meaning arrive behind them, carried up from somewhere deep, as if the child were a well and the language rose from water far below. This time she understood well enough.

"I know," Freya said. "I feel it too."

And she did. Beyond Thornmere's borders, the dead were not whispers through bone but a weather, a pressure, a tide against the inside of her skull. In the fens they had been angry. Here they were something worse. They were *frightened*, and their fear had nowhere to go, because no one in Cinder's Rest was listening to them at all.

*No one is listening.* She turned that over as they came down off the ridge, leading the mule they'd bought with the last of the priestess's silver. The bones in her pack—her traveling reliquary, three pieces wrapped in wool—had been murmuring since dawn, fragments without shape. A woman's voice saying *the market, the market, ask at the market*. A man repeating a name over and over: *Maren Ostrey, Maren Ostrey*. And beneath it all, a low grinding hum she had come to dread, the sound she'd first heard crossing the border, which the priestess's relic had named before dying into silence: the Thorn, feeding.

The relic-map they needed was here. Maren Ostrey had been a mapmaker of the old school, the kind who cut her charts into bone so they could not burn, and she had died eleven years ago in this town, and her grave—if the dead could be believed—had been robbed of everything except her finger-bones, which some pious soul had sealed into the wall of the market chapel. The dead did not lie, but they were often roundabout, and Freya had learned on the road to be grateful for even a finger's worth of guidance.

"Stay close," she told Wren. "Hold my hand. If I say *rowan*, you run for the treeline and don't look back."

Wren looked up at her with those eyes that were too old and too young at once, and said something in the ancient tongue that Freya, by now, understood without understanding: *I will not leave you.*

"No," Freya agreed grimly. "I was afraid you'd say that."

* * *

The market square of Cinder's Rest smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and, underneath, faintly, of ash—the old fire's ghost, or perhaps not a ghost. Stalls lined the square in neat rows, and the people moved among them buying and selling, haggling and laughing, doing everything a market's people do.

That was the horror of it, Freya realized within ten steps. They were doing *everything right*.

A fishwife called out her prices in a clear, pleasant voice. A boy chased a dog between the stalls, both of them making exactly the sounds a boy and a dog should make. A tinker argued cheerfully with a farmer over the price of a kettle. Every note was true, and together they made a song with no music in it, because Freya had spent thirty years among grieving people and she knew what living voices sounded like when they came from anywhere deeper than habit. These did not. These voices came from the surface only, like wind moving a curtain while the window behind it stayed shut.

She stopped at a stall selling apples and watched the seller weigh them. His hands moved correctly. His smile arrived on schedule. But his eyes—she had seen eyes like that once before, on the day her father died, on the face of a man who had walked into the river the next morning. Eyes with nobody home behind them, keeping the house tidy until someone returned.

"You're not from here," the apple-seller said. It was not an accusation. It was barely a question. His smile did not move.

"Just passing through," Freya said. "Bound south."

"South." He weighed an apple, set it down, weighed it again. "The roads south are good roads. The king keeps good roads." Something flickered then, deep down, like a fish turning under ice—something that might have been the man himself, drowning. "The king keeps *everything*."

"I'm sure he does," Freya said carefully, and bought three apples she didn't want, because refusing felt dangerous.

Wren's grip tightened on her hand. The child had gone very still, the way animals go still, and was looking not at the people but at the ground, at the spaces between the cobbles, where Freya knew—for she could feel it now, a cold rising through her boot-soles—something fed.

Not on blood. Not on flesh. On *consent*. On the small daily surrenders of will that no one counted as surrender at all. She could almost see it, if she unfocused her eyes: threads, fine as spider silk, running from the crown-shaped sigil stamped on the toll-gate arch, on the proclamations pasted to the chapel door, on the coins changing hands—to every temple behind every face in the square. Threads, all of them running south, converging somewhere far away, and along each thread a slow, patient siphoning, the way a leech drinks without waking the sleeper.

*So this is what it looks like,* she thought. *This is what the Thorn makes of a town.* Not ruins. Not screaming. Just obedience, polished until it shone, mistaken for peace.

She found the chapel at the square's north edge, a squat stone building with a bell tower whose bell had been removed—she noted the empty yoke of it, the rope still hanging, swaying slightly in no wind she could feel. Inside, candles burned before a plain altar, and the air was thick with the smell of tallow and old stone. Three worshippers knelt in the pews, motionless, and when Freya passed close she heard their breathing: slow, synchronized, identical, like one creature wearing three bodies.

The east wall. The dead had said the east wall.

She knelt at the altar as if to pray, positioning herself where her body shielded her hands, and pressed her palm flat against the cool stone near a seam in the masonry. *Come on,* she thought. *Maren Ostrey. Mapmaker. Show me your road.*

For a moment, nothing. Then the finger-bones behind the wall woke like embers breathed upon.

An old woman's voice, dry and precise, with the accent of the capital's better districts: *Who asks?*

"A carver," Freya breathed, lips barely moving. "Freya Hartwell, of Thornmere. I need your map, mistress Ostrey. The ways under the palace at Corvane. The catacombs."

A pause. Then, wry: *Eleven years dead, and still everyone wants directions.*

"Please."

*Everyone who comes asking for those ways dies, girl. Do you know why I cut maps into bone? Because paper burns, and parchment can be eaten, and ink can be changed. Bone remembers what was true even when the world decides otherwise.* The voice sharpened. *You're the one they're hunting. The witch and the changeling. It's in the proclamations. Even here, even in this tomb of the living, the proclamations come.*

"Yes," Freya said. "I'm the one."

*Then listen fast. There's a keeper's door in the wall behind the high altar at Corvane, third course of stone above the floor, marked with a sun that has been chiseled half away. My map shows the ways beneath—every vault, every ossuary, every passage the kings dug to hide from their own consciences. But bone cannot leave this wall; it is mortared in, and I am only fingers. So I will give you what I gave no one living: the whole of it, spoken. You must hold it in your head, carver. Hold it the way bone holds it.*

"I'm listening."

What followed was eleven years of a dead woman's craft poured into Freya's mind in the space of a hundred heartbeats—corridors and stairways, distances in paces, turns marked by carved saints and bricked-up doors, the smell of particular chambers, the places where the passages ran under the throne room itself. Freya's gift had always been hearing, not memory, but grief had taught her to hold last words the way other people hold songs, and she gripped Maren Ostrey's map with everything she had, feeling it settle into her like a splinter of light.

When it was done, the voice grew quiet, and then, unexpectedly, gentle.

*Girl. The thing that rules us—it came to me before I died. Offered me anything. I was old; I refused out of spite more than courage. You have more to lose than I did. Whatever you think the price will be, imagine it doubled, and go anyway. That is the only advice worth carving.*

"Thank you," Freya whispered.

*Don't thank me. Get out of this square. The bell-less tower isn't empty, whatever they told you. Something sleeps up there that counts the hours.*

Freya rose, heart hammering, and turned to find Wren—

—and the space beside her was empty.

* * *

Ser Dain Voss rode into Cinder's Rest at midmorning with six men behind him and a warrant in his saddlebag that he had read so many times he no longer needed to.

*By order of the Crown: the woman Freya Hartwell, carver, of Thornmere, wanted for abduction and sorcery. The child accompanying her to be taken alive and unharmed.*

He had recited it to himself across four hundred miles of road, and each time, the words fit worse, like boots resoled one time too many. Abduction. He had seen the child at the fen crossing, three nights ago, through the reeds—seen the woman kneel in the mud to lace the child's boot, seen her share her bread, seen her stand watch through the small hours so the little one could sleep. Kidnappers did not lace boots. Sorcerers did not stand watch with a drawn knife and dark-circled eyes, guarding a sleeping child from nothing but the night itself.

And then there was the word the child had spoken.

Dain had not reported it. He had told his lieutenant the wind had carried a nightjar's call, and Lieutenant Hask had accepted it, because Hask was a practical man and practical men preferred not to interrogate their commander's nightmares. But Dain heard it still, whenever the road went quiet. One word, in a voice forty years buried.

*Lain.*

His sister's voice, saying his name—or the child-name only she had used, the one that meant *little rain*, because Lain had been seven and could not pronounce Dain properly and had made him a nickname instead of an apology. He had stood in the reeds with his sword half-drawn and felt the world tilt on its axle, because his sister was dead, had been dead since the fever year, and yet something in that mud-flat fen had spoken with her mouth, and it had not sounded like sorcery. It had sounded like *home*.

He did not know what he would do when he caught the woman. That was the truth he carried like a stone in his boot. The warrant said one thing. The boot-lacing said another. And Ser Dain Voss had built his entire life on the belief that these two things—the law and the right—were the same thing, and he could not bear, yet, to look directly at the possibility that they had come apart in his hands.

Cinder's Rest did not improve his mood. He had passed through border towns before; they were usually sullen places, resentful of tolls and soldiers. This one was worse than sullen. It was *smooth*. The folk smiled at his men. They answered questions promptly and completely. When he asked the toll-keeper whether a woman and child had passed, the man described them in perfect detail—carver's tools, grey mule, child with sea-grey eyes—and volunteered that they had entered the market square not a quarter hour ago.

"Did anyone challenge them?" Dain asked. "Anyone suspicious?"

"Of what, ser?" The toll-keeper smiled his smooth smile. "We're loyal folk here. The king keeps us."

Something about the sentence crawled down Dain's spine. He had heard loyalty pledged a thousand times, in a hundred accents, and it had never sounded like that—like a lesson recited, like a lock clicking shut.

"Mount up," he said. "Market square."

* * *

Freya's heart stopped, started again, and drove her forward through the crowd like a hooked fish.

Wren was twenty feet away, standing in the open center of the square, head tipped back, looking up at the empty bell tower. Around the child, the market went on—but wrong now, subtly wrong, the sellers' heads beginning to turn one by one toward the small figure in their midst, the way iron filings turn toward a lodestone. Wren's lips were moving. Sound came out—not the ancient tongue this time, but something older still, a resonance Freya felt in her teeth, in the fillings of the graves around her, in the finger-bones walled up fifty feet away, which had begun, she realized with distant horror, to *hum*.

*Wren, no. What are you calling?*

She shoved through the crowd. A stall overturned; apples rolled everywhere; no one bent to pick them up. Every face in the square was turning now, slowly, in perfect unison, like a field of flowers following a sun that wasn't there. The apple-seller's smile had not changed, but tears were running down his cheeks, and he went on smiling, weighing apples no one would buy.

"Wren!" Freya dropped to her knees in front of the child and took her by the shoulders. Up close she could see what was happening: the child's eyes had gone white as skimmed milk, and beneath the skin of her throat something glowed faintly, like a lamp behind vellum. The voice pouring out of her was not one voice but many, layered, centuries deep—and Freya, who heard the dead for a living, heard them clearly at last, and wished she didn't.

They were the consumed. The ones the Thorn had eaten, generation upon generation, and they were not silent inside it—they were *pressed*, flattened like flowers in a book, and Wren's open vessel-nature had cracked the book open, and they were spilling out into the square of Cinder's Rest in a flood of grief and rage and terrible, terrible longing.

*Let us out. Let us out. We consented to nothing. We consented to NOTHING—*

The kneeling worshippers in the chapel began to scream. Freya heard it distantly, through the roar. Across the square, the smooth faces were cracking—some people simply standing and weeping, others clawing at the crown-sigils sewn on their own collars, one man beating his forehead against a stall post with rhythmic, patient violence, as if trying to knock loose something lodged behind his eyes.

And above it all, from the empty bell tower, something answered.

It was not a sound. It was an attention. Freya felt it descend over the square like a change in pressure before a storm—the Thorn, wherever it truly lived, turning its vast and patient regard toward the disturbance, the way a sleeper turns toward a mosquito in the dark. The threads she had sensed before pulled taut. Every person in the square stiffened at once.

Including, she saw with a lurch, the eight riders entering from the west gate.

* * *

Dain reined in at the square's edge and knew, in the marrow-deep way soldiers know battlefields, that something had already gone wrong here.

His horse refused the square. So did Hask's, and the horses of the six men behind them—all eight animals planting their feet at the same invisible line, ears flat, breath steaming though the morning was mild. Dain dismounted and drew his sword, not because he had an enemy to point it at but because his hands demanded occupation.

The square was screaming. No—the square was *weeping*, most of it, dozens of townsfolk standing amid their goods with tears streaming down their smooth faces, and at the center a woman knelt holding a child whose eyes were white as frost, and the sound coming out of that child was a chorus, and every hair on Dain's arms stood up because he recognized one thread of that chorus, one voice, thin and clear beneath the rest—

"Lain?" he said aloud, stupidly, into the chaos.

The woman at the center looked up. Their eyes met across sixty feet of screaming market, and Dain watched recognition bloom in her face—the wanted poster, he realized, his own face was on it too now, had been for a week—and watched her make the calculation every fugitive makes, and watched her gather the child and run.

"After her!" Hask shouted, but Dain's arm came down across his chest like a bar.

"Wait."

"Ser Voss, the warrant—"

"*Wait.*" Above them, the empty bell tower groaned, and a crack split its stonework from yoke to foundation, and whatever slept up there shifted its weight, and every person in the square fell silent at once, mid-scream, mid-weep, mid-prayer, hundreds of faces tilting upward in perfect unison.

Dain had served the crown for nineteen years. He had burned one village rather than let it fall to plague, on orders, and had never forgiven himself, and had never disobeyed again. He knew obedience the way farmers know weather. And what he was looking at now—this square full of people whose wills had been scooped out and replaced with smooth compliance, this kingdom-wide silence wearing ten thousand faces—was obedience perfected, obedience *weaponized*, obedience grown so total it had become the very thing his oaths claimed to defend against.

The child's chorus had shown him his sister's voice. This silence showed him his king's.

"Hask," he said quietly. "The woman and child went north, toward the shrine lane. Take three men and follow. Slowly. Bring them in gentle, you understand me? Gentle. Anyone raises a blade to that child answers to me."

"And you, ser?"

Dain looked up at the cracking tower, at the thing shifting behind its stones, and felt the last load-bearing wall of his life develop its first fracture.

"I'm going to see what our king keeps in his bell towers," he said. "And then, I think, I'm going to pray—which will be a first."

Behind him, unnoticed by anyone, a grey-muled woman and a white-eyed child slipped down the shrine lane and out of Cinder's Rest, carrying a dead mapmaker's map in a living head—and ahead of them, three days south, the capital waited with its coronation bells already being polished for a king who was not there.
