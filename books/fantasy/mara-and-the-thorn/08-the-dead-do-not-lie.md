## 8. The Dead Do Not Lie

The informant had been dead for three days when Freya found him.

She knew it before she saw him, the way she always knew—Wren had stopped walking two hundred paces back and simply stood in the middle of the road, head tilted like a bird listening to something beneath the earth, and pointed with one small finger toward the drainage culvert where the road dipped under the old wall of Ash Market's outer quarter.

"He's there," Wren said.

Not in the old language. In words. Plain, trade-tongue words, rough-edged from disuse. Since the night at the toll bridge, since Freya had spoken the name *Wren* aloud and watched it work like a hook through deep water, the child surfaced more often now—minutes at a time, sometimes a quarter hour, blinking at the world as though it were a room they'd been locked out of. The ancient voice still came through between those windows, that layered, resonant speech that made the air feel thin, but the gaps were widening. Freya had begun, cautiously, foolishly, to hope.

Now Wren stood pointing at a culvert, and Freya felt the pull too—the faint, insistent tug behind her sternum that meant dead bone was near, bone that still held its echo.

"Stay here," she said, and immediately regretted it. Wren did not stay anywhere. The child followed her down the embankment on silent feet, mud sucking at their boots, and crouched beside her at the mouth of the culvert without being asked, as if they'd done this a hundred times together instead of six days.

The body had been dragged partway into the drain and left there, face-down, arms folded beneath him as though he'd been made comfortable by someone in a hurry who still had a scrap of conscience left. Rain had gotten at him. So had animals. But the bones—Freya's eyes went to the bones the way other people's eyes went to faces—and the bones were intact enough. Ribs. The long bones of the arm. And the skull, turned half away, its crown caved in by a single blow delivered from behind.

He'd been a small man. A courier's satchel lay nearby, slashed open, its contents scattered: nothing worth stealing now except a few wax cylinders, cracked, their seals broken. Whatever message he'd carried had been read by his killers before they killed him.

Freya knelt in the wet grass and was quiet for a long moment. This was the part no one ever understood about her gift, the part that made neighbors cross the street and mothers pull their children close—that it was not a power. It was a courtesy. You did not dig into the dead. You waited to be asked.

But the dead man was already asking. She could feel it, a pressure against the inside of her skull like a hand pressed flat against a window from the other side. *Please. Please. They took the names. I hid the true list but they'll find it, they'll—*

"They killed him for what he carried," she murmured to Wren. "A list, maybe. Names."

Wren looked at her with those old, old eyes in a child's face. "Ask him," the child said simply. "That's what you do."

It shouldn't have startled her anymore, how plainly Wren spoke of it. No fear, no revulsion. To Wren, the boundary between the living and the dead was apparently a door left unlatched—a thing you walked through when you needed to. Freya wondered, not for the first time, what it must be like to carry a king's exiled voice in your chest and think nothing of it.

She drew off her right glove. Her carver's knife hung at her belt; she didn't draw it yet. First, contact.

She laid her bare palm against the man's forearm, over the ulna, and closed her eyes.

* * *

The world folded inward.

This was how it always began—not sound first, but weight. The sensation of a second body settling over hers, lighter than her own, colder, shaped differently. Then the voice came, and this one came ragged, breathless, still running even in death.

*They were waiting at the mill crossing. Two of them, gray cloaks, no colors—I thought no colors meant smugglers, God forgive me, I thought I could outrun them. He caught my horse's rein and the other one hit me and I woke here, in the dark, and they had the satchel open.*

Freya steadied herself. Dead men's memories came in fragments, in the order their dying minds had fixed them, not the order things happened. She let the current carry her.

*They read the list by lantern light. I heard the name Voss and my heart lifted, stupid, stupid—Voss is justiciar, Voss is the law, the law will sort it. That's what I told myself while they argued over whether to sell me or drown me.*

The memory sharpened. Lantern light through slats. Two voices, one young and nervous, one flat and bored. The nervous one reading names aloud: *"Tam Broadhurst, ferryman. The widow Cale. The miller's boy—how old is this list?"* And the flat voice answering: *"Older than you. Older than the order that wrote it. Doesn't matter. Cross-reference the convent watch-lists and send copies to the capital. The Serene Office wants every sympathizer marked before coronation."*

Convent. Watch-lists. Sympathizers. Freya's stomach tightened. Somewhere out there, someone had been keeping records of everyone who opposed the Thorn's slow takeover—and the justiciars were collecting them like a harvest.

*Then the flat one said the word that ended me,* the dead man went on, and his voice dropped, went reverent and terrified at once. *He said, "And the village? Greymarsh is still on the burn-order." And the young one—there was something wrong with him, something sad—he said, "Greymarsh was pardoned." And the flat one laughed. He actually laughed. "Pardoned by whom? Voss rode out with the writ himself and came back and reported it done. Ash and rubble. His own seal on the report. Twelve years ago and the man sleeps fine, far as anyone knows."*

*"Then why—"*

*"Because I was there, boy. Second rank, torch detail. And I'm telling you what I saw: we rode up to Greymarsh and Voss read the writ and then he looked at that village a long time—all those little houses, all that smoke from breakfast fires—and he turned around and told us the map was wrong, that Greymarsh was three miles east, and he rode us three miles east into empty fen and burned a stand of reeds and brought back a bag of ash from the fire and called it Greymarsh. And every man of us swore to it, because what else do you do? Because the alternative was—"*

The memory shuddered. The lantern guttered in the recollection, and Freya felt the informant's living terror flood through her own chest: *he knows I know, oh God, he's turning around, he's looking at me—*

The blow. White light. Then the long cold, and the rain, and the small animal sounds in the dark, and the fading.

Freya opened her eyes.

She was on her hands and knees in the wet grass beside the culvert, and her whole body was shaking, and Wren's small hand was resting on the back of her neck—warm, solid, human—the way you'd steady someone coming out of deep water.

"You cried," Wren observed, without judgment.

Freya wiped her face with the back of her wrist. Her palm still throbbed where it had lain on the dead man's arm, that peculiar ache that lingered after deep listening, as though the bone remembered her touch and resented letting go.

"There's a man," she said slowly, working it through aloud because saying it made it realer, harder to dismiss. "Ser Dain Voss. A justiciar. He's been hunting us since Thornmere—I've seen his seal on the notices, heard the dead talk about him on the road. He serves the crown. He serves whatever wears the crown." She looked down at the ruined body in the culvert. "Twelve years ago he was ordered to burn a village called Greymarsh. Instead he lied to his own men, falsified his report, sealed it with his own hand, and let a hundred people live."

Wren considered this with the unnerving gravity the child gave everything. "Why?"

"That's what I keep asking." Freya sat back on her heels. The rain had softened to mist, beading grey on everything, and somewhere above them a cart rattled past on the high road without stopping, without knowing. "Men don't do that for nothing. Not men like that. A justiciar who burns villages on command doesn't wake up one morning and risk his neck for strangers unless something broke in him first—or something in him was never broken at all."

"The Thorn breaks people," Wren said. It wasn't a question. The child had said it before, in the old tongue, in the nights when the ancient voice came through and spoke of the devouring spirit with the weary familiarity of an old enemy: *it does not make men wicked, carver. It makes them obedient. Wickedness they might resist. Obedience feels like virtue.* "Maybe it couldn't break him all the way."

"Or maybe it hasn't gotten around to him yet." Freya pulled her glove back on, flexing her fingers. "Either way, it means—" She stopped. Started again. "Everyone I've met on this road who serves the crown has been either hollow or afraid. The toll-keepers who wouldn't look at us. The constable in Harrow Ford who read our descriptions off the notice and then apologized to the notice. I'd started thinking of them as weather. As something that just *is*, something you hide from." She shook her head. "But this man lied to the Thorn's face twelve years ago and slept well enough to keep doing his job. That's not weather. That's a person."

* * *

They walked into Ash Market proper as the light failed, because Freya needed supplies and because the bones of the town would tell her more than any living tongue would. That was the other thing the road had taught her: outside Thornmere, the dead were everywhere, thick as sediment, and most of them had opinions.

Ash Market had earned its name honestly. A fire seventy years back had taken the eastern quarter, and rather than clear the ruins the townsfolk had built on top of them, so that the newer streets stood shoulder-high above the older ones, connected by stairs and buttressed alleys, the whole town a coral reef of habitation grown over its own dead. From the market square at the center, the rooftops climbed away in terraces on three sides, candlelit windows stacked like offerings.

And underneath it all, layer upon layer, the burned.

Freya felt them the moment she crossed into the lower streets—a low, continuous murmur, dozens of voices braided together, not angry exactly but *present*, the way a hearth is present in a room. Seventy years and they hadn't finished saying what happened to them. She kept her hand away from walls. Here, more than anywhere she'd passed through, contact would mean drowning.

The market itself was winding down. Stalls closing, awnings folding, a fishmonger hosing blood off his boards. Ordinary evening sounds. Except—

Except that it was too orderly. Freya noticed it gradually, the way you notice a room is too quiet. People moved with a strange smoothness, no jostling, no bargaining, no laughter. A woman paid for bread and thanked the baker in exactly the same tone the baker used to thank her. Two men loading a cart worked in perfect unison without speaking. At the square's center, a crier stood on the fountain's rim reading proclamations in a level voice—coronation announcements, curfew extensions, a reminder that harboring fugitives was treason—and nobody reacted at all, neither approval nor resentment. They simply absorbed it, the way stone absorbs rain.

Wren had gone still again. The child's hand crept into Freya's, small and cold.

"It's here," Wren whispered. "Under the town. Under the *old* town. It's been drinking here a long time."

Freya believed it. She could almost see it now that she knew to look—not the Thorn itself, but its residue, the greasy sheen on everything, the way the candle flames all leaned slightly in the same direction regardless of draft. A town fed on, patiently, until obedience wore grooves in it deep enough to pass for peace.

"We get what we need and we go," she murmured. "Lamp oil, salt, bread. There's a relic-seller on Copper Lane—if the dead man's memory was right, he keeps a map-table in back. Old survey charts. I need to see the fen routes north."

Copper Lane was a stairway street climbing the eastern terrace, lit by hanging lanterns that swayed without wind. The relic-seller's shop was easy to find: a narrow frontage crammed with objects, dried herbs, birdcages, and in the window, prominently displayed, a shelf of carved bone charms—crude things, mass-produced, sold to the frightened as protection. Freya looked at them with professional contempt. Half of them weren't even properly cleaned. Bone carried echoes; you didn't sell it blind.

The shopkeeper was a stooped man with ink-stained fingers and eyes that flicked to the door every time it opened. When Freya asked after survey charts, he named a price triple what they were worth, and when she paid it without haggling, something in his manner shifted—a flicker of curiosity, then caution.

"You're not local," he said. Not a question.

"No."

"Passing through to where?"

"North."

His gaze dropped, briefly, to Wren, then to the knife at Freya's belt—to the *carver's* knife, and she watched him recognize it, watched the recognition travel through him like current through wire. For one suspended moment she thought he might call for the watch. Instead he leaned across the counter, and his voice came out low and cracked and suddenly, terribly human—the first unsmooth voice she'd heard in the whole town.

"Are you the one they're calling the witch of Thornmere?"

Freya said nothing.

"Because if you are." He swallowed. "My brother's name is on a list. Some kind of registry. Men in gray cloaks came asking questions last month, and now there are lists, and people who talk too loud stop talking altogether, and everyone smiles at everyone like it's a funeral." His hands were trembling on the counter. "Is it true there's a place the gray cloaks can't follow? Is it true about the valley?"

For a heartbeat, Freya was tempted. *Come with us,* she almost said. *Bring your brother.* But the town's smooth surface pressed in around them, the leaning candles, the listening silence, and she thought of the informant in the culvert, dead for a list, and she said only:

"Go north if you can. Don't write anything down."

He nodded rapidly, wrapped her charts in oilcloth, and pushed them across the counter like a man passing contraband, which perhaps he was.

* * *

They were halfway down Copper Lane when the bells began.

Not the market bells—those rang the hours, familiar and dull. These came from the garrison tower on the western height, a rapid, urgent pattern, and beneath the bell-noise Freya heard the thing she'd been dreading since the culvert: whistles. Short, sharp, coordinated. Soldiers moving in pairs through the terraced streets, sweeping downward toward the square.

Below her, in the lamplit haze, she saw riders at the market's edge. Gray cloaks. And at their head, a tall figure in a justiciar's coat, sitting his horse with the stillness of a man carved from something denser than flesh.

Even at that distance, even through rain-mist and lantern-smoke, Freya knew him. Not by sight—she'd never seen his face—but the way you know a word you've only ever read aloud. *Dain Voss.* The man whose seal covered a lie that saved a hundred lives. The man hunting her across half a kingdom for reasons he probably believed were just.

He raised one gloved hand, and the sweep changed direction, and Freya understood with cold clarity that it was pointing toward Copper Lane.

Someone had talked. The relic-seller? No—his fear had been real. Someone else. Someone smoother. The town itself, perhaps, obedient down to its stones.

"Wren." She kept her voice level. "Back door of the shop. Now."

They went up the stair-street fast, past the shop, and the relic-seller was already holding the rear door open—he'd heard the bells too, and whatever else he was, he was a man who'd learned which way the wind leaned. Through a yard strung with drying fish, over a wall slick with moss, down into the older town beneath—the burned town, the sunken lanes where the original streets ran dark and narrow below the modern city, lit by the occasional grate in the ceiling far overhead.

Here the dead were loudest. The burned ones. Their murmur rose around Freya like water rising in a lock, and she felt them notice her, felt their attention turn like a thousand faces in a crowd.

*Help us,* they seemed to say. Or maybe: *remember us.* After seventy years, the distinction had probably worn away.

"This way," Wren said, and tugged her left where the lane forked, and Freya followed without argument, because the child had been navigating by senses Freya didn't have since before the valley borders. They descended through the buried town—under a collapsed archway, along a channel that had once been a street and was now a streambed of black water—until the whistles faded above them and the only sounds were dripping and the distant bells.

Only then did Freya let herself breathe. She leaned against a wall in the dark, Wren a warm weight against her side, and stared up through a roof-grate at a coin of grey sky.

"He was there," she said quietly. "In the square. Fifty yards away." Her hand found the carver's knife at her belt, not drawing it, just touching it, the way you'd touch a talisman. "I could have called out to him. Told him what I know. *You spared Greymarsh. I know. The dead told me.*" She laughed, short and bitter. "And he'd have put me in irons by sundown, because that's what the law requires, and he loves the law. That's the whole horror of him. He's a good man doing monstrous work, and he can't see the seam."

Wren tilted their head up at her. In the darkness the child's eyes caught what little light there was, pale as riverstone.

"The priestess said the binding fails at the coronation," Wren said. "Nineteen days now. The Thorn is gathering everyone it can bend. Every soldier, every clerk, every justiciar." A pause. "But it bends them. It doesn't make them. You said so yourself—there's a seam."

"A seam isn't a crack. A seam holds."

"For now." Wren's small hand found hers in the dark. "But the priestess also said the binding was made by people. And people can be unmade from it. Even the ones wearing its uniform." The child squeezed her fingers, surprisingly strong. "Even him. Maybe especially him."

Freya looked down at the shape of the child beside her, and thought of the informant's last terror, and of a village that should have burned and didn't, and of a good man riding through the rain hunting two fugitives he'd never met, believing with his whole honorable heart that he served justice.

*Does justice live in the law,* she wondered, *or does the law just wear justice's clothes?* Her whole life she'd hidden inside rules—carve quietly, hear secretly, harm no one, want nothing—and called the hiding safety. Six days out in the world had shown her the rules were thinner than she'd believed, and the thing wearing them larger.

Above them, muffled by earth and stone, the bells changed pattern. Slower now. All clear. Somewhere in the upper town, Ser Dain Voss was reading a report of a witch and a changeling seen fleeing into the buried quarter, and deciding, no doubt, where to ride next.

Freya pushed off the wall.

"The fens," she said. "North, through the Greymarsh wetlands. If his map says the village burned, his map says the fens are empty." She allowed herself the ghost of a smile in the dark. "Let's go prove his mercy right."

They climbed out of the buried town through a coal chute into the rain, and took the north road under the last light, and behind them Ash Market settled back into its smooth, terrible peace—while ahead, dark and trackless, the fens waited, and somewhere in their drowned miles, the hunter and the hunted were already walking toward each other along the same drowned roads, neither knowing yet how much the other had to give.
