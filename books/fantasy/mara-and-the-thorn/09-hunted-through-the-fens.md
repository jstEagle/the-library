## 9. Hunted Through the Fens

The fens began where the king's road ended, which Ser Dain Voss had always taken as a kind of honesty. Roads were promises. *This way lies the crown's protection.* The fens made no such promise. They simply spread out before him in the grey light of a rainless dawn, a country of black water and pale reeds that seemed to have been drowned on purpose and never mourned.

He rode slowly, because the ground would not let him do otherwise. His gelding, Bramble, picked his way along the raised causeway with the wariness of an animal that understood something his rider did not: that the fen was watching them.

Dain had been a justiciar for eleven years. He had hanged men for theft and pardoned them for murder when the truth demanded it, and he had learned early that the law was not a sword but a scale — that his whole office existed to hold the two pans level while lesser men loaded them with whatever they pleased. He believed in it the way other men believed in salt or bread. It was the thing that made him useful rather than dangerous.

And yet for three days now, riding out of Ash Market with the warrant warm against his chest, he had been aware of a small wrongness in himself, like a splinter worked under the skin. He kept returning to the market square. To the baker who had smiled at him with eyes like unlit lamps and said, *Yes, ser, I saw the witch, she went north with the changeling,* and had gone on smiling long after the sentence ended, smiling as though the smile itself had been assigned to him by someone else. To the woman who had stepped into the muck of the street to let him pass and stood there, ankle-deep in filth, nodding. Nodding. Still nodding when he looked back from fifty paces.

*They are good subjects,* he told himself. *They love their king.*

But they had not looked like people who loved their king. They had looked like people who had forgotten there was any other way to stand.

He shook it off and consulted the relic at his belt — a sliver of iron set in a leather disc, cold even in summer, which the justiciars called a truth-needle. It pointed, always, toward the thing it was sworn to find. Today it tugged gently northeast, off the causeway, into the reeds.

"The witch went north," Dain murmured to Bramble. "Into that."

Bramble laid his ears back.

* * *

He found the first sign at midmorning: a fire-ring of stones beside a hummock of dry ground, three days old, its ashes raked flat with a care that struck him as almost professional. Whoever had camped here had wanted no smoke, no light, no trace. Beside the ring, pressed into the peat, was a single footprint — small, narrow, a child's bare foot — and beside it the print of a woman's boot, worn at the outer heel.

A carver's boots, he thought. A trade that keeps you standing all day wears the heel like that.

He knelt and touched the ash. Cold, but the peat beneath held a faint sourness, like old milk. Bone-dust, maybe. Or nothing. He was reading too much into ash and mud because the trail had gone quiet and quiet trails made men imaginative.

The truth-needle pulled harder here. When he lifted it free of his belt it swung of its own accord, straining northeast like a dog at a leash, and Dain felt the familiar satisfaction of the hunt narrowing. Eleven years, and the needle had never lied. Whatever the witch carried — the stolen relic, the corrupted bones, whatever language the priests used in their writs — the needle wanted it found.

He followed the needle into the reeds.

The fen closed over him like water over a stone. Sound changed first: the wind died, and in its place came small wet noises, things moving beneath the surface, the drip and tick of water finding new channels. The light went greenish, filtered through reed-tops taller than his head. Bramble grew difficult, snorting, refusing, until Dain dismounted and led him, one hand on the rein, the other on the needle, and the third sense — the one every justiciar developed and no academy ever named — telling him that he was being watched by something that did not blink.

Twice he passed standing stones half-swallowed by the peat, older than any kingdom, carved with spirals that predated the crown's own sigil. The writs said such places were nests of the old magic, and that cleansing them was holy work. Dain had cleansed three in his career. He remembered only the silence afterward, how the birds would not return for a season.

By late afternoon the needle was pulling so hard it trembled in his grip, and he knew he was close. Close enough that he should have sent word ahead to the garrison at Harrow Ford, close enough that procedure demanded he mark his position and wait for support before engaging a fugitive of the witch-class.

Instead he tied Bramble to a dead alder and went forward alone, on foot, through the last screen of reeds.

* * *

The clearing was small and high — one of the fen's rare dry islands — and it held a ruined chapel, roofless, its walls furred with moss, its little bell-tower empty except for starlings. And there, in the shelter of the south wall, was a cookfire, banked low, and a bedroll, and a woman kneeling with her back to him, doing something with her hands.

She was thinner than the descriptions. Grey-streaked hair pinned carelessly. Sleeves rolled to the elbow, forearms scarred with fine white lines, dozens of them, old and precise — the marks of a knife, but surgical, deliberate, nothing like the ragged wounds of violence. She was carving. Even from twenty paces Dain could see the small economy of her movements, the way she turned the object in her hands without looking at it, the way a mother turns a child.

There was no child in sight.

His hand went to his sword. Procedure filled his mouth like cold water: *Freya Hartwell, you are detained under the writ of—* He had said the words so many times they had worn smooth as river stones, and he opened his mouth to say them again—

—and the child spoke.

It came from behind the fallen lintel of the chapel door, a voice high and thin and wrong, wrong in a way Dain's body understood before his mind did, the way a man understands a floor is rotten a half-second before it gives. One word. One syllable, really, in a language he did not know, a language with too many edges in it, a language that sounded like it had been buried alive somewhere and had spent centuries digging.

Then the voice changed.

"*Dain.*"

Not the child's voice. Not any living voice. It was a girl's voice, bright and fifteen forever, with the particular lilt of someone laughing halfway through a word — and Dain Voss stopped breathing, because he knew that voice the way he knew his own heartbeat, and his sister Maren had been dead for nineteen years, drowned in the mill-race at Corleigh when he was twelve, and he had pulled her out himself, too late, her hair full of weeds, her face already going the color of the water.

"*Dain, you're standing in the wet grass. You'll catch your death.*"

The words were nonsense. The words were nothing. But they were *her* nonsense, her scolding, the exact words she'd said a hundred mornings of their childhood, and they came out of the ruin in the child's voice wearing Maren's cadence like a stolen coat fits a scarecrow — recognizable, and horrifying, and *hers*.

The woman at the fire had gone very still. Slowly, without turning, she said, "Wren. No."

The child stepped out from behind the lintel.

It was small — seven, eight years old, mud-caked, hollow-eyed, wrapped in a blanket that might once have been white. Its face was ordinary. Its face was the most ordinary face Dain had ever seen, and that was somehow worse, because the thing looking out of it was not a child at all. It looked at him with an attention that had nothing young in it, nothing human, an attention like the fen's own — patient, deep, and cold.

And then, in Maren's voice, gently, kindly, the way you speak to a tired animal:

"*Come home, Dain. Put the sword down. You're so tired. You've been so tired for so long.*"

He realized his hand had left the sword-hilt. He did not remember it leaving.

"No," Dain said. His voice came out cracked. "You're not her. My sister is dead. I buried—" He hadn't buried her; there had been nothing to bury, only the miller's apology and a small coffin of river stones. "I *know* what this is. This is the witchcraft the writs describe. This is how it takes a man — it finds the wound and pours itself in."

"Does it?" the woman said quietly. She had risen now, and turned, and her hands were empty and open at her sides, and her eyes were tired in a way he recognized — the tiredness of someone who has been awake too long inside their own life. "Or does it find the wound and show you it was real? Tell me, justiciar. When your sister died — did anyone say her name to you afterward? Did anyone in your precious chain of command so much as write it down?"

He didn't answer. There had been no inquiry. A child drowned; the crown's justice concerned itself with treason and tax. He had been given a week's leave and a sermon about providence.

"Wren," the woman said, to the child, firmly, "that's enough. Come away now."

But the child took another step toward him, and its mouth shaped Maren's next words, and Dain heard his own name in his dead sister's voice for the third time, and something in him — the thing the academy had built, the thing the oath had mortared, the load-bearing wall of his whole life — put its shoulder against the crack and felt it give.

Because here was the splinter from Ash Market, the wrongness he'd been carrying for three days, suddenly named: he had been hunting a monster, and monsters did not grieve. Monsters did not carve small careful objects by a small careful fire. Monsters did not tell their captives — if the child was a captive — *no, come away now*, in a voice of genuine fear *for* it.

And the needle at his belt — the truth-needle, the never-lying needle — was pointing not at the woman, not at the child, but past them both, deeper into the fen, toward the capital, toward the throne, trembling like a thing in terror.

He had never noticed before that a needle could tremble *fearfully*. He had assumed it was eagerness.

"What are you carrying?" he asked hoarsely. "What does the needle want?"

"Nothing of yours," the woman said. "Nothing you'd believe yet."

"Maren died in the mill-race," Dain said, to the child, to the thing in the child, to whoever could hear him. "At Corleigh. In spring. If you're her — if anything of her is anywhere near you — then you know I couldn't reach her. You know I've carried that nineteen years. So if this is mercy, take it back. And if it's bait—" his hand found the sword-hilt again, and held, though he no longer entirely knew what he was holding it against— "then know that I will cut my own hand off before I follow it."

The child tilted its head. And the ancient voice came back, the buried one, the one with too many edges, and it said one more word — and this time Dain felt it land somewhere behind his ribs like a key turning, though he understood none of it.

Then the child blinked, and shivered, and was suddenly, horribly, only a child — small and shaking and lost, staring at the armed man in the reeds with the plain animal terror of the young — and hid its face in the woman's skirt.

The woman gathered it in without looking away from Dain.

"Well, Ser Justiciar," she said. "Here we all are. What does the law say happens now?"

* * *

What the law said was simple. Detain the witch. Secure the changeling. Send to Harrow Ford for escort. Deliver both to the capital for examination under the writ, and let the priests do the rest. He had recited it a thousand times. He could have carved it on stone.

Dain sat on the fallen lintel across the fire from them, his sword still sheathed, and did not move.

The woman — Hartwell, Freya Hartwell, the name on the writ — watched him with the patience of her trade. The child slept against her side, genuinely asleep now, its breath whistling faintly, and in sleep it looked like every child in every village he had ever ridden through on the crown's business. That was the trouble with the work, he thought. Eventually you learned exactly what children looked like, everywhere, always.

"The report," the woman said at last. "You'll be expected to send one."

"Yes."

"And what will it say?"

Dain looked into the fire. Beyond the chapel walls the fen had gone dark, and the reeds whispered together in a language of their own, older than the needle, older than the writs.

He thought about the nodding woman in Ash Market, ankle-deep in filth. About the trembling needle. About a girl of fifteen, laughing halfway through a word, gone nineteen years, whose name no document in the kingdom contained. About the oath he had spoken at twenty-two, with his hand on the crown's seal, promising to hold the scales level — and how no one had ever told him what to do when the weights themselves were false.

He thought: *the law is a scale. Someone has been loading it.*

"I'll write that the trail entered the fens and was lost after the rains," Dain said. "That the needle failed — they fail, in the deep fen, everyone knows it. That I'll continue the search east of Harrow Ford." He heard the words come out of his mouth and felt, distantly, like a man watching his own house burn and noticing, absurdly, the color of the flames. Nineteen years of perfect service. One sentence of ash.

Hartwell studied him for a long moment. "Why?"

"Because you'll die if I don't," he said. "And I don't know anymore that dying is what you deserve." He stood, stiffly, knees cracking, an old soldier's sound. "Don't mistake me. I'm not converted. I'm not your ally. I'm a man who can't prove what he saw tonight, and won't swear to a lie he doesn't understand. Go north. Stay out of my sight until I'm done lying, because I've never done it before and I may be bad at it."

He collected Bramble from the alder. At the edge of the clearing he stopped, and did not turn around.

"That word it said at the end," he said. "The old one. What does it mean?"

Behind him, softly: "It means *remember*."

Dain rode into the dark, and the fen closed behind him like water, and somewhere far to the south a needle trembled toward a throne.

Two days later, in the garrison at Harrow Ford, Ser Dain Voss filed the first false report of his career, in a steady hand, and signed it, and felt the scales in his chest tip — slightly, terribly, irreversibly — toward the weightless side.

And in the morning he saddled Bramble again, and instead of riding east as his report promised, he turned north, following rumors of a convent where quiet sisters kept dangerous books — because a man who has begun lying to the crown might as well learn, quickly, what the truth was worth.
