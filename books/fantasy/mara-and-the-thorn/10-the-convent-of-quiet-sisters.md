## 10. The Convent of Quiet Sisters

The road climbed for two days out of the fens, and the higher they went, the quieter the dead became.

Freya noticed it the way a deaf woman might notice a room growing warm—by absence, by the slow unclenching of something she had carried so long she had forgotten it was weight. The voices that had pressed against her since Thornmere's border, the drowned and the starved and the murdered, all of them howling their grievances into her skull like wind through a broken shutter—they thinned. Faded. By the second dusk they were murmurs, and by the third morning, when the pines closed overhead and the path turned to old flagstone slick with moss, they were gone.

She stopped in the middle of the trail and stood very still.

Wren stopped too, three paces behind, the way the child always did—never beside her, never ahead, always at the exact distance of a shadow that hadn't yet decided whether to belong to someone. Mud had dried in the creases of the small face like a map of somewhere no one had ever been. Those eyes watched her now, ancient and childlike at once, and Freya realized she was listening to silence for the first time since she'd crossed the border, and that the silence felt less like peace than like a held breath.

"They're quiet here," she said aloud, though Wren rarely answered in words anyone could use. "The dead. Someone has made them quiet."

Wren tilted her head. Then, in that voice that was not quite one voice—the old tongue sliding beneath the child's throat like a fish under ice—she said a single word. Freya didn't know it. But the bones in her pack did. The little reliquary of carved tokens she'd carried from Thornmere shifted against each other, and she felt through the leather the particular tremor they made when they were afraid.

*Sanctuary,* the bones said. *Or a trap. The word is the same in every language.*

They came upon the convent at midday, when the fog was burning off the valley below and the light lay gold on wet stone. It was smaller than Freya had imagined—a cluster of low buildings huddled against the mountainside like stones rolled there by some patient glacier, roofed in slate gone silver with age, surrounded by gardens that were more herb than flower. Rosemary. Feverfew. Yarrow in ragged white rows. And along the eastern wall, where the sun struck longest, a bed of blackthorn, blooming out of season, its flowers white as bone shavings.

No bell tower. No gate. No sign at all that the place expected visitors, which was itself a kind of sign, Freya thought. Places that wanted to be found announced themselves. Places that wanted to survive did not.

A woman was kneeling in the garden, cutting yarrow with a curved knife. She rose without hurry when they approached, and Freya saw that she was older than she'd first seemed—sixty, seventy, impossible to say, with a face weathered smooth as river stone and eyes the pale grey of wood ash. Her hands were scarred in a way Freya recognized before she understood why: fine white lines across the fingertips and palms, dozens of them, the marks of a life spent working edges.

Carver's scars. Or something very like them.

"You're the bone-listener," the woman said. Not a question. "From the valley that forgets."

Freya's hand found Wren's shoulder without her deciding to move it. "Who told you that?"

"The dead told us," the woman said. "Forty years ago. They said a carver would come out of Thornmere with a vessel wearing a child's skin, and that when she came, the counting down would have already begun." She set down her knife and wiped her hands on her apron, unhurried, as if she were greeting neighbors rather than the end of the world. "I am Mother Sera. Abbess of this house, though *house* flatters it. You are expected, Freya Hartwell. You have been expected longer than you have been alive."

* * *

Inside, the convent smelled of tallow and dried herbs and cold stone, and beneath all of it, faintly, of blood—not fresh, not recent, but soaked deep into the walls over centuries, the way old churches smell of incense. Freya's skin prickled. There was magic here. Old, disciplined, banked like a fire kept under ash for generations, and it made her bones ache in a way she hadn't felt since crossing the border.

They gave Wren bread and honey and hot milk, and the child ate with the desperate politeness of someone who had learned hunger young, and then—this was the strange part, the part Freya would turn over in her mind for days—fell asleep. Truly asleep. No murmuring in the dead language. No twitching. Just a small body curled on a pallet in a cell that smelled of lavender, breathing like any ordinary child, and Freya stood over her for a long time watching the rise and fall of those ribs and feeling something in her chest that was too large and too painful to have a name.

"She sleeps because the walls here hold the voice back," Mother Sera said from the doorway. "We built them for exactly that purpose, six hundred years ago. Though I confess we never thought we'd need them."

"Six hundred years." Freya turned. "You've been waiting six hundred years?"

"We've been *remembering* six hundred years." The abbess led her down a corridor lit by narrow windows, past doors that were all closed, past sisters who moved through the shadows without speaking—and Freya saw now why they were called the Quiet Sisters. They weren't under vows of silence. They simply didn't speak unless speech was necessary, and they looked at Freya as she passed with an expression she knew intimately, because she wore it herself every day in Thornmere. The look of women hiding in plain sight. The look of careful invisibility.

"The crown bound the Thorn," Mother Sera said, opening a door at the corridor's end. "But the binding was not the crown's idea. Come. It is easier to show you."

* * *

The chamber beneath the chapel was round, and its walls were lined with bones.

Not arranged, not displayed—*installed*. Femurs and ribs and skulls set into the stone itself, mortared in place, thousands of them, worn smooth by centuries of candle smoke until they gleamed like ivory in the lamplight. Freya stopped on the threshold, her gift rising instinctively even in this place of enforced quiet, reaching for the voices that should have been screaming from every surface—

And heard nothing. Only a hum, deep and collective, like a choir heard through a cathedral wall. Held. Contained. *Listening.*

"What is this place?" she whispered.

"A library," said Mother Sera. "The only kind the Thorn cannot burn." She lifted a lamp and walked slowly along the wall, and as she passed, the bones nearest her brightened faintly, each skull kindling with a soft inner light, and Freya understood with a lurch that the abbess was reading them—that this whole chamber was a book written in the only script that survived every conquest, every purge, every rewriting of history by the victorious and the devoured.

"When Aldric's grandfather's grandfather took the throne," the abbess said, "the Thorn was already old. Older than the kingdom. It had worn other crowns before his—it wears whatever will feed it best, and nothing feeds it like power. A king's hunger is a small thing. A kingdom's hunger, channeled through one crowned head—" She shook her head. "The binding was performed by nine women. Nine carvers, from nine valleys, who understood what was coming when the priests did not. They designed the ritual themselves, in secret, and they paid for the knowledge with everything."

The skulls along the wall were glowing brighter now, nine of them, spaced evenly around the circle, and Freya counted them twice because the first count could not possibly be right.

"There were nine carvers," she said slowly. "And there are nine skulls."

Mother Sera met her eyes across the lamplit circle. "The binding requires a vessel. Living bone, willingly given—a soul that consents to become the lock. That is the price the Thorn exacts for being bound at all; it must be *held*, not merely caged, and holding requires a hand. Nine hands, that first night. Each carver carved the ritual into her own living bone and poured herself into the work, and what remained afterward was—" the abbess paused, choosing the word with a craftsman's care, "—*remainder*. Enough to speak, briefly. Enough to leave instructions. Not enough to live."

The hum in the walls deepened. Freya found she had pressed her back against the doorframe without noticing.

"But the binding weakens," she said. "The priestess told me. Every generation, a little. Because—"

"Because locks wear, and keys rust, and the vessel's consent does not outlive the vessel." Mother Sera's voice was gentle, which somehow made it worse. "Each of the nine bound the Thorn for the span of her own life and a little beyond. When the last of them failed, the order rebuilt the binding as best we could—weaker, cruder, using what lore survived. That was four hundred years ago. It bought the kingdom time. It bought Aldric his thirty years of half-life, hollowed and screaming where no one can hear him. And now it is failing entirely, and the Thorn means to seal itself into the succession—to make the crown its body permanently, so that no vessel, no ritual, no consent will ever pry it loose again."

"The coronation."

"The coronation." The abbess set down the lamp. In its light her face was all planes and shadow, ancient as the walls. "Unbinding is possible. It has always been possible. The ritual is the mirror of the binding—carved into living bone, willingly offered, a vessel to take the Thorn's attention while the true voice returns to the true king. The dead can help you. The dead want to help you; you've heard them, haven't you? All those angry voices on the road?" A ghost of something moved across her face—not quite a smile. "They are the Thorn's leavings, Freya. Everyone it ever swallowed and spat out incomplete. They hate it more than anything that lives. Given a vessel strong enough to carry their voices, they will tear it apart."

Hope rose in Freya like water finding a crack—and then she heard herself, heard the shape of what the abbess had actually said, and the hope curdled even as it climbed.

"A vessel," she repeated. "Living bone. Willingly given." Her mouth had gone dry. "Given *how*, Mother Sera? What happens to the vessel?"

The abbess was quiet for a long moment. Somewhere above them, muffled by stone, Wren slept on, and Freya clung to the sound of that small steady breathing the way a drowning woman clings to driftwood.

"The nine who made the first binding left remainders," Mother Sera said at last. "That is your answer. That is the whole of it. The vessel holds the Thorn's attention—all of it, every hungry year of it, the fury of a thing that has eaten kingdoms—and pours the dead through itself like a blade through a wound. And when it is done, if it is done, what remains of the vessel is..." She spread her scarred hands. "Remainder."

"No." Freya heard her own voice crack on the word. "There's another way. There has to be. Six hundred years of remembering, an entire order built on preserving this—you're telling me the only door out of this is a door that closes behind whoever walks through it?"

"I am telling you what the nine wrote in their own dying bones," said the abbess, "because you deserve truth more than comfort. Yes. The ritual demands a willing vessel of living bone. Yes. The vessel does not survive as you understand surviving. And yes—" her ash-grey eyes did not waver, "—I know what travels with you. I know what sleeps upstairs wearing a child's body. The bloodline was bred for this, Freya. The vessels always have been. Whoever sent that child to your door knew precisely what they were sending."

The floor seemed to tilt. Freya gripped the doorframe.

"Wren is seven years old."

"Wren is a vessel," Mother Sera said softly, "and the Thorn is nearly free, and the heir is crowned in nineteen days. I am sorry. Truth is a carver's tool too, and it cuts the hand that holds it."

* * *

Freya did not remember climbing the stairs. She found herself sitting on the edge of Wren's pallet in the lavender-smelling cell, watching the child sleep, and the lamp was guttering, and outside the window the blackthorn along the eastern wall had begun to shed its out-of-season blossoms into the dark like snow falling upward.

*Nineteen days.* And the only door out was a door that closed forever.

Wren stirred. Small fingers curled against the blanket. And in sleep, unguarded, the child's lips moved, and what came out was not the old tongue at all but a whisper in plain, broken, ordinary words—the first Freya had ever heard from her:

"Don't let it be me. Please. Don't let it be me."

Then the ancient language flooded back in like a tide reclaiming sand, and the bones in Freya's pack trembled against each other, and far down the mountain, at the edge of hearing, a sound began that had no business on these roads at all.

Hoofbeats. Many of them. Moving fast.

Freya was at the window in three strides. Below, in the valley dark, torchlight crawled along the switchback trail like a line of fire ants—too many torches, moving with the rigid discipline of soldiers, and at their head a banner she knew from Dain Voss's world and dreaded in her own.

The justiciars had found the Quiet Sisters.

And the abbess, downstairs, was already lighting candles against the coming dark.
