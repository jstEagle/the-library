## 11. Coronation Eve

The capital smelled of rot beneath its perfume.

Ser Dain Voss had noticed it first at the gates—candles and incense burning in every window for the coronation, honey and myrrh and crushed roses strewn across the cobbles—and under all of it, faint as a whisper through a wall, something sweet gone wrong. Like fruit left too long in a dark room. Like a body that had not yet been found.

He had told himself it was the drains. Cities were cities. Thornmere's valley air had spoiled him, three weeks of riding through country where the wind carried nothing but rain and pine.

But now, standing in the great gallery of the palace of Caer Alden with a justiciar's white cloak on his shoulders and his sister's memory heavy in his chest, Dain watched the courtiers move through the candlelight and understood that the smell was not the drains.

They moved wrong.

It was small things at first, the things you might miss if you had not spent your life reading bodies—the way a man reads a liar's hands, the way he reads a dying man's eyes. The courtiers of Caer Alden did not fidget. That was the first strangeness. Dain had never seen a gathering of nobles anywhere that did not fidget: adjusting rings, shifting weight, touching collars against the heat of too many candles. These people stood like furniture arranged by a careful hand. When they walked, they walked in curves that wasted no motion. When they spoke to one another, their smiles arrived a half-beat before their words, like actors who had learned the lines but not the timing.

And their eyes. He kept coming back to the eyes.

Not empty. That would have been easier. Empty eyes belonged to corpses and to the mad, and Dain knew how to face both. These eyes were *occupied*. There was a person looking out of them still—he could see him in the young lord who laughed at the Master of Coin's joke, see the flicker of something human behind the glass—but the person was watching from very far away, from the bottom of a deep well, while something else wore his face into the light.

*You are tired,* Dain told himself. *You have ridden three weeks chasing rumors of witches. You slept badly in Ash Market and worse since.*

But he was not tired enough to unsee it.

* * *

The Hall of Anticipation stretched the length of two churches, its vaulted ceiling painted with the history of the Aldric line: kings crowned by angels, kings anointed by rivers, kings kneeling before a radiant figure whose face had been repainted so many times it had become no one at all. Scaffolding climbed one wall where workmen were setting the final panel—a new king, crowned, the paint still wet enough to gleam.

Prince Corin stood beneath it.

Dain had been granted audience—or rather, audience had been arranged for him; he had requested it four times and been refused twice before the third refusal came back strangely amended, a note in a clerk's hand he did not recognize: *Come at dusk. Come alone.* He should have reported that. A summons that bypassed protocol was either a trap or a confession, and both were justiciar business. But he had come anyway, alone, because there was no one left to report to whom he trusted. His report from the fens—the falsified one, the first lie of his career, *no trace of the woman or child found, pursuit abandoned*—had been accepted without question. Accepted too easily. As if someone above him had already known what he would write.

The prince was talking with the Lord Chamberlain, or rather the Lord Chamberlain was talking and the prince was standing in the way a well stands in a courtyard. Corin was twenty-three years old and had been raised in this palace among these people, and Dain thought: *he has never once been alone.* Not truly. Even now, even in the pause between words, the courtiers orbited him like gulls after a fishing boat, close enough to touch, never quite touching.

Then the prince turned and looked at Dain across the length of the hall, and Dain forgot every prepared sentence he had carried up the marble stairs.

Corin's eyes were wrong.

Not cold—Dain had met cold men, ambitious men, cruel men, and none of them looked like this. It was the angle of the gaze. People look *at* each other; even the proudest king looks slightly toward the thing he sees. Corin's gaze went through Dain as if Dain were a window, fixed on some point behind him, some point perhaps inside him, and there was a quality of attention in it that made Dain's skin crawl—the attention of something deciding whether a thing was ripe.

"Ser Voss." The prince's voice was pleasant. Well-bred. Perfectly modulated. "The justiciar who lost a witch in the marshes."

"I did not lose her, Your Highness. I concluded she was not where the crown needed her to be."

Something moved behind the prince's face. Not a smile. An adjustment, like a man shifting a mask that had slipped a fraction. "How convenient for her."

"The law is not a net, Highness. It is a scale. Sometimes it weighs nothing on one side, and that is justice too."

"Is it?" Corin tilted his head, and the chandeliers caught his eyes and for one instant—for one horrible instant—Dain saw them plainly, saw that the irises were ringed with a color that had no name, a brown so deep it shaded into black and then past black into something that drank the candlelight rather than reflecting it. "I wonder if you believe that. I wonder—" and here the prince stepped closer, and the courtiers parted around him without appearing to notice they were doing it, "—what you heard, Ser Voss. In the fens. They say the witch travels with a child that speaks in dead voices. They say it spoke to you."

Dain's hand wanted to go to his sword. He held it still, the way he had learned to hold it still over a dying man's wound. "Words in the dark, Highness. Marsh birds sound like many things."

"Do they." The prince smiled, and the smile reached every part of his face except the place where smiles live. "My father will be delighted to hear the roads are quiet. You'll attend the coronation tomorrow, of course. All the realm attends. All the realm is *glad*."

There it was again—that word spoken with a weight it could not carry, *glad*, pressed down like a thumb on a bruise. Around them, as if the word had been a cue, the courtiers turned as one toward the prince and smiled.

All of them. At once. In silence.

Dain bowed, because bowing was the only motion left that made sense. "I would be honored, Highness."

"I know," said Prince Corin. "That is what you are for."

* * *

He did not sleep. He told himself it was the hour, the noise of preparation carrying through the palace walls where the guest quarters had been given him—hammering, singing, the endless rehearsal of trumpets—but when midnight came the noise stopped all at once, between one beat and the next, as if a hand had closed over the city's mouth, and the silence that followed was worse.

Dain lay on the narrow bed in his boots with his sword across his chest, which was a habit from border service and which had saved his life twice, and stared at the ceiling, and listened.

At first there was nothing. Then, far off, muffled by stone and distance, a sound began.

Singing? No. Chanting. Many voices, low, in unison, rising and falling in a rhythm that was almost a rhythm, the way a limping walk is almost a walk. It came from below—from beneath, he realized, from the direction of the old catacombs that predated the palace, the ones sealed a generation ago by royal decree after King Aldric's illness. After the binding, said the old priests' histories, though the histories used other words. After the king's salvation.

Dain got up. He was not sure afterward whether he decided to get up or simply found himself already standing, but he took his sword and his cloak and went out into the corridors, following the sound down.

The palace at night was a drowned ship. Every corridor he passed through held people asleep—or holding still in the dark in ways sleepers do not hold still, lying straight as laid-out tools, faces slack, chests rising in perfect synchrony when he passed open doors, dozens of them, hundreds, breathing together like a single lung. He stopped in one doorway and watched a chambermaid sleep and told himself: *people breathe alike. Bodies are alike. This means nothing.* But his body knew better. His body had grown cold the way it grew cold on battlefields before the fighting started, some animal ledger in him tallying wrongness faster than his mind could read it.

The chanting led him down. Stairs he had not known existed, behind a door that should have been locked and was not, its lock bright with fresh oil—as if someone wanted it passable tonight of all nights. Down past storerooms hung with coronation banners, down past a wine cellar where the barrels had been pushed aside to reveal a low arch bricked shut long ago, the mortar around its edges crumbled now, picked away, a gap at the top wide enough for a man to duck through.

The chanting came through the gap.

Dain stood before it a long moment. He thought about his oath. He thought about the fens, and the word Wren had spoken in Lira's voice—*Dain*—and how he had knelt in freezing water and lied in his report to keep a witch alive, and how every step since had carried him further from the man who had taken that oath, and closer, perhaps, to whatever man he actually was underneath the white cloak. He thought: *if I go through that arch, I cannot unknow what I find.*

He went through.

* * *

The catacombs beneath Caer Alden were older than the kingdom. He understood that immediately, the way you understand the age of a tree by the size of its roots—the passages were cut in stone that predated masonry, rough-hewn, sloping downward along veins of rock that glittered faintly with a wet black shine, like scales. And the walls were carved. Thousands of figures, generations of them, spiraling inward toward the deep places: kings and priests and nameless kneeling crowds, and always, recurring, growing larger as the passages descended, a single image—a crown with thorns growing through it, roots descending from the circlet into a mouth.

Into a mouth that was eating.

The chanting swelled. He followed it around a final bend and stopped in the shadow of a broken pillar, and what he saw he would carry until he died.

A cavern, natural once, now shaped by hands into a hollow dome. At its center stood a throne—not the throne of the hall above, not gold and oak, but a chair of black wood grown rather than built, its arms twisted like briar, and on it sat a figure that had once been King Aldric.

Dain had seen portraits. Everyone had. The savior-king, the strong king, the king who had given himself to save the realm from the Blight Years, who had taken the Thorn-crown onto his own head so that it need not take the world. The portraits showed a broad man with a beard going grey and kind, tired eyes.

The thing on the throne wore that face the way a glove wears a hand.

It was thinner. It was *wearing thin*. The skin lay close over the skull beneath, and the eyes were open and they were the worst thing Dain had ever seen, because they were aware. Somewhere in that ruined face a man was still home, and he was looking out, and he was screaming, though his mouth did not move, though his throat made no sound at all—Dain could not have said how he knew, only that he knew, the way you know grief in a stranger's shoulders across a street.

Around the throne, kneeling in concentric rings, were the singers. Hundreds. Courtiers—he recognized half of them, lords and ladies whose faces he had watched smile in the gallery, clerks, guardsmen off duty, servants, the Master of Coin himself with his chain of office still around his neck. Their voices rose and fell in that limping rhythm, and with every rise, the thing on the throne breathed in.

Dain saw it feed.

There was no other word. With each swell of the chant, something passed from the kneeling crowd into the seated king—visible only as a dimming, a lessening, the way a candle flame leans when a door opens elsewhere in the house. The nearest kneelers were grey. Not aged—*drained*, their faces smooth and bloodless as wax, their eyes fixed on the throne with expressions of perfect, vacant devotion. And as Dain watched, a young woman in the second ring swayed, and her lips kept moving with the chant, and the color went out of her cheeks like water out of a cracked cup, and she smiled.

She smiled as it took her.

Dain's stomach turned over. He pressed himself back against the pillar, into shadow, and his hand on his sword hilt was shaking, and he did not draw, because there were hundreds of them and one of him and because drawing steel would accomplish nothing against a hunger that ate through song. He thought of the fens. He thought of a witch and a mud-caked child, of rumors he had been sent to bury and a report he had chosen to falsify, and the shape of it all rose in him at once, terrible and clean, like a coastline seen from a cliff:

*They sent me to stop the one person who could end this.*

The chant began to fade. The rings of worshippers stirred, rose, smoothed their clothes, and filed away up the passages in silence, passing within arm's reach of his hiding place, their footsteps soft, their faces calm, going back up to their beds to sleep until morning, when they would dress for a coronation and be glad.

Last of all came the Lord Chamberlain. At the arch he paused, and turned his head—not toward Dain, Dain was sure of it, sure and not sure—and said, in a voice as mild as a man discussing weather:

"He is nearly ready. The vessel ripens with the crown. Tomorrow there will be no separating them at all."

Then he too was gone, and Dain stood alone in the dark with the smell of rot-under-perfume thick in his throat, and above him, through fathoms of stone, the trumpets were rehearsing for a coronation.

He thought of his oath: *to uphold the crown and the lawful succession thereof.* He recited it in his mind the way a man touches a broken bone, testing.

It broke.

Tomorrow at dawn, Prince Corin would take the throne of Caer Alden, and whatever wore the king would pour itself into the heir through a rite older than the kingdom, and the realm would kneel and be glad, and the law—the law Dain had served with his whole life, the law he had believed was the same thing as justice—would stand at the door and hold it open.

Unless someone reached the binding-stone first.

He did not know yet how he knew that phrase; it surfaced in him like a fish rising, silver and sudden, from depths he had not known he had—perhaps from the carvings on the walls, perhaps from somewhere older. But he knew it, and he knew, with the certainty of a man watching the last bridge burn behind him, exactly where the witch and the changeling would be going.

Dain pulled his cloak tight, turned his back on the throne, and climbed toward the dying of the night.
