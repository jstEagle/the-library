## 12. The Price of Silence

The convent had no bells. That was the first thing Freya had noticed, three days ago, when Sister Maera led them through the postern gate in the grey hour before dawn: an absence, a silence shaped exactly like the sound that should have been there. Every other religious house Freya had ever heard of marked its hours with bronze. The Quiet Sisters marked theirs with nothing at all, as if they had long ago decided that anything which rang could also be used to call the wrong kind of attention.

Now, on the fourth morning, Freya stood in the scriptorium and understood the silence differently. It was not the silence of hiding. It was the silence of a held breath.

The abbess—Mother Osanna, a small woman with hands like knotted rope and eyes the pale grey of river ice—had spent three nights teaching her. Three nights of candle-smoke and cold stone, of vellum so old it had gone the color of tea, of words written in an alphabet that predated the kingdom's letters the way mountains predate roads.

"The binding was not made," Osanna said, tapping the vellum with one blunt finger. "That is the first lie everyone believes. Bindings are made. Cages are made. What was done to Aldric's crown was a *wound*—a wound deliberately inflicted, and dressed to look like a seal."

Freya looked down at the diagram inked there: a crown rendered in fine lines, and threaded through it, a shape that made her eyes water to follow, like a stair drawn by someone who had never seen stairs. Around the crown's circlet, in the old alphabet, ran a single repeated word.

"What does it say?"

"*It eats, and is fed. It feeds, and is bound.*" Osanna's voice was flat as slate. "The Thorn is not a prisoner in the crown, child. It is a tenant. The binding is its lease. And leases—" she looked up, and her ice-grey eyes were terribly tired, "—can be renewed. Or ended."

Wren sat in the corner of the scriptorium, wrapped in one of the sisters' rough brown cloaks, watching with those eyes that were sometimes a child's eyes and sometimes something much, much older. The child had not spoken since the fens—not in the old language, not at all. Freya had begun to find the silence worse than the speech.

"And ending it?" Freya asked. "The ritual. You said there was a text."

Osanna was quiet for so long that the candle between them guttered and steadied twice.

"There is," she said at last. "But I will not teach it to you tonight. Tonight I will teach you why you must think very carefully before you ask to learn it."

* * *

The raid came at dusk, as raids do, when the light is dying and men's courage is at its cheapest and its cruelest.

Freya heard it first in the bones. She had taken to carrying a small carved vertebra in her pocket—Sister Maera's gift, the relic of some sister dead four hundred years—and it had been quiet all day, the way relics were quiet in Thornmere. Then, as the sun went down behind the convent's western wall, it began to hum against her thigh like a struck tuning fork.

She was across the courtyard before she had decided to move.

"They're coming," she said to Maera, who was drawing water at the well. "Riders. Many. From the north road."

Maera did not question her. That was the thing about the Quiet Sisters, Freya was learning: they had spent generations being told impossible things by their abbess, and they had learned to believe first and grieve later. Maera dropped the bucket, rang the well-chain against the stone three times—the convent's only bell, and it rang only for this—and the whole house began to move with the practiced calm of people who had rehearsed their own destruction for two centuries.

Osanna met Freya at the scriptorium door, pressing a bundle into her arms: oilcloth-wrapped, heavy, the shape of books.

"The texts. All of them. You will take them and you will not stop walking until you reach the capital."

"Mother—"

"Listen to me." The old woman's hands closed over Freya's wrists, and her grip was astonishingly strong. "The justiciars have come for you, but they will burn us regardless, because we are what we are, and the Thorn hates what it cannot digest. That has been true since before I was born. Do not make our deaths heavier by adding your guilt to them." Her voice gentled. "Wren is in the undercroft with the others. Go. Through the cistern passage. It comes out in the orchard."

"And you?"

Osanna smiled, and it was a terrible smile, serene as the faces on tomb effigies.

"I am eighty-one years old, child. I have been the keeper of a secret my whole life, and I find I have very little interest in being the keeper of a grave." She turned Freya by the shoulders, firmly, toward the stair. "The dead will need a voice in the days to come. Make sure it is yours."

The first torchlight crested the north wall as Freya reached the bottom of the stair.

* * *

The undercroft was a low vault of stone, cold enough that breath showed, and it was full of sisters.

Not hiding—Freya saw that at once. They were kneeling in rows before the alcoves set into the walls, and in each alcove lay a skull, clean and pale, resting on a cushion of brown cloth. Dozens of them. Hundreds. The Quiet Sisters kept their dead close, and had for two hundred years, and Freya understood in a single vertiginous moment that she had walked into a house that was, in its way, a mirror of her own workshop.

Wren stood in the middle of the floor, small and still, surrounded by kneeling women.

"Freya." Sister Maera took her arm. "The cistern passage is behind the far alcove. But Mother Osanna—" and here Maera's composure cracked, just at the edges, like ice under a boot, "—Mother Osanna stayed above. She said someone must meet them at the gate. She said—" Maera stopped. Swallowed. "She said the house must not die empty."

Above them, muffled by two floors of stone, came the sound of the gate splintering.

The sisters began to sing.

It was not a hymn, or if it was, it was a hymn with no words—only a low, sustained tone, each woman finding her own note, the notes weaving into a chord that seemed to come up through the floor itself, out of the stone, out of the bones in their alcoves. Freya felt it in her teeth. She felt it in the vertebra in her pocket, which had gone from humming to singing, a thin bright thread of a dead woman's voice rising to join the living ones.

And Freya heard—she could not help hearing—what the bones in those alcoves were saying.

They were not afraid.

Two hundred years of dead women, and not one of them afraid. They said things like *I kept the lamp* and *I copied the fourth book twice, the ink smeared, forgive me* and *the apples were good this year* and *she is here, the one we waited for, she is here at last*—and that last voice came from the alcove nearest the cistern door, from a skull whose brow was scored with fine carved lines in a pattern Freya recognized from the vellum upstairs.

The one they waited for. Freya's throat closed.

"Take the child and go," said the skull, in a voice like wind through a dry reed, and every sister in the undercroft turned her head toward Freya at once, and Freya went.

* * *

The cistern passage was narrow, black, and ankle-deep in water that smelled of stone and old rain. Wren's hand found hers in the dark without fumbling, small fingers lacing through hers with the unselfconscious trust of a child who had decided, somewhere in the fens, that this bone-carver was a fixed point in a turning world.

They had gone perhaps two hundred paces when the light came.

It came down through the grates overhead—torchlight, flickering—and with it came sound: boots in the courtyard, the crash of a door, and then a voice, amplified by stone, that Freya recognized and would recognize in her sleep until she died.

Ser Dain Voss.

"Where is the woman?" His voice was strained, taut as a bow at full draw. "The witch, and the child. Where?"

And then Osanna's voice, calm as a still pond: "Gone, Ser Justiciar. Gone where you cannot follow, and gone where your master's long arm cannot reach. You are welcome to search the orchard. Mind the bees; they are temperamental in autumn."

"Mother." Dain's voice cracked on the word, and Freya pressed herself against the wet stone below the nearest grate, Wren pulled tight against her side, and listened to a good man being pulled apart. "I have orders. I have—there is a thing behind the throne, I have seen it, I have *felt* it, and it tells me that what I do is justice, and I believe it, God help me, I believe it, and the only mercy I can offer you is to do this quickly. Tell me where she went."

"Child," said Osanna, and her voice was suddenly, unbearably gentle, "I am going to tell you something that no one in that palace will ever tell you. The Thorn does not tell you what to do. It only makes certain that the things you already believe are the only things left to believe. You spared a village once. Ask yourself who ordered it burned, and ask yourself why you are still alive to wonder."

Silence, above. Long silence. Water dripped somewhere in the dark.

"You cannot know that," Dain said at last, hoarse.

"I am eighty-one years old. I know everything that matters and nothing that doesn't. Now—" a rustle, cloth moving, and Freya's blood went cold, because she knew what that sound was, "—I believe you said quickly."

"Mother, *no*—"

"Ser Justiciar. You will lower that sword or use it, but you will not stand there trembling, it is undignified in a man of your rank."

What happened next, Freya did not see. She heard it: a shout that was not Dain's voice, or was Dain's voice gone wrong—thickened, layered, two voices wearing one throat—and then the terrible wet sound of steel finding flesh, and then another sound, worse, a sound like roots tearing through soil, like something growing very fast in a place where nothing should grow, and the torchlight through the grate went from orange to a green so deep it was nearly black.

Wren made a sound against Freya's shoulder. Small. Broken.

Then the green light died. The torchlight returned, ordinary and shaking. Boots moved above them—many boots, then fewer, then one set, walking slowly, stopping directly over the grate.

"I'm sorry," Dain Voss said, to no one, to everyone, to the dark. His voice was ruined. "I'm sorry."

The boots walked away.

* * *

Freya did not remember deciding to go back up. She was simply there, in the courtyard, with Wren clinging to her back and the oilcloth bundle still in her arms, and the convent burning around her in the orderly way the sisters had surely planned—fire in the scriptorium, fire in the library, nothing left for the justiciars' scholars but ash and stone.

Osanna lay in the middle of the courtyard, and Freya made herself look.

The Thorn had been there. She could see it in the way the old woman's body was marked—not wounds, exactly, though there were wounds, but *patterns*, dark lines radiating from the sword-stroke in her chest like frost on glass, like the diagram of the crown, like roots. The Thorn had come through Dain's arm the way it came through Corin's eyes, and it had taken Osanna's last moments and tried to make them into a message, a warning written in her corpse for anyone who might read it.

But the Thorn had made one mistake. It had used her bones.

Freya knelt. She set down the bundle. She took Osanna's head in her lap, gently, the way she had held a hundred skulls in her workshop in Thornmere, and she put her palm flat against the old woman's temple, and she listened.

The courtyard was full of the dead now—Maera, and the sisters who had sung, and sisters dead two hundred years rising out of the burning house like heat-shimmer—and they were all screaming, or singing, or both, but through it all, one voice, close and clear and dry as autumn leaves:

*Child. Child, listen, there is little time and I have so little left to say it with.*

"Mother." Freya's voice broke. "Mother, I'm here."

*The ritual. You will have found it in the third book, the one with the cracked spine. You will have read it and thought it impossible. It is not impossible. It is only priced.*

The dead were pressing closer. The fire roared. Wren's small arms tightened around Freya's neck.

*The binding is a knot, and knots are not cut, they are *undone*—but a knot of that age, fed that long, will not loosen for anything dead or anything made. It must be held from the inside. It must be *worn*. Living bone, child. A vessel of living bone, willing, awake, and—*

The voice frayed. Freya gripped the skull. "And what? Mother, and *what*?"

*And the vessel does not survive the wearing. That is the price of silence, Freya Hartwell. That is the whole price. The Thorn is a hunger, and hunger can only be ended by something that lets itself be eaten—and does not stop being itself while it is eaten. Do you understand? Do you understand what I am telling you?*

Freya understood.

She understood it the way you understand a fall—not all at once, but in the stomach, in the sudden lightness, in the certainty of the ground rising. A vessel of living bone. A willing vessel. And the texts upstairs, the third book with the cracked spine, had said it in the old alphabet in words she had read and reread and not comprehended, because her mind had refused to comprehend them: *the vessel gives its voice to the bound, and its silence to the world.*

Her gift. Her hearing. Her whole self, poured into the knot to hold it open from the inside, and then—

And then nothing. An ordinary life. No—Freya felt something in her chest go very still—not even that. The texts had been clear. The vessel was *consumed*. The silence was total.

*Freya.* Osanna's voice was almost gone now, a thread, a breath of dry wind. *There is a girl on your back who has been bred for this since before the kingdom had a name. Her whole line was bred for it. She will offer. She is brave, and she is young, and she will offer, and it will be the easiest thing in the world to let her.*

"Mother—"

*Do not let her.* The voice flared, sudden and iron-hard. *A child's sacrifice is not justice, it is a failure wearing justice's clothes. Find another way. Find—* a rattling pause, *—find the way that costs the one who understands the price. That is the only sacrifice that means anything. That is—*

The voice stopped.

Not faded. Stopped, clean, like a door closing, and Freya knelt in the burning courtyard holding a skull that was suddenly, horribly, only bone—no voice, no warmth, no Osanna—and the silence where the old woman had been was the loudest thing Freya had ever heard.

The sisters' dead were withdrawing, drawn back into their burning alcoves, their work done, their message delivered. The last one to go—the skull with the scored brow, the one who had waited—lingered at the edge of the firelight and looked at Freya with the patience of two hundred years.

*She is here,* the dead sister had said. *The one we waited for.*

Freya looked down at her own hands. A carver's hands, scarred and strong, hands that had spent thirty years listening to the dead because the living had nothing she needed.

A vessel of living bone. Willing. Awake.

*Find the way that costs the one who understands the price.*

"Freya?" Wren's voice, from behind her—Wren's own voice, the child's voice, small and frightened and alive. "The fire's getting bigger. We have to go."

Freya rose. She picked up the bundle of texts. She took Wren's hand, and they walked out through the orchard, past the hives Osanna had told Dain to mind, into the dark of the road east, toward the capital, toward the binding-stone, toward the knot that needed wearing.

And all the way, in the pocket against her heart, her little carved vertebra sang on and on—a dead woman's voice, a gift she had always known was temporary, a thing she was already, without having decided it, beginning to say goodbye to.
