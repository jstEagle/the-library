## 13. Wren Speaks

The convent gave them a cell at the end of the east corridor, a narrow room with a window that faced the burial ground and a bed wide enough for two if neither of them moved much. Freya had not slept properly in three nights. The abbess's words still rang in her skull like a struck bell: *a willing vessel of living bone.* She turned the phrase over the way she'd once turned a knucklebone in her fingers, feeling for the flaw in it, the place where it would break.

Wren slept curled against her side, small and warm and breathing with the terrible steadiness of a child who had never once been afraid of the dark. That steadiness frightened Freya more than anything the justiciars could do. Children were supposed to wake screaming from dreams. Wren only ever woke quietly, eyes open, watching the ceiling as though reading something written there.

On the fourth night, Wren woke and did not go back to sleep.

Freya knew it before she opened her own eyes—not by sound, but by silence. The ancient language that lived in the child like water in a well had gone still. For six days now, since they had fled the burning convent, Wren had spoken in sleep, murmuring syllables older than any kingdom, syllables that made the bones in Freya's pack hum against each other like struck tuning forks. Tonight there was nothing. Just breathing. Just a child.

She opened her eyes.

Wren was sitting up in the bed, knees drawn to chest, looking at her. The moonlight through the window fell across the child's face, and Freya saw—really saw, for the first time since the storm-lashed doorstep in Thornmere—a girl of perhaps eight years, with mud-brown hair and a chipped front tooth and eyes that were, at last, only eyes. No depth in them. No age. Just the wary, exhausted gaze of someone who has been holding a door shut for a very long time and has finally been allowed to let go of the handle.

"You're still in there," Freya whispered. "You're still you."

"I'm always me." Wren's voice was rough from disuse, small in the dark room. "That's what nobody understands. It's not possession. It's a house with too many guests." The girl picked at a thread on the blanket. "The voice talks when I let it talk. When I'm tired. When I'm scared. When I—" She stopped. Started again, slower. "When I don't want to be alone with what I know."

Freya sat up slowly, careful, the way one moves around a wounded animal—or around a truth that might bolt. "How long have you been able to speak like this? Plain speech?"

"Since the abbess died." Wren's chin trembled, just once, and was mastered. "She held so much of it back, you know. The voice. All those years of lore, all those Sisters keeping the old words alive so the vessel wouldn't have to carry them alone. When she died, it was like—" the girl searched for the word, frowning, "—like a dam breaking. Everything came through at once. And then it settled. And I could hear myself again underneath."

"And the voice?"

"Asleep. Or pretending to be." Wren looked toward the window, toward the graves beyond. "It doesn't like it here. Too many quiet women buried in one place. It gets bored."

Freya's hands had gone cold. She folded them in her lap where the child couldn't see. "Wren. Tell me your name. Your true name. Not the one you gave me on the road."

The girl was quiet for a long moment. Outside, an owl called across the burial ground, and somewhere below them a Sister turned in her sleep, the cot creaking like a ship's timber.

"Wren," she said at last. "It really is Wren. My mother chose it. She said wrens are small and nobody notices them, and nobody notices us until it's too late to matter." A ghost of a smile, there and gone. "She thought she was being funny."

"Your mother. Where is she?"

"Dead. They're all dead." Wren said it the way children say things they have rehearsed saying, flat and fast, like stepping quickly over ice. "That's what I have to tell you. That's why I woke you. You keep looking at me like I'm something to be saved, and you won't listen, and we're running out of road, and I need you to *listen*."

Freya listened.

* * *

"There were nine families," Wren said. She had drawn her knees up tighter, wrapped her arms around them, made herself small in the way of children telling terrible stories—they shrink because the story is too big to hold any other way. "Nine families who swore themselves to the binding, back when King Aldric's grandfather was crowned. Before the Thorn ever touched him. The priestesses knew it would come back—it always comes back, it's been coming back since before the valley, since before the kingdoms—and they knew the binding would fail someday, because everything bound fails eventually. Rope rots. Stone cracks. Oaths get forgotten."

"So they made a key," Freya said slowly. Her mouth had gone dry.

"They made nine keys." Wren held up her small fingers, counted them off. "Nine bloodlines, each carrying a piece of the ritual, each raising their children knowing. Knowing what they were for. My line carried the vessel-part. The part that stands at the stone and holds the Thorn while it's cut loose." She lowered her hand. "Every generation, my family taught one child the whole of it. Every word. Every cost. So that when the day came, the child would walk to the stone with open eyes."

The owl called again. Freya found she was gripping the edge of the bed hard enough to hurt her fingers.

"The cost," she said. "Say it plainly. I've earned plain speech, I think. Six days of listening to dead languages—I've earned it."

Wren looked at her then, straight on, and there was nothing childish in her face at all. It was the face of the priestess from the relic, the one who had spoken through carved bone about failing bindings. Generations lived behind those eyes, compressed into eight years of flesh.

"I die," Wren said. "The vessel holds the Thorn while the king is unbound, and the Thorn does not let go of what holds it. That's the whole of the ritual. That's why it needs a willing vessel—because the Thorn can't take an unwilling soul, it can only be *given* to. My grandmother was willing. My great-grandmother. Nine generations of mothers teaching daughters how to die well, so that a king none of us will ever meet could keep his crown and his soul both." She shrugged, a small, terrible shrug. "I've known since I was four years old. Mother made sure I understood it was an honor."

"An honor." Freya heard her own voice come out strange, flattened, dangerous.

"It *is* an honor." Wren's voice cracked, and for a moment she was only a child again, indignant, defending the only structure her life had ever had. "Everyone I loved died believing in it. My mother walked out of our village to draw the justiciars away from me, and she knew they'd kill her, and she went anyway, because I'm the last vessel and the ritual dies with me. If I throw it away—if I decide my life matters more than nine generations of—of *everything*—then they all died for nothing. Then the Thorn wins. Then Aldric is eaten and his heir is crowned wearing it, and it spreads past the valley, past everything, and every person who ever kept the oaths will have been a fool." Her breath hitched. "I won't make them fools. I *won't*."

The silence after was very long. Freya became aware of her own heartbeat, slow and heavy, and of the pack resting against the wall where the relics lay—the dead priestess's finger-bone, the abbess's skull-fragment wrapped in linen, all the small voices she had carried out of Thornmere. They were silent tonight. Even the dead, it seemed, knew when to let the living speak.

"Wren," she said carefully. "Come here."

The girl hesitated, then unfolded herself and crossed the narrow space between them. Freya gathered her in, and Wren went stiff for a moment—children bred for sacrifice are rarely held, Freya thought, and the thought nearly broke her—then collapsed against her chest like something melting. Freya held her and looked out the dark window at the graves and felt grief arrive in her like weather, vast and slow and certain.

Because she understood. That was the worst of it. She understood the girl completely.

Freya had spent thirty years making herself invisible in Thornmere, carving other people's griefs into bone, hearing the dead's last words and swallowing them like stones, telling herself it was contentment, telling herself the valley's safety was worth the shrinking. She had built a whole life on the principle that some prices are simply paid, quietly, without complaint, because the alternative is unthinkable. She had called it duty. She had called it peace.

And here was a child, eight years old, offering up the same arithmetic: *my death against the world, a fair trade, who am I to argue.*

"I refused it too," Freya said into the girl's hair. "Did you know that? What I have—the hearing. It isn't a gift. It's a price I paid before I was born, or my mother paid, or someone did, and I spent my whole life pretending the price was fair because I couldn't imagine refusing it." She pulled back so she could see Wren's face. "But I'm learning, little bird. I'm learning late and badly and it hurts, but I'm learning that a price agreed to by people long dead isn't consent. It's inheritance. And you can refuse an inheritance."

"If I refuse, the Thorn wins."

"If you die, the Thorn wins anyway." Freya heard the words come out of her and knew they were true before she fully understood them. "Think. Nine vessels. Nine generations of your blood, walking to the stone, dying willingly—and the binding failed *anyway*. It's failing right now. Whatever the ritual buys, it doesn't buy forever. It buys a turning of the moon, a generation, a breath. The Thorn comes back. It has always come back. Your grandmothers didn't defeat it, Wren. They fed it. Slowly. One faithful daughter at a time." She took the girl's small hands in hers. "I will not add you to its table. Do you hear me? I would sooner carve out my own heart than carry your bones home to whatever grave your family keeps."

"But the ritual needs—"

"The ritual needs what it needs," Freya said. "And I need it to need something else. I've spent my life listening to the dead tell me what must be done. Well. This once, the living get a vote."

Wren stared at her. And then, horribly, wonderfully, the girl began to cry—not the controlled leaking of a child trained to endure, but the full, ugly, hiccupping collapse of a child who has just been told she is allowed to live. She cried into Freya's shoulder for a long time, and Freya held her and rocked her slightly, the way she had seen mothers do in the market at Thornmere, the way no one had ever done for her, and outside the owl called and called and got no answer.

When the crying finally ebbed, Wren pulled back and wiped her face with her sleeve, sniffling, and asked in a small wrecked voice: "Then what do we do?"

"We change the ritual," Freya said. "Or we break it. Or we find the third thing nobody wrote down because nobody dared imagine it." She said it with far more confidence than she felt, and she suspected the girl knew it, and she suspected the girl loved her a little for saying it anyway.

* * *

They talked until the moon set.

Wren told her everything the nine bloodlines had preserved—names, dates, fragments of the original rite as it had been performed before the priestesses' order fractured, details the convent's lore had lost. Freya listened with her carver's mind, sorting, weighing, the way she'd sort a knucklebone for the grain of it. And piece by piece, something began to assemble itself in her thoughts, half-formed and frightening: the ritual required a willing vessel of living bone. Everyone had assumed the vessel must be young, must be of the bloodline, must be *bred* for it.

But the old words—the ones Wren recited now in plain speech, stripped of their incantatory dressing—said only *willing*. Only *living*. Only *bone*.

Living bone. Hers was living bone.

She did not say it aloud. Not yet. There would be time on the road, or there would not, and either way the girl would fight her, and Freya found she hadn't the strength left tonight for one more battle. She banked the thought like a coal in ash and asked instead about the capital, the palace, the coronation—eleven days away now, maybe ten—and Wren answered in the flat competent voice of a child who had memorized maps before she could write, and somewhere in the small hours they both slept, tangled together like refugees, which is what they were.

Freya dreamed of the binding-stone. In the dream it stood in a chamber beneath the earth, veined with light, and it was singing in a language she almost understood, and every voice in it was hers.

* * *

She woke to hammering.

Not in the dream—in the world. Fists on the convent's outer gate, heavy and rhythmic, and beneath them the bell of the watch-tower ringing out the alarm the Quiet Sisters had not needed in a hundred years. Freya was on her feet before consciousness fully arrived, heart slamming, reaching for the pack, for the knife, for Wren—

The girl was already awake, already standing, already listening with her head cocked in that unnerving way she had.

"One rider," Wren said. "Alone. He's not attacking the gate. He's asking for sanctuary." A pause, and her brow furrowed. "He's asking for *you*, Freya. By name." Another pause, longer, and when she spoke again her voice had gone strange. "He says his name is Voss. He says he's come to beg."

Freya stood very still in the grey pre-dawn light, the dead silent in her pack, the living hammering at the gate, and understood that the shape of her road had just changed again—as it always changed, as it would keep changing until the stone or the grave stopped it.

"Let him in," she said.
