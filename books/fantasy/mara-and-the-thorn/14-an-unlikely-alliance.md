## 14. An Unlikely Alliance

The rain had followed them out of the fens, thin and needling, the kind of rain that did not fall so much as hang in the air, waiting to be walked through. Freya kept them off the road. The king's highway ran east toward the capital like a scar through the country, and roads meant eyes, and eyes meant justiciars—and one justiciar in particular, whose trail she had been reading for three days now in the way he moved his men: wide at first, a net flung across the countryside, then narrowing, narrowing, until it was no longer a net but a leash.

He was good. That was the worst of it. In the bones she carried—two finger-bones of a dead courier, taken from a cairn outside Ash Market with the man's family's blessing and a silver piece left in exchange—she could hear fragments of the hunt as the dead heard it, drifting on whatever wind carried voices between worlds. *The witch went north along the drainage cuts. No, west, into the black alders.* He corrected his own errors without pride, without cruelty. When a farmer lied to him about seeing a woman and a child, Dain Voss thanked the man for his trouble and rode on, and only later, from another mouth, did Freya learn that he had come back at dusk and burned nothing, fined nothing, taken nothing.

*He spared Hollowmere once,* the murdered informant's radius had told her, in its dry whisper of old grief. *A village the crown ordered burned for harboring a Thorn-touched child. He opened the back gate himself and stood in the smoke so his men would think everyone already fled.*

She turned this over as she walked, Wren's small hand in hers, the child's palm cool and damp with fen-water. Behind her, Wren hummed—not the ancient language, not anymore, not since the toll-bridge where Freya had first spoken her name aloud and watched the old tongue recede like a tide going out. Just a tuneless child's humming. It should have comforted her. Instead it made her afraid, because it meant Wren was becoming someone, becoming *somebody*, and everything Freya had learned said that somebodies were what the Thorn consumed.

They made camp that night in the shell of a shepherd's cot, its roof half-collapsed, its walls thick with moss that drank the light. Freya built no fire. She laid out the hard cheese and the last of the fen-bread, and Wren ate with the steady, unselfconscious hunger of a child who had learned long ago that food might not come again tomorrow.

It was near midnight when the dog spoke.

Not a bark—a low, conversational woof, once, from the darkness beyond the broken door, and then silence, and then the soft crunch of a boot deliberately placed on wet ground so that it *would* be heard.

Freya was on her feet with the carver's knife out of her sleeve before the second footfall landed. She put herself between the doorway and Wren, and Wren, bless her, did not cry out—only drew her knees up and watched with those old, old eyes that were growing younger by the day.

"I know you're awake," said the darkness. "I've known since the bridge at Marlow's Ford, when you paid the toll with a coin minted forty years ago. I let you get ahead of me after the fens." A pause. "I'm coming to the door. Unarmed. My sword is on my saddle, and my saddle is fifty paces back down the hill."

"Ser Dain Voss," Freya said, "does not walk anywhere unarmed."

The darkness was quiet a moment. "No," it agreed. "But Dain Voss is not walking to your door."

* * *

He came in slowly, hands open at his sides, a big man gone leaner than his armor liked, rain streaming off his oilskin cloak onto the earthen floor. His face was the face she remembered from the informant's bones and from the courier's gossip: honest, weathered, tired in a way that sleep would not fix. He stopped an arm's length inside the doorway and looked at the knife in her hand without flinching.

"That's a bone-carver's tool," he said. "My grandmother had one like it. She mended the church reliquary in our village. Everyone thought her holy." Something moved behind his eyes. "Everyone was wrong, but she was kind, so we let it be."

"What do you want?"

"To sit down, if you'll allow it. My knee has opinions about hills." He lowered himself against the wall with a grunt, deliberate, making himself small, making himself slow. Rain dripped from his beard. "And to show you something. Then you can put that knife in my throat or send me back down the hill, and either way I'll have done the only thing left that makes sense."

Freya did not lower the knife. "You tracked us across three shires."

"I did. On orders." He said it plainly, no apology in it, and she found she respected him more for that. "Warrant says a witch and a changeling, agents of the Thorn, to be brought to the capital in chains for judgment before the coronation." He looked at Wren then, and Freya watched something happen in his face—the same thing that must have happened in the fens, the crack she'd heard about secondhand through the dead, the word spoken in a dead sister's voice. Wren looked back at him, unafraid, chewing bread.

"She's not a changeling," Freya said.

"No. I worked that out around the time my report started lying." He reached into his cloak, slowly, and Freya's knife-hand tightened—but what he drew out was a leather tube, sealed with plain wax, and a folded bundle of papers gone soft at the edges from rain. "This is my report. Or it was. The version I sent ahead to the capital says the witch was seen crossing into the Greymarch, alone, heading away from the city. Dead end. Pursuit continues." He set it on the floor between them. "It's the first lie I've told in twenty-two years of service. It fit badly. Like a boot on the wrong foot."

"Why?" Freya asked. Not *why did you lie*—she thought she knew that. *Why are you here.*

"Because three nights ago I reached the capital ahead of my own dispatches. I have family there. A cousin in the palace guard." His voice went flat and careful, the voice of a man handling something with edges. "I saw the coronation preparations. I saw Prince Corin review the honor guard, and I saw—" He stopped. Started again. "I've served the crown since I was sixteen years old. I have knelt before King Aldric. I remember him. A loud man. A laughing man. He once gave his own horse to a groom whose daughter was sick, because the groom needed to ride for a physician and the king didn't. That was Aldric." He rubbed his face with a wet hand. "The man on the dais last week wore Aldric's face and none of Aldric. And the courtiers around him moved like—"

"Like puppets," Freya said quietly. "Like people being worn."

His head came up sharply. "You've seen it."

"I've heard worse. Sit still." She crouched, took the two finger-bones from the pouch at her belt, and held them loosely in her open palm between them. "These belonged to a courier who died carrying messages between the palace and the border garrisons. He remembers what he carried. Most of it's tax ledgers and love letters. But lately—" The bones stirred against her skin, cold warming to a strange heat, and the whisper came up through her arm like water finding a crack in stone. She gave it voice, because Dain Voss deserved to hear it from the source and not her paraphrase:

*"They feed it in the underchapel. Three times a week. They bring people in through the cistern gate and they don't bring them out. The guards draw lots to avoid the duty. The ones who lose the lot come back wrong. Smiling wrong. Corin walks the halls at night and the candles bend toward him like grass in wind."*

Silence. Rain on the ruined roof. Wren had stopped eating and was watching the bones with an expression far too old for her face.

Dain Voss stared at the finger-bones as though they were coiled snakes. "That's—" He swallowed. "That matches. God help me, that matches what I saw and couldn't name." He looked up at her, and there it was, the thing she had been bracing for and had not dared hope for: not doubt, not suspicion, but a man standing at the edge of everything he believed and choosing, visibly, deliberately, to step off. "Twenty-two years I believed the crown was the country. That serving one was serving the other. And the whole time it was—" His jaw worked. "What is it? What's wearing my king?"

"The Thorn. A devouring spirit bound to Aldric's crown a generation ago. The binding is failing. At the coronation, when the crown passes to Corin—who belongs to it body and bone—it seals itself permanently. It eats the king entire, it wears the heir forever, and then it comes for everywhere else. Thornmere included." She paused. "Especially Thornmere. Valleys that keep magic out are the one meal it can't reach. It's been patient a long time."

"And the child?"

"Wren carries the king's true voice. Exiled from him when the binding was made. She's the key to undoing it." Freya met his eyes. "And before you ask—no, I won't tell you how, and no, you don't want to carry that knowledge to the capital in writing. Some things should only exist in living memory."

Something flickered across his face—hurt, maybe, at being still half-trusted after all this—but he nodded slowly. "Fair. I've read enough sealed warrants to know how paper betrays people."

He unrolled the papers then, weighting the corners with stones from the cot's fallen wall, and Freya leaned over them by the grey light of the moon through the broken roof. Palace plans. Not the public ones sold to tourists and petitioners—these were the working drawings, the true ones, annotated in a clerk's precise hand: the catacombs beneath the throne room, the old king's crypts, the cistern gate the courier's bones had named, and the guard rotations for coronation week, every watch and every post, marked in red ink.

"My cousin copied these," Dain said. "He thinks I'm hunting you. As far as the palace knows, Ser Dain Voss is the most loyal hound they have." His mouth twisted. "There's a bitter joke in there somewhere."

"There's a dead courier in my pouch who'd appreciate it," Freya said. "Why the catacombs? Why give me the underways instead of the gates?"

"Because the gates will be sealed for the coronation. Because the cistern gate is where they take the feeding-stock, and feeding-stock isn't searched, only counted." He tapped the crypt levels. "And because the kings of the old line are buried down there. Twelve generations of them. If your bones talk the way I think they talk, there's no better congregation in all the Nine Shires."

Freya looked at him for a long moment. "You've thought about this."

"I haven't slept since the capital. Thinking is all I've done." He sat back against the wall. "Here's what I am, carver, so you know exactly what you're trusting, which is to say almost nothing. I'm a man whose whole life was obedience, discovering that obedience was the chain. I don't know how to serve a country without a crown to point at. But I know the palace. I know the guards' names and their sisters' names and which of them draws lots honestly. I know which post commanders drink and which pray. If you're going to do whatever it is you're going to do, you'll do it better with me than without me." He spread his big, rain-chapped hands. "That's all I have. Take it or don't."

* * *

Wren chose that moment to stand up.

She crossed the earthen floor with the grave deliberation of a much older creature, stopped in front of the kneeling justiciar, and studied his face the way she studied everything—thoroughly, without hurry, as if memorizing it for later testimony. Then she reached out and touched the scar on his jaw, the old one, white against the rain-flushed skin.

"Sister," Wren said.

One word. Common Tongue, clear as a bell, and Dain Voss went absolutely still, the way a man goes still when a wound he thought closed opens again.

"In the fens," he said hoarsely. "She said it in Lira's voice. My sister died when I was nineteen. Fever. I held her hand." He looked at Freya, and his eyes were wet and furious with it. "Is that her? Is that the child doing it? Is it a trick?"

"It's not a trick," Freya said gently. "And it's not Lira. Wren carries voices the way I carry them—borrowed, given, held in trust. The dead speak through her sometimes. I think—" she hesitated, then decided he had earned the truth, "—I think your sister has been trying to reach you for a long time, and Wren is the first door she's found that would open."

Dain Voss bowed his head. For a long moment the only sound was the rain, and the small, even breathing of a child standing witness. When he looked up, his face had changed—settled, the way silt settles after a flood, leaving the shape of the land new and true.

"Lira used to say I'd die for the wrong king," he said. "She said it as a joke. She wasn't joking." He looked at Wren. "Thank you. Whatever that was. Thank you."

Wren patted his scarred jaw once, gravely, and went back to her bread.

* * *

They talked until the rain stopped and the sky went the color of pewter, and then until it lightened further, to pearl. Freya laid out what she knew of the ritual's requirements—vaguely, carefully, keeping the terrible heart of it veiled even now—and Dain matched it with the practical architecture of impossibility: which corridors flooded, which stairs were watched, where the cistern gate's counting-clerk kept his ledger and at what hour he drank. Somewhere in the third hour the knife had gone back into her sleeve. Neither of them remarked on it.

"We go in two days before the coronation," Dain said, tracing the route with a blunt finger. "The city fills with pilgrims; the guard doubles but the doubling is chaos, half of them strangers to each other, nobody sure whose order outranks whose. That's our fog. We come up through the crypts on the coronation eve, not the day—Corin keeps vigil alone in the underchapel the night before, and the chapel above empties for the rehearsal. One window. Maybe an hour."

"And if the window closes while we're below?"

"Then we're below," Dain said simply, "and we make our own weather."

Freya almost smiled. Almost. "You understand that if this fails, there's no warrant that saves you. Treason doesn't come with appeals."

"I spent twenty-two years learning that the law and justice wear the same uniform and aren't related by blood." He began rolling up the maps with careful hands. "Loyalty's not a thing you owe, carver. I thought it was. It's a thing you choose, daily, like bread. I'm choosing it now. Just—" he glanced at Wren, asleep at last against the wall, her breath deep and even, "—aimed differently."

Freya looked at him—at the hunter who had become something else in the space of a single night, or perhaps had always been something else and had only now been given permission to say so. Trust was a strong word. She did not have it yet. But she had something adjacent to it, something that grew in the same soil: the recognition of a person who had also built their whole life on a foundation and had also watched it crack, and who was, like her, standing in the wreckage deciding what to build next.

"Dawn's coming," she said. "We move at first light. You'll walk ahead—you know the roads, and no one questions a justiciar traveling with prisoners." A ghost of dry humor. "Congratulations, Ser Dain. You've arrested us."

He huffed something that was almost a laugh. "Best arrest I ever made."

But as the sky paled and they broke camp in the grey quiet, Freya caught him standing over Wren, watching the child sleep with an expression he thought no one saw—and she heard, faint and far off, from the finger-bones in her pouch, a whisper that was not the courier at all but something older, something from deeper in the bones, something that had been waiting to be heard:

*The underchapel feeds at moonrise. Come before the moon rises. Come before it wakes.*

The bones had never volunteered anything before.

The dead, it seemed, were afraid too.
