## 15. Beneath the Crown

The entrance to the catacombs was a lie told in stone.

Dain had called it the Weepers' Gate—a drainage arch in the palace's lowest foundation, half-drowned in runoff from the aqueducts, so crusted with lime that it looked like nothing more than the city's old grief leaking out of its walls. He knelt at its mouth with a shuttered lantern and worked the rusted grate with hands that did not shake, though Freya could hear his heart. She heard everyone's heart now, down here beneath the capital, where the dead were not voices but weather—a pressure, a tide, pressing against her skull from every direction like the sea against a dam.

She had thought Thornmere's silence was quiet. She had been wrong. Silence was only ever the shape of what you couldn't yet hear.

"Three hours until the changing of the night watch," Dain murmured. "After that, we're ghosts."

"We are not the ones who need to be careful of ghosts," Wren said.

Freya startled. The child almost never spoke in their own voice—only in the old tongue, only when the vessel thinned enough to let something else through. But this was Wren's voice, small and rough as a sparrow's, and it was aimed at Dain with an expression far too old for the face wearing it.

Dain looked at the child for a long moment. "No," he agreed quietly. "I suppose you aren't." He pulled the grate free. "After you, then. Both of you. And if I lead you into a trap—"

"You won't," Freya said. It was not forgiveness. It was arithmetic. A man who had falsified his own reports, who had stood in the fens and let them walk away because a dead girl's voice came out of a child's mouth—that man had already chosen, even if he hadn't admitted it to himself yet. "The dead would have told me."

"The dead," Dain said, "are the only witnesses in this city who can't be bought." He swung the lantern low. "That's why he buried them all down here."

* * *

The stairs went down farther than stairs should go.

Freya counted two hundred steps before she stopped counting, and still the dark kept taking them, the air thickening around them like wet wool. The walls changed as they descended—dressed granite giving way to older work, blocks fitted without mortar, and then to stone so ancient it wasn't masonry at all but living rock, carved by hands that had wanted shapes rather than rooms. Somewhere above them the coronation bells were being polished. Somewhere above them Prince Corin was rehearsing his oaths in a voice that made the courtiers' eyes slide sideways and their smiles stay too long. Down here there was only the drip of water and the sound of Freya's own breath and, beneath everything, the hum.

She had felt it since they entered the city: a note too low to be heard, felt instead in the teeth, in the marrow, in the soft floor of the belly. The Thorn. It was everywhere up there—in the grain of the bread, in the ledgers, in the way the guardsmen stood too straight and answered too quickly. But here, beneath it, the hum was different. Here it sounded almost like fear.

Because the dead were here. Hundreds of years of kings, and queens, and the children of kings, and the nameless who had built the tombs and been folded into the foundations without ceremony. The palace above had forgotten them. The Thorn above had never bothered to look down. But bones remembered, and bone was Freya's country, and the moment her bare fingers touched the first sarcophagus lid in the antechamber at the bottom of the stairs, the whole necropolis woke up like a field of bells in wind.

*Oh,* said the bones. *Oh, oh, at last, at last.*

She staggered. Dain caught her elbow; she shook him off gently and pressed both palms flat against the cold marble.

"I'm all right," she said, though her ears rang. "They're just—they've been waiting a long time. No one comes down here. No one listens."

"What do they say?" Wren asked, drifting close. In the lantern light the child's eyes had that doubled quality again, pupil within pupil, and Freya spoke carefully, aware that whatever she said might travel further than the room.

"That the last king who walked these halls alive was Aldric himself. That he came down on the night of his binding, alone, and put his hand where mine is now, and wept." Her throat tightened. "He asked the dead to forgive him. He said—" She listened harder, leaning into the cold. "He said, *I have given my voice to keep yours.* He meant ours. All of ours. Everyone in the valley. Everyone under the crown."

Dain had gone very still. "The histories say he sealed the Thorn willingly. That it was a sacrifice."

"The histories are written by men who weren't there." Freya lifted her hands. "Come. They want to show us the way. There is a road through the dead that the living forgot, and the thing upstairs has never once looked down."

* * *

The kings led her by touch.

It was not walking, exactly, and it was not hearing, exactly—it was the thing she had done her whole life in miniature, now vast: she laid her palm on stone and read the intention in it the way a blind woman reads a page. Sarcophagus to sarcophagus, effigy to effigy, down corridors where the effigies lay with crossed swords and open eyes, past alcoves where lesser bones had been stacked like cordwood in plague years, and always the dead murmured their directions in the wordless way of bones—not language but pull, a tug in her sternum, left here, down here, *not that door, carver, that door is watched.*

Twice they passed doors that were watched. She knew it because the hum gathered near them like flies, and once, passing a bronze-bound gate crusted with royal seals, Wren stopped dead and made a sound Freya had never heard the child make—a thin, involuntary whine—and every hair on Freya's arms rose.

"Not that way," Wren whispered, in the old tongue, and then, struggling, in their own: "It sleeps against that door. Like a dog. Like a dog at a door."

"A watchdog in the cellar," Dain breathed. "For the catacombs."

"For *him*," Freya said. "In case anyone ever tried to reach him." She looked back the way they'd come, at the miles of sleeping kings between them and the stairs. "No one has tried in twenty years. Why would they? Everyone believes the king rules. Everyone believes the king is well."

"Do you think he is?" Dain asked. It was not a challenge. His voice had gone quiet in the way of a man asking about a wound he suspected he'd caused.

Freya didn't answer. She didn't know how to answer. She put her hand on the next sarcophagus and let the dead pull her onward, and the dead were kind, and the dead were patient, and the dead did not tell her what waited at the end of the road because they knew she would feel it soon enough herself.

* * *

They found the chamber an hour before dawn, and Freya knew it before the lantern reached it, because the screaming started.

Not with her ears. With her gift—with the sense that lived in her palms and the roots of her teeth, the sense that had been her whole life. The chamber ahead was full of bone, more bone than any tomb they'd passed, and every piece of it was *shrieking*, a discord like a hundred bowed strings about to snap, and threaded through the shrieking was a single voice, human, ragged, worn down to a thread:

*Is someone there. Is someone there. Is someone there. Please. Is someone—*

Freya ran.

"Freya—" Dain hissed, but she was already through the doorway, lantern high, and the light caught it all at once and she wished, afterward, that it hadn't.

The chamber was round, a king's hall turned upside down and buried, its vaulted ceiling lost in darkness. Every inch of wall was niched, and every niche held bones—but not arranged, not honored. Piled. Heaped. Bones of every age, some brown with centuries, some white as chalk, and among them, unmistakably, newer things: a boot sole. A wedding ring fused to a finger-bone. A child's jaw. This was not a tomb. This was a larder. This was where the Thorn brought what it finished with, and the pile went up beyond the reach of the light.

And in the center of the floor, on a throne of black iron that had been dragged down here from somewhere above—she recognized the make, the winged armrests, from the coins in Dain's purse—sat a man.

He was not old. That was the first horror. The histories put Aldric's binding in his thirty-fifth year, and the man in the throne could not be much past fifty, but he wore his years like a coat three sizes too large. He was gaunt in the way of famine, skin stretched over a skull that seemed determined to announce itself, hair gone white not at the temples but everywhere, hanging lank past shoulders that had once filled coronation robes. His hands lay on the armrests and they were the worst thing: elegant hands, long-boned, a carver's hands or a musician's, and they trembled without pause, a fine constant shiver, like a man perpetually cold or perpetually afraid.

His eyes were open. They were fixed on the middle distance. When the lantern light touched his face, they did not move.

"No," Dain whispered behind her. "No. That's not—he reviews the guard. I've seen him. I've *knelt* to him—"

"You've knelt to the thing wearing him," Freya said. Her voice came out flat and strange. She was already moving forward, already kneeling, already reaching—and she stopped herself, hand hovering over the king's wrist, because she had learned what happened when she touched bone uninvited, and this close she could feel it: the hum, the Thorn's low note, coiled through this man like a worm through fruit. If she touched him, she would touch *it* too.

But the other voice—the thread-voice, the one screaming *is someone there*—was coming from inside him. From his bones. From the living skeleton beneath the ruined flesh, and living bone sang louder than dead bone ever could, and King Aldric's skeleton was singing a scream that had been going on, she understood with a nausea that buckled her, for twenty years.

"He's awake," she said. "Dain. Wren. He's *awake*. He's been awake the whole time."

"That's not possible," Dain said. "The histories—"

"Hang your histories." Fury rose in her like floodwater, hot and sudden, scalding away the pity and leaving something harder underneath. "Twenty years. Twenty years locked in his own body, watching his hands sign the orders, watching his mouth smile at the men who serve his prison, and no one—no one *heard* him." Her hand closed at last around the king's wrist, flesh and pulse and bone, and the world opened.

She fell into him the way she fell into relics, but there was no falling like this one. Dead bone gave her moments, echoes, last words worn smooth by time. Living bone gave her everything, all at once, uncompressed: a young man in a coronation robe feeling the crown settle on his brow and thinking, absurdly, *it's heavier than I imagined*; the same man, hours later, understanding what he had invited; the binding, the tearing, the moment his own voice was ripped out of his throat and something else moved in behind it like a tenant into a burned house; and then years, decades, a gray procession of them, each one the same—waking in a body that was not his, watching through eyes that were windows painted shut, screaming and screaming and learning that screaming changed nothing, and never, not once, not in twenty years, stopping.

*I held the valley,* he was saying, had always been saying, would always say, the words worn to a groove like water on stone. *I held the valley. Whatever it costs. I held the valley. Let it take me, only let me hold the valley. Is someone there. Is someone there. Please—*

"Yes," Freya said aloud, and her voice broke in the middle of it. "Yes. I'm here. I hear you. I'm a bone-carver out of Thornmere, and I hear you, Your Majesty, and I have come to let you out."

The trembling hands stilled.

Just for a heartbeat—just long enough that Freya knew it was him, knew it was not the puppet's stillness but a man's, a sudden gathering of will like the last strength of a drowning swimmer—the king's ruined face changed. His eyes came down from the middle distance and found hers, and they were blue, ordinary, human, and wet.

"*Carver*," his mouth shaped. No sound came out. Nothing had come out of his throat in twenty years. But she read it off his lips the way she read bone, and then his lips moved again, forming the rest, slow and deliberate, each word costing him something enormous:

*"The heir. Stop. The heir."*

And then the light went out of his eyes like a lamp shuttered, the hum surged up through his bones and into her hand, cold as grave-water, and Aldric's face smoothed into the pleasant, empty mask she had seen on the courtiers above, and his mouth opened, and what came out was warm and courteous and utterly wrong:

"You shouldn't have come down here, little carver."

Behind her, at the top of the stair, a door slammed, and the sound of boots began—many boots, descending in perfect, terrible unison.
