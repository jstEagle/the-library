## 16. The Thorn Unmasked

Dain Voss had served the crown for nineteen years, and in all that time he had learned one truth above all others: betrayal never came from strangers.

It came from faces you had learned to trust.

Sister Maurelle walked three paces ahead of him through the catacombs beneath the palace, her grey habit whispering against the ancient stone, her lantern held high and steady. She was the last of the Quiet Sisters to escape the raid on the convent—so she said. She had wept when she told it: the abbess dead, the scriptorium burned, the sisters scattered or worse. She had come to Dain because he was the only justiciar she believed still honest. She had kissed his signet ring and called him *the crown's clean hand*.

And he had believed her. Gods forgive him, he had believed her, because he wanted so badly for something in this rotting palace to be clean.

"Here," Sister Maurelle said, stopping at a junction where three tunnels met. The air was cold and mineral-sweet, tasting of deep water and older things. "The passage to the binding-stone branches left, past the ossuary of the first kings. The carver will need light—bring torches, not lanterns. The dark down there is *hungry*."

Behind them, Freya Hartwell shifted the sleeping child higher on her hip. Wren had not woken since they'd entered the tunnels, and Dain did not like the quality of that sleep—it was too deep, too still, the sleep of someone being spoken to from very far away. The bone-carver's face was pale as the relics she carried, but her eyes were steady. She looked at Sister Maurelle the way she looked at bones, Dain had noticed. Reading what was written there.

"You've been generous with your knowledge, Sister," Freya said quietly. "Almost too generous."

"I have buried forty years of secrets, child. I would gladly spend them all to see the Thorn unmade." Maurelle smiled, and it was a warm smile, a mother's smile, and Dain felt the last of his suspicion dissolve in it like salt in rain.

That was how good the lie was. That was how long it had been growing.

They went left, as she directed. And Dain Voss, who had survived nineteen years of court intrigue by trusting his sword arm and his sworn oath and nothing else, did not see the small silver bell she pressed into an alcove as they passed—a bell old enough that its metal had gone black, shaped like a rose with too many petals.

He did not hear it ring. But something else did.

* * *

The ossuary of the first kings was a cathedral of the dead. Skulls lined the walls in niches carved with names worn smooth by centuries; femurs were stacked like cordwood in patterns that might have been decoration or might have been scripture. The bones of men who had ruled before Aldric's line, before the valley of Thornmere was ever walled against magic, before anyone thought to fear the thing that wore the crown.

Freya stopped walking so suddenly that Dain nearly collided with her.

"They're singing," she whispered.

"What?"

"The kings." Her hand hovered over a niche without touching it, the way a man might warm himself at a fire he dared not embrace. "All of them. They've been waiting down here so long they've forgotten their own names, but they remember why they're here. They're singing a warning." She turned, and in the torchlight her eyes were terrible and bright. "Ser Dain. We are expected."

The words had barely left her mouth when the torches guttered—all of them at once, every flame in the vast chamber bending sideways as if toward a single point, like iron filings answering a magnet. The temperature dropped so fast that Dain's breath bloomed white. Somewhere far above them, through fathoms of stone, a bell began to toll. Not the palace bells. Something deeper. Something that rang in the marrow rather than the ear.

Wren's eyes opened.

The child did not wake—there was no drowsy blinking, no confusion. The eyes simply opened, and they were wrong. The irises had gone the color of wet bark, dark and fibrous, and when the child's mouth moved, the voice that came out was not a child's voice at all. It was many voices braided into one, layered like the rings of a tree, and each layer spoke a half-beat behind the others so that every word arrived twice, echoing itself.

*"You brought it to me,"* the thing wearing Wren said, looking at Dain with those bark-dark eyes. *"The little vessel. You hunted it across half a kingdom, faithful hound, and you have delivered it to my door."*

"Sister Maurelle," Dain breathed.

"*Was never a sister.* The voices rippled with something like amusement. *She was mine before your king drew breath. I plant my garden a century before I need the harvest. Did you think a convent could hide from me for four hundred years? I let them gather. I let them write their little books. A gardener does not fear the weeds until he chooses which ones to pull.*"

Freya had backed against the wall of skulls, one arm wrapped around Wren's body, trying to pull the child away from—what? From the voice inside it? From the whole chamber, which now seemed to lean inward, the stacked bones trembling faintly in their niches?

"Wren," she said, low and fierce, pressing her lips to the child's hair. "*Wren.* Come back. Come back to me."

The child's body shuddered. For one heartbeat the bark-dark eyes flickered, and beneath them, briefly, a child's terror surfaced like a drowning swimmer breaking water—and then the darkness closed over it again.

*"Hold it still,"* the Thorn said through Wren's mouth, to no one, to everyone. *"I will open it myself."*

And the walls of the ossuary began to move.

Not the bones—the bones hung trembling, silent witnesses. It was the *stone* that moved, bulging outward in slow waves, and through the bulging stone came roots. Black roots, glistening, thorned, thick as a man's arm where they emerged and tapering to needles where they reached, unfurling from the living rock as though the rock were soil and the palace above were only the flower of some vast buried plant. They came slowly. They came inevitably. They filled the tunnel mouths like fingers closing around a held breath.

"Run," Dain said. He drew his sword, and the sound of steel leaving the scabbard was absurdly small in all that vastness. "Carver—take the child and run. The left branch. Go."

"And you?"

"My oath says I protect the realm." He set his feet. Nineteen years of drill lived in his stance, in the angle of his blade, in the stillness of his breathing. "It doesn't say from what. Go, woman. *Now.*"

But the roots were faster than running. They erupted across the floor between Freya and the tunnel mouth, weaving themselves into a lattice, and beyond the lattice Dain saw shapes coming—not roots this time. Men. Palace guardsmen in the king's livery, dozens of them, their torchlight arriving before they did, and every one of them moving with that terrible smooth obedience he had seen spreading through the capital for weeks. Their eyes were calm. Their faces were calm. They walked into a chamber of writhing black thorns the way farmers walk into a field at harvest, and not one of them hesitated.

"Guardsmen!" Dain shouted, out of habit, out of hope. "In the king's name, stand!"

The nearest one turned to him. Young, Dain saw. Barely twenty, with a farmer's broad hands and a mole on his cheek, and his calm eyes held nothing at all—no malice, no fear, no self. Just service, hollowed out and refilled.

"The king commands," the young guardsman said pleasantly, "that you die."

Then the world became violence.

* * *

Later, Dain would not be able to assemble the fight into order. Memory gave it to him in fragments, the way a shattered window gives you sky.

A root whipping toward Freya and meeting his blade instead, the impact wrenching his shoulder half out of its socket, sap hissing where the steel bit flesh that was somehow also wood that was somehow also neither. The smell of it—sweet rot, crushed green things, blood gone stale in a wound. Freya smashing a femur from the wall-niches and driving it two-handed through a root's eye-like node, and the root screaming in a chorus of stolen voices, and Dain understanding in one lurching instant that the Thorn's body was made of the people it had eaten, that every inch of the black growth was *someone*, that his sword was cutting through the dead.

A guardsman's spear taking him through the meat of his thigh. His own counterstroke opening the man's throat, and the man dying grateful, *grateful*, mouthing thank-you as the emptiness poured out of him.

Wren, small and terrible in the center of it all, standing rigid while the roots curved around the child like a cage being built rib by rib, and Freya fighting toward that cage with a bone-knife in each fist and no expression on her face at all except absolute refusal.

And above it, threading through everything, the voice:

*"Do you see now, justiciar? Do you understand what you have served?"*

The throne room lay directly above them. Dain knew the palace's bones as well as his own; he knew that the antechamber to the throne room sat over this ossuary like a lid over a pot. And he understood, with the clarity that comes only in the worst moments of a life, that the roots were not attacking upward. They were *opening*. The whole ceiling was softening, stone going pliant as wax, and through the widening seams above he could see torchlight, marble, the gilded pillars of the antechamber—and figures standing at the seam's edge, looking down.

Courtiers. Dozens of them, in coronation finery, come early for tomorrow's ceremony. They stood along the crumbling rim and gazed down into the battle below with mild, pleasant interest, the way people watch a play.

And at the center of them, dressed in white and gold, stood Prince Corin.

Dain had seen the heir a hundred times. Cold, yes—everyone said so, the prince is cold, the prince is precise—but a man nonetheless, a young man with his father's jaw and his mother's coloring, a man who signed documents and reviewed troops and performed the thousand small duties of succession.

The thing standing at the rim of the broken floor wore Corin's body the way a glove is worn.

Its posture was wrong—too fluid, the spine moving in increments no spine should find comfortable. Its head tilted past the angle of a neck. Its eyes, when they caught the torchlight, reflected it back the color of wet bark, and its smile was wide and patient and utterly without a boy in it.

*"Ah,"* the prince-thing said, and its voice fell into the chamber like a stone into a well, and every root, every guardsman, every courtier went still to listen. Even the Thorn's voice inside Wren stilled, the way a stream stills before it joins a river. *"There he is. My faithful justiciar. Ser Dain Voss. Do you know, you are the only piece on this board that moved itself? Every other piece I placed. The witch-laws—I wrote them, a century ago, through a chancellor whose name your histories have lost. The valley of Thornmere—I built its walls myself, a pen for the one bloodline that could hear my prisoners. The binding of Aldric—"*

The smile widened.

*"Who do you imagine taught the old king's priests the ritual? A binding is not a cage, Ser Dain. A binding is a *womb*. I have been gestating in this crown for thirty years. Tomorrow, when the boy Corin takes the throne in fact as well as form, I am born. This kingdom has been my egg since before your grandfather sired your father. And you—"*

One long, wrong finger extended, pointing down at Dain across the smoking ruin of the chamber.

*"You I merely grew. In a barracks. From an orphan with a grievance and a strong arm. Loyalty is such efficient husbandry. I never once had to command you. I only had to give you something to be loyal *to*."*

Dain knelt in the wreckage of his faith, his thigh bleeding, his sword arm shaking, and he found—to his own surprise—that the words did not break him. They explained him. There was a difference. Everything he had ever been proud of, turned in another's hand into a tool; and yet the hand that held the sword did not change. The oath was corrupted. The man remained.

"No," Dain said. He got one foot under himself. Then the other. "You grew a weapon and told it it was a shield. Fine. But you made one mistake, thing."

The bark-dark eyes regarded him with something almost like curiosity.

"You taught me to hold doors."

Above them, the seam in the ceiling yawned wider, and through it Dain could see the throne room doors—great doors of oak and iron, standing open onto the antechamber, standing open onto the coronation, and beyond them the sound of ten thousand gathered voices chanting the heir's name. The roots were pouring upward now, flowing through the broken floor into the heart of the palace, and Freya was fighting toward Wren's cage with blood sheeting down her left arm, and no one—no one—was guarding the doors.

If the roots reached the throne room, it was finished. Not tomorrow's ritual, not the binding-stone, not any desperate plan. Finished forever.

Dain Voss looked at the doors. He looked at the guardsmen closing in, a dozen of them, then more, their calm faces lit by torchfire. He looked at Freya, and she looked back, and for one suspended moment the bone-carver and the justiciar understood each other completely, without a word, across a chamber full of thorns.

"Go up," he said. "Take the child. Reach the stone. I'll keep the door."

"You'll die," Freya said. It was not an argument. It was a grief, offered plainly, the way she offered everything.

"I've been dying since the day I took the oath." He spat blood and grinned at her, and it was the truest grin of his life. "This is just the paperwork."

Then the ceiling gave way entirely, and the antechamber opened above them like a wound, and Dain Voss climbed out of the ossuary of the first kings into the golden light of the coronation eve, turned to face the doors of the throne room, and planted his feet.

Behind him, he heard Freya scream Wren's name. Ahead of him, the first wave of the empty-eyed came on.

He raised his sword.

* * *

The door held for eleven minutes. He counted.

He counted because counting kept the pain at arm's length, and because his father had been a carpenter who said a job was only real if you could measure it, and because eleven minutes was enough—had to be enough—for a bone-carver and a stolen child to reach the heart of the dark.

They came at him in waves, the guardsmen, and the roots came with them, and the courtiers watched from the edges of the antechamber with their pleasant faces, sipping wine. Twice the roots dragged him off his feet and he killed his way back up. Once a spear went through his shoulder and pinned him to a pillar and he tore free of it with a sound he would be ashamed of later, if there was a later. The marble under him went slick and red. His breath came in whistles. His arms belonged to someone else.

At minute nine, the throne room doors boomed—from the inside, a sound like a great heart slamming shut—and the chanting beyond faltered, and rose, and changed key, into something that was not chanting anymore.

At minute ten, the roots stopped coming for him and started pouring past him, ignoring him entirely, hurling themselves at the sealed doors, and Dain understood that somewhere below, the thing in the crown had realized what he already knew:

It was going to be close.

At minute eleven, a blade took him through the belly, and Dain Voss sat down against the pillar, still holding his sword, still facing the doors, and kept counting anyway.

Because a job was only real if you could measure it.

And he meant to measure every second he had left.
