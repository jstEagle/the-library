## 17. What Loyalty Costs

The antechamber had been built to impress, and it did. Even now, with the candles guttering and the air thick as wool, Dain Voss could see the intention in it: pillars of white marble veined with silver, a ceiling painted with the ascension of the first king, floor tiles laid in a pattern that drew every eye toward the great doors of the throne room. Everything here pointed forward. Everything here said: *beyond this lies power.*

He had stood post in this room a dozen times over his years of service, straight-backed, gauntleted hands folded, watching ambassadors and petitioners file past toward their audience with the crown. He had believed then that he was guarding something holy.

Now he stood with his back against those doors, sword drawn, and understood that what lay beyond them was the opposite of holy—and that he was about to die keeping it company a few minutes longer.

"Go," he said again, because Freya Hartwell was still hesitating at the mouth of the servants' stair, Wren pressed against her side like a shadow clinging to its owner. "I said *go*, carver."

Her eyes were wild in the half-light, and there was blood on her sleeve—her own or someone else's, he couldn't tell, and didn't have time to ask. The convent survivor's betrayal had cost them their silence; the alarm bells had been ringing for a quarter hour, and somewhere below, boots were climbing.

"They'll kill you," she said.

"Yes," Dain agreed. It was strange how easily the word came now, how much lighter everything felt once it had been said aloud. "That is rather the point of me standing here."

* * *

He thought, absurdly, of his sister's hands.

Maren had died when they were children—fever, the winter the river froze—and in the years since, her face had blurred in his memory the way faces do, softening into an impression rather than an image. But her hands he remembered exactly: small and chapped, always cold, always reaching for his. In the fens, when the changeling child had opened its mouth and spoken in Maren's voice—a single word, *Dain*, nothing more—he had felt something crack in him that had never properly healed. He had told himself it was trickery. Sorcery. A witch's weapon aimed at the softest part of him.

It had been all of those things. And it had also been true. That was the cruelty of it. The Thorn did not lie; it only showed you truths you had buried, and let you do the rest yourself.

Since that night in the marshes, since he had falsified his first report in twenty years of unblemished service—*trail lost near the fen crossings, requesting reinforcement of the southern roads instead*—he had been living in a country without borders. Not traitor, not yet loyal. A man walking between laws, discovering that the ground beneath both was made of the same mud.

The ground beneath *this* room, though. This ground was marble, and it was about to be very well acquainted with blood.

* * *

They came up the grand stair first—the ones who wanted to be seen coming. Six guardsmen in royal livery, halberds grounded, marching in step. Dain knew three of them by name. Berrow, who had a wife in the lower city and sang badly at festival. The twins, Aldous and Tam, who had joined the same season he had. Young Pell, barely nineteen, whose mother wrote him letters he read aloud in the barracks because he couldn't read them himself.

Their eyes were wrong. That was the thing no one would ever believe unless they saw it: not red, not black, not glowing with any theatrical fire. Just wrong. Too steady. Too patient. Eyes like doors left open in an empty house so that something could see out.

"Hold," Dain said, using the voice he had used on battlefields and barricades, the voice that had once turned a rioting crowd in Millbrook with four words. "By authority of the justiciarate, hold where you are."

Berrow stopped. They all stopped, in perfect unison, like puppets whose strings had been plucked by the same finger. And then Berrow smiled—not cruelly, which would have been easier to bear, but gently, the way a man smiles at an old friend who has wandered somewhere he shouldn't be.

"Ser Voss," Berrow said. His voice was his own voice, and that was worst of all. "You look tired, ser. You should put down your sword. You should sit down. You've been carrying so much for so long."

"I know what's looking out of you," Dain said. "I've seen it in the prince. I've seen it in the courtiers. I even saw it in myself, once, I think—or felt it trying the lock." He rolled his shoulders, settled his grip. "It doesn't get past this door."

"You misunderstand." Berrow's smile deepened by a degree. "We are not trying to get past you. We are trying to *help* you. Put it down, Dain. All of it. The duty, the grief, the doubt. Haven't you carried enough? Wouldn't you like to stop?"

And gods help him, some starving corner of him wanted to say yes. He could feel it, the offer's shape, smooth as a stone worn by water: surrender as rest. Obedience as peace. Every order he'd ever followed had asked less of him than this one simple kindness.

Behind him, faint through the throne room doors, he heard Freya's voice begin to chant—low, rhythmic, in no language he knew, though the bones of his teeth seemed to recognize it.

"No," Dain said, and found that the word came from somewhere deeper than the wanting. Deeper than fear. "I'll keep my weight. It's mine to carry."

Berrow sighed, and the gentleness went out of the room like a candle pinched between wet fingers.

"So be it," said the thing wearing his friend. "Pell. Aldous. Tam. Berrow will watch."

* * *

They were good soldiers. That was the tragedy he had not anticipated—that corruption had not made them worse at their work, only emptied the hesitation out of it. Pell came in low and fast, the way Dain himself had taught him during winter drills, feinting high and cutting for the thigh. Dain caught the halberd shaft on his crossguard, turned it, drove his knee into the boy's stomach, and felt something in his chest tear open with grief even as his body did its work.

"I'm sorry," he told Pell, as the boy crumpled. "I'm sorry. Sleep, lad. When you wake, if you wake, it won't be you that did this."

Aldous and Tam fought like one creature, the way twins fight, covering each other's lines without a glance. Dain gave ground—had to give ground, they were three swords' worth against his one—and gave it deliberately, backward, toward the center of the antechamber, away from the doors at his back. Every step he surrendered bought distance between the fighting and the chanting. He counted the chant's cadence without meaning to; a soldier learns rhythms whether he wants to or not. It was steady. She was steady. Keep it steady, carver.

Tam's blade found him under the ribs on the left side, sliding between two plates of his cuirass with a sound like a boot in wet clay. Cold first, then heat, then a bright white agony that made the painted ceiling swim. Dain killed Tam for it—one clean thrust through the throat, quick, merciful, the kind of death he would have wanted for the man Tam had been—and Aldous screamed, a real scream, human and raw, and for one heartbeat the wrongness in his eyes flickered and Dain saw his brother-in-arms looking out of them, horrified, drowning.

"Aldous," Dain gasped. Blood filled his mouth; he spat it. "Fight it. Fight *it*. You're a Vossmarch man. Your grandmother fed you honey cakes off her own—"

The flicker closed. The thing behind Aldous's eyes pulled the shutters to, and he came on again, silent now, tireless, and Dain understood that mercy was no longer available to either of them.

* * *

He lost count of time. The antechamber became a series of instants: the scrape of boots on marble. The singing of steel. The candles guttering in the draft of the opening doors. Berrow standing apart, watching, offering, always offering—*put it down, Dain, put it down*—in that gentle voice, while Dain bled and bled and bled.

He took a cut across the forearm that numbed his sword hand to the wrist. He took a pommel strike to the temple that dropped him to one knee and left the world ringing like a struck bell. He got up. He did not remember deciding to get up; his body simply refused the floor, the way it had refused every floor for twenty years, because a justiciar of the crown did not kneel except to the crown, and the crown—

The crown was the enemy. There it was, then. The whole architecture of his life, every oath and rank and earned honor, resting on a thing that ate kings from the inside. He laughed, and the laugh came out bloody and surprised him with its lightness.

"What amuses you?" Berrow asked.

"Nothing," Dain said. "Everything. I spent my whole life learning to serve something worthy, and it turns out the worthy thing was never the crown at all." He raised his sword again, arm shaking, vision tunneling. "It was the *choosing*."

* * *

Through the doors, the chanting changed. Rose. Fractured into harmonies that no single throat could make—Freya's voice braided with others, with the dead, with a hundred dead, and beneath it all a sound like stone remembering it had once been molten. The binding-stone. She had reached it. He could feel it in his marrow before any of the corrupted guardsmen could, because the whole palace shuddered, a long groan through the foundations, and every candle in the antechamber flared blue-white and bent toward the throne room like grass in wind.

"*No,*" Berrow said, and for the first time the gentleness cracked, and underneath it Dain heard the other voice, the old one, the hungry one, vast and patient and suddenly afraid. "*No. Stop them. STOP THEM.*"

Every remaining guard moved at once. No more drill, no more discipline—just speed and numbers and the mindless urgency of a body defending its parasite. Dain planted himself in front of the doors and met them the only way left to him.

He stopped fencing. Fencing was for men with time. He waded in, took a spear-scratch across the cheek that laid it open to the bone, hooked Pell's ankle and broke the boy's balance, drove his shoulder into Aldous's sternum hard enough to crack something. He fought like the fens had taught him to fight, ugly and close, and every second he stole, he spent like coin, knowing the purse was emptying.

A blade went through his right thigh. He heard himself roar—heard, distantly, as if from another room—and headbutted the man who'd done it until the world went white.

When it cleared, he was on his knees. The doors were at his back. His sword was still in his hand, though he couldn't have said which hand, or why it hadn't fallen. Four guardsmen remained standing, circling, wary now, bleeding, and beyond them Berrow watched with his head tilted like a bird's.

"Dain," Berrow said softly. "It's over. Let it be over. You've done enough. You've always done enough. Rest now."

And oh, the offer was sweet. The floor was cold and kind. Somewhere beyond the doors, the chanting climbed toward a note that hurt to hear, and Dain Voss, knight-justiciar of the realm, twenty years in service to a lie, knelt in his own spreading blood and took inventory of what remained.

His name. His grief for Maren, which had turned out to be a compass after all. One choice, entirely his own, made outside every law he'd sworn to.

That was enough. That had always been enough; he simply hadn't known it.

"My report," he said, and smiled through the blood running freely down his chin, "will read: *held the line.*"

He surged upward and threw himself at all four of them at once.

* * *

Afterward—for there was an afterward, a few minutes of it, bought at a price no treasurer could reckon—Dain lay on the marble of the antechamber floor with his back against the throne room doors, feeling them tremble against his spine like the flank of a living animal. The guards were down. Some dead. Some merely broken, and of those, one or two stirred with human moans, human confusion, the wrongness burned out of them by whatever storm was breaking beyond the doors. Good, he thought. Live, lads. Wake up and be ashamed, and then wake up tomorrow and be better. That's all loyalty ever was.

Berrow was gone—fled, or called, or consumed. It didn't matter.

Dain looked up at the painted ceiling, at the first king ascending in gold and lapis toward a heaven that had never existed, and he found he was not afraid. The cold had come into him now, thorough and democratic, taking his feet and hands and then his middle, and with the cold came a strange clarity, as if all his life he had been looking at the world through glass and someone had finally wiped it clean.

*Maren,* he thought. *Your hands. I remember your hands.*

And—this was the miracle, if he was granted one, and perhaps dying men are granted small ones—he thought he felt them. Small and chapped and cold, reaching for his in the dark, the way they used to on the road home from market when the ditches flooded and the way was frightening. He held on. He was good at holding on. It was the one skill his whole life had perfected.

Beyond the doors, the chanting broke. A sound followed that was not a sound so much as an absence—the sudden, total quiet of a bell after it has been struck, of a scream after it has ended, of a hunger finally, finally satisfied into stillness. The doors at his back went warm. Then they went ordinary. Wood and iron, nothing more.

Dain smiled. His breath made a small fog in the candlelight, smaller, smaller.

*Held the line,* he thought, and could not tell whether he said it aloud, and no longer needed to.

Footsteps, hurrying, uneven—hers, and the child's quick patter beside them. Hands on his wound, on his face, a woman's voice saying words he was too far away now to gather into meaning. He hoped she would understand. He hoped she would tell no one, actually; let the record show a justiciar died at his post, serving the crown to the last, and let the truth live somewhere better than records—in the memory of a bone-carver and a child who owed him nothing and might, someday, choose to remember him anyway.

Chosen, not commanded. That was the whole of it. That had always been the whole of it.

The doors were warm against his back. His sister's hands were cold in his. Between the two, Dain Voss let go of the world the way a soldier stands down at last watch—slowly, gratefully, with the wall still standing behind him.

* * *

Freya knelt in the widening pool of blood and pressed her palms flat against the justiciar's chest, against the ruin of him, and felt—nothing. No whisper. No last word rising up through flesh and bone to meet her gift. Only silence, vast and absolute, pouring out of the throne room behind her like floodwater, filling the whole palace, filling the world.

The binding was done. The Thorn was torn loose. And the dead, everywhere, all at once, had gone quiet.

She did not know yet what that silence meant, or what it had cost, or what it would take from her before the night was over. She only knew that a man who had hunted her across half the kingdom had died facing the other way, and that the least she owed him was to finish what he had bought with every minute of his blood.

"Wren," she said hoarsely, rising. Her arms were shaking. The carving on her forearm burned like a brand, deeper now than skin, deeper than bone. "Stay close to me. Whatever happens next—we go together."

The child looked up at her with eyes that were, for the first time since the storm-lit night at her door, entirely, ordinarily human—and nodded.
