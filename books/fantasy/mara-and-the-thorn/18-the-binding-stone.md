## 18. The Binding-Stone

The catacombs ended where the mountain began to breathe.

Freya felt it before she saw it—a slow exhalation of warm air rising through the fissured rock, carrying the smell of loam and old iron and something sweeter beneath it, like fruit rotting in a locked room. The passage narrowed, then opened without warning into a hollow the size of a chapel, its ceiling lost in darkness above, its walls veined with pale stone that caught the light of Wren's small lantern and gave it back wrong: greenish, underwater, the color of light falling through leaves that had never known sun.

And there, at the center, stood the binding-stone.

It was not what she had imagined. She had pictured something grand—a monolith, an altar, a thing carved by kings. Instead it was a boulder no taller than her hip, round-shouldered and unremarkable, as if some glacier had dropped it here ten thousand years ago and forgotten it. But when Freya's gift reached toward it—her gift, the hearing that had never once failed her—the stone *screamed*.

Not aloud. Not in any voice. It screamed the way a tooth screams, the way a splintered bone screams in the marrow: a silent, total refusal, a wall of pain thrown up between herself and the thing she needed most. She staggered, caught herself against the rough wall, tasted copper.

"Freya." Wren's hand found hers. The child's fingers were cold. "The voices. What do they say?"

She could not answer at first. Behind them, far back down the passage, steel rang against steel—Dain, holding the antechamber door alone against men he had trained beside, men who had eaten at his table. Each clash came muffled through the rock, spaced further apart now, or perhaps only drowned beneath the stone's screaming.

She knelt. She pressed both palms flat against the boulder.

Beneath the scream, beneath the pain, there were words. There were always words; that was the whole curse of her life. But these were not the dead speaking. These were older. These were the words of the living king who had knelt here a generation ago and let his crown be bound, let his voice be torn out and hidden in a bloodline of children, let himself be hollowed rather than let the Thorn walk free in the world.

*I consent,* the stone said, in a voice like Aldric's must have been before everything was taken from him—young, resolute, terrified. *I consent. I consent.*

Over and over. For twenty-six years. A man's last free sentence, worn smooth as a river pebble by endless repetition.

"Oh," Freya said softly. "Oh, you poor man."

Wren crouched beside her. In the lantern light the child looked very small and very old at once, the way vessels always did—the abbess had told her that much, in the convent's burning library, before the abbess died with raiders' blades in the doorway and her skull-bone whispered the final truth into Freya's hands: *a willing vessel of living bone.* Not dead relics. Not carved memory. Living bone, freely given, freely carved.

The ritual lay open in her mind now, every step of it, the way a song lies open in the mind after you have heard it sung once. She knew the cuts. She knew the order. She knew the words in the language older than kingdoms, which Wren had been teaching her all this road long without either of them quite admitting it—that was why the child had come to *her*, she understood now, why the bones in her workshop had answered the ancient tongue. A bone-carver who heard the dead. The valley had not been hiding her from her fate. It had been keeping her for it.

"Freya." Wren's voice. Not the ancient language—Wren's own voice, thin and young and frightened, the voice of a child who had surfaced more and more often these last weeks, who had learned to laugh twice, who had learned to say *warm* and *bread* and, once, looking up at the stars over the fens, *pretty*. "I know what has to happen. I've always known. That's why I'm the last one. Everyone else ran from it, generation after generation, but my mother didn't run, and her mother didn't, because someone has to be the door."

"Wren—"

"I'm not afraid." The child said it too quickly, the way children say things they are afraid of. "The abbess said the vessel doesn't die, exactly. It just goes quiet. Like a house with no one in it. And I'd rather be a quiet house than let the Thorn eat the whole world. I'd rather—"

"No."

The word came out of Freya harder than she meant it, hard enough that Wren flinched. Somewhere behind them, steel sang again, and went silent, and did not sing again, and Freya's heart clenched around the silence like a fist around a knife.

She took Wren's face in her hands. Small face, mud-streaked, the jaw still soft with childhood. Eyes that held twenty-six years of exile and a language older than any kingdom and, underneath all of it, underneath everything, a child who wanted bread and warmth and stars.

"You listen to me," Freya said. "You have carried a king's voice since before you could walk. You have walked out of a valley that forgets, across the fens, through Ash Market, past justiciars and burned convents, and you have done it carrying something no adult could carry. You have given enough. Your whole line has given enough. Do you hear me? Enough is enough. It ends with me."

"But the ritual needs—"

"I know what it needs."

She drew her carver's knife.

It was a small thing, absurdly small for the moment it now stood inside of—a bone-carver's knife, six inches of honed steel with a handle worn to the shape of her grip, the tool she had used on ten thousand relics, on the finger-bones of weavers and the skull-bones of priests, the tool that had made her living and made her lonely and made her, in the end, the only person alive who could do what needed doing. Her father had forged the blade. Her mother had sewn the sheath. Every choice of her life had led here, to a chapel-sized hollow under a palace, holding a child's face in one hand and a carver's knife in the other.

"Freya, *no*—" Wren grabbed her wrist, and the child's grip was stronger than it should have been, vessel-strong, ancient-tongue strong. "It has to be willing! It has to be *given*, the abbess said, if you take it from me it won't—"

"I'm not taking anything." Freya smiled, and was surprised to find the smile steady. "I'm giving. Watch me give."

She turned her left arm over. The forearm, inner side, where the skin was thinnest over the bone—she knew this terrain better than any map, better than any road. She had carved this bone a hundred times in her mind, the way a sailor knows a coastline in the dark.

"The ritual needs living bone, willingly offered, carved with the unbinding while the heart still beats," she said. Her voice sounded strange to her, calm, almost gentle, a voice for explaining grain-matches to grieving widows. "It doesn't say whose. It never said whose. We all assumed, because the vessels were bred for it, because that was the old plan. But plans are just stories people tell before the real story starts."

"You'll lose it." Wren's eyes were streaming now. "Your hearing. The dead. Everything. You'll be—"

"Silent." Freya laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her, ringing off the veined stone. "Do you know what I would give for silence? Twenty years ago I would have cut this bargain in my sleep. I built my whole life out of quiet, Wren. I hid in a valley where magic can't cross the border because the dead were too loud everywhere else. I told myself it was contentment. It was fear wearing contentment's clothes." She touched the child's cheek one last time. "But this isn't running from noise. This is choosing it. There's a difference. The difference is everything."

From the passage behind them came footsteps—not the ring of steel now but boots, running, many, and a voice shouting words she couldn't parse, and then a sound she could: stone splitting, the antechamber door giving way at last. Dain had bought minutes. The minutes were spent.

She set the blade against her forearm.

The first cut was the worst—not the deepest, but the first, the one her body fought, every nerve shrieking *wrong, wrong, you are the carver, you do not carve the living*. She breathed the way she breathed over delicate work, the way her father had taught her: in through the nose, out slow, the hand does what the mind commands. Blood welled bright and shocking in the lantern light, running down over her wrist, dripping onto the binding-stone, and where each drop fell the stone's screaming changed pitch—lowered, softened, became something almost like attention.

She carved.

The unbinding was not letters. It was older than letters—it was lines, angles, the geometry of release, the same grammar she had seen ghosting beneath every relic she had ever made without ever understanding she was reading it. Cut by cut she laid it into her own radius, into the living bone beneath the living skin, and each line she finished blazed with cold fire, and each blazing line pulled something out of her—not blood, not flesh, but the *gift* itself, the hearing, twenty years of the dead's last words pouring out of her like water from a breached dam. Her father's voice. The priestess in the relic-map. The murdered informant whose bones had shown her Dain's mercy. The abbess, still whispering. Ten thousand strangers' final sentences, every secret she had ever kept, every grief she had ever carried for other people because they could not carry their own—

She gave it all into the stone.

And the stone drank.

Boots burst into the hollow. Freya did not look up. She heard Wren cry out, heard the child plant itself between her and the doorway like a small fierce gate, heard the ancient language pour out of Wren's mouth—not the king's voice now, not entirely, but Wren's own voice wrapped around the king's words, a child and a crown speaking together—and heard the running boots falter, stumble, stop, as though the syllables themselves were a wall.

More lines. Deeper now. The knife was singing through her, and she was singing back, and somewhere in the middle of it the pain stopped being pain and became simply work, the finest and most terrible work of her life, the masterpiece no one would ever see. Her vision whited at the edges. Her hands—her precious hands, a carver's hands, the only inheritance she had ever valued—shook, steadied, shook again.

*Almost,* said the stone, in the young king's voice, and then, wonderingly, in a hundred voices at once, in the voice of everyone it had ever held: *almost, almost, almost.*

The last line waited. The keystone cut, the one that crossed all the others, the one that could not be undone. Through the roaring in her ears she heard Wren sobbing her name, heard boots moving again, cautious now, circling, heard Dain's name shouted by a voice that wasn't Dain's, and understood with a distant, terrible clarity that Dain Voss was dead, that the door had cost him everything, that his last act had been to hold a line for a woman he had hunted across half a kingdom.

She thought: *the dead do not lie.* She thought: *let me join them honestly.*

She made the last cut.

* * *

The world tore open.

There was no other word for it. The binding-stone split along a seam no eye could find, and what came out was not light and not darkness but *voice*—a flood of it, a century of swallowed souls erupting from the stone and the crown and the hollowed man somewhere above them, and Freya, who had spent her whole life listening, was suddenly inside the listening, was the listening, was every ear in the world at once.

And the Thorn was there.

It had no shape it could keep in a mind. It tried shapes—crown, serpent, forest, hunger—and discarded them, and settled finally on the simplest thing: a mouth. A vast, patient mouth that had been eating, slowly, politely, for a hundred years, and had grown so accustomed to being fed that it had forgotten what resistance tasted like.

Freya remembered.

Every soul it had consumed rose with her—the kings it had worn, the courtiers it had puppeted, the villagers ordered burned, the justiciars hollowed mid-prayer, the children bred as vessels down generations of quiet despair. They were not gone. The Thorn had swallowed them, but swallowing is not erasing, and a bone-carver knows better than anyone that what is kept is not what is lost.

They had been waiting, all of them, for someone who could hear.

"*Now*," said the abbess, in her skull-bone voice. "*Now, carver. Call the roll.*"

And Freya Hartwell, standing in a broken hollow beneath a stolen palace with her own blood cooling on a binding-stone, opened her arms to the dead—and called their names.

The Thorn began, for the first time in a hundred years, to scream.

Above them, through the splitting rock, through the shuddering earth, the coronation bells of the capital began to ring—and then stopped, all together, mid-peal, as if a hand had closed around them.

Something was coming down the stairs. Something wearing a prince's body and a crown's hunger, and it had heard its meal singing.

Freya did not let go of the names.
