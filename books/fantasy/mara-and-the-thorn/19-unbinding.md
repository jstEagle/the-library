## 19. Unbinding

There is no floor.

Freya understands this the way one understands a thing in a dream—completely and without evidence. There is no floor, no ceiling, no walls, and yet she stands somewhere, in a place that has the texture of standing. Beneath her feet (does she have feet?) runs a vein of gold light, thin as a hair, and she knows without being told that it is her own forearm bone, seen from the inside. The ritual carved into it glows like a road through a dark country, and she is walking it.

*Blood remembers,* the abbess had said, pressing her own skull-bone into Freya's hands with the last of her strength. *Bone remembers better. Go down into it.*

So Freya goes down.

The dark around her is not empty. It is full—the way a seed is full, the way a held breath is full—and as she walks the golden thread, things begin to surface in it. Faces first. Then voices.

She hears them the way she has always heard the dead, but there is no carving between herself and them now, no polite membrane of bone to keep the worlds apart. They are simply *there*, all around her, pressing close as moths to a lantern only she can see.

A woman's voice, cracked with cold: *I planted barley the day they came over the hill.*

A man's voice, very young: *Mother said the river would take me if I swam past the willow. I swam past the willow.*

An old voice, dry as chaff: *I was ninety-one years and I wanted one more spring. One more. Is that greed?*

*Stop,* Freya says, or means to say. The word comes out of her like light through water, bending, going everywhere at once. *Who are you?*

And the dark answers—not with one voice but with ten thousand, layered like sediment, like rings in a tree:

***We are what it ate.***

* * *

The Thorn is not what she expected.

She had imagined—she realizes now, absurdly—a beast. Claws, thorns, some writhing vegetable horror out of a woodcutter's tale. What she finds instead, when the dark finally gathers itself before her, is a *hunger wearing a shape*, and the shape keeps changing because hunger has no shape of its own. For a moment it is a bramble wall, black and glistening. For a moment it is a crown, gold gone brown as old blood. For a moment it is a doorway, open onto nothing, and every soul she has ever carved a relic for is falling through it, forever, silently.

It speaks without speaking. The words arrive already inside her, the way cold arrives through a window.

***Little carver. Little listener. You brought your gift to me like a lamb to market.***

"I brought nothing," Freya says. Her voice sounds small here, and human, and she clings to that. Human is a thing the Thorn does not have. "I brought myself."

***Yourself.*** The hunger tastes the word. ***Yes. That is the vintage I prefer. A gift freely given. Do you know how long I have waited for someone to walk in willingly? The king fought me for nine days. Nine! And then he only went quiet, which is nearly as good, but not—quite—the same.***

"You didn't take him," Freya says, and the knowledge rises in her from somewhere below thought, from the bone-road itself. "He let you in."

The Thorn goes still. Even hunger can be surprised.

***He was dying. His city was burning. His heir was a babe in a wet-nurse's arms. He came to me and asked me to save his kingdom, and I asked only for a door, and kings are not careful about doors.***

"He asked you to save his kingdom," Freya repeats. "You ate him instead. You've been eating him for thirty years, one memory at a time, so that when his son puts on your crown there'll be nothing left of Aldric to object."

***Nothing wasted,*** the Thorn says, almost gently, and that gentleness is the most obscene thing Freya has ever heard. ***That is the difference between us, carver. Your dead go into the ground and rot and are *wasted*. Mine are kept. Cherished. I remember every one of them. Ask me anything. Ask me the name of the first soul I ever swallowed.***

And it tells her. It pours the name into her unasked—a girl's name, a name from before any kingdom she knows, spoken in a language older than Wren's—and Freya staggers under it, because the name comes wrapped in everything the girl had been: the smell of her mother's bread, the sound of a dog barking at geese, the last thing she ever saw, which was—

"Stop." Freya presses her hands to her ears, then remembers she may not have ears, and presses them anyway, because the gesture is hers, human, stubborn. "You don't cherish them. You *hoard* them. There's a difference, and I know it, because I've spent my whole life carrying the dead for people who loved them, and love lets go. Love says *rest*. You have never once said rest."

***They do not wish to rest,*** the Thorn says. ***Ask them.***

And the dark turns its attention on her, all of it, ten thousand souls and more, and Freya feels them looking at her the way the drowned must look at a boat passing overhead.

* * *

She asks them.

Not because the Thorn told her to. Because it is what she does. It is the only thing she has ever known how to do: sit with the dead and listen, even when listening hurts, even when the grief comes through the bone like winter through a wall.

She kneels on the golden thread of her own bone and opens herself completely, for the first time in her life, with nothing carved between her heart and theirs.

The flood takes her.

She is a soldier drowning in mud at a ford whose name has been forgotten by everyone except the Thorn. She is a queen hiding a knife in her sleeve and praying to gods the Thorn has also eaten. She is a child in a plague year counting the bells. She is a midwife, a thief, a monk who copied one beautiful letter at the top of a page and died before the second. She is thousands of lives ending, all of them ending *into* this thing, funneling down into its patient gullet, and she understands suddenly why the dead in the wide world had sounded so angry when she crossed the valley border—it was not anger at her. It was anger at being *held*. At being kept past all keeping. Grief, she thinks, drowning in theirs, is just love with nowhere to go, and the Thorn has built an empire out of love with nowhere to go.

***You see?*** the Thorn murmurs, savoring her drowning. ***All this feeling. All this *life*, still warm in my keeping. And you want me to spill it out into the dirt.***

"No," Freya says.

The flood pauses.

"I want you to give it back."

* * *

Here is the thing the abbess never told her, or perhaps never knew, or perhaps knew and could not say aloud because hope is a fragile cargo: a binding-stone does not destroy. Nothing destroys the Thorn; it is older than destruction, older than the idea of endings. A binding can only *redirect*. Take the hunger and point it somewhere else. Seal it behind a willing vessel and let the vessel's living bone be the lock.

But Freya is a bone-carver. And bone-carvers know something no priest and no king has ever needed to learn: that bone is not solid. Bone is *channels*. Bone is how the living speak to the dead and the dead to the living, a lattice of tiny roads, and a road that carries traffic one way can carry it the other.

The Thorn eats souls. Very well.

Souls, it turns out, can eat back.

"They hate you," Freya says, rising to her feet on the golden thread, and her voice does not sound small anymore. It sounds like the moment before a storm breaks, like the pause in a song where everyone breathes. "Every single one of them. They've hated you for centuries, and you've never heard it, because you don't listen. Listening was always my work. Not yours. Never yours."

***They are mine,*** the Thorn says, and for the first time there is something beneath the hunger's calm. Something quick. Something that might, in a smaller creature, be called fear. ***I hold them. I *keep* them—***

"Keeping isn't holding." She spreads her arms. "Holding is this."

And she listens.

Not passively, not the way she listened in her workshop over half-finished relics, politely, at one remove. She listens the way a dam breaks listens to the river. She reaches down into the sediment of souls and finds the oldest one—the girl with the dog and the geese, the first course of an endless meal—and she says to her, in the language older than kingdoms, the words Wren taught her without teaching her, the words that mean *you are seen, you are named, you may go home*.

The girl screams.

Not in pain. In *recognition*. Thirty centuries of captivity cracking open like ice on a river, and the scream is a bell, and every other soul in the dark hears it and answers.

* * *

What happens next, Freya will spend the rest of her life failing to describe.

The dead rise—not bodies, nothing so crude—but their *selves*, their gathered weight, all the love and fury and unfinished longing the Thorn has hoarded like a dragon's pile, and it turns on its keeper. The dark that had pressed against her like deep water now churns like a storm-sea, and through it move shapes: the soldier from the ford with his mud-caked standard raised high; the queen with her hidden knife finally drawn; the child counting bells, counting them louder and louder until the count becomes a war-drum; the monk holding up his single beautiful letter like a torch.

The Thorn shrieks, and the shriek unmakes the shape it wore. No more bramble, no more crown, no more doorway. Just hunger, naked, thrashing, trying to swallow a sea with a throat built for lambs.

***YOU CANNOT—*** it howls, in a hundred stolen voices at once, in the girl's voice, in the king's voice, in a voice Freya recognizes with a jolt as the abbess's—***THEY ARE MINE, THEY ARE KEPT, THEY ARE—***

"They're *free*," Freya says, "and they remember you."

And they fall upon it.

Not to destroy—nothing destroys it—but to *carry* it. That is the work Freya gives them, shouting it into the storm in every language the dead possess: *do not fight it, lift it, lift it together, carry it out the way mourners carry a coffin.* Ten thousand souls take hold of the hunger that held them, and they bear it up and away, out of the crown's iron grip, out of the hollowed king, out along the channels of Freya's own forearm bone, which blazes now like a comet's tail, and the pain of it is beyond anything, beyond the abbess's death, beyond the fens, beyond every hurt of her quiet hidden life, and she welcomes it, because pain means she is still a body, still a woman with two hands and a carver's knife waiting on a stone table in the world above.

The Thorn fights. Of course it fights. It tears at the dead as it goes, ripping loose fragments of them, screaming bargains—***CARVER, CARVER, LITTLE LISTENER, TAKE ME INSTEAD, I WILL GIVE YOU BACK YOUR VALLEY, YOUR QUIET, YOUR MOTHER'S VOICE, I KNOW HER VOICE, SHE IS IN ME, SHE HAS ALWAYS BEEN IN ME—***

And that is the cruelest blow of all, because Freya's mother *is* there. She feels her, a small warm presence near the edge of the storm, humming the tuneless song she used to hum while carding wool.

For one terrible instant Freya wavers, and the Thorn feels the wavering and lunges toward it like a fish toward bait.

Then her mother's presence does something the Thorn did not expect, something no hoarded soul has ever done in all its centuries of keeping.

Her mother lets go.

Deliberately. Lovingly. She hums one last bar of the tuneless song, presses something like a kiss against the edge of Freya's awareness, and releases herself into the storm, adding her small weight to the great lifting, helping carry the thing that kept her away from her daughter forever.

*Love lets go,* Freya thinks. *You never learned it. That's why you lose.*

* * *

They carry it out.

The last of it goes through her like a spear of absolute cold, and then—light. Not gentle light; the light of a wound opening into morning. The golden thread of her bone flares white, and Freya feels the ritual complete itself above her, in the world of stone tables and blood and Dain's fading breath and Wren's small terrified face, feels the binding seal along lines she carved with her own hand, and hears—faintly, from very far away, from the other side of everything—a sound she will remember until she dies:

A king drawing breath.

Not the wet, labored rattle of the hollowed man in the catacomb. A true breath, deep and astonished, the breath of a man surfacing after thirty years underwater, and threaded through it, faint as a bell across a valley, a voice that is only a voice, human and confused and free:

*"...where... where is my son..."*

Then the light takes everything.

* * *

Freya falls upward.

Or downward. Direction has lost its meaning, and meaning is losing its grip too. She is drifting in a warmth like the inside of a loaf, like a coat fresh off the fire, and around her the dead are departing—one by one, in twos and threes, in processions and dances—going wherever the dead go when they are finally permitted, each of them pausing, as they pass, to touch her. Not physically. With something better than touch. The soldier presses his forgotten ford's name into her memory so it will not be lost again. The queen leaves her the knife, symbolically, and a wink. The child counts bells all the way to the horizon, growing fainter, happier.

Last of all comes the girl with the dog and the geese, the first of the eaten, and she stops before Freya for a long moment.

*Carver,* she says, in the language older than kingdoms, and though Freya cannot translate it, she understands it perfectly. *You gave us back our names. When you forget yours—as everyone does, someday—we will remember it for you.*

Then she is gone, and the dark is empty, truly empty, for the first time in thirty centuries, and Freya falls through the emptiness toward a distant point of light that smells of stone dust and lamp oil and blood and rain, and she is so tired, so impossibly tired, and the light is saying her name—two voices saying it, one small and one rough and both very far away—

*"Freya. Freya, please—"*

"—Wren," she tries to say. "Is the king—"

But the light closes over her like water, and the last thing she hears, echoing up from a great depth, is the sound of a carver's knife clattering onto stone, and silence—vast, ordinary, human silence—settling over the world like snow.
