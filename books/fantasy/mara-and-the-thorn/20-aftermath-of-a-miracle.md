## 20. Aftermath of a Miracle

The silence came first.

Freya had spent her whole life listening to the dead—their murmurs in marrow and ivory, their last words caught like flies in amber—and so she knew, before her eyes opened, that something in the world had changed. The great chorus that had carried her across the kingdom, the thousand voices she had wielded like a blade against the Thorn, was gone. Not quieted. *Gone*, the way a river is gone when it reaches the sea.

She lay on the cold flagstones of the binding-chamber and listened to nothing, and nothing answered, and she wept without knowing whether it was grief or relief.

* * *

The throne room still smelled of smoke and sap.

The Thorn had not died cleanly. Nothing that old died cleanly. When Freya had torn it loose from crown and king—from the living wood of it, from the centuries of swallowed souls—it had shrieked through every stone of the palace, and where it passed it left black resin bleeding from the walls like the tree-sap of some enormous wounded thing. Servants were already at work with scrapers and buckets, though the older courtiers would not come near the antechamber. They crossed themselves with the wrong hand and spoke of the smell as if it were merely fire damage.

Freya sat on the steps below the empty throne, Wren asleep against her shoulder, and watched them work.

Her right arm was bandaged from wrist to elbow. Beneath the linen, the carver's marks ran along her forearm bone in lines no physician could see—grooves cut into living bone by a knife meant for dead things, filled now with something that was neither ink nor blood but simply *absence*. She could feel it when she moved: a smoothness, a hollowness, like a tooth lost from the back of her jaw. Her tongue kept going to the gap.

"Freya."

Dain's voice. What was left of Dain, which was to say: a man propped upright against a pillar with his sword across his knees because he refused to let anyone carry him until he had seen it finished. He had held the antechamber door alone for eleven minutes. She knew this because the bones of the guardsmen he had killed told her—but no. They did not tell her anything anymore. She knew it because she had counted the strikes through the stone, the way one counts thunder.

"You should be lying down," she said.

"So should you." He managed half a smile. It cost him. "Is it true? Is the king—"

"He's waking." She nodded toward the small chamber off the throne room where the physicians had taken Aldric. "Whole. Or near enough. He asked for wine an hour ago, which the abbess would have said is a very kingly sign of health."

"And he remembers?"

"No."

The word fell between them and neither picked it up.

Dain looked at his hands. There was blood under the nails that was not all his own. "I wrote my report," he said slowly. "Three drafts. In the first, I told the truth—that I found the witch Hartwell in the fens, that I let her go, that I followed her into the palace not as a justiciar but as—" He stopped. Started again. "In the third draft, I wrote that I tracked a cult of Thorn-worshippers to the capital and destroyed them. That the witch was never found. That she likely perished in the fighting."

"That's a lie."

"It's the first lie I've ever told the crown." He said it the way another man might say *I have taken vows*. "It felt smaller than I expected."

Freya studied him. Two weeks ago—had it only been two weeks?—this man had hunted her across three counties with a writ of arrest and a face like winter justice. Now he sat bleeding against a pillar in a ruined throne room, having chosen, in the last hour of his usefulness to the law, to unmake himself for her sake.

"The king will need honest men," she said.

"The king will need men who can lie beautifully and sleep afterward." Dain's smile flickered again. "I'm told that's what justiciars are for. I'm only lately qualified."

A physician hurried past with fresh linen and did not meet their eyes. Beyond the broken doors, the city bells had begun to ring—not the alarm pattern, but the slow joyous chaos of a capital that had felt its nightmare end without knowing its name. Somewhere out there, criers were inventing explanations. A fever, broken. A plot, foiled. The prince Corin struck down by assassins' poison before his coronation, gods rest him, long live King Aldric.

No one would ever say the word *Thorn* aloud again if they could help it. That was how such things died—not destroyed, but *forgotten forward*, buried under the ordinary noise of a people who wanted their breakfast and their king and their bells.

Freya found she did not mind. Let them forget. Forgetting was a mercy she had spent thirty years carving into other people's grief; it seemed only fair that the whole kingdom should receive it at once.

Wren stirred against her shoulder and made a small sound—a child's sound, wordless, ordinary—and Freya's chest tightened around it like a fist around something breakable.

* * *

King Aldric woke in increments, the way a man surfaces from deep water.

He knew his name. He knew his father's face, his mother's lullabies, the weight of a sword in a boy's hand, twenty years of a life laid out behind him like a road in morning light. He knew he had been ill. Everyone said so. The physicians said a wasting sickness; the priests said an affliction cleansed by the gods' mercy; the justiciar Voss, gray-faced and bandaged, said only, "It is over, Majesty," in a voice that closed the subject like a door.

What Aldric did not know was the last nine years.

There was a hole where they should have been. Not darkness—he remembered darkness well enough, remembered it as one remembers a childhood fever, shapeless and hot. But somewhere between his thirtieth year and this morning there should have been a coronation, a queen, councils, wars, the slow accretion of rule. Instead: nothing. A blank stretch of road. He woke in a body that ached like an old man's, in a palace that felt like a portrait of a house he'd once visited, and everyone around him wept with joy to see him awake, and none of them could tell him why.

"They say the sickness took my memory," he said to Ser Dain on the fourth day, standing at the window of the royal apartments while the city rang its bells below. "Physicians love that word. *Sickness.* It explains everything and requires nothing."

"Justiciars prefer it too," Dain said.

Aldric turned. He was thinner than any coin-portrait, hollowed at the cheeks, and his eyes had a quality Dain found hard to look at directly—not wrongness, not anymore, but the memory of wrongness, the way a healed wound remembers the knife. "You were there," the king said. "Whatever it was. You have the look of a man who has stood in a burning house and been told it was candle wax."

"Majesty—"

"I am not asking for treason, Ser Dain. I am asking for truth, which I'm told is different." Aldric's mouth curved, dry. "Nine years. My son is dead—I'm told my son is dead, and I cannot even grieve him properly, because the boy I remember is twelve years old and laughing at me over a practice sword, and the man who died plotting against me is a stranger wearing his face. Someone did that. Something did that. And whoever stopped it—" His voice caught, and he mastered it the way kings master things, visibly and at cost. "Whoever stopped it is owed a debt by this crown. I intend to pay it. Help me find them."

Dain looked at his king—at the hollowed face, the hungry eyes, the terrible clean innocence of a man returned from captivity with no captor to name—and felt the third draft of his report sitting in his breast pocket like a stone.

"There is nothing to find, Majesty," he said. "Whoever they were, they're gone."

* * *

Wren ate like a bird and slept like the dead, and both things made Freya want to laugh and weep in the same breath.

For eleven days the child had spoken nothing but the old tongue—that language older than kingdoms, the voice of the vessel-line, words that had poured out of Wren's mouth in cadences no child should command. Freya had learned to read its music if not its meaning: rising phrases for warning, a falling lilt for sorrow, one particular syllable-cluster that meant *the binding fails* and had haunted her dreams since the night it first sounded in her workshop.

Now Wren said *bread*. Said *no* and *mine* and *look*, pointed at pigeons in the courtyard and demanded their names, asked questions in the flat imperious way of children everywhere. The ancient tongue was gone as though it had never been. Whatever current had run through the bloodline for generations—through Wren's mother, and her mother, back and back into the dark toward whatever ancestor had first volunteered a child to hold a king's exiled voice—that current had reached the sea at last, and Wren stood on the dry bank of it, blinking, ordinary, seven years old and hungry.

"Do you remember?" Freya asked her, on the fifth evening, in the garden the convent sisters' friends among the palace servants had smuggled them into. Roses gone wild over a broken wall. Wren sat cross-legged in the grass, tearing a dandelion to pieces with great seriousness.

"Remember what?"

"The journey. The road. Me."

Wren considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Bits. You gave me the heel of the loaf in Ash Market and said the pigeons here were fat liars." A pause. The dandelion suffered further dismemberment. "There was a bad place. Under the ground. There was singing, and it wasn't nice singing, and you held my hand too hard."

"Yes."

"And then there was a lady made of all the voices, and she was angry, and then she wasn't." Wren shrugged, the vast shrug of childhood, which can accommodate miracles and lost loaves of bread in the same motion. "Then I was here and I was hungry. Was I someone else? Before?"

Freya's throat worked. "You were always you," she said, which was true, and also not the whole truth, and she was learning—as Dain had learned—that beautiful lies could be a kind of mercy. "Someone else was riding along with you. Keeping you safe. They've gone home now."

"Did they like the ride?"

"I think," Freya said, "they were very glad of the company."

Wren nodded, satisfied, and lay back in the grass to watch the swallows, and Freya sat beside her in the rose-smelling dusk and let herself feel, for one full minute, the thing she had been refusing to feel since the binding-stone: that it had worked. That the child lived. That the king breathed free and the valley beyond the mountains would keep its borders and the moon would turn and turn and bring nothing with it but weather.

One minute. Then she pressed her palm flat against the grass, grounding herself, and let the minute go.

Because underneath the victory, quiet as a splinter, was the other thing. The silence. The gap in her jaw where her gift had been. Thirty years of hearing the dead, and now—nothing. Bones were bones. Ivory was ivory. The skull of the murdered informant whose testimony had turned her heart toward mercy: silent. The abbess's relics, wrapped in linen in her pack: silent. Even now, even here, if she pressed her ear to the palace catacombs where the bones of forty kings lay ranked in stone, she would hear only her own blood, her own breath, the ordinary animal noise of being alive.

She had traded the voices of the dead for one child's tomorrow, and she would make the trade again with both hands, and she missed them already with an ache that had no bottom.

Both things were true. She was learning to carry both.

* * *

On the ninth day, Freya went to say goodbye to a king who would never know he owed her anything.

She used the garden, and a servant who owed the convent favors, and the plain gray dress of a widow from the provinces, because a woman matching the description on a two-month-old warrant had no business walking through a palace, warrant or no warrant. Dain met her at the rose wall. He walked with a stick now and would forever; the physicians had been eloquent about how much forever remained, and Dain had been eloquent back, and they had agreed to disagree.

"He's asking about you," Dain said. "Not by name. He describes you. Gray dress, traveler's boots, a scar on the left forearm. He says you come to him in the space before waking, and you're holding a knife, and your mouth is moving, and he can't hear you." Dain's mouth twisted. "The priests tell him it's a saint. The physicians tell him it's memory returning. I tell him nothing."

"What do you think it is?"

"I think," Dain said, "that some debts don't need collecting to be real. He'll spend his reign feeling it. A shape where a person should be. Every king needs a ghost; his just happens to be alive and leaving the country." He hesitated. "He's repealed the witch-laws. Signed it yesterday. No trial, no stake, no seizing of goods. Effective across the realm. He doesn't know why it matters to him. He said it felt like *unfinished business*."

Freya laughed—a short, surprised sound, the first in days. "Unfinished business."

"His words." Dain reached into his coat and held out a folded paper. "And this. Before you refuse it, know that refusing will only make me creative."

She unfolded it. A letter of credit, drawn on the royal treasury, bearer's name left blank, the sum written in a careful clerk's hand. Enough to live on for years. Enough to raise a child.

"Dain—"

"He owes it," Dain said simply. "Not to you. To the shape in the space before waking. Consider yourself its banker." His eyes went past her shoulder, to where Wren was attempting to negotiate with a gardener for custody of a watering can, and something in the justiciar's hard face went young and uncertain. "Will you go back? To the valley?"

"To Thornmere." The name tasted like cold spring water. "It's the only home either of us has. And it's safe now—or it will be, once the story dies down. Valleys are good at forgetting. It's practically a civic duty there."

"And you? Will you be all right?" He said it carefully, the way one asks a question one has no right to. "Without your—"

"Voices?" Freya smiled, and found the smile didn't crack her open the way she'd feared. "I spent thirty years learning to listen to the dead, Ser Dain. I imagine I'll manage the living."

They stood a moment longer in the wild roses while the bells rang noon, hunter and quarry, liar and witch, the two people in all the world who knew exactly what had happened in the throne room's antechamber and would never, either of them, say it aloud.

Then Freya took Wren's hand, and the watering-can negotiation was abandoned mid-treaty, and the two of them went out through the garden gate into the ordinary noise of the city, and the palace closed behind them like a book.

* * *

That night, in a rented room above a chandler's shop, with Wren asleep and the letter of credit hidden in the false bottom of her tool-case, Freya unpacked her knives and her remaining stock of carved relics and laid them out on the table the way she had done ten thousand evenings in Thornmere.

The abbess's skull-bone. The informant's knuckle. Three small memorial beads from strangers whose names she had once known and whose voices she had once carried like coins in a purse.

She picked up the abbess's relic—the brave, fierce, stubborn woman who had died holding a doorway shut with her body and her will—and turned it in the firelight, and waited, out of habit, for the whisper.

Nothing. Smooth bone, warm from her hand, smelling faintly of lamp oil. A thing. Only a thing.

"Thank you," Freya said anyway, aloud, to the empty room. "All of you. Thank you."

The silence received her words and kept them, the way the dead had once kept hers, and Freya Hartwell blew out the lamp and sat for a long time in the dark, listening—with the whole of her ordinary human ears—to her own heartbeat, and to Wren's slow breathing, and to the far-off bells of a city that would never know her name.

In the morning they would start north. Toward the mountains. Toward the valley that forgot.

Toward home—if home was still a thing a silent carver could find her way back to.
