## 22. A Debt Unnamed

The king dreamed of hands.

Not the hands of his physicians, cool and competent, pressing fingers to his wrist to count the pulse of a man who had, by all accounts, nearly died at his son's coronation. Not the hands of his confessor, folded in prayer. Not the hands of his son—Corin's hands, which he had held once in the sickroom and found strangely cold, like gloves left out in winter, though the boy had smiled and the physicians had pronounced him whole and the court had exhaled as one.

These hands were rough. Working hands. They smelled of bone dust and beeswax, and they moved with a certainty that made the king's chest ache in a way he could not name, the way a man aches for a home he has never seen.

In the dream, the hands were carving something. A small figure, perhaps. A bird. And there was a voice—no, not a voice. A silence shaped like a voice, the way a mold is the negative space of the thing cast in it. The hands worked, and the silence spoke, and the king understood every word and could remember none of them upon waking.

He woke, as he always did, in the grey hour before dawn, with his hand pressed flat against his sternum as though holding a door shut.

"Your Majesty." Ser Osric, his night attendant, hovered with the usual cup of willow-bark and honey. "The dreams again?"

"The dreams again." Aldric sat up and drank, and looked out the window at the capital waking below—smoke from a thousand hearths, the bells of the Dawnmarket, the river running silver. His capital. His kingdom. He had ruled it for thirty years and nearly lost it in a single turning of the moon, and the physicians spoke of a fever, and the histories would record a fever, and only he seemed to know that something had been *taken out* of him, something that had lived in him so long he had forgotten it was a tenant and not himself.

He remembered almost nothing. That was the cruelty of it. He remembered the coronation—the incense, the choir, the weight of the crown coming down—and then a gap like a missing tooth, and then waking in his own bed, weak as a kitten, with a scar on his forearm that no physician could explain: a thin line of raised white tissue, old, long-healed, running from wrist to elbow like a seam.

Like a carving. Like something had been written into him and then written out.

"Send for the Lord Chancellor," he said. "And bring me the witch-laws."

Osric's face did the small, careful thing it always did at those words. "Your Majesty, the codices are—"

"I know what they are, Osric. I wrote half of them." He had. He remembered that much, remembered the fear that had birthed them, the burnings, the registers, the justiciars in their grey cloaks. He remembered believing, with his whole heart, that he was protecting his people. "Bring them anyway. And bring a fire."

* * *

The repeal was not announced. That was the point of it.

Aldric had learned, in the weeks of his recovery, to move quietly through his own court. There were faces he no longer trusted—not for any crime he could name, but because he could not remember trusting them, and a king who cannot remember must trust the ones who make his hands feel steady. The Chancellor made his hands feel steady. The young clerk Maren, who had a stutter and a memory like a ledger, made his hands feel steady. Ser Osric, always.

So the witch-laws died the way old laws die: not with trumpets, but with ink. A clause here, amended. A register there, dissolved. The justiciar orders quietly folded into the ordinary constabulary, their grey cloaks struck from the uniform rolls. Maren drafted the instruments; Aldric signed them in the small hours, in a hand that shook only a little; and by spring, a child born with a gift in a far village was no longer, by law, a thing to be reported.

It was not enough. He knew it was not enough. Laws are the bones of a kingdom, and bones take a long time to heal, and the flesh of fear around them would take longer. But when the old women came to him—there were three now, in a convent in the eastern hills, who had written to him in a cipher he found he could somehow read, though he had no memory of learning it—and told him that the registers must be burned publicly, that the fear must be *seen* dying or it would only go underground and grow teeth, he agreed to that too.

"You are a different man, Your Majesty," the Abbess's letter had said—the new abbess, a young one, for the old one had died the previous autumn, and the letter had not said how. "Or perhaps you are the man you were always meant to be, before. Either way: the kingdom is lighter. Do not ask us how. Some debts are paid by those who cannot be repaid, and it is the debt, not the debtor, that must be honored."

He read that line a hundred times. *Some debts are paid by those who cannot be repaid.*

He did not know who they were. That was the hole in him, the missing tooth, the door he pressed his hand against each morning. Someone had paid for his life. Someone had stood, in whatever darkness he could not remember, between him and the thing that had worn him like a coat for a generation—and the physicians' fever story was a story, and the scar on his arm was not.

He had asked. He had asked Osric, who looked at the floor. He had asked the Chancellor, who smiled with all his teeth and said, "Your Majesty was ill. There is no one to thank. Providence, perhaps."

Providence did not leave scars shaped like carving.

* * *

In the fourth month, he found the bird.

It was in the lining of his old traveling cloak, which he had worn to the eastern provinces and sent after for mending. A seamstress found it, a hard lump in the hem, and brought it to him in a fold of linen with an apology: a flaw in the garment, Your Majesty, some old padding gone to seed.

It was not padding. It was bone, carved—smaller than his thumb, a wren with its head turned back over its shoulder, every feather suggested in three or four strokes of a knife, the whole thing warm as a held stone and light as a lie. It was the finest work he had ever seen, and he did not know why his eyes filled when he held it, and he did not know why he said, out loud, alone in his study, in a language that was dead before his grandfather's grandfather drew breath:

"Thank you."

The word for it rose in him unbidden. He wrote it down because he did not trust himself to remember it, and the clerk Maren, seeing it later, said, "That's not any tongue I know, Your Majesty," and Aldric said, "No. It's older than tongues," and neither of them asked how he knew.

That night he dreamed of the hands again, and this time the dream gave him one thing more: a valley. Green and shut in by hills, with a river the color of hammered tin, and a single crooked chimney smoking at the edge of a village where the light fell soft and the air felt thick, muffled, like a room with the door closed. He had never been there. He was certain he had never been there. And he woke with the name of it in his mouth the way you wake with the taste of a fruit you ate in a dream.

*Thornmere.*

* * *

The letter came to Thornmere in the first week of the plum blossom, carried by a courier in royal livery who looked deeply unhappy to be so far from cobbles.

Freya watched him ride into the village from her workshop window, saw the seal on the packet he carried, and felt the old reflexes rise in her like water into a well: *hide, be small, be no one.* But the reflexes were slower now, and the village was different now, and when the courier stood in her doorway and asked for "the carver Hartwell," three neighbors were close enough to hear, and none of them looked away from her. Old Marta even nodded, once, the way you nod at something settled.

The witch-laws were dead. Everyone knew. The news had come up the valley in spring with the seed carts, and with it something stranger: stories out of the capital. A king healed of a long sickness. Grey cloaks burned in the Dawnmarket square by the king's own order. A register of names, decades of names, read aloud in the open air and then given to the flames, and the families of the named weeping in the crowd, not in grief.

Freya had heard the stories and said nothing, and carved, and slept, and did not dream. That was the strangest part, still, after a year. She did not dream. She had not dreamed since the throne room, since the stone, since the moment when every voice she had ever heard had spoken through her at once and then—

Silence.

She had thought, in the first weeks, that she would die of it. That silence would hollow her the way it had hollowed the king. The dead had been her weather, her music, her company for forty years; she had complained of them, resented them, hidden them, and she had never once imagined the world without them. Bones were bones now. She had tested it a hundred times in the first months, holding relics to her ear like shells, waiting for the sea of voices, and hearing only the blood in her own head, her own small human pulse, hers alone.

And then, somewhere in the winter, she had stopped testing. And then, somewhere after that, she had realized she was humming at her work. And then, one grey morning, Wren had come into the workshop with an armful of kindling and said, "You're smiling at that femur, Freya," and she had laughed—a real laugh, startling them both—and understood that she was not hollowed. She was *emptied*, the way a cup is emptied. There was room in her now for things that had never fit.

The courier cleared his throat. "From His Majesty, the King," he said, and held out the packet.

"Give it here, then." She took it. It was heavier than a letter ought to be. She could feel, through the paper, the shape of something small and hard.

"The King asks—" The courier faltered, clearly working from memorized instructions he did not understand. "The King asks that it be read. He asks—forgive me, mistress, these are his words—he asks that you read it, and that if you do not wish to answer, you need not, and that he will ask nothing further either way, and that—" the courier's ears went red—"that he is sorry he cannot say more plainly what he means."

"Does he," said Freya.

"He also said to tell you—" the courier squinted at a scrap of paper—"'the valley that forgets has not forgotten.' Those were his exact words, mistress. I don't know what they mean."

"No," said Freya. "Neither does he."

The courier blinked. She almost—almost—told him. It rose in her, the whole of it: the child at the door in the storm, the bones answering in a dead tongue, the priestess's skull whispering of crowns and thorns, the fens, the convent, the abbess's skull warm in her hands, the catacombs, the man who held the door, the stone, the knife, her own forearm opened like a manuscript. The whole impossible year, pressing against her teeth.

She let it sink back down. Some debts, the abbess had written once, are paid by those who cannot be repaid. The king owed a debt he could not name, to a carver he could not remember, and that was not a tragedy. That was the *gift* of it. Let him owe it to no one. Let him owe it to everyone. A king who walked through the world owing an unnamed debt to an unnamed someone would spend his life paying it forward, and there were worse engines for a kingdom than that.

"Tell His Majesty," she said, "that the letter will be received with thanks."

"And read?"

"That," said Freya, "is between him and the fire."

* * *

She did not open it for three days.

It sat on her workbench, in the good light by the window, while she carved a shepherd's dog for the Aldous family—their old bitch had died in her sleep, and the children had asked, shyly, the way people now asked, whether the carver might make something to remember her by. There was no magic in it anymore. There did not need to be. The children watched her work with the same awe they would have watched any fine craft, and when she gave them the finished dog—smaller than a fist, every curl of its coat suggested in three or four strokes of the knife—the youngest girl held it to her ear out of some instinct she could not have named, and Freya felt something turn over gently in her chest, and said nothing, and smiled.

The letter sat. The plum blossoms came down like slow snow. Wren—taller now, voice deepening, still given to falling asleep in the workshop chair with a carving knife loose in their hand, which Freya had given up scolding about—looked at the packet and looked at Freya and said, with the terrible directness of the young, "Are you going to burn it?"

"Probably."

"You always burn letters from important people."

"I burn everything from important people. It's a policy. It's kept me alive."

"You're not hiding anymore, though." Wren turned the wren—the little bone bird, which lived on the mantel now, though Wren did not know, would never know, that it had once ridden in a king's cloak, that it had been carved by hands that were now Freya's hands, in a life that was now Freya's life, the whole loop of it closed and silent. "That's what you said. Last autumn. You said hiding was over."

Freya looked at the child—no, not a child anymore, not quite—and could not argue. It was true. She walked to market with her face up now. She had signed her name, her full name, on the village register last spring, in front of everyone, and old Marta had wept, and Freya had pretended not to notice.

But the letter was different. The letter was a door, and doors swing both ways.

* * *

She burned it on the fourth evening, in the workshop hearth, alone.

Or—almost.

She broke the seal first. That was the almost. She told herself it was practical: the packet was heavy, there was an object inside, and one ought to know what one was burning. She told herself a great many practical things while she slid the contents out into her palm, and none of them were true, and she knew it, and did it anyway.

There was a letter, folded twice, sealed again with a plain wafer. There was a document of some length, covered in chancery hand, which she did not read beyond the first line—*an instrument of pension and endowment, in perpetuity, to the bearer*—and which she set aside for the fire with a certain grim satisfaction. And there was a scrap of paper, small, torn from a working notebook, on which was written a single word in a king's unsteady hand, in a language dead before his grandfather's grandfather drew breath.

The word for *thank you*.

And beneath it, in the same hand, smaller, as if added later, hesitantly, in the common tongue:

*I do not know your name. I know your hands.*

Freya sat for a long time with that. The fire ticked. Outside, the valley did what the valley had always done: the river ran, the plum petals fell, the light went gold and then blue and then dark, and no magic crossed the borders, and none needed to. The dead were silent. The living were enough.

*I know your hands.*

He had held them. She remembered that much—remembered it from the other side of the silence, from the fragment of herself that had gone into the stone and come back changed. He had gripped her hands in the dark beneath the crown, in the last moment before the unbinding took his memory, and she had felt him trying to hold on to something, and she had said—the words came back to her now with the smell of the catacombs, the cold, the distant thunder of the Thorn—

She had said: *Forget me. Remember the debt.*

And he had. He was. Somewhere in a palace a hundred miles north, a man who could not remember her was repealing laws and burning registers and sending pensions to forgotten valleys, and every one of those acts was her name, spelled out in a language only the two of them had ever spoken.

She put the scrap of paper in the fire.

Then she took it out again.

The edge was singed; the words were not. She looked at it a long moment, the king's unsteady hand, *I know your hands*, and then she folded it small, very small, and did not put it in the fire after all. Instead she went to the mantel, and took down the little bone wren, and opened the seam of her oldest cloak—the grey one, the traveling one, the one she had sworn she would never wear again—and sewed the scrap into the hem, beside nothing, beside the space where a bird had once been hidden by hands she could not remember either.

The debt went both ways. That was the thing she had not understood until now. He owed her a debt he could not name; she owed him a silence she could not break. And between the two of them, unspoken, unnamed, unpaid in any coin that either crown or conscience could mint, something was being kept alive that had no better name than *justice*—the kind that is done in the dark, by people who will never be thanked, and endures.

She banked the fire. She burned the letter and the pension both, and watched the chancery hand curl and blacken, and felt no regret and only a little laughter at the image of some royal clerk, years hence, discovering that the pension had never been claimed, and the carver of Thornmere had vanished from the rolls as completely as if the valley had forgotten her.

Let them wonder. Wonder was a door left open. Wonder was the open door.

* * *

In the capital, on the same evening, King Aldric stood at his window with the little bone bird in his palm, and felt—without knowing why—the certainty arrive in him whole, the way certain dreams arrive: *She read it. She burned it. She kept one piece.*

He could not have said how he knew. He could not have said who *she* was. But his hand closed gently around the carved wren, and he found he was smiling, and the ache beneath his sternum—the door he had pressed his hand against for a year—had, for the first time, the feeling not of something shut, but of something *latched*. Waiting. Patient.

"Osric," he said, without turning. "In the spring, I should like to travel. South. There is a valley I have never seen."

"The one in your dream, Your Majesty?"

"The one in my dream," said the king, "and in my hands."

Far away, in a valley that forgot nothing after all, a carver banked her fire, and slept without dreams, and was—by every measure that mattered—entirely at peace. But on the workbench, in the good light, she had already begun a new carving: a small figure, not yet shaped, that her knife seemed to know the way to without her. A king, perhaps. Or a door, opening.

She would decide by morning. There was time. There was, at last, nothing but time.
