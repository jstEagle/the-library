## 1. The God Who Stopped Answering

The rain in Vaelmoor fell the way it always did: patiently, as if it had all the time in the world and intended to use it.

Emrys Thackeray crouched beneath a leaning barn roof at the edge of Hollowmere village and watched his target argue with a goat. The goat was winning. It always did, according to the farmer who'd hired him — a round, apologetic man named Doss who had paid in silver and apologies in equal measure.

"That's him," Doss had said that morning, wringing his cap. "Corren Vail. Stole my deed, burned my south field, threatened my daughters. He's got a knife and no sense. I'd do it myself but—" he'd gestured vaguely at his own softness, "—well."

Emrys had taken the coin. That was the whole of it. A contract was a contract, and a mercenary of the Silent Order did not ask questions after the seal was pressed. He'd been doing this for nineteen years, since he was fourteen and a wandering priest of the Unspoken had laid two fingers on his brow and told him, *You have a listening soul, boy. The god without a name will lend you its strength, so long as you keep your own counsel and your contracts clean.*

The blessing had been real once. He remembered it the way you remember a childhood summer — warm, specific, gone. In his twenties it had come when he called: a stillness in the blood, a steadiness of hand, moments where the world slowed like honey off a spoon. By thirty-five it came rarely. By forty — last winter — it had been a rumor. A habit of faith rather than a fact of it.

He was forty-three now, and he knelt in the rain watching a thief lose an argument with livestock, and he told himself the old lie one more time: *the silence is temporary. The god is testing me. Keep the oath and the ear will open again.*

The goat headbutted Corren Vail into a water trough. Emrys decided that was probably enough comedy for one evening.

* * *

Corren ran, which saved Emrys the trouble of a formal challenge. He cut across the wet pasture, boots finding the firm ground out of long habit, and caught the man at the stone wall behind the mill where the village's lanterns gave out and the dark took over honestly.

"Corren Vail," Emrys said. "Doss wants his deed back."

Vail spun, knife out, breathing hard. He was younger than Emrys expected — twenty-five, maybe, with a scraggly attempt at a beard and eyes too frightened to be truly dangerous. "You're him. The Thackeray man." He said it like a name from a story mothers told badly-behaved children. "They say you never miss. They say the Quiet God walks with you."

"They say a lot of things," Emrys said. "Deed. Now."

Vail threw the knife instead. Of course he did. Emrys had seen ten thousand men throw knives, and every one of them believed they were the exception, and none of them were. His body knew what to do before thought arrived: shift left, let the blade pass through the space his ribs had politely vacated, close the distance, take the wrist, put the man down into the mud with the minimum violence required by professional standards.

Except.

The world did not slow. There was no honey-warm stillness, no borrowed grace. Emrys shifted left a half-heartbeat late and the knife opened a line along his forearm — shallow, insulting, the kind of cut a man got from falling into brambles. He closed the distance on ordinary legs, at ordinary speed, and when he took Vail's wrist the man nearly twisted free, because Emrys's grip was just a grip, flesh and tendon and forty-three years of wear, nothing more.

They went down together into the mud. Vail fought like a cornered animal, which is to say stupidly and with total commitment, and Emrys got his knee on the man's chest and his dagger drawn and the point resting in the hollow of Vail's throat, and then—

And then he waited.

This was the moment. Every contract ended here, in this held breath, and every time, the blessing came or it didn't, and either way the work proceeded. The Silent Order's way was clean: one stroke, quick, the god's mercy delivered through mortal hands. Emrys had done it so many times the motion lived in his wrists like a song learned young.

Vail stared up at him, rain pooling in his eye sockets, and whispered, "Please."

Emrys waited for the stillness. For the calm that made the next part bearable — not because it excused the killing, but because it carried him through it, the way a ferry carries a man across a river he cannot swim. Nineteen years, and the ferry had always come. Late, sometimes. Rusty, lately. But it came.

It did not come.

There was only the rain, and the mud soaking through his trousers, and the frantic pulse under his dagger's point, and his own heartbeat loud and mortal and alone in the dark. No god. No stillness. Just a man kneeling on another man's chest with a knife, about to do murder with nothing holy anywhere in sight.

"Please," Vail said again. "I only took the deed because Doss's brother holds my family's debt. I was going to trade it back. I swear it on—" he laughed, wet and broken, "—on whatever's listening."

*Whatever's listening.* Emrys almost laughed himself. Nothing was listening. He could testify to that personally.

A contract was a contract. The code was the code. Break it once and you were no longer a blade of the Order; you were just a man with weapons and opinions, and men with weapons and opinions died badly in ditches all over Vaelmoor. He knew the arithmetic. He had built his entire life on that arithmetic, stone by careful stone, and the stones had held even through the years of quiet, because the quiet was only ever a test, and tests end.

But his arm would not move.

It wasn't weakness. He was strong enough; the dagger trembled only the way any dagger trembles. It was something worse than weakness. It was the sudden, vertiginous understanding that the killing had never been *his*. That for nineteen years some other thing had lent him its certainty, and now the loan was called due, and what remained underneath was a tired man in the rain who did not particularly want to kill a frightened debtor over a piece of paper.

"Get up," Emrys said.

Vail blinked rain out of his eyes. "What?"

"Get up. Give me the deed — you have it on you, don't insult us both — and walk. North road, tonight, and don't stop until Vaelmoor's behind you. If Doss asks, I'll tell him you're dead. I'm apparently good at stories."

"You're letting me go." Vail said it the way men say *the sun rose in the west.*

"I'm sparing you," Emrys corrected, hauling him up by the collar. "There's a difference, and it matters to exactly one person, and that person is me. Deed."

Vail fumbled a leather tube from inside his coat. Emrys took it, sheathed his dagger, and stood there a moment longer than necessary, feeling the shape of what he'd just done settle onto his shoulders. Somewhere in the dark, in whatever place nameless gods go when they stop answering, an account had just closed. He felt lighter and unmoored at once, like a boat cutting its own anchor rope in a storm because the anchor had started to feel like drowning.

"Go," he said.

Vail went. Emrys watched until the dark swallowed him, then pressed his bleeding forearm absently and began the long walk back toward the village inn, where a bed, a bad meal, and a great deal of thinking waited.

* * *

The inn at Hollowmere was called the Drowned Lamb, which Emrys had always felt was less a name than a warning. He sat in the corner with a bowl of barley stew and a cup of thin cider and unwrapped the bandage Doss's wife had insisted on applying, tutting over the cut like it was a personal failing of the weather.

"The deed?" Doss asked, hovering.

"Recovered. Vail won't trouble you again."

Doss's face did something complicated — relief and grief wrestling for the same ground — and settled on relief. "You're a good man, Thackeray. They say the Quiet God—"

"Don't," Emrys said, more sharply than he meant. He softened it with a shrug. "Just a job."

He ate. Around him the inn did its evening business: a peddler complaining about tolls, two farmwives arguing amiably about whether the mist on the northern ridges was thicker this year or merely older, a child under a table gnawing a heel of bread and staring at Emrys's sword with frank admiration. Ordinary life, humming along, indifferent to the fact that one man in the corner had just lost the only conversation he'd been having with the divine for four decades.

That was the thing about the silence, he thought, turning his cup. You could live around it. You could tell yourself stories — *testing, dormant, the god is patient and so am I* — and the stories held up walls against the truth. But tonight he had stood in the rain with a knife at a man's throat and heard, clearly, for the first time, the actual sound of the silence. It wasn't absence. Absence would have been kinder. It was a door, shut from the other side, and locked, and — he understood this with a cold settling in his chest — it had been locked for years. Perhaps decades. Everything since his thirties, everything he'd called *dormant*, had been him knocking politely on a door that no longer had anyone behind it.

He had kept the oath. Kept it perfectly, miserably, through nineteen years of contracts and mud and blood. And the god without a name had stopped listening somewhere along the way without bothering to say goodbye.

"So what am I now?" he murmured to his cider.

"A man who spares thieves," said the child under the table, around a mouthful of bread.

Emrys looked down. The child looked back, entirely unbothered at having been caught eavesdropping. "A man who breaks contracts," Emrys said.

"Sounds better than the other thing," the child said, and crawled away toward her mother.

Emrys sat with that for a while. Outside, the rain went on falling with its bottomless patience, and the mist on the northern ridges thickened the way it had all year, older and stranger than mist had any right to be, pressing down the valley walls like a lid being fitted carefully into place.

He paid for his room. On the stairs, his forearm throbbed, and he noticed — for the first time in nineteen years — that the old blessing-scar on his palm, the pale mark the priest's fingers had left, had faded to almost nothing. Skin. Just skin.

That night he dreamed of a book. He didn't know why. He had never cared much for books. But in the dream there was a library under a ruined city, and a lamp burning in it, and someone down there in the dark turning pages, looking for a name.

His name.

He woke before dawn with the word *Cindralle* in his mouth — a word he had never heard, in a language he didn't speak, tasting of dust and lamplight — and lay staring at the ceiling while the valley's strange mist pressed against the window, waiting for him with what felt, horribly, like recognition.
