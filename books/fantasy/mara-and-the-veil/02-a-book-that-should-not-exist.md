## 2. A Book That Should Not Exist

The trouble with being the last archivist of a country that did not exist was that nobody ever came to visit.

Mara Quill had made her peace with this. Mostly. On good days she told herself it was a sacred solitude, the quiet dignity of the keeper of the last true record of Cindralle. On bad days — which were most days, if she was honest, and honesty was practically a professional requirement — she talked to the shelves.

"Don't look at me like that," she told the third shelf of the western alcove, where the tax ledgers lived. Tax ledgers judged. She could feel it. *Four hundred years of civilization,* they seemed to say, *and you've eaten nothing today but dried plums.*

"I'll eat when I find something," she said, which was what she always said, and which was why she had eaten nothing but dried plums for eleven days.

She descended another rung of the ladder into the deep stacks, lantern held high, and the dark beneath Cindralle folded around her like an old coat. Above her, the ruined capital went about its business of being ruined: broken spires, streets gone soft with moss, the twilight that never quite became morning because the sky over Cindralle had forgotten how. Below her, in the archive her grandmother's grandmother's grandmother had carved out of the bedrock when the Erasure began, everything the country had ever been sat waiting to be remembered by exactly one person.

Mara Quill. Last of her line. Keeper of the book. Sworn by blood-oath before she was old enough to understand what she was swearing to, which she privately considered a legal flaw in the whole arrangement.

"Catalogue shift," she announced to no one, because ritual mattered, even invented ritual. "Section Nine through Twelve. If anyone has opinions, speak now."

The archive said nothing, as it had said nothing for one hundred years, four months, and — she checked, because she kept count, of course she kept count — six days.

That was when the shelf sneezed.

Not literally. But there was a sound from behind Section Eleven, a soft *whump* of displaced air, like a book settling after a century of deciding not to. Mara froze on the ladder. Books settled all the time; the archive breathed the way old buildings do. But this sound had a quality she had never heard in it before, and she knew every sound this place made the way other people knew their own heartbeat.

It sounded like a page turning.

She climbed down so fast she nearly put the lantern out, and stood at the mouth of Section Eleven with her heart doing something undignified against her ribs. The aisle ran back thirty feet, lined with the genealogies — the saddest section, the one she avoided, full of families recorded down to the last child and then simply *stopped*, mid-sentence, mid-life, the way the whole country had stopped.

At the very end of the aisle, wedged into a gap in the shelving that she had catalogued a hundred times and knew contained nothing but dust and one dead mouse she'd named Chancellor, sat a book.

It had not been there yesterday. It had not been there six days ago. It had not been there any of the four hundred-odd days since she'd last done a full inventory, and Mara Quill did full inventories the way other people prayed.

"Well," she said. Her voice cracked on it. "Well, that's not possible."

She approached it the way one approaches a sleeping animal: slowly, palms out, narrating. "Hello. Hello there. I don't know who left you, but I should tell you this is a restricted collection and also I have a letter opener."

The book was small, bound in grey leather gone soft as cloth, with no title on the spine. When she lifted it — and it weighed almost nothing, less than it should have, the way a bird weighs less than it looks — the cover fell open in her hands as though it had been waiting to do exactly that.

The first page read:

*Being a True and Final Prophecy of the Country of Cindralle, set down by Sera Vane, Archivist of the Third Order, in the hundredth year of the Veil.*

Mara sat down. Not gracefully. Her knees simply resigned without notice and she ended up on the floor of the genealogy aisle with a century-old prophecy open in her lap and dust motes swirling around her like tiny astonished witnesses.

Sera Vane. She knew the name. Every archivist knew it — the Third Order had been the last, the ones who'd stayed behind when the Veil closed, who'd sworn to keep the record until someone came to collect it. No one had come. The Third Order had died out, or faded, or been taken by whatever took things in Cindralle, and their names survived only in the lists Mara maintained, ink on paper remembering people the world would not.

And now one of them had apparently delivered mail. A century late. To a shelf that had been empty yesterday.

"You couldn't have used a door?" she asked the book. "We had doors. Excellent doors."

Her hands were shaking. She noticed them shaking and made a note to be embarrassed about it later, and turned the page.

The handwriting inside was fine and slanted, the script of someone writing fast and sure, and it opened with words that reached up off the page and took Mara by the throat:

*To the keeper who comes after. You will be alone. Forgive us; we could not prevent it. But you will not always be alone, and tonight is the night I can prove it.*

Mara read the line four times. *You will not always be alone.* She read it a fifth time, aloud, just to hear it in a voice, even hers, even cracking.

"Oh, don't," she warned herself, because her eyes had gone hot and that was ridiculous, she was a professional, she was the last archivist of Cindralle and she did not cry over stationery. "Don't you dare."

She dared a little. Then she blew her nose on her sleeve and read on.

*The Veil does not only keep your country hidden. It keeps the world's memory of it thin, and thin things tear. Once a century, the tear widens — one night, one hour, when something on the far side can be pulled through. Tonight is such a night. I have seen it, because seeing is what archivists are for.*

*He is called the Thackeray man. He comes from the valley beyond the mountains, the magicless one, and he carries a god that has stopped speaking to him. Do not fear him. Fear for him, perhaps. He is the door's key whether he wishes to be or not, and he will wish it least of anyone alive.*

Mara's breath stopped entirely.

*Thackeray.*

She knew the name. Of course she knew the name — she knew every name in the archive, that was the entire point of her — but this name she knew from the marginalia, from the prophecies of the Second Order, scrawled references to *the Thackeray line* and *the sworn man* and once, memorably, *the Thackeray who breaks.* She had assumed it meant someone long dead, some ancestor, some figure of a war Cindralle had traded itself away to end.

But Sera Vane wrote in the present tense. And Sera Vane had written *tonight.*

Mara looked up at the dark ceiling of the archive, as if the ruined capital above might confirm it, as if the eternal twilight might suddenly produce a calendar. One hundred years, four months, six days since the Third Order's last entry. What were the odds? What were the odds that the once-a-century tearing of the Veil would fall on the watch of the one person still alive to answer it?

Actually, she thought, giddy and slightly hysterical, the odds were probably not odds at all. Prophecies were rarely casual about timing. This was probably the entire point of her. This was probably why the blood-oath had chosen *her*, specifically, out of everyone —

No one else was left, whispered the genealogy section, unhelpfully.

"Hush," she told it.

She read the rest twice more, slower. The rite was laid out precisely, in Sera Vane's tidy hand: three candles set in a triangle — she had candles, she had *hundreds* of candles, hoarding candles was one of her few vices — a bowl of water from the capital's fountain, which still ran, because some things in Cindralle persisted out of sheer stubbornness. The book itself, open to the marked page. And blood, one drop, from the keeper's oath-scarred palm, because everything in Cindralle cost blood eventually; that was practically the national motto.

*When the rite is done,* Sera Vane wrote, *he will arrive frightened and angry, and he will want to go home. Let him be angry. Anger means he is still deciding. And keeper — be kind to him. He has been alone longer than you have, even if his loneliness had company in it.*

Mara closed the book gently, the way you close something that might be dreaming.

Then she sat on the floor of the genealogy aisle and let herself feel it, all of it, the whole unbearable weight of it: terror and hope and a wild, indecorous joy that made her laugh out loud in the empty dark until the laugh turned wet around the edges. Because here was the truth she had never said aloud, not even to the shelves, not even to Chancellor's little grave behind Section Four: she had waited her whole life to matter to something. To anything. She had kept the record of a country no one remembered, for an audience of no one, and she had told herself duty was enough, and it had never once been enough, and now a dead woman a century gone had reached across the Veil to say: *you. Tonight. You are the one it happens to.*

She mattered. Something was coming. Someone was coming, frightened and angry and named Thackeray, and for the first time in one hundred years, four months, and six days, the archive was going to have a visitor.

"Right," she said, standing, wiping her face with both sleeves, snapping into motion because motion was easier than feeling. "Candles. Water. Letter opener, for morale." She paused. "And possibly I should eat something first. Summoning a stranger on an empty stomach is how mistakes happen."

She ate two dried plums standing up, which by her recent standards was a feast.

By the time the candles were lit in their triangle in the reading room — the one room she kept swept, the one with the table and the single chair, the chair she had never needed until now — her hands had steadied. Mostly. She filled the bowl from the ewer of fountain water she kept fresh out of habit, set the book open to the marked page, and drew the letter opener across her palm, over the pale scar the blood-oath had left there when she was seven years old.

One drop fell into the water. The candle flames leaned toward each other, all three at once, like conspirators.

And the archive — her silent, faithful, empty archive — exhaled.

The shadows between the shelves grew deeper and then thinner, as though the whole underground country had drawn a breath it had been holding for a century. The water in the bowl shivered without wind. Somewhere far above, in the ruined streets under the forgotten sky, Mara heard footsteps that had not been there a moment before: heavy, deliberate, the steps of a man walking a road he hadn't chosen.

She gripped the edge of the table, heart hammering, and whispered the last line of Sera Vane's page like a blessing, like a welcome mat:

"He is called the Thackeray man."

The darkness at the top of the archive stairs tore quietly open — and something fell through it.
