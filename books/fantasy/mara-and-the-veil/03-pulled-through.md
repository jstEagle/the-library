## 3. Pulled Through

The road out of Vaelmoor was the kind of road that made a man think about his choices.

Emrys Thackeray had been walking it for three days, and in those three days he had thought about exactly one choice: sparing the life of Aldous Vane, tax-collector and occasional murderer, when every professional instinct he owned had said *finish it*. His god — the silent one, the one whose name he'd sworn at nineteen with blood on his tongue and certainty in his chest — had offered no opinion on the matter. The god had been offering no opinions for years now, but this time the silence had felt less like patience and more like absence. Like a house where the furniture had been quietly carried out while he slept.

So he walked. Vaelmoor's grey hills rolled past on either side, magicless as ever, and Emrys told himself that by week's end he'd be back in Lowmark, drinking bad ale, taking a new contract, rebuilding the life that had developed a crack in its foundation.

He did not make it to week's end.

It happened at dusk, on a stretch of road between two hills so alike they might have been twins. One moment his boots were on Vaelmoor dirt. The next moment there was a sensation he would later describe to no one, because there was no honest way to describe it — something like being swallowed, something like being remembered — and then his boots were on cobblestone.

Not grey dirt. Cobblestone. Black stone, worn smooth, laid in patterns that spiraled outward from beneath his feet like ripples in a pond frozen mid-splash.

Emrys did what any sensible mercenary does when reality changes underfoot: he drew his knife and turned in a slow circle.

He was standing in a street. A *city* street, lined with buildings three and four stories tall, their facades carved with figures — kings, saints, things that were neither — all rendered in a style so old it had gone soft, like faces seen through water. Lanterns hung from iron brackets along both sides, and here was the second impossible thing: the lanterns were lit, but there was no flame. Just a soft gold glow held inside the glass like light that had forgotten it was supposed to come from somewhere.

And the air. Gods, the air. Emrys had spent his whole life in Vaelmoor, where magic went to die, where even the hedge-witches' charms curdled into nothing. He'd never felt it before, not once — but he felt it now, the way a man born deaf feels his first sound. It pressed against his skin. It hummed in his teeth. It was *everywhere*, thick as honey, and every instinct he had screamed that he was standing waist-deep in something that could drown him.

"Right," he said aloud, to the empty street. "Either I'm dead, or I'm dreaming, or someone owes me a very long explanation."

"I can do the third one," said a voice above him. "Though I'll warn you now, you're not going to like it."

Emrys looked up. On a balcony three stories overhead stood a woman, thin as a candle, wrapped in a coat that had been mended so many times it was more patch than cloth. She had ink on her fingers, ink on her jaw, and — he squinted — possibly ink in her hair. She was looking down at him with an expression he recognized from mirrors: the careful face of someone who has rehearsed a conversation for years and is now discovering that rehearsal is useless.

"You pulled me here," Emrys said. It wasn't a question. The humming air seemed to confirm it; he could feel threads of it running up toward her balcony like roots toward rain.

"I pulled you here," she agreed. "My name is Mara Quill. Before you do anything regrettable, I should tell you that violence is generally unwise in Cindralle. The city remembers who hurts people."

"The city *remembers*."

"It does that." She vanished from the balcony. He heard footsteps clattering down interior stairs, fast and uneven, and then a door banged open and she came toward him at a half-jog, stopping a cautious ten feet away. Up close she looked younger than the voice suggested and tireder too, with dark circles under bright eyes and the particular posture of someone who had spent a long time alone and gotten strange about it.

"Cindralle," Emrys repeated. "Never heard of it."

"No," she said. "You wouldn't have. Nobody would have. That's rather the point." She took a breath. "I know how this sounds—"

"Do you? Because I've got a list of how this sounds, and we'll be here all night."

"—but I need you to see something first. Then you can be angry. You're going to be angry anyway, I've read enough about men to know that, but you might as well be angry *informed*."

"That is genuinely the worst pitch anyone has ever made to me."

"Please," she said, and something in the word cracked open. Not begging. Worse than begging — the plain exhaustion of someone who had used up all her clever words and had only the true ones left. "I've waited eleven years. Please just look."

Emrys looked at her. He looked at the impossible lanterns, the spiraling stones, the carved faces watching him from four stories up. He thought about a silent god and a spared man and a road home that had, apparently, been yanked out from under his boots.

"Fine," he said. "Show me. But if this is a cult, I want it noted that I objected early."

"It's not a cult. Cults have members." She turned and led him down the street. "I'm the only one left."

* * *

Her archive was beneath the ruined capital, which turned out to mean: down a spiral stair behind a false wall in a building whose roof had caved in decades ago, into a chamber lit by more of the flameless lanterns, walled floor to ceiling with books.

Emrys had seen wealth. He'd robbed a merchant prince's vault in Harrowmere and walked away with more coin than his father had earned in a lifetime. But he had never seen anything like this room. Every book was bound in leather or cloth or metal; every shelf was labeled in a small, fierce hand; and everything — everything — was dusted, ordered, tended, like a garden kept alive in winter by someone who refused to let it freeze.

"This is your cult," he said.

"This is my country," Mara said quietly. "Or what's left of it."

She went to a lectern near the center of the room, where a single book lay open, weighted at the corners with smooth river stones. It was old — old the way the carvings outside were old, softened by time — and its pages were dense with handwriting in brown-black ink.

Mara turned the book toward him and pointed.

Emrys leaned in. And then he stopped leaning, because his body decided that standing was suddenly a complicated activity.

There, in the middle of a page of flowing script, in letters a century faded but perfectly legible, was written:

*And the Thackeray man shall come through the Veil on the eve of the Dwindling, drawn by the keeper's hand, angry as a kicked hive.*

"'Angry as a kicked hive,'" Emrys read aloud. His voice came out flat and strange. "A century ago. Someone wrote that a hundred years before tonight. Before I was born. Before my *father* was born." He looked up at her. "Who wrote this?"

"A seer named Ivellaine Corvane. Last prophet of Cindralle." Mara's fingers hovered over the page without touching it, the way you'd hover over a sleeping animal. "She wrote nine hundred pages about the fall of the country. This is page eight hundred and forty-one."

"And the rest of it?" Emrys scanned down the page. The script continued past his name, past the drawing-through, into lines about a door, a Warden, a price — the ink seemed to darken there, as though the pen had pressed harder. "What else does your prophet say about me?"

"That's the part I haven't finished translating," Mara said.

Emrys stared at her. Somewhere in the last minute, the wonder had drained out of the room and left something colder behind. "You're telling me you performed a summoning rite — you *ripped me across the world*, or across whatever this is — based on a prophecy you haven't finished *reading*?"

"I read the parts that mattered—"

"You don't know that! You don't know what mattered!" He was pacing now, boots loud on the stone, hands moving. "I had a contract. I had a *road*. I was three days from home, and instead I'm in a dead city underneath a dead country with a woman who treats a century-old book like instructions from a friend, and the one thing I know for certain is that some dead seer thought I'd be *angry*, which means she knew me better than you do, which means maybe she knew other things too—"

"She knew you'd spare Aldous Vane," Mara said.

Emrys stopped pacing.

"Page eight hundred and thirty-nine," she said softly. "*The Thackeray man will lower his blade against his code, and his god will say nothing, and in the silence he will begin to hear himself.*" Her eyes didn't leave his face. "Three days ago. I read it the morning after it happened. That's why I did the rite that night. I didn't have a choice, Emrys. The book said you'd come angry, and it was right, and I am *sorry*, but the way home runs deeper in, and I couldn't get there alone."

For a long moment the archive was silent except for the faint hum of the lanterns.

"The way home runs deeper in," Emrys repeated.

"Through the Veil-door. It's real. It's in the book. And it's at the heart of Cindralle, in the place the Erasure came from, and I—" She hesitated, and he watched her decide, visibly, how much truth he could carry. "I can't open it alone. The book is clear about that much."

"Of course it is." Emrys laughed, and it wasn't a kind sound. "Prophecies always are."

He looked at the page again — at his own name sitting in a dead woman's handwriting like a fly in amber — and felt the anger in his chest shift shape, cooling into something heavier and more durable. Because here was the thing he couldn't say out loud: the line about lowering his blade, about the god saying nothing, about hearing himself in the silence — that line had landed somewhere true. Whoever Ivellaine Corvane had been, she had seen into the exact hollow where Emrys lived.

Which meant the rest of the pages might land there too.

"Fine," he said at last. "Deeper in. We go deeper in. But Mara—" he tapped the page, once, hard, "—from now on, you translate *everything* before we act on it. No more surprises from the dead. Agreed?"

Something flickered across her face — quick as a fish under ice, gone before he could name it.

"Agreed," said Mara Quill, keeper of the book, and reached to turn the page.
