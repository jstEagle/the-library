## 5. The Company of the Forgotten

The Hollowed did not live in houses. That was the first thing Emrys understood, and the last thing he stopped being unsettled by.

They lived in *gaps* — a doorway with no wall around it, standing alone in a field of ash-grass; a staircase that climbed to nothing and yet had rooms on either side of it, furnished, warm, lit by candles that burned without shortening. The people inside those rooms were like the architecture: mostly there. A woman waved at them from a window and her hand passed through the glass without a sound, and she didn't seem to notice, or had long ago stopped minding.

"They're not ghosts," Mara said, watching his face do whatever it was doing. "Ghosts are what's left when someone dies. These are what's left when everyone else forgets you were alive."

"That's worse."

"Yes," she said simply. "It is."

The settlement — if you could call a scattering of impossible rooms a settlement — sat in the bowl of a valley that Emrys was fairly sure hadn't existed an hour ago. Cindralle did that. He was learning to stop arguing with it. The country rearranged itself around its own absence, folding space like a man hiding something valuable in the lining of his coat.

A boy was waiting for them at the edge of it.

He stood very straight, the way soldiers stand when they've been told standing straight is the difference between living and not, and he was — Emrys guessed — ten years old, maybe eleven, though his eyes had done time that aged everything behind them. He wore a coat three sizes too big with the sleeves rolled in careful folds, and across his back was a sword nearly as tall as he was, and he looked at Emrys the way a dog looks at a door it has decided is now its door.

"Oh no," said Emrys.

"He's been following us since the bridge," Mara said, delighted in a way Emrys found deeply suspicious.

"I know. I heard him. He breathes like a man twice his size trying to be quiet."

"He's been *protecting* us, probably. From his perspective." She crouched down to the boy's level and spoke slowly, the way you speak to someone you're not sure can hear you properly. "Hello. What's your name?"

The boy looked at her. Looked at Emrys. Opened his mouth.

Nothing came out.

Emrys had seen that before — not the silence itself, but the shape of it. He'd known men who came back from contracts with their voices packed away somewhere they couldn't reach, carrying everything they'd seen in the set of their shoulders instead. This boy's whole body was a report filed in silence.

"Sef," Mara said softly. "His name is Sef. It's in my book. One of the few names that survived with a face attached." She smiled at him, gentle as banked coals. "Sef, this is Emrys. He's grumpy but he's safe. Mostly. To himself, at least."

"I'm right here," Emrys said.

"I know," said Mara.

Sef stepped forward, drew the enormous sword with a scrape of effort that made Emrys wince on behalf of the boy's spine, and presented it hilt-first. An offering. An oath. Emrys stared at it.

"I don't want your sword, lad."

Sef's jaw set. He pushed the sword closer.

"He's giving it to you," Mara translated. "Or offering to fight for you. With Hollowed it's hard to say which, and honestly with Sef it might be both."

"Tell him I don't take oaths."

"You just swore one to a silent god for fifteen years."

"*Had* sworn," Emrys snapped, and immediately regretted it, because Sef flinched at the sharpness the way a horse does, all over, and then — this was the part that got under Emrys's ribs and stayed there — squared up again and held out the sword anyway. Unbothered. Certain. As if a mercenary snapping at him was weather, and weather could be waited out.

Emrys took the sword. It was heavier than it should have been, or he was more tired than he'd admitted. He turned it over once, found the maker's mark worn to a ghost of itself, and handed it back. "Keep it. You clearly use it better than I would."

Something happened to Sef's face then. It wasn't quite a smile — Emrys suspected the machinery for smiling had rusted some years ago — but it was the place where a smile would go, if invited.

That was how he ended up adopted by a child soldier, against his will, in a country that did not exist.

* * *

The Hollowed fed them, which raised questions Emrys decided not to ask, because the answers would have involved the word "remembered" applied to food. The meal was stew that tasted almost entirely of memory — rich, warm, faintly wrong, like a song hummed by someone who'd only ever had it described to them. It filled the stomach anyway. Around the fire — a fire that gave light and heat and no smoke, because smoke required a world that agreed you were burning something — perhaps thirty of them sat. Maybe forty. Their edges kept changing.

An old woman named Wren — or possibly the idea of a woman named Wren — told a story about the harvest festival in Cindralle's southern provinces, and halfway through she forgot the name of the fruit they'd piled in the temple square, and the story stumbled, and everyone waited politely while she groped for it, and it didn't come, and she finished the tale with a hole in it shaped like a fruit, and nobody minded, because every one of their lives was full of holes shaped like things, and holes recognized holes.

Emrys listened. He was good at listening; it was most of his trade. But somewhere between the second story and the third he realized he wasn't gathering intelligence. He was just... listening. Sitting near warmth. Letting a woman rest her hand briefly on his arm as she passed, her touch going through his sleeve like wind through a fence, her smile saying *thank you for being solid enough to sit beside.*

"When did you last eat a meal with anyone?" Mara asked quietly, beside him.

"I eat with people constantly. Contracts, caravans, garrisons."

"Eat *with*. Not across from."

Emrys considered lying to her. She had a way of looking at him that made lying feel like handing her a written confession instead. "I don't remember," he said finally.

"Mm," said Mara, who remembered everything, always, and carried the weight of it like a second spine.

Later, when the fire had gone low and the Hollowed had folded themselves into their impossible rooms, Emrys found Sef sitting awake at the edge of the settlement, sword across his knees, watching the dark. Keeping watch. Of course keeping watch. Somebody had taught him that watches mattered, and then the somebody had been forgotten out from under him, and the lesson remained, orphaned, still faithfully kept.

Emrys sat down next to him. Didn't speak. Soldiers' silence was a language he was fluent in, and after a while Sef leaned — barely, a featherweight of contact — until his shoulder rested against Emrys's arm.

It weighed almost nothing. It anchored Emrys like a mooring line.

He thought about his god. He thought about the fifteen years of kneeling in the dark before contracts, whispering the litany, feeling for that presence the way you press your tongue to a broken tooth — and the slow horror of realizing, mid-fight in Vaelmoor, that the tooth was gone entirely, had maybe been gone for years, and all he'd been praying into was the socket. His god had never needed him. Gods don't. You need them, and the needing is supposed to be enough, and when it isn't you call it faith and keep kneeling.

This boy needed him. Not his skills, not his blade, not his usefulness. Him. The specific weight of him, sitting here, solid, present, *staying*.

It was the most dangerous thing Emrys had felt in fifteen years, and he knew it, and he sat there anyway and let the kid lean.

"You should sleep," he said eventually. "I'll take the watch."

Sef shook his head.

"I'll wake you for second watch. Soldier's split. Fair terms."

Sef considered this with the gravity of a general reviewing terms of surrender, then nodded once, and handed Emrys the sword again — not as an oath this time, Emrys realized, but as equipment. Trust, in its plainest form: *here, you'll need this more than sleep.*

The boy curled up on the ground beside him and was asleep in four breaths, the way only children and the truly exhausted can be.

Mara appeared at his other side with the softness of someone who had spent a lifetime learning not to disturb the world. She settled onto the cold ground, opened the prophecy book across her knees, and began to read by the lightless firelight.

"What page are we on?" Emrys asked.

"The one I haven't translated yet," she said. There was something in her voice — a tightness, small as a stitch pulled wrong — and Emrys, who missed most things on purpose, chose to miss it on purpose now.

"And when you translate it?"

She was quiet long enough that the ash-grass moved twice in the wind.

"Then we'll know the price," she said, "of getting out."

Down the valley, where no wind came from, something vast shifted its attention toward the fire — Emrys felt it the way you feel eyes on the back of your neck, except the eyes were the size of the sky — and Sef woke instantly, hand finding the sword hilt in Emrys's grip, and Mara went white as paper and whispered:

"It knows we're close."
