## 6. The Price Written Down

The final page of the prophecy was written in ink that had gone the color of dried blood, and it was written sideways, and half of it was in a cipher that hadn't been used since before the Erasure, and Mara Quill loved it with her whole ridiculous heart.

She sat in the reading alcove beneath the ruined capital of Cindralle, surrounded by the soft green glow of preservation-lamps, with the book open on her knees and a pot of cold tea at her elbow and three dictionaries stacked like defensive fortifications. She had been deciphering for six hours. Her eyes ached. Her back had filed a formal complaint somewhere around hour four. She did not care. This was, she reflected, the happiest she had been in years, which said something either wonderful or deeply sad about her life, and she chose not to examine which.

*The door opens for the sworn man,* she translated, lips moving. *And the keeper of the book shall—*

She stopped.

She read the line again. Then a third time, slowly, the way you might re-read a letter that begins *Dear Mara, I regret to inform you.*

*And the keeper of the book shall remain, that the door may know its weight. One soul anchors the passage. The door sealed without its anchor devours those who pass.*

Mara set down her pencil very carefully, as though it were something fragile, or possibly explosive.

"Well," she said to the empty archive. "That's not good."

The archive, being an archive, did not answer. It never did. Twenty-two years of talking to shelves and she still occasionally hoped one of them would break the habit of silence.

She read it again anyway, because grief always demands a second reading. *One soul anchors the passage.* Not *may* anchor. Not *a volunteer is appreciated.* Shall. The old prophetic languages were maddeningly precise about obligation and uselessly vague about identity — and this page was no exception. *The keeper of the book.* Which keeper? A keeper could be anyone who held the thing. Emrys had carried it for two days while she slept. Sef had chewed one corner of it, which by some interpretations made the child technically a keeper, though Mara felt the theology of that was shaky.

But she knew. Of course she knew. She had known since the moment she'd found the book that it would end with her name in it somewhere; books like this always collected her the way gutters collect rain. She was the last archivist of Cindralle. She was bound by blood-oath to the country's memory. If any soul in creation qualified as *the keeper of the book*, it was the woman who had spent her whole life being kept by it.

The lamp above her flickered — the dying magic of the country, breathing in its sleep — and in the flicker she saw, quite clearly, herself standing alone in a doorway while two figures walked out into a dawn she would never see.

"Absolutely not," she told the vision, and the lamp steadied, chastened.

* * *

The problem, she decided over her fourth cup of reheated tea, was what to do about Emrys Thackeray.

He was currently upstairs in the archive's antechamber, ostensibly keeping watch and actually attempting to teach Sef to play knucklebones, an endeavor that consisted mostly of Sef winning and Emrys accusing the child of witchcraft in an increasingly admiring tone. Mara could hear them through the floor: the dry rattle of bones, Sef's rare bark of laughter, Emrys's theatrical groans. He had a laugh she'd only heard twice, low and surprised, as if it embarrassed him on its way out.

Here was the trouble with Emrys Thackeray, Mara thought, staring into her tea: he was honorable. Genuinely, inconveniently, catastrophically honorable. A man who spared a target he was paid to kill because his god's blessing failed and he couldn't bring himself to do the work un-blessed. A man who had been dragged across a country's borders against his will and had stayed anyway, because there was a child involved, and because Mara had looked at him with hope on her face and he apparently didn't know how to say no to hope.

If she told him about the anchor clause, he would do something noble and stupid. He would insist it be him. And the prophecy would refuse him — she was nearly certain of it, nearly — and then he would argue with the prophecy, and you could not win an argument with a prophecy, she had tried, professionally, for years — and in the end they would all stand at the Veil-door with the thing sealed and hungry, and everyone would die, and the last record of Cindralle would burn unread.

Whereas if she said nothing—

Whereas if she said nothing, she thought, turning the cup slowly on its saucer, then she was doing exactly what the archivists had always done. Keeping truth from people *for their own good*. Binding knowledge behind oaths. Her whole order had died of that instinct — had traded a country's existence for a war's end and called it mercy, had erased millions and called it peace. Every shelf around her was a monument to secrets kept too well.

And yet.

And yet if she told him, he would die trying to take her place. That was the arithmetic, and she hated it, and she checked it three times hoping for a different sum.

There was, she noted, a third option, which was to tell him everything and simply forbid him from being noble. She gave this option the serious consideration it deserved, which was none. You could no more forbid Emrys Thackeray nobility than you could forbid a river wetness. She'd watched him give his last strip of dried meat to Sef while claiming he wasn't hungry, and Sef had watched too, and Sef had quietly put half of it back in Emrys's pack when he wasn't looking, and Mara had watched *that*, and understood that she was living among people constitutionally incapable of looking after themselves.

Someone had to look after them. That was, when it came down to it, the entire job description of an archivist. To keep the things that mattered safe until they were needed.

Even if the thing that needed keeping, this time, was a secret. Even if the thing that needed keeping was herself.

* * *

She climbed the stairs at dusk with the book wrapped in oilcloth under her arm and her face arranged into what she privately called her *archival expression* — pleasant, competent, revealing nothing. She had practiced it in polished shield-bosses for two decades. It had never once failed her.

It lasted eleven seconds.

"You've got your thinking face on," Emrys said, not looking up from the knucklebones. "The one where you look like you're about to either save the world or alphabetize it."

"I am *always* about to alphabetize something."

"Yeah, but this is the dangerous version." He gathered up the bones and glanced at her, and his eyes were sharper than he liked people to think they were. Mercenary's eyes. They noticed things. "You found something in the last pages."

Sef went still, watching her with the particular intensity of a child soldier who had learned that adults' silences were maps of danger.

Mara smiled her archival smile. "I found the route to the Veil-door," she said, and it was even true. "Three days east, through the Hollow Court. There's a path along the old canal."

Emrys waited. He was good at waiting; it was half his profession. The silence stretched, and stretched, and Mara held it the way she held her breath underwater in the cisterns, comfortably, out of long practice.

"The canal," he said finally. "Through the place with the entity that ate a country."

"The Warden keeps to the court itself. We go around." Mostly true. Probably true. She would refine the probably later.

"And the rest of the page?"

"Accounting," she said lightly. "Lists of names. Prophets love lists; it's how they pad their word count." She patted the wrapped book. "I'll have the full translation by the time we reach the door. You'll know everything you need to know."

Something moved behind his eyes — not suspicion exactly, but its cousin, the one who visited at odd hours. Then Sef tugged his sleeve and rattled the bones meaningfully, and the moment broke, and Mara breathed again.

"Everything I need to know," Emrys repeated, and picked up the dice. "You know, in my experience, that phrase has a remarkable range of meanings."

"Then it's a good thing," Mara said, sitting down across from him and holding out her hand for the bones, "that you're traveling with a professional."

Sef dealt her in. She lost spectacularly, twice, and let herself laugh at it, and hated herself a little for how easily the lie sat inside the laughing, warm and small and patient as a coal.

* * *

That night she did not sleep. She sat in the alcove with the final page and every dictionary she owned, and she hunted for a loophole with the desperation of a woman digging through rubble for a heartbeat.

*The keeper of the book shall remain.*

Could a keeper delegate? Could the book be given — truly given, oath and all — to another? She tried the grammar six ways. The verb *remain* took no object; it did not care who held the book, only that the keeper stay. And *keeper* was rooted, in the old tongue, in the same word as *archivist*. Not coincidence. Prophecies were rarely coincidental; they were cruel in a very organized fashion.

Could the clause be broken? Rewritten? She held her pencil over the ancient vellum and her hand shook, because you did not write in prophecy books, you did not amend the dead, the oath in her blood rose up hot at the mere thought — and beneath the oath, quieter and older, something else stirred. A question. *Who made these rules? Who decided the price?*

An archivist, she thought. Some archivist, a century ago, translating the country's death into orderly lines. Writing down the price the way you write down anything — because someone had to keep the record.

Well. She was the last of that line. And if the record could be kept, perhaps —

No. Not yet. She was too tired, and the thought was too large, and large thoughts at midnight had a way of becoming disasters by dawn. She closed the book instead, and pressed her palm flat against its cover, and felt the faint warmth of it, the country's last living nerve.

"Hold on," she whispered to it. "I haven't finished reading you yet."

Above her, through the floors, she heard Emrys turn over in his bedroll, and Sef's small breath finding its rhythm, and the great slow pulse of Cindralle's dying magic moving through the stone like tide through sea-caves. Three days to the door. Three days of canal-path and Hollow Court and archival smiles.

She could do three days. She had done twenty-two years.

She was still telling herself so when the lamps guttered all at once — every lamp in the archive, in a single breath, as though something enormous had drawn in air — and from far away, from the direction of the Hollow Court, came a sound like a voice clearing its throat.

The Warden knew they were coming. And it had read the same page she had.
