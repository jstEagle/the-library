## 7. Hollow Court

The Warden did not come for him in the night. It came in the hour just before dawn, when the Hollowed's fire had burned down to a sullen orange eye and even Sef — who slept like a cat pretending not to sleep — had finally drifted off against a barrel that smelled of nothing at all, because nothing in Cindralle smelled of anything anymore.

Emrys knew it was there before he opened his eyes. Not by sound. The Warden made no sound. He knew it the way a man knows a hand has been laid on the back of his neck: by the sudden, total attention of the dark.

He sat up. The ruined chapel they'd sheltered in had changed. The broken arches were whole again, or seemed whole, hung with banners he didn't recognize and candles burning with flames that gave no heat and cast no shadows. The floor beneath him was smooth black stone, polished to a mirror, and in it he could see himself sitting — except the reflection was wearing his old Vaelmoor leathers, the good ones, the ones he'd lost three years ago in a card game he'd cheated at and still lost.

"Subtle," Emrys said to the empty air.

The air answered.

*You always talk first,* said a voice, and Emrys's whole body went cold, because he knew that voice. It was low and dry and patient, and it had lived in the back of his skull since he was nine years old, when he'd knelt on frozen stone and sworn himself to the silent god of the Thackeray line. It hadn't spoken in six years. He had told himself a hundred times that he didn't miss it.

He had lied every time.

"You're not him," Emrys said. His voice came out steadier than he felt, which was one of the few professional skills he had left. "My god doesn't speak. That's rather the point of him."

*I am what speaks in the space where he used to be.* The voice moved around him, unhurried, intimate as a confession heard through a wall. *I am the Erasure itself, mercenary. I am what Cindralle bought its peace from. And I have been listening to you walk through my country for six days, wondering what you would sound like up close.*

"And?"

*Louder than you look.*

Somewhere behind the banners, something laughed — a thin, papery sound, like pages turning. Emrys kept his hands loose on his knees and did not reach for the knife at his hip, because he had a strong suspicion the knife was currently a metaphor.

"You want something," he said. "Entities like you don't make conversation. You make offers."

*Clever. Mara Quill took four days to arrive at that sentence, and she's had a lifetime of practice with me.* A pause, and when the voice resumed it had shifted — softer now, almost gentle, the way a surgeon is gentle. *Very well. The offer.*

The mirror-floor rippled. Images rose in it like things surfacing in a pond: Vaelmoor, gray and magicless and honest. The valley road. The inn at Harrow's Bend where the stew was terrible and abundant. And then a man kneeling on stone, younger, thinner, swearing an oath while something vast and silent leaned over him like weather.

*You have spent your whole life serving a god who stopped answering,* the Warden said. *Do you know why he went quiet?*

"Vaelmoor eats magic," Emrys said flatly. "Everyone knows—"

*Everyone knows wrong. Vaelmoor is deaf, not hungry. Your god never stopped speaking, Thackeray. You simply crossed into a country where his voice cannot follow. He has been talking to you for twenty years. You have been walking out of earshot.* The images shifted: the failed blessing mid-fight, the moment his blade had wavered over a target's throat and found nothing behind it, no warmth, no certainty, no god. *That wasn't failure. That was distance. And I can close distances.*

Emrys's mouth was dry. "You're lying."

*Probably,* the Warden agreed pleasantly. *But consider how badly you want me to be telling the truth. Consider that you lay awake last night doing arithmetic — counting the years of silence, weighing them against the memory of the voice, and finding the silence heavier every time. I watched you do it. I watch everyone in my country do it. Grief is very predictable, mercenary. It always takes the same shape: a door, and someone willing to pay anything to hear what's behind it.*

The candles guttered though there was no wind. In their light, the reflection-Emrys stood up from the mirror floor — the one in the old leathers, the one who had never broken a contract, never spared a target, never sat in a ruin sharing terrible dried fruit with a child soldier and an archivist who flinched at her own name.

*Here is the bargain,* the Warden said. *Mara Quill has found the Veil-door. She believes it requires two. She is wrong, or she is lying; either way, it requires only one who is willing. Walk away from her. Take the door alone. I will open it myself — no anchor, no price, no prophecy fulfilled. And when you step through into Vaelmoor, you will step through hearing him.*

The silence after that was the loudest thing Emrys had ever heard.

*His voice,* the Warden murmured, *in your head, warm as it was when you were nine. Every morning. Every contract. Every cold night on the valley road. Twenty years of answered prayers, returned to you all at once, like a debt repaid with interest. All you must do is leave the archivist to me.*

"And what happens to her?" Emrys asked. His voice sounded far away, like someone else's.

*She remains. As everything here remains. Kept. Preserved. Remembered — by me, if by no one else.* A pause, delicate as a scalpel. *You would be saving her from a worse fate, you know. The prophecy she's hiding from you demands an anchor. One soul stays inside Cindralle forever. She has read that page, Thackeray. She has known for days. And she has decided — quietly, without consulting you — which of you it will be.*

The floor-mirror showed him Mara then: small and stubborn in the twilight streets, ink-stained fingers, the book clutched to her chest like a shield made of paper. Showing him the page she'd turned quickly past. Showing him the lie living behind her eyes like a splinter.

*She will die for you and call it duty,* the Warden said softly. *Or you can refuse to let her, and live. Betrayal, or sacrifice. Those are the only doors anyone ever builds. I am merely showing you yours.*

Emrys looked at the kneeling boy in the image. Nine years old, chapped lips, breath steaming, promising everything to a god who — if this thing was telling the truth, and oh, how he wanted it to be true — had been shouting his name across a border of silence ever since.

"I nearly accepted," Emrys said at last, and was surprised to find he meant it, and more surprised to find he could say it aloud. "Just now. For about three heartbeats, I was already gone."

*I know. I counted them for you.*

"But here's my problem." He stood. His knees cracked. Somewhere in the false chapel, the papery laughter stopped. "You keep calling it mercy. But mercy doesn't need the person it's helping to stab someone first. If your offer were real, you'd just give it. The fact that it costs me Mara tells me exactly what it's worth."

*And what is it worth?*

"Nothing," Emrys said. "It's worth nothing. Because I've spent six days being needed instead of employed, and I'd forgotten there was a difference, and I'm not going back." He looked down at his own reflection — the real one, in his current worn coat, with Sef's mended scarf still slung over one shoulder where the kid had dumped it for repair and forgotten it. "Also, you showed me a version of myself in nice leathers, which was honestly a bit much. Very theatrical. Two out of ten."

For the first time, the Warden's voice lost its silk.

*You would choose the archivist. Over your god.*

"I'd choose the archivist over a voice in my head offering me a discount on betrayal," Emrys said. "My god, wherever he is, can take it up with me personally. He knows where I sleep."

The candles went out all at once. The banners rotted back to rags. The mirror floor cracked like ice under spring sun, and through the cracks Emrys saw the true chapel again, gray and ruined and smelling faintly of ash — and saw, with a lurch, that the corner where Sef had been sleeping was empty.

Only a small, wordless scuff mark led out through the broken door, toward the deep places of Cindralle, where the Veil-door waited.

And written in the dust beside the scuff, in a child's careful hand, one word:

*GONE.*

* * *

Emrys ran.

He ran through corridors that rearranged themselves politely ahead of him, past Hollowed who turned their half-remembered faces to watch him pass, down stairs that hadn't existed an hour ago, shouting Mara's name until his throat tore. He found her at last in a gallery lined with empty frames, hunched over the prophecy book by candlelight, burning page after page in a little tin dish — and the flames were the color of wounds, and the walls were screaming, and she looked up at him with soot on her face and terror in her eyes.

"He took Sef," she said. "Emrys — the Warden took Sef. It's going to use him as the anchor."

Behind them, far below, something vast began to laugh.
