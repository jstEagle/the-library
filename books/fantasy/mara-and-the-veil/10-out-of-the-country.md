## 10. Out of the Country

*Mara*

The Warden came apart like a book left out in rain.

That was the only way Mara could describe it, standing before the Veil-door with ink on her fingers and her heart hammering against her ribs like a bird that had finally decided the cage was optional. The great shape that had consumed a country — that had worn Cindralle's absence like a coat — was unraveling into threads of grey light, and each thread hissed as it went, as if even forgetting itself hurt.

Emrys stood with his sword point-down in the stone, both hands resting on the pommel, breathing hard. He had renounced his god not five minutes ago, and Mara kept glancing at him the way one glances at a man who has just set fire to his own house and looks *relieved* about it.

"You're staring," he said.

"I'm cataloguing," she said. "It's what I do."

"Cataloguing what?"

"The moment everything changed. Future archivists will want details." She paused. "There are no future archivists. I'll want details."

Something crossed his face — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would build, if he ever got around to construction. "How much time do we have?"

Mara looked at the dissolving Warden, at the door behind it — a seam of pale gold in the air, still narrow, still stubborn, wide enough for a hand or a desperate shoulder and nothing more. Then she looked down at the prophecy book open in her arms, at the final line she had read aloud in a shaking voice while Emrys broke his oaths:

*One remains, and the door holds.*

One remained now: the Warden itself, its last tatters caught on the threshold like lint. It was anchoring the door by dying in it. Which meant —

Which meant the line was still true. And true lines, Mara had learned over one long terrible night, were the Warden's food.

"No time at all," she said, and knelt, and uncapped her pen.

* * *

*Emrys*

Emrys had watched people write before. Contract-scribes in Vaelmoor, scratching terms in triplicate; army clerks tallying the dead in tidy columns. Writing was a small thing. Writing was ink and patience.

What Mara did at the Veil-door was not small.

She braced the old book against her knee and wrote directly over the final line, her strokes deliberate, unhurried, as though she were copying out a recipe and not unmaking a century of fate. Where her ink touched the old words, the page *drank* them. He heard it — a faint sound like a sigh, like something letting go.

"What are you writing?" Sef asked from beside him. The child's voice was hoarse from screaming earlier, when the Warden had taken them, and Emrys had carried Sef back through collapsing corridors with his heart in his throat and no god anywhere in him to pray to. Sef had one fist knotted in his sleeve and had not let go since. He wasn't sure which of them needed it more.

"The truth," Mara said, "as I'd prefer it."

"That's allowed?" Sef asked.

"It's *required*," Mara said. "That's the whole discovery. A prophecy is just a story someone wrote down first." Her pen moved. "And I am the last person alive who writes this country down. So if anyone gets to decide how Cindralle ends—"

She stopped writing. Emrys saw her shoulders rise once, fall once.

"—it's me," she finished, quietly, and wrote the last word.

*No one remains.*

The door opened like an eye.

Not all at once — it widened the way dawn does, reluctantly and then all at once, the golden seam spreading until it stood taller than Emrys, wider than the corridor, pouring light across the ruined hall where a country had kept its ghost. The last grey threads of the Warden lifted off the threshold and scattered, and Emrys would have sworn — would have staked a contract on it — that they sounded almost grateful to go.

"Well," Mara said. She closed the book and held it to her chest. "I suppose we should see if the world's still there."

"It wasn't there for a hundred years," Sef said. "Why would it start now?"

"Sef," Emrys said.

"I'm being realistic."

"He's being realistic," Emrys told Mara.

Mara looked at the two of them — the mercenary and the child soldier, matching grim expressions, one fist still in his sleeve — and laughed. It was a rusty sound, a hinge unused for years, and it cracked halfway through into something else, and Emrys pretended very hard not to notice her wiping her eyes, because he was a professional, and professionals extended that courtesy.

They stepped through together.

* * *

*Mara*

Vaelmoor smelled like wet grass and woodsmoke and absolutely nothing magical whatsoever, and Mara loved it instantly.

Dawn was coming up pink over a valley of ordinary hills. Ordinary sheep. An ordinary river doing its ordinary work. After a night inside the Erasure — where the sky had been the color of a closed book and the streets of Cindralle had whispered her name in her mother's voice — ordinary felt like a feast.

Sef walked three steps past the threshold, turned around, and looked back at the door hanging in the morning air: a rectangle of twilight, through which the towers of Cindralle could just be seen, dim and patient, keeping their own counsel.

"It's still there," Sef said.

"For now," Mara said. "Without the Warden holding it shut, it will fade. Slowly. Years, maybe. Like a candle going down." She tightened her grip on the book. "But it will be *ours*, while it lasts. Not the Warden's. Ours."

Sef considered this with the gravity of a general reviewing troops. "Can we visit?"

Mara's throat did something complicated. "Yes," she managed. "We can visit."

"Good." Sef nodded once, decisively, and then, because children are built with a mercy adults lose, added: "You're crying again."

"I am *archiving moisture*."

"That's crying."

"It's a *technique*."

Emrys made a sound that in a lesser man would have been a snort. Mara turned on him with dignity. "You have dust in your eyes too, Thackeray. I saw it."

"Valley dust," he agreed solemnly. "Notorious."

They stood there a moment longer, the three of them, in the pink light, with the door of a dead country glowing gently at their backs. Then Emrys shouldered his pack — lighter now, without a god in it — and said, "There's a town two days east. If Vaelmoor has any logic left, nobody remembers me failing my last contract, because nobody remembers anything here, including their own boots half the time."

"And if they do remember?" Mara asked.

He thought about it. "Then I'll be a man who spared a life he was paid to take. There are worse reputations. I've had several."

* * *

*Emrys*

Two days later, in a town called Marrow's Rest, where the innkeeper charged fair prices and remembered none of their faces by morning, Emrys sat by the fire and watched Mara teach Sef letters.

She had bought a slate and chalk with coin Emrys earned hauling crates — honest work, no contracts, no targets, no silent god judging the ledger of his soul. It turned out he was good at honest work. It turned out honest work slept better than contract work did.

"Say it with me," Mara was saying. "C. As in *Cindralle*."

"C," Sef said. "As in *cat*."

"As in *Cindralle*."

"Cats lived in Cindralle?"

"Hundreds. The archive cats were legendary. One of them once ate a treaty."

"Then it worked," Sef said, with deep satisfaction. "No treaty, no war."

Mara opened her mouth, closed it, and shot Emrys a look over the child's head that said *you see what I live with now*. Emrys raised his cup in sympathy. He lived with it too. He had discovered, in two days, that he wouldn't trade it for his god's voice back at any price the Warden could have named.

On the table between them lay the book. Cindralle's last record. Mara had begun, in the empty pages at the back, to write — not prophecy, just memory. Names. Streets. The archive cats. What the market bell sounded like. What her mother's hands looked like kneading bread. Small things, saved from the flood.

Last night Emrys had seen the newest entry and gone very still. It read:

*Emrys Thackeray, who broke every oath he had and kept the only one that mattered.*
*Sef, who is eight, and fierce, and ours.*
*Mara Quill, last archivist of Cindralle, who rewrote the ending.*

Below it, in fresher ink, a fourth line had been added in a hand he recognized as his own, written while Mara slept:

*Vaelmoor, which forgot us, and Marrow's Rest, which never knew us, and the road between, which is enough.*

The world outside would never remember Cindralle. Maps would stay blank. Songs would stay unsung. But the book remembered, and Mara remembered, and Sef remembered, and Emrys — oathless, godless, freer than he had ever been — remembered.

A country, he was learning, was not the land. It was whoever carried it.

"Emrys," Sef said, looking up from the slate. "How do you spell *home*?"

Mara glanced at him too, chalk hovering, waiting — and Emrys found, to his mild surprise, that he knew the answer without any god telling him.

"H-O-M-E," he said. "But you spell it however the three of you say it does."

Sef wrote it wrong. Mara didn't correct him. Outside, the road went on east into the ordinary, miraculous day, and somewhere behind them, faint as a closing eye, a door in the air kept a small warm light burning — just in case they ever wanted to come home the other way.
