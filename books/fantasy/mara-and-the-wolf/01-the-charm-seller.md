## 1. The Charm Seller

The rain came down on Corvane like a verdict, and Mara Vale stood behind her counter and watched it through the gap in her shutters, counting the drops the way other people counted coins.

Not that she had many coins to count. The tin box beneath the floorboard held eleven coppers, a bent silver bit, and a button she kept because it was pretty and because pretending it was treasure made the box feel less like an insult. Eleven coppers. Two days of bread if she haggled. One night at the Drowned Lantern if she didn't eat. She'd done the arithmetic so many times the numbers had worn smooth as river stones.

The shop — *shop* was generous; it was the front room of a rented attic on Candlemaker's Row, with a counter of scarred pine and shelves she'd built herself from crates the fishmonger threw out — smelled of beeswax and dried lavender and the particular staleness of a room where someone lived alone too long. She knew that smell intimately. It was the smell of her own life.

On the shelves, in careful rows, sat her inventory: knotted cords dyed red at the ends, glass beads the color of pond water, pouches of ash and salt and crushed rosemary, copper rings gone green at the seam, little clay figures baked hard in her stove and painted with symbols that meant nothing at all. Charms. Wards. Blessings. Protections against the evil eye, the fever, the landlord, the warden's knock.

None of them worked. That was the whole point.

Magic was outlawed in Corvane — had been for sixty years, since the Edict of Ash, when the last hedge-witches were dragged to the square and the great libraries were burned and the wardens' black coats first appeared in the streets like crows settling on a corpse. Possession of a working charm was a hanging offense. Selling one was worse. But grief and fear don't read edicts, and the people of Corvane still needed to believe that something, somewhere, was watching over them besides the wardens.

So Mara sold them nothing, beautifully packaged, and told them exactly what they needed to hear, and took their coppers, and slept at night knowing that the worst thing she'd ever done was lie kindly.

Mostly she believed that.

* * *

The bell above her door — a cracked thing she'd bought from a junk cart because its voice was wrong, a flat dead note that wouldn't carry past the threshold — gave its broken clank at half past noon.

The woman who came in was wet through and wringing her hands, which told Mara everything before the woman said a word. Wet through meant she'd walked a long way or waited a long time. Wringing hands meant fear, and fear meant a buyer, and buyers with fear paid badly but paid desperate, and desperate customers were the only kind Corvane left anyone.

"Are you—" The woman stopped, glanced over her shoulder at the street, and shut the door behind her with the furtive care of the guilty. "They said on Tanner's Lane there's a woman sells protections."

"Depends what you're looking to be protected from." Mara kept her voice bored. Bored was safe. Eager invited questions, and questions invited wardens, and wardens invited everything else. She let her eyes drift to the window, the street, the ordinary gray business of the city going about itself. Never look too long at a customer. Never let your gaze stick. A woman who stared was a woman who could be described later.

"The fever." The woman's voice dropped. "My son. He's got the sweating sickness, the doctor says it's in his lungs now, and I've heard— they say you can get a charm. A real one."

There it was. *A real one.* Mara felt the old familiar tightening in her chest, the mixture of pity and contempt and weariness that every sale stirred up in her like sediment in standing water.

"Madam," she said, "if I sold real charms, I'd be dead by now, and so would you for asking. What I sell is this." She reached under the counter and brought out a cord, red-knotted at both ends, and laid it flat between them. "Ash from a hearth swept clean on a Sunday. Salt. Rosemary, dried in the dark. Knots tied while saying the child's name — not his true name, his calling name, the one he answers to. You hang it above his bed and you tell him it will keep him safe, and here's the truth of it, which you can believe or not: a child who believes he's protected rests better, and a child who rests better fights harder. That's all any charm ever did, before the Edict, and it's more than most doctors do now."

The woman stared at the cord. Her hands had stopped wringing. "That's all?"

"That's everything. Anyone who tells you different is either a fool or baiting a trap, and both kinds end up talking to wardens."

Something moved across the woman's face — disappointment, mostly, but underneath it a flicker of relief, the relief of a person who has been offered an honest deal in a city where honesty was just another way of being poor. She dug in her purse and came up with four coppers and the bent silver bit, and Mara looked at the silver for a long moment and then pushed three coppers back across the counter.

"Fever season. Take the knot-cord and the salt pouch together. Hang the cord, scatter the salt at the threshold. And boil your water," she added, because no one ever did, and because the fever came out of the wells as often as anywhere. "That'll do more than anything I sell you."

The woman left clutching her purchases like relics. Mara watched her go, then turned the silver bit over in her palm, then put it in the tin box, where it landed with a small honest clink among the coppers.

Eleven coppers and a bit. She was rich until Thursday.

* * *

She ate standing up, as she always did: half a loaf, hard cheese gone soft at the edges, an apple with a bruise she cut around. She ate facing the door, as she always did, because a person who lived alone learned to orient herself toward the entrances of rooms the way a plant oriented toward light. It wasn't fear, exactly. Or it was fear, but so old and so constant it had become something more like posture, like the set of her shoulders, like the way she slept on her side with her back to the wall and her knife under the pillow.

Her mother had taught her that. *Trust the door, not the visitor,* her mother used to say, back when they'd lived in the two rooms above the dye-works and her mother had still been alive and still been whole, before the coughing started, before the winter that took her. *People bring their troubles wrapped in smiles, Mara-girl. The door never lies about what's coming through it.*

Her mother had also taught her knots, and herb-lore, and how to read a face for the lie underneath it, and how to make a charm that was only wood and string and belief — though her mother would have hated what Mara had made of the teaching. Her mother had believed. Genuinely, stubbornly believed, in the small domestic magic of hearth-blessings and threshold-salts, the kind that was probably nothing, the kind the wardens didn't bother with unless they wanted an excuse. *It's not the power that's outlawed,* she'd said once, in the low voice she used for dangerous thoughts. *It's the belonging. A charm ties you to something outside yourself. The Edict wants everyone alone.*

Mara had thought about that sentence for fifteen years without deciding whether it was wisdom or madness. She suspected it was both, which was the worst kind of sentence, the kind you couldn't dismiss and couldn't live by.

She washed her plate, dried it, put it away. One plate, one cup, one knife, one spoon. When she'd first moved into the attic she'd owned two of each, in case of company, and the second set had sat in the cupboard gathering dust for a year before she'd sold it. That had felt like a decision at the time. Now it just felt like furniture.

Outside, the rain thickened. Down on Candlemaker's Row the lamplighters were making their rounds early, and their flames shivered in the wet, and the city went about its evening the way a beaten dog goes about its yard: carefully, close to the walls, watching.

* * *

The knock came at dusk, and it was the wrong kind of knock.

Mara knew every knock on Candlemaker's Row by sound alone. The fishmonger's boy knocked quick and double, wanting payment. Old Vesey from the stairwell knocked soft and apologetic, wanting nothing, just lonely. The landlady knocked like she was punishing the door. This knock was measured. Even. Three raps, spaced like a man counting.

She blew out the lamp first. Then she stood to the hinge-side of the door — never in front of it, never framed in it — and said, "Closed."

"Warden inquiry." The voice was flat, official, muffled by oak. "Open the door, please."

There was no *please* in it. There never was. Mara's heart began doing what it always did, a slow heavy roll like a millstone starting to turn, and she made herself breathe the way she'd taught herself to breathe: in for four, hold for seven, out for eight. Fear was a currency too, and panic spent it fast.

"I'm a trinket-seller," she called. "Knots and beads. Nothing charmed. You're welcome to look, but I'll want it noted that I opened freely and cooperated fully."

A pause. Then, grudgingly: "Open."

She unbarred the door and stood aside, and two wardens came in — a big one with a lantern and a thin one with gloves, and it was the gloves that frightened her, because wardens wore gloves when they intended to touch things. The big one held the lantern high and let the light crawl across her shelves, across the knotted cords and clay figures and pouches of ash, and Mara stood with her hands visible and her face arranged into the expression of a tired woman interrupted at supper, which required almost no arranging at all.

"Name," said the gloved one.

"Mara Vale."

"Trade?"

"You saw the trade. Trinkets. Souvenirs for the gullible. Half my customers are old women scared of fevers and the other half are young men scared of girls."

The gloved one didn't smile. Wardens didn't smile; she'd heard it said they weren't permitted to, and she'd also heard it said they'd simply forgotten how, and she believed the second version. He picked up one of her knot-cords, turned it over inside the glove, and held it up to the lantern light.

"This is a binding knot."

"It's a decoration. Red at the ends because red sells. If I told you it bound anything, I'd be confessing to a crime, and I'm not stupid enough to confess and you're not stupid enough to believe me. It's string, warden. It holds nothing but its own shape."

He looked at her for a long moment. She met his eyes with the flat patience of the very tired, and inside, behind the millstone of her heart, she counted: *in for four, hold for seven, out for eight.* Somewhere in the walls a mouse scratched, absurdly loud.

"Raid on Tanner's Lane last night," the gloved one said at last, setting the cord down. "We found a working charm in a chandler's cellar. Real one. Blooded ink on the reverse. The chandler's being questioned." He let the word *questioned* sit in the air like smoke. "Real charms move, mistress Vale. They pass hand to hand, seller to seller. And sellers talk to sellers."

"I don't know any sellers. I don't know anyone. That's rather the point of me."

"Someone will knock on your door eventually," he said. "They always do. Someone running, someone bleeding, someone with something to hide. And when they do, remember we asked after you tonight, and remember that cooperation is remembered too."

Then they left, taking nothing, which was somehow worse than if they'd taken everything. Taking everything would have been simple loss. They'd left her instead with the knowledge of her own name in their mouths, filed away, waiting.

Mara barred the door and stood in the dark a long time, listening to the rain.

* * *

She didn't sleep. She sat by the cold stove with her knees drawn up and turned the evening over, examining it from every side the way she examined a coin for clipping.

*A real charm. Blooded ink.* In six years of selling fake protections she'd never once seen a real charm cross her counter, and she'd have known — her mother had shown her, once, what living magic looked like, a moth-wing shimmer under the surface of things, there and not there. The chandler on Tanner's Lane was a fat cautious man who cheated on his wicks. He wasn't a magician. Which meant the charm had come to him from somewhere, passed hand to hand, seller to seller, moving through the city like a spark through dry grass.

And the wardens had come to *her*. Not because they suspected her — they suspected everyone, suspicion was free — but because they were mapping the trade. Following the spark. And they'd told her they were doing it, which was its own kind of message: *we see the web, little spider, and we know which strand you are.*

She should leave. The thought arrived with the clarity of cold water. Pack the tin box, abandon the stock, take the roof-road south over the tanneries and be on the morning barge before the wardens finished their breakfast. She'd done it twice before, in the early years, when a customer talked too loud in a tavern or a neighbor's eye lingered too long. Corvane was full of people who'd vanished overnight; the city barely noticed, the way a body doesn't notice one drop of sweat.

But Thursday's rent was due, and the tin box held twelve pieces of metal and a button, and leaving meant arriving nowhere with nothing, starting the whole slow accumulation again in some other gray city with some other gray edict. And besides—

Besides, she was tired. That was the truth under all the cleverness, the truth she kept in the same locked place as her mother's dangerous sentences. Six years of selling nothing beautifully. Six years of one plate, one cup, one knife. Six years of trusting doors instead of visitors, and the doors had never once lied to her, and the visitors had never once stayed, and somewhere in those six years the difference between caution and emptiness had quietly closed until she couldn't find the seam.

*You're alone because it's safe,* her mother's voice said in her memory. *Or you tell yourself it's safe. Safe like a grave is safe.*

"Hush," Mara said aloud, to the dark kitchen, to the memory, to herself. Her voice sounded strange in the empty room, and she realized she couldn't remember the last time she'd used it after sundown.

She banked the stove, lay down with her back to the wall and the knife under the pillow, and stared at the ceiling, where the rain made shifting shadows of the streetlamp's glow. In for four. Hold for seven. Out for eight. Sleep came at last, thin and shallow, the sleep of a woman listening even in dreams for the wrong kind of knock.

* * *

It came two nights later, near midnight, in the teeth of a storm that had the whole row shuttered and dark.

She woke to the sound of the street door below slamming — not knocking, slamming, the sound of a body hitting it — and then footsteps on the stairs, uneven, dragging, a rhythm like a heart failing. Mara was out of bed with the knife in her hand before her mind caught up, pressed to the wall beside her door, breathing the way she breathed: in for four, hold for seven, out for eight.

The footsteps reached her landing. Stopped. And then came a sound she knew from the fever years, from the winters, from the gutters of a city that ate its weak: the wet, laborious sound of a man trying to breathe through blood.

"Please." The word was barely a word. A knuckle rapped against her door, once, feather-light, nothing like a warden's knock. "Charm-seller. On the Row. They said— please. I haven't long."

Every instinct she owned, every rule her mother had ground into her, every lesson of six years of survival said the same thing: stay silent, stay hidden, let him die on the landing and be gone by morning. Strangers bring trouble wrapped in need. The door never lies about what's coming through it.

But her feet were already moving.

Later — in the sewers, in the forest, in the silver court, in all the strange country ahead — she would try to understand why. Why she lifted the bar. Why she opened the door on a bleeding stranger in a storm, against every law of the city and every law of her own heart. She would tell herself it was pity, or curiosity, or the millstone of fear finally rolling loose. None of it was true.

The truth was simpler and stranger: when she pulled the door open, the man on her landing looked up at her with eyes full of storm-dark and blood, and for one unaccountable instant, Mara Vale — who trusted no one, least of all herself — felt the door in her chest swing open too.

The stranger fell forward into her arms, and something folded and heavy pressed into her hands, and the last thing he said, whispered against her shoulder in a voice already halfway to elsewhere, was:

"The wolf remembers."
