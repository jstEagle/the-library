## 2. A Stranger at the Door

The rain came down on Corvane like a judgment nobody had bothered to write out.

Mara Vale watched it from behind her counter, chin propped on her fist, and counted the evening's takings for the third time. Eleven coppers and a button. The button was bone, carved with a spiral — someone had tried to pass it off as a luck-piece, and she'd kept it partly as a curiosity and partly as a reminder that everyone in this city was a fraud, herself included. Especially herself.

Her shop — *shop* was generous; it was a lean-to wedged between a tannery and a chandler's in the Sump, where the streets smelled of rendered fat and the walls wept even when it wasn't raining — her shop sold charms. That was what the hand-painted sign said, anyway: MARA'S TRUE CHARMS, PROTECTION GUARANTEED. What it actually sold was dried lavender sewn into red cloth, river stones polished smooth and wrapped in copper wire, knotted threads that were supposed to ward off bad dreams and did nothing except give restless fingers something to worry at night.

None of it worked. None of it could work. Magic had been outlawed in Corvane for sixty years, ground out of the city like a stain scrubbed from floorboards, and the Wardens made sure it stayed scrubbed. They came through the Sump every few weeks with their grey cloaks and their iron-tipped staves, kicking over altars, burning books, dragging anyone whose hands smelled too much of the old craft into the Hall of Questions. Mara had watched a neighbor — old Fen, who'd only ever grown herbs — taken away in chains because someone reported seeing him murmur over his seedlings. He'd been murmuring to his plants since before she was born. It hadn't saved him.

So she sold people nothing, and they paid her gladly, because hope was the one commodity Corvane never outlawed. A woman clutching a knot-thread against her breast could sleep. A man with a polished stone in his pocket could walk past a Warden without flinching. Mara told herself she was selling comfort, not lies. She told herself that a lot, usually late at night, usually after counting coppers, and usually it almost worked.

The bell above her door rang.

She looked up, already composing the smile — weary but warm, trustworthy, a girl who'd help you if she could — and then the smile died somewhere in her throat.

A man stood in the doorway, or mostly stood. He was tall, gaunt under a travelling cloak gone dark with rain, and he was holding himself upright by pressing one hand flat against the doorframe as though the frame were the only thing keeping the world from tilting. His other arm was clamped against his side, and even in the dim light of her single lamp, Mara could see the wet gleam spreading down his coat. Not rain-wet. Something thicker.

"Close the door," he said. His voice was rough, like a hinge that needed oiling. "Please."

Every instinct she owned screamed at once. This was how you ended up in the Hall of Questions: you let a bleeding stranger into your shop, and when the Wardens came asking — and they always came asking — you were the one with blood on your floorboards. You were the one who'd helped. In Corvane, helping was a crime with a longer sentence than most.

"I'm closing," she said. "Come back tomor—"

He took his hand off the doorframe and took one step forward, and whatever was holding him together gave out. He went down like a puppet with cut strings, catching the edge of her counter on the way, scattering her little display of knot-threads across the floor, and landed hard on his knees in front of her. Blood dripped from his sleeve onto her clean boards. Three drops. Four. She counted them the way she counted everything, because counting kept panic at arm's length.

"I'm not—" He coughed, and the cough had a wet rattle in it that she didn't like at all. "I'm not asking you to hide me. I'm asking you to take something. There's no time. They're close."

"Then go to them," Mara said, backing toward the back wall, where a loose board hid her meagre savings and a knife. "I don't know you. I haven't seen you. That's the best kindness I can do you, and believe me, it's more than—"

"They'll find it on my body." He looked up at her then, and his eyes stopped her words in her mouth. They were grey, the flat grey of the sky over the city, and they were *afraid* — not of dying; she'd seen enough fear to know the difference. Afraid of something else entirely. "They'll find it, and they'll read it, and everything I bled to keep hidden will be for nothing. Please. You sell charms. You understand what an object is worth. Take it. Sell it. Burn it. I don't care. Just don't let them have it."

From somewhere down the street — two streets over, maybe three — came the sound she knew better than her own heartbeat: the measured knock of Warden staves against cobblestone. On patrol. Moving this way.

Mara's mouth went dry. "What is it?"

"A map." He fumbled inside his coat, wincing, and drew out a folded square of paper — heavy paper, vellum maybe, stained dark along one fold. "It isn't what it looks like. Nothing about it is what it looks like. Don't open it here. Don't open it where anyone can see. And whatever you do—" he pressed it into her hands, folding her fingers closed around it with both of his, and his grip was fever-hot and shaking "—don't let it choose for you."

"That makes no sense," Mara whispered.

"No," he agreed, and something that might have been a smile touched his cracked lips. "It never does. At first."

The staves knocked closer. She could hear voices now, low and clipped, the particular bored authority of men doing a job they enjoyed.

"Go," she hissed. "Out the back, through the chandler's yard, there's a gap in the fence—"

"There's no going anywhere." He said it simply, the way you'd report the weather, and she understood suddenly that he'd known that all along. He hadn't come to her to be saved. He'd come to her because she was the last door on his road. "Listen to me. My name doesn't matter anymore — I gave it up a long time ago, and that was the smartest thing I ever did. But listen. When you look at it — and you will look, everyone looks — remember that it moves. It's supposed to move. And remember the wolf."

"The wolf?"

"The wolf remembers." His eyes had gone distant now, fixed on some middle distance where she couldn't follow. "That's the whole of it. That's the warning and the promise and the curse, all in three words. The wolf remembers. Everything else forgets. The wolf remembers."

His grip on her fingers loosened. Not gently — it failed, the way a rope fails, all at once. He sagged sideways against her counter, and Mara caught his shoulder on pure reflex, lowering him down to the floorboards as his legs folded under him.

"No," she said stupidly. "No, wait, I can't—"

She pressed her hands against the wound in his side and felt the warmth pulse out between her fingers, steady and terrible. She knew nothing of wounds. She knew knots and lavender and the price of river stones. Her hands were small and quick and good at counting, and they could not hold a man's life in his body.

"Is there someone," she said, hearing her own voice crack, "someone I can fetch, is there—"

"Don't." His hand found her wrist. His strength was nearly gone, but his eyes still held hers, and they were calm now, which frightened her worse than the fear had. "You fetching anyone brings them here. You've done enough. More than enough." A breath. A long one. "It chose you, you know. It's been choosing since the bridge. I just... carried it the last mile."

"What are you talking about? What *is* it?"

But his gaze had slid past her, to the ceiling, or through it, and when he spoke again his voice was barely a thread.

"The wolf remembers," he said. "Tell it... tell it I'm sorry. Tell it I kept it safe as long as I could."

And then he didn't say anything else at all.

Mara knelt on her own floorboards with a dead stranger's blood cooling on her hands and did not move for a long time. The rain filled the silence against the roof. Somewhere outside, the Warden staves knocked their patient rhythm, and then — she realized with a lurch — they had stopped. Nearby. Very nearby.

She looked down at the folded paper in her fist.

It was warm. Warmer than a dead man's pocket should have left it. Warmer than the room.

*Don't let it choose for you.*

The knock on her door was polite, which somehow made it worse. Polite meant official. Polite meant they already had a reason and were giving her the courtesy of pretending otherwise.

"Mara Vale?" A voice through the wood, muffled by rain. "Warden inquiry. Open the door, please."

She was on her feet before she'd decided to stand. Her eyes darted everywhere at once — the body, the blood, the back wall, the loose board, the window too narrow for a cat — and her mind, her quick, counting, surviving mind, laid it all out with horrible clarity: dead man, blood on her boards, blood on her hands, an object of unknown provenance in her possession. She could imagine the questions. She could imagine the Hall of Questions itself, the long table, the lamp swung close. Old Fen had murmured to his seedlings. What would they make of this?

"Miss Vale. We can do this politely or otherwise."

There was no version of this where she stayed. She saw that now, saw it whole, the way you see a whole street in a flash of lightning. Staying meant the Hall. Staying meant the end of Mara Vale, charm-seller, and the beginning of a file in a Warden archive.

The map was in her coat pocket. She didn't remember putting it there.

She blew out the lamp.

In the dark, she moved on memory alone: the counter, the back shelf, the loose board she pried up with fingernails that shook so badly she had to try twice. Coin purse. Knife. The little tin of needles she used for sewing the charm-cloths — she took that too, God knew why, some animal instinct that said *take the tools of your trade*. Then the back door, which opened onto the chandler's yard, where fat barrels breathed their greasy breath into the rain.

Behind her, the front door splintered. Not so polite after all.

Mara ran.

* * *

She ran the way she'd learned to run as a child in the Sump: low, fast, knowing every gap and gutter, every yard where a dog slept chained and every wall with footholds worn into its mortar. Rain hammered her hood. Behind her, boots splashed and voices shouted — they'd found the body, they'd found the lamp still warm, they'd found a shop belonging to a woman whose name they already knew, and the knowledge of that sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

*Wanted.* She was wanted now. Whatever else happened, however tonight ended, Mara Vale had crossed a line that didn't have a way back over it.

She went down through the Tanner's Walk, where the drainage channels ran black and reeking, and dropped into the storm-culvert beneath Foundling Street just as torchlight bloomed above her. The water was calf-deep and freezing. She waded into the dark until the shouts faded to echoes and the echoes faded to nothing, and then she found a dry ledge beneath a broken grate and sat down in the dark with her knees pulled up, shaking.

Not from cold. Or not only from cold.

Slowly, with fingers that refused to steady, she took out the map.

She shouldn't open it. Every sensible voice in her head said so — the dead man's voice among them, *don't open it where anyone can see*. But she was alone in a sewer with a dead man's gift and a dead woman's future, and sensible voices had never been much use to her anyway.

She unfolded it.

Even in the dark, she could see it. That was the first wrong thing — the ink seemed to hold its own faint light, silver-grey lines on stained vellum, drawing themselves out of the darkness for her alone. Roads. Rivers. Mountains rendered in fine cross-hatching, a coastline, a delta. And at the center, where any honest map would put a capital city, a single figure drawn small and precise: a crowned shape, seated, its details lost in shadow.

"It's just a map," she whispered, and her voice bounced strangely off the culvert walls, coming back to her doubled, tripled, like a crowd agreeing with her.

Then the ink moved.

She would spend the rest of her life trying to describe that moment and never quite manage it. A river shifted — actually shifted, its silver line sliding westward like a snake adjusting its coils. A mountain range rippled, settled, grew a peak it hadn't had a moment before. A road unspooled across the vellum, extending itself inch by inch, as though somewhere a cartographer were drawing it in real time, as though the world it depicted were still being made.

Mara dropped it.

The map landed face-up on the wet stone and did not stop moving. Roads crawled. Coastlines breathed. And the crowned figure at the center — she could have sworn, she would swear later, with her hand on anything holy — turned its head.

She snatched her hand back and pressed it against her mouth. Her heart was slamming so hard she could feel it in her ears, in her teeth, in the wound-sore palms she'd used to hold a dying man together.

*It moves. It's supposed to move.*

He'd known. He'd told her, and she hadn't understood, because there was nothing in her careful, small, counting life that had prepared her to understand. Maps didn't move. Dead men didn't hand you destinies in doorways. Charm-sellers from the Sump didn't get chosen by anything except bad luck.

Except.

Except he'd said *it chose you*, and the map lay there glowing softly in the sewer-dark, and she could not stop looking at it. That was the horror of it, she realized — the true horror, underneath the moving ink. She *couldn't* stop looking. The roads pulled at her eyes the way fire pulls at a moth, and when she finally dragged her gaze away, her chest ached with the loss of it, a physical ache, like a hook set behind her breastbone reeling in slack.

The map wanted her to follow it.

"No," she said aloud, to the map, to the dead man, to the whole rotten arrangement. "No. I'm not — I sell knot-threads. I'm nobody. Find somebody else."

The map, naturally, said nothing. It just went on breathing.

Above her, muffled by grates and distance, she heard the staves again. Knocking. Methodical. Working street by street, drain by drain. They would search the Sump tonight, and tomorrow, and the day after. Her face would be on notices by morning. *Mara Vale. Wanted for questioning regarding the death of an unidentified male.* And when they questioned her, they would find the map, and the map moved, and things that moved were magic, and magic was the Hall of Questions and worse.

There was nowhere in Corvane she could go. She understood that with the same lightning clarity as before, and this time the flash showed her something else: the map's roads, crawling westward, toward mountains she'd never seen, toward a crowned figure at the center of everything.

Toward the wolf.

"The wolf remembers," she repeated bitterly. "Wonderful. I'm running from wolves already. They're called Wardens, and there are hundreds of them."

But her hand was reaching for the map. She watched it reach, almost detached, as though it belonged to someone braver or more foolish. Her fingertips touched the vellum, and the warmth of it climbed up her arm like sunlight through water, and the hook behind her breastbone pulled, gentle and insistent, the way a hand pulls a child who has lingered too long at a shop window.

*Home,* the pull said, though maps cannot speak. *This way home.*

Mara Vale sat in the dark of the storm-culvert with blood drying under her fingernails and a living map whispering to her bones, and she thought about eleven coppers and a bone button, about a sign that promised protection guaranteed, about a life so small she could count every piece of it in one sitting.

Then she folded the map closed — it resisted, the folds wanting to stay open, like eyelids fighting sleep — tucked it against her heart, and stood up.

"Not because I want to," she told the dark, and the dark, at least, was honest enough not to argue. "Because staying means dying. That's all this is. Survival. Same as always."

She began to walk, following the culvert west, away from the knocking staves, away from the city that had been the whole of her world. The map pulsed warm against her ribs with every step, patient as a second heartbeat.

And far ahead of her — though she could not have said how she knew, only that the knowledge arrived whole, the way the map's light arrived, unasked — something in the deep places of the world lifted its head, and remembered her name.
