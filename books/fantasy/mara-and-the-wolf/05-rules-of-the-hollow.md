## 5. Rules of the Hollow

Bram Hartwell had carried a great many things out of Corvane — exile papers he'd never shown anyone, a brass compass that pointed at nothing useful, and the particular shame of a man who had drawn a door and then watched what came through it. He had never before carried a person.

Mara Vale weighed almost nothing. That was the first thing he noticed as he hauled her over his shoulder at the forest's edge, her boots trailing through the fern-wet dark. The second thing was the map, folded into her fist so tightly that prying it loose would have meant breaking fingers, and he wasn't going to do that. Not to her. Not to *that*.

The Hollow's forest did not have trees so much as it had opinions about trees. The birches grew pale and close together, their bark scarred with whorls that might have been eyes if you were tired enough, or honest enough, to admit what you were seeing. Bram walked with his lantern shuttered to a knife-slit of light and counted his steps the way other men said prayers. Three hundred to the stream. Six hundred to the split stone. Nine hundred to the place where the path forgot itself and you had to walk by memory or not at all.

Behind him, Mara stirred and muttered something in her sleep. A name, maybe. He hoped not. Names were currency here, and she'd arrived already spending.

He'd found her at dusk, sprawled where the world thinned — one moment the scrubland outside Corvane's walls, the next this green cathedral with its patient dark. She'd come through the map. Of course she had. It pulled people the way rivers pulled leaves, and it had been pulling for three years now, ever since Aldren Voss had pressed it into Bram's hands on a night much like this one and made him swear to finish what he'd started.

Bram hadn't finished anything. He'd run instead, all the way back across the breach, and let the wardens strip him of his guild sash and his good name and call him mad in the official record. *Cartographer Hartwell suffers from an affliction of invention.* Eleven words to end a career. He'd had them memorized within the hour, the way you memorize a wound.

But the map had come back. It always came back. And now it had brought him a charm-seller with ink-stained fingers and a grip like a drowning woman's, and the debt had come due whether he liked it or not.

* * *

She woke at dawn, which in the Hollow was less an event than a suggestion — the light came down gray-green and sideways, filtered through a canopy that didn't quite agree on the season. Bram had built a small fire from deadfall that was actually dead, which took some finding, and set a kettle over it. When her eyes opened he pushed a tin cup toward her without looking up.

"Drink. Slowly."

She drank. Then, because she was clearly the sort of person who needed to know the shape of a room before she trusted it: "Where am I?"

"The Hollow King's realm. Formally, the Verdant Marches. Informally—" he gestured at the birches, the half-light, the silence that pressed against the ears like water "—nowhere. This place isn't on any map but mine, and I'm no longer permitted to make maps."

"You're Bram Hartwell." She said it like an accusation.

"I'm surprised you know that."

"The map told me." She pulled it from her coat — she'd slept with it against her chest, he noticed; smart — and unfolded it across her knees. The ink had shifted overnight. Roads he remembered drawing had migrated eastward. A river had grown a tributary that forked like a lightning strike. And there, at the center, the crowned figure sat exactly where it always sat, patient as a spider.

"It does that," Bram said. "It's not a picture of the realm. It's a *participant*. Draw it wrong and it corrects you. Follow it wrong and it corrects you too." He looked at her over the fire. "You bled on it. In your shop, when the stranger grabbed your hand. I can see the binding in the lines — there's a thread of red running through the northern road that wasn't there three years ago."

Her jaw tightened. "So I'm stuck with it."

"We're both stuck with it. It chose you, and it led you to me, and I owe its previous bearer a debt I'd very much like to stop owing." He stood, shouldering his pack. "Which means keeping you alive long enough to reach the capital. Which means teaching you how to survive here. Because everything you know is wrong, Mara Vale, and in the Hollow, being wrong is fatal."

* * *

He taught her the first rule at the stream, where the water ran clear over stones the color of old teeth.

"Never give your true name." He crouched and filled the kettle. "Not to me, not to the trees, not to anything that asks politely. Especially not to anything that asks politely. Things here trade in names the way Corvane trades in coin, and they're better bargainers than you are."

"That seems paranoid."

"Everything here seems harmless right up until it has your name. Then it owns a piece of you, and pieces add up." He handed her the kettle. "You'll be asked. You'll be asked sweetly, by voices that sound like people you've lost, by children who are lost, by merchants and monks and things wearing the shapes of both. The answer is always the same: give them something small and false. A nickname. A lie. I've been 'Hartwell' for eleven years and my true name hasn't crossed my lips since the day I crossed over. It's the only reason I still have all of myself."

Mara turned the kettle in her hands. "In Corvane we don't give our names either," she said finally. "To wardens, to tax collectors, to anyone who might write them down. Half my customers buy charms under false names because they're afraid of being recorded." A short, bitter laugh. "I left a city where names were dangerous and walked into a forest where they're worse."

"Then you're ahead of most travelers." He almost smiled. It felt strange on his face, like a joint that hadn't bent in years. "First rule learned. Two more, and then you might live a week."

* * *

The second rule he taught her with mushrooms.

They'd stopped in a clearing where the ground rose into soft hummocks, and growing among the roots was a ring of pale caps, fat and inviting, arranged with a neatness that no fungus had any business possessing.

"Fairy ring," Bram said. "Or the Hollow's version of one. Inside that circle, time runs differently. Eat what grows inside it and you belong to whatever planted it." He nudged one cap with his boot; it didn't move, though the ground beneath it seemed to flinch. "Which brings us to rule two: never eat what's offered. Anything offered freely in the Hollow has a hook in it. Berries on a bush that parts its branches for you. Bread left on a doorstep. A feast laid out in an empty hall with the chairs still warm. Hunger makes people stupid, and this place counts on it."

"What if I'm starving?"

"Then you eat what you carry, what you catch yourself, or what you grow yourself from seed that came from outside. There are ways. I'll teach you the snares tonight." He glanced at her. "You look like someone who's gone hungry before."

"Corvane," she said, as if that explained everything. It did. He'd been poor once too, before the guild, before the fall. Poverty taught a specific kind of arithmetic — how many days a loaf could stretch, how many meals a pride could survive losing. He recognized the calculation behind her eyes and didn't comment on it. Some debts you acknowledged by staying silent.

They walked on. The forest thickened, and the rules thickened with it. Don't step on the moss that glows. Don't answer echoes that repeat your words back wrong. Don't follow lights. Don't trust doors that stand open in the middle of nowhere, because a door needs two sides and if you can't see the second one, you're the second one.

Mara absorbed it all with a merchant's efficiency, cataloguing, cross-referencing, occasionally arguing.

"Why the moon?" she asked, when he reached the third rule. They'd made camp in the hollow of a fallen oak, and above them, through a tear in the canopy, the moon hung swollen and silver, brighter than it had any right to be. "Never look at it twice. That's the strangest one yet."

"Because the moon is the king's eye." Bram kept his voice low, though there was nothing nearby to hear. Probably. "The wolf wears the crown, but the crown watches through the sky. Look once and it sees you — a traveler, nothing, forgettable. Look twice and you've *acknowledged* it. You've told it you know who rules here, and knowing is a kind of allegiance, and allegiance is a kind of invitation." He pulled his blanket up. "There are people in these woods who looked twice, years ago. Some of them are still walking. Still looking up. Every night, forever. The moon remembers interest the way a moneylender does."

Mara lay back and stared deliberately at the underside of the oak's root ceiling. "Three rules," she murmured. "No names, no gifts, no moonlight. Your whole survival doctrine fits on a coin."

"So does most wisdom. It's the living by it that takes up room." He banked the fire. "Sleep. Tomorrow we reach the village, and you'll want your wits for that."

"What village?"

"People who traded themselves away to stay safe. You'll see." He closed his eyes. "And Mara — the map. Keep it against your skin. If it starts to warm up, wake me. Warm means it wants something, and I'd rather know what before it takes it."

* * *

They reached the village on the third day, at the hour when the light went thin and gold and the shadows of the birches stretched out like fingers counting the ground.

Bram smelled it before he saw it: woodsmoke, yes, but underneath it something sweeter and stranger, like bread and beeswax and rain on hot stone. Then the trees opened onto a valley — a small one, cupped in hills, with a stream running through it and perhaps forty houses clustered along its banks. Thatched roofs. Gardens. Laundry strung between doorposts, shirts and dresses turning gently in a wind that touched nothing else in the valley.

It looked, Mara said quietly, "like somewhere people are happy."

"It looks like somewhere people *stopped*," Bram corrected. "There's a difference. Watch."

He led her down the slope, and as they crossed the stream on stepping stones, the villagers emerged. Not all at once — that would have been alarming — but gradually, the way fish rise. A woman at a well. An old man mending a net. Children who paused mid-game to watch, their faces open and unafraid, which was somehow more unsettling than fear would have been.

"Bram Hartwell," the old man said warmly, rising to clasp his arm. "Back again. And you've brought someone."

"This is—"

"*Don't,*" Bram said sharply, and Mara's mouth snapped shut around whatever she'd been about to say. He turned to the old man. "A traveler. A friend. Passing through."

"Of course, of course." The old man's smile didn't flicker, but something behind it did — a flicker like a candle guttering, there and gone. "We're glad of company. We're always glad of company. Stay for supper. Stay as long as you like." His eyes, Bram noticed, were the same gray as the stream stones. All their eyes were. He'd forgotten that, or made himself forget.

"They can't leave," Bram murmured to Mara as they walked deeper into the village, past the well, past a shrine to a god whose statue had been worn faceless by weather or by hands. "That's what this place is. Generations ago they made a bargain — their names for safety. No warden could hunt them, no debt collector could find them, no war could conscript them, because officially they had never existed. The Hollow honored the bargain. It erased them. From every record, every memory outside this valley, every ledger and parish book and mother's tongue." He nodded at the laundry, the gardens, the children. "Safe as stones. And stones don't leave."

Mara had gone very still. "They can't even die?"

"Not properly. They end, eventually — everything ends here, even if slowly — but there's no passing on. No going anywhere. They just... thin out. Become part of the valley." He watched her take that in, watched her merchant's mind turn it over and find the price tag. She was quick. He'd known that within a day. What he was only now understanding was how much it cost her to be quick — how each new cruelty she catalogued got filed somewhere behind her eyes, added to a ledger she'd apparently been keeping since childhood.

"And they just stay," she said. "For safety. For *this*." Her voice held something between pity and horror and, faintly, horribly, recognition.

"Most people trade something for safety, Mara. Most trades are just smaller." He thought of his own guild oath, sworn away to keep his freedom. Thought of her trinkets sold as charms to desperate people, harmless lies that kept bellies fed. "Don't judge them. You won't like what you find if you dig for the difference between you and them."

Before she could answer, a voice called from the doorway of the nearest house — thin, cracked, urgent.

"You. Girl. With the map."

An old woman stood there, leaning on a stick, her shawl pulled tight despite the mild air. Her eyes were not gray. They were brown, fierce, human, and fixed on the outline of the map beneath Mara's coat with an expression Bram had seen only once before — on Aldren Voss's face, the night he died.

"I know that shape under your coat," the old woman said. "I knew its first owner, before he bound it, before he bound *anything*. Burn it, girl. Burn it while you still can. While it's still only showing you roads." She gripped her stick until her knuckles whitened. "Because I promise you — the day it shows you the crown, it will already be showing you *yourself*."
