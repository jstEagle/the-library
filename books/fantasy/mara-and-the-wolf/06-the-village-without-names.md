## 6. The Village Without Names

Mara woke to the smell of bread.

Not the hard, ash-crusted loaves she'd known in Corvane's lower wards — bread that was mostly sawdust and hope — but real bread, dark and sweet with something like honey, baking somewhere close. For a moment she lay still on a bed of dried bracken and wool and let herself believe she was home, in the narrow room above her shop, and the smell was drifting up from old Marta's bakery two doors down.

Then she opened her eyes and saw leaves moving overhead where a ceiling should have been, and remembered everything: the map pulling her through itself, the forest swallowing her whole, Bram Hartwell's face above hers — older than his voice, lined like something left out in weather — saying *you're lucky you landed at all.*

She sat up too fast. The world tilted. A hand steadied her shoulder, and she flinched away from it before her mind caught up to what her body already knew: it was Bram, kneeling beside her pallet with a bowl of something steaming, his expression carefully neutral, the way a man is careful around an animal he hasn't decided about yet.

"Slowly," he said. "The Hollow doesn't like to be rushed. Neither does your head."

"Where—"

"Village. What's left of one." He set the bowl in her hands. Barley and mushrooms and something green she didn't recognize. It smelled better than anything she'd eaten in a year. "Eat. Then we talk."

She ate. She hated how fast she ate, how her hands shook around the bowl, how grateful she was and how much she resented being grateful. In Corvane, gratitude was a debt, and debts were leashes. She'd spent ten years making sure no one held hers.

The map was folded into her coat still. She could feel it against her ribs, warm as a sleeping animal, faintly pulsing when she breathed. She'd tried to leave it behind twice since waking — once tucked under bracken, once wedged in the crook of a tree root — and both times it had been back inside her coat before she finished walking away. It didn't move when she watched. It only moved when she didn't.

*The wolf remembers,* the dying man had said. She was starting to think the map did too.

* * *

The village came out of the trees the way a secret comes out of a mouth — reluctantly, and only because someone asked the right question.

One moment they were walking a deer path through birches so pale they looked skinned; the next, the path widened into a lane, and the lane was lined with houses. Not abandoned ones. Lived-in houses, with smoke rising from chimneys and herbs drying under eaves and a dog asleep in a dust wallow who lifted his head, considered them, and put it down again without barking.

"They won't see us until we're already here," Bram said quietly. "That's not magic exactly. Or it is, but it's theirs, not the forest's. The Hollow respects a boundary that's paid for."

"Paid for with what?"

He didn't answer. That was answer enough.

There were perhaps forty houses, built of timber and pale stone, each one different and each one somehow the same, as if forty people had been given the same dream and built it separately. Gardens grew in the spaces between. A woman beat a rug on a fence rail. Two children chased each other around a well, shrieking in the particular wordless way children shriek when they've forgotten anyone might be listening.

And every single person stopped what they were doing when Mara and Bram crossed the threshold of the lane.

It wasn't hostility. That was the strange part. It was recognition — the weary, unsurprised recognition of people who had seen strangers arrive before and knew exactly what came next. The woman with the rug lowered it slowly. The children went silent mid-shriek, which was somehow worse than any scream. Somewhere a door closed, gently, the way you close a door when you don't want to admit you're hiding behind it.

An old man stepped out of the nearest house. He was bent like a question mark and dressed in good wool gone soft with years, and he looked at Bram for a long moment before he looked at Mara, and when he looked at Mara he looked at her coat.

"You brought it back," he said.

"I don't—" Mara started.

"The map." His voice was flat as slate. "Someone always brings it back."

* * *

They gave them a house for the night. Of course they did. That was the first thing Mara understood about the village, sitting on a bench outside while soup was pressed into her hands and blankets were fetched without being asked: these people could not refuse you. Not would not. Could not. Hospitality poured out of them the way water pours from a cracked jug — constantly, involuntarily, whether or not there was anything left inside.

The old man introduced himself as Elder, and when Mara asked his name he smiled with the whole of his face except his eyes.

"That's the whole of it," he said. "Elder. The one who is oldest. We gave up the rest a long time ago."

"Gave up," Mara repeated. She'd learned long ago that when powerful people used passive language — *gave up, was lost, happened to be* — someone else had done the taking. "Who took them?"

"No one took." Elder settled onto the bench beside her with the groan of old joints, holding his own bowl of soup he didn't seem to eat. "We traded. That's the part your face wants to argue with, girl, so let it argue now and be done. There was a bad year. Warden purges in Corvane, and the reach of the wardens growing longer every season, and some of us — we were record-keepers, teachers, healers, people whose names were written down in places names shouldn't be written — we ran. And the forest offered us terms."

"The forest offered."

"The Hollow King's forest offers everyone terms eventually. Most just aren't listening." He sipped his soup. "Our names, in exchange for our lives. Written out of every ledger, every memory, every mouth beyond the treeline. Our mothers, if they lived, forgot they'd borne us. Our debts dissolved. Our crimes unrecorded. The wardens hunted us to the edge of the trees and found nothing to hunt, because there was nothing left to find. No name, no trail. No trail, no warrant."

"And in exchange you can never leave."

"In exchange we can never leave," Elder agreed pleasantly, as though confirming the price of turnips. "Nor die. Those are rather the same thing, out here. You can't cross the boundary — the nameless have no weight to carry past it, no self to push against the world. And death needs a name to write in its book. We are, all of us, unwritten."

Mara looked down the lane. The woman was beating her rug again. The children had resumed their game, quieter now. Smoke rose from forty chimneys into air that never seemed to move.

"How long?"

"Longer than I can say, which is rather the point." He chuckled, dry as leaf-fall. "I remember having a name the way you remember a coat you outgrew. I remember it fit, once. I don't remember what it felt like."

* * *

Bram found her at the well at dusk.

She'd gone there to fill a bucket she didn't need, just to have something to do with her hands, and instead stood watching the water — black and perfect, holding the first stars — and thinking about a shop on Ropewalk Lane with a false bottom drawer and a sign that said CHARMS, HONESTLY PRICED, which was the most honest lie she'd ever told.

"Bram," she said, without turning. "How many times has the map come here?"

He leaned on the well beside her. Up close, in the last light, she could see the exhaustion carved into him, deep as plow furrows. "Three times that I know of. Before me, twice. Both times carried by someone running from Corvane. Both times handed off within a season." He paused. "The man who gave it to me — Tam Voss. He stayed here a year after I drew the map. Trying to decide what to do with it. The villagers fed him, clothed him, loved him as best as nameless people can love. And then he walked into the deep forest alone and never came back, and the map turned up in my workshop in Corvane three days later, folded inside my own old field notes."

"He sent it to you."

"He sent it to whoever would take it next. That's what the map does. It doesn't want to be kept. It wants to be *carried.*" Bram's voice dropped. "Elder will tell you to burn it. They all will. Listen to why before you decide anything."

"Why do you care what I decide? You exiled yourself from this place same as anywhere else."

Something moved across his face — guilt, maybe, or its exhausted cousin — and was gone. "Because I drew it," he said simply. "Every line. Every road that shouldn't exist. I mapped the Hollow King's realm when mapping it was a hanging crime, and I told myself it was for knowledge, for truth, for the beautiful stupid reasons clever men tell themselves. And everything that's happened since — Voss, the villagers, whatever's coming for that map right now — grew out of my ink." He looked at her, and for the first time since she'd met him, he wasn't performing calm. "So yes. I care what you decide. More than you know."

Before she could answer, a voice called from down the lane — thin, cracked, urgent — and they both turned.

* * *

The old woman's house was the smallest on the lane, set apart at the far edge where the gardens gave up and the trees began again. She was waiting in her doorway before they reached it, as if she'd known, and maybe she had; Mara was learning that in this village, knowing things was the only power anyone had left.

Her face was a topography of wrinkles, deep valleys and ridges, and her eyes were the pale washed-out blue of very old glass. She gripped a walking stick carved with spirals and pointed it at Mara's chest — at the coat, at the shape beneath it — before either of them spoke.

"Burn it," she said.

"Elder Maren—" Bram began.

"Don't you *Elder* me, cartographer. I knew you when you still had ink on your fingers and lies in your mouth." But her eyes never left Mara. "Girl. Child. Whatever you are. Burn the map. Tonight. While the fire's hot and your hand still belongs to you."

"It doesn't burn," Mara said. "I've tried."

The old woman laughed, and it was a terrible sound, joyless as a bell in a drowned church. "Oh, it burns. It burns fine. It just doesn't *die.* You'll feed it to the flames and in the morning it'll be folded in your pocket again, warm as a bird's egg, and it will have learned something new about you in the burning. That's how it works. That's how it's always worked." She stepped closer, and Mara caught the smell of her — woodsmoke and dried herbs and underneath it something faintly sweet, like fruit going over. "I held it once. Did Elder tell you that? Before he was Elder. When we still had names, I was the one who carried it across the boundary. I thought I was saving us. I thought the forest's bargain was mercy."

"What was it?"

"A hook." Her voice shook, and she steadied it with visible effort, the way you steady a table with a broken leg. "A hook baited with survival. The map led us here, girl. Do you understand? It didn't find us fleeing. It *led* us. Every step of the way, the roads redrew themselves to funnel us toward the treeline, toward the bargain, toward this—" she gestured with the stick at the lane, the houses, the smoke, the whole soft prison of it "—and by the time we understood what we'd traded, our names were already ink on its pages. It keeps us. All of us. Forty-one souls written into its margins like footnotes, and every stranger who carries it past our boundary adds another thread to the net."

Mara's hand had drifted, without her permission, to her coat. Beneath the wool, the map pulsed — slow, patient, content. Like something sleeping after a meal.

"If I burn it," she said slowly, "you said it learns something about me. Learns what?"

The old woman's gaze was pitiless and pitying at once, which Mara hadn't known eyes could manage. "What you love. What you fear. What you'd trade." She reached out and, before Mara could pull back, laid one finger against the coat, over the hidden fold of paper. The pulse beneath quickened — Mara felt it, a second heartbeat answering a touch — and the old woman snatched her hand back as if scalded.

"There," she whispered. "You feel that? It knows you already. Better than you know yourself. And somewhere in the deep of this forest, something wearing a crown just felt you touch it back." She looked past Mara, toward the darkening treeline, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Burn it. Burn it tonight. Because the map isn't leading you to the wolf, child. It's leading the wolf to you — and it has been since the moment a dying man bled on your floor."

Behind them, down the lane, every door in the village closed at once.

And in Mara's coat, warm against her ribs, the map turned a page.
