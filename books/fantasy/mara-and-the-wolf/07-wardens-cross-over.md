## 7. Wardens Cross Over

Bram Hartwell had spent three years learning the Hollow's weather, and he knew before the birds went quiet that something had crossed over.

It wasn't the silence itself. The forest fell silent often — when the moon turned her second face, when the wolf-king rode out with his antlered retinue, when something old and hungry moved through the deep pines where even the nameless villagers refused to gather firewood. Silence in the Hollow was a language, and Bram had learned to read its dialects. This one was different. This was the silence of held breath, of every small creature pressing itself flat against the earth because something *lawful* had entered a place that had no law.

He was mending a snare at the edge of the village when it happened — a loop of sinew between his fingers, his thoughts on Mara Vale, who was three days recovered from her crossing and still flinched whenever the map rustled in her satchel like a sleeping animal. The birds stopped mid-song. Not scattered. Stopped, all together, as if a hand had closed over the forest's throat.

Bram set down the snare and stood very still.

The air to the east had gone wrong. He couldn't have said how, not precisely — the light hadn't changed, the wind hadn't shifted — but there was a flatness to it, a pressed-and-smoothed quality, like a page that had been ironed. He knew that flatness. He had seen it once before, three years ago, in a warden's office in Corvane, in the space between a judge's gavel falling and the sentence being read aloud.

*Order*, that flatness said. *The law has arrived.*

"No," he said aloud, to no one. "No, no, no."

He ran.

The village watched him go — he felt their eyeless attention on his back, the villagers who had traded their names for safety turning their faces toward him in unison, the way they always did when the Hollow stirred. Old Sefa raised one hand as he passed, whether in warning or farewell he couldn't say. There was no time to ask. He crashed through the bracken, took the deer trail at a dead sprint, branches whipping his arms, his lungs burning with air that tasted of pine and cold stone and, beneath it now, faint but unmistakable, the smell of Corvane. Of lamp oil and wet cobblestones and the particular ash they burned in warden braziers. Home. The word rose in him unbidden and he hated it.

Three years of exile, and his body still called that city *home*.

He crested the ridge above the crossing-point — the thin place where the map had pulled Mara through, where the veil between worlds wore soft as old cloth — and looked down into the clearing, and his stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

They had come through in formation.

Six wardens in grey lacquered armor, moving with the drilled precision Bram remembered from the training yards, fanning out across the clearing in a pattern designed to cover each other's blind sides. Their halberds were drawn, and along each blade ran a faint silver script — writs of authority, literally inscribed on the metal, so that the weapons were not weapons but *judgments*. And at their center, standing in the trampled grass with her boots planted like she owned the ground beneath them, was Warden-Captain Sera Ilvane.

She hadn't changed. That was the first obscene thing about her. Three years, and Sera Ilvane hadn't changed at all — same close-cropped dark hair going grey at the temples, same lean frame under the captain's longcoat, same face that might have been handsome if it ever did anything other than hold itself in check. She stood surveying the Hollow's treeline the way she'd once surveyed crime scenes: cataloguing, assessing, already drafting charges against everything she saw.

In her left hand she carried an instrument Bram recognized, and recognizing it made his mouth go dry. A surveyor's compass. Warden-issue, brass-cased, its needle not pointing north but spinning slowly, tasting the magic in the air like a tongue.

Her right hand rested on the hilt of a sword that was not a sword. It was a *severance blade* — warden-forged, capable of cutting not flesh but connection. Cutting a binding. Cutting a life-thread. Cutting, if the stories were true, a soul loose from whatever anchored it.

"Sera," Bram said, before he could stop himself.

Her head came up. For a moment — just a moment — something crossed her face that wasn't the law. Something tired, and old, and almost human. Then it was gone, ironed flat like the air itself, and she was the Captain again.

"Hartwell." Her voice carried across the clearing without effort; she'd never needed to shout. "You're under arrest. Again. Though I confess the jurisdiction is... novel."

"You can't be here." He came down the slope slowly, hands visible, an old habit from a hundred searches. "This place doesn't run on Corvane's law. You bring your writs in here and they'll—"

"They'll what?" She gestured with the compass, and its needle swung toward the deep forest, toward the distant glint of silver antlers on the horizon, and steadied there. Pointing home. Pointing at the crown. "I've read your testimony, Hartwell. All of it. Every page you wrote before the tribunal, every claim you made about this realm, its king, its *wolf*. They locked it in the archive and called it the fever-dream of a disgraced man. But I believed you." She said it simply, without warmth. "That was my first mistake. My second was waiting three years for you to come back and fix it yourself."

"I was fixing it."

"Were you?" She stepped closer, boots crunching on frost-brittle grass. Up close he could see the new lines around her eyes, the ones that hadn't been there three years ago, and understood that she *had* changed after all — inwardly, invisibly, the way a river changes the land it cuts through. "Because from where I stand, you found the map, found the girl carrying it, and then walked her deeper into this forest instead of out of it. That's not fixing, Hartwell. That's *nesting*."

"The map isn't yours to destroy."

"Isn't it?" Something flickered behind her eyes. "A living artifact of forbidden cartography, bound by blood to an unlicensed charm-seller, drawing power from a breach between worlds that has already killed—" She stopped. The stopping was deliberate; he watched her do it, watched her fold something away behind her ribs the way she'd once folded away everything else. "The map dies, the breach seals. That's not my theory, Hartwell. That's the finding of the Arcanum Division, confirmed twice. Destroy the artifact, collapse the passage, and whatever's trapped on this side stays trapped here. Permanently contained."

"And the king?"

Sera was quiet for a moment. When she answered, her voice was level in the way that ice is level — perfectly, treacherously flat.

"What king?"

"The man underneath. Aldric. The one your *finding* would murder along with the beast wearing him."

"There is no man underneath." But her hand had tightened on the compass case, the knuckles whitening, and Bram seized on it the way a drowning man seizes driftwood. "There's a hostile entity wearing the shape of sovereignty. There's a breach bleeding corruption into both realms. And there are two criminals standing in front of me obstructing the remedy."

"Two?"

"Your charm-seller will be found." Sera turned away from him, surveying the treeline again, and her voice dropped into something almost gentle, which from Sera Ilvane was more frightening than any threat. "The compass tastes blood-bindings, Hartwell. It's been tasting hers since we crossed. She can run, but the map runs with her, and the map is singing to me like a kettle." She paused. "Give it to me. Give her up. Walk back through with me and testify, and I'll see her charged as a victim rather than an accomplice. You know how the tribunals work. Cooperation weighs."

"You exiled me," Bram said. "You stood in that courtroom and read the sentence yourself. You didn't believe me then."

"I believed you *then*." For the first time her composure cracked — not broke, cracked, a hairline fracture running through the iron. "That's what none of you understand. I believed every word. I argued for your mapping program. I fought the tribunal myself until they threatened my commission. And then I went home and I thought about what you'd described — a realm where a king devours himself year by year, where names are currency and memory is meat — and I realized that believing you changed nothing. Belief isn't a remedy, Hartwell. *Law* is a remedy. Law is the only thing standing between people and places like this." She lifted the compass, and the needle trembled toward the far silver gleam. "So yes. I exiled you. Because exile was the lawful sentence, and I serve the law even when the law is wrong. Especially then."

"That's not service. That's surrender with good posture."

Something that was almost a smile touched her mouth and died. "Still the cleverest man in every room. It got you exiled once." She turned and snapped two fingers, and the wardens began to move, spreading into the trees in pairs, halberds high, silver script catching the grey light. "Seal the clearing. Sweep pattern four. If the girl surfaces, take her alive — the Arcanum will want the binding examined before we burn it."

"Burn it," Bram repeated. "You mean destroy it while it's still tied to her veins. Do you know what happens to a blood-bound bearer when the binding breaks? Have any of your Arcanum clerks actually *tested* that?"

Silence. Just for a heartbeat. Just long enough.

"That's what I thought," Bram said quietly. "You're not bringing justice across the veil, Sera. You're bringing a fire, and you haven't asked who's standing in the house."

For a moment — one long, suspended moment — he saw her waver. Saw the crack widen. Saw three years of certainty shift on its foundation like a house settling. Her thumb moved on the compass case, once, twice, a gesture so small and so human that it hurt to watch.

Then a horn sounded in the trees. One of her wardens, signaling a find.

Sera Ilvane straightened, and the law closed over her face like water over a drowned thing.

"Take him," she said.

* * *

They bound his wrists with writ-chains — cold iron links inscribed with the same silver script as the halberds, so that the restraint was also a verdict, *guilty* pressed against his pulse — and marched him back toward the clearing. Bram went without fighting, because fighting six wardens was arithmetic, not courage, and because every step bought him time to think.

And he thought about Mara. Three days in the village, learning the rhythms of the nameless, and Bram had watched her do the thing she evidently did best: survive quietly, edges tucked in, making herself small and useful and unremarkable. She'd helped Sefa sort dried mushrooms. She'd mended a roof thatch. She'd learned six villagers' faces and carefully, pointedly, asked none of their names. She would have heard the horn. She would already be moving.

But the compass tasted her binding. He'd seen the needle swing. And Sera Ilvane did not lose things she tracked — he knew that better than anyone alive. He'd been her friend once, before the tribunal. Before the exile. Back when he'd still believed the law and the truth were the same animal.

The clearing again. The wardens regrouping around a kneeling figure at the treeline — and Bram's heart lurched, because the figure was small and dark-haired and had Mara's build, had Mara's satchel—

No. He squinted. Not Mara. One of the village children, a nameless girl of perhaps ten years, clutching the satchel she must have taken from wherever Mara had hidden it, holding it out to the wardens with the terrible fearless honesty of a child who has never been taught to lie to authority.

"Found this," the girl was saying. "It was warm."

Sera crouched beside her. Even from twenty paces, Bram saw the Captain's expression change as she opened the satchel and found it empty — saw the flatness ripple, saw her look at the child with something that was almost respect.

"A decoy," she said softly. "Clever." She rose, and her voice cut across the clearing like the severance blade itself. "Hartwell. Where is she?"

And Bram, wrists chained, guarded by six wardens, standing in a world where his testimony had once counted for nothing — Bram smiled.

"Gone," he said. "Deep. Where your compass can't follow and your writs don't reach. You should have asked me *before* you arrested me, Captain. You always did skip the part where you learn the terrain."

Sera studied him for a long moment. The needle of her compass was spinning now, wild and useless, and somewhere beyond the ridge, in the deep green heart of the Hollow, a wolf began to howl — low, and long, and unmistakably amused.

"It knows we're here," one of the wardens whispered.

"Yes," Sera said. She looked east, toward the distant antlers, and her hand tightened on the hilt of the severance blade. "It does. Set camp. Double the watch." And then, quieter, to Bram alone, almost tenderly: "You should have stayed exiled, Hartwell. Whatever's coming out of that forest tonight — I hope for your sake it kills us all quickly."

Above the treeline, the sun was setting, and the moon was rising, and it was beginning to show its second face.
