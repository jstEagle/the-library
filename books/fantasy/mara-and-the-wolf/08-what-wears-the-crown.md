## 8. What Wears the Crown

They climbed through the last hour of dark, and the forest let them.

Mara had learned to read the Hollow's moods the way she'd once read weather over Corvane's rooftops — by the angle of the roots, the hush or restlessness in the undergrowth, the way Bram's shoulders tightened before a path turned treacherous. This morning the trees leaned back from the trail like courtiers making way, and she didn't trust it. Nothing in this realm gave ground without wanting something.

"Nearly there," Bram said, for the third time since midnight. He hadn't slept. She'd watched him not sleeping, sitting upright against a beech trunk with his sketchbook open on his knees, drawing nothing, his pencil hovering over blank paper as though the lines he needed were too heavy to lift.

The trail bent eastward and began to climb, and the trees thinned, and then there was no forest at all — only a spine of grey rock rising out of the mist, and above it, a sky going pale at the seams.

Mara crested the ridge and stopped breathing.

Below her, cradled in a valley that should have held nothing but dark, lay a city.

It was beautiful. That was the first thing, the thing that caught in her throat before thought could catch up: it was *beautiful*, the way a snare is beautiful when you see it too late, all silver wire and patient geometry. Towers rose from the valley floor in spirals, white as bone bleached in sun, and every tower branched at its crown into great spreading arcs of silver — antlers, hundreds of them, thousands, reaching upward and outward until the whole city looked like a single enormous creature mid-transformation, half stone and half stag, caught forever between one shape and the next. The antlers caught the first light and threw it back in long cold lances, and where the light fell, the streets below gleamed like veins of mercury.

"It's called Vaelcoran," Bram said quietly. "The City of Antlers. I mapped it three years ago." He hesitated. "It wasn't silver then."

Mara couldn't answer. The map was awake against her chest.

She'd carried it folded inside her coat since the sewers, since the night it pulled her through, and she'd learned its rhythms the way you learn a heartbeat that isn't yours — the slow warm pulse of it, the way it shifted against her ribs when the road ahead mattered. Now it pressed against her sternum like a fist, like a hand reaching through her flesh to point, and beneath her breastbone she felt the pull: steady, insistent, tidal. Not dragging. *Hooking.* As if somewhere down in that silver sprawl a line had been cast, and the barb of it had found purchase in her, and something patient on the other end had begun, very gently, to reel.

"There," Bram said, and pointed.

At the city's heart stood a palace built around a single colossal antler-crown — a dome of branching silver so vast it made the towers around it look like saplings. Even at this distance, even in the pre-dawn grey, Mara could feel it watching. Not the palace. What sat inside it.

*The wolf remembers.*

The dying stranger's words rose in her unbidden, and with them came the map's heat, and with them — worse — something else. A feeling she had no honest name for. It was the feeling of standing outside a lit window on a winter street, looking in at a table set for people who are not you. It was homesickness pointed the wrong direction. It was the map's longing bleeding through the blood-bond into her own chest, wearing her loneliness like a stolen coat.

She hated it immediately. She knew loneliness intimately; she'd built a life inside it, brick by careful brick, and she did not need a dead man's map teaching her to want things.

"Mara." Bram's voice, careful. "You've gone white."

"I'm fine."

"You're gripping your coat hard enough to tear it."

She looked down. Her knuckles were bloodless, her fingers twisted in the wool over the place where the map lay. She made herself let go, finger by finger, the way she'd once made herself release a buyer's wrist when he'd tried to haggle a love-charm down to nothing. Control was a muscle. You didn't lose it all at once; you lost it an inch at a time, and you got it back the same way.

"What am I feeling?" she asked. She meant it as a question. It came out closer to an accusation.

Bram studied her face, then the city, then her face again. "That's the bond," he said. "The map chose you when the old man bled on it. And the map was drawn to serve the crown. So when you get close enough—" He made a small, helpless gesture at the valley. "It wants to go home. And it doesn't know the difference between what it wants and what you do."

"Wonderful," Mara said. "I'm possessed by stationery."

Something flickered across Bram's mouth — not quite a smile, but the ghost of where one might live, if he ever let one out. "You get used to it."

"I don't intend to get used to anything here." She turned back to the city, and the words died in her throat, because the sun had cleared the eastern ridge while they spoke, and Vaelcoran had caught fire with light.

Every antler blazed. The streets ran molten. The palace dome threw a corona across the valley floor, and in that light Mara could see details she'd missed: banners without heraldry hanging from the towers, long and pale as shed skins. Figures moving along the high walkways, small as ants, all of them converging toward the central dome like iron filings toward a lodestone. And around the city's edge, where the fields should have been, a ring of standing stones — no, not stones. She narrowed her eyes. Antlers again, driven into the earth like fence posts, each one taller than a man, forming a boundary as clean and absolute as a warden's cordon.

"A border," she said. "The whole city's walled itself off."

"That's new too." Bram had his sketchbook out now, working fast, his pencil whispering. "When I was here, the roads ran open in every direction. Anyone could come and go." His pencil stopped. "The wolf has pulled everything inward. Like a wound closing around something it swallowed."

Mara felt the pull strengthen as the light grew, a slow tightening behind her ribs, and she understood suddenly why the forest had let them climb so easily. It hadn't been courtesy. It had been delivery. The whole realm was a hand, and the ridge was the edge of the palm, and she and Bram were being curled gently, inevitably, toward the thing at the center.

*The wolf remembers.*

Remembers what? That was the question she'd been carrying since the stranger died with it in his mouth. Remembers the king it ate? Remembers the world outside? Remembers *her*?

The map pulsed, and for one vertiginous instant Mara saw through more than her own eyes.

It lasted less than a heartbeat. One moment she stood on the ridge in the cold morning wind; the next she was *also* elsewhere — high in a hall of branching silver, seated on a throne grown rather than built, looking out through eyes the color of winter rivers at a figure on a distant ridge. Small. Dark-haired. Standing very still with her hand pressed to her chest.

Herself.

The vision snapped shut like a trap. Mara staggered, caught herself on a boulder, and tasted copper. Her nose was bleeding freely, two bright threads dripping onto the grey stone.

"Mara!" Bram caught her arm. "What happened? What did you see?"

"He saw me." Her voice came out scraped. "He's sitting in that palace right now, and he looked up, and he *saw me*. Three miles away, through my own eyes, like I was—" She wiped her nose with the back of her hand and stared at the red smear. "Like I was a window someone opened."

Bram had gone very still. When she looked at him, his face wore an expression she recognized from her shop's worst days — the look of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't want to finish.

"The bond runs both ways," he said slowly. "I thought it might. The map binds you to the crown, but binding is a road, and roads carry traffic in both directions. If you can feel him—"

"Then he can feel me." Mara laughed, and it came out ugly. "Of course. Of course. I flee one city full of men who hunt me for what I carry, and I walk straight into the arms of the one creature in any world who can find me wherever I stand." She pulled the map out of her coat and held it up between two fingers, this folded scrap of breathing skin that had ruined her life in the space of a single evening. "I should have burned it in the sewer. I should have burned it the moment it started moving."

"No," Bram said, sharp and immediate, and then softer: "No. You don't understand what you're holding. That map is the only complete record of how the binding works. The old man carried it for thirty years because it's the only thing in existence that shows both the cage *and* the door. Destroy it, and whatever chance exists of undoing what's been done to that king dies with it." He paused. "And the man dies anyway. Slowly. Alone. Eaten a year at a time by something wearing his face."

"And if I keep it, I'm a lantern in the dark for a monster." She folded the map away, roughly, and the pull behind her sternum flared in protest, hot as a brand. "Some choice. Some *road*."

The sun climbed. Below them, Vaelcoran gleamed and glittered and went about its morning, and Mara forced herself to look at it properly, past the beauty, hunting the wrongness the way she'd once hunted a marked deck. It didn't take long. The city was too quiet. Even at this distance she should have heard it — markets, bells, carts, dogs, the thousand-voiced hum of ten thousand lives. Instead the silence rolled up the valley floor like fog, total and seamless, broken only once, faintly, by a sound she needed several seconds to identify.

Singing. A single voice, high and wordless, drifting from the central dome. It rose and fell in patterns that almost resolved into melody and never quite did, and every time it swelled, the pull behind Mara's ribs swelled with it, harmonizing, answering.

"They're singing to him," she whispered.

"Bells used to ring," Bram said. His pencil had stopped moving. "Three years ago, the city rang bells at dawn and dusk. I wrote it down. Someone has replaced the bells." He closed the sketchbook with a soft clap. "We can't stay on this ridge. If he's seen you once, he'll look again, and next time he'll look harder. We need cover, and we need a plan, and we need—" He exhaled. "We need masks. True-name masks, woven, if we want to walk into that city without the wards peeling us open like letters."

"Masks made of *what*?"

"Names. Real ones. Given up willingly." He wouldn't meet her eyes. "There are brokers in the foothills who deal in them. The Tallyman, they call the one I knew. Everything has a price with him, and the price is always the same currency."

Mara looked at the city one last time. The light had gone fully golden now, and Vaelcoran burned like a reliquary, gorgeous and sealed and silent, and at its heart the singing rose again — that almost-melody, that hunger dressed in harmony. Behind her sternum the map pulled, and pulled, and pulled, gentle as tide, patient as grief, and Mara understood with cold clarity that she was already halfway reeled in. Whatever she decided on this ridge, her feet had known their direction since the sewer.

*Beautiful,* she thought. *And wrong. And both at once, which is the most dangerous thing a thing can be.*

"All right," she said. "Find me your Tallyman."

She turned from the ridge, and behind her, faint across the miles of morning air, the singing stopped — mid-phrase, cleanly, like a head turning.

Then, after a moment, it began again. Softer now. Almost tender.

Almost like an answer.

* * *

They descended through woods that had stopped pretending to be indifferent. The trees watched them go — Mara could feel the attention of them, a pressure along her shoulder blades like sunlight through glass — and the paths, which had run straight and obliging toward the ridge, now wandered, doubled back, offered choices where no choices should be. Bram navigated by compass and stubbornness, muttering surveyor's curses under his breath, and Mara walked beside him with her hand flat against her coat, holding the map still through sheer force of will.

It didn't work, exactly. But it slowed the pulling. Enough.

"How far to this broker?" she asked, an hour down.

"Half a day, if the forest permits."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then longer, and less pleasantly."

She nodded and kept walking. The pull tugged at her with every step away from the city, a dull ache like a tooth remembering its cavity, and she catalogued it, measured it, filed it away with all the other costs she'd learned to carry. That was the one skill Corvane had given her that the Hollow couldn't touch: the ability to survive a price. To look at what a thing cost, and pay it, and keep walking, and never once let them see you count.

Behind them, far above, the antlered towers caught the noon light and threw it back in silver spears, and Mara did not look back.

She didn't need to. She could feel it — the whole shining weight of it, the throne, the wolf, the man beneath — resting against her sternum like a second heart.

Waiting for her to come home.
