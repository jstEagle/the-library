## 9. The Bargain Market

The road to the city was paved with teeth.

Not metaphorically — Mara had learned, in the Hollow, that very little was metaphorical. The causeway that wound up from the forest's edge toward the silver antlers on the horizon was surfaced in pale, curved shapes set into dark stone like mosaic, thousands upon thousands of them, worn smooth by centuries of feet. Bram walked ahead of her and did not look down. She made the mistake of looking down.

"Whose are they?" she asked, though a part of her already knew.

"Bargains," Bram said. "Every tooth in this road is a debt someone paid. Or thought they paid." He kept his eyes forward, his jaw tight. "The market doesn't take coin. Coin means nothing here. It takes what you *are*."

They had been walking since dawn, or what passed for dawn beneath the Hollow's bruise-colored sky, where light came filtered through a canopy of trees whose leaves were thin as parchment and rustled with writing no one could read. Mara had stopped asking questions somewhere around the third hour. Now she asked one anyway, because the alternative was to think about what she was about to do.

"And if we don't pay?"

Bram glanced back at her. He had a cartographer's face, she'd decided — all angles and measurement, a face built for squinting at horizons. But there was something underneath it, some sediment of guilt laid down over years, and it showed whenever he looked at her for too long. At the map. At the blood-bond she carried like a second pulse under her skin.

"Then the gates stay shut," he said. "And the wardens find us standing in the open with nowhere to hide. Sera can't be more than two days behind us." He paused. "Masks woven from true names. That's the toll. The city wards hunt identity the way a hawk hunts movement. Walk up to those gates as yourself, and every ward in Corvane's reach will know your name before your foot touches the threshold."

"So we walk in as someone else."

"We walk in as no one. There's a difference, and the Tallyman will explain it to you at length, because he charges by the explanation."

* * *

The Bargain Market sat in a valley folded between two hills that didn't quite agree on their own geography. From the ridge above, it looked like a wound in the land — a sprawl of tents and stalls and lantern-lit lanes, all of it arranged in spirals that seemed to tighten the longer you looked, drawing the eye inward toward a central pavilion of grey silk. The sound reached them first: not shouting, not haggling, but a low continuous murmur, like a congregation praying in a language made entirely of prices.

Bram stopped at the ridge's crest and turned to face her fully. "Last chance to turn around."

"You know we can't."

"I know." His mouth twisted. "I've done this before. Three years ago I stood on this exact spot and told myself I'd only trade something small." He looked out at the spiral of lights. "There's no such thing as something small, Mara. That's the first thing he'll teach you. Everything you are is load-bearing. Pull out one brick and you never know which wall comes down."

"What did you trade?"

For a long moment she thought he wouldn't answer. Then: "My sister's face. Not my memory of her — hers. I sold the way she looks, so that when I dream of her now, she's a shape with a voice. I couldn't tell you if she has freckles. I couldn't tell you if she's alive." He started down the slope. "Come on. The Tallyman hates waiting, and he charges interest."

* * *

Inside, the market was worse than outside, because inside it was beautiful.

Lanterns of colored glass swung overhead on wires strung between poles, casting pools of amber and violet and green across lanes crowded with vendors whose wares defied the word. One stall sold bottled footsteps — dozens of small vials, each holding the echo of a stride, and buyers held them to their ears with expressions of terrible longing. Another displayed a rack of first kisses, preserved in wax seals, each labeled in a delicate hand. A woman with silver thread sewn through her lips offered childhoods by the year, her fingers flicking through a ledger thick as a tombstone.

No one shouted their prices. No one bargained aloud. Deals happened in murmurs, in the press of a palm against a scale, in the slow nod that followed. And everywhere, threaded through the crowd like minnows through kelp, moved the brokers — pale, precise people with ink-stained fingers and ledgers chained to their belts, who watched the shoppers the way moneylenders watch drowning men.

Mara felt the map shift against her ribs where she'd bound it beneath her coat. It had been restless all day, tugging faintly toward the city, toward the crowned figure at its center, and now it pulled harder, as though the market itself were a current feeding into that greater draw. She pressed her hand flat over it. *Not yet. First the mask. First survival.*

"The central pavilion," Bram murmured. "Stay close. Don't sign anything. Don't say your name — your real one — even in passing. And whatever he offers you first, refuse it. The first offer is always bait."

The pavilion's grey silk walls glowed from within like paper around a candle. Its entrance was hung with beads of black glass, and passing through them sounded like stepping through rain. Inside, the noise of the market fell away entirely, replaced by a silence so complete Mara could hear her own blood moving.

The Tallyman sat behind a desk of black wood at the pavilion's center, and he was nothing like she'd expected. She had imagined something monstrous. Instead he was small and neat and elderly, with spectacles perched on a nose like a knotted root, dressed in a clerk's sober brown, his white hair combed with care. Before him lay an abacus whose beads were tiny spheres of glass, and inside each sphere floated a fleck of light that pulsed like a heartbeat.

He looked up and smiled, warm as a grandfather.

"Mara Vale," he said.

Her whole body went cold. Beside her, Bram stiffened.

"Ah — no," the Tallyman said quickly, raising a hand. "Forgive me. Not your true name. I would never be so crude, and besides—" he tapped the abacus, and one glass bead chimed, "—the map would object. I simply mean the name you wear. The name you sell charms under. The name your mother gave you when you were small enough to be carried." He removed his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve. "Names have layers, child. Like onions. Like wounds. Sit, please. Both of you. Bram Hartwell — welcome back. Your account remains open. Interest accrues."

"I'm not here for me," Bram said.

"No one ever is." The Tallyman gestured again, and two chairs slid across the floor unbidden, stopping precisely behind their knees. "You're here for masks. Two masks, woven from true names, sufficient to pass the Silver Court's wards. Yes, yes, I know what you need before you say it; anticipation is half my craft." He laced his ink-stained fingers. "Now. The question is payment."

"I have coin," Mara said. "From Corvane. Real minted—"

"Paper," the Tallyman said gently, without malice, the way one corrects a child reaching for a hot stove. "Pretty paper. It burns well, I grant you. Here, value is measured differently." He drew from his desk drawer a single object: a small brass scale, old and dented, its pans hanging from chains fine as hair. "We weigh what you are. Memories. Years. Fears. Loves. Regrets. Talents. The color of a morning you remember. Your mother's voice saying your name." His eyes, behind the restored spectacles, were pale and clear and utterly without cruelty, which was somehow the worst thing about him. "Everyone who walks through those beads carries a fortune and thinks themselves poor. You are not poor, Mara Vale. You are merely unspent."

"I want to know the price first," she said. "Before anything. What do two masks cost?"

"A question I cannot answer honestly until I know what you're carrying." He spread his hands. "A memory of a stranger weighs less than a memory of a mother. A fear of spiders weighs almost nothing; a fear of being forgotten can buy a house. Grief, child, is the densest substance in any world. It holds its shape. It resists time. Brokers fight over grief the way merchants elsewhere fight over gold."

Bram shifted beside her. "What did you offer him?" he asked quietly. "Three years ago. When you said—"

"I offered him a choice," the Tallyman said, "and he chose badly, and he has regretted it with admirable consistency ever since, which is why his account stays open rather than closed." He turned back to Mara, and his voice softened into something almost kind. "But that is his ledger. Let us open yours."

* * *

It worked like this: she placed her hand on the left pan of the brass scale, and the Tallyman touched the right, and memories rose in her like fish surfacing in a pond — visible, distinct, each one catching light.

She saw her mother's kitchen. The chipped blue bowl where the bread dough rose under a cloth. The smell of yeast and woodsmoke. Her mother humming off-key, flour to her elbows, turning to smile at seven-year-old Mara with flour dusted on her cheekbone like a beauty mark.

"There," the Tallyman said softly. "That one. The kitchen. The bowl. The humming. Do you know how rare it is, a whole memory intact? Most people's mothers have been traded away piecemeal by the time they reach me — the voice gone here, the hands gone there, until all that's left is a fact instead of a person." He weighed something invisible in his palm. "This one is heavy. This one is worth much."

"It's mine," Mara said. Her voice came out flat and strange, as if someone else were speaking through her. "I'm not selling it."

"No. Not yet. We're only looking." He moved his hand, and the memory sank, and another rose: her mother's hands braiding her hair by the fire, the tug and release, the low murmur of a lullaby whose words Mara realized, with a jolt, she could no longer fully recall — the middle verse had gone soft and blurred, worn away by years of not needing it.

"That one's already damaged," the Tallyman observed, not unkindly. "Time got there before I did. I'd pay poorly for it. But the kitchen — the kitchen is pristine."

"Something else," Bram said. "Take something else. Take a fear, take a—"

"Her fears are load-bearing," the Tallyman said, without looking at him. "Strip a woman's fears and she walks into the Silver Court bold as a general, and generals die there. No. If she wants a mask strong enough to fool wards woven by the wolf-king's own charters, she must pay in love. There is no cheaper currency that works. I don't make the exchange rates, Master Hartwell. I only keep them."

The silence stretched. In it, Mara became aware of the map against her ribs, pulsing slowly, patiently, like a heart waiting for her to finish this errand and return to its purpose. *The wolf remembers,* the stranger had whispered. *The wolf remembers.* Everyone wanted something from her — the map, the bond, the choice she hadn't yet been asked to make. The Tallyman was only the first to name his price out loud.

"If I sell the kitchen," she said slowly, "what exactly do I lose? Do I forget her?"

"You forget the room. The bowl. The humming. The smell of yeast and woodsmoke." He ticked each item off on his ink-stained fingers. "You keep the fact of her. You keep that she existed, that she loved you, that she died — I assume she died; grief this dense usually means death — you keep that you loved her. You keep everything that can be written in a ledger. What you lose is everything that can't." He leaned forward. "You will know your mother loved you the way you know Corvane outlawed magic: as information. True, useful, and cold as river stone. And on winter nights, when you try to remember what loving her *felt* like, you will find a clean, smooth absence, shaped exactly like a kitchen, and you will press your hand against it, and it will give you nothing back."

"That's monstrous," Bram said.

"That's honest," the Tallyman replied. "Monstrous would be lying to her. Monstrous is what the other brokers do — 'just a sliver, just the edges, you'll barely miss it' — and then the customer leaves missing the center of herself and blaming everyone but the knife." He opened a ledger to a fresh page. "So. The kitchen. The bowl. The humming. For two masks, woven strong, plus safe passage past the outer wards. Final offer, and I warn you, child, my offers curdle. Come back tomorrow and the price rises, because tomorrow you'll be more desperate, and desperation is also a commodity."

Mara looked at the scale. At the glass beads of the abacus, each one holding someone's fleck of light, someone's surrendered morning. She thought of the village without names, of old women who had bought safety with themselves and could neither leave nor die. She thought of Bram's sister, a shape with a voice.

*Everything you are is load-bearing.*

But the alternative was Sera Ilvane's warrant, and the wardens' iron, and the end of every path except the one the map demanded. Survival had always been her trade. She had spent ten years selling fake charms to desperate people, telling herself the lies were harmless, telling herself the fear in their eyes wasn't her doing. She knew exactly what it cost to stay alive in Corvane. She had simply never before been asked to pay in the only coin she'd sworn never to touch.

"When," she heard herself ask, "would I feel it? The loss?"

"Immediately. Completely. Grief, once weighed, does not return." The Tallyman dipped a pen. "Well?"

She thought of her mother's hands in her hair. The tug and release. The lullaby's missing middle verse — at least that was already lost, already gone to time; she was only losing what remained, and what remained was a room, a bowl, a tune hummed off-key—

No. That was the lie brokers told. *Just the edges.* It was the whole kitchen. It was the last place her mother had been alive in her memory, and she was about to sell it to a man with an abacus so she could walk through a gate wearing a dead woman's name as a disguise.

"Do it," Mara said.

"Mara—" Bram started.

"You said it yourself. Load-bearing. So I choose which wall comes down." She put her hand on the pan. It was cold, colder than brass should be. "Do it before I'm wise enough to stop."

The Tallyman wrote three lines in his ledger, unhurried, beautiful script. Then he reached across the desk and covered her hand with his own, and his fingers were dry and light as moth wings.

"Thank you for your custom," he said.

* * *

The loss came like a tide going out.

One moment the kitchen was there — the blue bowl, the flour, the humming, the warmth of the oven door against her hip as a child — and then the water withdrew and left behind a smooth, swept expanse where the room had been. She gasped. Her hand flew to her sternum as though she could hold the shape in, press it back like a wound. But there was nothing to press. Only the clean absence, exactly as he'd promised: perfectly formed, perfectly empty, shaped like a kitchen.

She still knew her mother loved her. She knew it the way she knew the capital punishment for charm-selling. Information. Cold as river stone.

"Mara." Bram's hand on her shoulder. His voice rough. "Look at me. What's your mother's name?"

"Ede," she said. "Ede Vale. She raised me alone. She died when I was nineteen. Fever." Each fact arrived intact, correct, useless. "She used to—" She stopped. Used to *what*? There had been something. Something in a room. Something warm. She pressed against the absence and it gave her nothing back, and the nothing was so precisely shaped, so exactly kitchen-sized, that she began to shake.

"Enough," Bram snapped at the Tallyman. "It's done. Give us the masks."

"In a moment." The Tallyman was already working, drawing threads from the air — from *her*, she realized, from the space where the memory had been, spinning the withdrawn stuff into gossamer strands that caught the lantern light. His fingers moved with a weaver's speed, and the strands began to knot themselves into shapes: two faces, blank and waiting. "A gift, since you paid full price and paid in the heaviest coin. Ask me one question, freely given, and I'll answer truly. It's policy. Even brokers fear the ledger's audit."

Mara wiped her face with the heel of her hand. Her cheeks were wet. She hadn't noticed crying. "Why does the map need me? Why blood-bind it to a charm-seller? Of all the people in all the worlds — why me?"

The Tallyman's hands went still. For the first time, the grandfatherly warmth drained out of his face, leaving something older underneath, something careful.

"Because," he said, "the map doesn't choose bearers, child. It chooses debts. And yours—" he nodded at the hidden weight against her ribs, "—is the oldest account in my book. Someone paid dearly to put that map in your hands, and the payment specified *you*. I'd ask who Ede Vale really was, if I were you. Before the wolf asks first."

The masks settled into his palm, finished, weightless, two blank faces of woven light.

And Mara stood in the silence of the pavilion, holding a kitchen-shaped hole where her heart used to keep a room, and understood that she had just learned the market's true lesson: survival was not the price. Survival was the *down payment*.
