## 10. Court of Antlers

The masks itched.

Bram had told Mara they would be woven from true names — that was the price the broker demanded, and the Tallyman's price was never small — but knowing a thing and wearing it were different creatures entirely. The mask sat against his face like a second skin grown from someone else's memory: cool at first, then warm, then faintly pulsing in time with his heartbeat, as though it were learning to be him. Or as though he were learning to be it. He could not decide which thought frightened him more.

Through the eye-holes of the mask, the world came silvered. The road to the capital unspooled ahead of them through forest that grew stranger with every mile — trees whose bark had been polished to a mirror shine by centuries of wind, each one reflecting not the traveler who passed but someone else entirely, some other walker on some other road, in some other year. Bram kept his eyes forward. He had made that mistake once before, three years ago, on his first and only approach to the Silver Court. He had seen himself at nineteen, laughing, ink-stained, unburdened, and he had wept for an hour afterward without fully understanding why.

Beside him, Mara walked with her hood up and her chin down, the map hidden beneath her cloak where it pressed against her ribs like a second heart. He could tell she felt it. She had been feeling it since the ridge, since the city first came into view — a hook set behind her sternum, reeling her in by inches. He saw it in the way her steps quickened when she wasn't paying attention, and how she caught herself, and slowed, and then quickened again.

"You can fight it," he said quietly. "But don't fight it here. Not on the road. The road notices."

"I noticed," she said. Her voice was flat, controlled, the voice of a woman who had spent years selling lies to desperate people and knew exactly what desperation sounded like from the inside. "It's like being remembered by something I've never met."

"That's precisely what it is." Bram adjusted his mask, though adjusting did nothing. "The Hollow doesn't forget anyone who enters it. That's its cruelty and its mercy both. Out there—" he tipped his head back toward Corvane, days behind them now, "—you can vanish and no one will ever know you existed. In here, you can't."

Ahead, the forest opened like a held breath released.

* * *

The city of silver antlers rose out of a valley of white grass, and no description Bram had ever attempted had done it justice, so he did not attempt one now. He simply let it strike him again, the way it had struck him at twenty-six, when he had come as a cartographer with royal warrant and imperial ambition, certain that any realm could be reduced to lines on vellum if only the hand were steady enough.

The towers were grown, not built — antlers of pale bone-silver branching upward from the earth, interlocking overhead into vaults and spires, hung with lanterns that burned a color Bram had no name for except *almost-gold*. Between them, streets of packed white stone wound downward toward a great hall at the valley's heart, and everything — every wall, every archway, every gutter — was carved with wolves. Wolves running, wolves sleeping, wolves howling at moons that had been chiseled into the stone and then, in places, deliberately defaced. He remembered noticing that on his first visit too. Someone had hated the moon here, long ago. Or loved it too much.

"It's beautiful," Mara said, and her voice cracked slightly on the word, and he understood why. It was beautiful. It was also wrong, the way a perfect portrait of a dead man is wrong — accurate in every line and hollow at the center. The streets were full of people, and the people moved gracefully, and they smiled at one another, and there was no sound. No market-cry, no laughter, no footfall. The whole city murmured at the edge of hearing like a conversation in the next room.

"Don't speak above a whisper past the gates," Bram said. "Sound is a kind of name here. Spend it carefully."

"And what do we do if someone asks us ours?"

"Then we've already failed, and the rest is just deciding how to die."

She glanced at him sidelong. Even through the mask he could feel the look — dry, unimpressed, the look of a woman who had survived warden raids in Corvane by being harder than everyone around her. "You're very comforting, Hartwell."

"I'm honest. Comfort is a different trade."

At the gates — two antlers crossed like swords, wide enough to drive a cart between — stood guards in armor of woven shadow, and they did not ask names. They asked nothing at all. They simply looked at the masks, and whatever they saw there satisfied them, and the crowd carried Bram and Mara inward like a slow river.

* * *

The great hall at the city's heart had been a cathedral once. Bram was sure of it. The proportions were wrong for anything else — the ceiling lost in antlered gloom, the windows tall and narrow and filled with glass the color of deep water. But the pews had been pulled apart and rearranged into concentric rings, a court in the round, and where an altar should have stood there was a throne.

The throne was made of the same silver-bone as the towers, and it was occupied.

Bram had seen the wolf-king once before, from across this same hall, three years ago, on the day his mapping was declared heresy and his exile pronounced. He had been too far away then, and too afraid, to see clearly. Now the court had drawn them close — the masks opened doors, opened ranks, opened the rings of seated figures until Bram and Mara stood perhaps thirty feet from the throne, close enough to see everything.

Close enough to see that the king was weeping.

He sat with one hand draped over the armrest and the other pressed flat against his own chest, as though holding something in, or holding something together. His body was a man's body, tall and spare, dressed in robes of grey that might have been mist given tailoring. His head was a wolf's head — not the snarling monster of Corvane's forbidden woodcuts, but something older and sadder: muzzle long and fine-boned, ears pricked forward, eyes of a gold so pale it was nearly white. And from those eyes, even across the distance, even through the mask, tears ran freely down the fur and dripped from the jaw onto the floor, where they did not splash but sank, absorbed into the white stone as though the city itself were drinking its king's grief.

"He's crying," Mara whispered.

"Watch," Bram said. "And count."

* * *

The court proceeded around them, and Bram watched Mara watch it, and he watched the king.

A petitioner approached — a woman in a dress of woven reeds, carrying a bundle wrapped in cloth. She knelt. She spoke, and though her words did not carry, the shape of them was plain: a plea, a grief, an offering. The wolf-king listened. His ears turned toward her with terrible attentiveness, the way a dying man listens to a doctor's verdict. Then he raised one hand — a man's hand, elegant, human, emerging from a sleeve of grey — and touched two fingers to his own brow, and inclined his head.

The woman bowed lower, and when she rose, she was smiling, and she walked away lighter than she had come, and Bram felt his stomach turn, because he knew what had just happened. He had charted it. The king had taken something from her — a memory, a sorrow, a weight — and she would walk home tonight unable to remember what she had come to mourn, and she would call that mercy, because from the outside it looked exactly like mercy.

"That's what he does," Bram murmured. "That's the whole kingdom. You bring him your worst memory and he eats it, and you leave happy, and the realm stays fed, and nobody asks what happens to the memories after."

"Where do they go?"

"Nowhere good. Look at him."

Mara looked. And Bram watched her see it — the flicker.

It came in the middle of the next petition, a farmer kneeling with a dead lamb in his arms. The wolf-king reached out to take the animal's death, and mid-reach, the hand hesitated. Trembled. The great head dipped, and for three heartbeats — Bram counted, he always counted — the posture changed. The shoulders drew in. The head lifted differently, smaller, the way a man lifts his head when he has just realized where he is and cannot remember how he arrived. The pale eyes went wide, and human, and horrified, and they swept the hall as though searching for someone, anyone, who could explain—

And then the wolf closed over the man like water over a dropped stone, and the hand completed its motion, and the lamb's death passed into the crown, and the farmer left smiling.

Mara's breath had gone ragged beside him. "Did you—"

"Yes."

"There's someone in there."

"There's always been someone in there." Bram kept his voice below even a whisper, a murmur shaped more by breath than sound. "Three years ago I mapped this court. I charted the flickers the way you'd chart tides. They're getting longer. Whatever's left of him under all that fur, it surfaces more often now, and each time it does, the wolf pushes it down harder. It's drowning him slowly, or he's drowning it. From in here, I genuinely cannot tell which."

"The true king," Mara said. "Aldric."

"You remember the name?"

"The map showed me. Before we crossed over." She said it lightly, too lightly, and Bram let it go. There were things she hadn't told him yet. There were things he hadn't told her. That was the shape trust took between two people who had learned to survive alone: a shared silence, carefully maintained, each waiting to see if the other would break it first.

On the throne, the wolf-king's gaze began to move along the rings of the court. Slowly. Methodically. A sovereign reviewing his subjects — or a creature hunting for something specific.

* * *

It found them.

There was no other way to describe it. The pale gold eyes traveled the concentric circles, passed over dozens of masked faces, and stopped — stopped with the suddenness of a compass needle finding north — on Mara.

Bram felt her stiffen. Felt the map beneath her cloak respond, a heat he could sense from a pace away, as though she carried a coal against her ribs. The pull he had warned her about on the road, the hook behind the sternum — he could see it working now, see her lean a half-inch toward the throne against her own will, and correct, and lean again.

The wolf-king rose.

The court rose with him, hundreds of figures unfolding in silence, and Bram sank into a bow and dragged Mara down with him by the sleeve. Above them, footsteps — soft, deliberate, the gait of something that had learned to walk like a man and never quite stopped finding it strange — descended from the dais. They came closer. Closer. Stopped directly before them.

"Look up," said the wolf-king.

The voice was the worst part. Bram had prepared himself for the voice three years ago and failed then too. It was a beautiful voice — low, resonant, cultured, the voice of a man educated in courts and softened by centuries of courtesy — coming from a wolf's throat, and the dissonance of it did something to the spine that fear alone could not do. It was the sound of a locked room. Of something kept.

They looked up.

Up close, the king's face was a landscape of contradictions. The muzzle was beast; the expression was not. The pale eyes held a weariness so vast it had gone past sorrow into something almost gentle, the way a flood goes gentle when it has drowned everything there is to drown. Tears still tracked the fur, though the king seemed entirely unaware of them, the way a man becomes unaware of rain.

"You bring a map into my court," the king said to Mara, and his gaze dropped to her cloak, to the place where the living ink beat against her ribs like a trapped bird. "You think I cannot smell it? Three years it has wandered my borders, and now it walks into my hall wearing your heartbeat." The great head tilted. One ear swiveled. "I have been looking for it for a very long time."

"We're travelers," Bram said. "Pilgrims. We came to see the court."

"You came because it called you." The king's attention shifted to Bram, and Bram felt the regard of those eyes like cold water poured down his collar. "Both of you. It calls, and you answer, and you tell yourselves it was your own idea. It was mine too, once. Everything in this hall answered a call, once." He gestured, one elegant hand taking in the silent, smiling, hollow-eyed court. "Ask any of them what they were before they arrived. Ask them kindly. Watch their faces while they try."

"Your Majesty," Mara said, and her voice was steady, and Bram marveled at it, because his own had gone to dust somewhere in the last minute. "We didn't mean to intrude."

"Intrude." The wolf-king tasted the word. Something moved behind his eyes — the flicker, rising early, rising fast — and for a moment the shoulders hunched, and the hands curled inward, and the voice that came out was thinner, younger, cracked with panic rather than melancholy:

"—where is she, where is my sister, someone must know where she—"

The wolf slammed down over the man like a portcullis. The king straightened. Smoothed his robes with one hand, an oddly human gesture, absurdly domestic. When he spoke again, the beauty and the sadness had returned, seamless, as though the interruption had been a trick of the light.

"You see my difficulty," he said mildly. "My court requires tending. My realm requires feeding. And I am so very tired, little pilgrim, and your map is the only thing in three hundred years that has ever promised me an ending." He extended his hand — palm up, an invitation, a supplication, a trap; Bram could not tell which, and suspected the king could not either. "Stay. Both of you. Be guests of the Silver Court. There are rooms in the eastern tower that have waited a long time for occupants. And perhaps, in a day, or a week, when you have rested—" the pale eyes fixed on Mara, and the hunger in them was so old and so patient that it looked, from the outside, exactly like loneliness, "—you will show me what you carry. Freely. No one in my realm is ever compelled. Compulsion is the one law I have left."

Around them, the entire court had turned to watch. Hundreds of masked faces, silent, smiling, waiting to see what the strangers would do.

Bram's mind raced through exits and found none. Refusal, here, in front of the whole court, would be a kind of suicide. Acceptance was a slower one. And beneath the calculation, beneath the cartographer's habit of measuring every room for its dangers, something else stirred in him — pity, unwelcome and enormous, for the flickering thing on the throne that wept without knowing it and begged, in its stolen seconds of humanity, for a sister no one would name.

Mara's hand found Bram's sleeve. Her grip said: *say yes*. Her grip also said: *and then get us out of this*.

"We would be honored," she said.

The wolf-king smiled. It was a wolf's smile, all teeth, and it was also, unmistakably, a relieved one.

"Splendid," he said. "The eastern tower. And little pilgrim—" he leaned close, and his breath smelled of snow and old paper, of libraries buried under ice, "—do sleep with the map where you can see it. Things that breathe grow restless in the dark. I would hate for it to wander off before we've properly met."

He withdrew. The court bowed as one. And as the king ascended back to his throne of silver bone, Bram caught the last flicker of the day — a single heartbeat, no more — in which the pale eyes found Mara's again across the hall, and the face behind them was a young man's face, terrified and pleading, mouthing two silent words before the wolf closed over him forever:

*Help me.*

* * *

The eastern tower rooms were beautiful, warm, and windowless, and the door locked from the outside with a sound like a book closing.

Bram stood with his ear against the wood for a long time, listening to the footsteps of their unseen hosts recede down the spiral stair. Behind him, Mara had drawn out the map at last, and it lay glowing on the table between them, its ink crawling, its crowned figure at the center burning brighter than he had ever seen it — brighter, he realized, than it had ever burned in the presence of the throne itself.

Because it wasn't burning for the throne.

It was burning for the man beneath it.

"Bram," Mara said quietly. "When he flickered. The last time. Did you see—"

"I saw."

"He mouthed something."

"I know what he mouthed." Bram turned from the door. Outside, somewhere far below, the almost-gold lanterns of the Silver Court were being dimmed one by one, and the city settled into its soundless sleep, and beneath the floorboards, beneath the foundations, beneath three hundred years of eaten memories, something vast turned over in its cage and began, patiently, to plan.

"He didn't say *help me*, Mara." Bram met her eyes across the lamplit table. "He said *help it*."
