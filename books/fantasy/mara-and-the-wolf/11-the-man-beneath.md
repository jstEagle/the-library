## 11. The Man Beneath

The servant came for her at the hour when the silver court grew quietest — that strange, breathless lull between the last of the evening revels and the first pale stirrings of the antlered dawn. Mara had learned to mark it by sound: the way the music in the great hall thinned to a single sustained note, then stopped altogether, as though the whole city of Corvane's dark mirror had drawn one long inhale and forgotten to let it go.

She was pretending to sleep. She had gotten good at pretending, these three days in the Court of Antlers — good at wearing the mask woven from a stranger's true name, good at walking with her chin tilted at exactly the angle the courtiers used, good at not flinching when the wolf-king's gaze slid over her and lingered, patient as winter, on the shape of the map hidden against her ribs.

A soft knock. Not on the door — on the wall beside the door. Three taps, a pause, two more.

Mara rose without lighting a candle. In the Hollow, light was a language, and she had learned not to speak unless spoken to.

The corridor outside was dim as the inside of a closed hand. The servant stood waiting, a slight figure in grey livery, face shadowed beneath a hood that all the palace servants wore — though Mara had begun to suspect the hoods were less about modesty than about mercy. It was easier to serve a wolf-king if you never quite saw his face.

"You're the one who asked about the old rooms," the servant said. Voice low, sexless, careful. "The chambers below the east wing. You asked a gardener where they'd gone."

"I asked if there *were* any."

"And she told you no." A pause. "She should not have answered at all. But she is old, and old people in this place forget which silences keep them alive." The hood turned toward the stairwell at the corridor's end. "Come. We have until the second bell. After that, the wards wake, and I cannot be responsible for what they find."

"Why are you doing this?" Mara kept her voice as flat as she could manage. Trust was a currency she'd stopped carrying; the Tallyman had taught her its exchange rate, and Sera Ilvane had taught her how quickly it could be stolen.

The servant was silent long enough that Mara thought the question would go unanswered. Then: "Because I carried water to him for eleven years. Because everyone else who remembers him is dead or worse than dead. And because you looked at the king yesterday the way no one looks at him — like you were trying to see *through*." The hood dipped. "You want to know what's underneath. So do I. Come."

* * *

They went down.

Down past the kitchens with their fires banked to embers, down a servants' stair worn hollow in its center by centuries of feet, down into a part of the palace where the silver antler-architecture gave way to something older — rough stone, unornamented, the bones beneath the beautiful skin. The air changed too. Upstairs the Hollow's air tasted of cold iron and crushed flowers, sweet and wrong. Here it simply tasted of dust, and time, and something faintly green, like a garden shut up in a chest.

"The king doesn't come here," the servant said, lifting a lantern whose flame burned a color Mara had no name for — not gold, not blue, but the grey-white of moonlight through fog. "He *can't*. There is a rule older than he is, older than the crown even. What a devourer eats, it must not look upon again. He ate this man. To walk these halls would be to taste him twice, and the binding—" the servant's voice caught, steadied, "—the binding does not permit it. So the rooms were sealed. Forgotten. Officially, they do not exist."

"Unofficially?"

"Unofficially, someone has to bring him water."

The stair ended at a door. It was small, human-scaled, made of plain oak gone soft with age, and it had no lock — only a latch of cold iron, and above it, carved so shallowly it might have been an accident of the grain, a single word:

*ALDRIC.*

Mara's breath went out of her. She hadn't realized she'd been holding it since the stairwell.

"That's his name," she said. "His real name. Everyone here trades names away, hides them, burns them — and his is just sitting on a door."

"It is the safest name in the Hollow," the servant said quietly. "Because it belongs to no one anymore. A name only has power over the living." The lantern trembled slightly. "Open it yourself. He should see your face first, not mine."

* * *

The chamber beyond was nothing like a prison.

That was the first thing Mara understood, standing on the threshold with her heart hammering against the hidden map. It was nothing like a prison, and that was somehow infinitely worse. It was a bedroom — a gentle, beloved, carefully tended bedroom, the kind a person keeps out of love rather than necessity. A bed with quilts faded to the ghost-colors of their original dyes. A writing desk with a chair pushed back at the angle of a man who meant to return. Shelves of books with cracked spines. A window — an actual window, though beyond its glass there was only stone, the room having been swallowed by newer construction decades ago, so that the view was of the inside of the palace's own walls, a rectangle of grey brick that never changed.

And in the chair by the dead window sat a man.

He was pressed thin. That was the phrase that came to Mara and would not leave — pressed, the way flowers are pressed, laid flat between the pages of years until all the moisture went out of them and what remained was the shape of the thing, perfect and papery and still. Aldric had been a tall man once; she could see it in the length of his folded hands, the long line of him even collapsed into the chair. His hair had been dark. Now it was the color of ash that has settled. His skin was translucent at the edges, and where the lantern light passed through his fingers she could see the faint architecture of bone, like leaves held up to the sun.

But his eyes were open. And his eyes were alive.

"You're not supposed to be here," he said. His voice was dry as the turning of a page, but there was warmth buried in it, deep down, the way embers live under ash. "No one is supposed to be here. That's rather the point of me."

"I'm sorry," Mara said, because she couldn't think of anything else.

"Don't be. Eleven years of water-carriers, and every one of them looked at the floor. You're looking at my face." Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, but the memory of where a smile used to live. "I'd forgotten what that was like. Being seen. Sit down, please. The chair by the desk. Mind the third floorboard; it complains."

Mara sat. Her hands would not stay still in her lap, so she pressed them flat against her knees. Up close, Aldric smelled faintly of the green thing in the air — dried lavender, she realized. Someone had hung bundles of it from the bedframe, brown and brittle, replaced so many times that the nails holding the strings had left little rusted stars in the wood.

"Eleven years," she said. "You've been here eleven years."

"Has it been? I lose count. Time moves strangely when you're mostly memory yourself." He studied her with those living eyes, and she felt the strange sensation of being read — not the wolf-king's hungry reading, but something gentler, a man cataloguing a visitor the way he might once have catalogued a petition or a poem. "You're not from here. Your mask is good work, but you wear it like a coat, not like skin. And there's ink on your sleeve. Cartographer's ink, or I'm a turnip."

"It's not mine. I'm... carrying it. For someone."

"For the map." His eyes flickered — and for one vertiginous instant Mara saw something move behind them, vast and dark and crowned, a shadow passing behind frosted glass. Then it was gone, and he was just a tired man in a chair. "Ah. Yes. I can feel it, you know. When the map comes close. Like hearing your own heartbeat from outside your chest." He laughed softly, and the laugh crumbled halfway through. "Forgive me. It's been a long time since I had a reason to be nervous."

* * *

He talked. That was the thing Mara had not expected — that he would talk, freely, hungrily, a man who had been saving up words for eleven years and could finally spend them.

He told her about the kingdom before. Not the Hollow — the kingdom, real and sunlit, before the first cartographer drew his terrible lines. He had been a younger son, never meant for the crown, and he spoke of his brother — *the king*, he always said, with a reverence that had outlasted everything else — with such tenderness that Mara had to look away at the dead window to keep her composure.

"He was better than me in every way that mattered. Braver. Kinder. He remembered the names of every servant in the palace, every farmer who sent tribute, every child born in the outer villages. He said a king who forgets names has already lost his kingdom, whatever the maps say." Aldric's hands unfolded, refolded. "Ironic, isn't it. That I'm the one who's left. The one who remembers."

"What happened?" Mara asked, though the map had shown her fragments, and Bram had told her more, and she suspected she already knew. She asked anyway, because some things needed to be said aloud in the room where they had happened.

"The wolf came first. Before the crown, before the binding. It was old — older than the kingdom, older than the forest. It had kept the borders of this place since before there were borders, holding back the nothing that lives beyond the maps. And it was starving. The land was changing; the old feeding grounds were being plowed, fenced, named. A guardian can't eat gratitude forever." His voice stayed level, but his translucent hands had gone very still. "My brother went out to meet it. Alone, because that was the kind of fool he was. And he came back with a bargain half-made and a sickness in him, and the first cartographer — the man whose map you carry — he said he could fix it. He said he could bind the hunger into the crown, cage the wolf inside the king's shape, and everyone would be safe."

"And instead—"

"Instead the cage became a feast. The wolf wears my brother's shape and eats his memories, one year each year, and every year it grows more of him and less of itself, and the realm grows hungrier around them both, because a binding built on starvation feeds on starvation." Aldric smiled, and it was the saddest thing Mara had ever seen. "I tried to stop it. I stood in the throne room and I said *no*, and the binding — it needed somewhere to put the parts of my brother the wolf couldn't reach yet. The love. The loyalty. The little brother who said no. So it pressed those parts into me." He gestured at himself, at the papery thinness, the pressed-flower fragility. "I am what wouldn't fit in the cage. Kept here, in the shape of a life, so the binding stays balanced. If I die, the seal cracks. If I leave, the seal cracks. If anyone remembers me too loudly—" he tapped his temple, "—the seal cracks. So the wardens scrub me from every record, and the servants are forbidden to know my name, and I sit by a window that looks at brick, and I wait."

"Wait for what?"

"For someone clever enough to break it properly," he said. "Or for the wolf to finish eating my brother. Whichever comes first. At the rate things are going, I expect the wolf will win. It's very patient. Patience is the one appetite that never fills."

* * *

The second bell was coming. Mara could feel it the way you feel a storm approaching — a pressure behind the ears, a hush in the stones. She knew she should ask the questions that mattered: about the binding's weak points, about the first cartographer, about whether the wolf could be killed without killing the man beneath. Pragmatic questions. Survival questions. Questions that would justify the risk of being here.

Instead she heard herself say: "Tell me about him. Your brother. Tell me something the wolf hasn't eaten yet."

Aldric went very still.

Then he laughed — really laughed, a dry rustling sound like wind through autumn reeds — and something in his pressed-flat face opened, unfurled, briefly alive.

"He danced badly," Aldric said. "Terribly. The King of the Realm, terror of border wars, beloved of ten thousand subjects, and he danced like a scarecrow in a gale. At his coronation ball he stepped on the High Priestess's foot so hard she limped for a week, and he apologized by naming a river after her. There's a river in the south called the Meren. Nobody remembers why." His eyes shone wet and thin as glass. "He used to sneak down to the kitchens at night and make bread. Bad bread. Burnt on the bottom, raw in the middle. He'd bring it to my rooms at dawn and we'd eat it anyway, both of us laughing, because we were princes and we could have had anything, and what we wanted was terrible bread at dawn."

The words settled into the room like snowfall. Mara found her eyes burning and did not fight it.

"Say it again," she whispered. "The bread. Say it again."

"Mara Vale." He said her name — her *true* name, the one she'd sold a piece of but not all of, the one she'd guarded like a coal in cupped hands since Corvane — and she didn't flinch, because the way he said it wasn't power. It was care. It was a man placing something precious into keeping. "When you do whatever it is you've come here to do — and you will, I can see it in you, you have the look of people who break things open — the wolf will fight you. Not because it's cruel. Because it's afraid. It has been eating a man for thirty years to avoid being nothing, and it thinks nothing is the only thing waiting for it outside the crown." He leaned forward, and the lantern light passed through his cheekbones like light through ice. "So remember him. Remember the dancing and the bread and the river named for a bruised foot. Remember *both* of them — the beast and the man, the hunger and the heart. Memory is the only knife that cuts a binding without cutting the bound."

"I don't know if I can save you," Mara said. The honesty tore out of her before she could dress it up. "I don't know if there's a version of this where you walk out of this room. I've done the arithmetic. Every path I can see, either the wolf dies or you do, or both, or worse."

"I know." He said it gently, without bitterness, the way you'd confirm the weather. "I did the same arithmetic eleven years ago. That's why I'm asking you not to save me."

Mara's breath caught. "What?"

"Saving me means restoring me — pressing me back up out of the page, making me a king or a claimant or a symbol, some new weight for the realm to hang its fears on. I've seen what this place does to symbols." He shook his head, slow, papery hair stirring. "I don't want a throne. I don't want my brother's crown, or his war, or his terrible burden. I'm so tired, Mara Vale. I've been awake inside my own forgetting for eleven years." His living eyes held hers. "I'm asking for something smaller. Ask your cartographer if it can be done — ask whether a binding can be unwritten without erasing what it held. Because if it can, then don't save me. *Remember* me. Carry the dancing and the bread and the burnt-bottom loaves out of this room and into whatever comes next. Let me end the way a story ends instead of the way a prisoner dies. Let me be a memory that someone chose to keep."

Outside, far above, the second bell began to toll — a sound like a silver tooth cracking.

The servant appeared in the doorway, hood trembling. "We have to go. Now. The wards—"

Aldric reached out and touched Mara's wrist, and his fingers weighed almost nothing, and they were warm.

"One more thing," he said quickly, urgently, as the lantern guttered. "The wolf. When you face it — and you will — look for the moment it hesitates. It always hesitates. Thirty years, and every year, for one breath, it stops, because some part of my brother inside it looks out through its eyes and sees the moon and remembers he used to howl at it as a boy, badly, off-key, to make me laugh." His grip tightened, feather-light and fierce. "That breath is yours. Whatever you build, build it in that breath."

Then the servant pulled her away, and the door of oak closed, and the shallow-carved name above it — *ALDRIC* — seemed to glow in the lantern-light like a word written in a book that someone, somewhere, had promised to keep reading.

Mara climbed the stairs with tears freezing on her cheeks and a new arithmetic running behind her eyes — not *kill the wolf or kill the man*, but something no law allowed, something Bram would call impossible and Sera would call treason and the Tallyman would price at more than she had left to pay.

*Remember him,* Aldric had said.

And Mara Vale, charm-seller, map-bearer, woman who trusted no one — least of all herself — climbed toward the sleeping silver court and made a promise she had no idea how to keep:

*I will remember both of them.*

Above her, unseen, the moon was rising over the Court of Antlers — and somewhere in the high tower, something crowned and starving paused mid-step, lifted its head, and scented the change in the air.
