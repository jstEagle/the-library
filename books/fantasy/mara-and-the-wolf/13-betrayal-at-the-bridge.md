## 13. Betrayal at the Bridge

The bridge was older than the law that had outlawed it.

That was Mara's first thought when she saw it, standing at the forest's edge where the Hollow thinned into the grey country of wardens and warrants. It arched over a gorge of moving mist, built of pale stone that seemed to have grown rather than been laid, its balustrades carved with figures whose faces time had rubbed away. No cartographer had ever charted it, because no cartographer had ever been permitted to admit it existed. It was the one road between the Hollow and everything else, and it had stood there for centuries, patient as a debt.

Sera Ilvane stood at the near end of it with six wardens behind her and a warrant in her hand that she did not need. The warrant was for Corvane. Here, in the grey country, her word *was* the law, and they both knew it.

"Put the map on the ground," Sera said. "Walk away. You have my oath you'll live."

Mara laughed. She couldn't help it; it came out of her like something escaping a cage. "Your oath. From the woman who exiled Bram Hartwell for drawing a picture."

"I exiled him for what the picture cost." Sera's voice didn't rise. That was the worst thing about her — nothing about her ever rose or fell. She spoke the way winter spoke, evenly, inevitably. "Three people died mapping that realm before he finished it. He knew the price and paid it in other people's coin. I don't make threats, Vale. I make arithmetic."

Behind Mara, Bram made a small sound. She didn't turn to look at him. She'd spent the last hour looking at him — at the warden-irons on his wrists, at the blood on his temple where they'd taken him, at the way he kept his eyes fixed on her with an expression she refused to name because naming it would mean deciding what to do about it.

They had caught him at dawn. She had heard it happen — the crash in the underbrush, the shout, the wet sound of a warden's baton finding the back of his skull — and she had run, because running was the first thing she'd ever learned and the only thing she'd never unlearned. She'd run half a mile through bracken before her legs stopped and her mind started, and by then the choice had already assembled itself inside her, cold and complete, the way all her worst choices did.

She could keep the map. The map was everything: the way home, the way forward, the whole breathing geography of the Hollow King's realm folded into a rectangle of living skin. With it she might bargain with the wolf-king, might find some third path no law allowed, might do any of the ten thousand impossible things Bram had outlined in his low urgent voice around their fire at night.

Or she could trade it for Bram Hartwell's life, which was worth nothing to anyone, least of all to him.

"You're hesitating," Sera observed. "That's interesting. I had you marked as someone who doesn't hesitate."

"I'm not hesitating." Mara crouched, slowly, keeping her eyes on the warden-captain's face, and set the map down on the frost-brittle grass. The ink stirred under her fingers as she let go — it always did now, reaching for her like a child for a hand — and she snatched her fingers back before it could catch. "I'm saying goodbye."

"Mara." Bram's voice cracked on the second syllable. "Don't. Whatever you think this buys, it doesn't. She'll take the map and burn it with us both on it. I've read her verdicts. There's no version of this where we walk away."

"There is," Sera said quietly. "One. This one."

And here was the thing, the terrible thing, the thing Mara would chew on for years afterward in the silvered quiet of her stolen life: she believed him, and she believed *her*, both at once. She believed Bram when he said Sera would burn them. And she believed Sera when the captain looked at the map lying in the grass with an expression not of triumph but of grief — the look of a woman picking up something heavy she had carried so long she'd forgotten there was any other way to hold it.

Sera had loved someone once. Bram had told her that much, in the bad hours before dawn, when guilt loosened his tongue. Someone the Hollow had eaten, slowly, from the outside in, until what remained hadn't been a person but a doorway wearing a person's shape. Sera Ilvane had watched it happen and sworn afterward that no door would ever open again, whatever — whoever — had to be nailed shut to keep it closed.

Mara understood that. That was the horror of it. She understood it completely, because she had spent her whole life doing arithmetic with other people's survival, weighing a trinket against a week's bread, a lie against a beating, and calling it prudence. Sera just did it on a larger scale, with better tailoring.

"Step back," Sera said. One of her wardens moved toward the map. Another unlocked Bram's irons, and Bram stumbled forward, free and furious and alive.

"Take him and go," Sera said. "Now. Before I decide mercy is a luxury."

Mara took Bram's arm. His sleeve was torn and his wrist bled where the iron had bitten, and he leaned on her more than she liked, and none of it mattered, none of it, because they were walking away, both of them, across the frost, toward the treeline, and behind her she could hear the wardens' boots on the grass and Sera's voice giving orders in that flat winter tone—

—and then the map screamed.

There was no other word for it. The ink rose off the vellum in a black wave and the sound it made went through Mara's teeth and bones, a note like a struck bell made of grief, and every warden within twenty feet dropped to their knees clutching their ears. Sera alone stayed upright, staring at the map as it writhed on the ground, redrawing itself faster than Mara had ever seen, lines crawling like veins, and at its center the crowned figure was no longer still. It was standing. It was *looking out.*

"Oh," Mara said, very softly, to no one. "Oh, you stupid, greedy—"

The Tallyman. The memory arrived whole and ice-cold: the broker's long fingers, the gentle voice, the memory of her mother lifting out of her like a splinter drawn from flesh — and the Tallyman's parting smile, the one she'd thought was sympathy. *Everything traded is told,* he'd said. *It's how I keep my books balanced.* She'd thought it was a figure of speech.

He had sold her. Her location, her movements, the shape of her days — sold to the wolf-king, who wanted his map back, who had been hunting it since the day a dying stranger pressed it into her hands. And the wolf-king had known exactly where she would be, because there was only one bridge, and desperate people are predictable, and she had walked herself onto the board like a piece that thought it was a player.

The mist in the gorge began to move against the wind.

"Fall back," Sera snapped, and for one absurd moment warden-captain and charm-seller were giving each other the same order, and then the world stopped being orderly altogether.

The wolf-king did not arrive. He simply became present, the way cold becomes present, the way dark does — one moment the ridge above the gorge was empty, and the next it was occupied by a tall figure in a crown of silver antlers, and the light around him bent politely out of his way. Up close he was worse than beautiful. Up close he was *careful*, every movement weighed like a merchant weighing coin, and his eyes found Mara across the chaos with the unhurried certainty of something that had already won.

"My map," he said. His voice was mild. It was the mildest thing in the world. "You've been carrying it very far, little seller of false charms. Did you know your broker keeps excellent accounts? He sold me your whole week. Tuesday was my favorite."

Bram's hand closed hard on her shoulder. "Run," he breathed.

But the map was between them all, screaming on the frozen grass, and the wolf-king descended the slope toward it with the terrible patience of ownership, and Sera Ilvane — Sera, whose entire soul was built on the principle that doors must be shut — drew her blade and put herself between the king and the map, and shouted words of sealing that glowed old and gold in the air.

The wolf-king looked at her almost kindly. "Captain. You burned a village once to close a smaller door than this. Do you imagine I'm afraid of you? I am the reason you're afraid of everything else."

What happened next, Mara's memory would keep only in fragments, the way a map keeps coastlines and loses the interior. Fire — Sera's wardens burning wards that flared blue and died. The gold seals cracking like eggshell. Bram dragging her bodily toward the trees while she twisted to watch the map lift itself off the ground, fold itself with a sound like wings, and slide into the wolf-king's shadow as though the shadow were a pocket sewn into the world.

And then the bridge.

She never learned whether the wolf-king burned it deliberately, sealing the road behind him, or whether the collision of his power and Sera's seals simply broke it, the way a rope breaks when two people pull in opposite directions. But the pale stone went up in white flame from both ends at once, the carved faceless figures glowing like coals, and the heat of it reached them even under the trees, and the mist in the gorge rose up shrieking and fell back down again, and the only road between the Hollow and everything else was gone.

Gone, with six wardens on it. Gone, with Sera Ilvane standing at the far end, on the wrong side, her warrant and her certainty and her whole flat winter architecture of law stranded in a realm that had just eaten its own exit.

Mara sat down hard against a pine trunk. Bram slid down beside her. For a long time neither of them spoke. The fire on the bridge settled into embers that burned without smoke, which was somehow worse than smoke, which was somehow worse than anything.

"He has the map," Bram said finally. His voice was scraped hollow. "He has the map, and Sera's trapped in there with him, and the village—" He stopped. Put his head back against the bark. Closed his eyes. "The villagers. If he holds the bridge, he holds the roads. Every road. Everything I charted, he can redraw."

Mara stared at the embers. Betrayal, she thought, was supposed to come one at a time. That was the courtesy of it. First the stranger dying in her shop and leaving her bound to a thing that breathed. Then the Tallyman, smiling, selling her week by week to a king made of appetite. Now the map itself, leaping to the hand that had made it suffer longest — because of course it had gone to him, of course; it had been drawn in his realm, bled on by his prisoners, and things went home, even cruel things, even cages.

Everyone she touched, she thought. Everyone and everything. She was a charm-seller. She sold people pretty lies about protection, and the ones who bought them got hurt anyway, and now she had handed a living map to a wolf because she couldn't walk past a man bleeding in the grass without trying to save him.

Trust, she thought, watching the last ember of the bridge gutter and die. Trust was a luxury. She'd known that since she was nine years old. She had simply, somewhere on the road through the Hollow, beside a guilty cartographer and a village of the erased, allowed herself to forget it.

"We can't stay here," Bram said. He hauled himself up, wincing, and held out his hand to her — held it out steadily, palm open, asking nothing, and that small stubborn decency undid her more than the burning bridge had. "The king will sweep this forest by nightfall. And Mara — if Sera's alive in there, if she reaches the capital—"

"She'll try to kill him with the map in his own court," Mara said. "Yes. I did the arithmetic too."

She took his hand. It was warm, and human, and it asked nothing, and she hated how much she needed it, and she held on anyway.

They went north, deeper into the Hollow, toward a city of silver antlers that now held everything they had lost: the map, the captain, the road home, and — though neither of them knew it yet, though the ink had not yet finished whispering it to the wind — the first owner of the map, who was not as dead as Mara had believed, and who had left one more message folded into the lines.

The wolf remembers, the stranger had whispered, dying on her floor.

Mara was beginning to understand that the wolf was not the only one.
