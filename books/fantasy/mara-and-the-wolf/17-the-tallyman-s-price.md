## 17. The Tallyman's Price

The forest had gone quiet in the way that meant it was listening.

Mara felt it in the soles of her feet as she walked the deer-path toward the Bargain Market, the ground holding its breath beneath her. Behind her, at the village without names, Bram was sharpening quills and arguing with Sera about wards, and the villagers were grinding ink from ash and riverstone, and every one of them believed — because she had told them to believe — that there was a way to rewrite a binding three years old and older than that, older than the first cartographer's dying hand. A map drawn by many hands. A cage rebuilt as a home.

But Mara had sold something at this market. She had traded her mother to the Tallyman — not her mother herself, never that, but the memory of her: the smell of rosemary on wet stone, the sound of her humming off-key while she worked, the particular warmth of a hand pressed to a fevered forehead. She had carried that absence for weeks now like a stone sewn into her chest, and she had learned to walk around it, to not reach for it in the dark hours when reaching would only find nothing.

And now she needed it back. Because the new binding required true things — names, loves, losses, the raw material of a self — and you could not draw a map out of holes.

* * *

The market had moved. It always moved, but it moved the way a dream moves, and Mara found it exactly where she wasn't looking: down a slope that hadn't been there yesterday, between two trees too old to have grown in any living wood. Lanterns hung from branches that bent to hold them, though there was no wind. The stalls were draped in grey silk, and the merchants behind them had faces like half-remembered acquaintances — familiar until you tried to name what made them so.

The Tallyman's stall stood at the center, as it always did, as it always would. He sat behind a desk of black wood, and his ledger lay open before him, its pages shifting like water. He wore a coat stitched from receipts, each scrap of paper a bargain someone had regretted, and when he looked up at her, his face was pleasant and utterly without mercy, which was the most honest face in the Hollow.

"Mara Vale." He said her name the way a jeweler says the name of a stone he intends to cut. "You've come back. They usually come back."

"I want to buy something back."

"Of course you do." He turned a page in the ledger with one long finger. The ink on it moved, restless. "Memory of a mother. Sold by the bearer, freely given, price: passage into the Silver City. Paid in full. No refunds."

"Everything has a price," Mara said. "You taught me that. So name mine."

Something flickered across the Tallyman's face — not surprise, exactly. Interest. The interest of a man who has been playing solitaire for a century and finally sees another player sit down.

"You're different," he said. "Last time you came here, you haggled like a woman buying bread. You flinched at the cost and paid it anyway, and you hated yourself after. Now—" He tilted his head. "Now you've come to spend something larger, and you already know what it is. Who told you?"

"No one. I did the arithmetic myself." She kept her voice level. Her hands, hidden in the folds of her cloak, were shaking. "A memory is worth what it costs to live without it. I know that now. So I'm not going to insult you by offering coins or trinkets or someone else's secrets. I'm going to offer you the only thing I have left that you'd want."

"And that is?"

"My future."

The lanterns swayed, though still there was no wind. Somewhere in the market, a merchant stopped mid-transaction to listen.

"Every year I haven't lived yet," Mara said. "All of them. Whatever I was going to be — the years themselves, the growing old, the— whatever comes after. Take them. In exchange, give me back my mother. All of her. Every piece you hold."

The Tallyman was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke again, his voice had lost some of its polish; underneath it was something older and hungrier, the voice of the thing that had first invented the idea of a price.

"Do you understand what you're offering? Not the shape of it — anyone can say the words. Do you understand it?" He leaned forward. "I have taken futures before. From kings who wanted one more victory. From mothers who wanted their children healed. They all said the words the same way you just did. And then they lived one more year, and one more, because the taking doesn't happen all at once, and every year they woke up and found they were still alive and still paying, and they came to hate me, and they came to hate themselves worse. You will grow no older than you are now. You will feel time pass through you like water through a net. Everyone you love will go on ahead of you into an age you'll never see. Is that the trade?"

"Yes."

"Say it plainer."

"I will never be older than I am today. My years are yours, all of them, from this moment forward. In exchange: everything you took from me about my mother, returned whole. No fragments. No residue held back in your ledger. Whole."

The Tallyman studied her for a long time. Then, slowly, he opened the ledger to a fresh page, and the page itself seemed to lean toward her, eager.

"There is one more term," he said. "There always is. The ink must be fed with consent — real consent, not desperation wearing consent's clothes. Desperation I can get anywhere. So tell me why. Not why you need the memory — I know why; the binding needs true things, and your mother is the truest thing you ever lost. Tell me why you're willing to pay this. Convince the ink."

Mara looked at him. And because lying to the Tallyman seemed both impossible and obscene, she told the truth.

"Because I spent my whole life surviving," she said. "In Corvane I survived by trusting no one, wanting nothing, keeping myself small enough that the wardens' nets would slide past me. And then I came here, and I kept doing it — surviving, always surviving, letting other people pay when the bill came due. Bram paid with his exile. Sera paid with everything she loved. Aldric paid with his entire self. Even the wolf paid, though nobody asked it to. And every time I gave something up, I told myself I had no choice. The stranger died and put the map in my hands — no choice. The wardens came — no choice. The Tallyman wanted a memory — no choice." She shook her head slowly. "But that's a lie I told to stay comfortable. There's always a choice. There was always a choice, and I chose survival because survival was the only god I worshipped."

She stepped closer to the desk.

"So this is the first thing I've ever given up freely. Not because a warden was at the door. Not because someone I love was bleeding. Because it's right, and I choose it, and I will keep choosing it tomorrow and the day after, every morning I wake up still owing you. That's my reason. Feed your ink with that."

For the first time since she'd known him, the Tallyman looked away first.

He drew a contract from a drawer — actual paper, actual vellum, old enough that it had gone soft as cloth at the folds. He wrote in a script that hurt to look at directly, each letter a small door standing open onto somewhere else. When he finished, he turned it toward her and set down a pen whose nib was a sliver of bone.

"The terms, read aloud, so the market hears," he said, and his voice carried strangely, and every merchant in every stall went still. "Mara Vale sells her remaining years, in their entirety, to the bearer of this ledger. In exchange, the ledger returns, whole and unfragmented, all memories of the seller's mother held within it. The seller will age no further. The seller will die when her span ends regardless of years remaining, and her span now ends when her years run out — which is to say, now, and yet not now, for the debt is collected as life collects itself: slowly, gently, one unlived day at a time. Signed in the seller's own blood-ink, witnessed by the market entire."

The silence afterward had weight, like snow about to fall.

Mara picked up the pen. Her hand had stopped shaking. That surprised her — she had expected terror, and instead there was a strange, clean calm, the calm she imagined Bram felt over a fresh sheet of vellum, the calm of a line about to be drawn that could not be undrawn.

She thought of her mother's hands. She couldn't remember them — that was the whole point, the wound of it — but she knew they had been warm, and she knew they had been kind, and she knew that somewhere in the Tallyman's ledger those hands were waiting to be hers again.

"For her," she said quietly, and signed.

The pen bit deeper than a pen should. Not pain, exactly — more like a thread being pulled out of her, a long silver filament running from her chest down her arm and out through the nib onto the page, where it coiled into letters that glowed briefly and went dark. The market exhaled. The lanterns brightened. Somewhere far away, a bell rang once in a tower that did not exist.

And then—

Rosemary on wet stone. Humming, off-key. A hand cool against her forehead, saying *there now, there now, you're safe.* The whole of her mother came back into her in a single flood, so sudden and complete that Mara gripped the edge of the desk to stay upright, gasping, laughing, weeping, all three at once. Twenty-three years of absence refilled in a heartbeat. She could smell the rosemary. She could hear the humming. She could remember being small and sick and loved, which was perhaps the rarest memory in any world.

"Oh," she whispered. "Oh, you kept her safe. All this time, you actually kept her safe."

"I keep everything safe," the Tallyman said. "That's what no one understands about ledgers. Nothing is lost with me. Only priced." He closed the book, and for a moment his face was almost gentle. "You have until dawn tomorrow before your friends expect you back. Spend it well. Unlived time spends faster than lived."

* * *

She walked back through the forest slowly, because for the first time in her life she had nowhere she needed to hurry toward and nowhere she was fleeing from, and the strangeness of that nearly undid her more than the signing had.

The trees let her pass without touching her. Once, near the stream, she saw the wolf — not the king, not the crowned horror on the silver throne, but the wolf itself, grey and enormous, drinking at the water with its head low. It looked up at her, and its eyes were old and tired and terribly patient, and it did not growl. It simply watched her go, the way one watches someone carrying something heavy and fragile, and Mara understood that it had smelled the transaction on her. That it knew what she had done. That maybe — and this thought stayed with her the rest of the way home, warming her like a coal cupped in both hands — maybe it was the one creature in all the Hollow who understood exactly what it meant to give up your future for someone else's sake.

At the edge of the village, Bram was waiting. She saw him before he saw her, silhouetted against the firelight, sleeves rolled, ink to his elbows, arguing with Sera about whether a binding drawn in live ink would hold if the signatories wept while signing. He looked up mid-sentence, and whatever he saw in her face made him stop talking entirely.

"You got it back," he said. "The memory."

"Yes."

"At what cost?" His eyes went to her hair, searching for silver that wasn't there yet. "Mara. What did it cost?"

She smiled at him, and it was almost steady.

"Bram," she said, "how much ink do we have left?"

His face changed. He understood — cartographers always understand prices; it's the first thing the trade teaches you, that every line drawn is a line that can't be undrawn. He didn't ask again. He just stepped aside, and held open the flap of the tent, and said, quietly:

"Then we'd better make every line count."

Inside, spread across the table, the map waited — her map now, bound to her blood, its ink stirring faintly like something dreaming. And as Mara bent over it and dipped her own quill, she felt the first unlived year settle onto her shoulders, light as snow, patient as winter.

Somewhere in the ledger, a page turned.

The drawing began.
