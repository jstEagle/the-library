## 1. The First Dish

The stew arrived at my table in a bowl of chipped blue clay, and I did what I always did first: I looked at it.

People assume tasting is about the mouth. It isn't. It's about the eyes, and the nose, and the small animal part of the brain that has been keeping humans alive since before we had words for *hemlock* or *arsenic*. The stew was venison and dark ale, thickened with barley, crowned with a raft of root vegetables cut into coins. The surface had cooled enough to form a faint skin, which meant it had sat uncovered for at least ten minutes — long enough for someone to lean over it. I tilted the bowl toward the window light and watched the broth slide. It caught the light cleanly, no oily rainbow, no glitter of crushed mineral. That ruled out the cheap poisons, the ones that killed in an hour and left the cook time to run.

I smelled it next. Venison, juniper, bay, the sweet rot-adjacent depth of long simmering. Underneath all of that, so faint I might have invented it, something green and bitter, like the memory of a leaf.

My mouth had gone dry. That was normal. That was, in fact, the first ingredient of every dish I'd ever tasted.

"Take your time," said Magistrate Corvel from across the room, though his tone made it clear he wished I would take less of it. He was a broad man with the complexion of someone who had spent forty years indoors counting other people's grievances, and today he was sweating through his good coat because the Duke of Aurel's party had arrived two hours early and the whole of the valley's hospitality now rested on a kitchen that had never once cooked for royalty.

"I always do," I said.

The kitchen behind me had gone silent in the particular way kitchens do when they are pretending to work. I could hear the scrape of a knife that wasn't cutting anything. I could hear Bren, the undercook, breathing through his mouth. And beneath all of it, steady as a drip from a cave ceiling, I could hear old Maren at the hearth, turning a spoon in an empty pot because her hands needed something to do and her mind had already fled.

Maren's hands. I would come back to those.

I took up the horn spoon, broke the skin of the stew, and stirred — three turns clockwise, watching how the broth moved, whether anything settled too quickly or clung to the spoon with unusual eagerness. Nothing. Then I dipped the spoon's edge only, wetting my lower lip with a film of the broth, and waited.

This is the part no one understands about my work. They think the danger is in swallowing. The danger is in waiting. A drop on the lip will tell you about burners and blisters, about the numbness of certain alkaloids, about the metallic kiss of salts that have no business in food. But the slow poisons — the ones that ride quietly through the skin of the mouth and settle into the blood like guests who intend to stay — those you learn by patience. You count your own pulse. You watch your own fingertips for the first prickle of pins. You become, for the length of the wait, a country whose borders you patrol alone.

I counted to three hundred. My pulse stayed even. My fingertips stayed honest.

Then I took a proper spoonful, held it on my tongue, and let it sit while I read it the way Maren had taught me to read dough by feel and my grandmother taught me to read weather by the ache in old bones. Venison, juniper, bay. Barley cooked long. Salt, correct. And there — the green bitterness again, but stronger now, blooming against the back of my tongue like a bruise coming up.

Not arsenic. Not foxglove or monkshood; those announce themselves like drunks at a funeral. This was subtler. This was *water hemlock*, or its cousin, ground fine and coaxed into the fat where its sharpness would hide among the juniper. Someone had known this kitchen. Someone had known this dish.

Someone had wanted the Duke of Aurel to die in the valley of Vhalen, where no one outside would ever think to look for him.

I swallowed.

I want to be clear about what that means, because I've spent eleven years making sure it doesn't mean what people want it to mean. Swallowing is not bravery. Bravery is a story told afterward, by other people, about a moment that felt nothing like a story. What swallowing actually was, for me, was arithmetic. Water hemlock carries cicutoxin, and cicutoxin kills by seizing the body until the body tears itself apart from the inside — but the body can be taught, slowly, in increments measured in grains and drops over years, to recognize the toxin and refuse it passage. My grandmother had begun teaching mine before I had all my teeth. Bittercress in spring water, one leaf to a cup, then two. Hemlock root shaved so thin you could read scripture through it, dried and crumbled into honey. Every dose a little braver than the last, every recovery a little quieter, until the poison and I had reached an arrangement: it could visit, but it could not stay.

Eleven years of that arrangement. Eleven years of a tongue that tasted everything twice — once for flavor, once for threat — and a body that had learned to treat its own survival as a craft rather than a gift.

So I swallowed, and I put down the spoon, and I said, in the flat professional voice I keep for exactly these moments:

"There's hemlock in it. Ground fine, in the fat. Enough to kill a grown man inside an hour." I turned to face the kitchen. "The venison itself is clean. Whoever did this added it after the stew came off the fire."

The silence changed shape. Silence, in a kitchen, is never empty; it has texture and grain, and I have spent my life learning to read it the way I read broth. This silence had a crack running through it, and the crack ran straight to the hearth, where Maren stood with her spoon still turning in her empty pot, and her hands —

Her hands were trembling.

Now, Maren is sixty-one years old and has cooked for the valley council since before I was born, and her hands are steady in the way that river stones are steady. She could bone a quail in the dark. She could pull sugar threads fine as hair. Her hands do not tremble, and if they ever did, they would not tremble *like this* — not with fear, exactly. Fear I know on sight; I wear it myself most days, pressed flat and starched into the shape of competence. This was something else. This was the trembling of a woman standing very still beside a thing she had seen and could not unsee.

"Maren," I said gently. "Who came into the kitchen this morning?"

"No one." Too fast. "No one, girl. The duke's man brought the venison, that's all, that's all—"

"The venison is clean."

Her spoon stopped. For a moment the whole kitchen hung on the sound of that absence, and then Corvel was shouting for the guards, and the guards were shouting for each other, and the kitchen erupted into the noisy chaos that kitchens prefer to any difficult truth. Through it all, Maren looked at me — just me, across the heads and shoulders of everyone else — and her eyes said something I didn't have a word for yet. Something between *forgive me* and *you don't understand what walks through that door when the doors open wide.*

I have thought about that look every day since. But that morning, I only filed it away, the way I file everything: carefully, in the dark, where it keeps.

* * *

The Duke of Aurel ate pheasant instead, and lived, and complimented the valley's hospitality in a voice like oiled hinges, and by evening the whole episode had been folded up and put away the way Vhalen folds away everything inconvenient — quickly, completely, and without witnesses. The guards took Maren's scullery boy for questioning, a slack-jawed lad named Perrin who cried so hard they released him by nightfall, having concluded what everyone in the valley had already concluded: that whoever poisoned the stew had come from outside, because nothing bad had ever come from inside Vhalen, not in four hundred years, and it was important to keep believing that.

I walked home along the river path in the last gray light, and I did what I always do after a tasting: I took stock of myself the way a merchant takes stock after a fire.

Tongue: intact, taste intact, the bitter ghost of the hemlock already fading. Hands: steady, though they hadn't been during the wait — they never are; I simply hold them behind my back where no one can see them conduct their small private earthquake. Heart: beating in its usual rhythm, which is to say mostly regular, with the occasional skipped beat that Dr. Ilse has told me, twice, is nothing, and once, when she thought I wasn't listening, told her assistant was *expected, given what the girl does to herself*. Vision: the gray edges creeping in a little further at dusk, the world going soft at its borders like a drawing left in the rain. Numbness: the pads of my fingers, permanently, so that I read textures now with the sides of my hands, and the cold, in winter, arrives late, like news from a distant war.

These are the costs. Nobody asks about the costs. They ask about the work — *is it true you drank hemlock tea at nine? is it true you can eat belladonna berries like cherries?* — and I answer yes, yes, in the modest voice, and let them believe it costs me nothing but appetite. Let them believe immunity is a wall I built once and live behind safely. The truth is that immunity is not a wall. It is a debt, paid daily, in a currency my body minted out of its own substance, and someday — I have always known this, the way you know a roof will eventually need replacing — the debt collector comes.

But not today. Today I was alive, and the duke was alive, and the valley was safe, and I had done the only thing I am good at.

Grandmother was waiting with supper when I got home — nettle soup and brown bread, the smell of woodsmoke and dried thyme hanging in the low room like a blessing. She didn't ask how the tasting went. Word travels faster than I walk in a valley this size. Instead she studied my face for a long moment with those pale, unhurried eyes, and then ladled the soup, and said:

"You swallowed."

"You knew I would."

"I hoped you wouldn't." She set the bowl in front of me. "There's a difference."

We ate in silence for a while. Outside, the mountains stood around the valley like patient wardens, their peaks already white with the season's first snow, and the border stones glimmered faintly down by the pass — I couldn't see them from here, but I never need to; I feel them the way you feel a closed window in a warm room. Somewhere beyond them was the rest of the world, with its kings and dukes and duchies, its roads that went somewhere, its prayers that — Grandmother says — are heard, out there, by things that answer.

Nothing answers here. That is the oldest rule of Vhalen, older than the stones, older than the name. You may speak to the sky all you like; the sky keeps its own counsel. We are a country of unanswered prayers, and we tell ourselves this is peace.

"Grandmother," I said, because the soup had loosened something in me, "the cook's hands were shaking. Before anyone found the poison. Before I even said anything. Like she'd seen something."

Grandmother's spoon paused above her bowl — a pause so brief that anyone else would have missed it, but I have been trained since birth to notice pauses; they are where the poison hides.

"Old hands shake," she said. "Eat your soup."

"It wasn't old hands shaking. It was—" I searched for it. "*Recognition.*"

She looked at me then, fully, and for a moment I saw something move behind her eyes like a fish passing under ice — there and gone, leaving only the sense of depth below.

"Ines." My name in her mouth always sounds like a small bell struck once. "You taste what is put in front of you. You survive it. You do not go looking for what stands behind the dish. That way lies—" She stopped herself, which she almost never does, and finished, more softly: "That way lies questions with teeth."

I ate my soup. I said no more. But that night, lying in my narrow bed under the eaves, listening to the wind come down off the mountains and find no crack in the valley wide enough to enter, I turned the day over one more time, the way I turn a suspicious broth toward the light.

A duke had come to Vhalen. Dukes do not come to Vhalen; the world forgets we exist so thoroughly that cartographers apologize for the blank space where we should be. Yet a duke had come, with venison and guards and oiled-hinge courtesies, and someone had tried to kill him with a poison ground fine enough to hide in juniper, and an old woman who had fed this valley for forty years had looked at me with recognition in her trembling hands.

And underneath the green bitterness of the hemlock — I was nearly asleep when I finally placed it, and it sat me upright in the dark with my heart hammering its small irregular drum — underneath the hemlock, masked by it, deliberate as a signature, there had been a second taste. Faint. Green. Not a plant at all.

It had tasted the way the air tastes just before lightning finds the ground.

I lay back down. I told myself it was the hemlock talking, the residue of toxin in a body that had spent eleven years learning to hear poison speak.

But I did not sleep, and in the morning, when the summons came — though of course I did not yet know it was a summons, since Vhalen receives no post, and the rider who brought it should not have been able to find us at all — I was awake to meet it.
