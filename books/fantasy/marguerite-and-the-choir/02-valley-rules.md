## 2. Valley Rules

The frost had come early that year, and it came the way everything came to Vhalen — quietly, without announcement, as though the valley itself were trying not to be noticed by the sky.

Ines walked the border stones with her grandmother on the morning after the first hard freeze, her breath pluming white in front of her and her boots crunching a rhythm into the silence that felt almost like speech. The stones began at the river's northern bend and ran in an uneven line up the shoulder of the mountain, forty-one of them in all, each one taller than a man and older than any record anyone in Vhalen had bothered to keep. They were not carved. That was the first thing every child learned, standing before them with their mouths open: nothing had ever been cut into these stones, no chisel mark, no inscription, no name. They had simply been *set*, the way grief is set in a body, whole and unexplained.

"Walk on my left," her grandmother said, as she said every time. "The ground remembers feet better than it remembers words."

Ines moved to her grandmother's left without asking why. There was no why, in Vhalen. There was only *this is how it is done*, repeated until the doing became the reason for itself.

They walked. The valley fell away behind them in terraces of grey-green barley gone to stubble, orchards with their last apples clinging like stubborn thoughts, the slate roofs of the village smoking gently from morning fires. From up here, Vhalen looked like something cupped in two hands. Held. Protected. Ines had thought so all her life, and she thought so now, and beneath the thought — as always, as faithfully as her own pulse — ran the other one, the one she did not say aloud:

*Held* could also mean *kept*.

Her grandmother stopped at the seventh stone and laid her palm flat against it. She did this at every stone, or so it seemed; Ines suspected there were particular ones that mattered, but her grandmother had never distinguished them, and so Ines had learned to treat them all as equally sacred, which was perhaps the lesson itself.

"Cold today," her grandmother said, though her hand stayed on the stone a long while. "Good. Cold keeps things honest."

"What things?" Ines asked, because she asked every time, and because her grandmother answered every time, and because the answer never changed, and this too was a kind of rule.

"The borders between what is and what wants," her grandmother said. "Cold makes a thing stay what it is."

They walked on. A crow lifted off the ninth stone as they approached, unhurried, insulted, and Ines watched it go with a small ache she couldn't have named if you'd put a knife to her throat and demanded the word for it. It flew south, over the valley, out beyond the ridge where the world supposedly continued — roads, cities, kings, the whole loud machinery of elsewhere — and she watched it until it was a fleck and then nothing, and she thought: *it can leave. It doesn't even know it's a gift, that it can leave.*

She didn't resent the valley. That was important, and she held to it the way you hold to a railing in a rocking boat. The valley had fed her, had taught her the names of every herb on its slopes, had given her a childhood of long dusks and well-water and neighbors who knew the weight of her moods before she did. Her mother was buried here. Her whole self had been assembled here, piece by careful piece, like a stew built from what the pantry offers.

And yet.

At the fourteenth stone they sat, as they always sat, on the flat boulder beside it, and her grandmother unwrapped the bread and hard cheese and the little jar of honey that made the walk worth it, and for a while neither of them spoke, and the silence was the good kind, the kind that holds two people instead of separating them.

It was Ines who broke it, because it was always Ines who broke it.

"Grandmother. Tell me again about the prayers."

Her grandmother's hands paused over the honey jar. She was a small woman gone wiry with age, hair the color of ash and iron, eyes so dark brown they read as black unless the light caught them just so — and then they were the color of strong tea, warm and unreadable. People in the village said she'd been beautiful once. Ines believed it the way you believe a thing about a mountain: true, but impossible to picture from where you stood.

"You know about the prayers."

"I know what you've told me." Ines pulled her knees up, wrapped her arms around them. "I want to hear it again. Today I want to hear it properly. Not the short version for children."

Her grandmother looked at her for a long moment. Something moved behind her eyes — Ines saw it, a flicker like a fish turning under ice — and then it was gone, and her grandmother tore the bread and handed her half, and began.

"You know that outside the stones, people pray," she said. "Everyone does. It's the oldest habit our kind has, older than cooking, older than burial. When something is bigger than you, you speak to it. You ask. You beg. You barter." She chewed, swallowed. "And outside the stones, sometimes — rarely, but sometimes — something answers."

"And inside?"

"Inside," her grandmother said, "nothing answers. Nothing has answered in four hundred years. No prayer spoken in Vhalen has ever been heard, because prayer doesn't travel here. It's like shouting into water. The words leave your mouth and drown before they're three feet from your lips."

"Why?"

"That's the wrong question."

"It's the only question."

Her grandmother smiled, and it was not entirely a kind smile; there was an old tiredness in it, a weight carried so long it had become posture. "No," she said. "The right question is *what would you do with an answer*. But very well. You want the story. Eat while I talk, you're thin as a winter branch."

Ines ate. Her grandmother looked out past the stones, down the mountainside, to where the land changed — subtly, but changed nonetheless. Beyond the border line the forest grew differently, wilder, the trees leaning as if listening. Beyond the border line, Ines knew, magic pooled like rainwater in a cellar. Here, inside, it could not cross. Whatever the stones were, whatever they did, they kept it out. Kept *them* out.

"When the valley was closed," her grandmother said, "the closing was total. That was the price of it working at all. You cannot seal a door halfway; a door halfway open is just a door. So the ones who sealed it — and don't ask me who, because the names are lost, or worse than lost, *unspoken* — they sealed everything. Magic, yes. But magic is only half of it. Prayer is the other half. Prayer is how magic finds you. It's the scent trail. It's the rope thrown down the well. Cut off the prayer and the magic has nothing to hold onto. Nothing to answer. Nothing to *come through*."

"So we're deaf to them," Ines said slowly, working it through, "and they're deaf to us."

"We are quiet to them," her grandmother corrected. "Deafness is an affliction. Quiet is a choice someone made on our behalf, four hundred years ago, and we live inside that choice the way we live inside our own skins. Every prayer spoken here falls dead at your feet. Say one now, if you like. Try it. *Please let it snow by feast day.* Nothing will happen. The sky doesn't even hear you say it."

Ines was quiet for a moment. Then, because she was twenty-two years old and had spent her whole life being careful and methodical and obedient, and because some part of her had been waiting years for permission she would never be given —

She whispered it.

Not snow. Something smaller. Something she would never have said aloud in the village, never said to anyone, something she barely admitted to herself in the dark hour before sleep. She whispered: *let me matter somewhere larger than this.*

The words left her mouth and died, exactly as promised. She felt them die — that was the strange part, the part her grandmother had never mentioned. She felt the sentence falter mid-flight like a bird hitting glass. There was no echo. No residue. The valley took the prayer and gave back nothing, not even silence-with-attention in it, just silence, plain and mineral and old.

Something in her chest folded closed, quietly, like a hand withdrawing.

"Well?" her grandmother said.

"Nothing," Ines said. "You're right. Nothing."

"Mm." Her grandmother packed the honey away. "That nothing is the safest thing you will ever own, girl. Remember that. Everything outside those stones that answers a prayer takes a payment, whether the pray-er knows it or not. Nothing in this world gives without taking. Out there, when the gods listen—" she said *gods* the way other people said *floods* or *fevers* — "they are not listening to love you. They are listening the way a spider listens. For the tremor. For the location of the thing caught in the web."

"You make them sound hungry."

"They are hungry," her grandmother said simply. "Everything old is hungry. That's what old means. And we—" she gestured with her chin at the valley, the smoke, the stubble fields, the whole cupped and quiet country — "we are the one larder in the world with the door shut. Be grateful for the shut door."

Ines said she was. She said it easily, because it was mostly true, and because the untrue part of it was nobody's business but her own, and she had gotten very good, over the years, at knowing exactly where the seam between those two things ran.

* *

They walked the rest of the line through the afternoon. At the twenty-second stone her grandmother told the story of the shepherd who'd prayed for rain during the drought of her own grandmother's time, and how the rain hadn't come, and how the valley had survived anyway on cisterns and discipline and neighborliness, and how the shepherd had lived to ninety and sworn till his dying day that the unanswered prayer had saved him, because a man who expects rescue learns nothing, but a man who expects nothing learns everything.

"A pretty way to dress up deprivation," Ines said, and immediately wished she hadn't.

But her grandmother only laughed — a dry, real laugh, rare as summer snow. "Yes," she said. "It is. Hold onto that sharpness. Most people sand theirs down by thirty and spend the rest of their lives sliding off things." She squeezed Ines's arm, her grip still strong. "Your mother had it too. The sharpness. And the wondering."

Ines went still. "You never talk about her wondering."

"Don't I?" Her grandmother resumed walking, and Ines had to hurry two steps to catch up, and the conversation was over, or seemed to be, the way conversations in Vhalen ended — not concluded but simply set down, like tools at dusk, to be taken up again or not.

But walking behind her grandmother along the thirtieth stone, watching the old woman's shadow stretch long and crooked across the frozen grass, Ines turned the sentence over. *The wondering.* As if her mother had wondered too. As if wondering were an inheritance, passed down the female line like a nose or a temper or a lullaby.

Because her mother had sung to her. That was one of the few things Ines remembered whole, uncorrupted by retelling: her mother's voice, low and unhurried, singing a song with no name that her mother called *just an old tune, my darling, older than me, older than the stones maybe*. Eleven verses. Ines could still sing all eleven, note-perfect, though she'd never once heard anyone else sing them, and when she'd hummed the melody at the well once, years ago, an aunt had gone pale and told her never to do that in public, and Ines had never asked why, because in Vhalen you learned early which questions were doors and which were walls.

She sang it to herself sometimes at night. Not as a prayer. Never as a prayer. Just as a song. Songs were safe. Songs weren't asking anything of anyone.

*Just an old tune.*

She filed the wondering away with all the others, in the room inside her that had no windows.

* *

They finished the line at dusk, at the forty-first stone, the last one, where the mountain dropped away steeply and the view opened southward — and there, far off, past the forests and the foothills, the lowlands glittered faintly under the coming stars. Other country. Real country. Country with kings in it, and courts, and cathedrals full of people praying their hearts out to things that might, might, *might* be listening.

Ines stood looking at it longer than she meant to.

Her grandmother noticed. Her grandmother noticed everything; it was her single most reliable feature. But she didn't scold. She came and stood beside Ines, small and iron-grey against the darkening sky, and together they looked out at the enormous elsewhere.

"Do you wonder," Ines said carefully, "what it would feel like? To be heard?"

The wind moved through the grass below the stones. Somewhere downslope, a fox screamed once and went silent.

"I stopped wondering before you were born," her grandmother said.

"That's not an answer."

"No," her grandmother agreed. "It isn't." She turned then, and looked up at Ines, and in the last blue light her tea-dark eyes were unreadable and ancient and suddenly, briefly, terribly sad. "Listen to me, girl, and take this however you like. Being heard is not what you think it is. You imagine it feels like warmth. Like being seen at last, truly seen, by something vast and loving. But attention is a weight. Attention from something vast is a crushing weight. Everything that has ever been heard by the old powers has been *changed* by hearing, and not one of them was improved." She reached up and touched Ines's cheek, her fingers cold and light as the frost coming down. "The unheard life is a small life. But it is *yours*. No god has a claim on a single breath of it. Guard that."

"I know," Ines said. "I know. I'm content."

"I know you're content," her grandmother said. "Contentment and longing can live in the same house, child. They've always been poor relations sharing a bed." She turned back toward the path home. "Come. It's freezing, and your uncle will have started the stew without us, and you'll want to check his seasoning before he ruins it."

Ines laughed and followed. Behind them the forty-first stone stood black against the last light, patient as everything else in Vhalen, keeping its long silence, keeping its long watch.

They were nearly home — the village lamps lit, woodsmoke sweetening the cold air, her uncle's silhouette already moving in the kitchen window — when Ines saw the horse.

It stood at the edge of the common, steaming, lathered, clearly ridden hard for a long way. And the rider dismounting beside it wore colors Ines had only ever seen in the peddler's forbidden picture-book: deep royal blue, trimmed in gold, and on his breast a wax-sealed satchel, and on his face the expression of a man who has traveled farther than the world says he should have.

He looked directly at her. Across the common, through the freezing dusk, he looked directly at her, and called out a name.

Not *Ines Almeida*, the name the valley knew.

Her mother's surname. The one no living person in Vhalen used.
