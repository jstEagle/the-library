## 3. A Summons in Wax

The courier came on a Tuesday, which was the first impossible thing about him.

Tuesdays in Vhalen were for mending. Every household in the valley observed it without ever having agreed to — a habit so old it had worn itself into the shape of law. My grandmother and I sat on either side of the long table by the window, she with her needle and a shirt that had needed mending since before I was born, I with my ledger of doses, recording the morning's measure of foxglove tincture in my small, precise hand. Outside, the rain was doing what Vhalen rain always did: falling straight down, patient as arithmetic, asking nothing of anyone.

The knock, when it came, was wrong in three ways at once.

First, no one knocked on our door. In a valley of forty-one households, everyone simply called out a name before entering, or didn't enter at all. Second, the knock had rhythm to it — three sharp raps, a pause, two more — the cadence of a man who had been trained to announce himself rather than merely arrive. And third, most terrible of all, the knock was *confident*. It assumed an answer. Nothing in Vhalen assumed anything; we were a people who checked the weather twice and trusted neither report.

My grandmother's needle stopped mid-stitch. She did not look up. "Ines," she said quietly, "go and see who is standing in our yard, and do not open the door while you do it."

I went. Through the gap between shutter and frame I saw a horse — a real horse, tall-boned and glossy with road sweat, the kind of animal that cost more than our whole valley earned in a season — and a man beside it in a gray traveling cloak beaded with rain. He wore a satchel across his chest, the flat rectangular kind used for carrying documents, and he stood the way soldiers stand when they are trying very hard not to look like soldiers. His boots were ruined with mud from roads Vhalen did not have.

We had no roads. That was the fourth impossible thing, and by then I had stopped counting.

"Ines Almeida," the man said through the door, in an accent that rounded its vowels like coins. Not a question. A reading. As if my name were written somewhere he could see it, hanging in the air between us. "Ines Almeida, daughter of Marguerite Amaral. I have a summons."

I want to record, honestly, what happened inside me at that moment, because later — after everything — people would ask me when I first understood, and the truth is I understood nothing. I only felt. And what I felt was this: my own name sounded foreign to me in his mouth, and the name he'd given for my mother's was one I had never heard spoken aloud in my entire life.

*Almeida* was my grandmother's name, given to me because names in Vhalen were practical things, like roof tiles. But *Amaral* —

*Amaral* was the word my grandmother never said. It was the silence at the center of every story she told about my mother, the hole shaped exactly like a word. When I was six I had asked her once why Mama had no family name, and my grandmother had looked at me for so long that I thought she hadn't heard, and then she'd said, "Because she gave it back," and had gone out to the garden and stayed there until dark.

The man knocked again. Three raps, a pause, two more.

Behind me, my grandmother had risen from her chair without sound. She moved the way old women in stories move — impossibly fast when it matters — and her hand closed around my wrist, and her grip was cold.

"Do not answer him," she said.

"He has a summons—"

"There is no such thing." Her voice was low and even, which is how I knew she was frightened. My grandmother raised her voice for joy and anger both; only fear made her quiet. "There is no post into Vhalen. There has been no post into Vhalen for four hundred years. Whatever that man carries, it did not come here by any road that exists. Do you understand me? It found us anyway. Things that find us anyway are not to be opened."

But the man outside had begun to speak again, and his voice carried strangely through the wood, as if the door itself were listening and passing his words along:

"I'm instructed to wait for acknowledgment. I'm instructed to wait as long as it takes." A pause, and then, almost apologetic: "They told me you'd say that. The grandmother. They told me she'd refuse the door."

My grandmother went very still against my back.

"They told me," the courier continued, "to tell her that the seal will hold regardless. That burning changes nothing. They told me to tell her—" and here he hesitated, and I heard him swallow, "—they told me to tell her that Marguerite's debt has come due, and the crown knows whose daughter is living behind those stones."

* * *

What happened next happened quickly, and I have turned it over so many times since that I can no longer be sure of the order of things. I remember fragments. The fire, already banked for the evening, coaxed back up. My grandmother holding the letter — a heavy envelope, cream-colored, sealed with a disc of dark red wax stamped with some crest I couldn't read from where I stood — held at arm's length as if it might bite. The courier still standing in the yard, hat in hand now, rain running off his chin, watching our windows with the expression of a man who has delivered worse things than paper and expects to again.

And I remember my grandmother's face as she fed the letter to the flames.

She didn't burn it in anger. That's what struck me, even then. She burned it the way you'd bury something — carefully, completely, with a kind of grim tenderness, turning it with the poker so that every corner caught, watching the cream paper curl and blacken and lift apart into flakes that glowed orange and then died to ash. She burned it thoroughly, the way she did everything, and when it was done she stirred the ashes down among the coals until there was nothing left that could be read, and she stood there breathing hard, and she said, to the fire or to me or to four hundred years of silence:

"There. It's done. It stays done."

Then we both saw the wax seal lying on the table.

It sat in the middle of the scarred oak, upright, unburned, dry, catching the lamplight along its curved edge like a small red moon. The crest was crisp and perfect — a tower, I saw now, and beneath the tower a chalice, and around them a ring of letters too fine to make out. It had not been on the table before. It had been on the letter, and the letter was ash, and the ash was in the stove, and the seal was *here*, resting on the wood as lightly as if someone had just set it down, and it was warm. I know it was warm because I touched it. I know I shouldn't have touched it. My grandmother says — said — that touching is how these things get their hook into you, and perhaps she's right, but I was twenty-two years old and a seal had appeared out of a fire onto my table, and my hand moved before my sense did.

It was warm the way a stone is warm after a day of sun. Warm the way skin is warm.

"It's a trick," I said. My voice came out steadier than I felt. "Some court sleight. Glue and resin. They wanted it to survive the fire — it's theater, Grandmother, it's meant to frighten us—"

"Yes," she said. "Probably." She did not sound convinced, and neither did I.

She picked up the seal herself — she was braver than I was, or older, which amounts to the same thing eventually — and held it to the lamp. And I watched her face change as she read the ring of letters around the tower and the chalice, letters too fine for my eyes but not, apparently, for hers. I watched her lips move over them silently. I watched all the color leave her face in a slow tide, the way water leaves a marsh.

"What does it say?" I asked.

She set the seal down on the table very gently, the way you set down a sleeping child, and she looked at me for a long moment, and whatever she decided in that moment — whether to tell me, whether to lie, whether the time for protecting me had finally, finally run out — I saw the decision happen behind her eyes like weather crossing a valley.

"It says *Quod clausum est, custoditur*," she said. "What is closed is kept." Her hand, when she smoothed it over the tablecloth, was trembling. "That is not the royal seal of Ardenne. That is not any seal I have seen in eighty years of living. Except once." She sat down slowly, in her mending chair, and put her hands flat on her knees, and looked suddenly very old and very far away. "Except once, on the wrist of a woman who walked out of this valley and never came home."

"Mama," I said. Not a question either.

"Mama," she agreed, and closed her eyes.

* * *

We did not sleep, either of us. We sat by the banked fire while the rain kept up its patient work on the roof, and the seal lay on the table between us like a third person who refused to leave. Twice I caught my grandmother looking at it. Twice she caught me looking at her looking at it.

Near midnight she spoke, unprompted, in the voice she used for the oldest stories — flatter, slower, each word set down like a stone in a wall.

"You should know some things, in case. Your mother didn't run away, whatever the valley gossips. She was sent. She went out through the border stones on a night much like this one, and she kissed my cheek, and she said, *Keep her name simple, Mother. Keep her ordinary. Ordinary is armor.*" My grandmother's jaw worked. "I have spent your whole life keeping you ordinary. Foxglove and hemlock, measured in drops. A talent for bitterness. I told myself it was a trade skill. I told myself a great many things."

"And now?" I asked. "Is it armor?"

She was quiet for so long I thought she wouldn't answer. Then: "The armor held for twenty-two years. Something out there has finally sent a man who knows where the joints are."

Toward dawn, the knocking started again.

Not at the door. At the *window* — the small east window, the one that faces the hills, the one that faces nowhere, because beyond those hills there is nothing but more hills and then the border stones and then the world, and the world cannot reach in. Three raps. A pause. Two more.

I crossed the room and pulled the shutter wide.

Nothing. Gray pre-dawn, wet grass, the hills dissolving into mist. No courier, no horse, no tracks in the soft ground. Only, resting on the outside sill, neatly centered as if placed by a careful hand, a second seal. Dark red wax. Tower and chalice. Unburned. Dry, despite the rain that had fallen all night.

Warm, when I picked it up.

Behind me, my grandmother said, in a voice gone quiet with surrender, "Get your boots, Ines. It seems the past has learned to knock."
