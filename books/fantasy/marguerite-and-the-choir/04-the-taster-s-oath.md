## 4. The Taster's Oath

My grandmother did not say goodbye at the border stones.

She stood at the kitchen door with her arms folded into her apron the way she folded them when bread was rising or when she was deciding whether to forgive someone, and she said only: "The stones will feel you leave. They always do." Then she turned back to her dough as if I were going to market for salt, and I understood that this was her way of not weeping in front of me, and that if I looked back even once I would not go.

So I did not look back. I let the road take me north through the orchards, past the millpond gone silver with morning fog, past the last field of barley that anyone in Vhalen would plant this year under my family's name. The courier rode ahead on his gray mare, and he did not speak, and neither did I. He had already said the impossible things. There was nothing left to say until one of us crossed a line that officially did not exist.

The border stones came up out of the mist like teeth.

There were seven of them once; six remained standing, each taller than a man, each worn smooth on the inner face by four hundred years of hands touching them — children daring each other, lovers swearing oaths they didn't mean to keep, old women like my grandmother pressing their palms flat and murmuring *stay, stay* to no one in particular. The outer faces were rough, unweathered, as though the wind itself had agreed not to touch them. Moss grew on the inside. Nothing grew on the outside at all.

I had touched these stones ten thousand times. Every child in Vhalen had. They were warm in summer and cold in winter and entirely, stubbornly ordinary, the way a wall is ordinary until you remember it is holding something back.

"Miss Almeida." The courier dismounted and held out his hand for my satchel. "It's easier if you don't hesitate. The valley doesn't like hesitation."

"The valley," I said, "doesn't like anything. That's rather the point of it."

He almost smiled. Almost. Then his face went carefully blank again, the way courtiers' faces do, and I understood I was already being practiced upon — that everything I said past this point might be carried back to someone who collected words the way my grandmother collected dried herbs, labeled and shelved against future need.

I stepped between the stones.

The world changed the way water changes when it boils — no movement you can name, but suddenly everything is different and you cannot return to what it was a moment ago. Magic brushed my skin like cold water. That is the only description I have ever managed, and I have tried many, because the moment deserved better than I could give it. It was cold water, yes — but cold water that *noticed* me. Cold water with attention in it. It slid over my arms, pooled in the hollows of my collarbones, ran down my spine like a finger drawn by someone deciding whether I was worth keeping.

I gasped. My whole life I had lived in a house where the fire never quite caught, where bread rose slowly and reluctantly, where prayers fell from the lips like stones down a well and struck nothing. I had thought that was simply what the world was. Now the fire caught everywhere at once. The grass beyond the stones was greener than any grass in Vhalen — greedily green, showing off. A bird sang three notes and the third note *echoed wrong*, hung in the air a half-second too long, as if the air were tasting it.

"So it's true," I whispered. "None of it crosses."

"Nothing crosses," the courier said behind me, and there was something in his voice — pity, or its courtly cousin — that made me turn.

He was looking at me strangely. Not at my face. At my hands.

"What?"

"Nothing, miss." He remounted. "Most people cry, crossing. You didn't."

"I built immunities," I said, before I could stop myself, "to most things that make people cry."

This time he did smile, briefly, and it did not reach anywhere near his eyes, and we rode north into a country that hummed.

* * *

The palace announced itself an hour before we saw it — first by smell (woodsmoke and roasting meat and, beneath both, something mineral and old, like the air in a cave), then by sound. Bells. Dozens of bells, ringing without pattern, without occasion, as though the city simply liked the noise of its own devotion. In Vhalen we had one bell, cracked, rung only for deaths. Here the bells rang for everything, and I began to understand, riding through streets crowded with shrines — little stone niches full of wilting offerings, honey cakes going gray, coins green with age — that this was a country that talked constantly to its gods and had recently, terrifyingly, begun to be answered.

Servants took my satchel. A chamberlain with ink-stained cuffs read my name from a card and pronounced it correctly, which unsettled me more than mispronunciation would have. *Ines Almeida.* My mother's surname. My grandmother had burned the summons; the wax seal had reappeared on our table, unburned, patient as a cat. Whoever wanted me here had wanted me by that name specifically, and I resolved, walking marble corridors that swallowed the sound of my boots, to find out why before I tasted so much as a cup of well water.

They brought me to a chapel antechamber instead of a throne room, and I should have known then.

The oath room was small and round and windowless, lit by candles that burned with flames the color of old honey — I watched them a long moment, because in Vhalen candle flames are just candle flames, and these leaned toward me as I passed, all of them, all together, like flowers toward sun. The floor was black stone shot through with veins of white, and the veins moved. Slowly. The way clouds move. I fixed my eyes on the middle distance and decided I had not seen that.

Queen Sabine sat on a plain wooden chair — not a throne, I noted, and wondered later whether that was humility or superstition — beneath a canopy of white cloth. She was younger than I expected, maybe forty, with the kind of beauty that looks like it has been argued with: powder over sleeplessness, jewels over trembling hands. She wore mourning grey beneath her crown, though no one at court had died, as far as I knew, in months.

Beside her stood a priest whose vestments were embroidered all over with closed eyes.

"Kneel," the chamberlain said, and I knelt, and the black floor was warm under my knees like a living thing's back, and I made myself stop cataloguing and pay attention, because the words about to come out of my mouth were the kind that cannot be taken back.

The priest unrolled a scroll. His voice was dry and liturgical and I heard every word the way you hear a diagnosis:

"Do you swear to place your tongue between your sovereign and all harm? To take into your body what was meant for hers? To die, if dying comes, without complaint, without investigation, and without inheritance of grievance?" A pause. "Do you swear to taste what is set before you — and to report truthfully its flavor, however strange the flavor?"

*However strange the flavor.* I turned that phrase over while the candles leaned. An ordinary taster's oath concerns arsenic and nightshade, rage in the kitchens, a rival duke's purse. No ordinary oath needed a clause for strange flavors.

"I swear," I said.

"Do you swear that your body is your own to spend, and that you give it freely to this office?"

That one snagged. My body was not entirely my own to spend — it was a ledger of debts, thirty years of small deliberate poisonings, numbness in two fingertips, a left eye that saw dawn as dusk-colored. But I had crossed the stones. My grandmother's face was a day's ride south and hardening into memory. And underneath everything, under the fear, was the thing I had not admitted to the courier or to myself: the humming country, the leaning flames, the magic running over my skin like cold water — some traitorous part of me had *wanted* this. Had spent a childhood pressing palms to border stones wondering what it would feel like to be heard, to be *in* the world where things answered.

"I swear," I said again.

"Then rise, Ines Almeida, Taster to the Crown of Vhalen's throne, and kiss the ring of your queen."

I rose. I crossed the warm black floor to Queen Sabine, and she extended her hand, and that was when I saw it: she would not look at me.

Not the studied indifference of royalty, which I had been warned about — the deliberate downward gaze that says *you are furniture*. This was different. Her eyes came up, found mine for half a heartbeat, and fled. Found and fled, found and fled, like a tongue returning to a broken tooth. Her hand, when my lips touched the ring, was cold and damp and shaking very slightly, and the ring itself was warm, warmer than skin has any right to be, warm the way the border stones were never warm, warm like something freshly fed.

She smelled of incense and, beneath it, faintly, of milk. Of a nursery.

"Your grandmother," the queen said, and her voice was low and roughened, a voice used to praying more than talking, "refused the summons."

"She burned it, Majesty."

"And yet you came."

"You sent it twice," I said. "The second time it arrived before the first."

Something moved behind her eyes — recognition, maybe, or dread confirming itself. She withdrew her hand and looked, finally, directly at me, and I wished she hadn't. There is a look people get when they have seen something answered. I had seen it once before, on my grandmother, the single time in my childhood I'd asked her why no prayer spoken in Vhalen was ever heard, and she had looked at me like that — like I was standing too close to a door — and said, *Be grateful for the silence, child. Silence means it isn't listening.*

Queen Sabine looked at me the way you look at a person standing too close to a door.

"You will begin tonight," she said. "There is a banquet. The Duke of Carris. Taste everything. Report—" and here her voice did the thing voices do when a sentence has been rehearsed and still fails, "—report the flavor. However strange."

"As Your Majesty swears me to."

She dismissed me with a flick of two fingers, and as I turned to go, she spoke once more, so quietly I nearly lost it under the sound of the bells outside, still ringing their patternless devotion:

"Taster. If anything you eat tastes of — of *honey*, and underneath the honey of iron — you will come to me at once. Before you swallow. Whatever the protocol says. Before you swallow."

I bowed. I walked out through corridors that hummed, past shrines heavy with offerings, past servants who watched me with the particular reverence reserved for people marked for sacrifice — I had seen that look too, on the faces of neighbors watching the priestess line walk to the border stones, four hundred years of tradition sitting in their eyes like cataracts.

And in the antechamber, waiting under guard for my escort to the kitchens, I opened the small leather case I had carried across the border against my grandmother's explicit instruction, and checked my instruments. Vials of antidotes. Scales. A spoon of horn. And the little book, written in my own hand over thirty years, titled in careful letters: *What I Can Survive.*

I had built myself, dose by dose, into the only vessel in two kingdoms that could hold what was coming. I had believed I did it for the work. For the craft. For the quiet pride of a woman who could drink what killed dukes.

Standing in the humming heart of a palace whose queen could not meet my eyes, whose candles leaned toward strangers, whose bells rang for gods newly awake — I felt, for the first time, the shape of the trap around my life. Someone had known what I was building. Someone had counted my doses from very far away, the way you count the turns of a moon.

Somewhere below us, the kitchens were already cooking for tonight. And somewhere much closer than the kitchens, something ancient had begun setting a place at its table — and had written my name on the card.

The escort arrived. I closed the case. I went down toward the smell of roasting meat, tasting, before a single dish reached my tongue, the flavor of being exactly where something wanted me.
