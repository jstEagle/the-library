## 5. Prayers with Teeth

Tomas had served the palace of Vessarine for nineteen years, and in all that time he had learned one rule above every other: the dead are easier to manage than the living. Dead things stay where you put them. Dead things do not ask questions with their eyes. The kitchen boy — Pellam, fourteen years old, quick hands, a laugh like a dropped bell — had been dead for six days now, and still Tomas could not make him stay put.

Not his body. His body lay in the cold crypt beneath the east wing, sewn into linen, waiting for a priest who kept postponing the rites. It was everything else that would not stay buried. The way Pellam's mother looked at Tomas when he brought her the coin purse. The way the other servants went quiet when they passed the chapel doors. And most of all, the thing itself — the answered prayer — which sat on the chapel altar behind three locked doors and a curtain of oiled silk, because no one in the palace could bear to look at it twice.

Tomas was carrying the keys to those three locks when he came to collect the new taster.

* * *

He found her in the scullery corridor, of all places, standing very still before the great copper cisterns, watching the water. She had that quality about her already, four days into her post: she moved through the palace like someone reading it. Most new servants learned the building by its routes — kitchen to hall, hall to stair. Ines Almeida seemed to learn it by its silences. She stopped at each one and listened to it as though the silence might confess something.

"Mistress Almeida." He inclined his head. "Her Majesty asks that you be shown the chapel."

"The chapel." She turned. Her eyes were the gray of valley slate, and they did not widen at the summons, though everyone else's did. "I understood my duties were confined to the tables."

"Your duties," Tomas said carefully, "are whatever survives."

She considered that. He watched her consider it — saw the small mechanical patience of her, the way she weighed words like a woman tasting them. He had heard the stories from the steward of the household accounts, who had heard them from the border folk: the taster from Vhalen who drank poison the way other children drank milk. He did not believe the stories. But he noticed that she held her left hand slightly apart from her body, fingers loose, as if it were an instrument she kept tuned and ready, and he thought: *that is not the posture of a liar. That is the posture of a professional.*

"Then show me," she said.

* * *

The chapel of Vessarine had been built by a king who wanted to be forgiven for something, though the records disagreed on what. It was long and low, its ceiling ribbed like the inside of a shell, and its windows were old glass gone the color of pond ice. For two hundred years it had smelled of candle wax and dust and the particular sweetness of lilies rotting in vases too far from any altar that mattered. The gods it had been raised to honor were the old ones — the nameless many, the hungry quiet — but the dynasty had grown embarrassed of hunger, and so the altars stood bare, the offering bowls dry, and the court prayed instead to a single tidy god of harvests and heirs who asked nothing and gave less.

That arrangement had ended in the spring.

Tomas unlocked the first door. Then the second. At the third he paused, keys in hand, and made himself say what he had come to say while there was still a door between them and the thing.

"You should know why I am showing you this," he said. "It is not cruelty. Her Majesty believes — we all believe — that your work may extend beyond the table. That you may be able to taste... other things."

"Other things," Ines repeated.

"Gifts," he said. The word came out wrong, too loud in the stone hush. He tried again. "Answered prayers."

The taster said nothing. She waited, with that terrible patience of hers, and Tomas discovered that silence was a pressure, and that he had been holding it off for six days and could hold it no longer.

"Pellam was a kitchen boy," he said. "Fourteen. He had a sister with a bad lung — the wet sickness, the kind that fills a child up like rain fills a boot. Every night for a year, after the household slept, Pellam came to this chapel and knelt at the old altar, the bare one, the one nobody used. We found his kneeling board worn pale. A year of it. He prayed to whatever was listening, because the harvest god wasn't, and the physicians weren't, and the Queen's charity fund had rejected the family twice on technicalities of paperwork." Tomas's jaw tightened. "In the spring, the sister recovered. Overnight. The wet sickness simply — drained out of her. The physicians called it miraculous. They wrote it down. They were pleased with themselves."

"And Pellam?"

"He came back to the chapel to give thanks." Tomas turned the third key. "Whatever he said in thanks, it was heard. And the answer came for him. It took three days to kill him, and the physicians could not name what killed him, because there was nothing in his blood, nothing in his gut, nothing on his skin. He simply... emptied. Like a cup tipped over. They opened him and found his organs perfect and his veins full of a substance that evaporated the moment it touched air. One of the physicians fainted. Another resigned." He pushed the door open. "Come and see what thanks looks like, Mistress Almeida. You are the only person in this palace whose stomach I trust."

* * *

The inner chapel was small, round, older than the rest — you could feel the age in the way sound behaved there, arriving a half-beat late, as if the walls were considering whether to repeat it. The oiled silk curtain hung across the apse, and behind it, on the old bare altar, sat the gift.

Tomas drew the curtain aside.

The thing was roughly the size of a loaf of bread and roughly the shape of a heart, if a heart were carved from wet amber and lit from within by a slow golden pulse. Veins of darker gold branched through it. It sat in a basin of consecrated salt that had to be changed every morning, because by morning the salt had gone black and clumped like burnt sugar. The pulse was slow — slower than a sleeping man's heart — and steady, and it never stopped, and it never sped, and Father Anselme had declared it neither alive nor dead nor holy nor profane, and then had taken to his bed for a week.

"That is what answered Pellam?" Ines asked.

"That is what Pellam's gratitude bought." Tomas kept his voice level the way he kept all his voices, level as a shelf. "There have been others since. Smaller. A woman in the laundry prayed for her husband's safe return from the northern roads, and three days later he arrived home — but arrived silent, without his voice, which has not come back, and which the physicians say is not damaged, merely *absent*. A guard prayed for courage before a difficult duty and afterward could not feel fear at all, which sounds like a blessing until you watch a man walk calmly toward a fire because the part of him that would have refused is gone." He looked at her. "The pattern, Mistress Almeida, is that the old gods answer exactly. To the letter. And the letter always has teeth."

Ines stepped closer to the basin. Tomas's hand twitched toward her shoulder and stopped. She leaned over the amber heart the way she leaned over plates of suspect food — he had seen her do it at the duke's banquet, that slight forward tilt, nostrils flaring, the whole body become an instrument of assessment — and the golden pulse, impossibly, slowed. Just slightly. As if it had noticed her.

"It doesn't smell," she said.

"No."

"It doesn't smell of anything at all. Not even the salt." She straightened. "Everything smells. Rot smells, and roses smell, and clean water smells of the well it came from. This smells of nothing. That's worse than any stench I've encountered." She looked at him with those slate eyes. "What do you want from me, Steward? Say it plainly. You've been circling it since the corridor."

Tomas had prepared a diplomatic phrasing. He abandoned it.

"Taste it," he said. "Or rather — tell me if you can taste it. From here. Without touching. Her Majesty wants to know if these gifts carry poison the way food does. If they do, you are the only person alive who can approach them safely. If they don't—" He spread his hands. "Then we know nothing more than we knew, except that we've spent our last hope on a superstition."

"And if it kills me?"

"Then Her Majesty will write a very generous letter to your grandmother."

Something flickered across the taster's face — not fear; he realized with a small shock that it was recognition, or perhaps memory — and then it closed again, smooth as a drawer sliding home.

"No," she said.

Tomas blinked. "No?"

"I won't taste it. Not yet." She was still looking at the amber heart, and her voice had gone quiet and precise, the voice of a woman laying out knives in order. "You misunderstand what I am, Steward. I am not immune to poison the way a wall is immune to weather. I am *trained*. My body has been taught, dose by dose, year by year, to recognize certain poisons and refuse them. Arsenic. Hemlock. The bitter alkaloids, the heavy metals, forty-one compounds my grandmother could name in her sleep. Whatever killed Pellam is not among them. I can see that from here — it isn't in any of the families I know. If I taste it blind, I am not a taster. I am a meal." She turned to face him fully. "But I can tell you this much, and it is worth more than a taste: that thing is not trying to be eaten. Poison hides. Poison pretends to be dinner. That—" she nodded at the altar "—is sitting in the open, pulsing, waiting to be *noticed*. It doesn't want to get past me. It wants to be let in through the front door, with witnesses, with reverence. Poison works on bodies. That works on consent."

The silence that followed had weight. Somewhere above them, muffled by stone, the royal choir had begun its afternoon rehearsal, and the hymn came down through the ceiling in fragments — a melody none of the servants could date, older than the oldest ledger, sung every day because it had always been sung every day. Tomas noticed, distantly, that the amber heart pulsed once, out of rhythm, when the choir's sound reached them. He decided not to notice it. There was only so much a man could notice in one week and remain functional.

"You speak of it as if it were a person," he managed.

"I speak of it as if it were a guest," Ines said. "A guest who was invited by a child who didn't know better, and who has decided the house is interesting." She looked around the round chapel, at the dry offering bowls, the bare altars, the two centuries of polite neglect. "Who else has prayed here, Steward? Recently. Since the answers started."

Tomas hesitated. This was the part he had hoped to avoid — the part that lived in him like a swallowed hook.

"There is a book," he said finally. "A petition book. Old custom; nobody's used it in living memory. Petitions written and left on the altar, on the theory that the old gods read slowly." He laughed, and hated the sound of it. "We found it last month, after Pellam. Someone had been using it. Pages and pages, all in the same hand, all recent. Prayers for an heir. Dozens of them. Years' worth, apparently — the earliest pages are dated back almost a decade." He watched the taster's face carefully. "The prayers stopped being requests somewhere around the fifth page. By the tenth, they're negotiations. By the last page, they're signed."

"Signed how?"

"With a name," Tomas said. "And a seal."

And because he had carried this alone for six days, because the hook in him needed air, he heard himself add the rest:

"The name is Sabine. The seal is the Queen's."

* * *

They stood in the round chapel while the choir sang overhead, and Tomas watched the new taster absorb the shape of what he had told her. She did not gasp. She did not clutch at piety or treason. She did what she had done at every table since her arrival: she catalogued. He could nearly see the inventory forming behind her eyes — the queen's desperation, the gods' appetite, the arithmetic of a dynasty that had traded its tidy harvest god for something with teeth because the tidy god would not give it children.

"The gifts," she said at last. "The answered prayers. Are they all kept here?"

"All but one." Tomas's mouth had gone dry. "The largest one. It answered a prayer made nine days ago. It sits in the Queen's private oratory, and Her Majesty will not permit it to be moved, examined, or discussed. She prays beside it. Daily. Hourly, some say." He met the taster's slate gaze. "You asked me earlier what survives. Now you understand. My work, Mistress Almeida, is deciding what survives *of whom*, and lately the arithmetic has gotten away from me. Pellam prayed for his sister and paid with himself. The laundress's husband paid with his voice. The Queen has been praying for ten years, and the gods have begun sending installments." He paused. "I am showing you this because Her Majesty commanded it. But I am telling you the truth of it because someone in this palace must begin counting the cost out loud, and I find I cannot do it alone anymore."

Above them, the choir reached the verse of the ancient hymn that everyone knew best — the one the chambermaids hummed, the one mothers in the city sang without knowing its origin — and the round chapel's strange acoustics caught it and held it, and for one moment the walls themselves seemed to hum along, a resonance too deep for throats, rising up through the floor like water rising in a well.

On the altar, the amber heart flared gold.

Ines Almeida went very still. Tomas saw her lips move — not in prayer, he thought; in *recognition* — mouthing the melody a half-second ahead of the choir, as if she had known this hymn before she ever entered the palace, as if someone had sung it to her in a cradle in a valley that appeared on no map.

When the verse ended, the flare faded. The pulse resumed its slow, patient beat. Patient as a guest. Patient as a debt.

"Mistress Almeida," Tomas said quietly, "what do you know about that hymn?"

She took a moment before answering, and when she did, her voice was careful as a foot placed on river stones.

"My grandmother taught me a song once," she said. "She told me never to sing it past the border stones." She looked up at the ribbed ceiling, where the choir's echo still hung like breath in cold air. "Steward — how many verses does the royal version have?"

"Eleven," Tomas said. "Why?"

"Because the one my grandmother taught me," said the taster from Vhalen, "has twelve."
