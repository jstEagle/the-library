## 6. The Choir Loft

The palace had a way of teaching you its geography through sound before it ever taught you through sight.

Ines learned this in her first week as taster, walking the long gallery between the kitchens and the queen's apartments at the hour when the oil lamps were lit and the stone gave back every footstep twice — once from the floor, once from the vaulted dark above, so that she always seemed to be accompanied by a slower version of herself. She learned which corridors swallowed noise and which ones fed on it. She learned that the west wing breathed: the windows there were old, older than the dynasty's banners, and when the wind moved through them the whole wing exhaled a low note that the servants called the house clearing its throat.

And she learned, on the fourth evening of her service, about the choir.

She had been sent to carry a message to the steward — Tomas, who spoke to her in the careful voice of a man handling something that might be either precious or cursed — and the message could wait, he said, until after vespers rehearsal, because the choirmaster did not suffer interruption and the last person who interrupted her had been reassigned to the coastal garrisons, where the wind was said to drive men quietly mad. So Ines waited in the gallery outside the chapel, and that was where the hymn found her.

It came through the chapel doors like water under a door. Not loudly. The choir was not singing *at* anything; they were singing the way rain falls, without audience or ambition, forty voices braided into a line that rose and folded and rose again. Ines set down the tray she was carrying because her hands wanted to be free of it. She stood very still in the lamplight and listened, and what she felt first was not beauty but recognition — a cold, vertiginous recognition, like hearing your own name spoken in a language you don't speak.

She knew the melody.

Not the words. The melody itself, note for note, the way it turned downward at the end of the second phrase like a hand letting go of another hand. Her grandmother used to hum it over the wash basin, over the stew pot, over the grave-shaped silence where Ines's mother should have been. A valley song. A no-name song, the kind that belongs to no one because everyone has always had it. Ines had assumed it was Vhalen's, grown there like the river willows, rooted nowhere else.

And here it was, four hundred miles and one impossible border away, sung by forty strangers in a royal chapel, in words she couldn't make out through the doors.

Her heart did the thing it sometimes did now — the stutter, the small skipped beat that her immunity-building had bought her along with the numbness in two fingers of her left hand — and she pressed her palm flat against the gallery wall to steady herself.

That was when the wall answered.

It began below her hand, or maybe behind it; she could never afterward say which. A hum. Not the choir's hum — this was lower, older, the register of the building itself, of stone remembering pressure. It rose through the mortar into her fingers and up her arm, and it was not random. It held pitch. It held *the* pitch, the tonic beneath the hymn, sustained and patient, the way a dog holds still under a familiar hand. The wall was humming the hymn's root note, and the choir above it was building on that root whether they knew it or not, and Ines stood in the gallery with her palm against warm stone and understood that she was listening to a conversation — that the singing and the humming had been going on together for a very long time, longer than any of the singers, maybe longer than the singers' grandmothers, and that she was the first person in years to stand in exactly this spot with exactly this attention and notice.

The phrase turned downward. The hand let go. The hum deepened to match it, and somewhere in the depths of the wall — she would have sworn this later, under oath if anyone had asked, though no one ever did — something shifted, the way furniture shifts in an empty room.

Then the doors opened, and the music spilled out and broke apart into ordinary voices, coughing and tuning and complaining about the cold, and the hum stopped so completely that Ines wondered if she had imagined it, the way you wonder about a face in a crowd.

A woman stood in the doorway.

She was small and dry as a winter apple, with white hair cut brutally short and eyes the color of iron filings — gray, but with a glint in them that suggested they'd been drawn to something magnetic once and never quite forgiven it. She wore black, unadorned, and she looked at Ines the way Ines herself looked at an unfamiliar dish: assessing, cataloguing, deciding what might be in it and what it might do.

"You're the new taster," the woman said. Not a question. "Almeida."

"Ines Almeida."

"I know who you are. I asked the steward to send you down to me three days ago and he's been finding reasons not to." She stepped out into the gallery and closed the chapel door behind her with a click that sounded final, the sound of a lid on a jar. "I'm Odile. I keep the choir."

"Choirmaster."

"Keeper," Odile said, and did not explain the difference, and somehow Ines understood that no explanation would be forthcoming, and that the difference mattered.

They regarded each other. Ines had spent her life being looked at by people deciding whether she was useful, and she recognized the arithmetic in Odile's face, and she also recognized — with a small cold surprise — that Odile was trying to hide something behind the arithmetic. Fear, maybe. Or its close cousin, guilt.

"You were listening," Odile said.

"The walls here are thin."

"The walls here are four feet thick." Odile came closer. Up close she smelled of candle smoke and ink and, faintly, of something mineral, like the air inside a cave. "What did you hear?"

Ines considered lying. She was good at lying; a taster has to be, has to smile around a mouthful of arsenic-laced fricassee and compliment the seasoning. But the hum was still in her fingertips, a phantom vibration like a bell that won't stop ringing after it's been struck, and lies require steady hands.

"The third hymn," she said. "The one without a name. My grandmother sings it. In Vhalen."

Something moved behind Odile's iron-filing eyes — not shock. Confirmation. The look of a woman whose ledger has finally balanced, and who hates the total.

"Come with me," Odile said.

* * *

The choirmaster's rooms sat above the chapel loft, reached by a spiral stair worn into a smooth groove by two centuries of feet. They were narrow, crammed, and lit by a single lamp, and every surface that could hold paper did hold paper — folios, loose sheets, quires bound with string, pages covered in a notation Ines half-recognized and half-didn't, as if someone had taken musical notes and bent them slightly out of true. There was no portrait on the walls, no saint, no king. Only a single framed page, yellowed, bearing eight lines of that same bent notation and nothing else.

Odile poured wine without asking and pushed a cup into Ines's hands. It was good wine, which surprised her; she had expected vinegar from a woman who dressed like a crow in mourning.

"How long have you been in the palace?" Odile asked.

"Nine days."

"And how many times have you heard the choir?"

"Once. Tonight."

Odile sat down across from her, and for a moment the iron glint went out of her eyes and left only tiredness, vast and old, the tiredness of a woman who has been keeping watch over something for decades and has begun to suspect the watching is a form of feeding.

"Then let me tell you what you heard," she said, "and you can decide how much of it to believe, and then you can decide to stay away from my chapel, and we'll both sleep better."

Ines said nothing. Waiting was a skill her grandmother had drilled into her alongside poison lore: *the truth comes out of people the same way cream rises, girl, if you can bear the silence while it climbs.*

"The hymn has no composer," Odile began. "No date. No earliest manuscript. I have spent thirty years — thirty-one, if we count the year I wasted believing a forgery in the southern archive — trying to find its origin, and it has none. Every attempt to trace it runs backward into fog. The oldest copy sits in the palace archive and predates the archive's own founding catalogue by sixty years, meaning someone put it there and then lost track of having done so. The singers learn it from their mothers and fathers before they ever learn it from me. Children hum it in the nursery who have never attended a rehearsal." She lifted her wine and didn't drink. "And the words — you wouldn't have caught them through the doors, but the words are in Old Court Tongue, the ceremonial language, except the grammar is wrong. Deliberately wrong, or wrongly preserved. As if someone translated it from another tongue and let the translation calcify."

"What do the words say?"

"That depends on the verse." Odile set the cup down. "Some verses are praise. Some are lament. And some are—" she paused, choosing with visible care, "—directions."

"Directions where?"

"To places." The choirmaster's gaze drifted to the framed page on the wall. "There is a country that appears in the oldest land-grants and disappears from every map made after the Reconciliation. Four hundred years gone. Erased by treaty — not conquered, not renamed. *Erased.* The treaties don't even record its name; they record only that a certain territory is 'closed to record.' And the hymn names places inside it. Rivers. Hills. A ford. A standing stone. Verse by verse, like a walking song, like something meant to guide a traveler home through country that officially does not exist."

The lamp flame leaned. Ines became aware of her own heartbeat again, that unreliable stutter, and pressed her numb fingers against her thigh.

"My grandmother sings that melody," she said again, because it was the only thing she had.

"I know," Odile said. "Or rather, I know that someone from the closed territory sings it, and that the court summoned a taster from the closed territory, and that the walls of this chapel have begun to hum during rehearsals in a way they did not hum thirty years ago, and I am paid — poorly — to notice patterns." She leaned forward. "So here is my warning, taster, freely given, and you may weigh what it costs me to give it. Stay away from the choir. Stay away from the hymn. You have a gift for hearing things, and gifts like yours draw attention, and the attention this place draws is not the kind that wants to admire you."

"The walls hummed tonight."

"They hum," Odile said flatly. "They have hummed since before my time. The masons blame settling. The priests blame the organ bellows. I blame nothing, because blaming requires believing you understand the cause, and I have given up understanding." She rose, suddenly brisk, suddenly all business, the confession shut away again behind iron. "You came down here with a message for the steward. Give it to him and go back to your kitchens, your trays, your tasting spoons. That work will keep you alive. Mine has nearly killed me twice, and I'm too old and too stubborn to die properly."

Ines stood. At the top of the spiral stair she paused, one hand on the cool stone rail, and asked the question she had been holding since the gallery:

"Why warn me? If you want me away from the hymn, why tell me any of this? Silence would have served you better."

Odile looked at her for a long moment. The lamp threw her shadow huge against the papered wall, and for an instant the shadow seemed to lean toward Ines like a thing with intent.

"Because you said your grandmother sings it," the choirmaster said. "And in thirty-one years, you are the first person who has ever told me that the hymn exists anywhere outside these walls." Her voice dropped. "Which means either you're lying to me, taster — in which case, well done, you frightened an old woman — or the song has been waiting in the closed country all this time for someone to carry it back out." She turned back to her papers. "Good night."

* * *

Ines did not go straight back to her cell in the servants' wing. She told herself she was taking the long way to find Tomas, and she almost believed it, but her feet carried her past the steward's office, past the kitchens with their banked fires and their sleeping smells, up the west stair to the gallery outside the chapel, where the lamps burned low and the vaulted dark kept its doubled echoes.

The chapel was empty now. Locked, probably. Certainly dark.

But the gallery was not silent.

The hum was there — fainter without the choir to feed it, thinner, like a hearthstone holding warmth hours after the fire dies. Ines stood with her back against the four-foot-thick wall and let it move through her shoulder blades, and she thought about her grandmother humming over the wash basin, and about the border stones of Vhalen where no prayer was ever answered, and about the summons written in wax that would not burn, and about the queen who would not meet her eyes, and about the word Odile had chosen — *directions* — and the direction those directions pointed, which was home.

*A walking song,* Odile had called it. *Something meant to guide a traveler home.*

Ines pressed her ear flat against the stone, the way she had pressed her ear against her mother's grave-marker as a child, hoping the dead would say something, hearing only her own blood moving.

The hum changed.

It was subtle — a shift in timbre, a warmth in the tone, the difference between a room that merely contains you and a room that notices you. Ines pulled back. The hum held its new note, patient, attentive, and she had spent nine days in this palace learning the taste of things that watched without eyes, and she knew this texture. This was the texture of the prayer-gifts in the chapel reliquary. This was the texture of being *heard.*

Someone was listening through the wall. Something was.

"Ines?"

Tomas stood at the end of the gallery, lamp in hand, his careful steward's face carefully blank. "You shouldn't be here after dark. The choirmaster's rules—"

"I know." She stepped away from the wall. Behind her, gently, the hum sank back toward its resting pitch, a dog lying down again. "The message. From the head cook. About Thursday's banquet."

Tomas took the folded paper she offered, but his eyes went past her to the chapel doors, and his throat moved in a swallow. "You heard it," he said quietly. "The humming."

"You've heard it too."

"Everyone hears it eventually. Most people decide not to." He looked at her then, and there was something raw under the steward's polish, something that wanted to confess and was terrified of the wanting. "The kitchen boy who died — Pieter, the one the answered prayer killed — he used to sing that hymn while he scrubbed pots. Badly. Off-key. He said it was the only song he'd ever learned." Tomas's grip tightened on his lamp. "The morning they found him, the pots were still clean, and the whole kitchen swore they hadn't heard him stop singing. Just that the song kept going a moment after everything else went quiet."

He walked her back toward the servants' wing without either of them agreeing to it, their footsteps doubled in the vaulted dark, and at the turning of the stair he paused.

"There's a court assembly tomorrow," he said. "The astronomer presents his seasonal findings before the throne. He's been agitated for days — locked in his tower, sending frantic notes. Whatever he's found, the queen has already heard it, and whatever the queen heard, it's why she hasn't slept." He lowered his voice to almost nothing. "Be near the hall tomorrow, taster. Whatever he announces, I think you'll want to count what it means."

Ines climbed to her narrow cell and lay awake under the wool blanket, listening to the palace breathe, and far off in the west wing the old windows exhaled their long low note, and beneath it, faint as a pulse through four feet of stone, the chapel wall went on humming the root of a hymn that belonged to no one — to no one, unless you counted a dead woman's daughter, and a valley erased from every map, and whatever waited patiently in the dark between the verses, keeping time.
