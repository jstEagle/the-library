## 7. Thirteen Turns

The observatory sat at the top of the palace like a stone eye that had been watching the sky for so long it had forgotten how to blink.

Ines had never been invited anywhere in the palace before. She was summoned to places — the tasting chamber, the queen's anteroom, the chapel with its gifts that bit — but invitation was a different animal entirely. The card that arrived with her breakfast tray was cream-colored, heavy as a coin, and bore no seal at all, which somehow made it more official than any wax could have. *The Court Astronomer requests the presence of the Taster at the evening reading.*

She turned the card over twice, looking for the trick in it. There was always a trick now. She had learned that much in her six weeks behind these walls: nothing here came without a hook hidden somewhere in its flesh, and the finer the paper, the deeper the barb.

Tomas found her still holding it in the corridor outside her rooms. He had a way of appearing when she was least certain of herself, materializing out of doorways with his ledger-clutching, apologetic posture, as though he were permanently on the verge of confessing to something and hadn't yet decided what.

"You got one too," she said. It wasn't a question. He was carrying the same cream card the way a man carries a summons to his own trial.

"Everyone's gotten one." He glanced up and down the corridor, empty except for the two of them and the portraits of dead monarchs who watched everything with painted patience. "That's what worries me. Master Ellery hasn't held a public reading in nine years. He says the sky has been too boring to bother describing."

"And now?"

Tomas's throat moved. "And now he's asked for every soul in the palace to attend. Cooks. Grooms. Laundresses. He's never once cared whether the laundresses knew where the moon was going." He hesitated, then added, quieter, "He asked for you by name, Ines. Not 'the taster.' You."

*By name.* She thought of the courier who had come to Vhalen bearing a summons written with her mother's surname, the name her grandmother had tried to burn and failed. Names were currency here, and someone kept spending hers.

"Then I suppose we'd better find out what the sky wants," she said, and hated how steady her voice sounded. Steadiness had become her native language, the tongue of a woman who swallowed death for a living and called it breakfast.

* * *

They gathered in the great courtyard as the light went out of it, the whole palace poured into the open air like water finding its level. Ines stood near the back with the kitchen staff, close enough to smell the sweat of the scullery boys and the rosewater of the ladies who pretended not to notice them. Above them all, the first stars were pricking through a sky the color of a bruise healing.

She had expected a platform, an orator, pomp. Instead there was only a thin old man on a wooden ladder, adjusting a brass tube longer than she was tall, muttering to himself with the absent irritation of someone interrupted mid-prayer. Master Ellery looked less like a scholar than like a heron that had learned to wear robes — all neck and angles and sudden, unnerving stillness.

Queen Sabine sat beneath a canopy at the courtyard's edge, wrapped in gray silk, her face lit from below by a brazier so that her expression seemed to flicker between grief and hunger without ever settling on either. She did not look at Ines. She had not looked at Ines since the oath, six weeks ago, and Ines had begun to understand that this avoidance was not indifference. Indifference would have been a mercy. This was the look of a woman avoiding a debt.

Ellery climbed down from the ladder, consulted a sheaf of papers covered in diagrams that looked like spiderwebs drawn by a mathematician, and climbed back up. When he finally spoke, his voice carried across the courtyard with the strange flatness of a man reciting something he had rehearsed until the feeling had been worn off it like the gilding on old armor.

"The moon," he said, "will complete a thirteenth turn before the season ends."

Ines didn't understand at first why the courtyard went quiet the way a forest goes quiet — not silent, but *listening*, every small sound withdrawing as if the air itself had drawn breath. A thirteenth turn. She counted moons the way Vhalen counted them, by planting and harvest, and some years held thirteen full turnings; it was arithmetic, not apocalypse. Her grandmother had baked bread under thirteenth moons without comment.

But the court around her was not breathing. Somewhere in the crowd a woman began to pray, low and fast, the words tumbling over each other. And Ines felt the hair rise along her arms — because she had been in this palace long enough now to know what happened when people prayed here, and the knowing sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone.

Ellery continued, merciless in his precision. "The last recorded thirteenth turn occurred four hundred and eleven years ago. I have read the accounts. I will share them, because ignorance is a kindness we can no longer afford." He lifted the papers, and his hands, Ines noticed, were shaking — not the tremor of age but the tremor of a man who had spent months hoping he was wrong. "In the year of the last thirteenth turn, the borders of the kingdom were different. There was a valley in the eastern mountains that does not appear on our maps. There was a hymn sung in this court whose composer no record names. And there are forty entries in the choir roster of that year, of which thirty-nine can be accounted for."

He lowered the papers.

"The fortieth singer left no grave, no descendants, and no explanation. On the night the thirteenth turn completed, every prayer spoken in this kingdom was answered at once. Every single one. The chronicles call it the Night of Plenty. They also record that within a month, one in five households had buried someone they loved, and the word 'plenty' stops appearing in the text."

The brazier popped. Queen Sabine's hands were folded in her lap, knuckles white as candlewax, and she was staring at Ellery with an intensity that had nothing to do with astronomy.

"The dynasty has feared this omen since before it had a name," Ellery said. "Four hundred years of almanacs, each one checked against the sky, each one praying — if you'll forgive the word in this company — that the arithmetic would stay kind. It has not stayed kind. The thirteenth turn begins in nineteen days." He folded his papers with terrible care. "I am sorry. That is the whole of my report. The rest is not mine to tell."

He climbed down from the ladder and did not look at anyone, and the courtyard erupted into a murmur like a hive struck with a stick.

Ines stood very still among the cooks and the grooms and let the cold move through her slowly, the way she had trained her body to let poison move through it — acknowledged, measured, refused permission to do damage. Nineteen days. Forty singers. A valley that did not appear on maps.

A valley she knew intimately, because she had been born inside its silence.

* * *

She did not go back to her rooms. Her feet made the decision, carrying her through corridors gone strange with rumor — servants crossing themselves with the wrong hand, guards whispering about their mothers' prophecies, a page boy running with his arms full of candles as if light itself could be stockpiled against what was coming — until she reached the chapel.

It was empty at this hour. The answered prayers still sat in their alcoves where the priests had not dared remove them: the silver rattle that whispered, the honeycomb that wept, the little carved bird whose feathers were warm no matter the season. The gift that had killed the kitchen boy had been locked in an iron box in the crypt below, but Ines could feel the shape of its absence anyway, the way you feel a missing tooth.

She stood in the doorway and let herself think the thoughts she had been refusing for six weeks, arranging them now in order, the way she arranged symptoms after a tasting.

One: the gods had begun answering prayers in this kingdom, and the answers had teeth.

Two: the last time the moon turned thirteen times, a choir sang, and a woman vanished, and a valley disappeared from every map — and her grandmother had taught her, as a lullaby, a song with more verses than any lullaby needed.

Three: a courier had crossed an impossible border to fetch her, by name, by her mother's name, to stand in a palace and put things in her mouth.

Her whole life she had believed her immunity was a craft — her grandmother's patient work, bitter teas and graduated doses, a valley girl made careful. Useful, yes. Remarkable, perhaps. But *hers*, built by her own swallowing, earned drop by drop.

Standing in the chapel with the warm feathered bird watching her with carved eyes, she let herself consider, for the first time, the other possibility. That she had not been built. That she had been *made*. That somewhere in Vhalen, a grandmother who burned royal summonses and spoke to no god had nevertheless raised a granddaughter to be exactly the thing a dying dynasty would need to send into a palace full of hungry divinity — a vessel that could not be poisoned, mortal or otherwise.

You do not build a lock unless you know there is something that needs keeping out.

Or keeping in.

"Ines."

Tomas stood in the chapel entrance, lamplight behind him, and his face told her before he spoke that he had come to say something he had been carrying for weeks, something heavy enough to have bent him gradually into that apologetic shape she had mistaken for his nature.

"You heard Ellery," he said. It wasn't a question either. Everyone had heard; the whole palace was one held breath now. He came closer, stopped, wrung his hands. "There's something you should know. About why the court wanted a taster who could survive divine poison. About why they sent for *you*."

"I'm listening."

He looked at the alcove of gifts, at the iron box's absence below, at anything but her face. "The taster's post has always been two posts," he said. "There's the one in the charter — the one about arsenic and nightshade. And there's the older one. The one from four hundred years ago." His voice dropped as though the walls might be taking notes. "When the thirteenth turn comes, the court doesn't try to stop what happens. It can't. What it does — what it has always done, according to the sealed records — is provide a vessel. Someone who can stand at the center of it and survive. Someone the gods can't kill outright, so that whatever comes... has somewhere to go that isn't the rest of us."

The cold in Ines's chest finished its work and settled, permanent as a scar.

"A sacrifice post," she said.

"They don't use that word." Tomas's laugh was a dry, broken thing. "They call it 'the office of preservation.' They gave it apartments and a stipend and a title. But Ines — the last taster, the one before you, Marguerite—" He stopped. Went pale. Stopped again, harder, as if he'd stepped on a stair that wasn't there.

Ines felt the chapel tilt. "Say that name again."

"I shouldn't have—"

"*Tomas.*"

He stared at her, and she watched him do the arithmetic she had just done — the dead mother's surname on the summons, the valley off the maps, the lullaby with too many verses — and watched him arrive, horrified, at the sum.

"That was her name," he whispered. "The fortieth singer. The one with no grave. The taster who survived everything and vanished on the night of the last thirteenth turn." He pressed his hand over his mouth. "Gods keep me. They didn't send for a taster. They sent for a *daughter*. They've been waiting a generation for someone with her blood, because the last one worked too well — because whatever the Door needed, it needed *her*, and it will need—"

He didn't finish. He didn't have to. The chapel's carved bird sat warm in its alcove, and somewhere above them both the moon was already climbing toward its impossible thirteenth turning, patient as appetite, and Ines understood at last that she had never been invited to this palace at all.

She had been *collected*.

Nineteen days, said the astronomer. Nineteen days, and tomorrow evening there was a banquet, and the queen would expect her at the table, tasting.

Ines straightened her spine the way she did before every dish, professional and calm, and inside her the betrayal turned over once, slowly, like a stone in the bed of a frozen river — and began, very quietly, to sharpen itself into purpose.
