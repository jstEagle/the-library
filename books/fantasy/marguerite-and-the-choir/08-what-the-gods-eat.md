## 8. What the Gods Eat

The banquet was for the ambassador from Serevan, which meant the fish would be excellent and the wine would be older than the ambassador's country, and both would pass through my mouth before they reached anyone else's.

I had learned the rhythm of these evenings the way I had once learned the rhythm of Vhalen's seasons: not by being told, but by watching what the world did with its hands. The stewards moved in a slow orbit around the high table. The queen sat at the center with her hands folded and her eyes on the middle distance, the way she always did when she was listening to something none of us could hear. The king drank too much and laughed too loudly at his own jokes. And I sat one place below the salt, in the taster's chair that had been built too small for me, eating small deliberate portions of everything meant for the royal table while forty pairs of eyes pretended not to watch me swallow.

I had stopped tasting food as food months ago. Everything arrived at my tongue already translated — this is almond, this is arsenic's cousin, this is merely butter gone slightly past its prime. My mouth had become an instrument of measurement rather than pleasure. Grandmother would have said that was the price of the work. Grandmother would have said a great many things about prices, most of them true.

The fish came seventh, after the quail and before the sugared plums, laid out on a silver platter shaped like a leaping trout so elaborate it barely resembled anything that had ever swum. The sauce was pale gold, threaded with saffron, and it smelled — wrong.

Not spoiled. Not poisoned in any way I had a name for. Wrong the way a room is wrong when someone has just left it, the way a hymn is wrong when one voice drops out mid-phrase. I stood over the first bite for three full seconds longer than protocol required, and I felt the whole table lean toward me without moving, the way water leans toward a drain.

I ate it.

I want to be honest about why, because honesty has become the only thing I own outright. I ate it because I was tired. Because I had tasted eleven dishes that night and found nothing, and because some small stubborn part of me — the part that had spent nineteen years building walls inside my own body, grain by grain, venom by venom — wanted to believe that all this careful ruin I had made of myself was for something. That if death ever came for the royal house of Sabine's line, it would find me standing in the doorway with my borrowed blood and my grayed vision and my stuttering heart, and it would turn away, cheated.

So I ate the fish, and the saffron sauce was sweet at first, and then it was not sweet at all.

* * *

What happened next did not happen to my body. That is the only way I can describe it, and I have tried, in the weeks since, to describe it to exactly no one.

There was no burning. No cramping, no numbness spreading up my arms, none of the familiar catalog of harms I knew the way other girls knew dance steps. Instead there was a sensation of being *read*. Of something vast and patient passing over me the way a finger passes over the spines of books on a shelf, looking for a particular title. I felt it pause on me. I felt it consider.

And then it moved on, the way a wolf moves past a scarecrow.

I set down my fork. My hand did not shake, which surprised me; everything else in me was shaking. Around the table the conversation continued, the ambassador saying something diplomatic about river rights, the king laughing his wet laugh. No one had seen. Whatever had come in on the fish had come for the royal blood at that table, and it had found mine instead, and mine had been — insufficient. Uninteresting. A valley girl's blood, thick with four hundred years of poisons deliberately survived, registering to whatever this was as nothing at all. Not even food.

I looked down the length of the table at Queen Sabine. She was looking at me. Not at my face — at my hands, resting on the tablecloth, ordinary hands, a taster's hands. Her expression was one I had never seen her wear in public: naked, hungry, afraid. It lasted less than a breath before she smoothed it away behind her crown, but I had seen it, and seeing it changed the shape of the evening entirely.

Because I understood then, in the wordless way one understands falling, that the poison had not failed. It had simply found nothing to eat.

* * *

They took me to the infirmary afterward anyway, because procedure demanded it, and because Steward Tomas had appeared at my elbow within moments of my setting down the fork, materializing the way he always did, a tall gray shadow smelling of ink and candle smoke.

"You've gone white," he said.

"I'm always white."

"Whiter." He walked me through the service corridors, his hand hovering near my elbow without touching, the way he'd done since the day we met — a courtesy that never quite became contact, as though he were unsure whether I was a person or a piece of palace furniture that might bruise. "Describe it."

"There's nothing to describe. Nothing happened."

"Ines." He said my name carefully, the way you handle a dish that might be hot. "The kitchen boy who died in the spring — Marta's boy, Pieter — they found him beside a prayer-gift with no mark on him at all. No wound, no toxin we could name. His face was—" He stopped walking. In the torchlight his face was the color of the corridor stones. "His face was *empty*. Like something had eaten the expression off it. If nothing happened to you, tell me what nothing felt like."

And because Tomas had shown me the chapel, and shown me the gifts that bite, and because he was the only soul in this palace who spoke to me as though I were more than a functioning organ, I told him.

I told him about the reading. The finger along the shelf of me. The pause, the consideration, the turning away. He listened with the terrible attentiveness of a man taking dictation from a ghost, and when I finished he was quiet for a long moment, and then he said:

"It didn't want you."

"No."

"Do you understand what that means? The things in the chapel — the answered prayers — they take. That's what answering *is*, now. They take something. Hair, years, names, faces. They took Pieter whole." His voice dropped until it was barely above the sound of the torches. "Whatever was in that fish looked at you and found nothing worth taking."

"Thank you," I said. "That's very comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be." He started walking again, faster. "It was meant to frighten you. Because it frightens me, and I find misery loves accuracy."

* * *

By morning, the story had made its way through the palace the way water makes its way through plaster — invisibly, everywhere, leaving stains.

They say the taster ate the ambassador's gift and did not die. They say the old gods themselves reached for her and closed their hands on air. By the time I crossed the courtyard to the chapel for my morning duty, the servants had begun doing a thing I had never seen servants do: making way. Not the polite parting of bodies around a superior — something older and stranger than that. They stepped aside and *looked down*, some of them, the way country folk step around a standing stone. A scullery maid pressed something into my hand as I passed — a crust of bread, wrapped in cloth — and fled before I could return it.

An offering. She had given me an offering.

I stood in the middle of the courtyard holding a heel of bread like a live coal, and I understood that something in me had shifted overnight, some border redrawn. Last week I had been a tool. Tools are used and maintained and ignored. This morning I had become a thing people placated. And there is no loneliness quite like the loneliness of being feared correctly — of having the crowd's instinct confirm, in their bowed heads and averted eyes, the thing you have been trying not to know about yourself since the fish.

That I am not like them anymore. That perhaps I never was. That the valley built me into a vessel, and vessels do not get to be guests at the feast.

* * *

Queen Sabine summoned me that afternoon.

Her receiving room was small, for a queen's — she preferred small rooms, I had noticed, rooms where prayers would have nowhere to echo. She dismissed her ladies with a flick of two fingers and stood at the window with her back to me for long enough that I began counting the folds in the curtain for something to do. When she finally turned, her face was composed into its usual serene mask, but the mask fit poorly today. It kept slipping at the mouth.

"You ate the ambassador's gift," she said.

"Yes, Majesty."

"And lived."

"Yes, Majesty."

"The physicians found nothing in your blood. Nothing in the fish, either — the entire platter has been fed to the hogs and the hogs are thriving. Which means either the poison was never there, or it was somewhere the physicians cannot look." She crossed the room slowly, her rings clicking against one another, and stopped close enough that I could see the fine map of sleeplessness under her powder. "Which do you think it was, taster?"

I thought about lying. I had gotten good at the small lies a servant tells — yes, Majesty, no, Majesty, I couldn't say, Majesty. But lying to Sabine felt like lying to a well: the words went down and down and never came back, and meanwhile she was drawing up buckets of me.

"It looked at me," I said. "Whatever it was. It looked, and it wasn't interested."

Something moved behind her eyes — hope, I realized. Hope, sharp and awful, the way hunger is sharp and awful. "Not interested."

"My blood isn't— " I searched for words and found only the truth, bare and strange. "My blood doesn't register. To that kind of poison. The kind that comes from *them*. I'm from Vhalen, Majesty. We were sealed away from magic for four hundred years. Whatever runs in us, it doesn't — it doesn't speak their language."

She was quiet for a moment. Then she laughed, one short note, without any joy in it whatsoever.

"Four hundred years," she said. "My dynasty has ruled this country for six hundred, and in all that time we never once needed a girl from a valley that isn't on any map. And now." She turned back to the window. Below, in the courtyard, the fountain played its endless meaningless music. "Do you know what my grandmother's grandmother wrote in her private book, the night before she died? One line. *The thirteenth moon comes for every house, and eats the ones that pray.*"

"Majesty—"

"That will be all, taster." She didn't turn around. "Eat well. Sleep well. Take care of that blood of yours." A pause, and then, quieter, almost to herself: "It may be the only thing in this palace the gods can't digest."

I bowed to her back and let myself out, and in the corridor outside, my hands finally began to shake.

* * *

That evening I did something I had not done since arriving at the palace. I went looking for the choir.

Not to listen — I told myself it was not to listen, though the lie was thin enough to see through. The hymn had been following me for days now, humming up through the flagstones whenever the choir rehearsed, a sound like the building itself remembering. And after the fish, after the queen's grandmother's warning scratched in some dead woman's book, after the scullery maid's bread in my pocket, I wanted — I needed — to stand near the one thing in this palace that predated the gods' returning. The one thing nobody could date. The one thing that had been here the last time, whatever *the last time* was.

The rehearsal loft sat above the chapel, reachable by a spiral stair that smelled of dust and candle fat. I climbed until the singing filled my ears, and then I stopped on the landing beneath the floor grate and let it pour over me.

Forty voices. I counted them without meaning to, the way I counted courses, ingredients, heartbeats. Forty voices braided into a melody that had no beginning I could find — it seemed to start in the middle of itself, as though it had been going on forever and the singers had merely stepped into it. And underneath the sung notes there was the other sound, the one I had heard my first week here and convinced myself I'd imagined: the walls, answering. A low harmonic rising out of the stone itself, out of the mortar, out of the ground the palace stood on, so deep I felt it in my teeth, in the bones of my feet, in the bread still wrapped in my pocket.

The hymn was not being sung *in* the chapel. The hymn was being sung *to* it. Or — the thought arrived cold and complete, uninvited — the hymn was being sung *through* it, the way a key is turned in a lock it was cut for.

A door opened above me. Light spilled down the stairwell, and with it a voice like dry paper.

"You again."

Odile, the choirmaster, stood at the top of the stairs. She was small and straight-backed and old in the way of trees — bent toward her work, not toward her years. In her arms she carried a ledger, a great swollen thing bound in cracked leather, and she held it the way a soldier holds a weapon: ready, and tired of the weight.

"I wasn't—"

"You were listening." She descended two steps and stopped, studying me with eyes the color of rainwater. "You were listening the way your mother listened."

The stone seemed to tilt beneath my feet. "You knew my mother?"

For a long moment she said nothing. Somewhere below us, the choir reached the end of a phrase and hung there, unresolved, waiting for a verse that never came — I heard them falter, restart, falter again, forty voices circling a gap in the music like birds circling an empty nest.

Then Odile stepped back and held the ledger against her chest, and looked at me the way no one in this palace had ever looked at me: not as a tool, not as a talisman, not as a relic. As a missing piece.

"Come up, taster," she said. "We need to talk about verse eleven."
