## 9. Odile's Ledger

The choirmaster's rooms smelled of tallow and old paper, of ink gone brown in its pots, of the particular dust that gathers only on things no one has been permitted to touch for a very long time.

Ines stood just inside the doorway while Odile bolted it behind her — three bolts, then a fourth she kept in a drawer, iron, older than the others, which she seated into a bracket worn smooth by decades of use. The lamplight was low and amber and made the room feel like the inside of an instrument: shelves rising on every wall like ribs, scores stacked in drifts, a harpsichord under a dust sheet in the corner that someone had loved badly and often.

"You came," Odile said. She did not sound pleased about it.

"You told me if I ever wanted to know why the walls hummed—"

"I told you to stay away from the choir loft." Odile crossed to the hearth, where a kettle sat waiting over coals banked to a dull red. "And instead you stood in the loft for the better part of an hour with your mouth open like a landed fish."

"It wasn't humming," Ines said. "That's what I couldn't stop thinking about. Walls don't hum. Stone doesn't hum. It was—" She searched for the word and found it had been sitting in her chest since that afternoon, patient as a stone at the bottom of a stream. "It was *listening*. The way a room listens when everyone in it has stopped breathing."

Odile's hands went still on the kettle handle. Steam began to rise, thin and silver, between them.

"Sit down, taster," she said quietly. "And before you drink anything in this room, I want you to remember that I am the only person in this palace who has never once tried to poison you."

"That's a strange thing to boast of."

"In this palace it is the highest compliment available." Odile poured two cups of something dark and bitter-smelling — chicory, Ines guessed, and not much else; the choirmaster drank like a woman who had forgotten food existed — and set one within reach. Then she went to the shelf behind her desk, the one locked behind a grate of blackened wire, and drew out a book.

Not a book. A ledger. It was the size of a paving slab, bound in leather so dark it might once have been black or might simply have absorbed everything it had ever touched, and it was thick enough that Odile carried it the way porters carry stone, against her body, both arms, her chin steadying the top edge. She laid it on the desk with a sound like a coffin lid settling.

"Thirty years," Odile said. "Thirty years I have worked on this. I was younger than you when I started. I had better eyes, then, and worse hands." She flexed her fingers, and Ines saw for the first time how the knuckles were swollen, the way a stonemason's are, or a laundress's. Not a singer's hands at all. A copyist's. "I have transcribed every note of the Vesper Hymn — that's what the court calls it, the Vesper Hymn, because calling it by its real name would require admitting it has one — four hundred and eleven times. I have copied it onto vellum, onto paper, onto slate, once onto my own forearm when the ink ran out at midnight and I could not bear to lose the phrase. And in thirty years, taster, I have learned exactly one true thing about it."

She opened the ledger.

The pages were not music. Or rather, they were not *only* music. Yes, there were staves, five lines ruled in faded red, and notes in a hand so precise it looked printed. But around the staves, crowding them, climbing the margins, filling every empty corner of the page, were drawings. Maps. Ines leaned closer without meaning to and saw rivers rendered in careful ink-hatching, mountain ranges drawn as rows of small teeth, a coastline that curled like a sleeping animal along the bottom edge of the page. Place names in a script she did not recognize, though the letterforms teased at something — they resembled the letters stitched inside the collar of her grandmother's winter coat, the ones her grandmother said were just old-fashioned decoration.

"What is this country?" Ines asked. "I don't know these names."

"No," Odile said. "You wouldn't. Nobody would. That is rather the point." She turned the page. More staves, more margins, more map. A second coastline. A third. "Every verse of the hymn marks a location. Verse one is a well — here, see, the notation descends through two octaves, down and down, the way water goes down. Verse three is a bridge; the melody crosses itself, the alto line passes back over the soprano line at exactly the point where the drawing shows the river crossing the road. Verse seven is a hillfort — the harmony stacks in fifths, tier on tier, like walls. I thought for years I was imagining it. I told myself a lonely woman sees patterns in lace and clouds. But then I found the correspondence."

"The correspondence?"

Odile turned to the back of the ledger, past a hundred pages of transcription, to a pocket sewn into the cover, and drew out a single sheet, brittle and foxed with age. It was a fragment of an old survey — Ines recognized the format from the palace archive, the crown's cartographers' house style — but the territory it depicted matched nothing on any modern map. The rivers were wrong. The mountains were in the wrong places. The coast ran where inland plains should have been.

"This survey predates the dynasty," Odile said. "Predates the treaty, predates the border settlements, predates everything the history books agree to begin with. Look at the river names."

Ines looked. Her mouth had gone dry, and not from the chicory. One name in particular she read three times, tracing the hatched blue line of a river that ran through the heart of the vanished country, and the name was *Vhalen*.

The word seemed to move off the page and stand in the room with them.

"There was a country," Ines said slowly. "Before. Where the valley—"

"Where your valley is now, yes. Where half the border provinces are now. Where the capital itself may have stood, though the scholars get very quiet and very busy when you ask them that directly." Odile took the survey back with the tenderness of someone handling a wound dressing. "The hymn is a map of it. Every verse, a place. Every place, a verse. Sing the whole hymn in order and you have sung your way across an entire nation that officially never existed. I have spent thirty years proving that, and do you know what the court gave me for it? A larger allowance for candles. They think I am a crank. A harmless, obsessive old woman who collects dead songs. Which suits me fine, because the alternative — the alternative is that someone in power takes me seriously, and then people start asking the question I have spent thirty years trying not to ask out loud."

"Which is?"

"If the hymn is a map," Odile said, "what is it a map *to*? Maps are made for travelers. Someone wrote directions across forty verses of sacred music, hid them in plain sight in a royal chapel, and let four hundred years of choirs sing them every vespers without knowing. You don't make a map like that for a picnic, taster. You make it for someone who needs to find their way — or for someone who needs to make sure no one else ever does."

* * *

The lamp burned lower. Odile trimmed the wick with a pair of small silver scissors and the light steadied, and in that steadied light Ines saw the ledger properly for the first time — saw that the last third of it was different. The transcription continued, verse after verse, but the hand grew looser, faster, almost desperate, and the marginal maps thinned, and then, somewhere past verse ten, they stopped altogether.

"Here," Odile said, and her voice changed — flattened, the way voices flatten when they press down on something heavy. "Verse eleven. This is where thirty years ends."

The page showed the opening bars of the eleventh verse and then nothing. Blank staves, ruled and waiting, running down the rest of the page and onto the next and the next. Dozens of them. Empty lines like unplanted furrows.

"There's no text for it?" Ines asked. "No score?"

"There's text. There's even score — fragments. A bar here, a phrase there, salvaged from a psalter that burned in the reign of the second Sabine, from a funeral roll nobody can date, from the memory of a basso who sang it once as a boy and remembered only that it made his mother weep." Odile tapped the blank staves with one swollen knuckle. "But the verse as a whole? Gone. Every source that contained it whole was destroyed, or lost, or — and this is the part that keeps me up at night, taster, the part I will tell you now and then deny telling you — *removed*. Deliberately. Systematically. Four hundred years ago somebody went to extraordinary lengths to make sure verse eleven of the Vesper Hymn would never be sung again. And they nearly succeeded."

"Nearly."

"Nearly." Odile looked at her then, fully, and Ines felt the look like a hand laid flat against her sternum. "Because a melody is a stubborn animal. It hides. It gets into mothers and comes out of them again in nurseries, disguised as nothing, disguised as a song to rock a child to sleep. For thirty years I have chased fragments. And tonight I watched you stand in my loft, hearing the Vesper Hymn for the first time in your life, and when we reached the gap — when the whole choir fell silent where verse eleven should be — you *moved*. Your lips moved. Your throat moved. You were singing something, girl, something that fit the silence the way a key fits the lock it was cut for."

The room was very quiet. The coals ticked in the hearth. Somewhere far above, muffled by floors and stone, the palace went on being a palace — doors, footsteps, the ordinary machinery of a court that believed its oldest hymn was only a hymn.

"I don't know what you mean," Ines said.

"Don't lie to me. I'm too old and too tired and I have given you the only secret I have left, which makes us, God help us both, allies of a kind." Odile's voice cracked on the word and she covered it with a cough. "Allies. I never wanted one. Allies get people burned. But the moon is turning wrong, the gods have started answering prayers with teeth, and a kitchen boy is dead, and I find I no longer have the luxury of working alone. So I will ask you once, plainly, and you will decide what kind of person you want to be in the chapters ahead."

She turned the ledger around so the blank staves faced Ines. Empty lines. Waiting lines. Thirty years of a woman's life, ending in silence.

"Where did you learn verse eleven?"

* * *

Ines did not answer immediately. She wrapped both hands around the cooling cup of chicory because her hands needed occupation or they would have shaken, and she looked at the fire, and she let the truth come up in her the way water comes up in a well — slowly, bucket by bucket, from a depth she had never bothered to measure.

It was a lullaby. Of course it was a lullaby. It had always been a lullaby.

Her grandmother had sung it on winter nights when the wind came down off the ridge and made the shutters talk, sitting by the hearth with her mending in her lap, her voice low and rough and unhurried. It had no words her grandmother would explain — or rather, it had words, but they were in the old tongue, the coat-collar letters, and when child-Ines asked what they meant her grandmother said, *they mean sleep, little one, they mean the valley keeps you,* and sang the next verse. There were verses Ines had never heard anywhere else. There was one verse especially, the one her grandmother saved for last, the one that always made the fire seem to lean in —

The one that came after the tenth.

"I know it as a lullaby," Ines said at last. "From Vhalen. From my grandmother. Everyone in my village knows pieces of it — the cradle songs, the harvest rounds — I thought they were ours. I thought they were just *ours*, valley songs, the way valley things are." She heard her own voice go strange. "You're telling me they're not. You're telling me my grandmother has been singing me a piece of a map my whole life. A map to a country that doesn't exist. A verse that someone four hundred years ago killed to bury."

"I'm telling you," Odile said, "that the missing verse survived. In the one place in the world the destroyers couldn't reach." She let that sit, and Ines felt it settle over her shoulders like a wet cloak. "Think, taster. Think about what kind of place survives a purge of songs. What kind of place keeps what the rest of the world was made to forget."

"A place outside the maps," Ines whispered.

"A place erased from every treaty. A place no courier reaches, no priest visits, no prayer leaves." Odile's eyes were bright and terrible in the lamplight. "A sealed room preserves whatever was locked inside it. Your valley isn't a refuge, girl. It's an archive. And you —" she reached out and closed Ines's fingers, one by one, around the cup she'd forgotten she was holding — "you walked out of that archive carrying the one verse I have spent thirty years digging graves for. So. Will you sing it for me?"

Everything in Ines wanted to say yes. It rose in her like the hum in the chapel walls, like the listening stone, like the longing she had carried since childhood on the border stones — *to be heard, to be answered, to matter somewhere beyond the valley's edge.* Here was a woman who had given her hands, her eyes, her whole quiet life to this, asking for the one thing Ines had gotten free, without knowing, in a grandmother's lap.

And that was precisely why she didn't say yes. Not yet.

Her mother had sung it too. She knew that the way you know things you've never been told — her grandmother had mentioned it once, only once, in the tone reserved for the dead: *your mother had the truer ear for it.* Her mother, whose surname the royal summons had used. Her mother, who had left Vhalen and never explained, and died beyond the stones, and been buried without a marker.

A lullaby didn't need to be hidden. A map did. And if her mother had carried a hidden thing out of a sealed country, into the world, toward this palace—

"Not tonight," Ines said.

Odile's face closed like a door. "After everything I've—"

"After everything you've shown me, yes. And I'm grateful, and I'll help you, and I'll swear that on anything you like. But I want to write to my grandmother first. I want to ask her what our lullabies really are before I give the last verse of yours to a stranger in a palace where the gods have started answering prayers." Ines set down the cup and stood. "You waited thirty years, choirmaster. Wait a few days more. If I'm right about what my family has been carrying — and I think I'm right, and I think you think so too — then neither of us wants to sing that verse alone in this room."

For a long moment Odile said nothing. Then, slowly, she laughed — a dry, rusted sound, like a hinge finding oil after decades.

"Alone," she repeated. "Yes. Well. You're smarter than the last person who found this ledger." She was already moving to the wire grate, returning the great book to its shelf, her back to Ines. "There wasn't a last person, actually. You're the first. Congratulations. Go home, taster. Bolt nothing behind you — the locks in this palace are decorative. And Ines—"

Ines paused at the door, her hand on the cold iron ring.

"When you write to your grandmother," Odile said, without turning around, "ask her one question for me. Ask her why a valley that bans all magic teaches its children, in their cradles, the exact melody that opens—" She stopped herself. Pressed her lips together. "Ask her that. And then come back and tell me, because I have wanted to know the answer for thirty years, and lately I've begun to suspect it's the same answer to everything."

Ines walked back through the sleeping corridors with the ledger's blank staves printed on the inside of her eyelids, and the lullaby rising unbidden in her throat, sweet and low and terrible, a cradle song that was also a road, a road that ended — she understood now, walking through the dark toward her narrow bed — at a door.

And her grandmother had taught it to her first.
