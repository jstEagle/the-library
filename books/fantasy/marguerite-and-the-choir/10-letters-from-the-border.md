## 10. Letters from the Border

The letter came on a morning of thin rain, the kind that did not fall so much as hang in the air like a held breath.

Ines found it on her windowsill when she drew back the curtain — a square of pale paper, sealed with plain wax, lying precisely in the center of the stone ledge as though it had grown there. No courier had climbed the palace wall. No servant had entered her room; she had locked the door herself the night before, an old habit from the valley where you learned early to guard what little was yours. The latch was still thrown. The window opened onto a sheer drop and a courtyard four stories below, where two grooms were leading out the queen's gray mare through puddles that reflected the sky like spilled mercury.

She stood for a long moment with her hand still on the curtain, watching the rain move through the light without touching anything.

Then she picked up the letter.

The paper was valley-made. She knew it before she knew she knew it — knew it the way you know your mother's voice in a crowd, by something beneath recognition. It was beaten from flax stalks rather than rags, and it carried a faint sweetness, the ghost of the ash reeds that grew along the Vhalen river and that no one outside the border stones had ever managed to cultivate. Her grandmother had made paper every autumn, pressing the pulp between woolen cloths, hanging the sheets in the rafters where they curled and dried like strange pale leaves. Ines had spent whole childhood afternoons doing exactly this: holding a sheet up to the window, tilting it until the fibers caught the light.

She tilted this one. The fibers caught the light.

Her hands were steady. That was the first thing she noticed about herself, the way you notice a stranger's odd gesture — that her hands did not shake, because she had trained them not to, because a taster whose hands trembled would one day spill a poison into her own mouth at the wrong moment or drop a suspect dish and start a panic. Steadiness was not calm. It was only a shape fear had learned to take in her body, the way water takes the shape of whatever holds it.

The seal was unmarked wax, pressed with nothing but a thumbprint. She broke it and unfolded three pages of small, slanted handwriting she had known since before she could read.

*Little wren.*

*I do not know if this will reach you, or how, or what it will cost me to send it. I have never written to anyone beyond the stones. There has never been anyone beyond the stones worth writing to, and no way to write to them besides. But the old rules are loosening. I can feel it the way you feel weather coming in your knees. Things are moving that should not move.*

*You are there because I failed to stop you, and I am sorry, and I am proud, and both of those things are true and they do not cancel each other. You went through the Door. Understand me now, while you are still far enough away to hear this clearly:*

*The Door opens from the inside too.*

*I let you believe the valley was sheltered. Every child in Vhalen is raised on that word — sheltered — as though we live under a great bell of glass and everything wild is kept out. It is a kind story. It is even mostly true. But listen:*

*A wall keeps things out. A lock keeps things in. What your mother died building is not a wall.*

*Do not taste anything given freely. Do not pray, even in jest, even in anger, even alone. If anyone asks you what Vhalen smells like in autumn, lie.*

*I will try to send more. Burn this after reading.*

*Your grandmother, who loves you and lied to you, in that order.*

Ines read it three times. Then she sat down on the edge of her narrow bed, in her narrow room, in a palace built by a dynasty that had forgotten why it feared the moon, and she held the letter very still, the way you hold a bird that has flown into your house — gently, and with the growing understanding that something is in the wrong place entirely.

*Burn this after reading.*

She almost laughed. Her grandmother had seen the summons burn and rise again as an unbroken seal on the table; of course she would know that fire was no longer a reliable answer. Still, Ines rose, lit the stub of candle on her desk, and touched the flame to the corner of the first page.

The paper caught. Burned clean and quick, flax-paper always did, sweet-smelling, the ash reeds giving up their ghost a second time. She fed all three pages to the flame over the basin and watched the last curl of handwriting go black, and when the ash had cooled she stirred it with a spoon until it was indistinguishable from any other burnt letter.

Then she turned around, and the letter was lying on her bed.

Whole. Unburned. Sealed again with its blank thumbprint of wax, resting on the gray blanket as neatly as if it had been placed there by a servant — except that the door was still latched, and the window still opened onto rain and empty air, and the basin at her feet held the true ash of the true paper, which she could see when she looked down, fine and gray and real.

Ines looked at it for a long time.

Then, deliberately, methodically — because method was the only prayer she trusted — she folded the letter along its original creases, wrapped it in oilcloth against damp, and hid it inside the false bottom of her spice box, beneath the tin of powdered chalkroot she used to settle her stomach after hard tastings. Some things you burned because burning was the instruction. And some things you kept because the instruction itself had been written by someone who expected the fire to fail, and wanted you to understand that too.

* * *

She went looking for Tomas.

She found him in the steward's corridor below the chapel, arguing quietly with a laundress about a shortage of bleaching ash, and she watched him for a moment before announcing herself — watched the careful economy of his movements, the way he listened with his whole body, the way his eyes flicked once to the chapel door behind him and away again. He had been kind to her since her arrival, in the specific way of a man who knows where the bodies are buried and has decided you should know where the shovels are kept. He had shown her the chapel. He had shown her the gifts. He had told her about the kitchen boy, Piers, fourteen years old, who had prayed for his sick mother and received an answer that killed him.

"Tomas," she said, when the laundress had gone. "A question about post."

"Post?"

"How does a letter enter this palace?"

He gave her the look he gave her whenever she asked a question that was simpler than it sounded. "By road, usually. Courier to the gatehouse, gatehouse to the under-steward, under-steward to me. Why?"

"No other ways?"

He counted on his fingers, dryly. "Carrier pigeon, if the message is short and the sender trusts hawks. Diplomatic pouch, which is just a courier with better boots. Smuggler, during market season, though they bring wine and take silver rather than the reverse." He paused. His fingers stopped moving. "And the river," he said slowly, "though nothing comes up the river anymore. The mills were abandoned before my grandfather's time. Why do you ask?"

"Did anything arrive for me this morning? Anything at all, from anyone, however strange?"

"No." He said it flatly, and then heard what she hadn't said, and his face changed. "Ines. Did something arrive?"

"I don't know yet." Which was the truth, and not the whole truth, and they both knew the shape of the gap between those things. "If something arrives for me that no one delivered — don't log it. Don't report it. Just tell me."

"Ines—"

"Please."

He studied her. Rain-gray light came through the corridor's slit windows and lay across the flagstones in long pale bars, and somewhere above them, muffled by floors, the choir had begun its morning rehearsal — a low drone of warming voices, rising and settling like something breathing in its sleep. Since the day in the loft, Ines had been aware of that sound the way you become aware of a heartbeat: constantly, and only ever in the background, until it stops.

"All right," Tomas said at last. "Nothing gets logged. But you'll tell me eventually."

"Eventually," she agreed, and did not believe either of them.

* * *

That afternoon she walked the palace library.

Not the archive — Odile had warned her off the archive, or rather had warned her off being *seen* in the archive, which in a court was the same thing done more politely. The open library was safe. Safe was useful. Safe was where you went to read histories of the kingdom's borders, and histories of borders were, it turned out, remarkably difficult to find.

She had assumed this would be simple. Borders were the bones of every atlas; surely someone had written about the drawing of them. But the books gave her strange silences instead. There were treaties referencing territories by name — Corvane, Estmere, the Marchlands of Ille — and maps showing their boundaries, and chronicles describing wars fought over them, and nowhere, in none of it, was there a single mention of the country that lay between the Marchlands and the western sea. Not as conquered. Not as ceded. Not as vanished. Simply absent, the way a missing tooth is absent: the surrounding records leaned inward slightly, toward the gap, without acknowledging there was a gap at all.

One map, older than the others, cracked along its folds and stained with what might have been wine or might have been blood, showed the region as blank parchment — not unexplored, not sea, but *blank*, with a single notation in faded brown ink along its edge:

*Here the surveyors' instruments turned. Do not amend.*

She copied the words onto her cuff in pencil, then sat for a while with the map open on the table in front of her, listening to the rain against the high windows and to the small sounds of the library — pages, footsteps, the scratching of a copyist's pen two tables away — and tried to make herself think clearly.

Her grandmother had taught her the valley's history as a catechism. Long ago, the priestess Almeida — their blood, their name, though the grandmother said it reluctantly, as though the name cost something — had sealed Vhalen away from magic to save it. Magic was a flood; the priestess had built a levee. Inside the levee, prayers went unanswered because there was nothing to answer them. Outside, the world went on. Simple. Clean. A wall.

But walls had two sides.

She thought of the border stones, and her chest tightened with a homesickness so sudden and physical it felt like injury. She had walked them every year of her childhood, her grandmother's hand on her shoulder, counting them: forty-one stones in a ring around the valley, each one taller than a woman, each one carved with the same spiral worn nearly smooth. Her grandmother had called them markers. *This is where our country ends, little wren. Everything past them belongs to the rest of the world.* And Ines had pressed her palm to the cold spiral and felt — nothing. That was the whole point. Nothing crossed. Nothing leaked. The valley slept inside its glass bell.

Except.

Except that the summons had come to a valley that received no post, borne by a courier who should not have known the road existed, addressed in her dead mother's surname — and it had burned and risen unburned. Except that her grandmother, who had spent eighty years teaching her that nothing crossed the stones, had somehow sent a letter across them, on valley paper, in a single night, by no road and no hand. Either the wall had fallen overnight — or it had never been a wall, and her grandmother had known, and had chosen the word *sheltered* the way you choose a soft blanket for a child: not because it is true, but because the truth would keep her awake.

*What your mother died building is not a wall.*

A lock keeps things in.

She laid her hand flat on the blank space on the map, over the place where Vhalen should have been, and the parchment was cool under her palm, cooler than the room, cool in a way that had direction — as though the blankness were not an absence of ink but a presence of something else, something the cartographer's pen had refused to touch.

And she understood, in the way you understand a fall only once you've already left the ground, that she had gotten the story backward her entire life.

Vhalen was not sheltered from magic. Magic was imprisoned *with* Vhalen. The priestess had not built a levee against a flood; she had built a cell, and put the flood inside it, and put her people inside it too — as wardens, or as hostages, or as food, and Ines's stomach turned cold at the third possibility and refused, for the moment, to examine it. Forty-one stones. Not markers. Bars. And every unanswered prayer spoken in the valley for four hundred years — every dying wish, every desperate whisper, every child's plea for rain or mercy or a mother's recovery — every one of them had gone unheard not because no one was listening, but because the listeners were *in there too*, pacing behind the stones, hearing everything, answering nothing, because answering was the one thing the lock prevented.

Her grandmother's knees ached before storms. Ines had always thought it a figure of speech.

*Things are moving that should not move.*

The thirteenth turn. The astronomer's white face in the observatory, the dynasty's four-hundred-year dread. Four hundred years — the same span as the silence. The lock was old, and locks grow tired, and somewhere in the palace a hymn hummed in the walls and a queen prayed to gods who had begun, after four centuries of enforced fasting, to answer.

And the letter's last instruction surfaced in her memory with new teeth: *If anyone asks you what Vhalen smells like in autumn, lie.*

Because the smell of ash reeds and woodsmoke and turned earth was a signature. Because somewhere in this palace there were things that knew that smell, and hungered after it, and her grandmother — practical, unsentimental, loving in the order of love-then-lie — had understood that her granddaughter smelled like home, and that home was the prison, and that prisons have doors, and that the things inside had waited four hundred years for someone to carry the key out.

Or to be the key. Or — she remembered the summons, the taster's post, the queen who would not meet her eyes — to be *sent for*.

The copyist's pen scratched. The rain hung in the air without falling. Somewhere overhead the choir moved into the hymn without a date, and the floorboards under Ines's feet hummed, faintly, in sympathy, and she realized she was gripping the edge of the table hard enough to whiten her knuckles.

*The Door opens from the inside too.*

Which meant, she thought, standing very still in the safe library with the unsafe map open before her, that it also opened from the outside. That she had walked through it in one direction already. That her grandmother's warning was not a warning about the valley at all.

It was a warning about her.

* * *

She was crossing the courtyard on her way back, hood up against the hanging rain, when the queen's chamberlain fell into step beside her — a soft-footed man named Aurel whom she had never once heard raise his voice.

"The Queen requests you," he said. "Tonight, after compline. The chapel."

"The chapel?" Ines kept her pace even. "I taste at table, sir. Prayers aren't on my menu."

"They are now." Aurel's voice remained pleasant, unhurried, terrible. "Her Majesty has received a gift. She wishes it tasted — and she wishes you to tell her, precisely, what it tastes *of*. Not whether it's poisoned." He glanced at her, and his eyes were mild and bottomless. "Whether it's answered."

Above them, through the rain, the choir's evening rehearsal had begun, and the hymn without a date drifted down into the courtyard like smoke finding a chimney, and Ines walked on toward the chapel with her grandmother's letter hidden against her ribs and the ground shifting, silently, under everything she had ever believed.
