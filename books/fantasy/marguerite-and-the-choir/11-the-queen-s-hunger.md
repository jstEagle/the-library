## 11. The Queen's Hunger

The chapel had been built for a god who no longer lived there, and Sabine had spent nine years of her marriage learning to pray in it anyway.

She knelt at the hour before dawn, when the candles burned lowest and the stone under her knees held the cold of the whole night. The old rite called for the petitioner to press her forehead to the floor, but Sabine had stopped doing that years ago. She kept her chin lifted now, her eyes on the altar, because a queen did not grovel — even to gods. Especially to gods. She had learned that the posture of her body mattered to them in ways she could not explain and did not like to examine. When she bowed too low, they answered too quickly.

"Give me a child," she said, for the thousandth time, for the ten-thousandth time. Her voice was steady. She had practiced steadiness the way other women practiced embroidery. "A living child. Mine own blood, breathing."

The silence that followed was not empty. That was the horror of it now — that she could tell the difference. Nine years ago the silence after her prayers had been the ordinary silence of stone and candle-smoke, the indifference of heaven. Now it had texture. It had weight, like a hand laid flat against the back of her neck. It listened.

And then, as it always did lately, it answered.

Not with words. Words would have been a mercy, something she could refuse or mishear. Instead the air above the altar thickened, the way water thickens before it freezes, and something gathered there — a pressure, a presence, a patience so vast it made her feel like an insect pressed between the pages of a very old book. She felt it considering her. She felt it *liking* her, in the way a farmer likes a field.

Then it was gone, and on the altar lay a gift.

It had not been there when she knelt. Nothing had entered the chapel; the doors were barred from within, guarded without, watched by men who feared her enough to be honest. And yet there it sat in the candlelight: a small clay vessel, no bigger than a fist, sealed with wax the color of dried blood. It was warm. She could see the warmth of it, a faint shimmer above its mouth, like heat rising off a living thing.

Sabine did not touch it.

That was the first rule she had learned, and she had learned it the way one learns anything that matters: by watching someone else die. The kitchen boy — Pellam, his name had been Pellam, fourteen years old, freckles, a habit of humming while he worked — had found a gift like this one on the chapel steps eight months ago and had carried it inside cradled against his chest like a kitten, because it was warm and he was kind. By evening his kindness had killed him. Whatever had been inside the vessel had opened him the way a letter is opened, patiently, along all his seams. The physician-sage who examined the body had wept and then resigned and then, three weeks later, walked into the river. His note said only: *They are answering. God help us, they are answering.*

The court had called it plague. The court called everything plague when the truth was unspeakable.

Sabine rose from her knees slowly, joints aching, thirty-eight years old and feeling every one of them in the dark. She looked at the gift for a long moment. Then she rang the small silver bell beside the altar, once, and waited for Tomas.

* * *

Tomas came as he always came, swiftly and silently, with the particular hunched readiness of a man who has spent years being summoned to bad news. He was grayer than he'd been a year ago. They were all grayer. The palace had the look of a household where something has died and no one will name it — the servants moved softly, the laughter in the corridors came shorter, and everyone had begun, without discussing it, to leave offerings. A bowl of cream outside a door. Fresh bread on a windowsill. Small bribes to something no one had seen but everyone had heard.

"It came during the prayer," Sabine said. No greeting. Greetings were for people who had time.

Tomas looked at the vessel and did not step closer. "Third this month, Majesty."

"The third I've told you about." She let that sit. There were others. There were gifts turning up all over the palace now, unasked-for, arriving like weather: a comb of yellowed bone left in the laundress's basket, a ring of braided hair in the queen's own slipper, a jar of honey in the nursery — the *empty* nursery — that no one dared throw away. The gods had stopped waiting to be asked. That frightened her more than she could say, though she could not have told anyone why, since admitting fear meant admitting what she had done to earn it.

"What does Your Majesty wish done with it?"

"I want it tasted."

Tomas went still. Even in the low light she could see the color leave his face, gray going whiter. "Majesty—"

"Not you." She turned away from the altar, toward the east window, where the sky was beginning to bruise into morning. "The new taster. The Vhalen girl."

"Ines Almeida." He said the name carefully, the way a man handles a blade he suspects is cursed.

"You know something about her." It was not a question. Sabine had ruled long enough to hear the shape of concealment in a man's voice; it had a certain hollowness, like a drum. "You recommended her. Out of every taster in the kingdom, out of every guild-trained palate in four provinces, you sent a courier into a valley that receives no post, to fetch a girl whose family hasn't sworn fealty to the crown in four hundred years. You knew something, Tomas. I have chosen not to ask until now. Consider this me asking."

He was quiet for so long that the candles guttered twice. When he spoke, his voice was low and roughened. "I didn't choose her, Majesty. Not exactly."

"Explain."

"They told me where to send the letter." He would not look at her. He studied the floor tiles as if their pattern might save him. "After Pellam. After the physician. I prayed — I hadn't prayed since I was a boy, but I was afraid, and afraid men pray — and I asked them how to protect the court. How to protect *you*. And they answered." His jaw worked. "They gave me a name. Her mother's name, actually — a name I'd never heard, written in my own hand on a scrap of vellum I don't remember writing. And instructions. *She cannot be poisoned*, they said. *By anything.*"

The word *anything* hung in the chapel air, and Sabine found she could not make herself reach for it. Anything. Arsenic, certainly. Wolfsbane, obviously. But the gifts were not arsenic. The gifts were not of this world's pharmacopoeia at all, and the physician-sage had died rather than keep studying them, and this girl from a valley erased from every map —

"She survived the duke's stew," Sabine said. "I read the report. She identified the poison, named its source, estimated the dose, and then ate a full breakfast."

"So I'm told, Majesty."

"And she has tasted two of the gifts already. The bone comb. The honey." Both times, Sabine had ordered it through intermediaries, unable to watch, unable *not* to ask afterward. Both times the girl had reported the same thing: flavor, unmistakable, describable — and no harm. The honey had tasted, Ines said, of "someone's grief, salted." The comb had tasted of "old wood and a promise being kept." She described them the way a sommelier describes wine, calm as a millpond, and Sabine had lain awake both nights afterward wondering what kind of child becomes a woman who can taste grief and call it a vintage.

"Send for her," Sabine said. "Now. Before the court wakes. And Tomas—" she caught his sleeve as he turned, and felt him flinch at her grip, and did not loosen it, "—if they told you where to find her, then they chose her. Do you understand what that means? They put her here. Which means either she is a shield they've given me, or a door they've opened. I intend to know which before the moon turns again."

"Yes, Majesty." A pause. His eyes flicked up, briefly, and in them she saw something she had never seen in twelve years of his service: pity. Pity, aimed at *her*. "Majesty — forgive me — but do you sleep?"

She released his sleeve. "No," she said simply, and turned back to the window, and heard him go.

* * *

They brought the girl to her private audience chamber an hour past dawn, while the fog still stood in the gardens like a congregation that refused to disperse. Sabine dismissed the guards. This conversation would happen nowhere near ears, royal or otherwise.

Ines Almeida curtsied correctly, deeply, without warmth. Up close she was younger than Sabine had expected — twenty, perhaps twenty-one — with the kind of plain, watchful face that courts taught people to overlook, which was perhaps its own kind of armor. Her hands were remarkable, though. Sabine noticed hands; a queen learns to. They were a taster's hands, scarred faintly at the fingertips, steady as furniture.

"Your Majesty sent for me."

"I did." Sabine gestured, and a maid — sworn, blind in one eye, devoted — set the clay vessel on the table between them and withdrew. The thing sat there in the morning light, warm, patient, its wax seal unbroken. "Do you know what this is?"

Ines looked at it for a long moment. Not greedily, not fearfully. The way one looks at a snake one has been assured is defanged. "Another gift, Majesty. From the chapel."

"You've tasted two already. You'll taste this one."

Something moved behind the girl's eyes — not refusal; Sabine would have smelled refusal a mile off — but calculation. Weighing. Then, quietly: "May I ask what it's for? The gift. What was prayed for."

Sabine had prepared for many questions. Why me. Is it safe. What happens if it harms me. She had not prepared for that one, and it went through her defenses the way cold water goes through cloth.

"Why do you ask?"

"Because last time, the honey tasted of grief. If I know what was wanted, I can tell whether the flavor matches. Whether the gift is honest." The girl's voice stayed level, professional, but her gaze did not waver, and there was something underneath the professionalism — a current, a hum — that reminded Sabine uncomfortably of the chapel silence. Of being listened to. "Gifts lie sometimes. I'd like to know if this one does."

*Whether the gift is honest.* As if honesty were a property a god's offering could possess or lack. As if one could stand at a market stall and test the wares of heaven.

Sabine walked to the window. Below, the fog was lifting off the roses, and somewhere a gardener was singing — an old hymn, one of the choir's, the tune drifting up thin and broken. Four hundred years her dynasty had ruled this land, and in all that time the gods had slept, and the sleeping had been *safe*. Prayer had been a formality, a ritual, a coin tossed down a well with no expectation of echo. She had been raised on the comfortable theology of silence.

Then her father had died, and she had taken the throne, and the well had begun to echo.

"A child," she said, without turning around. "I prayed for a child. An heir. My husband is dead nine years and the council circles like wolves over the succession, and my sister's sons sharpen their knives in the north, and every year I grow older and barer and more breakable. So I prayed. To the old gods, the ones under everything, the ones the hymns still remember. And they answered." She laughed, and hated the sound of it, dry as ash. "They answer *everything* now. Did you know that? I asked for a child and they sent me a jar of honey and a comb and a ring of hair and a hundred other things I haven't shown the court, each one warmer than the last, each one more *eager*. They want to give me what I asked for. That's what terrifies me. They're not reluctant. They're *hungry to please*."

Silence behind her. When she turned, the girl was looking at the vessel with an expression Sabine recognized, and it chilled her worse than any fear could have: recognition. Ines Almeida looked at the god-gift the way one looks at a familiar face across a crowded room.

"Taste it," Sabine said. "Tell me the flavor. Everything. Spare me nothing, however strange."

The girl picked up the vessel. Her scarred fingers broke the blood-dark seal with a sound like a sigh, and the smell that rose out of it made Sabine's stomach clench — warm milk and iron and something floral underneath, something like the lilies they buried people with. Ines dipped one fingertip, touched it to her tongue, and closed her eyes.

The chamber was utterly silent. Sabine realized she was holding her breath.

"Milk," Ines said at last, softly. "Warm milk, and lilies, and — copper. Blood, but old blood, dried." Her brow furrowed. She touched her tongue again, deeper this time, and Sabine watched her throat move as she swallowed, watched her sit very still for the space of ten heartbeats, testing herself, confirming she was unharmed. Then those steady eyes opened and fixed on Sabine with terrible gentleness.

"There's a craving in it, Majesty. Under the flavors. Whoever made this — whatever made this — wants something very badly, and the wanting is the strongest taste of all." A pause. "It tastes of a nursery. Of a cradle, and a woman rocking it, and the rocking never stopping. It tastes of love that's been praying so long it's forgotten how to do anything else." The girl hesitated, and Sabine saw her decide, deliberately, to say the rest. "It isn't honest, Majesty. The gift promises a child. But the flavor underneath — the craving — it isn't shaped like giving. It's shaped like *keeping*."

The word landed in Sabine's chest like a stone dropped down a well.

*Keeping.*

She thought of the honey in the empty nursery. She thought of the ring of braided hair — whose hair? she had never asked, had never dared — and the comb, and the hundred eager gifts, and the silence in the chapel that listened, and the vast patient liking, the farmer's liking, the field's—

"Get out," she said.

"Majesty—"

"*Get out.*" Her voice cracked on it, and she hated that too, hated the crack, hated the girl's flinch, hated the pity that was surely rising in that plain watchful face. Ines set down the vessel, bowed, and went, quick and silent, and the door closed behind her, and Sabine stood alone in the morning light with her heart hammering and her hands, for the first time in months, shaking.

On the table, the vessel sat open and steaming gently, like a bowl of soup set before a beloved child.

"No," Sabine whispered to it. To them. To whatever was listening, and they were always listening now, that was the whole unbearable truth of her reign — "No. You'll give me a child that breathes on its own. That lives when I sleep. That is *mine* and not yours. Swear it. Swear it, or I'll burn every hymn book in this kingdom and starve you back to sleep if it takes my last breath—"

The warmth of the vessel pulsed, once, like a heartbeat. Like agreement.

Like a hook setting itself.

She sank into the chair behind her, suddenly exhausted down to the marrow, and stared at the open gift, and did what she had done every dawn for a year, what she would do every dawn until her arms gave out.

She began, in a voice worn smooth as river stone, to pray.
