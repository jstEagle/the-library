## 12. Immunity Is a Debt

The ledger of my body, kept in the same hand that kept the ledger of doses:

*Foxglove, three drops in wine, taken every seventh day for two years. Cost: a ringing in the left ear that never fully leaves, like a struck bell heard through water.*

*Arsenic, ground fine into almond paste, begun at nine years old. Cost: the skin of my palms, thickened and pale as candle wax. I cannot feel the difference between silk and sacking unless I look.*

*Hemlock, steeped as a tea, on the advice of Grandmother, who learned it from her mother. Cost: the gray at the edges of my vision, which I mistook for weather until I was fourteen and realized other girls could see the stars at the rim of the sky and I could not.*

I wrote these entries by lamplight in the taster's quarters, in a small book I kept beneath my mattress, because a taster who admits her body is failing is a taster who is replaced, and a replaced taster in this palace had a way of becoming a dead one. The ink was cheap and brown. My handwriting was steady. It was the steadiest thing about me.

There were other entries I did not write down.

That my heartbeat stuttered sometimes — a skipped beat, then a lurching catch-up, like a singer losing her place in a hymn and scrambling to find it. That it happened most often after the god-touched dishes, the ones Tomas brought me with his face carefully arranged into nothing. That some mornings I woke and had to lie still and count — *one, two, three, four* — before I trusted the rhythm enough to stand.

That my tongue, which had been trained into an instrument of astonishing discrimination, had begun to taste things that were not there. Old flavors. Sweetness like rotting fruit. Something mineral and cold that I had no name for, which I privately called *the well-water taste*, because it reminded me of the deep spring behind our house in Vhalen, the one Grandmother said was older than the valley's sealing.

I did not write those entries because writing them would make them real, and I was not yet ready to be real in that particular way.

* * *

Tomas found me in the chapel on a morning when the light came through the eastern windows in long amber blades and lay across the floor like felled timber. I had taken to sitting there when I could not sleep, because the chapel was where the answered prayers lived, and there was a grim comfort in being near evidence. If the gods were real — if they were taking petitions and returning them with teeth — then my body's strange failures might have a reason. Reasons could be fought. Unexplained decay was just decay.

"You shouldn't be here alone," Tomas said. He stood in the doorway with his hands clasped behind him, the posture of a man who has spent thirty years making himself unobtrusive and can no longer do otherwise.

"Neither should anyone," I said. "And yet."

He came and sat beside me on the cold pew, at a respectful distance, the way courtiers sit near a relic they have been told is holy but suspect is haunted. Lately everyone sat near me like that. Since the banquet — since the dish that had been poisoned with something older than arsenic, something that should have stopped my heart in four breaths and instead passed through me like wind through an open window — the court had begun to look at me sideways. Reverent. Hungry. Afraid.

"The physician wants to bleed you again," Tomas said.

"I know. I've refused."

"He thinks your blood will tell him why you live."

"My blood will tell him what my blood always tells physicians: that I am anemic and stubborn." I turned the small book over in my hands. "Why are you here, Tomas?"

For a long moment he said nothing. The chapel hummed faintly around us — it had begun doing that since the choir resumed the old hymn, a vibration too low to hear properly, felt instead in the fillings of the teeth and the hollows of the bones. I had stopped noticing it. That frightened me more than the humming itself: how quickly a body acclimates to wrongness.

"When you took the post," Tomas said finally, "you asked me what happened to the taster before you. Marguerite's successor. And I told you she died of poison."

"You told me she died of hemlock in the winter of the year before last."

"Yes." He looked at his hands. They were fine hands, steward's hands, neat and dry. "That was what was written in the record. Records here are written for the living, Ines. They are meant to give the living something bearable to hold."

The humming rose slightly, or perhaps my ears did. I waited.

"She died of what the poison let in," he said.

The words went through me with a strange slowness, the way cold water goes through cloth.

"Say it plainly."

"The poisons didn't kill her. Her body — like yours — refused them. She survived everything we gave her. Foxglove, arsenic, the god-toxin at the autumn feast, all of it. She was immune the way you're immune. But immunity isn't a wall, Ines. It's a door held shut from inside. And holding a door shut, year after year, against something that *wants* through—" His voice thinned. "It costs. She started hearing prayers that hadn't been spoken aloud. She started knowing things. Small things first — what the queen dreamed, where a lost ring lay. Then larger. She knew the date of her own death and told me, laughing, certain it was a joke. She stopped sleeping because when she slept, something spoke with her mouth."

My skipped-beat heart performed its stutter then, a full bar of silence followed by a panicked rush. I pressed my palm — the numb, waxen palm — flat against my sternum and waited for the rhythm to return.

"And the poison?" I managed.

"There was no poison in the cup the night she died. We tested it afterward, all of us, three times. Clean wine. Whatever killed her came up through the channel the toxins had worn open. The physician wrote 'hemlock' because a palace needs its causes simple." He turned to look at me at last, and his eyes were wet and steady. "You are building the same immunity. You are wearing the same door thinner every day. I watched her do it and I said nothing, because the queen required a taster and I require my position and I have always been a man who mistakes survival for virtue. I will not say nothing twice."

* * *

After he left I sat in the amber light a long while and made myself do the arithmetic I had been avoiding since childhood.

Grandmother had begun feeding me poison when I was six. Not cruelty — she was never cruel — but inheritance, administered in drops, the way other families handed down land or names. *The world beyond the stones will want to use you,* she said. *Better it finds you already armored.* And I had drunk my armor gladly, drop by drop, year by year, proud of my body's thickening silence, proud that foxglove only rang in my ear and hemlock only grayed my sky. I had called it strength. I had built a career on it, crossed a border on it, sworn an oath on it.

But a debt is still a debt whether or not you keep accounts willingly. And I had been borrowing against myself since I was six years old, and the creditor had finally sent word.

I opened the small book and, beneath the ledger of doses, began a second column. Not what I had taken. What it had taken.

*Numbness: both palms, both soles, spreading slowly upward like frost on a window.*
*Vision: gray at the edges, closing in by approximately the width of a finger each year. At this rate, blind by fifty.*
*Hearing: the bell-tone in the left ear; also, lately, voices under voices, which may be the humming or may not.*
*Heart: irregular. Worse after god-touched meals.*
*Taste: phantom flavors. The well-water taste. Increasing frequency.*
*Sleep:* — and here my pen hovered, because the honest entry was *Sleep: something speaks with my mouth*, and I was not Marguerite's successor, I told myself, I was not her, I was only tired —

I wrote: *Sleep: disturbed.* A coward's entry. I underlined it anyway, because even a coward's accounting is better than none.

Then I closed the book and pressed it to my chest and felt my treacherous heart hammering against it, skipping, catching, resuming, and understood at last what Grandmother had never said outright: that she had not armored me against the world. She had ripened me. A taster who cannot be poisoned is not a shield. She is a vessel, and vessels exist to be filled.

Outside the chapel windows, the bells began to mark the hour, and beneath their bronze certainty I heard — or imagined, or did not imagine — the low hum of the walls answering, verse by verse, as if the palace itself were learning a song it had forgotten it knew.

And beneath that, fainter than either, from somewhere behind my own ribs, in a voice I almost recognized:

the well-water taste of words, rising.
