## 13. Verse Eleven

The chapel at midnight was a different country than the chapel by day.

By day it belonged to the court — to rustling silk and the queen's cold incense and the priests who no longer quite believed but performed belief beautifully, the way one performs grief at a funeral for someone one barely knew. By day it was theater. But at midnight, when the last candle had been pinched out and the guards had made their rounds and forgotten, the chapel became what it had always been underneath: a throat. A hollow place built for sound, waiting.

Ines stood in it and felt that waiting in her teeth.

She had come three nights running now, telling herself each time that she was only walking, only restless, only unable to sleep in a palace where the walls hummed like struck glass. Three nights of standing beneath the rose window with her hands folded like a good valley girl at prayer, saying nothing, because she had learned young that prayers spoken in Vhalen went nowhere and she had never unlearned the silence. But tonight she had brought the ledger.

Odile's ledger lay open against her forearm, its pages soft as cloth from thirty years of handling. The choirmaster had not given it to her. She had taken it — or rather, Odile had left it on the choir loft bench where Ines sat every afternoon, positioned with the precision of a woman who arranged everything, including her own generosity, so that it could be denied later. *I never gave you anything,* those placement said. *You stole it. That is your sin, not mine.* It was a kindness shaped exactly like deniability, and Ines was beginning to understand that this was how Odile loved: sideways, in margins, in things left where they would be found.

The eleventh verse was marked in faded brown ink — iron gall gone rusty with age — and beside it, in Odile's cramped hand: *location unknown. Verse corrupt? Or verse withheld?*

Not corrupt. Not withheld either, exactly. Just carried out of the country forty years ago in the mouth of a woman who sang it to her daughter as a lullaby, who died before she could explain why a song about a sleeping river should make a grown priestess weep.

*Hush now, the water remembers the stone,*

*hush now, the stone remembers the door,*

*and what was shut in the dark of the world*

*waits at the turning, and turns no more.*

Ines had sung it ten thousand times. She had sung it into pillows and over washbasins and once, memorably, to a litter of orphaned kittens in her grandmother's barn who had opened their eyes at the third line as though something in it were addressed to them specifically. It was a lullaby. It was nothing. It was the sound of her mother's voice lowered to embers, and she would have known it drunk, deaf, dying.

But here, in this chapel that was built like a throat, she had begun to notice things about it. How the melody refused to resolve — how it circled the final note the way a moth circles flame, approaching, approaching, never landing. How the rhythm stumbled in the same place every time, a hitch on the word *door*, as if the song itself flinched. How her mother had always, always sung the last line softer than hearing, so that Ines had spent her childhood straining after words that might not have been words at all.

Songs like that didn't survive four hundred years by accident. Songs like that survived because someone wanted them carried somewhere specific.

And she was here. And it was midnight. And the moon outside the rose window was fat and wrong, one turn short of an omen, pouring light onto the flagstones like spilled milk.

"Right," Ines said aloud, and her voice fell into the dark and did not echo, which should have frightened her more than it did. "No one's listening."

That was the lie she needed. That was the valley girl's armor: *no prayer spoken here is ever answered.* Her grandmother's voice, worn smooth as a river stone from repetition. Ines wrapped it around herself, took a breath the way she took all breaths now — measured, deliberate, a taster's breath, held briefly against the palate of the air to check it for poison — and began to sing.

*Hush now, the water remembers the stone—*

The first line did nothing. Of course it didn't. A lullaby in an empty room.

*Hush now, the stone remembers the door—*

The candles she hadn't lit flickered anyway.

It was a small thing. A trick of draft, she told herself, of the chapel breathing through its old bones. But the flames bent toward her, all of them, in a chapel with no fire, bending like grass under wind that touched only them, and Ines kept singing because stopping suddenly felt more dangerous than continuing — the way you don't jerk your hand back from a snake.

*And what was shut in the dark of the world—*

The humming started.

She had heard the walls hum before, during choir rehearsal, a resonance so low it lived more in the floorboards than the ear. This was different. This was the hymn answering itself — her own voice coming back to her a half-beat late, a half-tone low, from inside the stone. The chapel was singing harmony. The chapel knew the part.

Her voice shook on *waits at the turning*, and the walls caught the shake and steadied it, bore it the way a current bears a swimmer, and Ines understood with a lurch that she was no longer singing alone, that she had perhaps never in her life sung alone, that every time her mother had hushed her to sleep in Vhalen the valley itself might have been holding the lower line —

*— and turns no more.*

The hitch. The stumble. The note that wouldn't land.

Ines landed it.

She didn't decide to. Her voice simply went where the melody had always been reaching, down past the comfortable close, down to a note she had never been taught because her mother had never sung it — and the note came out of her sounding ancient, sounding practiced, sounding like something that had been waiting forty years in her blood for permission. The chapel swallowed it whole.

And the wall opened.

Not opened — she would correct herself later, shaking, wrapping her arms around herself in the dark — *absented*. There was no grinding of stone, no crack of mortar, no drama. One moment there was the east wall, granite blocks fitted without visible seam, cold and certain as any wall in any palace; and then there was a doorway-shaped place where the wall was not, a tall arch of pure absence, edged in a faint silver shimmer like heat-haze in winter, and through it — through it was not the corridor that should have lain beyond. Through it was dark that moved. Dark that breathed. A darkness with depth the way a well has depth, and somewhere far down in it, or very near, something turned over in its sleep and sighed, and the sigh smelled of myrrh and river mud and her mother's shawl.

One breath. That was all it lasted. Ines drew one breath standing before the impossible, tasting it — because tasting was what she did, because her body knew no other response to the unknown except to take it in and wait to see if it killed her — and the taste of it was cold honey and old iron and, underneath, unbearably, home. Valley rain. Vhalen soil. The smell of the border stones at dawn.

Then the second breath, and the wall was a wall again, seamless, smug, as though it had never been anything else.

Ines stood in the ringing silence and discovered she was on her knees. She had no memory of kneeling. Her palms stung against the flagstone. The ledger had fallen open beside her, and moonlight lay across the eleventh verse, and beneath Odile's notation, in ink so old it had gone the color of dried blood, someone else had written a single line that she had somehow never seen in all her nights of reading:

*The key does not find the Door. The key is found by it.*

"You're bleeding," said a voice from the dark of the choir loft, and Ines's heart slammed against her ribs hard enough to hurt.

Odile descended the stairs with a candle she must have carried unlit — unlit, in a chapel where candles lit themselves for Ines's voice — and touched flame to wick only when she reached the floor, as though even fire required her permission. The choirmaster looked, for the first time since Ines had known her, undone. Her spine, that instrument of authority, had softened into a curve. Her hands, which conducted with surgical economy, were trembling around the candlestick.

"How long," Ines managed. Her voice came out scraped. "How long have you been there."

"Since the second line." Odile set the candle down with exaggerated care, the way one sets down something that might go off. "Thirty years I have sung that hymn. Taught it. Dissected it. I have transcribed every surviving copy in three kingdoms and traced the melody back through four centuries of corruption, and I will tell you what I know, girl, and you will listen, because what I am about to say has cost me everything I ever pretended to be." She crouched, stiffly, and took Ines's bleeding palm in her dry ones, turning it over with a physician's detachment. "Every singer who has ever attempted the eleventh verse alone has failed. Every one. Their voices slide off the true note like water off wax. I assumed the verse was lost. Corrupted beyond recovery." Her grip tightened. "But it wasn't lost. It was *carried*. Out of the country. In a lullaby. In a cradle."

"My mother," Ines said. It wasn't a question. Nothing about her mother had been a question for weeks now; her mother had become a series of answers arriving too late.

"Your mother." Odile released her hand and sat back on her heels, and in the candlelight her face was stripped of thirty years of armor, and what lay beneath was not age but grief — old grief, worn smooth, carried so long it had become structural. "Marguerite. Alto. First alto, the line that holds the hymn together, the line everyone forgets because it doesn't sparkle. She stood exactly where you're kneeling, twenty-two years old, and she sang the eleventh verse for the archive, and the wall—" Odile stopped. Started again, quieter. "The wall did what it just did. And the next morning she was gone. Deserted, the record says. Traitor, the court said. And I spent forty years hating a dead woman for cowardice."

"She wasn't a coward."

"No." Odile's laugh had no humor in it at all. "She was sent. Or she fled because she understood what she'd done, or both — I don't know, and I have hated not knowing longer than you've been alive. But I know this." She reached out and closed Ines's fingers back over her own bleeding palm. "The Door knows you. It heard your mother's blood in your voice and it opened like a wound. You are not carrying a map, Ines Almeida. You are carrying the thing the map leads to."

Ines looked at the east wall. Seamless. Silent. But she could still feel it — the way you can feel a tooth that's been pulled, an attention where nothing is, a shape of absence pressed into the ordinary stone. The taste of it lingered on her tongue. Cold honey. Old iron. Home.

"It opened for one breath," she said.

"One breath is enough to kill a man, if the breath is wrong." Odile rose, joints cracking, and retrieved her candle. At the edge of the dark she paused, and did not turn around, and spoke to the wall instead of to Ines. "I wept, if you're wondering. When it opened. Thirty years of work validated in a single second, and I wept like a girl at her first mass, and I am not ashamed of it, and I will deny it tomorrow if you ever mention it." A pause. "Come to the loft at dawn. Bring the ledger. Bring your voice. And Ines—" Now she turned, and the candlelight made her eyes look banked, coals rather than embers. "Do not sing it again alone. Whatever you heard in there, whatever called to you — the Door opens from both sides. Your grandmother's people learned that lesson once already. I'd rather you not learn it twice."

Her footsteps faded up the stairs. The candlelight went with her.

Ines knelt alone in the chapel, in the enormous listening quiet, and pressed her bleeding palm flat against the east wall. Granite. Cold. Perfectly, deliberately innocent.

But deep beneath her hand — deeper than stone, deeper than the palace, deeper maybe than the ground itself — something that had waited four hundred years behind a lullaby shifted its weight, and settled, and waited with her.

And far above, through the rose window, the moon slid one degree further along its thirteenth turn.

* * *

She did not sleep. She told herself it was the excitement, the adrenaline still fizzing in her blood, the natural aftermath of touching the impossible — but lying in her narrow bed in the taster's quarters, watching moonlight crawl across the ceiling, Ines catalogued her body the way she catalogued every dish: pulse, breath, temperature, tremor. Pulse steady. Breath shallow. Hands cold. And under everything, faint as a flavor at the back of the throat, the taste of the Door still lingering. Cold honey. Old iron. Home.

It wasn't fading. Tastes faded. That was the entire architecture of her profession — poison announced itself, peaked, and passed, and the taster's body returned to neutral. But this taste was not passing. It was settling in, patient as a debt, and sometime in the smallest hour of the morning Ines admitted to the dark what she had already known in the chapel: it wasn't a residue. It was an invitation. Something on the other side of that absent wall had tasted her back.

She got up. Dressed. Did not bring the ledger — Odile had it now, or would at dawn — and walked the corridors with the soft certainty of a woman who had spent weeks learning which floorboards betrayed her. Not toward the chapel. Away from it. Toward the kitchens, toward work, toward the honest grammar of broth and blade, because her grandmother had taught her that fear was best handled the way one handled arsenic: in small doses, on a full stomach, with something warm afterward.

The kitchens were empty at that hour except for embers and one boy asleep in the corner who was not the kitchen boy who had died — that boy's pallet had been struck weeks ago, his name already wearing smooth from overuse — and Ines put water on to heat and stood watching it, and that was when she saw the bowl.

It sat on the worktable where no bowl had been. Earthenware, old-fashioned, glazed the particular blue-green of Vhalen pottery — pottery made forty miles away in a valley that received no post, no visitors, no couriers, nothing. Inside the bowl, covered with a cloth, was bread. Still warm. Her grandmother's bread — she knew the crumb, the caraway, the crust scored with the family mark, three lines like water ripples — bread that could not exist in this palace, on this table, at this hour, and yet did.

A note lay beneath the bowl, written in the strong slanted hand that had taught Ines her letters:

*You sang. We heard. The Door opens from the inside too, child — and something just knocked.*

Ines stood in the empty kitchen with warm bread in her hands and the taste of the Door on her tongue, and listened.

Beneath the palace, beneath the chapel wall, beneath the whole drowsing weight of the kingdom, faint and unmistakable and patient as a heartbeat: knocking.
