## 14. The God Who Answers

The chapel at midnight did not feel like a place where anything holy had ever happened.

Ines had learned the chapel's daytime language by now: the dust that hung in the light like something waiting to be named, the candle smoke that smelled of tallow and old fear, the way the petitioners' voices dropped when they passed the third pew, as though the air itself were listening and they didn't want to be overheard. She knew which prayer-gifts rotted fastest — the ones wrapped in silk went bad quickest, as if the gods disliked being handled through finery — and which ones kept, sullenly, for weeks. She had catalogued eleven answered prayers since her arrival. Two had teeth. One had wept.

But at midnight the chapel spoke a different dialect entirely.

She came because she could not sleep, and because Odile's ledger lay open on her cot with its missing verse gaping like a pulled tooth, and because Tomas had told her — reluctantly, guiltily, the way he told her everything now — that the chapel was never truly empty after the bells stopped. *Something keeps the hours*, he'd said. *Something has to.*

So she sat in the fourth pew, where the wood was worn smooth by four hundred years of kneeling bodies, and she waited the way she waited for poison to announce itself: still, attentive, cataloguing every sensation so that nothing could arrive unannounced.

The candles guttered all at once.

Not blown out — there was no draft, she checked, the way she always checked — but bent, all twelve flames leaning toward the altar like courtiers bowing to someone entering a room. The wax smell thickened. Under it rose something older: river water and crushed thyme and the particular sweetness of bread left too long in a warm kitchen.

Her mother's kitchen.

Ines's hands closed on the pew rail before she understood why. Her body knew before her mind did. Her body had been raised on that smell; her body had spent twenty years learning to survive things that arrived smelling like safety.

"You sit very straight," said the dark near the altar. "Your grandmother taught you that. Sit straight, taste slow, swallow nothing you haven't named."

Her mother's voice.

Not an imitation. Not the flattened echo of memory, the way voices lived in dreams, slightly wrong in their warmth. This voice had texture. It had the small roughness at the back of the throat that appeared only when her mother was tired and happy at once, singing lullabies with flour still on her hands. It had the exact cadence she used on the word *grandmother* — three syllables, the middle one softened, a private family music.

Ines did not move. Every instinct she had honed — the taster's discipline, the valley's caution, the simple animal wisdom of prey — screamed at her to stay exactly where she was and name what this was before she swallowed any of it.

"You're not her," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved.

"No," the darkness agreed, and it did not apologize, and somehow that was worse than if it had lied. "But I have eaten a great many prayers spoken to her. Grief is nourishing. You would be surprised how much of your mother is still in circulation. Prayers are not requests, little taster. They are portraits. Everyone who ever missed her painted her in words, and I drank them all."

The flames leaned further. Something was coalescing at the altar — not a shape, not yet, but a pressure, the way the eye finds a face in wood grain once someone points to it. Ines made herself look at the candles instead. Name what you taste. Never let the unknown arrive unnamed.

"What do I call you?" she asked.

"Whatever your mother called me." A pause, delicate as a knife laid down between courses. "She called me nothing. That was the point of her. That was the whole architecture of Vhalen — a country built around a woman who refused to speak my name. Do you know how hungry that makes a thing? Four hundred years of a locked door, and behind the door, one family who will not even knock?"

The pressure shifted. And then, horribly, gently, the dark took a shape: a woman standing at the altar steps with her back half-turned, hands folded the way her mother folded them when kneading, head tilted in that listening posture Ines had inherited along with her cheekbones.

She did not wear her mother's face. That was the obscene mercy of it. The figure was featureless, smooth as an unfinished statue, and yet the posture, the tilt, the fold of the hands — those were perfect. Those were devastating. The god had understood that Ines would recognize her mother more truly by gesture than by features, and it had chosen accordingly, the way a careful cook chooses seasoning.

"I know why you came," it said. "You came because of the ledger. Verse eleven opened a door-shaped absence in this wall, and you have been lying awake counting verses like coins, wondering what the whole hymn would buy. I can tell you where the Door stands. I can tell you what your mother sang, and why, and what she was sent here to do forty years ago. I can give you the alto line, Ines Almeida. I hold it. Your mother gave it to me the night she died — or rather, I took it, but from her it was given, and gifts cannot be stolen back, only traded."

The chapel was very cold. Ines realized she was shaking, and made the shaking stop the way she made everything stop: deliberately, muscle by muscle, the discipline that had kept arsenic and belladonna and one nameless banquet toxin from killing her.

"You want something," she said.

"I want a taste."

*

It said it the way a host offers wine.

"A taste of your blood. Nothing more. A sip, if you like — I am courteous. You have built yourself into the most remarkable vessel in two worlds, did you know that? Every poison you swallowed to save dukes and duchesses, you swallowed for me too, though you didn't know it. Arsenic teaches the blood a grammar. Belladonna teaches it a song. You are fluent, Ines. You are the first fluent thing to walk out of Vhalen in four hundred years, and I have been starving in the anteroom of your country since before your grandmother's grandmother learned to keep her mouth shut."

The featureless figure drifted closer. It did not walk; walking would have required feet, intention, a direction. It simply became nearer, the way weather becomes nearer.

"One taste," it repeated. "And I will sing you the verse your mother sang. The Door's true location. The reason the moon must turn thirteen times. Everything Odile has spent thirty years failing to find, handed to you in your mother's own voice, because I keep everything that was ever prayed, and your mother prayed to me once — only once, at the end, when the sealing was half-done and she was afraid. Fear makes such honest prayers."

Ines found she was standing. She had no memory of rising. That frightened her more than the voice did — her body moving without her permission, her discipline developing gaps, and she thought suddenly of chapter twelve of her own life, the numbness in her fingertips, the gray at the edges of her vision, the heartbeat that stuttered like a reader losing its place. Everything has a cost. She had written that truth into her own flesh one poison at a time. She would not forget it now, at the moment it mattered most.

"Why blood?" she asked. "You've answered prayers with gold and monsters and a dead boy's resurrection gone wrong. Why does a god need blood?"

The figure stilled. When it spoke again, the warmth was gone from her mother's voice, and what remained was the thing underneath, vast and patient and appallingly reasonable.

"Because blood is the only prayer with substance. Words are breath. Gold is earth. But blood — blood is a promise the body makes. Your blood especially. It carries the lock your ancestor set into the Door. If I taste it, I know the lock. If I know the lock, I can teach myself its shape. And then, little taster, when the choir sings and the hymn opens what it opens, I will not need to be invited through. I will already know the way a key knows its keyhole."

Silence. The candles burned crooked and low.

"You just told me your price would open the Door for you," Ines said slowly. "You told me the trap before I stepped in it."

"I did." The featureless face inclined, almost graciously. "Because I wanted to see whether you were your mother's daughter or merely her echo. She also heard the terms plainly. She also understood. She refused too, at first." A pause, soft as snowfall. "Refusal is such a brief condition, in my experience."

*

Ines walked to the altar.

Later she would not be able to explain why. Not courage — courage would have stayed in the pew and bargained from distance. Not surrender. It was the taster in her, perhaps: the old iron rule that you never judge a dish by its smell, that you go to the plate, that you take the spoon in your own hand, because a poison feared is a poison half-swallowed already, and knowledge is the only antidote that works every time.

She stood before the figure that wore her mother's gestures, close enough to see that it cast no shadow, though twelve candles burned around it.

"My mother sealed Vhalen," she said. "She spent her life closing what you want opened. And you think I'll hand you the map to her work in exchange for a lullaby."

"I think," the god said, with terrible gentleness, "that you have been homesick since the day you crossed the border stones. I think you dream of thyme and river water. I think you would give a great deal to hear her finish the song she used to hum when she thought you were asleep — the one she never finished, because she died mid-verse, and the ending has been sitting in my keeping like a coin under a tongue." The voice lowered, and oh, it knew, it knew exactly what it was doing, it poured tenderness into her mother's cadence like honey into tea. "Wouldn't you like to know how it ends, Ines? Wouldn't you like to be sung to sleep, just once, by the whole of it?"

Her eyes were burning. She hated it. She hated that a thing with no face could find the exact seam in her and press.

"Yes," she said honestly, because lying to a thing that ate prayers seemed both futile and dangerous. "Yes, I would. And no. No, you can't have my blood."

She drew the small knife from her belt — the taster's knife, the one she used to pare fruit and test rinds — and held it out flat on her palm, blade up, an offering displayed and simultaneously withheld.

"But I'll tell you what I know instead, for free, the way Vhalen gives gifts. My grandmother says nothing prayed inside the stones is ever answered. For four hundred years we believed that was our protection. Now I think it was our mercy — yours and ours both. Because a god who has never been fed learns patience, and a god who has tasted even one honest prayer—" she looked at the crooked candles, at the pressure in the air, at the hunger she could feel now, radiating off the figure like heat off a stove "—learns appetite. I won't feed you. Not a drop. Not a sip. My blood is the lock, and locks don't open out of loneliness."

For a long moment the chapel held its breath.

Then the god laughed, and it was her mother's laugh, and Ines felt it go through her like a fishhook.

"Oh, you are hers," it said. "Word for word, almost. She stood where you're standing. She said nearly the same things, in nearly the same order — did she write a script for you, I wonder, or is defiance simply the family recipe?" The figure leaned down, though it had no face to lean with, and its presence filled the space between them like water filling a cup. "Very well. Refusal noted. Refusal honored. I am old, Ines Almeida, and old things keep their word; it is the only currency we have left. You gave me nothing, so I take nothing."

It paused.

"Except payment for the audience. You listened. Listening is a transaction. You heard my voice — my true voice, wearing its best coat — and hearing has a tariff. Everyone forgets the tariff."

"What tariff?" Ines's hand tightened on the knife. "Name it."

"I just did," the god said softly. "I named it when I arrived. Think, little taster. What did you lose in the last quarter hour? Search yourself the way you search a stew. Taste backward."

And Ines searched, the way she had been trained, sensation by sensation, shelf by shelf of the mind's pantry —

— and found the gap.

Small. Neat. The size of a plum stone, sitting in the middle of a summer afternoon. She reached for it and her fingers closed on nothing: a day, a specific day, her mother in the garden—

No. Not the garden. There had been a garden, hadn't there, her mother kneeling in dirt, showing her something, saying something—

There had been a garden. There had been her mother. There had been words. The container remained, labeled and intact, and the contents were gone, scooped out clean, leaving not pain but a horrible polished absence, the way a tooth leaves a smooth socket the tongue returns to again and again.

"The afternoon," Ines whispered. "The — there was a day, she was teaching me—"

"The garden," the god agreed, gentle as rot. "She was teaching you the names of herbs. You were six. You got marjoram and oregano confused and she laughed — she laughed so hard she had to sit down in the dirt. It was a good memory. Sweet. Honest. Unprayed, therefore unclaimed, therefore mine to levy." The figure began to thin, dissolving upward into the candle smoke like steam off soup. "That is the tariff, Ines. You may refuse my prices, but you cannot refuse my fees. Every word you hear from me costs a memory of equal weight. The next conversation will cost more. The one after, more still. I am very patient, and you have such a rich pantry."

"Don't," Ines said, and her voice cracked on it, and she hated that too. "Give it back. Take something else, take—"

"Take what? Your immunity? Offered freely, that would be a feast — but you won't offer it, and theft requires invitation, that is the oldest law, older than your priestess, older than your Door." The voice was nearly gone now, fraying into the hiss of candlewick, and it saved its last words for her mother's softest tone, the bedtime tone, the tone that meant *sleep now, I'm here*. "Guard your pantry well, daughter of Marguerite. And do ask your grandmother about the garden. She was there that day too. Grandmothers remember everything. It is their curse and their craft."

The candles snapped upright. The chapel was ordinary again — dust, tallow, silence, the door-shaped absence in the wall that was only masonry after all, or seemed to be.

Ines stood alone in the fourth pew's shadow with a knife in her hand and a hole in her childhood, tasting backward over and over for a memory that was no longer on her tongue.

Garden. Marjoram. Laughter. Dirt.

*She laughed so hard she had to sit down.*

She could see the sentence. She could not see the laughter. It was like holding a recipe for a dish she'd eaten once and would never taste again.

She sank onto the pew and did not cry, because crying was a kind of prayer, and she had just learned what prayers fed.

When she finally walked back through the sleeping palace, past the guard who nodded at her without seeing, past the queen's wing where a light still burned at this hour — Sabine's window, glowing steady as a vigil, as it had burned every night that week — Ines carried one new certainty alongside her grief:

The god had called her *daughter of Marguerite*.

And she did not know who Marguerite was.

But somewhere below, in the choir loft, Odile's ledger waited with its thirty years of transcriptions and its roster of singers, and Ines was going to read every name.
