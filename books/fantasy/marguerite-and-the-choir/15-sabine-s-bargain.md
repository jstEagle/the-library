## 15. Sabine's Bargain

The child moved.

Not kicked — kicked was what living children did, and this thing in her arms had not yet earned a living verb — but *moved*, a slow ripple beneath skin the color of candle wax, as though something inside it were turning over in its sleep. Or turning over in hers. Sabine could never quite decide whose sleep it was that mattered.

She sat in the nursery she had furnished eleven years ago and refurnished every year since, in the stubborn arithmetic of a woman who refused to subtract. The cradle was Alderic oak, carved with hawthorn branches by a craftsman who had died before finishing the second corner. The blankets were linen washed in rosewater and blessed by three different priests, two of whom were now dead and one of whom had fled to the coast. The mobile above the cradle was silver, shaped like the moon in all its phases, and it turned without wind, slowly, always clockwise, because the gods had asked for it to be so and Sabine had learned — oh, how thoroughly she had learned — that when the gods asked for small things you gave them the small things, quickly, and without weeping.

"Again," said the voice from the corner of the room.

There was no one in the corner of the room. There was never anyone in the corner of the room. But the voice came from there anyway, the way cold comes from a window that is shut.

"She's tired," Sabine said. Her own voice sounded foreign to her — flattened, practical, the voice of a woman discussing grain tariffs. "She prayed for six hours yesterday."

"*It*," said the voice, with the mild correction of a tutor. "And it is never tired. It is only unwilling. There is a difference, Majesty, and the difference is everything."

Sabine looked down at the bundle in her arms. The child — she would not say *it*, not even in the privacy where no one but gods could hear; let them correct her until the moon fell out of the sky, she would say *child* — the child had eyes now. They had arrived on the ninth day, simply present one morning where blank lids had been, and they were gray, the gray of river ice, and they did not blink often enough. When she prayed over the child, the chest rose. When she stopped, it stilled. This was the arrangement. This had been the arrangement for nineteen days.

Nineteen days without sleeping more than an hour at a time. Nineteen days of praying in the bath, praying while signing decrees, praying through her teeth during audiences with ambassadors who thought the queen of Vhalenne's decline was merely grief for the old king. Praying with her mouth full at state dinners, the words tucked behind the food like contraband. She had learned to pray the way spies learn languages: silently, constantly, under any conditions, until the practice wore grooves into her that she suspected would still be there if someone scraped her soul flat to look.

*Give them breath,* she prayed now, lips barely moving, the child's wax-cool weight settling against her sternum. *Let my breath be theirs. Take whatever—*

"No," said the voice, sharp as a dropped knife. "You do not offer. You have nothing left worth offering. You pray the words as written. The words are the payment. Do not improvise with us, Sabine."

Her name. That was new, still. For months they had called her *Majesty*, which she had understood as courtesy, and then *the barren one*, which she had understood as cruelty, and then, ten days ago, *Sabine* — plain, intimate, the way her dead husband used to say it when he was trying to coax her out of a sulk. They knew how names worked on people. That was the worst discovery of the nineteenth century of her family's reign: that the old gods understood flattery better than any courtier alive.

She prayed the words as written. She had copied them out herself, forty-one times, onto vellum slips she kept sewn into the lining of every gown she owned, so that even undressed she was dressed in prayer. The words were old — older than the chapel, older than the dynasty, older than the language they'd been translated into, which Odile the choirmaster had told her once, in a voice like a woman reporting a fire, was itself a kind of warning. The words tasted of iron and honey. They always had. She had assumed, in the beginning, that this was what holiness tasted like.

Now she knew it was what appetite tasted like. Hers or theirs. Possibly both.

The child breathed. A thin, whistling draw of air, in and out, in and out, and Sabine counted the breaths the way other mothers counted fingers and toes. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen. At twenty she allowed herself to stand, to cross to the window, to look out over the palace grounds where the frost was coming early this year and the gardeners muttered about it being an ill omen, and the gardeners were right, and she could have told them exactly how ill, and instead she smiled at them from behind the glass because a queen's face was the last thing in this palace that still belonged to her.

*Statecraft.* That was the word she used. She had used it last week to Lord Chancellor Verre, when he had come to her pale and shaking with a report that forty choristers' families had received gifts on their doorsteps — bread that did not stale, coins that did not tarnish, small mercies with the unmistakable smell of a down payment. He had asked her, carefully, in the oblique grammar courtiers used for accusations, whether Her Majesty knew anything of the matter.

"The crown secures its succession," she had said. "By whatever means the crown finds available. That is not scandal, Chancellor. That is the job."

He had looked at her the way men look at a ship taking on water — wanting to help, calculating the depth, deciding against swimming. And she had watched him decide, and she had felt nothing. That was the part that would have frightened her once, back when she was a princess who cried over injured falcons: the nothing. Corruption, she had learned, did not feel like rot. It felt like efficiency. It felt like finally being good at your job.

Because here was the ledger, and she kept it honestly, she kept it better than her treasurers kept theirs:

A dynasty four hundred years old, ending. Three stillbirths. Two infants who lived a day each and whose tiny funerals she could still smell if she let herself, the lilies and the tallow and the terrible smallness of the coffins. A council already whispering about the Duke of Arlesse's claim, about "stability," about her cousin's robust sons. A kingdom that had spent four centuries pretending the old gods were myths, and then — the moment the first prayer got answered, the moment a scullery boy's drowned cat walked out of the well dry and alive — had fallen on its knees with a speed that would have been funny if it weren't so damning. They hadn't needed convincing. They had needed permission. She had given them that. Every answered prayer since had been read, in the streets, as royal favor. As proof the queen was chosen.

And in exchange — in exchange for what she had given, which was access, which was the chapel reopened, which was the choir singing the old hymn again after four hundred years of silence, which was a queen's voice raised nightly in petition — the gods had promised her a child who lived.

They had delivered a child who breathed *when she prayed*. Which was not the same thing. Which was, she understood now, looking at the gray-eyed bundle, not even adjacent to the same thing. It was a demonstration. It was a merchant showing her the sample and letting her imagine the shipment.

"They're starving," she said to the window. To the frost. To her own reflection, which had gotten thinner this season in a way the portraitists were instructed to ignore. "That's what this is. All of it. The gifts that bite, the answers that take. They've been shut out so long they're starving, and I opened the pantry door, and now they're eating."

"You say that," said the voice, almost gently, "as though it were a flaw in the arrangement rather than its entire logic. Everything eats, Sabine. Kings eat kingdoms. Mothers eat their daughters' futures and call it guidance. We are merely honest about our table." A pause, and then, silk over stone: "Besides. We prefer the word *tithing*."

Sabine turned from the window. "You said a living child. An heir. Flesh of my flesh, blood of the line, a child that breathes when I am asleep, when I am dead, when I am a hundred years of dust on a slab in the crypt. That was the promise. I have it written—" She stopped. She did not have it written. Nothing with the gods was ever written; writing was for people who expected to be held to things.

"We said a living child," the voice agreed, patient as erosion. "We did not say *independent*. Independence is such a modern idea. In the old days, Majesty, children belonged to their houses, their gods, their purpose. This child belongs to its purpose. As do you. As does everyone who sings in your chapel." Another pause, longer, and she felt it the way one feels a hand hovering just above one's shoulder. "Though speaking of the choir. There has been a development."

"I don't want to hear it."

"You want very much to hear it. You simply want to want not to. We can tell the difference; it is one of our few remaining pleasures." The voice moved — not the speaker, there was no speaker, but the *voice*, drifting from the corner to the cradle to somewhere just behind her left ear, close enough that she could almost feel breath that did not exist. "Your choirmaster's little project. The hymn. Did you know it is a map? Did you know your own chapel wall hums along in places it should not? Did you know that the new taster — the valley girl, the one who cannot be poisoned — sang a verse last week and the stones remembered themselves for a full breath?"

The child stirred in her arms. Or something stirred in the child. Sabine found she had stopped breathing and made herself start again, one long careful exhale, the way she'd been taught as a girl for managing temper.

"What do you want me to do about Odile?"

"Nothing. Not yet. Arresting her would be loud, and we are not yet finished needing the choir quiet and singing. No — what we want is smaller. What we want is what we have always wanted." The gray eyes of the child had opened. They were fixed on Sabine with the unblinking attention of something learning her face for later use. "Forty voices, Majesty. Forty voices bound to the hymn, carrying it in their blood the way your taster carries her poisons. Forty doors, and every one of them opening inward. Give us the choir — freely, knowingly, crowned in the giving — and the child will breathe on its own by the thirteenth turn. You will sleep. You will hold an heir who lives whether you pray or not. You will give your dynasty what four hundred years of ordinary praying could not."

"And if I refuse?"

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight and grain, like velvet dragged across skin the wrong way. In her arms, the child's breathing slowed — one breath, another, shallower — and Sabine's heart went with it, stumbling down the same staircase, and she understood with perfect clarity that this was the answer to her question, that the refusal was already being demonstrated, that she was holding the negotiation in her arms and it weighed less than a loaf of bread.

"Yes," she said. Her voice did not shake. She noted that with a strange, distant pride — the pride of a woman watching her own ruin arrive punctually. "Yes. The choir. Forty voices. Done."

"Done," the voice repeated, satisfied, and the satisfaction was the most obscene thing she had ever heard, because it sounded exactly like relief, exactly like love, exactly like her own voice on the night she had first conceived and lost and conceived again, and she hated that she recognized it.

The child breathed deep. The silver moons turned overhead, clockwise, always clockwise. Somewhere below, in the chapel, the evening choir would be gathering for rehearsal, forty throats warming up on scales, laughing, complaining about the cold, entirely unaware that their queen had just signed them over the way her ancestors had signed away forests and river rights — *the crown secures its succession by whatever means available.*

She carried the child to the cradle. She laid it down. She arranged its hands, which were perfect, five fingers each, cruelly perfect, the way bait is perfect. Then she knelt beside the cradle, folded her hands on its rail, and began the evening prayer, the words as written, iron and honey, and as she prayed she watched the small chest rise and fall, rise and fall, and told herself the story she had become expert at telling:

That a queen's first duty was the succession. That personal sin was irrelevant next to dynastic survival. That history would judge her by outcomes, and the outcome would be a living heir on a living throne, and no historian would ask what the choir had cost, because historians never asked about the choir. That she had not wanted this. That wanting had nothing to do with anything, anymore, and had perhaps never had anything to do with anything, and wasn't that, in the end, what it meant to wear a crown — to be the place where wanting went to die?

The prayer finished. The child breathed. Sabine remained kneeling a moment longer than the words required, forehead nearly touching the oak rail, and in that unguarded inch of silence she heard — faintly, from far below, up through the floors of the palace like sound traveling through water — the choir beginning to sing.

The hymn. Her hymn. Their hymn now.

And underneath the forty voices, so low she might have imagined it, something else was humming along. Learning the tune. Counting the voices.

One, it seemed to say. Two. Three.

Thirty-nine.

Thirty-nine, and waiting.
