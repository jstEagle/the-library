## 16. The Valley Remembers

The dream began the way all her dreams of home began: with the smell.

Not the palace smell — beeswax and stone dust and the faint copper tang that had seeped into everything since the prayers started being answered — but the green, wet, mineral smell of Vhalen in late spring, when the snowmelt came down off the high shoulders of the mountains and the terraces drank it in and the whole valley exhaled. Ines stood on the path above the lower fields, and the path was exactly right, the same packed earth worn pale at its center by four hundred years of feet that had nowhere else to go, and she thought, with the strange lucid grief of dreams: *I am asleep, and I am still here, and this is what my mind does when it is starving.*

She walked down into the valley anyway.

The barley was coming up silver-green in rows so straight they looked ruled. The beehives sat along the south wall of the Almeida dooryard, their entrances facing east, because her grandmother said bees kept better accounts than any tax clerk. The cherry tree by the well was in blossom, which was wrong — it was autumn in the capital, and even dreams usually kept the seasons honest — but the petals were falling anyway, drifting down through a light that had no sun in it, just brightness without a source, the way things were lit inside closed eyelids.

"Grandmother?" she called.

No answer. But the valley answered instead, or something wearing the valley's voice did: the wind moved through the barley and made the sound wind makes, and then, underneath it, a second sound, a low sustained tone like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass, and Ines felt the hair rise along her arms because she knew that sound. She had heard it once before, muffled through three floors of stone, standing outside the choir loft while forty voices held a chord no one could date.

The hymn. The valley was humming the hymn.

*You don't know the whole of it,* Odile had said, pressing the ledger into her hands as if handing over a wound. *Nobody knows the whole of it. That's rather the point.*

But the valley knew it. The valley hummed it from the roots up, from the bedrock, and each verse rose out of a different place — the first out of the well, the second out of the beehives, the third out of the cherry tree's black roots — and Ines understood, in the way one understands things in dreams, that every landmark she had ever loved was a line of music, and that she had grown up living inside a song without ever hearing it whole.

She followed the melody downhill, toward the border stones.

* * *

They stood where they had always stood, eleven of them in a broken arc across the valley's throat, gray granite furred with moss, each one taller than a man and older than memory. Her grandmother had walked her past them a hundred times, naming them like relatives: *This one is for the river. This one is for the dead who died kindly. This one we do not name, and neither will you.* As a child Ines had pressed her palm against the unnamed stone and felt nothing at all, which was the whole teaching, wasn't it — that Vhalen was a place of nothing at all, a pocket sewn shut, where prayers went up like smoke under a lid and came back down unchanged.

In the dream, the stones were singing too.

And behind them, pacing, were the gods.

She saw them the way you see wolves at the tree line — not all at once, but one shape resolving out of shadow, then another, then the terrible arithmetic of realizing there were more than you could count. They moved along the far side of the border stones in long, patient circuits, never crossing, never stopping, and each one wore a face she knew.

There was Marta, the cook whose hands had trembled over the duke's stew, whose death Ines had tasted in the bitter almond ghost beneath the thyme — except Marta's face was wrong now, smoothed and serene, the tremor gone, the fear gone, everything human gone, leaving only the shape of a woman with the patience of weather. There was the kitchen boy, Pieter, twelve years old, whom an answered prayer had killed; he paced on his knees, dragging his legs behind him like something learned to walk only recently and badly. And there — Ines's breath stopped in her chest, dream-breath though it was — there was a figure with her mother's shoulders, her mother's way of tilting the head when listening, walking the circuit with the others, turning at the end of the arc the way a dancer turns at the barre, precise and endless.

*Mama?*

The word didn't come out. In dreams, some words are locked.

The gods did not look at her. That was somehow worse than if they had. They paced their circuit behind the stones, patient as water finding a crack, and the hymn poured out of the ground beneath their feet, and Ines realized with a lurch that the hymn was not keeping them out. It was keeping them *in*. The border stones were not a wall around Vhalen. They were a wall around *this* — around the pacing shapes, the patient hunger, the whole pack of them — and Vhalen was not sheltered inside the wall.

Vhalen was the cell. And her family had been the lock for four hundred years, and she had spent her childhood pressing her hand against the bars and calling it peace.

One of the shapes stopped.

It was the one with her mother's shoulders. It turned its head, and the head turned too far, the way an owl turns, the way a thing turns that has watched bodies move but never quite mastered the mechanics, and where her mother's face should have been there was a smoothness, a blankness like an egg, like a stone worn featureless by four hundred years of prayer sliding over it — and then, slowly, features surfaced on it the way a drowned body surfaces, rising from below: her mother's mouth first, then her mother's eyes, arriving one at a time, blinking into focus like lamps lit in a dark house.

"Ines," said the god, in her mother's voice, and the voice was perfect, and that was the worst thing yet, that it was *perfect*, that whatever paced behind the stones had listened to her memories so long and so closely that it could reproduce her mother down to the softness at the back of a vowel. "You've been eating very well in the big world."

"I'm dreaming," Ines said. Her voice worked. Small mercies.

"You're listening," the god said. "That's rarer. Do you know how long we have waited for someone on the other side of the stones who could hear us? Your grandmother prays at us every night, did you know that? Little angry prayers. *Stay there. Stay there. Stay there.* Four hundred years of your family praying at us like a curse. We have heard every word." It smiled with her mother's mouth. "We are very good listeners. It is most of what we are."

"What are you?"

"Hungry," it said simply, and the simplicity was obscene. "Your dynasty prays to us now. Did you know that? A queen on her knees every night, asking, asking, asking. Every prayer is a crack in a jar. Every answer costs us strength we do not have, but we spend it, because spending is how you get the jar open. And you—" the god's blank face inclined toward her, and Ines felt the attention of it like a physical weight, like a hand laid flat on her sternum, "—you are the interesting one. You crossed the stones. You carry the lock in your blood and you walked out through the door and nobody stopped you, because the last priestess's line was supposed to die in there, quiet, obedient, forgotten. Instead here you are, tasting poison for queens. Here you are, *hearing*."

Behind it, the other shapes had stopped pacing. All of them. The whole pack stood still at the border stones, faces surfacing one by one out of blankness — Marta, Pieter, faces she didn't know, faces from the ledger, faces from four hundred years of Vhalen's dead — and every one of them looked at her with the same gentle, bottomless attention.

"We remember the valley," the god said, softer now, almost tender, and it was her mother's tenderness, the exact register her mother had used for fever-nights and skinned knees, and Ines wanted to scream. "Every prayer your people ever spoke here, unanswered, unheard, unspent — it fell like snow, and we lay down under it, and we remember all of it. The girl who prayed for rain in the drought year. The man who prayed his wife would stop coughing. She didn't. He stopped speaking to the sky after that, but we heard him anyway. We hear everything. We are so full of hearing." The mother-voice frayed at the edges, and underneath it was something vast and abraded, something that had been screaming politely for four centuries. "Open the door, Ines. You sing so beautifully. Your mother sang so beautifully. Open the door and let us come home."

"This isn't your home."

"It's the only one we've ever been given." The god resumed pacing, and the others resumed with it, the circuit closing, the wolf-pack moving again, and the hymn swelled up out of the ground, and Ines saw now what she hadn't seen before: the border stones were cracked. Not broken — cracked, hairline fractures running up through the moss and the granite, and through each crack seeped a thin luminous thread, like water finding its way under a door, like light under a curtain, and the threads ran uphill toward the village, toward the dooryard, toward the well where she had learned to draw water and the cherry tree where she had learned to climb.

Magic was leaking into Vhalen. Or Vhalen was leaking out. From inside the dream she couldn't tell the difference, and suspected there wasn't one.

"The moon," the god said conversationally, pacing, not looking at her anymore, which was worse, being demoted from audience to eavesdropper. "Thirteen turns. Your astronomers count on their towers. Your queen counts on her rosary. We count on our fingers, and we have so many fingers. When the thirteenth turn completes, the stones won't need opening. They'll open themselves. Old locks get tired, little taster. Everything gets tired." It paused at the end of its circuit, at the place where the arc bent back on itself, and looked at her one last time with her mother's borrowed eyes. "But it would be kinder if you opened us. Kinder for everyone. Ask your grandmother what kindness costs. Ask her what's buried in the dooryard."

"What's buried in the dooryard?"

The god smiled, and the smile was the last straw of horror, because it was warm, it was genuinely warm, it was the smile her mother had worn in the one portrait that survived, and it said:

"Wake up, Ines. You're digging."

* * *

She woke with her hands burning.

For a moment she didn't know where she was — the narrow servant's cell in the east wing, the window slit letting in the gray beginning of morning, the ledger heavy on the table where she'd fallen asleep over it, her cheek printed with the grain of the wood. Then the pain arrived properly, a deep hot ache in both palms and all ten fingertips, and she brought her hands up into the light and saw the dirt.

Earth, packed hard, brown-black, ground into the creases of her knuckles and packed under every nail to the quick. Not palace dust. Not garden loam from the queen's clipped parterres. This was mountain soil, cold-soil soil, the dense mineral earth of Vhalen's terraces, and she knew it the way you know your own heartbeat, and her hands had torn it open in her sleep hard enough to bleed — two fingernails were chipped, and the heel of her right palm was scraped raw, and there was dirt in the blood, and the blood in the dirt, and she sat very still on the edge of the cot and shook.

*I'm digging,* the god had said.

She went to the basin and washed her hands, and the water went brown, and she washed them again, and again, scrubbing with the pumice stone until her skin was pink and stinging, and the dirt under the nails would not entirely come, dark crescents lodged deep, and she stared at them and thought: *I am four hundred miles from Vhalen. There is no Vhalen soil in this room. There is no Vhalen soil within a week's ride of this room.*

Unless her hands had gone somewhere without her.

Or unless something had sent the soil to meet them halfway.

She dried her hands on her shift and forced herself to think the way she thought at the tasting table — methodically, professionally, cataloguing evidence while the fear stood quietly in the corner waiting its turn. One: the dream was not an ordinary dream. Ordinary dreams did not leave physical residue; she had built her career on knowing exactly what substances did to bodies, and sleep-walking did not import foreign soil under the nails. Two: the god — if it was a god, if the word mattered — had spoken true things she could verify. The queen did pray nightly; Tomas had told her so. The moon was on its thirteenth turn; the astronomer had announced it with shaking instruments. Three: the god had known about the dooryard, had told her to ask her grandmother what was buried there, and Ines had spent twenty years believing the dooryard held nothing but bees and a cherry tree and her grandmother's stubbornness.

Four: the cracks in the stones. She had seen the border stones a hundred times and never seen a crack. But she had been a child then, and children see what they're taught to see, and her grandmother had taught her to see a wall, a comfort, a nothing.

*Ask her what's buried in the dooryard.*

She sat down at the table and pulled the ledger toward her, Odile's thirty years of transcription, verse after verse mapped onto a country that no longer existed, and she found her own hands trembling the way Marta's hands had trembled over the stew, and she hated that, and she made herself write anyway. On a fresh page, in her smallest hand, she recorded the dream in full — the pacing shapes, the borrowed voice, the cracks, the leaking light, the instruction. If she was going mad, let the madness at least be documented. If she wasn't, then someone after her would need the record, because everyone who touched this business seemed to end up arrested, or dead, or changed.

Underneath it, after a long moment, she wrote the question the god had planted, the seed of it already sending up roots through everything she believed:

*What is buried in the dooryard?*

The chapel bell rang prime. Down in the courtyard, voices — more voices than usual, agitated ones, and the ring of armor that had become the palace's new morning music. Ines blew out her candle though the day had come, tucked the ledger into the false bottom of her sewing basket, and opened her door onto the corridor's noise.

Two guards were marching toward the choir loft. Between them, spine straight as a psalm-stave and wrists already bound, walked Odile — and the choirmaster's eyes found Ines's across the stone floor, and in them was no surprise at all, only a fierce urgent message, shaped silently, clearly, twice:

*Sing nothing. Sing nothing.*

Then the stairwell swallowed her, and the palace bells began to ring wrong.
