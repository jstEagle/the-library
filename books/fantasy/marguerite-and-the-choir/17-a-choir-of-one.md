## 17. A Choir of One

They came for Odile at dawn, when the chapel candles had burned down to stubs and the incense hung in the air like a held breath.

Ines heard them before she saw them — boots on stone, too many, moving with the particular care of men who have been told they are doing something holy and are not yet sure they believe it. She was already awake. She had been awake most of the night, sitting in the third pew where the door-shaped absence had opened in the wall, running her fingers along the mortar as if the stones might confess what they had briefly forgotten how to be.

The chapel still smelled wrong. Not of heresy — of *possibility*, which was worse. The court could forgive a smell it recognized.

Four guards in the queen's livery crossed the threshold, and behind them came Chancellor Veyre, wrapped in gray wool against the morning chill, his face arranged into an expression of regret so practiced it looked carved. Ines rose from the pew slowly, letting them see her, letting them count her — one woman, unarmed, standing between the altar and the choirmaster's loft stairs.

"Mademoiselle Almeida." Veyre inclined his head. "You should not be here."

"Neither should you," she said. "No one prays here anymore. They only listen."

Something flickered across his face — annoyance, or perhaps the exhaustion of a man who had heard too many true things said in rooms where truth was contraband. He gestured, and two guards moved past her toward the stairs. She did not stop them. She had learned, in her months at this court, which battles were walls and which were doors, and this one was already open whether she stood in it or not.

Odile descended the loft stairs on her own feet, spine straight, ledger nowhere visible. She had dressed properly — dark wool, her silver hair pinned with the same severe combs she wore every day, as if she had known. As if she had spent her last free night choosing dignity over flight, and folding her thirty years of transcription into some hiding place only she would ever find.

Or so Ines thought, until Odile passed her on the way to the door and their eyes met, and the old woman's gaze dropped — once, deliberately — to the organ bench. To the space beneath it, where the hymnals were kept that no one played anymore.

Then the guards took her arms, and Odile Vasquez-Deverell, choirmaster of the royal chapel for three decades, walked out into the pale morning like a woman going to oversee a rehearsal that had simply been moved to an inconvenient location.

The charge, read aloud at the chapel door for the benefit of any servants lingering within earshot, was *improper devotion* — the legal silk wrapped around a simpler word. Heresy. The chancellor pronounced it carefully, the way one handles a dead bird: by the wings, at arm's length, without looking down.

Ines stood in the doorway and watched them take her across the courtyard, and understood, with the cold clarity that had been settling over her since the banquet, that she was watching the future arrive early. They had taken the choirmaster because the choir sang things older than the crown. Which meant that sooner or later, they would come for the voices themselves.

And she was not one of the forty. She was something else now — the woman who survived what killed everyone else. The relic. And relics were not arrested. Relics were *kept*.

* * *

She went back inside alone.

The guards had left the chapel untended, which told her everything about how frightened the court truly was: they did not want to stand in this room any longer than duty required. Even faith, apparently, could be posted elsewhere.

The organ bench lifted away from its base easier than she expected, as if the wood had been waiting. Beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth and bound with the flat black ribbon Odile used for funeral scores, lay the ledger.

It was heavier than a book had any right to be. When she carried it to the light of the east window and unwrapped it, she understood why. This was not one book but many — pages of different ages sewn together across decades, vellum beside cheap rag paper beside a strip of bark-paper so old its edges had gone soft as cloth. Thirty years of a single obsession, bound in the order of a life rather than the order of a library.

The first page bore the title in Odile's younger hand, the ink still confident:

*The Hymn of the Door, being a map of Vhalen in thirteen verses, transcribed from the mouths of the choir.*

Below it, smaller: *Verse eleven is missing. Verse eleven is always missing.*

Ines turned the pages with hands that were not quite steady. Each verse occupied its own section — the words of the hymn, then beneath them, in Odile's meticulous script, the geography: *the second verse bends east at the river that no longer has a name; the fifth verse climbs; the seventh verse marks the ford where the border stones were set.* Marginalia crowded the edges — dates, cross-references, fragments of interviews with choir members long dead. *Mme. Corvain, alto, 19 years in the loft, remembers her grandmother singing the eleventh while shelling beans. Says it made the beans rattle in the bowl. Refused to sing it herself. Wept instead.*

Wept instead. Ines thought of her own childhood, of her grandmother's voice in the dark teaching her a melody that was never called a hymn, only a lullaby, only *hush now*. Of the way the valley went quiet afterward, a silence with weight to it, like snowfall.

She had thought it was just a song. She had thought everything was just a song, just a stew, just a taste, right up until the moment the chapel wall forgot itself for one breath and showed her a door-shaped nothing humming with cold.

Near the end of the ledger, the handwriting changed — grew faster, less certain, the letters leaning as though written by someone racing a deadline only she could see. The final entry was dated nine days ago. Nine days before the chapel incident. Before Ines sang the eleventh verse and the wall opened.

*She has the verse. The taster girl. I watched her lips move during rehearsal and knew the shape of it — the alto line, the mother's line. God forgive me for what I am about to do, letting her find it on her own. But a key must turn itself. If I tell her what she carries, she will carry it like a burden. If she discovers it, she will carry it like a name.*

Ines closed the ledger and sat for a long time in the empty chapel, listening to the building settle around her the way old buildings do — groaning, remembering.

A key must turn itself.

She had been turning all her life, then. Every dose of poison, every numb fingertip, every grayed hour of vision — all of it training her body to survive the very thing she apparently was. She thought of Tomas's confession, weeks ago now, that the previous taster hadn't died of poison but of what the poison let in. She had assumed he meant madness. Sitting here, with the ledger warm in her lap, she wondered if he had meant something more literal. Something with a door in it.

When she finally rose, her knees ached and the candle stubs had drowned in their own wax, and she discovered she was not alone after all.

* * *

The soprano was standing in the shadow of the baptistery — Maren, youngest of the forty, seventeen at most, with the kind of voice that made grown men weep and the kind of nerve that made choirmasters weep for different reasons. She had clearly been there a while. There was dust on her skirt from the floor behind the font.

"I saw where she looked," Maren said, without preamble. "Under the bench. I've been waiting for you to leave."

"You've been waiting to see if I'd take it."

"I've been waiting to see what you'd do with it." The girl stepped forward, chin up, and Ines saw that her hands were shaking even though her voice wasn't. "Thirty of us know pieces of it. Verses our families kept like seeds through the winter. Odile collected us one by one — you think she chose the best voices? She chose the best *memories*. Every singer in that loft is carrying a fragment of a country that doesn't exist, and none of us knew why until the wall opened."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because they'll come for us next," Maren said simply. "Not all at once. One singer at a time, reassigned, transferred, married off to distant provinces. That's how you kill a choir without spilling blood — you scatter it. Odile taught us that. She said the last time the Door nearly opened, forty years ago, the crown didn't arrest anyone. It just gave scholarships."

The ledger seemed to grow heavier in Ines's arms. "What do you want from me?"

"I want to know if you're going to finish it." Maren's eyes were wet and furious. "She spent thirty years collecting our grandmothers' songs like a woman gathering scattered coins, and she got within one verse of the whole sum, and now they've taken her, and someone has to—" The girl stopped, swallowed. "Someone has to be the choir now. Even if it's a choir of one."

Outside, the bells began the morning office, and neither of them moved. Ines looked down at the ledger, at Odile's younger self declaring on the first page that verse eleven was always missing, and thought of the lullaby sleeping in her own throat, patient as a seed.

"I don't know anything about leading people," she said.

"Good," said Maren. "Neither did she, at first. She used to throw up before rehearsals, the first year. Tomas told us. He tells everyone everything eventually — it's his whole tragedy." A ghost of a smile crossed the girl's face and vanished. "Come to the loft tonight. After compline. Those of us who still dare. We'll teach you what we carry, and you—" she nodded at the ledger— "you keep the map."

* * *

They came for Ines at midday, as she had known they would, but not with chains.

Chancellor Veyre received her in a small room with one window, the kind of room built for conversations that would later be denied. Tea was poured. Neither of them drank.

"The choirmaster acted alone," he said. "The court wishes it understood that no other member of the chapel staff is under suspicion."

"How generous of the court."

"Ines Almeida." He set down the pot with exaggerated care. "Do you know why you were brought here? Truly? Not the oath, not the tasting. Do you know?"

She said nothing. Silence had become, in recent months, her sharpest instrument. You could taste almost anything in a silence — fear, mostly, and underneath the fear, want.

"Because you cannot be poisoned," Veyre said. "By man or — by other means. Because whatever is happening to this kingdom, whatever has begun answering prayers, it cannot touch you. Do you understand what that makes you, in a court full of frightened people?"

"A relic," Ines said. "I've heard."

"It makes you *safe*." His voice dropped, and for a moment she saw the man beneath the chancellor — tired, middle-aged, afraid in the specific way of administrators who can calculate every risk except the ones that matter. "There are forty singers in that loft, and every one of them is now a liability. But you — you can walk into the chapel, walk into the archive, walk anywhere the gods have touched, and come out unchanged. The queen requires someone to inventory the chapel's... acquisitions. The prayer-gifts. Someone immune."

"And if I refuse?"

Veyre smiled without warmth. "Then I'm afraid the investigation into improper devotion will need to widen. Singers are such mobile people. So many relatives in distant provinces."

There it was. Not a request — a hostage exchange, with the choir held on one side of the scale and her obedience on the other. She thought of Odile walking across the courtyard with her spine straight. She thought of Maren's shaking hands and steady voice. She thought of her grandmother saying, once, that power never asks; it only offers you the smallest available cage and calls it shelter.

"I'll need access to the loft," Ines said. "After hours. For the inventory."

Veyre blinked, evidently surprised the negotiation had gone so smoothly. "Granted."

"And the choirmaster's effects released to me. Papers, scores. For cataloguing."

"Granted."

He believed he had won. She let him believe it. It cost nothing, and it bought her exactly what she needed: the keys to every locked room, worn openly, unremarkable — a taster doing her grim duty among dangerous objects, immune, reliable, safe.

Safe. She almost laughed on the way out. Nothing in her life had ever been less safe than this moment, walking a corridor with a stolen map of a dead country under her arm and forty scattered voices depending on a stranger who couldn't be killed but could certainly be broken.

* * *

The loft that night smelled of dust and candle smoke and, faintly, of the hymn itself — she was beginning to believe places could hold a song the way wood holds heat, long after the fire is out.

Eleven of them came. Eleven of the forty, slipping in through the cloister stair with hoods up and instruments left behind, because instruments could be confiscated and throats could not. Maren counted them twice against the wall, her lips moving, and Ines understood the counting was not about numbers. It was about who was missing, and why, and whether the missing ones had chosen absence or been chosen for it.

An elderly tenor named Bram sat down heavily on the front bench and looked at Ines with the flat appraisal of a man who has buried two choirs already.

"Odile used to stand there," he said, nodding at the conductor's platform. "Thirty years. Never raised her voice. Never needed to." He folded his hands. "So. You're the one who opened the wall."

"I'm the one who sang a lullaby in the wrong room."

"That's what opening a wall is." He said it without malice, the way one states weather. "The question is whether you can close one. Or whether closing it was never the point."

The others watched her — a mezzo with a nursing infant somewhere in the city, a bass who had gone gray overnight since the chapel incident, two sisters who sang the descant lines and had not spoken since their brother, a kitchen boy, died of an answered prayer. Ines set the ledger on the music stand, and the sound of it — old paper settling, decades shifting — quieted the room better than any command.

"She hid this under the organ bench," Ines said. "She knew they were coming. She had time to run, and she stayed, and she let them take her, because a choirmaster who flees teaches her choir to scatter, and a choirmaster who stands still teaches them to hold the line." She opened the ledger to the first page, to the young confident ink. "*Verse eleven is missing. Verse eleven is always missing.* Not anymore. I have it. My grandmother taught it to me before I could write. She called it a lullaby. It isn't. None of it ever was."

Silence, the good kind — the kind a choir knows how to hold.

"So here is what happens now," Ines continued, and heard Odile's cadence enter her voice uninvited, steady, low, never needing volume. "They will scatter you if they can. Transfers, marriages, scholarships — Maren told me. Don't wait for the order. Refuse nothing openly, obey everything slowly, and stay within reach of this city until I say otherwise. I will catalogue the chapel's gifts for the chancellor, which gives me lawful reason to touch everything the gods have left in this palace, and I will learn what they are feeding on and what they are preparing for. And each of you will teach me your verse — the one your family kept, the one you thought was just a song your grandmother hummed. All of them. Until I can hold the whole hymn alone."

Bram's eyebrows rose. "Alone? No one holds the whole hymn alone. That's rather the point of a choir."

"Then I'll be the point that fails," Ines said, "until I'm the point that doesn't. Odile spent thirty years assembling this map one memory at a time. I intend to memorize it in weeks, because we don't have thirty years. The moon is already turning its thirteenth lap, and when it finishes, whatever the gods have been planning finishes with it."

The mezzo with the infant spoke for the first time, softly. "And Odile? What happens to her?"

The question landed in the room like a stone in still water. Ines thought of the holding cells beneath the chancery, of trials conducted in whispers, of the word *heresy* doing its slow work on an old woman's name.

"I get her out," Ines said. "That comes second only to the hymn itself. She built this choir of memories. I won't let it be remembered as a choir of one."

She looked down at the ledger then, at the map of a country erased from every treaty, drawn in the margins of a song every singer in this room had inherited without knowing what they carried. Burden, Maren had called it, or meant to. But standing in the loft with eleven frightened, stubborn voices watching her, Ines felt the weight settle differently — not like a load but like a mantle, the way her grandmother had said a lock feels the key approaching: not heavier, exactly. More aware.

They began, quietly, to sing.

One verse at a time, passed voice to voice like water down a line of cupped hands — the second verse bending east, the fifth climbing, the seventh marking the ford — and Ines drank each one, memorizing not just notes but the geography humming underneath, the shape of a hidden country emerging note by note in the dark above a sleeping palace.

It was nearly midnight when the last verse settled into her, and Maren blew out the candles, and the choir of eleven became shadows becoming footsteps becoming gone.

Ines remained a moment longer, alone in the loft, the ledger under her arm. Below her, through the floor, she felt it again — the faint tremor in the chapel walls, the hum that had answered the hymn since before anyone could date it. Patient. Waiting. Counting turns of the moon along with everything else.

And beneath the hum, so faint she might have imagined it, another sound threaded through — a melody in the alto register, rising from somewhere far below the chapel, far below the city, from the direction of a valley that no map admitted to.

Her grandmother's lullaby. Sung by no throat she knew.

Coming to meet her, verse by verse, across four hundred years of silence.
