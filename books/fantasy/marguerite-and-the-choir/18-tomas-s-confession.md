## 18. Tomas's Confession

The lamplight in the steward's office was the color of old honey, and it made everything look sweeter than it was — the ledgers stacked like headstones, the decanter of plum brandy sweating on its silver tray, the man sitting behind the desk with his hands folded as though he were attending his own funeral.

Ines had come because he had asked her to, and that alone was strange enough to make her cautious. In three months at the palace, Tomas of the household stewardship had never once asked her for anything. He was the one who brought things: her wages, her instructions, her meals to test, each tray covered with a cloth he lifted with the same careful gravity a priest brings to communion. He was a man made entirely of errands, and errands did not sit down across from you and pour two glasses of brandy with hands that would not quite hold still.

"You wanted to see me," she said.

"I wanted to tell you something." He pushed one glass toward her and did not touch his own. "And I'd ask you to hear all of it before you decide what to do with me."

She sat. The chair creaked. Somewhere above them, muffled by floors and stone, the choir was rehearsing the evening offices, their voices coming down through the building like water through limestone — present everywhere, originating nowhere. Ines had learned to hear the hymn even when she wasn't listening for it. It lived in the walls now. It lived, she suspected, in her.

"Tomas," she said, "if this is about the kitchen—"

"It's about Vhalen."

The word landed in the room and stayed there. She felt her whole body go quiet the way a forest goes quiet before weather. No one at court said the valley's name. They said *the border provinces*, they said *the interior*, they said nothing at all and let the silence do the work. Her homeland existed here only as an absence, a hole in every map shaped exactly like her childhood.

"Say it again," she said.

"Vhalen." He said it carefully, the way a man handles a blade he knows is sharp. "I sent the courier."

* * *

He began, as guilty men often do, too far back.

"You have to understand what the chapel was like last winter. Before the answers started. We prayed the way people sweep a floor — without thinking, without expecting the dust to thank us. Queen Sabine prayed for an heir every morning of her marriage. The king prayed for rain in the eastern holdings. I prayed—" He stopped. His thumb moved along the rim of his untouched glass, tracing a circle, a seal, a small orbit. "I prayed for my sister. Marta. She took a fever in the autumn and the physicians bled her until she was more ghost than girl, and I went to the old chapel because the new one had done nothing, and I knelt where the floor is worn down, and I said the words my grandmother used to say, the ones nobody remembers anymore. And the fever broke."

"That was kind of the god," Ines said, and could not keep the edge out of it. She had seen what answered prayers looked like by then. She had seen the prayer-gifts in their lined boxes: the comb carved from a single piece of amber that wept sap forever; the loaves that rose warm from cold ovens but tasted, when she tested them, of iron filings and river mud; the little silver bell that rang itself at dusk and made the dogs howl. Kindness, from the old gods, was a hook dressed as bread.

"It was kind," Tomas said. "That's what no one understands. It was *kind*. Marta woke up hungry. She ate three bowls of porridge and complained about the salt. For six weeks I was the happiest man in this palace." His hand flattened on the desk. "And then the god came back to me. Not in a dream. Not in fire. It came back the way a creditor comes back. Politely."

"What did it want?"

"At first, nothing. Attention. Gratitude. I lit candles. I said thanks. And then one night it asked me a question, and the question was: *where does your queen find a taster who cannot die?*" He finally looked up at her, and Ines saw that his eyes were rimmed red, that he had not been sleeping, that this confession had been eating him alive from the inside the way she imagined the gods ate everything. "Because that's what they needed, Ines. That's what they've needed since the answers began. A body that poison can't touch. Not mortal poison. *Their* poison. Every gift they send has a taste, and every taste needs testing, and every tester dies. Four of them died before you. The kitchen boy was the fourth. Did you know that? Everyone says he died of a bad oyster. He died of a psalm. He tasted a honey-cake meant for the queen's table and the honey-cake had a verse baked into it, and the verse went into him and *hollowed him out* like a gourd."

Ines thought of the boy — Ferren, his name had been, fourteen years old, quick with a joke, always singing under his breath. She remembered the closed casket, and how the palace had hurried the funeral the way you hurry past an accident.

"The gods told you where to find me," she said. Her voice sounded far away to herself. "They gave you my name."

"They gave me more than a name." He reached into the drawer at his knee and set something on the desk between them: a scrap of paper, soft with handling, covered in writing that was not writing — long looping strokes that resolved into legibility only when she wasn't looking directly at them. "They told me there was a girl in a valley that isn't on any map, daughter of a woman named Marguerite, who had spent her whole life drinking small poisons so that large ones couldn't find her. They told me her grandmother kept bees and her mother was dead and her blood was a locked door. They told me the exact road, the exact ford, the exact hour the border mist thins. And they told me—" his voice cracked, and he pressed on through the crack like a man wading a cold river, "—they told me that if I sent the summons, Marta would live out her natural life. Untouched. Unbothered. Safe."

The lamp flame leaned in a draft that hadn't existed a moment before. Ines watched it right itself.

"So the courier," she said slowly. "The impossible courier. The one that arrived in a valley that receives no post. That was you."

"I paid him myself. Out of my own purse. I told him it was a matter of state and that if anyone asked, he'd never made the journey." Tomas laughed, and it was the worst sound she'd heard from him yet — hollow, self-scraping. "A matter of state. Yes. The state of my own conscience, which I sold for one healthy sister."

Above them the choir moved into the second verse of the evening hymn, and the walls hummed their low agreement, and Ines sat very still and let the truth settle over her like silt in still water.

She should have been furious. Some part of her was — a hot, clean coal of anger buried under the breastbone, aimed at this tired man and the gods who had played him like a fretted instrument. But underneath the anger, colder and more terrible, was understanding. Because she knew what it was to love someone in a valley full of silences. She knew what she might have promised, what she might have paid, to hear her grandmother's cough cured, her mother's grave marked. The gods had not corrupted Tomas. They had simply found the price tag already hanging on him and read it aloud.

"Why are you telling me this?" she asked. "Now?"

"Because of Odile." His jaw tightened. "Because they took her away in irons for heresy, and everyone in this palace is pretending that's justice, and I helped build the world where that's justice. Because the moon is nearly done turning and I can feel it, Ines, the way you feel a storm in your teeth. And because—" he turned the scrap of god-writing toward himself, then away, unable to look at it, "—because they've started asking me for things again. New things. And I don't think I have another sister in me to trade."

* * *

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The brandy sat untouched between them, going flat in the honeyed light.

Ines thought about her grandmother burning the summons, and the wax seal reappearing on the table, unburned, patient as a cat. She thought about the queen who wouldn't meet her eyes. She thought about the Door-shaped absence in the chapel wall, open for one breath, and Odile weeping in front of it like a woman shown the face of a drowned child.

"You could have refused," she said at last. "You could have gone to the choirmaster. To the archivist. To anyone."

"To whom?" Tomas spread his hands. "Who in this palace believes a steward who says the old gods are dictating his errands? I didn't even believe it myself for the first month. I thought I was ill. I drank willow bark tea like it was going out of fashion." He shook his head. "There was no one to refuse *to*, Ines. That's the trick of it. You can't refuse a letter that arrives inside your own skull. You can only decide whether to deliver it."

"And you delivered it."

"And I delivered it." He met her eyes now, fully, and did not flinch from whatever he found in them. "I have rehearsed this conversation forty times and in thirty-nine of them you call for the guard. So. Call for the guard. Or take the brandy. One of those things is owed to you, and I'll leave it to you which."

Ines looked at the glass. Then at him. Then at the scrap of god-writing, which seemed, in the corner of her vision, to be breathing.

Here was the thing she had learned in three months of tasting other people's deaths: that the body always knows before the mind admits it. Her body was telling her now. It was telling her that Tomas of the household stewardship was not finished — that the guilt in him was not a wound but a *door*, and that doors, in this kingdom, opened both ways.

"I forgive you," she said.

His breath went out of him like a felled tree. His shoulders dropped; his hands, which had been clenched white around nothing, opened. For one naked moment he looked younger than her, boyish, emptied — a man setting down a weight he had carried so long he'd forgotten his own posture without it.

"Thank you," he whispered. "I don't— you don't know what—"

"Don't thank me yet." She leaned forward and took the glass after all, and drank, and set it down with a click that sounded, in the hush, like a lock engaging. "Because forgiveness is not the same thing as absolution, Tomas, and I need you to understand the difference. I forgive you the way you'd forgive a man who burned your house down while sleepwalking. It wasn't his fault. But the house is still ash, and someone still has to rebuild it, and he's the only one who knows where the embers fell."

He stared at her. Above them the hymn ended, and the silence it left behind had edges.

"What do you want from me?" he asked quietly.

"Everything you haven't given anyone else." She counted it off on her fingers, methodical, the way she tested a spoonful — first the smell, then the color, then the smallest possible taste. "First: the letters. Whatever the gods write to you, whenever they write it, I see it within a day. Second: the names. Every person in this palace you suspect of hearing them. The courtiers who've stopped eating at their own banquets. The maids who sing verses they shouldn't know. I want the list, and I want it honest, even if your own name is on it. *Especially* then."

"My name is on it," he said simply. "It's the only name I'm sure of."

"Good. Third." She paused. This was the part she had rehearsed on the walk over, though she hadn't known then what she was rehearsing for. "Third: the courier. The man you sent into Vhalen. I want to know everything about him. His name, his route, what he was paid, and whether he ever came back."

Tomas blinked. "Why does the courier matter?"

"Because he crossed the border stones carrying a royal summons sealed in wax that burns and doesn't burn." Ines held his gaze. "Because my grandmother tells me the stones cracked the week he passed through them. Because I think your gods didn't just use you to fetch a taster, Tomas. I think they used you to test whether the Door could be knocked on. And I want to know what answer they got."

The lamp flame leaned again. This time it did not right itself quickly.

"He came back," Tomas said slowly, reluctantly, a man opening a door he'd rather keep shut. "Three days later. Half-mad with cold. He said the valley wasn't where the maps used to say it was — that he rode the coordinates twice and found only fog and a ridge that moved. And then on the third try the fog just... opened. Like it had decided to let him in." He swallowed. "He said a woman met him at the stones. Old woman. Kept bees. She wouldn't take the letter from his hand — made him set it on the ground and back away. And then she burned it right there, in front of him, in a little tin dish, and stood watching until it was ash, and then she looked at him — he said it was the worst thing, sir, the way she *looked* at him, like she was counting his teeth — and she said one sentence."

Ines's heartbeat stuttered, as it sometimes did now, a skipped stitch in the weave of her. "What sentence?"

"She said: *"Tell them the lock remembers who built it."*"

The words settled into the room. Ines felt them settle into her deeper, down past the trained numbness, past the grayed edges of her vision, into some cellar of herself that her mother's blood had stocked long before she was born. *The lock remembers who built it.* Her grandmother, standing at the border stones, speaking to gods through a trembling courier, in the only language the priestess line had left: warning.

"She knew," Ines said. "From the moment the letter crossed the stones, she knew who sent it and why."

"Then she's wiser than the entire court of Sabine the First." Tomas rubbed his face with both hands. When he lowered them, he looked ten years older than he had at the start of the hour, and lighter too, the way a man looks after bleeding. "You'll have your lists. Tonight. And the letters — there's one waiting now, as it happens. It came during the evening offices. I haven't opened it." He drew a folded paper from his breast pocket and laid it beside the scrap of god-writing. This one was ordinary paper, ordinary ink, addressed in a fine slanted hand: *To the Taster.*

"You haven't opened it," Ines repeated.

"I'm done opening things alone." He slid it across the desk to her. "Whatever it says, I'd rather be damned in company."

Ines picked it up. The paper was cool, cooler than the room justified, and beneath the smell of ink she caught something else — faint, sweetish, familiar. Honey. The same note she'd caught in the poisoned honey-cake that had hollowed out Ferren the kitchen boy.

She broke the seal. Inside, in that fine slanted hand, was a single line:

*The alto voice is missing from your choir. We know where it sleeps. Ask the steward what he buried in the chapel garden.*

Ines looked up. Tomas had gone the color of candle tallow.

"There's nothing buried in the garden," he said. "I've tended that garden for eleven years, there's nothing but boxwood and—"

He stopped. Ines watched him stop. Watched the memory surface in his face like a drowned body rising, slow and inevitable, and she understood — with the cold professional clarity she brought to every cup, every spoonful, every gift from the gods — that she had forgiven this man not because he deserved it, but because a guilty man will follow you anywhere, and she was going to need someone to dig.
