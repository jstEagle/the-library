## 19. The Twelfth Turn

The moon completed its twelfth turn on a Tuesday, though no one in the palace called it that afterward. They called it the night the milk soured in every churn from the capital to the coast, all at once, at the same hour, as if the whole kingdom had been struck by a single thought.

Ines knew it was coming the way she knew most things now — through her body before her mind caught up. She woke three nights before the turn with her teeth aching and the taste of iron at the back of her throat, the taste she had learned to read the way sailors read weather. Something was gathering. Something was leaning toward the world with its mouth open.

She sat up in her narrow bed in the taster's quarters and watched the moon through the leaded window. It was fat and wrong-colored, a bruised yellow like an old bruise going green at the edges. Twelve turns. The astronomer had announced it from the observatory steps with the flat voice of a man reading his own death warrant: *the moon will complete a thirteenth turn before the season ends.* Four hundred years of court records said what that meant. The dynasty had built chapels against it. Had buried kings with their hands folded over prayer books they'd never dared open.

And now Ines lay in the dark and felt the kingdom holding its breath.

* * *

The first unasked gift arrived on a Wednesday morning, which was fitting, because Wednesdays were when the palace laundress, Marta, prayed — not formally, not kneeling, just a small muttering over the wash basins: *let my sister's hip mend, let her walk again, she's only forty-two.*

Marta's sister lived two hundred miles away, in a fishing village Ines couldn't have found on a map. On Thursday morning a letter came to Marta, written in her sister's hand, saying: *I got out of bed and walked to the well and I don't hurt anymore, praise be, but Mama's grave is empty, Marta. Dug clean. What did you do?*

Marta brought the letter to Tomas because she didn't know who else to bring it to, and Tomas brought it to Ines because he didn't know who else to bring it to either, and the three of them stood in the steward's office with the paper trembling between them while outside the window the ordinary business of the palace went on — guards changing shift, a cart of cabbages rattling across the courtyard — all of it suddenly looking like a stage set, like something painted on cloth that a strong wind could tear away.

"Empty," Marta kept saying. "The grave was empty. Not robbed. Empty. The dirt wasn't even disturbed."

"The god took the body," Ines said quietly. "As payment."

"But I never asked for anything. I just — I just wanted—"

"That's all a prayer is." Ines heard her own voice come out flat and cold and hated it. "Wanting, said out loud where something can hear."

Tomas took the letter from Marta gently and set it on his desk, weighting the corners with candlesticks, as if it might blow away into some other part of their lives where it would make more sense. His face had gone the color of tallow. Ines had been watching him change for weeks now — the steward who had once shown her the chapel with reverent terror in his eyes was becoming a man who flinched at doorways, who checked under tables, who had started sleeping in his office because his own rooms felt watched.

"There were four more this morning," he said. "Four more letters. A cooper in the lower town prayed for rain for his apple trees. He got rain. He also got a flood that took his neighbor's house, and his neighbor's family was inside it." He stopped. Started again. "A girl prayed to be beautiful. She is. Everyone says so. But she says when people look at her now they can't look away, and it frightens them, and she can't make them stop, and last night her own mother wouldn't sit at table with her."

"And?"

"And a man prayed for his debts to be forgiven. All his creditors died in their sleep. Peacefully, the physicians say. Smiling." Tomas looked at the window, at the cabbage cart, at nothing. "Smiling, Ines."

Outside, a bell began to ring — not the chapel bell, not the hour bell, but the alarm bell from the gatehouse, ragged and urgent. They heard shouting in the courtyard. Neither of them moved to look. There was a strange comfort in standing still together in the small office, in pretending that if they didn't move, the world wouldn't finish tipping.

It tipped anyway.

* * *

By evening the city was burning in small places.

Not the great fires of war or riot — nothing so legible. Little fires. A baker's stall overturned because someone swore the bread had risen overnight into loaves shaped like faces, and whose faces, whose, whose. A tailor's shop shuttered because the mirrors in it had begun showing customers a moment behind themselves. A crowd gathered around a well that had started giving sweet water after a century of brackish, and half the crowd drank and wept with gratitude and half the crowd screamed that drinking it was how it got *into* you.

Ines walked through it because walking was the only thing that quieted the ache in her teeth, and because Odile had sent word — a scrap of paper passed hand to hand through three servants, the way everything passed now, since the choirmaster trusted no messenger and no room with more than one door — *come to the loft after vespers, come alone, come before the moon rises.*

The streets smelled of woodsmoke and fear-sweat and, underneath it, something floral and wrong, like lilies left too long in a closed room. She passed a shrine to one of the new-approved saints and saw that someone had smashed it, carefully, methodically, leaving the saint's face intact and destroying only the hands. She passed an old woman kneeling in the gutter, praying aloud, loudly, deliberately, arms spread wide: *take me, take me, I'm ready, take me instead of the children.* Ines stopped. The old woman's eyes were clear and sane and absolutely certain of being heard.

For the first time in Ines's life, she envied that certainty. And then, immediately, she pitied it. Both at once, tangled together like roots.

"You shouldn't pray here," Ines said. "Not out loud. Not now."

"They're listening," the old woman said, radiant. "Can't you feel it? They're finally listening."

That was the thing Ines could not explain to anyone who hadn't grown up in Vhalen. Everyone else in the kingdom experienced the answered prayers as a miracle arriving after four centuries of silence. Ines experienced them as a border failing. She knew what it meant when the walls between wanting and getting grew thin. She had been raised on the other side of those walls, in the country the silence had been built to protect — or to imprison; she no longer knew which story was true, and every day the two stories leaned harder against each other inside her chest.

She walked faster.

* * *

Odile was waiting in the choir loft with every lamp lit, which was itself a message. For thirty years the choirmaster had worked by single candle, hoarding light like coin. Tonight the loft blazed, and Odile sat in the middle of it surrounded by her ledger — the great leather-bound book of verses and locations, thirty years of transcription — with pages fanned out around her on the floor like the spokes of a wheel.

"Look at the sky before you look at me," Odile said, without turning around.

Ines went to the loft's east window. The moon was rising, huge and bruised-yellow, one turn from done. Below, the city spread out in its little fires and its crowds and its smashed shrines, and beyond the city the dark fields ran to the horizon, and somewhere past all of it, past mountains she had crossed once in a courier's cart with her heart in her throat, there was a valley that no map remembered.

"My grandmother used to say," Ines said, "that the moon counting past twelve was the sound of a lock turning. Thirteen clicks. We just never knew if it was locking or unlocking."

Odile snorted. It was the closest thing to laughter Ines had heard from her in weeks. "Your grandmother and four hundred years of frightened astronomers agree on something. That's nearly a miracle in itself." She gathered a handful of pages and held them out. "Come. Sit. I need your eyes and I need them tonight, because tomorrow there may not be an tonight."

The pages were Odile's transcriptions of the hymn — verse by verse, each annotated with the location it mapped, the chapel ruins, the river fords, the standing stones of a country that had been erased so thoroughly that Odile had spent decades finding its bones. Ines had seen the ledger before. But tonight Odile had added new pages, and the ink on them was still sharp-smelling.

"These aren't from the hymn," Ines said.

"No. These are reports. Every answered prayer in the kingdom since the eleventh turn, mapped." Odile's blunt finger traced across the page, and Ines saw it — saw the pattern that made her stomach drop through the floor. The gifts weren't scattered. They clustered. They fell along lines, along routes, like travelers following roads. Like pilgrims.

"They're moving," Ines said.

"They're *converging*. Whatever answers the prayers isn't everywhere at once. It's traveling. It's coming from somewhere toward somewhere else, and it stops along the way to pay for what it takes." Odile's voice was dry as old paper, but her hands weren't steady. "I've charted forty-one answered prayers. Draw the line yourself."

Ines drew it. The line ran from the western coast, where the first gifts had fallen, eastward, curving slightly south, passing through towns she knew by reputation and hills she didn't know at all — and terminating, if you extended it past the edge of the chart, past the blank space where cartographers wrote *here be nothing*, in a valley that appeared on no map because it had been removed from every map.

Vhalen.

"It's coming home," Ines whispered. Then she corrected herself, because the word felt wrong in her mouth. "No. It's coming back to the border. To the Door."

"To *you*," Odile said. Not cruelly. Just precisely, the way she corrected a singer's pitch. "You're the vessel that doesn't break. You're the taster who survives divine poison. Did you think that was an accident? Did you think the gods told Tomas where to find you by chance?" She shook her head slowly. "Everything that's happened since the courier rode into your valley has been a table being set. And the twelfth turn is the soup course."

Ines looked down at her own hands. Steady. Her hands were always steady; that was the first thing they trained into a taster, steadiness above all. But beneath the steadiness she could feel the other thing, the inheritance thing, the priestess-blood thing, humming in her marrow like a struck bell that wouldn't finish ringing.

"What happens on the thirteenth turn?" she asked. "The records say the dynasty fears it. Fear of what, exactly?"

Odile was quiet for a long moment. In the loft below them, the empty pews of the chapel threw long shadows in the lamplight, and the walls — the walls that hummed when the choir sang the old hymn — were humming now, faintly, unprompted, a note so low it was less a sound than a pressure.

"No record survives from the last thirteenth turn," Odile said finally. "Which is itself a record. Kingdoms keep accounts of their disasters. Plagues, famines, wars — all written down, all mourned in ink. But whatever happened four hundred years ago, someone burned every page of it and then, I believe, burned the people who remembered it." She looked up, and her eyes in the lamplight were the color of the ledger's leather. "Except the singers. Except the families who carried the hymn in their mouths because paper burns and memory doesn't. Your mother's family. My grandmother's family. Forty bloodlines, Ines, each holding one verse like a coal carried in cupped hands through a long dark night."

"And the thirteenth turn is morning?"

"The thirteenth turn is *whatever we sing it to be*." Odile rose, joints cracking, and went to the window beside Ines. Together they watched the bruised moon climb. "One turn left. Maybe twenty-eight days. Maybe fewer — time is going strange, haven't you noticed? Bells ringing late. Bread rising wrong. Days folding into each other." She exhaled. "I need the alto verse. You know that. I've needed it for thirty years. Without it the hymn is a map missing its center, a circle that won't close."

"I don't have it," Ines said. "My grandmother never taught me the alto line. She taught me eleven verses as lullabies and refused, always refused, to go past eleven. I asked once, when I was small. She hit me. The only time she ever hit me." Ines touched her cheek, though the blow had been decades ago and on the other cheek, and the touching was pure habit, pure superstition. "She said the twelfth verse wasn't mine to carry yet. *Yet.*"

"Then the gods will offer it to you," Odile said simply. "They offered everything else. That's how they work — I've read enough answered prayers now to see the shape of them. They give you exactly what you want, priced exactly at what you can't afford. And you—" she turned, and took Ines's chin in her rough fingers, tilting her face to the lamplight like a jeweler assessing a stone, "—you want your homeland safe, your mother's mission finished, and your own name cleared of being the thing they built you to be. Three wants. Three prices. Mark me, Ines Almeida: before the moon finishes its turn, something is going to put all three on a table in front of you and ask you to taste."

Below them, faintly, the chapel walls hummed.

* * *

She found Tomas in the courtyard at midnight, standing in the open with his face turned up to the moon, and for one horrible heartbeat she thought he was praying — that the steward who feared the gods more than any man in the palace had finally broken and joined the kneeling multitudes.

But he was only smoking, a filthy habit he hid from everyone, the ember of the cigarette a tiny orange defiance against the vast yellow dark. He lowered it when he saw her.

"Can't sleep indoors anymore," he said, by way of greeting. "The rooms listen. I know how that sounds."

"It sounds accurate."

He almost smiled. Then the smile fell off his face, the way smiles had been falling off faces all week, and he nodded toward the gatehouse, where torches bobbed in a ragged procession. Refugees, already — from the eastern farms, from the villages along the convergence line Odile had drawn. Families with carts and goats and children asleep against their shoulders, coming to the capital because the capital had walls, because walls had always meant safety, because no one had told them that the thing coming was not the kind of thing walls were for.

"A boy arrived an hour ago," Tomas said quietly. "Ten years old. Walked nine miles. His little sister broke her arm falling from a hayloft, and his mother prayed over her — just once, just *please let her arm heal* — and it healed. Perfectly. Before their eyes. Bone knitting like thread pulled through cloth." He drew on the cigarette. His hand shook. "The sister doesn't cry anymore. Doesn't laugh either. Sits and stares and her arm is perfect and the physician says there's nothing wrong with her, nothing at all, except that when he shone a lamp in her eyes, the pupils didn't answer."

Ines closed her eyes. Behind her eyelids she saw the fishing village, the empty grave, the smiling dead creditors. The pattern of it. Gifts that took. Healings that hollowed. The gods paying for what they gave with the only currency they had ever truly valued — not gold, not grain, but the interior of a person, the secret rooms of the soul, emptied out one answered wish at a time.

"They're not feeding," she said. "They're stocking a pantry."

Tomas looked at her. "For the thirteenth turn."

"For whatever they intend to do on the thirteenth turn." She opened her eyes. "Tomas — the queen. Has she—"

"She prays constantly now. You know that. The whole palace knows that. She's locked herself in the west chapel and the prayers come through the walls at night, and the servants won't walk that corridor after dark." He dropped the cigarette and ground it under his heel with unnecessary force. "And yesterday a gift arrived at her door. Unasked. Unwrapped. Sitting on the threshold like a cat bringing in a bird."

"What was it?"

"A pair of children's shoes." His voice cracked on the second word. "Perfectly made. The right size. For a child that doesn't exist yet." He met her eyes, and in his face she saw the thing she had seen growing in him for weeks, the guilt that he had fed her escape from Vhalen like a hook baited with a summons, the guilt that was eating him alive and that she had already learned, shamefully, skillfully, to use. "Ines. If the gods are stocking a pantry — what do you suppose they intend to cook?"

Above them the moon climbed, one full turn from done, and the chapel walls hummed their low unfinished note, and somewhere far to the west, along a line only a mad old choirmaster had thought to draw, something patient was walking toward a valley that no longer existed, stopping at every doorstep to leave a gift.

Ines stood in the courtyard until the cold drove her inside, and all the while her teeth ached, and all the while she thought: *twenty-eight days. Twenty-seven. And somewhere in my grandmother's house, in a language of lullabies, is the verse that ends the song — or begins it.*

She dreamed that night of her mother singing, and could not, waking, remember a single note.
