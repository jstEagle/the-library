## 20. What Her Mother Sang

The archive smelled of dust and tallow and something older beneath both — a sweetness, faint and wrong, like flowers left too long in a closed room. Ines had noticed it the moment the archivist unlocked the door for her, had watched his nostrils flare as he stepped inside, as if he could taste it too. He was a narrow man named Pellin with ink-stained cuffs and the careful manner of someone who had learned that curiosity in this palace was safer when rationed.

"You'll want the choir records," he said. "Third gallery, east wall. The ledgers are chronological. Mind the damp on the lower shelves."

"Thank you," Ines said.

He hesitated at the threshold, one hand still on the door. "The steward said you were looking for a name."

"I'm looking for several."

He nodded slowly, the way men did when they understood less than they pretended. "Names in this building have a way of finding their owners," he said, and pulled the door shut behind him, leaving her alone with forty years of breathing paper.

* * *

She had come to the archive because of Odile's ledger.

Three nights now she had sat with it after the choir slept, tracing Odile's cramped transcription hand across verses that mapped a country erased from every map and treaty. Verse by verse, location by location, the hymn drew Vhalen in ink — the river bend, the millstone ford, the border stones themselves standing in the notation like rests, like held breaths. And threaded through it all, in a different hand, older and more certain: annotations Odile hadn't made. Someone had been correcting the ledger. Someone who knew the hymn not as scholarship but as inheritance.

Ines had recognized the handwriting before she let herself admit it.

Her mother's letters home had been rare, brief, practical — *the flour is good here, tell Papa the cough is only weather* — written in a slanted, impatient script that leaned forward like a woman walking into wind. The annotations in Odile's ledger leaned the same way. She had told herself it was coincidence. She had told herself many things since crossing the border stones, and each one had cost her something to believe.

So: the archive. Third gallery, east wall. Ledgers in chronological order.

Forty years ago was a long shelf away.

* * *

The choir rosters were bound in blue leather gone gray at the corners, spines cracked like old knuckles. Ines carried them two at a time to the reading table beneath the high slit of a window, where the afternoon light fell in a single dusty blade across the pages. She worked backward from the present year, past the reign of Sabine's father-in-law, past wars recorded only as gaps in the roster where tenors had been conscripted and never returned.

The names were ordinary. Aldric. Maren. Two brothers named Fenn who sang bass and fought constantly, judging by the marginal notes in some long-dead choirmaster's hand. Ordinary names belonging to ordinary voices, gathered season after season under a roof that hummed when they sang the old hymn. Ines turned the pages with the same methodical care she brought to tasting — noting everything, assuming nothing, letting the evidence accumulate until it spoke.

It was how she had built her immunity, dish by dish, grain by grain. It was how she would do this.

The roster for the year of the Thirteenth Turn — the last one, four hundred years ago being legend, but forty years ago there had been an eleventh turn, a near-miss that the histories barely mentioned and the archive could not stop mentioning — that roster was fatter than the others. More singers. More annotations. More of that leaning-forward handwriting in the margins.

Ines smoothed the page flat with both palms, the way she'd smooth linen before cutting poison doses into it, and read down the alto line.

She found her mother's name between the second verse mark and the third.

*MARGUERITE ALMEIDA.*

Not Vhalen. Not her grandmother's surname, the one Ines wore like borrowed clothing. Almeida — her dead mother's surname, the one the impossible courier's summons had used, the one that had burned and then reappeared unburned on her grandmother's table. The name followed her. It had followed her here, printed in faded iron-gall ink by a clerk forty years dead, beside a voice part and a date of induction and a small annotation in the margin:

*sent by the valley*

Ines sat very still.

The window's blade of light crept across the page. Somewhere below, muffled by floors of stone, the royal choir was rehearsing — she could feel it more than hear it, a vibration in the soles of her feet, in the armrests of her chair, in the water glass trembling faintly beside her elbow. They were working through the early verses. The walls were humming along.

*Sent by the valley.*

Her whole life, the story had been simple the way valley stories were simple: her mother had left. Had crossed the stones and never come back, and the valley did not speak of those who left, and her grandmother's silence on the subject had been a wall Ines had spent twenty years learning not to press against. *She went out,* the story went. *She didn't come back.* Four words containing a whole geography of grief.

But sent was not went. Sent implied a sender. A purpose. A door closing from the inside, or opening —

Ines turned the page with fingers that had gone cold despite the stuffy warmth of the gallery.

The annotations thickened around her mother's name. Marguerite Almeida, alto, appears in every rehearsal record for three years. Marguerite Almeida, soloist, the Feast of the Turning. Marguerite Almeida — and here the choirmaster's hand gave way entirely to that leaning-forward script — *has the eleventh verse entire. Says her line was given to her as a lullaby. Says her mother sang it to her, and hers before.*

Ines read it twice. Three times.

Her grandmother's lullaby. The wordless melody, the one with no lyrics anyone in Vhalen would explain, the one Ines had hummed to herself in the dark of the taster's quarters on nights when the palace felt like a held breath. Her grandmother sang it still, on the porch, facing the border stones. Ines had thought it was a comfort. It was a map. It was a *verse*, and her family had carried it the way other families carried heirlooms — except heirlooms didn't hum back.

And her mother had stood in this city, in this choir, forty years ago, carrying the alto line the way Ines now carried it without knowing what she carried — and the roster said nothing more about why. Only the next entry, dated a year later, in the clerk's neutral hand:

*Almeida, M. — departed. No successor noted for alto line.*

Departed. As if she'd resigned a post. As if she'd walked out of the palace and back over the border stones and into the valley and the silence, and no one had asked why, or everyone had known and no one had written it down.

Ines pressed her thumb flat against the entry until the paper dimpled. Departed. Her mother hadn't fled Vhalen. There had been nothing to flee *from* — the valley was the place people fled *to*. Her mother had gone out into the world of magic and answered prayers and gods with teeth, sung in this humming gallery, held the missing verse in her mouth like a stone under her tongue — and then she had gone home.

Or been recalled.

Or failed.

The thought arrived without permission: *the last time the Door was nearly opened.* Odile had said it once, offhand, bitter. Forty years ago. An eleventh turn. A near-miss that the histories barely mentioned. And in that same year, a girl from a valley erased from every map had been sent — *sent* — into the royal choir, carrying the one verse that completed the Door's location, and when the crisis passed, she had departed, and the alto line had gone silent, and the hymn had remained forever incomplete, and the Door had remained forever shut.

Her mother hadn't run from anything. Her mother had been the lock, walking.

Just as Ines was now.

* * *

She did not remember leaving the reading table. She found herself standing at the east wall, pulling the oldest volumes, the ones from four hundred years ago, because if forty years ago had a Marguerite Almeida, perhaps four hundred years ago had another — the first priestess, the one whose blood ran thin and stubborn in Ines's veins. But the ancient rosters were charred at the edges, water-eaten, and the names had dissolved into brown blooms of ink. Only one fragment survived legible, a corner of a page preserved inside a wax sleeve:

*...the alto shall be kept within the valley, for the lock must sleep behind the stones, and woe to the generation that—*

The rest was eaten.

*Woe to the generation that.* That what? That forgot? That sang? That produced a daughter foolish enough to answer a royal summons? Ines slid the fragment back into its sleeve with hands that were not quite steady. Outside the slit of window, the light had gone amber, then gray. Below, the rehearsal had ended; the walls' hum faded the way a bell fades, reluctantly, holding its note to the last.

She thought about her grandmother burning the summons, and the seal reappearing unburned. She thought about the grave her grandmother had not yet explained. She thought about the letter delivered by no one: *The Door opens from the inside too.* She had read it as warning. Now, sitting in the dust of the archive with her mother's name drying on the air before her, she read it differently.

From the inside. From the valley. From the lock itself.

Her mother had been sent to close the Door. Which meant the Door had been open — or nearly — which meant someone had tried to open it, and her mother had gone out to stop them, and she had succeeded, and she had come home, and she had died in the valley anyway, young, and been buried in an unmarked grave while her daughter grew up eating measured grains of poison and learning to survive everything except the truth.

*You are not the key,* Ines thought, testing the shape of it. *You are the lock.* No — that was chapter twenty-seven's revelation, not yet hers. What she had, here, now, was smaller and sharper: her mother had not abandoned the valley. Her mother had been the valley's hand, reaching out into the world. And the valley had reached again — through a courier who should not have existed, through a summons bearing a dead woman's surname, through a taster's post that was really a sacrifice post — and the hand it had reached with was Ines's own.

She laughed, once, quietly, and the sound embarrassed her in the empty gallery. All those years of wondering what her mother had wanted, out there beyond the stones. All those years of the valley's silence, which she had read as shame. It wasn't shame. It was *duty*. The whole valley — the unanswered prayers, the border stones, the lullaby with no words — it was all one instrument, tuned and waiting, and her family were the strings.

The archivist's knock came soft at the door. "Mistress Almeida. The gallery closes at dusk."

"Of course." She gathered the ledgers, reshelved them in their proper order, and paused with her mother's roster half-slotted into place. Then, deliberately, she tore the corner of the page — just the corner, just the name and the annotation, *sent by the valley* — and folded it small and put it inside her bodice, against her heart, where the lullaby lived.

Pellin would notice. Pellin might even report it. Let him. Some documents were meant to be carried out of archives, the way some daughters were meant to be carried out of valleys.

At the door she stopped. "Master Pellin. Forty years ago — the year of the eleventh turn. Was there trouble? With the chapel?"

The archivist's face did something complicated. "There was a fire," he said. "In the chapel loft. The records say a lamp was knocked over during rehearsal." He lowered his voice, though they were alone. "The records also say the choir sang the hymn complete that night. All twelve verses. And the walls of the palace rang for a day and a night afterward, and the queen of that time ordered the twelfth verse struck from every copy and the alto dismissed."

Struck. Dismissed. *Departed.*

"And the singer?" Ines asked. "The alto. What happened to her?"

Pellin looked at her for a long moment, and Ines saw him decide — the way cooks decided whether to tell the taster about the tremor in their hands — that some knowledge was worth the risk.

"She walked out of the ashes," he said, "and took the north road, and the gate guards swore she never passed them going out. But the valley folk will tell you—" He stopped himself. Shook his head. "Forgive me. Old men gossip."

"The valley folk will tell me what?"

"That she went home," Pellin said. "That she went home singing."

* * *

Dusk had settled over the palace gardens when Ines finally emerged, the folded page warm against her skin. Lamps were being lit along the colonnade, and somewhere above, in the queen's tower, a candle burned that never went out — Sabine praying, always praying, day and night now, over the child that breathed only while she prayed.

Ines stood in the garden dark and let the truth rearrange her.

Her mother had not fled. Her mother had been *sent* — chosen, armed with a lullaby, dispatched into the world's throat to close a Door, and she had done it, and she had paid for it, and she had gone home to die with the verse still safe inside her, passed it to her daughter in the cradle before words meant anything, hid it inside a melody sweet enough that no god would think to look for a map in it.

And now the moon was climbing toward its thirteenth turn, and the gods were feeding, and the Door was stirring, and the alto line had walked back into the palace wearing her mother's surname, summoned by a seal that would not burn.

Ines put her hand flat over her sternum, over the folded page, over the heartbeat that stuttered sometimes now, the tax her immunity had levied on her body.

"Mama," she said softly, to the garden, to the dark, to whatever listened in this place where prayers got answered with teeth. "You closed it once."

Above her, unseen, the moon was rising — one turn short of ruin, fat and pale and patient.

"I need you to teach me how."

Behind her, at the edge of the lamplight, something in the shadows of the colonnade shifted its weight — and began, very quietly, to hum the alto line back to her.
