## 21. The Queen's Child

The child came at moonset, in the hour when even the candles seem to doubt themselves.

Sabine had been awake for nine days by then. She had stopped counting after the seventh, because counting made the hours heavier, and she needed to be light — light enough to be lifted, light enough to be heard. The midwives had been dismissed from the birthing chamber three days prior, one by one, each with a purse of gold and a warning delivered so gently it sounded like affection: *You attended a difficult labor. The child did not survive. Grieve quietly.* The last midwife, old Marta with the burn-scarred wrists, had looked at Sabine for a long moment before she bowed, and in that look Sabine had read everything Marta understood and would never say aloud. She let her go anyway. There was no other way to keep her.

So when the moment came, there was no one in the room but Sabine, the god's presence pressing against the walls like water against a dam, and the thing they had promised her, arriving exactly as agreed.

It did not come through her body. That bargain had been struck differently, and she had signed it with her own blood on the chapel floor, and she did not regret it, she told herself, she did not, because a queen who regrets her statecraft is a queen who has already lost. It came the way the answered prayers always came: out of nothing, out of the seam between one breath and the next. One instant the birthing bed was empty, its linens laid and waiting for forty years, and the next the linens were not empty.

He was small. Smaller than she had imagined across all those nights of imagining. His skin was the color of candlewax, his hair dark and damp as riverweed, and he lay so still in the nest of white cloth that for one terrible, endless heartbeat Sabine thought the gods had cheated her — thought this was another gift with teeth, another answered prayer designed to teach her the price of asking.

Then she saw his chest. It moved. Once. A flutter, shallow as a moth trapped under glass, and then nothing.

"No," Sabine said, and her voice cracked down the middle like old ice. "No, you *promised*—"

The presence in the walls did not answer. It never answered in words anymore. Words were for the early days, the courtship days, when the prayers had come back sweetened and strange and the whole court had whispered *the gods are listening again* as though it were a blessing and not an appetite. Now the gods communicated the way weather communicated, the way plague communicated: by consequence.

She gathered him up. He weighed almost nothing — less than a loaf, less than a cat, less than the crown she set aside each night. His skin was cool. Not cold, precisely. Cool the way marble is cool, the way the crypt beneath the chapel is cool, and some part of Sabine that still remembered being a girl who feared the dark registered that comparison and flinched away from it.

*Pray*, said the silence. Not in words. In pressure. In the way the candle flames leaned toward the bed though there was no draft.

And Sabine — Queen Sabine, fourth of her name, who had knelt for ambassadors and knelt for her father's coffin and never once knelt for anything that could not promote her — knelt beside the birthing bed with her son in her arms and prayed.

She did not know what she said. Later she would try to reconstruct it and fail; it was not language, or if it was, it was a language older than hers, assembled from the raw materials of terror and want. What she knew was this: the child breathed. On the first words, his chest rose, and held, and fell, and rose again, and a color crept back into his lips that was not the gray of wax but the pink of a living mouth, and Sabine felt something open in her chest that she had believed had closed forever at her father's funeral, at her wedding, at every ceremony where the priests spoke of mothers and she sat among the barren and the bereaved with her hands folded like a woman attending someone else's feast.

He breathed *because she prayed*. She understood that immediately, the way one understands fire is hot — not as knowledge but as fact, immediate and total. And when, some unknowable time later, her voice gave out and she stopped — just for a moment, just to swallow, just to weep — his breathing stopped too.

The silence that followed was the loudest thing she had ever heard.

She prayed again. He breathed again.

That was the shape of it. That was the whole architecture of the miracle, laid bare within the first hour, plain as a trap with the door standing open: the child lived on her voice the way a lamp lives on oil. The gods had kept their promise to the letter. They had given her a living child. They had simply neglected to mention that *living* would be a verb she performed rather than a state he possessed.

She should have refused. She knew that even then, in that first dawn, holding him while the candles burned down to their sockets and her throat went raw. Some cold clerk of a mind deep inside her drew up the ledger and entered the transaction honestly: *a child purchased at the price of your voice, your sleep, your freedom, forever.* The clerk noted that the previous taster had died of what the poison let in, and wondered idly what might get in through a mother's opened throat.

But he had her chin. Her father's brow. When he slept — truly slept, in the shallow borrowed way he slept — his fist curled against her collarbone, and his fingers were long, and she was already inventoring which of them would hold a sword and which a pen, and the cold clerk could write whatever it liked. Sabine had spent her whole life making bargains colder than this one. This was merely the first bargain she had ever wanted to keep.

* * *

They named him Aurelian, for the dawn, because Sabine had a sense of theater and understood that a kingdom fed on symbols the way the gods fed on — she did not finish the thought, even privately. The christening was held in the royal chapel, the old chapel, the one where the petitions had begun to be answered, because the cathedral priests had started refusing to officiate over anything since the kitchen boy died, and Sabine found she preferred it that way. Fewer witnesses. Fewer questions.

The court filed in and saw their queen radiant, restored, transformed — the barren winter finally broken, spring at last, God be praised, whichever god one chose now to praise, and wasn't it marvelous how many choices there were again. They saw the infant in his christening gown, asleep in the cradle of state. They did not see what happened every time the ceremony required the queen to be silent.

Tomas saw. Tomas, her steward, her shadow, her most useful man, stood at his post by the door with his hands clasped behind him, and Sabine watched him watch her, and watched him understand. When the bishop droned through the blessings and Sabine stood mute beside the font — because she had learned, in those first weeks, that she could not stop praying even for the length of a blessing, that her son's breath ran on her voice like a millwheel runs on water — Tomas's face did something small and terrible. It aged. Ten years, twenty, all in the space around his eyes.

She caught his arm afterward, in the antechamber, while the court toasted the prince in the hall beyond.

"You will say nothing," she said.

"Your Majesty." He bowed. His voice was steady but his hands were not. "May I ask how long you have slept."

"I don't," she said. "I pray instead. It turns out they're very similar. Both are just talking to the dark and hoping something answers."

"Your Majesty—"

"Tomas." She softened her voice, because she needed him, and because cruelty was a tool for people with surplus strength, and she had none. "I know what you are going to say. I have said it to myself in every register I possess. But look at him." Through the doorway, across the hall, the cradle of state was visible, surrounded by courtiers, and in it her son lay breathing — breathing, because she had stationed two relays of ladies whose duty was to keep her supplied with honeyed wine and whispered prompts whenever ceremony forced her lips closed, and because she had not left his side in six weeks except at a distance of thirty feet, the farthest she had tested, and at thirty feet her voice thinned and his breath thinned with it, and she had never tested forty. "Look at him and tell me what you would have done differently."

Tomas looked. And Sabine watched him try, genuinely try, to find the answer, and watched him fail, and understood that she had just bound him to her more tightly than any oath could. Guilt was better than gold. She had learned that from her father.

"The taster," Tomas said at last, quietly. "Ines Almeida. She has been asking questions about the chapel. About the gifts. Odile has been showing her things — a ledger, I think. Old verses."

Something moved through Sabine then, cold and quick, a fish turning in dark water. "What kind of verses?"

"Hymn verses, Majesty. Very old ones. The choirmaster claims they're only music." He hesitated, and Sabine knew Tomas well enough to know that his hesitations were load-bearing. "There is talk among the older servants. That the hymn is a map. That it names a country that isn't on any map anymore. That the girl's mother sang in the choir once, before Vhalen was — before."

Before. The word the whole court used now, the polite word, the word that papered over four hundred years of a valley erased from every treaty. Sabine had signed the papers herself, three years ago, renewing the old prohibitions without reading them closely, because Vhalen was nothing, Vhalen was a rounding error, Vhalen was a superstition her great-grandmothers had used to frighten tax collectors.

But the gods had come from somewhere. And the Door — the word surfaced in her memory unbidden, from some late-night petition, some whisper traded between herself and the presence in the chapel walls — the Door was spoken of the way misers speak of vaults.

"Find out what the girl knows," Sabine said. "Gently. She is valuable. Everything is valuable now, do you understand? Everything that touches the gods is either a key or a lock, and I intend to know which is which before anyone else does."

"And if she is a lock?"

Sabine looked across the hall at her son. At the rise and fall of his impossibly small chest. At the nurse leaning over him, murmuring nonsense syllables, believing herself to be comforting him, having no idea that her lullaby was decoration, that the true engine of the child's life was kneeling in the antechamber with a bruised throat and a heart full of arithmetic.

"Then we find the key," Sabine said. "We always find the key. That is what crowns are for."

* * *

The nights were the worst, at first. Then the days became the worst, and the nights grew almost gentle by comparison, because at night she could pretend that the silence between her prayers was rest, when in truth it was nothing of the kind.

She had tried everything. Relays of readers, chanting psalms on her behalf while she slept propped upright in a chair beside the cradle — the child's breath had gone ragged within minutes, as though he could tell the difference between devotion and recitation, as though the gods themselves were particular about whose mouth the words came from. She had tried singing. She had tried silence punctuated by written prayer held against his skin. Nothing worked but her own unbroken voice, addressed upward and outward, into the seams of the world.

So she prayed without pause. She prayed through council meetings, her ministers learning to conduct business around the low continuous murmur of their queen, the way sailors learn to work around weather. She prayed through audiences, through suppers, through the long gray afternoons when the rain came off the mountains and the palace smelled of stone and smoke. Her voice developed a texture the court physicians had no name for — not hoarse, exactly; worn, the way a stair is worn, hollowed in the middle by use. Chambermaids began leaving cups of honeyed water outside her door without being asked. The whole palace had learned to move quietly around her, reverent and frightened in equal measure, and Sabine told herself this was awe.

Some nights, in the smallest hours, she conducted audits. She sat by the cradle with her son breathing against her breastbone and her voice running like a thread through the dark, and she took inventory of what she had become.

*I am a queen,* she told herself. *Queens make trades. I traded my voice for an heir; my grandfather traded three border provinces for a peace that lasted eleven years; my mother traded her health for my existence and died of it smiling. I got the better price.*

*I am a mother,* she told herself. *Mothers give. That is the entire office. My body would have been given to him in blood and milk if the ordinary way had been open to me. Instead I give him breath. It is only a different currency.*

*They promised me a living child,* she told herself, *and I have a living child, and a promise kept is a promise kept, and whatever the ledger says, the ledger is not the child.*

The child. Who opened his eyes sometimes — dark eyes, watchful, too still for an infant — and looked at her while she prayed, with an attention that no newborn should possess, and Sabine would feel, for a fraction of a second, that she was not looking at her son but through him, that something behind his face was checking its work. Then he would blink, and fuss, and root against her like any hungry baby, and she would weep with relief and pray harder, and hate herself for the relief, and pray harder still.

Once — only once — she stopped entirely. Deliberately. For ten seconds, maybe twelve, she sealed her lips and let the silence stand, because the cold clerk in her demanded data, demanded to know the terms of the contract down to its finest print.

Her son's breath failed on the third second. By the eighth his lips had gone gray. On the eleventh, the pressure in the walls changed — the presence gathering itself, attentive, interested, the way wolves attend — and Sabine heard, or dreamed she heard, or told herself afterward she had dreamed it, a single syllable of approval. As though her stopping had been a test. As though she had passed.

She prayed then as she had never prayed, wildly, gorgeously, with tears streaming down her face and her voice breaking and mending and breaking, until the color flooded back into him and he cried — actually cried, angry and alive — and she gathered him in and rocked him and did not let the clerk finish its entry.

*Love is captivity,* she thought, rocking him in the ruined dark. *Everyone knows that. Every mother since the first mother has known that. The only question is who holds the chain.*

She had assumed, all her life, that the answer was: the beloved. That love's captivity was freely chosen, mutual, a knot tied from both ends. She had built a queenship on that assumption, had married a man she did not love on the theory that chains were chains and one might as well be gilded.

Now she held her son and prayed without pause, day and night, season upon season, and the thread of her voice ran from her throat to his lungs to whatever waited beyond the walls listening, and she understood that the knot had been tied from only one end. Hers. And that the other end of the thread went up, and out, through the chapel roof, past the moon that was turning and turning toward some number the astronomers dreaded, into the dark where the old gods had waited four hundred years for a queen foolish enough to ask them for something.

She prayed on. What else was there? The alternative was unthinkable, and so she did not think it, and the not-thinking became a discipline, and the discipline became a faith, and the faith looked, from the outside, exactly like devotion.

On the morning the astronomer came to court — pale, apologetic, clutching his charts, speaking of a thirteenth turn that no sane sky should permit — Sabine was in the chapel, praying over her sleeping son, and she heard the news through the doors and felt the presence in the walls go taut with something that could only be called hunger, and she prayed louder, and did not stop, and did not stop, and did not stop.

But that night, alone above the cradle, she caught herself humming — between prayers, in the gaps, a small tuneless thread of melody — and realized with a chill that spread slowly from her spine outward that the tune was not one she knew. It was older than anything she knew. It was a hymn fragment, half-remembered, in a mode the cathedral had banned for centuries, and it had come into her mouth the way the child had come into the world: out of the seam, unasked.

The gods were teaching her songs now.

She pressed her lips together and prayed instead, and did not sleep, and in the morning sent for the steward, and asked him — in a voice worn smooth as river stone — to bring her the taster.
