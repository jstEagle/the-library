## 22. Forty Voices

The choir loft smelled of dust and candle tallow and, beneath both, of something older — a sweetness like hymnals left open too long in the sun, paper going to sugar. Ines climbed the narrow stair with Odile's ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, and at the top she paused, listening.

Forty voices had sung here once. Now the loft held eleven, and they were not singing. They were waiting for her, arranged in their usual crescent on the risers as though this were any rehearsal, as though the choirmaster had not been taken from them nine days ago in irons, as though the chapel wall had not opened like a mouth and breathed.

"You came," said Perrin, the youngest tenor, and his voice cracked on the second word. He was seventeen and still had the habit of looking toward the door whenever anyone mentioned the gods, as if one might be standing there taking attendance.

"I came," Ines said. She set the ledger on Odile's lectern — Odile's lectern, though the wood was cold now, unclaimed — and unwrapped it slowly, the way her grandmother had taught her to unwrap anything that might be alive. "And I need you to listen before you decide whether to stay."

* * *

She had spent three days finding them. That was the part no one would ever write down, she thought — that hope was mostly errands. Hope was knocking on the servants' stair door where Maren the contralto slept and whispering through the crack, *I need you tonight, bring nothing, tell no one.* Hope was standing in the laundry yard while steam rose around her, explaining to a washerwoman with a voice like low brass that yes, she meant it literally, the hymn was a map, and watching the woman's hands go still in the wet linen because her grandmother had sung her a verse once, only once, when she was small, and had made her swear never to forget it, and she hadn't, she hadn't, she could sing it now if Ines wanted —

They all could. That was the miracle of it, or the design of it, depending on how you looked at a thing four hundred years old. Each singer of the royal choir carried one verse of the oldest hymn in the kingdom, handed down in secret like a coin sewn into a hem. No family held more than one. No singer had ever been told what the whole cloth looked like. They had thought it was tradition. They had thought it was superstition, a game the old families played, a way of marking their children as choir-born.

Odile had known better. Thirty years at that lectern, transcribing verse after verse as each new voice joined the loft, asking quiet questions at weddings and funerals until the pattern emerged: forty verses, forty locations, a country drawn in music. A country that no longer existed on any map because it had been erased from every map — except this one, sung into the walls every feast day by people who did not know they were reading aloud.

And one verse missing. The alto line. Her mother's line.

Ines opened the ledger to the page where Odile's handwriting stopped mid-word, the ink dragged into a blot, as if the pen had been pulled from her hand. Around the gap, thirty-nine entries stood in careful columns: verse numbers, first lines, the names of the families who carried them, and beside each, in Odile's smaller hand, a place-name rendered phonetically — *Vhalen-est-march, the stone that drinks, the ford of white willows* — geography from a country that had been unwritten.

"Odile believed the hymn is a map," Ines said. "You've heard what happened in the chapel. You know I'm not asking you to believe it. I'm asking you to remember it."

Maren raised her hand like a schoolgirl, then seemed embarrassed and lowered it halfway. "My gran always said our verse was about a river. I thought — I don't know what I thought. That it was just an odd song." Her jaw tightened. "They took Odile in front of everyone. They'll take us too."

"They might," Ines said. "Yes."

It was not the reassurance they wanted, but she had learned in the valley that lying to frightened people only bought you time you'd have to pay back with interest. She let the silence sit. Candle flames leaned in the draft from the stairwell.

"The moon has twelve turns behind it," she went on. "One turn remains. Whatever the thirteenth turn means — and the court astronomer has been praying about it loudly enough that we all know what he fears — whatever happens when it completes, I would rather be standing on the map than hiding under it. Odile gave thirty years to this. Tomas gave—" She stopped. Tomas was gone; the gods had him; she did not know in what condition, or whether *condition* was even a word that applied anymore. "Tomas gave everything. I'm asking for one night."

Perrin spoke without raising his hand. "If we sing it — the whole thing — what happens?"

Ines thought of the chapel wall, the door-shaped absence that had opened for a single breath, the smell of cold air from a country that wasn't there. She thought of dirt under her fingernails when she woke. She thought of her grandmother's letter, hidden now inside her boot where she read it each night until the ink was wearing soft: *The Door opens from the inside too.*

"I don't know," she said. "But I think the Door knows where it is, and right now we're the only ones who do."

* * *

They rehearsed in whispers first, which was its own strange liturgy. One at a time, each singer came forward to the lectern and sang their verse alone, barely voiced, breath more than tone, and Ines followed along in the ledger, checking Odile's transcription against living memory.

Most matched. Two had drifted — a word changed here, a note flattened there, the way a path shifts over centuries of feet — and Ines marked the variants in pencil, unwilling to erase either version. A third singer, old Halvard, bass, who had been in the loft longer than anyone and whose knees cracked when he climbed the risers, sang his verse and then stood trembling.

"That's the first time I've heard it out loud since my father died," he said. "He made me learn it the winter I turned six. He said, *you won't understand it, you just have to keep it.* Like keeping a coal in ash." He looked at his own hands. "Forty years I kept a coal and didn't know there was a fire."

"There's a fire," Ines said quietly. "That's rather the problem."

But it was when they began to stitch the verses together — two voices, then three, then seven, threading the fragments into sequence — that the loft itself began to answer.

It started as a hum in the floorboards, so faint Ines thought it was her own blood. Then the window glass shivered with a tone below hearing, and the candle flames stretched tall and still, and the dust in the air began, very slowly, to drift in a slow spiral above the center of the room, turning like something asleep adjusting its position.

Everyone stopped singing at once. The silence rang.

"It did that in rehearsal too," whispered Perrin. "Before. When Odile was here. We thought it was the wind."

"It isn't the wind," Ines said. Her heart was hammering, but underneath the fear was the other thing, the thing she had felt crossing the border stones, magic brushing her skin like cold water — recognition. The hymn was not a description of the Door. It was addressed to it. Every rehearsal for four hundred years had been a knock, and the Door had been listening the whole time, patient as masonry.

"We're going to need all forty," she said. "Not for volume. For accuracy. Every verse is a landmark, and if one is missing, the map doesn't fail gracefully. It fails completely."

"And the missing one?" Maren asked, though her eyes said she already knew. They all knew. The whole court knew the shape of Ines's history by now; they looked at her like a relic, like a key someone had lost and found in a drawer.

"The alto line," Ines said. "My mother's line. My grandmother taught me a lullaby when I was small. I've sung it my whole life without knowing what it was." She wrapped her arms around herself, suddenly cold. "I can't sing it here. Not yet. There's a reason it was kept apart from the others, kept in the priestess line instead of the choir, and I think the reason is that the alto verse isn't a location on the map."

Halvard frowned. "Then what is it?"

"I think it's the edge of the page."

No one laughed. Outside, far off across the palace roofs, a bell began to toll the hour, and the sound came into the loft strangely bent, arriving a half-beat late, as if the night between here and the bell tower had thickened.

* * *

They set the night for the dark of the moon's last quarter — three nights hence — and dispersed by twos and threes down different stairs, out different doors, into the palace's many mouths. Ines watched them go from the loft window: Maren walking with a kitchen maid for cover, Halvard leaning on Perrin's arm like a grandfather being seen home, the contralto from the laundry yards carrying her basket of wet linen as though nothing in the world had changed. Fragile. All of it fragile. Forty threads and every one of them cuttable.

She was alone in the loft for perhaps a minute. Long enough to close the ledger. Long enough to notice that the spiral of dust had settled into a pattern on the floor — not random, not quite. A ring. And within the ring, a smaller shape, roughly the size of a person standing.

Long enough, also, for the temperature to fall.

"Ines Almeida."

Her mother's voice. It came from everywhere in the loft and nowhere, from the rafters and the risers and the space just behind her ear, warm and dead and perfectly remembered. She did not turn around. Turning around was what it wanted; she had learned that much from the last bargain, learned it at the cost of a memory she still caught herself reaching for — her grandmother's face on a particular morning, gone now, a hole in the cloth of her childhood with neat stitched edges.

"You have your singers," the voice said, gentle, admiring. "Thirty-nine little lamps. How industrious. Your mother gathered them too, once."

"She closed the Door," Ines said to the empty air. "Whatever you're selling, she already refused it."

A pause. When the voice came again it had moved closer without moving at all, and there was something new threaded through the warmth — hunger, thin and high, like a wire under tension. Twelve turns of the moon behind it. Four hundred years of unanswered prayer pressing against a border.

"Three nights," it said. "Bring the lullaby. Or don't, and watch what the thirteenth turn brings to a city full of people who have just remembered how to ask us for things." A softness then, terrible in its tenderness: "We were so patient with your mother, little lock. Be patient with yourself."

The cold lifted. The candles stood up straight. On the floor, the ring of dust remained, and Ines stood a long time looking at the person-shaped space inside it before she understood that it wasn't a warning.

It was a place setting.
