## 23. The Price of the Alto

The dream came again on the third night after Odile's arrest, and this time it did not pretend to be a dream.

Ines stood in the palace chapel at an hour that had no name — the candles burned without flickering, which was the first wrongness, because candleflame always trembles when a room is breathing. The walls were the same pale stone she had come to know too well, the same hairline cracks where the mortar had given up its arguments with gravity. But the absence was still there. The door-shaped nothing in the eastern wall where she had sung Verse Eleven, the gap that had opened for one breath and closed like a mouth.

It was open now. Not wide. A seam. A thread of darkness pulled taut between two stones, thin as the space between a held note and its release.

"You came back," said her mother's voice.

Ines did not turn around. She had learned that much in the weeks since the first visitation — that turning was a kind of agreement, that facing the voice gave it a face, and faces were how gods got their hooks into you. She kept her eyes on the seam of dark and said, "This is not my mother."

"No." The voice admitted it with a gentleness that was worse than denial would have been. It came from everywhere the candlelight wasn't. "But we wore her once. Briefly. When she stood where you are standing, forty years ago, we listened to her sing. Do you know what it is like for us to listen, Ines? Four hundred years behind stone, and the only music that reaches us is prayer — and prayer all sounds the same. Begging. Always begging. But your mother sang a hymn. A map. And for three minutes we remembered what it was to be addressed instead of asked."

The candles guttered, all together, though there was no draft.

"What do you want?" Ines asked. Her voice sounded small in the chapel, and she hated it, and she made herself say the next part louder. "You've already taken from me. A memory. My grandmother's kitchen — the smell of bread, the sound of her humming. I woke up and it was gone, like a page torn out of a book with the binding still intact. So don't dress this up as conversation. What do you want?"

Silence. Then, softly, with something that might have been respect:

"The alto verse."

* * *

She had gone looking for it, of course. That was the shame she carried now, the thing she turned over at night like a stone with something damp and pale beneath it. After Odile was taken — two guards at either elbow, the choirmaster walking between them with her spine straight as a psalm staff, thirty years of transcription confiscated in a single canvas satchel — Ines had gone back to her narrow room above the taster's quarters and done what she always did when the world took something from her. She had inventoried what remained.

Odile's ledger was gone, seized with everything else. But the ledger had never been the whole of it. Odile herself had said so, that last night in the choir loft, her ink-stained fingers pressed flat against the hymnal as if holding down a bird: *Every singer carries one verse in family memory. That was the design. No single throat could open it alone.* Forty voices, forty verses, scattered through forty bloodlines like seeds sewn by a farmer who feared the harvest.

And one verse — the alto line, the keel of the whole hymn, the line that held the others above water — belonged to no choir roster. It belonged to a lullaby. It belonged to a valley. It belonged, if the shape of her childhood meant anything, to her.

Ines had lain awake reconstructing it. Not singing — she was afraid to sing it, afraid of what the walls would do, afraid of the seam in the chapel wall hearing her through six floors of stone. Just remembering. The melody her grandmother had hummed over the washbasin. The melody her mother had sung, according to the one story her grandmother told twice and then never told again, on the night before she left Vhalen and never returned. The words were in the old dialect, half nonsense to a child, syllables that slid off the tongue like river stones: *velen orath, velen su*, over and over, a circle of words with no beginning she could find.

*A valley locked. A valley kept.*

She had thought, lying there, that she understood it. The Door needed the whole hymn; the hymn needed the alto; the alto lived in her. Therefore she was the key, and keys are simple things — you put them in locks and turn them and doors open and you walk through. She had even imagined the moment: the choir gathered, the moon full, her own voice rising over the others, and Vhalen made real again, mapped again, prayed-in again. She had imagined her grandmother's face.

She had not imagined that the gods were listening to her think.

* * *

"They will give it to you," the voice said now, in the candleless chapel, and Ines felt the words land in her chest like cold coins. "The alto verse. Whole. Every syllable your grandmother has hoarded for sixty years, every note your mother carried out of the valley and died rather than sing. We have it. We have had it since before your grandmother was born — we taught it to the first priestess ourselves, did you think the lock designed itself? We can pour it into you in a breath. You could stand before the choir tomorrow and be complete."

"And in exchange."

"In exchange," her mother's voice said, warm as a kitchen, terrible as a kitchen fire, "you give us the other thing you carry. The immunity. The long work of your life — every poison sipped, every toxin courted, every small death survived until your blood forgot how to die. Give us that. Let us drink it. And you will have your verse, and your choir, and your Door, and your valley made real. Everyone gets what they want, Ines. Isn't that what bargains are?"

The candles went out. All of them. And in the dark, Ines finally understood — not the offer, but the architecture underneath it, the way she understood poison: not by its taste but by what it did to the body after swallowing.

Her immunity. They wanted her immunity.

Not her blood — they had asked for her blood once, in an earlier visitation, and she had refused, and the refusal had cost her the memory of bread and humming. Blood was a sip. Immunity was the meal. And why would beings who had spent four hundred years starving behind a border of silence want a taster's tolerance for arsenic and nightshade?

Because it wasn't tolerance for arsenic and nightshade. Not anymore. Somewhere along the years of deliberate, methodical self-poisoning — the discipline her grandmother had taught her as survival, the craft that had made her valuable enough to summon across an impossible border — her body had become something else. Tomas had told her what happened to the last taster: not death by poison but death by what the poison let in. A body trained to hold what should kill it. A vessel with no bottom.

And she was standing inside a valley-shaped prison where gods paced behind border stones like wolves, and the only way out of any prison is a door, and the Door was a hymn, and the hymn was sung through a body —

"Oh," Ines said aloud, to the dark. "You don't want the Door open."

The silence had a texture to it now. Attentive. Patient. The patience of something that had waited four hundred years and could wait four hundred more, though it very much preferred not to.

"You want *me* open."

* * *

She walked back through the sleeping palace because she did not trust herself to run. Running was panic, and panic loosened the mind, and loose minds leaked. She climbed the six flights to her room with her hand flat against the wall, feeling the stone's faint hum — the palace had hummed ever since the prayers started being answered, a vibration below hearing, like a hive considering.

In her room she lit the stub of candle she kept for inventory — her nightly accounting of her own body, the taster's discipline applied inward. She sat at the desk and drew the list the way she drew all her lists, in columns, in her small careful hand.

*Numbness: both hands, to the second knuckle. Tongue: retains sweetness, bitterness, salt; has lost the taste of plain water entirely. Vision: gray at the edges, worse at dusk. Heart: stutters on the third beat of every seventh measure — she had counted, because counting was the only prayer Vhalen had ever permitted her. Sleep: shallow. Dreams: occupied.*

That was the price already paid. That was the debt already owed. And they wanted the rest of it — not the damage, she realized, staring at the candle flame until it doubled and trebled, but the *capacity*. The thing that remained after all the damage. The hollow, bottomless, unkillable capacity of her. They didn't want to take her health away. They wanted to take her *resistance* away, and then pour themselves into the space it left, and walk out of Vhalen wearing a girl who could survive anything, because anything was exactly what they intended to be.

The trap was beautiful. That was the worst of it. She could admire it the way she admired a well-made poison: elegant, economical, tailored to its victim. They hadn't threatened her. They hadn't taken Odile's ledger and come for the verses by force — though she suspected now that Odile's arrest had been arranged, that the heresy charge was a pressure applied to Ines herself, another finger on another scale. Instead they had offered her exactly what she most wanted — the missing verse, the completed hymn, the way home — priced in exactly the coin she could least afford, and dressed in her mother's voice so that the spending of it would feel like inheritance rather than loss.

And if she refused? The last refusal had cost her a memory. This refusal would cost more. They would simply wait. The thirteenth turn was coming; the moon was nearly round with it; the answered prayers were multiplying across the kingdom like mold on bread. Time was their ally and her enemy. Every day she delayed, the court grew more devout and more frightened, and frightened courts did arithmetic: one girl's life against a dynasty's salvation. Sabine would do that arithmetic soon. Sabine was probably doing it now.

Unless.

Ines put down the pen. She looked at the inventory list, at the neat columns of everything her immunity had cost her, and she made herself ask the question underneath the question, the one the god's offer had been built to prevent her from asking.

Why did they need her consent at all?

They were gods. They wore voices, entered dreams, killed a kitchen boy with a gift of answered prayer. If her body was the vessel they wanted, why bargain? Why offer? Why dress coercion as commerce?

Because they couldn't take it. Because the immunity — the capacity, the hollow place — was not a thing that could be seized. It had been *built*. Deliberately, drop by drop, choice by choice, over fifteen years of a girl deciding, every morning, to swallow what should kill her. It was made of consent. Thousands of small consents, layered like lacquer. It was the one substance in creation that could not be stolen, only *given* — and that was why they needed her willing, and that was why they needed her deceived, and that was why the offer sounded like a gift.

Her grandmother's voice rose in her memory, unbidden, in the tone she used for the valley's oldest rules: *Nothing that asks nicely is asking for something small.*

Ines blew out the candle.

In the dark she sat very still and did the thing she was best at in all the world. She tasted. Not wine, not stew, not the god-toxin that had rolled off her valley blood like rain off wax. She tasted the offer itself, held it on the tongue of her mind, and noted its components the way she noted the components of a poisoned dish: here the sweetener, there the bitter agent, here the slow corrosive working under a pleasant warmth.

Verse for immunity. Map for vessel. Freedom for possession.

And underneath it all, so faint she almost missed it, a flavor she recognized from the chapel, from the gifts that bit, from every answered prayer the old gods had sent into the world since they woke: hunger. Old, enormous, patient hunger, the hunger of things that had eaten worship for millennia and been starved of it for four hundred years, and had learned, in that starvation, that worship was not the finest food. The finest food was a soul that could contain them. The finest food was a bottomless cup.

They didn't want the Door open so they could walk through it.

They wanted the Door open so that *she* would walk through it first — out of Vhalen, into the wide magic-drunk world, carrying them, the only vessel that could hold what they were, the living lock turned inside out into a living key. Her grandmother had told her once, in the border-walking days: *the priestess line was sealed inside on purpose.* She was the lock. And a lock, if you corrupt it thoroughly enough, becomes a key.

The trap was not the trade. The trap was that every path forward ran through her, and they had made sure of it, and they could afford to wait while she discovered it, because discovery changed nothing. Refuse, and they waited for Sabine's soldiers. Accept, and they poured into her like water into a cup. There was no version of the coming moon in which she was not the door.

There was only the question of who walked through — and carrying what.

* * *

Near dawn, the voice came one last time, softer now, almost tender, and it did not use her mother. It used no one. It was just the dark between the heartbeat and the next one, speaking.

"You understand, then. Good. Understanding makes the choosing honest. Keep the verse, refuse the trade, rally your forty voices, sing your hymn. Open us a door, little lock. We will be patient about the rest. We have always been patient about the rest."

"Or," Ines said to the empty room, and her voice did not shake, because she had trained it not to, the way she had trained everything, "I find a third thing. Something you haven't priced. Something you haven't thought of, because you've been starving in the dark for four hundred years while I've been learning how tables work — how poison works, how appetite works, how to give people exactly what they think they want until it kills them."

The silence laughed. It was not an unkind sound. That was somehow the most frightening thing yet.

"Then hurry," it said. "The queen is praying again. And this time, little taster, she is praying about *you*."

Far below, in the chapel, a bell began to ring for matins, and Ines Almeida sat alone in the gray light with dirt under her fingernails she had no memory of earning, and began, very carefully, to plan a theft.
