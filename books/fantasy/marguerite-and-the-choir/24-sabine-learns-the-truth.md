## 24. Sabine Learns the Truth

The child did not cry at dawn.

Sabine had learned to mark time by the small sounds of the nursery — the wet hiccough of a newborn's breath, the rustle of swaddling cloth, the thin mewling that had been, for nine years of her reign, the sound she most dreaded never hearing. Now it filled the eastern tower every hour of every day, and she had learned its silences too. The silence was the thing she listened for.

She was listening for it now, kneeling beside the cradle with her palm flat against her son's chest, when the breathing faltered.

It always began the same way: a hitch, a hesitation, as though the small body were asking permission from somewhere else before continuing. Sabine bent lower. Her lips found the shape of the prayer without her willing it — the old words, the ones the gods had taught her in exchange for the bargain, syllables that tasted of iron and honey and something older than either. She prayed. The chest beneath her hand rose again, obedient, and the child slept on with his mouth slightly open, his face slack as an unwritten page.

*Alaric.* She had named him for her grandfather, who had died well and old and unremarkable, which was all she had ever wanted for anyone she loved. Nine days he had lived now. Nine days of this.

"Your Majesty." The nurse stood in the doorway, gray-faced. She had stopped meeting Sabine's eyes on the third day. "You haven't eaten."

"I'll eat when he sleeps past matins without me."

"He won't, Majesty."

"I know," Sabine said quietly. "That's why I'm here."

The nurse withdrew. Sabine remained kneeling, her knees aching against the stone — she had refused cushions, refused chairs, refused every comfort that would let her rise, because rising felt like abandonment and abandonment felt like death, and she could no longer tell which of them she feared more: losing the child, or admitting what she had done to get him.

Outside the window, the moon hung above the courtyard like a coin someone had nailed to the sky. Twelve turns complete. The thirteenth beginning. The astronomers had gone quiet in their towers; the courtiers had gone loud in their chapels; and somewhere below her, in the cells beneath the cathedral, Odile the choirmaster sat in the dark singing hymns to herself because they had not thought to take her voice away along with her freedom.

Sabine had signed that order. She remembered the weight of the seal in her hand, the way the wax had taken her crest so cleanly, so easily. *For the stability of the realm.* That was what she had written in the margin. Four hundred years of her line had written those same words over worse things.

She kissed her son's forehead and went down to find out what the gods wanted next.

* * *

They came to her in the chapel, as they always did, though no door opened and no footsteps sounded. One moment she was alone with the candles; the next, the air at the altar had thickened into presence, the way water thickens near a drain.

"You are tired," said the god wearing her mother's voice.

Her mother had been dead eleven years. Sabine knew this. She knew it the way she knew the year of her coronation and the number of her father's wars — as fact, cold and catalogued. But knowing had never once stopped her body from responding, from softening, from leaning toward that voice like a plant toward light. That was the cruelty of it. They had not chosen a stranger's voice, or a herald's trumpet, or thunder. They had chosen the one voice that made her feel, even now, like a girl who had done nothing wrong yet.

"I am awake," she corrected. "There is a difference."

"There is," the voice agreed, warm as a hearth. "You are the only one left in this palace who is fully awake, Sabine. Do you know that? The others sleepwalk through their prayers. You pray with your whole throat. It is why we chose you."

"Why you *chose* me." She laughed, and hated the sound of it — brittle, a court laugh, a mask laughing. "I chose you. I knelt first. I asked first."

"And we answered." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "Have we not answered everything?"

Everything. Yes. She thought of the ledger of it, the terrible arithmetic she kept locked in the back of her skull where she pretended not to look at it: a living son, in exchange for forty voices. An heir for the dynasty, in exchange for the dynasty's own choir — the oldest institution in the kingdom, older than the crown itself, their bloodlines braided through every noble house. She had told herself it was statecraft. She had told herself it was arithmetic too: forty singers against the end of a four-hundred-year line. Any steward would make that trade. Any king in history would have made it faster.

But the singers had faces. She had watched them rehearse from behind the loft screen since she was a girl. She knew which soprano chewed mint leaves before services, which tenor had a lame brother, which alto — Marguerite's daughter, the taster, the one who would not die — hummed under her breath when she thought no one heard. Forty voices. Forty faces. And she had sold them with a signature and called it governance.

"What do you want?" she asked. "You didn't come here for flattery."

"No," the god said. "We came because you have begun to wonder. Wondering is permitted, Sabine. We only ask that wondering not become *knowing*, because knowing makes women do rash things — burn ledgers, free prisoners, run to borders." The warmth in the voice cooled by a single degree, the way a room cools when a window opens somewhere far away. "So. Ask us what you are wondering, and let us answer honestly. You will find honesty is our most persuasive garment."

Sabine's hands had gone cold. She folded them anyway, the way her mother had taught her to fold them before petitioners — a queen's hands, still and sovereign even when the body beneath them wanted to shake.

"The child," she said. "When I stop praying, he stops breathing. Why?"

Silence. Long enough that the candle flames leaned and righted themselves twice.

"Because he is new," the god said at last, gently, "and new things must be held together until they learn to hold themselves."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the truest one we have that will not hurt you."

"Then hurt me." Her voice cracked out across the chapel, startling a roosting dove in the rafters. "I am the Queen of Vhalen's grave and everything the maps forgot beyond it. I have bargained with things older than my dynasty's name. Do you imagine I cannot bear the truth? I have borne *lies* longer, and lies weigh more."

Another silence. When the voice came again, it had changed — not colder exactly, but *plainer*, the warmth set aside like a mask removed after a masque, revealing not a monster underneath but something worse: a bureaucrat. Patient. Procedural.

"You want to know if the leash is around his neck or yours."

"Yes."

"Both," the god said. "The leash is one cord, Sabine. It passes through him and around you and back through him. As long as you pray, he breathes. As long as he breathes, you pray. There is no knot in it. There is no end of it. He will grow. He will walk and speak and ask you questions, and every night of his life you will kneel beside his bed and keep him alive with your voice, and if your voice ever fails — if you sicken, if you die, if you simply grow *tired*, as mothers do — then he goes with you. Not painfully. We are not cruel. He will simply stop, the way a song stops."

The chapel tilted. Sabine caught herself against the altar rail, the carved saints biting into her palms. *A song stops.* She thought of Alaric's chest beneath her hand, that hitching pause before each breath, the little body asking permission. Asking *her*. All this time she had believed she was keeping death at bay with prayer. She had not understood that the prayer was the wall of the cage and the child was the bird inside it and she — she was the keeper, and the keeper is always also kept.

"You said a living child," she whispered. "You promised a living child."

"He is alive," the god said. "Every moment you sustain. What did you imagine a miracle was, Sabine? A gift with no string? We are old. We have been hungry for four hundred years. Nothing we give is given. Everything we give is *lent*."

* * *

She did not go back to the nursery at once. She walked instead, aimless, through corridors lit by the low blue hour before dawn, her feet carrying her by habit toward the council chamber before she turned them aside. She could not sit among her ministers. She could not look at the map of the kingdom spread on that long table, the valley marked with the old cartographer's contemptuous blankness, *here be nothing*, while she now knew — had known for weeks, had refused to know — that the nothing was full.

Because there was a second question. She had asked the first one about the child because the child was the wound she could bear to touch. The second question she had been circling for days the way the tongue circles a broken tooth.

She asked it aloud to the empty corridor, because the gods heard prayers whether she addressed them or not. That was the other thing she had learned: there was no such thing as speaking privately anymore.

"When the Door opens," she said, "what happens to my line?"

The corridor grew cold. Not metaphorically — frost feathered up the arrow-slit glass, and her breath came out white, and somewhere below her the dogs in the kennels began to howl in a chorus, all of them, as if a bell had struck inside their skulls.

"You will be exalted," said the voices — plural now, layered, her mother's voice braided with her dead father's and a cook's and a kitchen boy's and others she did not recognize but understood, somehow, were hers by inheritance, voices of everyone her family had ever wronged or worshipped. "Your name will be first among names. Your dynasty will endure forever."

"Forever," Sabine repeated. "Say it plainly. I am a plain woman from a plain line that has survived by hearing plainly."

The frost crept. The candle she had carried guttered down to a blue bead of flame.

"When the Door opens," the voices said, "the choir sings the threshold, and the threshold must be worn. A Door needs a house, Sabine. A house needs a family. Yours is the oldest. Yours is the closest. Yours has already learned to kneel."

"Worn," she said. "You mean *inhabited*."

"We mean honored."

"You mean you will put us on." Her voice rose, cracked, became something she barely recognized — not a queen's voice, not a mother's, but a woman's, raw, alone in a freezing corridor at the hour when the dead are supposed to be safely elsewhere. "Like a coat. Like a *coat*. You will wear my son's face and sign my seal and sit my throne and call it exaltation, and there will be no one left to know the difference, because everyone who could know will be—" She stopped. The word would not come. *Worn.* Everything worn. The choir worn as a key. Her line worn as a coat. Herself worn as — what? The hand that opened the lock?

The voices said nothing, which was answer enough. In four hundred years of silence, the gods had learned that silence, given back at the right moment, was more honest than any lie they could fashion.

Sabine stood in the frost-rimed dark and did the arithmetic one final time, and this time she did not look away from the sum.

She had traded forty lives for one child, and the child was a leash. She had traded her dynasty's soul for its survival, and the survival was a costume waiting to be worn. Every prayer she had ever breathed into the dark — every desperate midnight petition for an heir, for a strong son, for a line that would not end with her — every one of them had been heard, and hoarded, and priced, and the price had always been going to be this. Not because the gods were cruel. Because she had been *hungry*, and hunger recognizes hunger, and hungry things do not bargain. Hungry things feed.

"My mother prayed too," she said to the empty air. Her voice had gone very quiet. "Did you wear her?"

The frost crackled. Somewhere far off, the thirteenth moon-turn ground another degree across the sky.

"She wore herself," the voices said, almost kindly. "As you are doing now. It is a family art."

* * *

She went back to the nursery at first light.

Alaric was awake — the waking that was not quite waking, the gray-eyed staring at nothing that newborns did, that had once seemed to her mystical and now seemed to her like a boy looking through a window at whatever stood outside holding his breath. She lifted him. He weighed almost nothing. Nine days old and already the heaviest thing in the kingdom.

She fed him. She sang to him — not the hymn, never the hymn, she had forbidden that — but a nonsense song from her own childhood, about a fox and a miller, tuneless and foolish, and while she sang she prayed, because she had to, because the leash required it, the words running underneath the melody like a river under ice. Two songs at once. That was her life now. That was what she had made of motherhood: a duet in which one part kept the singer alive.

When he slept, truly slept, she laid him in the cradle and knelt beside it and prayed without stopping, and while her lips moved she looked at the shelf above the changing table, where her prayer book lay.

It had been her grandmother's before it was hers. Calfskin, soft as skin indeed, gone bald at the corners from generations of thumbs. Inside were the petitions of five queens: for rain, for sons, for victory, for mercy, for rain again. The old gods' names were written in it in ink that had faded to the color of dried blood. She had added her own entries in her own hand, neat and slanted, dates and requests and — later, after the answers began — dates and *payments*. The last thirty pages read like a merchant's ledger. She had told herself that too was statecraft.

Sabine took the book down.

She did not open it. She knew every word in it, and she had discovered, in the frozen corridor, that knowing was the one thing the gods feared — not armies, not abjurations, not the church's empty rituals, but a woman who finally knew the shape of her own bargain and declined to pretend otherwise.

There was a brazier in the corner, lit against the morning chill. She crossed to it and held the book over the coals.

The gods spoke at once, all of them, filling the nursery like floodwater: *Sabine. Sabine. Think of the child.* And beneath it, slyer, in her mother's voice alone: *He breathes because you believe. Burn the book and see what your belief is worth.*

"I know," she said. "I've known since the corridor. That's the horror of it — I'll burn this and then I'll kneel back down and pray, because he stops if I stop, and you built it so I couldn't even damn you properly. You've made my love the instrument. So yes. Watch me burn the book. Watch me pray anyway. And know that I know, every word, every day, exactly what I am feeding and why."

She let the pages fall.

They caught slowly, the way old vellum does, curling at the edges like leaves in autumn, and the names of five queens' worth of gods went up in a smell like burning hair, and the frost on the windows wept and ran, and somewhere deep beneath the palace — she felt it through the floor, through her knees, through the milk-heavy ache of her breasts — something vast shifted its weight and settled in to wait.

Because that was the last truth, the one she had saved for last, the one she would carry now like a stone in her chest: they did not need her faith. They needed only her function. The book was ash; the leash remained. Burning the prayer book changed nothing except that it told the gods, plainly, that she knew — and knowing, she understood, had just made her dangerous in exactly one direction.

Not against them. Against herself.

Sabine knelt back down beside the cradle. She took her sleeping son's hand, so small it closed around one of her fingers like a ring, and she began to pray, eyes open, watching the ash settle in the brazier.

And as she prayed, she began, very quietly, to plan.

The taster. The girl who would not die, whose blood the god-toxins could not read, whose mother had sung the alto line and been sent into the valley to close the Door. The girl was the lock. The gods needed her open. Which meant that everything the gods wanted ran through Ines Almeida — and everything Sabine now wanted, everything she had left, her son's breath and her line's survival and her own soul's last scrap of uncorrupted ground, ran through the same narrow channel in the opposite direction.

She sent for her captain of horse before the ash had cooled.

"Ready the guard," she said. "Full company. We ride for the border stones."
