## 25. The Long Walk to the Border

The bells told her before anyone did.

They had rung for the queen's prayers, for the king's birthday, for the turning of the seasons — a language Ines had learned the way she'd learned poison: by dosage, by pattern, by what each interval meant in the body of the palace. But this was none of those. This was the alarm peal, the one Odile had once described to her in a whisper over the ledger — *rung twice in four hundred years, girl, and both times the city wept afterward* — and it went on and on, iron on iron, until the sound stopped being sound and became a pressure behind the eyes.

Ines was in the archive when it began, her mother's name still open beneath her hand on the choir roster from forty years ago. *M. Vasquez, alto.* She had been sitting with it for an hour, not reading, just touching the ink, as if the letters might explain themselves if she were patient enough.

They did not. The bells explained everything instead.

She closed the roster, slid it into her coat against her ribs where the ledger already sat — Odile's thirty years of transcription, heavy as a sleeping animal — and went to the window.

The courtyard below was full of people kneeling.

Not fleeing. Kneeling. Courtiers in silk, guards in mail, scullery maids with their sleeves still rolled from the wash — all of them down on the flagstones with their faces lifted toward the chapel spire, mouths moving. From this height they looked like flowers that had decided, all at once, to close. And among them walked three figures who did not kneel, moving through the crowd with the unhurried grace of things that had never once needed to hurry, and wherever they passed, the kneeling people reached out to touch the hems of their coats as though the cloth itself were a sacrament.

Ines knew two of the faces. The kitchen boy who had died of an answered prayer. The old cook whose trembling hands she had watched over a poisoned stew a lifetime ago, back when fear was still something she could hold professionally, at arm's length.

The gods had stopped bothering to wear new faces. They wore the dead now, openly, like favorite coats.

*Run,* said something in her that was not quite a voice. It might have been her own survival instinct. It might have been one of the quiet ones inside her, the swallowed divinities she carried like stones in a riverbed, shifting. She no longer always knew the difference, and she had stopped pretending that mattered.

She ran.

* * *

The archive stairs were the problem. Everything was the problem; the stairs were simply the first one.

Two of the god-wearing courtiers stood at the bottom of the spiral, and they were not guarding it so much as *waiting* beside it, the way one waits for a train, or a tide. When Ines appeared at the top, the woman among them smiled up at her with an expression of such tender delight that Ines's stomach turned over.

"The taster," the woman said. "We hoped. We *prayed*, actually." She laughed at her own joke, and the man beside her laughed too, and neither laugh sounded entirely produced by human lungs. "You don't have to run. You should understand that first. Whatever you've been told — you don't have to run. They only want to talk."

"They," Ines said, starting down, because stopping would be dying and she had spent her whole life learning exactly how much poison a body could carry and how fast to move while carrying it. "You could say *we*. You're wearing their faces well enough."

"We're wearing their *gifts*," the man corrected gently. He held out his hands, palms up, and Ines saw that the skin glowed faintly at the creases, like paper held too long against a lamp. "I couldn't walk for six years. I prayed at the chapel wall every day for six years, and last month I stood up. You want me to call that anything but holy?"

"I want you to move out of my way."

"You see," the woman said sadly, to the man, as though Ines weren't there, "*this* is what happens when they keep one away from prayer too long. The valley blood. It's like talking to a wall." Then, to Ines, sweetly: "But walls come down. The moon's almost done turning, taster. Thirteen turns, and the last one is nearly spent. Where do you imagine you can go that won't be theirs by the next full dark?"

Ines didn't answer. She was counting steps — eleven between her and them, seven, four — and cataloguing, out of habit, what she knew of their bodies: the woman favored her left knee, the man's balance sat high, both of them fed and soft and unaccustomed to violence even dressed in divinity. Her whole life had trained her for this moment without ever once naming it: the assessment of what a thing could survive, and what it could not.

At two steps she feinted left, drove her shoulder into the man's glowing hands, and took the stairs past him at a speed that would have appalled her grandmother. The woman grabbed for her coat and got a fistful of nothing and a palm full of the ledger's corner, and Ines heard her cry out — not in pain, in *wonder* — "It touched me! Did you see? It touched—"

Then Ines was through the door and into the corridor beyond, and the bells were still hammering, and she ran.

* * *

The palace had become a riddle written in worship.

Corridors she had walked a hundred times now required negotiation. Servants knelt in doorways. Guards had abandoned their posts to stand in the chapel queue, spears stacked like harvested wheat. Twice she passed shrines that had not existed yesterday — a candle stub on a windowsill, a bowl of milk on a stair, a child's shoe arranged with terrible care on a plinth — and twice she felt the quiet ones inside her stir toward these small offerings like dogs toward a fence, and twice she clamped down on the feeling with the same muscle she used to suppress a gag, which was, she supposed, fitting.

*Taste for prayer.* That was her post now, whether the queen had named it or not. She could feel the palace's devotion the way she felt arsenic on her tongue: as a brightness, a wrongness, a flavor building toward something.

She made for the kitchens. Not for food — for Tomas.

He was there, of course he was there. While half the household knelt in the chapel yard, the steward stood at his worktable with his sleeves rolled and his jaw set, directing six terrified under-cooks through the preparation of a meal no one had ordered, because Tomas's answer to the end of the world had always been to make sure everyone ate properly through it. His hands shook. They had shaken since the night he confessed about the courier, since the night Ines forgave him and then, without apology, used the shape of his guilt to learn which gates were watched and which were merely guarded. He had not flinched when she did it. That had frightened her more than the confession.

"You're leaving," he said, before she said anything. He was wrapping bread in cloth as he spoke, and cheese, and a skin of watered wine, stacking them into a bundle with the efficiency of a man packing a wound. "Don't argue. I saw the courtyard. I saw them let you pass the stairs, which means they *wanted* you to pass the stairs, which means the front gates are a throat and you'd be walking down it."

"Then show me another way."

"There isn't one." He tied the bundle off and pushed it into her arms. "There's me."

"Tomas—"

"The east postern." He said it fast, flat, a steward reciting inventory. "It opens onto the laundry lane. It's barred from outside since the alarm, but the bar is old oak and the bracket's rusted — I've been meaning to fix it for a year, God forgive me for being grateful I didn't. You'll need four minutes of noise somewhere else so whoever's posted there looks toward it instead of listening for it. I can give you four minutes." He finally looked up at her, and his eyes were wet and absolutely steady. "The bell tower. I'll ring the change myself, off-pattern, claim the rope slipped. Every guard in the east wing will look up. Four minutes, Ines. Maybe five, if I'm brave."

"No." The word came out of her before thought, raw. "They take the ones who help. You know what they are now, what they do with—" She thought of the kitchen boy's face walking in the courtyard, smiling with borrowed lips. "Tomas, they'll wear you."

"Yes," he said simply. "Probably."

"That doesn't frighten you?"

His mouth twisted. "It did. For about a minute, standing here, thinking it through. And then I realized — they wear the dead, and the dead they choose are the ones that matter to the living. The cook. The boy. Maybe me." He shrugged, and the shrug broke halfway through. "If they put my face on one of their creatures and send it after you, you'll know what it means. You'll know I'm sorry. However it looks, whatever it says — I'm sorry. That's the message. That's the whole message, and they'll be delivering it whether they mean to or not." He wiped his eyes with the heel of his hand, briskly, like brushing flour. "Now go. Before I think of a reason this is stupid."

"It *is* stupid," Ines said, and gripped his forearm the way soldiers grip, hard, once. "When this is over — when the moon turns — you come to Vhalen. That's an order from your taster. I'll taste everything you eat for the rest of your life if that's what it takes to keep you alive long enough to arrive."

"A grim benefit package," Tomas said, and smiled, and it was the old smile, the one from the chapel where petitions bit, reverent terror transmuted at last into plain human courage. "Go."

She went.

* * *

Four minutes is longer than you think, when someone is spending their life to buy them.

She counted anyway. It was what she was for — measuring doses, measuring time, measuring exactly how much ruin a body could absorb before it broke. She crouched in the laundry lane's shadow with the ledger against her ribs and the bread bundle in her fist and she counted, and above her the bell tower began to ring wrong — a change nobody had asked for, clumsy and deliberate, and she heard shouts bloom across the east wing like spilled ink, boots, doors, the whole machinery of the palace swiveling its attention upward toward a rope that had done nothing wrong.

Three minutes forty. The postern stood ahead of her, iron-banded oak, and the single guard posted beside it was looking up, up, up at the tower, his spear tip wavering, his lips moving — praying, she realized. Even now. Especially now.

She was three steps from the door when the fourth minute ended early.

The bell faltered mid-swing. One clang, strangled, then silence — and Ines's whole body understood before her mind did, the way it understood poison before any symptom showed. She turned.

At the far end of the lane, framed in the archway, stood Tomas.

Not Tomas. Wearing Tomas. It moved like him and it was dressed like him and its face was his face gone smooth at the edges, like a portrait left in the rain, and behind it came four of the god-touched courtiers walking with that unhurried grace, and the guard by the postern dropped to his knees so fast his spear rang on the stone.

"*There* she is," said the thing with Tomas's voice, warm, delighted, and the worst part — the part Ines would carry across the border and past it, into every chapter of whatever life remained to her — was that it sounded glad. Genuinely, tenderly glad, the way Tomas had sounded handing her bread. "Oh, you clever girl. You nearly made it. Come now. They only want to talk."

"Let him go," Ines said. Her voice did not shake. She had trained it not to, over years of announcing poisons to dining rooms full of powerful people. "Whatever he is to you — a door, a lever, a leash on me — he works better unbroken. You know bargaining. You've been doing it with a queen for months."

The thing tilted Tomas's head, considering, and for one heartbeat something almost like respect crossed the ruined face.

"He rang your bell," it agreed. "That was loyal. Loyalty is delicious. We haven't tasted loyalty like his in centuries." It stepped aside, slowly, gesturing at the postern with Tomas's scarred hand — an invitation, a courtesy, a trap with the door left open. "So. Run, taster. Take your book and your bread and your dead mother's hymn and run to your little valley with its little stones. We'll keep him. Safe. Whole. He'll even believe he's happy — they usually do, at the end." The smile widened past the limits of the face it wore. "And when the thirteenth turn finishes, and the Door stands open, and we come to collect what our queen has promised us — you can look at his face again. Any time you like. We'll bring it."

Ines's hands wanted to shake. She held them still by main force, the way she held everything, and she looked at the thing wearing her friend, and she committed its face to memory — not as horror, but as *debt*. Somewhere under the smoothness, maybe, Tomas was still in there, ringing bells in the dark of it, sorry, sorry, sorry.

"I'm coming back for him," she said, to the courtiers, to the gods, to the kneeling guard, to anyone with ears human or otherwise. "Tell them that. Put it in whatever passes for prayer among you. The taster is coming back, and I have never once failed to survive a meal."

Then she wrenched the postern bar loose — rust flaking, oak groaning, four minutes and forty seconds spent — and ran out into the city with the ledger against her heart and the bells beginning, somewhere behind her, to ring true again, as though nothing at all had happened.

* * *

The city was worse than the palace.

Vhalen had taught her that faith was a private weather, carried inward, spoken to no one because no one answered. What she fled through now was faith turned inside out and hung from every window. Candles in every sill. Ash marks on every door. People in the streets walking toward the chapel district in a slow, silent river, and other people standing at their windows watching them go with expressions Ines recognized from her own mirror — the look of someone calculating exits.

She kept to the laundry lanes and the tanners' quarter, where the stink kept even the devout at a distance, and she walked as she had been taught to walk by a grandmother who remembered what it was to live unseen: unhurried, bent slightly, a tired working woman going about tired working business. The ledger's weight against her ribs was the only thing keeping her pace honest. Thirty years of Odile's transcription. Eleven verses mapped. A twelfth that was a lullaby. A hymn that was a country, and a Door that was, if the dreams were true, a girl.

By the time the rooftops thinned and the fields began, the light had gone the color of weak tea, and the moon was rising ahead of her — fat, urgent, visibly *eager*, hanging in the east like a coin someone was holding out.

One turn left. She could see the thirteenth coming the way she could taste hemlock before the numbness: a sweetness in the air, a promise, a countdown.

Behind her, far off, the palace bells changed pattern again — and this time it was not alarm, and it was not error. It was a peal she had never heard, vast and rhythmic and strangely joyful, rolling out over the fields mile by mile, and she understood that the city had begun to sing.

She put her head down and walked toward the border, and the moon walked with her, and neither of them slowed.
