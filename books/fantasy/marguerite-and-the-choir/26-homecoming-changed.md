## 26. Homecoming, Changed

The ledger rode against my ribs like a second heart.

I had wrapped it in my spare shift and bound it with the cord from my cloak, and still I could feel it — not the weight of the paper, which was nothing, but the weight of what was written there. Thirty years of Odile's hand. Forty voices' worth of family memory pressed into ink. A map of a country that did not exist, drawn in verses that hummed when you held them too long. The courtiers who had come for me at the river ford had wanted it badly enough to wade in after me with their torches raised like offerings, and I had learned something watching them: people who worship do not chase. They *process*. They had moved through the water singing, and their song had been the hymn, but wrong — bent at the eleventh verse, sweetened where it should have ached, as though someone had taken the oldest thing in the kingdom and taught it to lie.

I had run anyway. Singing processions are slower than fear.

Three days from the palace, two from the ford, and now the road climbed through pine country that smelled of resin and cold stone, and I knew without being told that I was close, because the birds had stopped. Not fled — stopped. They sat in the branches above the track in perfect rows, silent, all facing the same direction, all facing *home*, like an audience waiting for the overture.

I walked faster.

* * *

You would think a person would know her own valley by its smell. Wet granite and sheep-fat and woodsmoke and the particular green rot of the millpond in autumn — these were the smells I had carried out of Vhalen sewn into the lining of me, and I had imagined, on every hard night of the journey south, that breathing them again would be like setting down a load I'd forgotten I was carrying.

But the valley announced itself wrong.

I crested the last ridge at dusk, the one where Grandmother used to stop and make me look back at the road so I would remember how far away the rest of the world was, and the first thing I noticed was the light. It came up out of the valley floor instead of down onto it — a faint, sourceless luminescence, blue-white, the color of the hour before dawn, except that it was evening and the sun was dying red behind me. The whole bowl of Vhalen glowed like a lamp seen through cloth.

The second thing I noticed was the stones.

There were nine border stones on the western approach. I had counted them a thousand times as a child, hopping from shadow to shadow of them, daring myself to touch the seventh because Grandmother said the seventh remembered. They were granite, older than any building in the valley, taller than a man, and they had stood — so far as anyone knew — uncracked since before the sealing, since before the erasure, since before the treaties that pretended we had never been.

Now every one of them was split.

Not broken. Not toppled. *Split* — a single seam running down each stone from crown to earth, fine as a hair, and out of those seams came the light, bleeding upward into the dusk like water finding the cracks in a dam. Where the seams met the ground, the grass had grown strange: silver-veined, trembling when there was no wind, bending toward the valley's center as if the whole meadow were listening to something underground.

I stood at the ridge until my legs shook, and then I went down anyway, because behind me the road held torchlight and singing, and ahead of me was the only place I had ever belonged, even if belonging now meant walking into a house whose walls were coming apart.

* * *

The first farmstead was empty.

That was not itself alarming — the Aldous place had stood empty for years, its roof fallen in soft as a rotted fruit. What was alarming was the well. As I passed it, the water rose. Not flowed — *rose*, climbing the stone throat of the well in a slow, glassy column until it stood at the lip and looked at me. I don't know how else to say it. Water cannot look. But it held itself there, perfectly still, perfectly round, and I felt regarded the way I had once felt regarded by the god wearing my mother's voice: seen down to the marrow, catalogued, weighed.

"Grandmother," I said aloud, stupidly, to the water. "Is she—"

The column sank. All at once, like a held breath released, and left the well ordinary and dark, and I stood there with my hand pressed flat against the ledger under my coat, telling myself that a taster does not flinch, a taster observes, a taster names what is in front of her even when naming it makes it real.

Magic. That was the name. Magic, leaking into Vhalen like water under a door, after four hundred years of the valley being the one dry room in a drowning world.

And I understood, walking the lane past hedgerows that whispered in no wind, why the gods had wanted the Door open so badly. This was what they were starving for. Not worship — worship was only the taste of it. This. A whole country steeped in the stuff, sealed away like a cellar of wine, and the lock of the cellar had been my family, and the lock was standing in the road with dirt on her boots feeling the seams of the world split under her feet.

A prayer rose in me — unbidden, reflexive, the way hunger rises — and before I could swallow it back, the hedge answered. Every leaf shivered silver. Somewhere down the lane, a dog began to sing in a voice no dog should own, three notes braided into one.

I clapped my hand over my mouth like a child and kept walking.

* * *

The village was lit.

Every window, every doorway, lamps and candles and little fires in iron braziers set out in the streets, and the people of Vhalen stood in their doorways or gathered around the fires, and nobody spoke. That was the thing that made my skin crawl worse than the well or the hedges: four hundred years of silence had made my people fluent in quiet, and this was not our quiet. Our quiet was full — of work-songs hummed low, of gossip conducted in glances, of the enormous eloquence of a community that had learned never to raise its voice toward heaven lest heaven mistake it for asking. This quiet was the quiet of people listening to a sound they could not yet hear.

They saw me pass. I saw them see me. Old Marta Ferreiro, who had taught me to gut fish, put her hand to her mouth. The Vasquez twins, grown now into men I barely recognized, stepped back from their fire as I went by, not from me — I understood that much — but from whatever followed me like smoke, whatever the road south had rubbed into my skin. The court had begun to look at me like a relic. My own valley looked at me like a weather coming.

At the well in the square — the good well, the drinking well — the water was rising too. And around its rim, laid out with terrible care, were offerings. Bread. Salt. A comb of honey. A child's shoe. Small honest things, the kind you leave for saints, except that Vhalen had never had saints, had never been permitted the luxury of believing anything was listening, and now here was my whole village improvising devotion in a single day, the way a body improvises fever, because something had gotten into the blood.

An old man knelt at the well's edge, murmuring. I caught only the end of it as I passed:

"—and I only ask it once, I only ever asked the once, and if you're there now, if you can finally hear me—"

The water in the well turned over. Slowly. Like something rolling in its sleep.

I did not wait to see whether he was answered. Some part of me — the part trained by poison, by the chapel, by the kitchen boy who died of a gift that bit — knew already. Answered prayers in the kingdom came with teeth. Here, where four hundred years of unanswered prayers had pooled and thickened like sediment, I could not imagine what teeth they would grow.

* * *

Grandmother's house stood at the valley's head, where the river came down off the mountain in three cold threads, and its windows were dark.

Of everything I had seen since the ridge — the cracked stones, the glowing seams, the listening water — that darkness frightened me most. My grandmother did not keep a dark house. She said lambs died and milk soured in a dark house; she kept a lamp in every window from dusk to dawn her whole life long, a habit she claimed was thrift and everyone knew was vigilance, the old priestess-line instinct to stand watch at the edge of things.

I let myself in through the kitchen, because the front door was barred from within, and found the great hearth swept clean and cold, the table bare, the chairs pushed in with a neatness that read less like order and more like departure. On the table, weighted under the salt cellar, lay a note in her hand, the letters tall and severe as fence posts:

*Behind the chapel. Come alone. Leave the book in the house — it is safer among the living than beside the dead.*

I took the ledger out from under my coat and set it on the table, and the moment my hands left it, the room felt emptier, as if the book had been doing some portion of the humming that I had mistaken for my own blood. I almost picked it up again. Her instruction held me. Grandmother's instructions had a way of being load-bearing.

Behind the chapel meant the churchyard path, and the churchyard path meant the yew trees, and the yew trees meant—

I was running before I finished the thought.

* * *

She was standing at the graveside with a lantern in one hand and a spade leaning against her shoulder, and she was not digging. The grave was already dug. It gaped beside her in the lantern light, rectangular and patient, lined with raw earth the color of opened muscle, and my grandmother — eighty-one years old, built like the border stones themselves, a woman who had buried a husband and two sons and half her generation without ever once letting me see her hands shake — stood beside it with her chin lifted, watching me come down the path, and her eyes were wet and furious and triumphant all at once.

"You crossed the stones," she said. Not a question. "You crossed them cracked."

"Grandmother—"

"They were cracked when you crossed them." She set the lantern down on the yew root with a care that was somehow more frightening than violence. "Say it plainly, girl. You came home through a door that is already open."

"The whole valley—" My voice came out thin. "The stones are split, all nine, the light is coming through, the wells are rising, the hedges answer when you speak, Marta Ferreiro looked at me like—"

"Like a relic," Grandmother said. "Or a storm. Both are accurate." She looked at the grave, and then at me, and something in her face rearranged itself — the fury settling down into something older and sadder, the way silt settles in water. "Come here, Ines. Stand where you can see into it. You'll want your legs under you."

"I'm not digging anything tonight."

"No," she agreed. "Nothing needs digging. Everything that goes in that ground has been in it forty years." She picked up the lantern again and held it out over the grave, and the light fell in, and I looked, because she had told me to and because some traitor part of me had known since the village square, since the silent windows, since the word *chapel*, exactly what I would see.

The grave was empty. Of course it was empty. But it was not bare. At its bottom, laid out straight and covered in a linen gone gray with age, was a shape the length of a woman, and on the shape's breast, pinned where a brooch would sit, was a small wooden bird — the kind the choir used to wear at midwinter, the kind I had seen carved once in a palace archive, next to a roster of forty names.

The lantern trembled. It took me a moment to understand that the tremor was in my grandmother's arm, and another moment to understand that mine were shaking worse.

"Her name was Marguerite," my grandmother said, in the voice she used for prayers she didn't believe in — level, precise, pitched to carry across water. "You've been singing her lullaby your whole life, child. And tonight, while you were walking home through a valley coming apart at its seams, the thirteenth turn of the moon began."

Far overhead, past the yew branches, past the leaking light, the moon hung fat and white and wrong — one night fuller than it had any right to be, one turn further along than the sky should allow.

"And it began," she said, "with you inside the walls."
