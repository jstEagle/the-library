## 27. The Grave That Was Dug

The valley had changed its smell.

Ines noticed it before she noticed anything else — before the cracked border stones, before the thin gray light leaking through the pines like water through a roof, before her grandmother standing at the edge of the kitchen garden with dirt to her elbows and a spade planted in the earth like a flag. The air of Vhalen had always smelled of bread and cold stone and the particular green sweetness of a place that kept its own weather. Now there was something under it. Something metallic. Something that tasted, when she breathed too deep, like the chapel in the palace — like incense that had gone wrong, like prayers left too long in a closed room.

Magic was getting in. She could feel it on her skin now, now that she knew what it felt like: that cold-water brush, that faint electric wrongness. It came through the stones in threads, in trickles, pooling in the low places of the valley the way fog pooled in the hollows before dawn.

Her grandmother did not say *you're home*. She did not say *I knew you'd come*. She looked at Ines across the garden — at the mud on her hem, the ledger strapped to her back, the four hundred miles of road written into her face — and said only:

"You're late. The ground's been ready for three days."

Ines followed her grandmother past the bean rows, past the beehives humming their ordinary, unmagical hum, to the far corner of the garden where the soil turned dark and rich over the old compost beds. A grave. Rectangular, neat, lined with river stones. Dug with care, dug by hands that knew this work, and empty.

"Whose?" Ines asked, though some part of her already knew, the way she had always known things about her mother without being told — the way she knew the alto line of a hymn she'd been told was a lullaby, the way she knew the taste of every poison in the duke's provinces without ever having read a manual.

Her grandmother leaned on the spade. She was smaller than Ines remembered, or perhaps Ines had grown; four months of palace bread and terror had put weight on her shoulders and years behind her eyes. Her grandmother's hair was white as birch bark, braided tight, and her hands — those hands that had taught her to build immunity drop by drop, tincture by tincture, a childhood measured in milligrams — were steady as bedrock.

"Your mother's," she said. "It should have been dug forty years ago. I'm sorry it took me so long."

* * *

They buried nothing that afternoon. That was the point, Ines would come to understand. They sat instead, on the upturned wheelbarrow and an old stool, while the light went long and gold through the leak in the world, and her grandmother told the story the way you tell a story you have rehearsed alone for forty years — in order, without weeping, with terrible precision.

"Her name was Marguerite," her grandmother began, and Ines's whole body went still, because Odile's ledger had whispered that name in a palace loft half a kingdom away, inked in a dead choirmaster's careful hand. "But here we called her Margo, because a girl named after a flower gets teased, and because names are doors, and we were trying very hard to keep ours shut."

"Margo Almeida," Ines said.

"Margo Vasquez, when she married. Almeida was her working name — the name she used when she sang." Her grandmother's mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly grief. "You took it when the courier came. I saw you flinch when he read it. I burned that letter hoping the burning would end it. It didn't. Nothing ends. That's the first thing you have to understand, Ines. Nothing in this valley has ever ended. It has only been held."

The wind moved through the pines. Somewhere down the slope, one of the cracked border stones gave off a faint sound, like a wineglass sung at its rim.

"Tell me," Ines said. "All of it. Don't decide what I can carry."

Her grandmother studied her for a long moment — studying her the way Ines studied a dish, looking for what was hidden underneath, for what might kill.

"All right," she said. "Then start with what you know. Tell me what they told you at the palace."

Ines told her. About the choir and the hymn no one could date. About Odile's ledger, thirty years of transcription, every verse a map-point in a country erased from every map. About verse eleven opening a door-shaped absence in a chapel wall. About the god who spoke in her mother's voice. About the thirteenth turn, and the queen's child that breathed only while its mother prayed, and the forty voices gathering at the border stones even now, deserting their queen, walking toward a valley most of them had never heard of until a month ago.

"And the alto line," her grandmother said quietly. "The missing verse. You know where it lives."

"In me." Ines touched her own throat. "You taught it to me as a lullaby. You taught it to your daughter as a lullaby. I thought it was just — an old song about a river."

"It is about a river." Her grandmother looked out toward the stream at the bottom of the garden, moving slow and silver over its stones. "It is about the exact river. Sing it in your head and follow the water — every phrase is a bend, every rest a pool. Your mother sang it to me once, all the way through, the night before she left, and I understood that she wasn't singing to comfort me. She was showing me the way home, in case I ever needed to find her. Or in case I ever needed to find *it*."

"The Door."

"The Door." Her grandmother said the word the way you'd say the name of a disease that had taken someone you loved. "And now I'll tell you the part that isn't in any ledger, because Odile could only transcribe what the hymn says, and the hymn doesn't say this. The hymn is a map. But maps don't tell you why the country was erased."

She stood, joints cracking, and walked to the grave's edge. She looked down into the neat rectangle of dark earth as if she could see something in it.

"Four hundred years ago, the gods were not starving," she said. "They were fat. They ate everything — prayers, yes, but also harvests, firstborns, memory, color, the names of small villages. The priestesses of the old religion tried every binding they knew, and the bindings failed, because you cannot bind a god from outside. A god is too large. So the last high priestess did the only thing left. She didn't bind them. She *walled herself in with them.*"

Ines felt the ground tilt, very slightly, the way it tilts in the moment before a fever breaks.

"She sealed Vhalen," her grandmother continued. "Not to protect it from magic. To imprison magic inside it. Every god that answered prayers in these lands — she called them home with the hymn, all of them, the way you call animals in before a storm, and then she shut the border, and she stayed inside with them. Her bloodline stayed inside. Generation after generation. Because the seal isn't made of stones, Ines. Stones crack. Look at them." She gestured at the gray light threading through the trees. "The seal is made of priestess-blood, alive, walking around, planting beans, singing lullabies. We are not hiding from the gods. We are the lock they are locked inside of. And a lock, my darling, is the one thing in the world that cannot leave the door."

The silence after that was enormous. Ines heard her own heartbeat, that stuttering, damaged, magnificent heartbeat that had survived arsenic and henbane and something older than both, and for the first time she heard it for what it was: not a rhythm but a mechanism. A tumbling of pins. A lock turning over in its sleep.

"I'm not the key," she said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears.

"No."

"Odile thought — the court thinks — the god said—"

"The god lied. Gods lie the way water wets. Of course they want you to believe you're the key. A key opens a door and walks away free. But a lock—" her grandmother's voice gentled, which was somehow worse than if she had shouted, "—a lock stays. A lock holds the door shut from the inside, forever, or until it is broken. Your mother understood. That's why she left. Not fleeing, Ines. Patrolling. She went out beyond the stones to learn what was scratching at the walls, and she found the court, and she found the choir, and she learned that the dynasty had begun praying again, and she tried to stop it from the outside. And when she couldn't stop it, she came back, and she spent the last years of her life teaching you the alto line without ever telling you why, because telling you would have been the same as breaking you, and a broken lock opens just as easily as a stolen key."

"When did she die?"

"Eleven days after you were born." Her grandmother said it flatly, factually, the way she'd taught Ines to report the results of a tasting. "Childbirth, the midwife wrote. The midwife believed it. I dug the grave myself and then I filled it in with nothing, because we do not mark the graves of locks, Ines. Marking a grave tells the world where the body is. And where the body is, the door is. For forty years I let the whole valley believe your mother ran away and died nameless somewhere beyond the hills. I let *you* believe it. I let you grow up thinking you were a cook's granddaughter with a strong stomach, instead of—"

"Instead of the door itself." Ines stood. Her legs held. She was surprised by that. "All those dishes. All those poisons. You weren't building my immunity so I could survive the world."

"I was building it so the world couldn't survive *in* you." Her grandmother finally wept then, just two lines of water down a face like carved wood, quickly wiped. "A lock has to be proof against picking. Against force. Against everything that might be poured into it to make it fail. You think I fed you nightshade drops in honey because I wanted you to take a royal post? I did it because the gods have been testing the hinges for four hundred years, and the tests were going to reach you eventually, and I could not — I could *not* — let them find you soft."

* * *

Night came down through the broken border like poured ink, and with it came the sounds.

Ines stood at the garden fence and listened to the valley's accumulated silence begin, at last, to speak. It was exactly as the astronomer's omen had promised, exactly as the plan had warned: with the moon climbing toward its thirteenth turn, every prayer ever spoken in Vhalen and never answered was becoming audible — four hundred years of unanswered petitions rising out of the soil like groundwater. A farmer's plea for rain in a drought year. A mother begging for a fevered child. Lovers, thieves, the dying, the lonely, the proud. They layered over each other in voices that were not quite voices, a tide of human wanting that had soaked into the earth of this valley because there was nowhere else for it to go.

And beneath it, under it, threaded through it — something listening. Something pacing. She thought of her dream: wolves behind the border stones, wearing patient faces. The gods were not starving quietly anymore. The lock was failing, and everything inside it had begun to press.

"They'll ask nicely," her grandmother said, coming to stand beside her. "When they reach the stones. That's the worst part. They'll wear faces you love and make offers you'll want to accept. Your mother's face. Mine. Maybe Tomas's, if they've taken him — and they will have taken him, if he covered your escape the way I'd have covered hers."

"He did cover it." Ines's throat tightened. "He's in their hands because of me."

"He's in their hands because of them. Learn the difference, or they'll use your guilt as a handle." Her grandmother turned to face her fully. In the dark her eyes caught the strange gray light and held it. "Now listen to me, because tomorrow the moon begins its final turn, and whatever happens after that happens fast. There is one thing I haven't told you. One thing your mother made me promise to hold until you were standing in this valley, at this grave, knowing what you are."

Ines waited.

"The alto line isn't a map to the Door," her grandmother said. "It isn't even the Door's location. It's the Door's *name*. Sing it whole — not the fragments, not the lullaby version, but the whole verse, with the words your mother never got time to teach you — and you won't open a doorway in a wall. The Door opens in the lock. In the priestess. In the blood." She reached out and laid her palm flat, gently, over Ines's sternum, over the stuttering heart. "Your mother sealed the gods in with her life. You can seal them in with yours. Or—"

"Or," Ines said.

"Or you open yourself, and let them out, and pray — to nothing, to no one, since no one is left to answer — that the world survives what comes through." Her grandmother's hand dropped. "That's the choice. That's the whole inheritance. I'm sorry. It was always going to be this. From the night you were born, it was always going to be this."

Far off, at the valley's northern edge, one of the border stones rang — a clear, sweet, terrible note, like a bell struck by a finger. Then another. Then another, closer, a line of them sounding down the slope toward the garden, and under the ringing Ines heard, very faintly, carried on wind that shouldn't have been able to cross the border at all:

Forty voices. Singing.

The choir had reached the stones.

Her grandmother picked up the spade from beside the empty grave and held it out to her, handle first, like an oath.

"Then we'd better teach you the rest of the verse," she said. "Before they finish theirs."
