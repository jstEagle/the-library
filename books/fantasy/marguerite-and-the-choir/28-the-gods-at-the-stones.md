## 28. The Gods at the Stones

They came at dusk, which was the first courtesy.

Ines had expected them to arrive like a storm — the sky splitting, the border stones shattering, her grandmother's wards burning blue and dying all at once. She had rehearsed that version of the end so many times in the three days since she'd learned to read the cracks in the stones that she had almost made peace with it. Storms were honest. Storms did not pretend to be anything but what they were.

Instead, the light simply began to fail in the wrong direction.

The sun was still up — a low, copper coin caught in the western peaks — but a shadow moved across the valley floor from the north, slow and deliberate, the way water finds the lowest ground. Ines stood at the window of her grandmother's house and watched it come, and it took her an absurdly long time to understand that the shadow had edges. That it was walking. That there were three figures on the road from the pass, and the dusk was pouring off them like a garment they trailed behind them out of politeness.

"Ines." Her grandmother's voice from the doorway behind her. Not alarmed. Worse than alarmed — resigned, the way she sounded when the first frost came early or when a neighbor's cow went lame. "Come away from the glass."

"They're here."

"I know. I've known since noon. The bees stopped." Her grandmother crossed the room and drew the shutter closed with a wooden peg worn smooth by forty years of her thumb. The room went dim and lamplit and small, and for one merciful moment it smelled only of rosemary and tallow and home. Then Ines heard it: her grandmother bolting a door that had never once been bolted in Ines's lifetime. Vhalen did not lock its doors. Vhalen had never needed to.

"Grandmother. What do we do?"

"What we have always done." The old woman turned, and the lamplight caught the lines of her face the way light catches the grain of old wood. "We do not answer."

* * *

The valley gathered without being told to gather. That was the oldest rule of Vhalen, older than any written law: when the stones spoke, everyone heard, and everyone came. By full dark there must have been two hundred souls standing in the market square, breath steaming in the autumn cold, faces turned toward the northern road where the three figures had stopped — precisely stopped — at the line of the border stones.

Ines could see them from the square's edge, where she stood with her grandmother's hand clamped around her wrist like a manacle. Torchlight reached maybe halfway down the road. Beyond that, the three shapes existed as suggestion more than substance, backlit by a moon already too fat, already beginning its thirteenth turn.

"They can't cross," someone said near her — Bramel the miller, his voice pitched high and hopeful. "The stones hold. They've held four hundred years."

"The stones are cracked," said another voice, flat with fear. "You've seen them. We've all seen them."

"The stones are cracked because something is pushing," said the blacksmith's widow, Marta, who had buried a husband and two sons and therefore had no patience left for comfort. "Pushing is not crossing. My grandmother pushed against death for six years and never once got through."

A few people laughed — the thin, involuntary laughter of the terrified — and then stopped all at once, because the nearest of the three figures raised its hand.

It waved.

Not a summons. Not a gesture of power. A wave — open-palmed, friendly, the wave of a neighbor seen across a field at harvest time. And then the figure stepped forward into the torchlight, and Ines felt her grandmother's grip go from iron to nothing, because the old woman's knees buckled, and Ines caught her, and together they stared at the face at the edge of Vhalen.

It was Cook.

Not a cook. *The* cook — Master Ferren, who had presided over the palace kitchens with flour on his sleeves and a temper like a struck flint, who had trembled so badly ladling the poisoned stew that Ines had noted it in her ledger of small suspicions and thought nothing more of it. He had died eleven days after she left the palace; Tomas had told her, in a letter that arrived by no courier, that Ferren had gone in his sleep with a smile on his face, which the palace physician had called a mercy and Tomas had called nothing at all, because he had learned by then not to call answered prayers mercies.

He looked well. That was the obscenity of it. He looked rested, ruddy, his gray beard trimmed, his eyes bright as a boy's. He stood just beyond the border stones with his hands folded in front of him like a man waiting to be welcomed to supper.

"People of Vhalen!" he called, and his voice carried the way voices carry in chapels, filling every corner without effort. "I bring no harm. I bring only words, and words cost you nothing. May I speak?"

Silence. Two hundred people holding their breath.

"He cannot cross," Marta said loudly, to the crowd, to herself. "Whatever wears that face, it cannot cross."

"No," agreed the thing wearing Ferren's face pleasantly. "I cannot. You've built your wall very well, and I honor it. I'll stand exactly here, and you may stand exactly there, and we will be perfectly safe from each other. Is that not civilized? I have traveled a very long way to be civilized with you."

Ines's skin crawled. It was not the voice — though the voice was wrong in ways she couldn't name, a half-tone too warm, like milk just beginning to turn. It was the *manner*. Poisoners, she had learned, came in two kinds: the ones who wanted you afraid, and the ones who wanted you comfortable. The second kind were always the more skilled. Fear made people check their cups. Comfort made them drink.

She leaned close to her grandmother. "Why him? Why his face?"

Her grandmother's answer came through teeth that barely moved. "Because he was answered. A prayer was spent on him — or through him. The gods wear what they've eaten. Remember that, child. Everything polite at those stones has already been swallowed."

* * *

The second figure stepped forward while Ferren-thing was still smiling, and the crowd's murmur died into something worse than silence.

It was the kitchen boy.

Piet. Fourteen years old, freckled, forever scalding himself and laughing about it. Ines had known him for one season at the palace, had taught him how to tell by smell whether cream was about to turn, and then a prayer-gift had killed him — a silver apple left on the chapel altar, beautiful, irresistible, its flesh full of something that stopped a heart between one bite and the next. She had not been able to save him. She had not even been asked to try. She had stood in the corridor outside the chapel and listened to the screaming stop, and she had added the silver apple to her mental ledger under the column she kept for things she would carry until she died.

Now Piet stood at the border of her homeland, whole and freckled and fourteen forever, and he cupped his hands around his mouth the way boys do, and called:

"Ines! Ines Almeida!"

Her whole body flinched. Her grandmother's hand found hers.

"Don't," the old woman breathed. "Don't answer. Don't nod. Don't even look like you're listening. They feed on being heard — that's the whole of their nature, child. A god is only a prayer that got fat."

But the boy-thing kept calling, gentle, patient, hurt in exactly the way that makes you want to soothe it. "Ines, I'm not angry. I want you to know that. What happened to me wasn't your fault. I've come all this way to tell you that, because I know you carry it, I can *see* you carrying it, you carry everything, that's what you are, that's what you've always been—"

"Stop," Ines whispered, and hated herself for whispering, because whispering was answering, whispering was proof of hearing.

"—and I know you're tired," the boy-thing went on, softer now, almost tender. "I know what the immunity cost you. Your hands shake now. Did you know that? When you think no one's watching. Your heartbeat skips on the third beat, every third beat, like a singer losing the count. We could fix that. We could take all of it back — the numbness, the gray in your eyes, the stutter in your chest. We only want to give things back, Ines. That's all prayer ever was. Someone asking, and someone giving. Your valley forgot the giving. We never did."

The horror of it was not the threat. There was no threat anywhere in the words. The horror was that every word was true — the shaking hands, the skipped beat, the gray creeping in at the edges of her vision since the banquet — and that some vast, patient intelligence had assembled those truths into a shape designed to slide past every defense she owned. It knew her the way her grandmother knew her. Better. Her grandmother knew what Ines did. This thing knew what Ines *felt*.

That was when she understood why they had chosen these faces. Not Ferren, not really, not Piet. They had chosen *guilt*. They had chosen the two deaths she carried, because guilt opens doors faster than love does. Love asks to be let in. Guilt doesn't ask. Guilt stands in the rain looking pitiful until you open the door yourself.

* * *

The third figure waited until the moon cleared the eastern ridge.

It let the crowd stew, let Ferren-thing make small pleasant observations about the weather and the quality of the valley's wheat — *I remember the wheat, we milled Vhalen wheat in the old days, before the maps forgot you* — let Piet-thing fall silent at last with the expression of a boy patiently forgiving someone. The crowd had gone from terror to a horrible, exhausted attentiveness. Ines recognized the progression. It was the same arc a poison victim's family followed at a bedside: rage, bargaining, and then the terrible intimacy of watching something take hold.

Then the third figure walked into the torchlight, and Ines stopped breathing.

"Tomas," she said aloud. Just the name. Just the fact of it, torn out of her.

He looked like Tomas. He looked exactly like Tomas — the steward's stoop, the ink-stained fingers, the kind worried eyes that had confessed to her in a candlelit corridor that he had sent the courier into Vhalen, that the gods had told him where to find a taster who couldn't be killed, that he had sold her to save his own skin and regretted it every day since. He had covered her escape from the palace. He had stood in the gatehouse and lied to armed men with his hands shaking, and the last she had seen of him was his face turning back toward the soldiers who took him, wearing an expression that said: *this is fine, this is what I owe.*

And now here he was at the border of Vhalen, and Ines's heart broke open before her mind could intervene, because she had forgiven him once already and forgiveness, she realized with a lurch, leaves a door propped open.

"Ines." His voice was his voice. Every roughness, every hesitation. "I got free. Or — I was let go. I don't fully know which, and I won't lie to you about that, because lying to you is the sin I'm trying to put down." He spread his hands. "I came to warn you. That's all. Sabine is coming."

The name moved through the crowd like wind through wheat. Somewhere a child began to cry and was hushed.

"She's marching," the Tomas-thing continued, and its face was so precisely his face, so precisely grieved, that Ines had to dig her nails into her palm to keep her footing in the world. "Forty soldiers and the faithful — and there are more faithful than she knows, Ines, half the court prays openly now. She believes if she gives the gods what they want — if she delivers the alto verse, the last voice, *you* — her child will breathe free. She's wrong. But she's coming. Three days, perhaps four. I thought you should have time to run. That's all I came to do. Give you time." He paused, and the pause was pure Tomas, humble and wounded. "Will you let me give you that much?"

* * *

Later — much later, when the figures had withdrawn into the dark beyond the stones, promising politely to return tomorrow, hoping everyone had slept well, wishing the valley a good night, *a good night*, the words hanging in the freezing air like a curse — the elders met in the meetinghouse, and Ines stood at the back and listened to her people come apart.

"We could give them what they ask," said Bramel the miller. He was not a coward; Ines had seen him pull a drowning child from the millrace. He was simply a man who had heard a dead boy speak with love in his voice and could not unhear it. "They haven't demanded anything. Have you noticed? They haven't demanded *anything*. They asked to speak. They offered to heal. They warned us of an army. What exactly is the crime?"

"The crime," said Marta, "is existing."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer there is. You don't negotiate with a flood because it *asks nicely* to come indoors."

"It wore Piet's face," someone else said, younger, shaking. "It knew things only Piet knew. It knew about the cream. It knew about—" and the young woman stopped, stricken, and Ines understood that the thing had spoken to many of them in the hours of standing there, quietly, individually, while the crowd watched the spectacle. It had been working the edges the whole time. Finding the griefs. Filing them.

Her grandmother spoke from the front, and the room stilled, because the old woman had not spoken in council in twenty years.

"You are asking the wrong question," she said. "You ask what they want. I will tell you what they want, because I sealed my own ears against them when I was younger than any of you, and I remember. They want to be let out. That is all. Every gift, every warning, every dead child's voice — it is all one sentence, repeated until we open the door: *let us out.*" She looked at them, one by one, and Ines saw that her grandmother's hands, resting on the table, were trembling — not with age. With effort. "They cannot force the Door. Understand that. Four hundred years, and they have never once forced it. The Door opens from the inside. It has only ever opened from the inside. Which means the war was never at the stones, neighbors. The war is in here." She touched her own breastbone. "And they have just brought their best weapons to bear on it: our dead, and our guilt, and our hope. Sleep if you can. Tomorrow they will be kinder. That is how you will know it's getting worse."

* * *

Ines did not sleep. She sat in the kitchen with the banked fire and the ledger of her mother's hymn open on her knee, and she listened to the house settle, and she thought about doors.

*The Door opens from the inside too.* Her grandmother's letter had told her that weeks ago, and she had understood it then as geography — that the seal worked both directions, that Vhalen was not sheltered but imprisoned alongside its prisoners. She understood it differently now. The gods could not open the Door. But a door that opens from the inside needs only one person inside to choose. One grieving mother. One frightened miller. One girl with dirt under her nails and a dead boy's voice still ringing in her ears saying *we could take all of it back.*

They didn't need to break the lock. They needed her to turn it.

And the worst part — she set down the ledger and pressed her palms against her eyes and admitted it to the dark — the worst part was not the faces. It was that she *wanted* to believe. Some starved animal part of her, the part that had stood at the border stones as a child wondering what it would feel like to be heard, had lit up like a lamp when Piet's voice said her name. Four hundred years of unanswered prayers lived in every soul in this valley, and tonight the answers had finally come walking up the road, wearing the faces of the dead, asking so nicely, and some part of every person in Vhalen was leaning toward them the way frost leans toward warmth.

Three days. Maybe four. Then Sabine's army, and behind the army whatever the gods truly intended, and somewhere in between, the thirteenth turn completing itself overhead like a key sliding home.

The fire ticked. Outside, far off, at the edge of hearing, at the edge of the valley, something began to hum.

It took her a moment to place the melody. When she did, her blood went to ice water.

It was the hymn. Verse by verse, patient and sweet, hummed just beyond the border stones — the map, the Door, the song her mother had died carrying — sung back to her by voices that had no right to it, in the dark, like a lullaby.

Like an invitation.

Ines closed the ledger, and held it against her chest, and made herself a promise in the language her grandmother had taught her for exactly this: *we do not answer.* But as she climbed the stairs to the sleepless bed, one thought followed her up every step, quiet as the humming below:

Tomorrow, they would be kinder.
