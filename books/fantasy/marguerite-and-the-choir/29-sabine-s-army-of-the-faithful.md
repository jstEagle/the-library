## 29. Sabine's Army of the Faithful

The child did not breathe while she slept.

Queen Sabine learned this on the ninth night, when exhaustion finally dragged her under for the length of a bell, and she woke to a silence in the cradle that was worse than crying. She had knelt beside it with her heart hammering out prayers before her eyes were fully open — *breathe, breathe, my love, breathe* — and the small chest had risen, obedient as a courtier, only when the words left her mouth.

So she did not sleep anymore. Not properly. She dozed the way soldiers doze, one hand on the cradle's rail, one ear tuned to that terrible listening quiet, and between the dozes she prayed. The gods had promised her an heir. They had delivered something that required her voice the way a lamp requires oil, and they had never once mentioned that the wick would burn from both ends.

She had stopped thinking of it as a bargain somewhere around the twelfth turn of the moon. It was not a bargain now. It was a debt being collected in installments, and she was the collateral.

* * *

The war council met in the map room at dawn, though there was little enough dawn left these days; the thirteenth turn had swollen the moon fat and yellow as an abscess, and its light bled into the mornings until the sun seemed an afterthought. Sabine stood over the table with her hands flat on the parchment, on the little inked mountains that marked the border of Vhalen, a country her own grandfather's grandfather had signed away and then pretended had never existed.

"The valley cannot be found by ordinary means," said Lord Chancellor Veyre, who had served her father and spoke to her now with the careful gentleness men used for queens they believed were ill. "The roads forget themselves. The couriers who've tried—"

"I know what the couriers have tried." One had come back. Only one, months ago, with a summons that should have been impossible, carrying a name that should have been forgotten. The gods had drawn the map for him. The gods were drawing it again now; she could feel it in the way her prayer book's pages had begun to warm under her hands, eager as a dog hearing its name. "And I know that what we seek is not a country. It is a door."

She let the word sit in the room. Around the table, the lords exchanged glances — fear dressed up as prudence, prudence dressed up as loyalty. They all knew about the answered prayers by now. There was no courtier in the palace who had not seen a gift arrive unasked on some doorstep, or heard of a healing in the lower city that had taken the healed man's shadow in payment. Reverent terror had become the court's native weather.

"Your Majesty," Veyre began, "if I may speak plainly—"

"You may not." She said it without heat, which somehow made it worse. "You may speak usefully. Tell me how many soldiers can march in three days."

"Four thousand. Perhaps five, if we strip the garrisons."

"Strip them."

"Your Majesty, the northern border—"

"The northern border is guarded by men who pray to gods that are currently answering." She lifted her eyes from the map and let the silence do its work. "There is no northern border, Chancellor. There is only the moon, and what comes when it turns. We are marching toward the only place in this kingdom where anyone has ever known how to shut a door."

That much was true. That much she clung to like a railing in dark water. It was even, in its way, the truth entire: she told herself every night that she rode to close the Door, to end the answering, to save her dynasty from gods that meant to wear it like a coat. She had burned her prayer book. She had renounced them. She was marching as a queen, not a supplicant.

But beneath the truth, the way silt lies beneath clear water, was the other thing. The thing she did not say aloud even in her own skull.

The gods wanted the alto. The gods needed the vessel. And if the vessel were given — freely, publicly, irreversibly — then perhaps the debt would be paid, and the leash would slip from her child's throat, and she could sleep.

* * *

They marched on the third morning, four thousand strong, and Sabine rode at the head of them because a queen who sends soldiers where she will not go herself has already lost whatever she was fighting for.

The army was not what armies were supposed to be. Half her officers had taken to wearing small wooden charms carved in shapes no herald would recognize — knots, doors, open mouths. The men sang on the road, but not marching songs; they sang petitions, low and ragged, asking the old gods for safe passage, and Sabine did not forbid it because forbidding it would mean admitting she could hear every word.

By the second day she understood that she was not leading an army so much as delivering one. The faithful had attached themselves to the column like burrs — pilgrims, penitents, mothers carrying sick children toward the valley where prayers went unanswered, believing perhaps that nearness to the Door might work some opposite miracle. They walked at the edges of the formation with their eyes fixed on the swollen moon, and when Sabine passed they did not cheer. They bowed. Deeply. The way one bows to an altar.

She hated it. She hated how much a part of her accepted it.

On the third night, camped within sight of the hills that hid Vhalen, the god came to her tent.

It wore the face of the kitchen boy — the one whose answered prayer had killed him, back at the beginning, when all of this was still deniable. He had been fourteen. She remembered his name, Pellam, because she had made herself remember it, the way she made herself remember the names of everyone the gods had spent. The face was his face, gentle and slightly stupid with youth, but the eyes behind it were patient as sediment.

"You're going the wrong way about it," the boy's mouth said. It sounded almost kind.

"Get out of my tent."

"You burned your book. Very dramatic. Very final." The boy sat down on her campaign chest uninvited, folding his hands like a visiting nephew. "But you kept the last page. You tore it out and sewed it into your sleeve. Did you think we wouldn't know our own words?"

Her hand moved to her cuff before she could stop it. Inside the linen, folded eight times, was the promise the gods had written in their own hand: *a living child, in exchange for the choir.* Forty voices. She had promised forty voices, and thirty-nine of them had deserted her, run off into the night after Odile's arrest, scattered like startled birds — and the fortieth, the alto, the vessel, had fled home.

"We don't want your soldiers," the god said through the boy. "Soldiers are loud. Soldiers break things we're still using. Bring us what you promised, or bring us nothing, but know that the child breathes on your voice alone, and your voice is getting tired, Sabine. We can hear it fraying from here."

"How do I free him?" The question came out of her raw and fast, before pride could catch it. "Tell me how to free my son."

The boy's face smiled, and it was the smile that undid her — because it was not cruel. It was relieved. The way a creditor smiles when the debtor finally asks the right question.

"Give us the girl. The lock must be opened from the inside. Put her at the stones, put her among the singers' ghosts, and when the moon turns, hold her there. What happens after is not your concern. Your part ends. His begins." The boy stood, brushing off his knees like any farm lad. At the tent flap he paused. "You'll tell yourself it's statecraft. They always do. But you'll know, Sabine. You'll carry it the way you carry everything else. Alone, at night, next to a cradle."

Then he was gone, and the tent was just a tent, smelling of canvas and horse, and Sabine sat alone with her hand pressed flat against her sleeve, over the folded page, over the words she had never been able to burn.

* * *

They reached the border stones at dusk on the fifth day.

The valley opened below them like a held breath — green, ordinary, lamplit, utterly unremarkable, which was itself the most remarkable thing about it. Four hundred years erased from every map, and it looked like any other pocket of farmland going gold in the evening. Smoke rose from chimneys. Somewhere a dog barked. Sabine stared down at it and felt the strange vertigo of looking at something that had been officially nothing.

The border stones stood in a broken crescent at the valley's mouth, and they were cracked. She could see the cracks from the ridge, pale seams running through the old granite like veins in marble, and from the deepest of them seeped a light that was not light — a shimmer, a wrongness, the way air looks above a fire. Magic leaking out of a country that had been built to keep it in. Or to keep it *off*, she corrected herself. Her grandmother's grandmother would have known which. Sabine only knew what the gods had told her, and the gods lied the way rivers ran: constantly, and downhill.

Below the stones, figures had gathered. Small at this distance. A handful of villagers, an old woman standing very straight, and — her chest tightened — a cluster of robed singers who had no business being there, forty-odd deserters who had marched ahead of her army or around it, drawn to the stones like iron filings to a lodestone. And at their center, unmistakable even at half a mile, the girl. Ines Almeida. The taster. The lock.

Sabine raised her hand, and the army halted behind her with a sound like the sea drawing back.

This was the moment. She felt it settle over her shoulders like a mantle cut too heavy. Below her, the girl turned and looked up the ridge — Sabine would swear later that across that whole distance, their eyes met — and the old woman beside her stepped forward, planting herself between the valley and the army like a fence post grown from bedrock.

*Lesser evil,* Sabine told herself, and the words rang hollow as a cracked bell. *One life weighed against a kingdom. One girl against my son.*

But the boy's voice was still in her ear, patient as sediment: *you'll know, Sabine. You'll carry it alone.*

She thought of her son's chest, rising and falling only at the rhythm of her voice. She thought of the kitchen boy, Pellam, fourteen years old, dead of a gift. She thought of the girl below, who had swallowed poison for strangers her whole life and never once been thanked properly.

"One life," she said aloud, to no one, to the yellow moon already climbing over the hills, "against everything."

The moon did not answer. But behind her, four thousand throats had begun, softly, horribly, to sing.
