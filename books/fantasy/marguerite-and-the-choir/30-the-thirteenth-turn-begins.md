## 30. The Thirteenth Turn Begins

The moon did not rise that night so much as arrive.

Ines stood at the window of her childhood room — the narrow one with the shelf of clay cups she had used for her first immunity draughts — and watched it clear the eastern ridge of Vhalen like a thing climbing out of a grave. It was swollen past fullness, bruised at the edges, and it moved wrong: too fast, too low, dragging its light across the valley floor as though it were searching for something it had lost four hundred years ago.

Behind her, in the kitchen, her grandmother was sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening.

"You should sleep," Mireia said without turning around.

"I don't think anyone in this valley is going to sleep tonight."

Her grandmother set the knife down. The whetstone's rhythm stopped, and Ines realized how long she had been leaning on that sound — the small, stubborn industry of it, the way it said *we are still here, we still prepare, we still have hands*. Without it the house was very quiet, and under the quiet was something else. Something Ines had been hearing since sundown and had not yet found the courage to name.

A hum. Faint as a hairline crack in glass. Not in the house. In everything.

"Grandmother," she said. "What is that sound?"

Mireia came to the window. Up close, in the moon's strange light, her face looked older than it had a week ago — as if the days since the border stones cracked had been counted against her skin at double rate. She looked out at the valley: the terraced fields gone silver, the orchards, the river running pale as spilled milk, and beyond them all, the ring of standing stones on the high pasture, each one fissured now, each one leaking a thin luminous fog that clung to the ground like breath on a winter morning.

"That," her grandmother said, "is the sound of four hundred years."

"Of what?"

"Of everything we ever asked for." Her hands folded together, knuckles like river stones. "Sit down, child. And when it starts — because it will start — I need you to remember one thing. None of it is speaking *to* you. That is the only mercy left in this night."

* * *

It began, as most terrible things do, with something small.

The neighbor's dog started barking at nothing — or rather, at the well. Old Petre came out in his nightshirt to quiet it, and Ines, watching from the window, saw him stop mid-shout and go rigid, his head tilting toward the well mouth the way a man tilts toward a voice he half-recognizes. Then he crossed himself, which no one in Vhalen had done in living memory, because crossing yourself was a prayer-shaped gesture and prayer-shaped gestures were pointless here, everyone knew that, everyone had always known that—

Except the knowing was coming apart.

Because Petre knelt down by the well, put both hands over his face, and began to weep — loudly, helplessly, the way men weep when they have been holding something for decades and the holding finally fails. And through the open window, across the silvered distance between houses, Ines heard him say:

"—let her live, please, let her live, she's only six, take me instead, I'll give anything, I've given everything, why won't you *answer*—"

The words hung in the cold air. They did not fade. They repeated.

Not an echo — echoes degrade. This was the prayer itself, replaying, looping, layering over itself until Petre's voice became a chorus of Petres, six years old and sixty, all of them begging at the lip of the well where his daughter had drowned before Ines was born. Ines knew the story. Everyone in Vhalen knew the story. What no one in Vhalen had ever known — what had been taken from them along with their maps and their treaties and their magic — was that the asking had never stopped. It had only gone somewhere they couldn't hear.

Now the somewhere was opening.

Down the lane, the widow Sera stepped onto her porch carrying a lamp, and from her open door poured another voice — hers, younger, raw: *bring him home from the war, bring him home, he promised, he promised—* and beneath it, threaded through it like a darker thread in cloth, a man's voice saying *I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I'm sorry* in the cadence of someone who has said it every day for forty years and never once been heard.

"It's the silence," Mireia said behind her. Her voice was steady the way a rope bridge is steady — functional, terrifying. "Every prayer ever spoken inside these stones went nowhere. But *nowhere* isn't empty, Ines. Nowhere is a room. And the room is full."

"How can I hear them? I'm not—" Ines stopped. Swallowed. "I'm not from here. Not entirely."

"You are more from here than you know." Her grandmother's hand settled on her shoulder, heavy and warm. "You are the lock. Locks are made of the same material as doors. When the Door stirs, everything attached to it stirs with it."

Outside, the valley was waking — not to danger, not yet, but to grief. Doors opened along the whole curve of the village street. Lamplight bloomed in windows. And from every threshold, voices: the accumulated unanswered wanting of four centuries, spilling out of wells and walls and fence posts and the hollow places under old bridges, each prayer finding its speaker again, or finding a stranger to speak itself through. A child's voice from the churchyard asking for rain during the drought year. An old woman's voice from the millrace thanking a god who had never listened for a harvest that had come anyway. A young man's voice, furious and broken, from the border stones themselves: *if you're real, curse me, curse me, anything but this silence—*

And underneath all of it, beneath the human voices, something else. Something that made the hairs on Ines's arms lift and her teeth ache at their roots. A sound below sound. The gods pacing behind the stones like wolves behind a fence, and the fence singing under their weight.

* * *

They walked out into it, because there was nowhere else to go and because Mireia said, "If the valley must scream, it should not scream alone," and Ines understood that this was the closest thing to theology her grandmother had ever offered her.

The village square was full of people who had not gathered so much as leaked together, drawn out of their houses by the voices of their own dead hopes. Petre sat on the well edge with his daughter's name in his mouth like a stone he could finally spit out. The widow Sera held her lamp up as if light were an argument. Children cried, not understanding, and their crying joined the chorus, and the chorus took it, and gave it back multiplied.

Ines felt it enter her through the soles of her feet.

That was the horror of it — not that the prayers were loud, but that they were *familiar*. Because she had grown up inside this silence. She had been shaped by it, the way a tree is shaped by wind. Every unanswered wish of her own life — the childish ones, the shameful ones, the ones she'd whispered into her pillow at fourteen wishing for a mother, for magic, for a world larger than the valley — all of them rose up now out of the ground beneath her and spoke themselves back to her in her own voice, aged and layered, a geology of longing.

*I want to be heard.*

She staggered. Mireia caught her elbow.

"They're not attacking us," Ines gasped. "They're just — they're *here*, they were always here—"

"The cruelest prison is one where the prisoners forget they're imprisoned." Her grandmother's eyes were wet but her voice didn't waver. "We told ourselves the silence was peace. We told ourselves the gods had abandoned us, and that abandonment was safety. We built a whole country on that lie and called it contentment." She looked up at the bruised moon, now halfway up the sky and visibly, horribly, moving faster than it should. "And now the thirteenth turn begins, and the lie has run out of room."

Above the standing stones, the air had begun to shimmer — not with the soft leak-fog of the past weeks but with something structured, something with edges. Shapes moving behind the shimmer. Tall shapes. Patient shapes. And Ines, who had spent her life learning to taste what could not be seen, tasted the air off the ridge when the wind turned:

Hunger. Old, vast, disciplined hunger. The kind that has learned manners.

"They know," she said. "The thirteenth turn — it's not just an omen, is it. It's a schedule. Theirs."

"Four hundred years ago my grandmother's grandmother helped seal them behind these stones," Mireia said. "The seal was never meant to be permanent. It was meant to be *maintained*. A lock needs tending. A song needs singing." Her hand tightened on Ines's arm. "Your mother went out to find the song. You went out and found it. And now the last turn of the moon is the last turn of the lock, and everything the valley ever swallowed is coming up at once — because the valley knows, child. Even the stones know. Everything in this place understands that the end of the silence is coming, and it cannot tell anymore whether that ending will be a rescue or a breach."

One of the shapes at the ridge turned its head.

It had no face that Ines could see, and yet she felt seen — felt known the way poison knows a body, entering at the weakest point, seeking the organ that will carry it deepest. She thought of Tomas covering her escape. She thought of Odile in a cell, transcribing verses from memory with a nail. She thought of Sabine marching somewhere in the dark with an army and a wrong idea, and of the choir — her choir — walking toward the border with forty throats and one hymn.

The shape raised something like a hand. And across the whole valley, every voice, every prayer, every looped and layered grief fell silent at once —

— and in that silence, perfectly clear, wearing her mother's cadence if not her mother's voice, something said:

*Almost time, little lock.*

Then the prayers came back louder than before, and the moon cleared the ridge-top entirely, and the thirteenth turn had begun.

Mireia pulled her toward the house. "Come inside. There's something I have to teach you before dawn — the last thing your line kept, the thing we sang you as a lullaby without telling you what it was."

"What is it?" Ines asked, digging in her heels, looking back at the ridge, at the shimmer, at the hungry patience of the things behind it.

Her grandmother's face in the moonlight was terrible and calm.

"The alto verse," she said. "It isn't a map to the Door, Ines. It never was."
