## 31. The Alto Verse

The grave was still there when I woke, a dark seam in the frost-browned grass behind my grandmother's house, and I had stopped pretending I didn't know whose name would go on the stone. My mother had been dead for twenty years and unmarked for all of them, and now, in the last days before the moon finished its impossible arithmetic, my grandmother had chosen to dig.

She was in the kitchen when I came down, standing at the table with her back to me, her hands flat on the wood as if she were holding the house down against a wind only she could feel. The fire was lit. Two cups sat waiting. She had known I would come before I knew it myself — that was always the way of her, the way of our line, though I had not yet learned to call it anything but stubbornness.

"You didn't sleep," she said.

"Neither did you."

"I slept enough." She turned. In the firelight her face looked carved rather than aged, the lines deep as plow furrows, and her eyes — my mother's eyes, my eyes — held something I had never seen in them before. Not fear. Not grief, exactly. Something older than both. *Readiness.*

"The moon begins its thirteenth turn tonight," she said. "Sit down, Ines. It's time you learned what we've been singing."

* * *

I want to say I understood immediately. I want to say the truth arrived like light through a window, clean and whole. But the truth came the way most truths come in Vhalen: slowly, sideways, wrapped in something familiar until you bit through and found the bone.

My grandmother poured the tea — yarrow and pine, the same blend she'd made every winter morning of my childhood — and then she did not drink hers. She folded her hands around the cup instead, warming them, and looked at me across the steam.

"What do you remember of the lullaby?" she asked.

It seemed an absurd question. I had been singing it since before I could speak. "All of it," I said. "You sang it every night. When I couldn't sleep at the palace, I sang it to myself." I hummed the first phrase without thinking, low, almost under my breath — and the kettle on the stove shivered. A fine tremor ran through the iron, and the flames in the hearth leaned toward me like grass bending under wind.

My grandmother's hand closed over mine, and the humming stopped.

"Not here," she said quietly. "Not yet. Come outside first. There's something you need to see with your eyes open."

* * *

We walked up past the terraced fields, past the orchard where the apples hung frozen on their branches like small red lanterns, up to the ridge where the border stones stood in their ancient, patient line. The valley spread below us in its winter grays and browns, smoke rising straight from forty chimneys, everything still and ordered and *sealed*. Above us the sky was the color of pewter, and the moon hung in it pale as a fingernail paring, one turn from done.

The stones themselves had changed since I'd crossed them going out. Then they had been merely old — lichen-eaten granite, waist-high, unremarkable except for what they meant. Now cracks ran through them like lightning frozen mid-strike, and from those cracks seeped a faint luminescence, a glow like foxfire, like the memory of light rather than light itself. Magic, leaking in. Or leaking *out*. I no longer knew which direction it traveled, and I suspected that question had never had an answer.

"When your mother was your age," my grandmother said, "she stood on this exact spot. Right there, where the third stone has split. And she asked me the same question you're about to ask me."

"Which is?"

"Why the lullaby works outside the valley when nothing else does."

I hadn't been about to ask it. But hearing it spoken, I realized I had been carrying it since the chapel in the palace, since the moment verse eleven left my mouth and the wall opened like a held breath released. Every hymn Odile had transcribed mapped the country that no longer existed. Every verse marked a place — a ford, a hill, a threshold. The hymn was a map. She'd spent thirty years proving it.

But the lullaby wasn't in Odile's ledger. The lullaby had never been written down at all.

"It's not in the map," I said slowly, working it through aloud, "because—"

"Because maps point to things," my grandmother said. "And the alto verse doesn't point." She crouched by the cracked third stone and laid her palm against it, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "Your whole life, Ines, you've thought of yourself as someone looking for a door. Your mother too. Her mother before her, all the way back to the priestess who sang the valley shut. We taught our daughters the song and let them believe it was a key they might someday find a lock for." Her voice caught — actually caught, my granite grandmother, my unbreakable woman — and steadied. "We lied. Kindly. The way you lie to children about where meat comes from. But we lied."

The wind moved along the ridge and carried the smell of snow coming. Below us, a dog barked twice and went quiet. Everything ordinary. Everything about to end.

"The priestess didn't hide the Door somewhere in Vhalen," my grandmother said. "She didn't bury it under a stone or sink it in the lake or fold it into a wall of the old temple. You can't hide a Door from gods, child. They are patient beyond patience. They would have found it in a century. In ten years. So she did the only thing that could not be found." She looked up at me, and the foxfire glow from the stones lit her face from below, making her look suddenly, terribly young. "She became it."

The cold went through me then, deeper than weather.

"She sang herself into the lock," I whispered. "The sealing — the ritual —"

"The ritual needed a living threshold. A body that magic could pass through and be bound within. She gave hers. And because a Door made of one body dies with that body, she passed the keeping of it down. Mother to daughter. Voice to voice. Four hundred years of women teaching their daughters a lullaby." My grandmother rose, joints cracking, and faced me fully. "Every woman in our line has carried the Door inside her chest. Your mother carried it. She was sent out of the valley to close it from the other side, and she failed, and she died trying, and the Door passed to you the hour you drew your first breath."

"And the song," I said. My own voice sounded far away, underwater. "The lullaby isn't about the Door."

"No."

"It *is* the Door. Sung, it opens. Sung backward, or broken, or—" I thought of the god wearing my mother's voice, offering me verses like coins. "That's why they want it. That's why they've wanted it for four hundred years. The map tells them where. The alto verse tells them *how*."

My grandmother nodded once, slow as a stone rolling.

"They don't need to break the border stones," I said. "They don't need Sabine's army or the choir or the thirteenth moon. They just need one of us, alive and willing, singing the whole verse with meaning. Singing it *open*."

"Now you understand why we never wrote it down. Why I taught it to you as nonsense syllables when you were small — pretty sounds, a song about a nightingale and a river, nothing more. The words only become the Door when the singer knows what they mean. Knowing is the hinge." She reached out and took both my hands in hers. Her skin was papery and warm and shaking very slightly, and I understood that she had been afraid my entire life, every single day, in the quiet methodical way of people guarding a secret that would end the world if it slipped. "And now you know. And I am so sorry for it, Ines. I am sorry in the marrow of me. But knowing was always coming for you, and better it come from me than from *them*."

* * *

We went back down to the kitchen, and she taught me.

Not the melody — I had carried the melody in my bones since infancy. She taught me the *meaning*, word by word, and each word landed in me like a stone dropped into deep water, the ripples spreading outward through everything I thought I was.

The nightingale was the priestess. Of course it was. *The nightingale sings in a cage of her own building* — the valley, sealed by its own guardian, a prison chosen freely. *The river runs beneath the floor and does not wet the boards* — magic flowing beneath the valley, imprisoned alongside it, the truth my grandmother's letter had hinted and I had refused to see. *Open the little window, open the little door, let the singer out, let the singers in.* I had sung that line ten thousand times into my pillow, drowsy and safe, while it waited inside me like a blade wrapped in wool.

"Say it back," my grandmother said, after the third hour. "Not sung. Spoken. Tell me what the song says."

I told her. My voice stayed level through most of it. It broke on the last part — *the door is not in the wall, the door is the throat that sings it, and the throat must be willing* — and my grandmother reached across the table and pressed her thumb hard against my sternum, right over my heart, over whatever lived there now that I knew it was architecture and not flesh alone.

"Willing," she repeated. "That word matters more than all the others. The Door cannot be forced open from either side. Your mother proved that with her death — the gods took her at the border, they tried to make her sing it open, and she kept her mouth shut around the meaning until they burned the meaning out of her. She saved the world stupidly, stubbornly, magnificently, and no one ever marked her grave." Her eyes went bright and fierce. "Until now."

"Grandmother." I made myself say it. Made myself look at her while I said it. "If I sing it — if I open it —"

"Then you will be the threshold, and everything that crosses will cross *through you*." She said it plainly, without flinching, because she had had twenty years to practice saying it and had practiced on my mother's ghost every night of them. "I won't lie to you anymore. That's what opening means. That's what it has always meant, for every woman in our line who ever hoped it wouldn't come to this."

I sat with that. Outside, the light was failing; the moon was climbing, fat and wrong, beginning its final arc. Somewhere beyond the ridge, beyond the leaking stones, things that wore the faces of answered prayers were walking toward us, asking nicely. Somewhere south, a queen marched with an army and a stolen hope. And somewhere on that road, if Tomas's last letter told true, forty voices were deserting their queen and carrying a hymn home.

They were coming to sing the map. They didn't yet know the map ended in me.

"There's another thing," my grandmother said, and her voice changed — softened, the way it used to when I was sick as a child and she sat up with me through fevers. "When your mother left, I taught her the verse the same way I've taught you. And at the gate she asked me to sing it with her, once, together, the way we used to when she was small. Mother's line and daughter's line, the harmony our blood was built for." She swallowed. "I refused. I told her there was no time. I have regretted it every day for twenty years, and I will not make that mistake twice."

She stood. Held out her hand.

"So before the moon finishes, before the choir comes, before any of it — we sing. Once. For no purpose at all except that a mother should sing with her daughter while she still can."

* * *

We sang in the kitchen, by firelight, with the door barred and the frost flowering on the windows.

I took the melody, as I always had. She took the line beneath it — the low, patient thread I had never known was missing from every version of the song I'd ever sung alone, the harmonic foundation that made the nonsense syllables into sense. Our voices found each other the way water finds its level, immediate and inevitable, and the sound we made together was not beautiful, exactly. It was *true*, which is a different thing and rarer.

The fire stood up straight and tall. The frost on the windows melted into running water though the night outside was bitter. Under the floorboards, beneath the foundations, beneath the roots of the valley itself, something vast turned over in its sleep — and I felt it turn, felt it in the new architecture of my chest, the Door feeling its own hinges tested, gentle, gentle, only a test, only a mother and daughter remembering what they were.

And the gods beyond the stones heard it.

I knew it the way you know a change in pressure before a storm. The humming under the earth paused. Every crack in every border stone flared at once, a heartbeat of white light, and in that light I heard — faint, miles off, at the edge of hearing — a sound like forty throats inhaling at once. Not the gods. Something else. Something human and approaching, footsore and defiant, carrying a hymn home through the dark.

My grandmother and I stopped singing. The fire settled. She looked at me across the table, and for the first time since I'd come home, she smiled — a small, tired, unguarded smile, gone almost before it arrived.

"They're here," she said. "Early. The fools. The brave, dear fools." She was already moving, pulling her coat from its peg, taking down the lamp. "Come, Ines. If your mother's choir has walked all this way to sing the map, then it falls to us to tell them where the map ends."

I followed her out into the blue dark, toward the ridge, toward the stones, toward the thin bright line of voices coming up the valley road — and beneath my ribs, patient as a held breath, the Door waited to be sung.
