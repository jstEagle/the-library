## 32. The Choir Gathers

They came out of the dark like a procession of candles.

Ines saw them first as light — dozens of small flames bobbing along the road that wound down from the pass, where for four hundred years no road had led anywhere worth going. Then the light resolved into lanterns, and the lanterns into hands, and the hands into people: forty of them, walking in loose formation through the frost-burned grass, singing.

Not the hymn. Not yet. A walking song, something low and workmanlike that Ines did not recognize, but she felt it in her sternum before she heard it with her ears, and behind her she heard her grandmother's breath catch the way it always caught when the valley remembered something it had been made to forget.

"They're coming up the pilgrim's path," Odile said beside her. The old choirmaster had been freed from her cell only three days ago, and prison had carved new lines into her face, but her voice was as dry and certain as ever. "I taught them that song when they were novices. It's what we sang to keep time on long processions. They're keeping time." She paused. "They're afraid, and they're keeping time anyway. That's discipline. That's what I built."

The border stones stood in their broken ring around the valley's mouth, cracked down their centers since Ines had come home, weeping thin threads of light like sap from wounded trees. Magic leaked through the gaps now — Ines could feel it on her skin whenever the wind came off the pass, cold water again, the same brush she'd felt the day she'd first crossed out. Her grandmother said the valley tasted different. Said the bread rose wrong, or right, depending who you asked. Said the goats were giving milk that glowed faintly if you left it in the dark too long, and that they should probably stop drinking it, and that everyone kept drinking it anyway.

The choir reached the outermost stone and stopped.

For a long moment there was only the wind and the hiss of forty lanterns and the sound of forty people breathing hard from the climb. Ines counted them twice, because counting was what she did, because counting had kept her alive through every poisoned dish and every answered prayer: thirty-eight. Two short.

Then two figures detached themselves from the rear of the column and came forward together, one supporting the other, and Ines stopped counting because her heart had forgotten how.

"Tomas," she said.

He looked worse than she had let herself imagine. When the courtiers had taken him on the palace steps — when he had turned to face them so she could run — she had told herself the gods wouldn't kill him, that they wanted him the way a farmer wants an ox, whole enough to work. She had been right, in the narrow sense. He was alive. He was walking. But whatever the gods had done to him in the weeks between, they had done thoroughly. His left hand was wrapped in linen to the wrist. His eyes had gone pale at the edges, the irises bleached like things left too long in sun, and when he blinked it was slow, deliberate, the blink of someone relearning a skill. The man beside him — a bass from the second row whose name Ines half-remembered as Corvin — held his elbow and steered him gently over the uneven ground.

"I can see you," Tomas said, before she could speak. His voice was hoarse but it was his voice, wry underneath the ruin. "Mostly. You're a blur with a lantern. But I'd know your outline anywhere. I've spent a year watching you eat things that should have killed you. A man memorizes that silhouette."

"What did they do to you?"

"Showed me things." He said it simply, the way he reported kitchen inventories. "That was the punishment for helping you run. Or the gift. They weren't clear on which, and honestly neither am I." He lifted the bandaged hand slightly. "This is from touching a prayer-gift without a taster. I thought — after everything, after watching you survive all of it — I thought maybe some of it had rubbed off on me." A ghost of his old smile. "It hadn't. Immunity isn't contagious. Turns out that's the entire point of you."

Behind her, her grandmother made a small sound, and Ines realized the old woman had come down from the house without being asked, wrapped in her shawl, standing among the border stones like a fourth stone herself, older and less cracked.

"You brought them through the pass," her grandmother said to Odile. "Forty voices. Across open country. With the moon one turn from full and every god in creation leaning toward this valley like wheat toward sun."

"I brought them because they refused to stay," Odile said. "I've conducted choirs for fifty years, madam. I know when the music is pulling the singers rather than the other way around. By the time they released me, half my altos were already gone. Deserted. Left notes." Her mouth twitched. "One of them left a note apologizing for leaving a note. That's the caliber of person I train."

* * *

They made camp inside the ring of stones, because there was nowhere else and because — though no one said it aloud — everyone understood that the valley's interior was the only ground in the world where the gods' attention came filtered, diluted, passed through the lock of Ines's bloodline before it touched anything living. Inside Vhalen, the voices were quieter. Ines watched the choir members realize this one by one as they set down their packs: the moment each of them crossed the last stone, their shoulders dropped a fraction, their breathing deepened, and several of them looked around with the startled expression of people stepping out of a storm into a doorway.

"It's silent here," whispered a soprano named Liss, who couldn't have been more than nineteen. "How is it silent? We could hear them on the road. All night. Like — like moths against glass."

"The valley was built to be silent," Ines's grandmother said, moving among them with a kettle of barley tea, and something in her tone made even the adults sit straighter. "Four hundred years ago a priestess decided that silence was kinder than answers. You may thank her or curse her as you like. Supper's at the fire."

Ines found Odile at the ring's edge afterward, standing where the cracked stone wept its thin light onto the grass, holding her ledger against her chest like a breastplate. Thirty years of transcription. Every verse mapped to a place in a country that no longer existed, except the last verse, the alto line, the one that wasn't a location at all.

"They'll sing it," Odile said, without turning. "You're wondering whether frightened people will actually do it, when it comes to the moment. They will. Fear makes choirs better, not worse. Fear makes them listen to each other." She glanced sideways. "What I'm wondering is whether you understand what happens after. The Door opens in you, girl. Whatever comes through comes through *you*. I've written music for cathedrals. I know what a vessel has to be shaped like to hold that much sound."

"I'm a taster," Ines said. "Holding things that should kill me is the one thing I've ever been good at."

Odile studied her for a long moment, lantern-light moving across the ruined map of her face. "Yes," she said finally. "I believe that's exactly what your mother said."

* * *

Tomas found her later, past midnight, sitting alone by the cold hearth in her grandmother's house because sleep had become a country she visited rarely and never willingly. He navigated the dark room by touch, pale eyes catching what little light the embers offered, and lowered himself onto the bench across from her with the careful movements of a man whose body had been returned to him with some parts missing.

"Corvin says I should sleep facing the door," he said. "Says the gods wear faces now. Dead cook, dead kitchen boy." He flexed the bandaged hand. "Me, eventually, if they get hungry enough. He thinks I'll look up one night and see myself asking to be let in."

"Do you?" Ines asked. "Sleep facing the door?"

"I haven't slept in nine days." He said it without drama, an inventory item. "Something about what they showed me. Sleep feels like opening a window in a house where something's waiting outside. So I don't open windows anymore." He was quiet a moment, and then, in a lower voice: "I want to tell you what I saw. While I was theirs. Because you're going to stand in a doorway soon yourself, and nobody's told you what's on the other side, and I think someone owes you the truth even if the truth is useless."

Ines waited. Outside, faintly, she could hear the choir's sentries walking their rounds, humming to keep the fear at bay, and beneath the humming the deeper hum of the border stones, patient as digestion.

"They showed me the valley," Tomas said. "From the outside. From their side of it. Four hundred years of it, all at once." His good hand opened and closed slowly on the table. "Do you know what a sealed country looks like to a starving god? It looks like a jar of honey welded shut in a famine. They didn't hate Vhalen, Ines. Hate would have been simpler. They *wanted* it. The way you want water. And every prayer those people ever spoke inside the border — every unanswered prayer your grandmother told you about — it didn't vanish. It pooled. Against the inside of the seal. Four hundred years of prayers, pressed flat against the Door like flowers in a book." He looked up at her, and his bleached eyes were wet. "I heard them. That's what they showed me. All of it at once. Your grandmother's prayers. Your mother's. Yours — the ones you said into your pillow when you were small, wondering what it would feel like to be heard." His voice broke and he mastered it, taster's discipline, kitchen-boy stoicism. "Someone heard all of them, Ines. They were heard the whole time. Just not by anything kind."

The fire ticked. Somewhere in the rafters the house settled, groaning like an old ship.

"That's why they need me," Ines said quietly. "Not the Door. Me. The only vessel that can carry them out and live."

"And tomorrow night they'll offer you anything to walk through carrying them," Tomas said. "And they'll make the offer in voices you love. So." He pushed himself upright, swaying slightly, and managed the ghost of his smile again. "I'll be facing the door tomorrow. Whatever's wearing my face on the other side of it — I'd like to be awake to tell it that it got the details wrong."

* * *

Dawn came gray and enormous over the pass, and with it the moon — visible even in daylight now, swollen past reason, three-quarters of its final turn complete and visibly still growing, as though the sky itself were inhaling. The choir gathered at the border stones in the early light, forty voices minus none, plus one: Odile had taken the alto line herself overnight, working by candle, learning by rote the verse Ines's grandmother had finally surrendered — the lullaby, the map that was not a map, the Door that was not a place.

"We rehearse once," Odile announced, planting herself before them like a general before a hopeless battle she intended to win anyway. "Once, in daylight, in safety, with mistakes allowed. Tonight there will be no mistakes because tonight there will be no second chances, and I refuse to conduct a hymn under those conditions without having heard it whole at least one time in my life." She raised her hands. "From the top. And singers — when we reach the eleventh verse, watch our taster. Watch Ines. The hymn was always going to end in her. Let her hear, tonight, what she's carrying us toward."

Forty throats opened. The first note went up into the gray morning like a bird released from a long captivity, and the border stones began, softly, to sing back.

Ines stood among them — not singing, not yet, saving the alto line for the hour it was made for — and felt the harmony move through the broken ring of stones and out across the valley, waking things that had slept for four hundred years. The weeping cracks in the granite brightened. The goats fell silent in their paddock. Far up the valley, at the mouth of the old mine, something that might have been an echo answered a phrase and then fell shyly quiet.

And then, from beyond the pass — from the direction of the capital, down the pilgrim's road the choir had climbed — came the sound of drums.

Many drums. Marching feet. The disciplined tread of an army that had learned, somewhere between here and the palace, to keep time to a hymn it had never been taught.

The choir faltered. Odile's hands hung in the air, holding a chord that no one was singing anymore. Ines turned toward the pass, and the wind coming down it carried the smell of woodsmoke and steel and, beneath both, faintly, sweetly, unmistakably, the scent of answered prayers.

Her grandmother appeared at her shoulder, squinting into the distance, and said, in the tone she used for reporting a spoiled harvest:

"Well. She brought the whole court."
