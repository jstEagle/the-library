## 33. Sabine at the Stones

The moon hung low and swollen over Vhalen's border stones, one night shy of its thirteenth turn, and Queen Sabine thought: *I have walked through fire for less than this.*

She had not slept in nine days. She no longer needed to. Sleep was a country she had surrendered her passport to, because every hour her eyes closed was an hour the child's breath thinned in his cradle. She had learned to pray walking, praying eating, praying mid-sentence with ambassadors whose names she could no longer be troubled to remember. The words had worn grooves into her the way water wears stone — *breathe, breathe, let him breathe* — until she sometimes caught herself mouthing them during audiences, her lips moving like a woman chewing on a prayer that would not go down.

Her soldiers flanked her now, two hundred of them, torchlight shivering off their breastplates. Behind them, the faithful — courtiers who had once sneered at old gods and now knelt in the frost with their foreheads to the earth. Sabine heard them murmuring behind her, a sound like wind through a wheat field, and she did not correct their theology. Let them believe what they liked. Belief was ballast. She needed the ship moving forward.

Ahead, past the last of the torches, the border stones of Vhalen rose from the ground like broken teeth — waist-high, moss-eaten, unremarkable except for the way the air above them *shimmered*, faintly, as though the valley beyond were seen through heat-haze or held behind glass. Her grandmother's grandmother had signed away this valley without ever seeing it. Sabine had read the treaty as a girl and thought it a curiosity. Now she understood it for what it was: a cage door, and someone had thrown away the key on purpose.

And the key was standing on the other side.

She saw Ines Almeida before Ines saw her — a slim figure in traveling wool, dark hair loose, standing between two of the cracked stones with her hands at her sides. Not armed. Not flanked. Alone, except for an old woman beside her who stood with the stillness of a fence post, and, further back, a scatter of figures Sabine recognized with a jolt: choir robes, forty of them, pale as moths in the dark. Odile among them, freed from whatever cell they'd kept her in, her silver braid catching torchlight.

They had come out to meet her. That was either surrender or invitation, and Sabine had learned, these past months, that the difference between those two things was often just wording.

"Your Majesty." Ines's voice carried across the frost-flat ground without effort. "You've brought an army to a border that's older than your crown."

"I've brought an army to a negotiation," Sabine said, and was distantly surprised at how steady her own voice sounded. Nine days without sleep and her voice held. Perhaps that was what the gods gave you in exchange for everything else. "Where is my son?"

"In the palace. Breathing. As long as you keep praying."

The words landed like a slap, precisely because they were true. Sabine felt the truth of them in her chest, in the raw place where the prayer-grooves ran deepest. *As long as.* Three small words that had become the architecture of her entire life.

"You know," Sabine said, taking a step forward, "when I first summoned you, I told myself it was statecraft. A taster for the court. A sensible precaution in uncertain times." Another step. The frost crunched under her boots like tiny bones. "Then I told myself the gods' demands were tests. Then bargains. Then investments." She stopped an arm's length from the nearest stone, close enough to see the shimmer bend the light around Ines's face. "Do you know what I call it now?"

"No," Ines said quietly. "But I think you're about to tell me."

"Motherhood," Sabine said. "I call it motherhood. Everything I have done — every prayer, every promise, every voice I promised them — I did it for a child who wouldn't exist without me. You can judge me if you like. Everyone does. But you weren't there when the physicians shook their heads. You weren't there when I knelt on chapel stone until my knees bled, asking *anything* — anything at all — to answer. And something finally did."

Something moved in the shimmer then — or perhaps only in the air behind Ines, in the dark of the valley where the torchlight couldn't reach. Sabine felt it rather than saw it: attention, vast and patient, turning toward her like a lighthouse beam.

"They're here," Sabine said softly. "Aren't they? At the stones. Watching."

"Yes," Ines said. "They've been here since sundown. They asked nicely to come in." Her jaw tightened. "That's the worst part. They ask nicely."

"Then they'll appreciate what I've come to offer." Sabine lifted her chin. This was the speech she had composed in the sleepless dark, polished like armor, rehearsed against the headboard while her son breathed his borrowed breaths. "A trade, Taster. One trade, and everyone walks away whole. You give me the alto verse — the line your mother carried, the line that completes their hymn. You sing it to them, freely, here, tonight. And in exchange—" her voice did not waver, she made certain of it, "—my son breathes on his own. Unleashed. No more leash, no more prayer, no more debt. The gods release him entirely, and I take my army home, and no one burns, and no one bleeds, and Vhalen keeps its stones and its silence and its little unmapped life. One verse, Almeida. One song. Weighed against a child's life and a kingdom's peace."

She watched the words land. Watched Ines's face do something complicated — not temptation, exactly. Something sadder.

"You've met them," Ines said. "You've prayed to them for months. And you still think a bargain with them ends where they say it ends?"

"The terms are simple—"

"The terms are *never* simple." For the first time, Ines's voice sharpened, and beneath the sharpening Sabine heard something that sounded almost like pity. "Your Majesty, I have tasted their gifts. I have tasted what they consider generosity. A kitchen boy is dead. My friend Tomas was taken apart and reassembled into something that smiles wrong. They offered me the alto verse once already — in exchange for my blood, then my immunity, then my memories. They don't want the hymn completed so the Door can open and they can go home. They want it completed so *I* can open it, because I'm the lock, and a lock has to be willing." She spread her hands. "You're not trading a verse. You're trading the only person alive who can hold the Door shut — and once I'm theirs, your son's breath won't matter to them at all. It never mattered. It was bait. Like the heir was bait. Like *you* were bait."

"Enough." The word came out colder than Sabine intended, and she was glad of it. Cold was useful. Cold was a queen's tool. "You stand there and lecture me about bait. About being used. Girl, I have been used by gods and ministers and my own mother since before you were born. I know exactly what I am to them." She stepped closer to the stones, close enough that the shimmer brushed her skin like static, like cold water, and somewhere behind her the faithful gasped as one. "But I also know that my son stops breathing when I stop praying, and I know that they told me — *promised* me — that the alto verse buys his freedom. So here is what happens now."

She turned. Raised one hand. Two hundred soldiers shifted, steel whispering.

"You will give me the verse," Sabine said, "or I will march through those stones and take it. I have two hundred men, a kingdom behind me, and nothing left to lose that matters more than a child's next breath. I did not want it to come to this. I want you to understand that. I have admired you, Taster, from the moment you survived what should have killed you. But admiration is not a currency the gods accept, and neither, apparently, is mercy."

Ines looked at her for a long moment. And what Sabine saw in her face then was not fear, and not hatred, but recognition — the look of one exhausted woman measuring another across an impossible distance, each holding something the other needed, both of them having already paid more than they'd ever agreed to.

"I'm sorry about your son," Ines said simply. "Truly. But the answer is no."

Sabine closed her eyes. Opened them.

"So be it," she said, and dropped her hand.

The army came forward like a tide answering a pulled moon — two hundred men crossing frost and stone, torches tilting, the faithful rising from their knees with their arms raised, chanting something that might have been a hymn or might have been hunger. Sabine stood rooted at the border, watching her soldiers reach the shimmer, watching it part around them like water around wading legs—

—and that was when the gods began to laugh.

It came from everywhere and nowhere, from the stones themselves and the air above them and the ground beneath, a sound like bells breaking underwater, like a cathedral full of people weeping with joy. It rolled across the valley mouth and up into the hills and it did not stop. The soldiers faltered. Some fell to their knees. The chanting of the faithful dissolved into whimpers, then silence, because even the devout could hear what lived inside that laughter: delight. Pure, bottomless, patient delight.

Sabine's blood went cold in a way that had nothing to do with frost.

Because she understood, all at once, standing at the cage door with her hand still raised — they hadn't wanted the verse from Ines at all. They had wanted Sabine to come here. They had wanted the army. They had wanted the refusal, the marching, the *conflict* — because conflict cracked things, and cracks were doors, and every boot that crossed the border stones was another hairline fracture in four hundred years of seal.

She looked down. The stone at her feet — the oldest one, moss-black, carved with symbols no scholar had ever dated — was splitting. A thin bright line ran up its face like a vein filling with gold.

Behind her, the laughter swelled, warm as a hearth, terrible as a famine.

And in the valley, Ines Almeida began to sing.
