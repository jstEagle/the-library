## 34. The Hymn

The moon began its last climb an hour before midnight, and the valley went quiet in a way that was not silence.

Ines had grown up inside that silence. She had thought she knew it the way she knew her own hands — the hush of Vhalen, the prayerless air, the weight of four hundred years of unanswered words pressed down into the soil like sediment. But tonight the silence had turned over in its sleep and begun to speak.

It came up through her boots first. A low vibration, felt rather than heard, as though the whole valley floor were a drumhead struck once, centuries ago, still ringing. Then it rose into the stones of the border circle, where forty singers stood in the cold with their breath hanging silver around them, and Ines understood that what she was hearing was not one sound but all of them — every prayer ever spoken in Vhalen and refused, arriving at once at the place where they had always been aimed.

The accumulated silence, screaming.

*Stand firm,* Odile had told them at dusk, her voice rough from prison and from weeping. *Whatever you feel when the hymn opens, whatever comes through — keep singing. The moment any one of you stops, the line breaks, and a broken hymn is worse than no hymn at all.*

Now Odile stood at the center of the circle beside the cracked border stone, her ledger open on her forearm, her lips moving through the verses without sound — a conductor's silent counting, thirty years of transcription distilled into the movement of her mouth. Tomas stood at the circle's edge, facing outward, toward the dark fields where Sabine's army waited and where other things waited too. He had come back changed; his shadow did not move quite when he moved. But he had come back, and he stood his post, and that was enough for tonight.

The choir arranged itself by verse. That was the beauty and the terror of it — each singer carried only their family's fragment, one verse apiece, handed down in lullabies and work songs and funeral keens until no single voice remembered the whole. Marguerite's design, or her desperation. A map cut into forty pieces so that no king could torture the Door's location out of any one throat.

And at the center, the alto line. The verse that was not a location on the map but the map itself. The verse that was not a door in a wall but a door in a person.

Her grandmother had taught it to her three nights ago, kneeling by the fresh-turned grave, holding Ines's hands against her own sternum so Ines could feel where the notes were supposed to live.

*You don't sing it,* her grandmother had said. *You let it sing you. There is a difference, child, and the difference is everything.*

* * *

The first voice to rise was a child's.

Perrin, the youngest of the choir, twelve years old and freckled, whose family had carried the opening verse in a bread-baking song for eleven generations. His note went up thin and clear into the moonlight, and the valley answered before the second singer joined him.

The grass bent flat in a ring around the border stones. Not wind — there was no wind. The grass simply lay down, the way a crowd kneels.

Then Marta's verse, the second, rising out of the old harvest lament, and the two lines braided together, and Ines felt the hair on her arms lift. The hymn was not beautiful, exactly. It was *true*, which is a different and more dangerous quality. Every note landed somewhere in her chest like a foot finding a known stair in the dark.

Verse by verse, the circle filled itself in. The tenor line that had been a fisherman's chant on a river Vhalen no longer remembered having. The soprano descant that had survived as a wedding blessing, its original meaning worn smooth as river glass. Forty voices, forty fragments, and between them the shape of a country assembled itself in the air — not seen, exactly, but *remembered*. Ines could feel the hills of old Vhalen in the intervals between notes, the rivers in the rests, the Door in the structure underneath it all, patient as bones.

And under the singing, the screaming silence of four hundred years found its pitch at last and harmonized.

That was the horror of it. That was the wonder. The prayers of the valley — every sickbed plea, every prayer for rain, for children, for mercy, every dying whisper addressed to gods who could not hear — rose up through the ground and wrapped themselves around the hymn like ivy around a trellis. They did not drown the music. They *completed* it. Four centuries of longing, given melody at last.

Several of the choir were weeping as they sang. Perrin's clear note wavered and held. Old Gaspard, who carried the seventh verse, sang with his eyes shut and tears running freely into his beard, and his voice had never sounded stronger.

Ines stood at the center and waited for her turn.

* * *

The eleventh verse came near the end of the hymn, and when it arrived, the chapel wall happened again — or its memory did. In the air above the border stones, for the length of one held note, a door-shaped absence opened: not darkness, not light, a *shaped nothing*, an outline of where something should be and was not. The choir flinched as one body. Odile's silent counting faltered and resumed.

But that was only the eleventh verse. Only the map showing its work.

The alto verse came last.

Ines drew the breath her grandmother had taught her to draw — low, below the ribs, the breath you take before diving — and began.

The first note left her mouth and she stopped being the one singing.

She had no better word for it, though she would spend years afterward searching for one. The verse moved through her the way blood moves, the way grief moves, the way poison moves through a body trained to receive it. She felt it find the numb places — the fingertips she had burned dead building her immunity dish by dish, the gray-edged margins of her vision, the heart that stuttered like a bird with a hurt wing — and it did not avoid them. It *used* them. Every cost she had ever paid, every deliberate damage, every meal she had eaten knowing it might kill her: the hymn took all of it up into itself, the way a river takes up mud, and made it part of the current.

She understood then, with a clarity that felt like falling, why it had to be her. Not because of her mother's name. Not because of the priestess blood. Because a Door must be made of something that has already survived being broken open. Her whole life had been the making of a threshold. The immunity was not armor. It was *hollowing*.

The last note of the alto verse left her, and the Door opened.

Not in the wall of the ruined chapel. Not in the border stones. Not anywhere in the world at all.

It opened in Ines.

* * *

There was no pain. That surprised her most, looking back. There was a sensation like the moment of stepping from shade into full sun — a total, skin-filling brightness — except it was not light but *presence*, and it did not fall on her from outside. It poured out from inside her own ribs.

She looked down at herself and saw, faintly, beneath her skin, the outline of a doorway. Her sternum glowed like a seam of dawn under the breastbone. Her heartbeat became audible to everyone in the circle — a deep, slow boom, boom, boom, like a knell rung from inside a mountain — and with every beat the Door swung wider.

The gods came.

They did not pour across the fields from the direction of the palace. They came *up*, out of the ground, out of the border stones themselves, out of the four-hundred-year prison her ancestor had built and her grandmother had kept and her mother had died defending. They rose through the soil like heat-shimmer given hunger, and they wore faces.

Some wore the face of the cook whose trembling hands had started everything. Some wore the kitchen boy the answered prayer had killed. One wore Tomas's face, standing at the edge of the circle while the real Tomas gripped his lantern and did not look at it. They wore the faces of answered prayers because that was all they had ever been able to take from this world — the shapes people gave them when they begged.

They were beautiful. That was the worst discovery of the night. Starved for four centuries behind a border that no longer officially existed, pressed thin against the seal, they had become something like light strained through gauze — vast, attenuated, radiant with want. Ines felt the whole choir sway toward them, felt forty bodies lean like plants toward sun, and heard Odile's voice crack aloud for the first time all night:

*SING.*

The hymn rose again, louder, the circle closing ranks around her, and the gods reached the border of the singing and stopped.

Because the singing was a border now. The completed hymn had redrawn the old boundary in sound, and within it, Ines was the gate.

They turned to her. All of them. Hundreds of faces, thousands, the accumulated visages of every prayer ever answered in this broken kingdom, and they looked at her the way the starving look through a window at a lit table.

And they asked nicely.

*Little taster,* said the one wearing her mother's voice, the same voice that had offered her bargains in the dark of the chapel, the same voice that had taken a memory from her and left a smooth blank where her ninth winter used to be. *You were made for this. You know it now. Every dish you swallowed, every poison you survived — you were building a vessel. Let us in. We will be gentle. We will be grateful. We will make your valley real again, and your kingdom whole, and no one will ever pray into silence again.*

Behind her, at the circle's edge, she heard Sabine's army stirring in the dark fields — steel and torchlight and the queen's voice raised in some final command — and she heard, closer than all of it, the child-queen's true prayer, the one that never stopped: *let my baby breathe, let my baby breathe, let my baby breathe.*

The gods pressed against the hymn like tide against a seawall.

And Ines Almeida, who had spent her whole life tasting what might kill her, who had built her body into the one vessel in the world that could hold what no vessel could, stepped forward to the edge of the singing and did the only thing she had ever truly been trained to do.

She opened her mouth — and tasted them.

* * *

The first god went down like arsenic in honey.

She felt it cross her tongue, her throat, her lungs — felt it *register*, the way she had trained herself to register every toxin since childhood, cataloging burn and bloom and aftertaste. This was older than arsenic. Older than hemlock, than nightshade, than the first mushroom some trembling ancestor ate and survived. It tasted of altars and animal fat and the first time a human being knelt to something in the storm and asked it for mercy. It tasted of being answered.

Her body held.

The second god followed the first, drawn into the channel the first had opened, and the third, and the fourth. They poured toward her across the border of the hymn like water finding a breach, and she stood in the breach and drank them, and the Door in her chest stretched wide enough to pass a cathedral, and it did not break, because it had been breaking her whole life and had learned how.

Somewhere very far away, Odile was screaming the count. Somewhere closer, her grandmother stood at the circle's edge with her hand pressed over her mouth, watching her granddaughter do what four generations of priestesses had sealed themselves away to prevent — and to prepare for. Somewhere beyond everything, the moon reached the top of its thirteenth turn and hung there, balanced, waiting to see what the world would weigh.

Ines tasted them all.

She tasted the god of thresholds and the god of small mercies. The god who had lived in the chapel's answered prayers, fat and vicious on a dynasty's desperation. The ones who had worn the cook's hands and the kitchen boy's face and Tomas's shadow. The hungry ones, the patient ones, the ones who had paced behind the border stones like wolves for four hundred years, and the ones who had forgotten they were gods at all and had become only appetite wearing radiance.

They were enormous. They were endless. They were, she discovered with something like pity, *smaller than they believed* — starved so long that their vastness had collapsed inward, each of them no larger, really, than the prayers that fed them. A god is the size of its last meal. Their last meals had been crumbs.

Her heart stuttered. Held. Stuttered again. The gray at the edges of her vision closed in like dusk, and she sang through it, the alto verse still moving through her, keeping the Door shaped, keeping herself shaped, holding the threshold open with nothing but breath and bone and the stubborn, methodical discipline of a girl who had once survived a poisoned stew meant for a duke by swallowing it calmly and noting the tremor in the cook's hands.

The last of them came slowly, because it was the largest and the most afraid.

It wore no face at all. It wore the silence of Vhalen — the true silence, the original one, the hush her grandmother had walked her through as a child along the border stones. And Ines understood, in the last moment before it crossed her lips, that this was the oldest one, the first one, the god who had been locked in with the valley rather than out, the prisoner the prison had been built around.

It paused at her threshold. Even now. Even starving.

*You will carry us forever,* it said, in no voice at all. *There is no putting us down. Do you understand what you are choosing?*

Ines, hollowed and blazing, the Door of a dead country swinging wide in her chest, the moon balanced at the top of the sky, thought of her mother's name in a choir roster forty years old. Thought of a lullaby that was a lock that was a door. Thought of a valley that would never again be safe, but would finally be real.

*I'm a taster,* she said. *I've never once been allowed to spit it out.*

She swallowed the oldest god whole.

The moon completed its turn.

The hymn ended — not broke, *ended*, resolved, the final chord landing in forty throats at once like a key turning — and the silence that followed was a new silence, clean, unburdened, the silence of a valley whose every unanswered prayer had finally been received.

Ines stood alone at the center of the border stones, glowing faintly at the sternum, the Door closing in her flesh fold by fold like a hand relaxing. Inside her, something vast shifted, settled, and grew quiet.

Not dead. Never dead. Quiet — the way wolves are quiet when they have eaten.

Then the glow guttered, and Ines Almeida fell face-first into the grass, and the last thing she heard, rising up from everywhere and nowhere beneath the earth, from inside her own ribs, from the space between one heartbeat and the next, was humming —

— forty voices' worth of gods, learning her lullaby.
