## 35. The Final Meal

The Door opened in her the way a mouth opens — not all at once, but with intention.

Ines felt it first as a loosening behind her sternum, as though something that had been laced tight since birth had finally been untied by patient fingers. Then the hymn reached its final verse, and forty voices at the border stones lifted the alto line — *her* line, her mother's line, the lullaby that had been a lock and was now a key — and the night split along a seam no eye could find but every bone could feel.

She stood at the center of it. She *was* the center of it.

*Do not run,* said the part of her that had spent twenty years learning poisons one drop at a time. *You have never once run from the table.*

The gods came like a tide comes: not running, not roaring, simply arriving, everywhere at once, inevitable as water finding the low places in a shore. Ines saw them the way one sees wind — by what they moved through. The grass bent away from them in long silver strokes. The border stones sweated light. The faces they wore flickered and reassembled: the cook whose trembling hands had betrayed him, the kitchen boy who had died of an answered prayer, Tomas with his guilt worn like a bruise, her mother, her grandmother, Queen Sabine, Odile, Odile young, Odile old, a thousand petitioners whose names had been spoken upward for four hundred years and never once downward into answer.

They were hungry. That was the whole of them, Ines understood suddenly, the way she understood arsenic by its smell of bitter almonds. Not evil. Not cruel, except the way famine is cruel. They were appetite given shape by centuries of prayer, and prayer is a food that promises everything and delivers nothing, and so they had grown thin and sharp and endless.

And here, standing in the open Door of herself, was the only meal in creation that could actually fill them.

"Come, then," Ines said.

Her voice did not shake. She noticed that with a strange, clinical calm — the same calm she had felt at her first tasting, when the duke's stew had gone bitter on her tongue and she had swallowed anyway because swallowing was the work. Fear was still there; fear had never left her, not once in twenty years. But fear, she had learned, was just another flavor to be catalogued. Bitter. Metallic. Familiar.

The first god touched her lips.

It tasted of cold candle smoke and the inside of a bell, of every prayer ever whispered into cupped hands, and beneath all of it, faintly, heartbreakingly, of milk. Of being fed. Of the first hunger anyone ever has, and the first comfort.

Ines opened her throat and let it in.

* * *

What follows cannot be told in the order it happened, because order was one of the first things to go.

She tasted the god of thresholds, who tasted of doorways left open on winter nights and the particular loneliness of hallways after midnight. She tasted the god of small mercies, who was smaller than she expected — no bigger than a swallow — and who wept salt down her esophagus as it went, weeping because it had waited four hundred years to be held by anything at all.

She tasted the god who had worn her mother's voice. It fought her. Of course it did; it was the oldest and the leanest, and it knew what she was doing before she did. It poured itself against her teeth like floodwater against a gate, whispering in Marguerite's timbre: *you will break, little taster, you are only meat, we have unmade kings—*

"I know," Ines said, around it, through it. "I've been breaking myself for years. Drop by drop. You'd be amazed what a body learns to hold if you introduce the poison slowly enough."

Because that was the truth of it, the truth she had carried out of Vhalen without knowing she carried it: immunity was never the absence of harm. Immunity was the body's long negotiation with harm, the slow teaching of the blood, the patient accumulation of survived doses until the poison became, in some unspeakable way, a resident rather than an invader. Her grandmother had taught her the method with foxglove and hemlock. The valley had taught her the purpose without knowing it. And now, at the end of the thirteenth turn, with the moon hanging fat and wrong above the border stones, Ines Almeida understood that her entire life had been a preparation conducted by people who thought they were preparing her for nothing.

Every numb fingertip. Every grayed morning where the world came to her through gauze. Every stutter of her heart that she had catalogued in her ledger like a merchant counting costs. None of it had been damage. All of it had been *training*. Her body was a table set over twenty years for this one impossible banquet, and the gods were the dish, and she was the only palate in two worlds equal to the tasting.

So she tasted.

The choir sang on behind her. She heard them as if from very far away, or very deep — Odile's voice cracked and glorious, forty throats holding the hymn like hands holding a rope over a cliff. The music was doing something she couldn't name, something structural, keeping the Door of her open the way a keystone keeps an arch. If they faltered, she would close. If she closed—

She didn't finish the thought. There wasn't time, and there was so much left to eat.

* * *

Somewhere in the middle of it — the middle lasted hours or lasted a heartbeat; the moon seemed to hang still as a painted coin — the gods stopped resisting.

That was worse than the fighting. Fighting she could brace against. But one by one, the way a frightened animal finally goes limp in the arms that have caught it, they surrendered to being swallowed. She felt their relief like heat. She felt their terror like cold. Both at once, braided together, because they were hunger and hunger does not get to choose between gratitude and dread.

*We ate prayers,* one of them said, from inside her now, its voice a vibration in the walls of her own ribs. *Do you know what prayers taste like? Promises. Only promises. Four hundred years of promises, and never once the thing promised. We forgot there was anything else.*

"There isn't," Ines said. Her mouth was full of godhood; she spoke anyway, because a taster reports. "There's only this. Substance. What actually goes into you. I've spent my life learning the difference between what a dish looks like and what it is."

*And what are we?*

She considered. Around her the night churned, the last of the gods streaming toward the threshold of her open body, drawn by the gravity of the first ones already settled there. Above, the moon trembled at the top of its impossible thirteenth arc.

"You're the meal," she said. "I'm the table. Hold still."

* * *

The last one was the smallest.

It came limping up the hill of the world when all the others had gone in, and it wore no one's face — it had been prayed to so rarely that it had never learned to borrow. It stopped at the edge of her, at the shimmering absence that was her skin seen from the other side, and it hesitated the way a starving dog hesitates at a stranger's hand.

"You too," Ines said gently. "All of you. That was the bargain."

*What bargain?*

"The one I'm making right now." She knelt — or felt herself kneel; distance and size had become negotiable — and held out whatever she had instead of hands. "You come inside. You stay quiet. You eat what I give you, which will be honest things: bread, wine, grief, joy, the small true offerings people make without wanting anything back. No more prayers with teeth. No more gifts that bite. You will be fed, and you will be *held*, and in exchange you will stop eating my world."

*Forever?*

"For as long as I hold," she said. "Which is the only forever anyone gets."

The small god considered her with the whole of its tiny ancient attention. Then it stepped across the threshold of her, and it weighed almost nothing, and it curled up somewhere behind her navel like a coal banked for winter, and Ines felt the strangest sensation of her long strange night:

She felt full.

Not stuffed — full. The way the valley felt at harvest. The way a hymn feels at its resolving chord. Twenty years of careful, methodical, self-destroying discipline had built a vessel, and the vessel had done the only thing vessels are for.

* * *

The moon completed its turn.

She felt it pass the way one feels a fever break — a single, silent click deep in the machinery of the sky, and then the terrible tension of the season simply *released*, pouring out of the air like water out of a cracked dam. The hymn ended. Forty voices closed on the final note and held it, held it, held it, and the note went up and out and became ordinary sound again, and then silence, real silence, the kind Vhalen had known for four hundred years.

Ines opened her eyes.

She was on her knees in the wet grass at the border stones. The stones themselves were dark now, cool, merely stones — the light had gone out of them, or perhaps had gone *into* her; she suspected the latter. The grass around her lay flattened in a perfect circle, silvered with frost that had no business existing in that season, and at the circle's edge stood the choir.

Forty faces looked down at her. Odile's was streaked with tears and grime and something that might have been awe or might have been horror; with Odile it had always been difficult to tell the difference, and Ines loved her for it.

"Ines," Odile said carefully, the way one speaks to someone standing on ice. "The moon turned. It's past. It's—" She stopped. Started again. "Child, what happened? Where did they go?"

Ines put a hand flat against her own sternum. Beneath her palm, her heartbeat came steady — steadier than it had come in years, actually, the stutter smoothed out as though something in there had taken up residence specifically to keep time. Behind the beat, deeper, quieter, she could feel them: the vast drowsy weight of the fed gods, settling into the architecture of her like silt settling in a harbor. Not dead. Sleeping. Listening.

"They went to the table," she said. Her voice came out rough, scraped, human. "And the table held."

She got up. Her legs shook — of course they did; she had eaten the divine, and even a trained body pays some freight — but they held, and she stood, and the frost-circle glittered around her under a moon that was, at last, only a moon.

Tomas pushed through the choir then, wild-eyed, half-running, and caught her by the shoulders as if to confirm she had edges. "You swallowed them," he said. "I saw it. We all saw it. Ines, you *swallowed the gods*—"

"I tasted them," Ines corrected. "There's a difference. A taster doesn't swallow. A taster holds it in the mouth, learns it, decides whether the person she protects can survive it." She looked down at herself, at the ordinary hands that had done the extraordinary thing. "But no one was left to protect. So this time, I swallowed."

Odile made a sound that was half laugh, half sob. "And now?"

Now. Ines listened inward. The gods stirred like dogs hearing their names, and she felt each one of them distinctly — the small merciful one, the threshold-walker, the lean old liar who wore her mother's voice and who was, even now, watching her from behind her own eyes with something that felt disconcertingly like respect.

"Now," Ines said slowly, "I keep them fed."

She turned toward the valley. Beyond the border stones, Vhalen lay dark and quiet under the passed moon, and somewhere in that darkness her grandmother was waiting beside a grave that had finally found its purpose, and somewhere beyond the valley an army was marching with a queen at its head who believed the thirteenth turn would cost her everything.

The army did not know yet. The news would reach them by morning, traveling faster than any rider: the gods were answered, the gods were fed, the gods were *housed* — and the house had a name.

Ines took a breath that tasted of candle smoke and milk and the inside of bells, and began to walk home.

Behind her navel, something ancient hummed the alto line, softly, in her mother's voice — and for the first time, she hummed back.
