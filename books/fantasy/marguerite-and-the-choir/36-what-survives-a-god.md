## 36. What Survives a God

I woke to the taste of iron and honey.

That was the first wrongness — that I woke at all, that waking had a flavor, that the flavor was *familiar*. Iron and honey was what my grandmother's fever-tea tasted like, the one she brewed when a child of Vhalen came down with the winter lung. I hadn't drunk it in years. I hadn't been home in months. And yet there it was on my tongue, thick and sweet and mineral, as though someone had poured it into my mouth while I slept.

The second wrongness was the quiet.

Not silence. I knew silence; I had grown up inside it, inside a valley where no prayer was ever answered and even the wind seemed to keep its opinions to itself. This was different. This was *occupied* quiet — the hush of a great hall after the court has filed out, when you can still feel how many bodies it once held. Something enormous had recently been here. Something enormous was still here, folded up small, breathing slowly, waiting for me to notice it.

I opened my eyes.

I was lying in my own narrow bed in the taster's chamber, the one with the window that looked out over the kitchen gardens and the chapel beyond them. The chapel's roof had caved in along its eastern side, and morning light fell through the gap in a long slanted column full of dust. The thirteenth turn had passed. The moon had finished its impossible circuit and hung now where moons were supposed to hang, thin and ordinary and white as a fingernail paring. Somewhere below, someone was sweeping glass. The sound came up through the floor in small patient strokes, like a heartbeat that belonged to the palace instead of to me.

My hands were clean. That surprised me. I remembered dirt — remembered clawing at the border stones, at the earth of Vhalen, at my own arms — but my hands had been washed, the nails trimmed, a salve worked into the torn cuticles. Someone had tended me the way you tend something precious and slightly frightening. The way you tend a relic.

I sat up, and the world tilted, and then it didn't, and I understood that this too was new. For years my body had been a ledger of small debts: the numbness in my fingertips from the arsenic years, the grayed edges of my vision from the nightshade, the heart that stuttered like a bird striking glass whenever I climbed stairs too fast. I had catalogued those costs the way Odile catalogued verses, methodically, because naming a debt was the only way to keep it from swallowing you whole.

Now I lay still and took inventory, and found the ledger blank.

No numbness. My fingertips registered the rough weave of the blanket with an almost painful clarity, every fiber distinct. No grayed vision — the dust motes in the light-column were sharp as stitched beads. I pressed two fingers to my throat and waited for the stutter. It didn't come. My heart beat on beneath them, slow and even and deep, deeper than any heartbeat I had ever owned, as if it were beating for more than one body.

*Immune now to everything,* I thought, *except myself.*

I didn't know yet how true that was. I would learn it in the weeks that followed, learn it the way you learn a scar — by touching it over and over until the touching stops meaning anything. But lying there in the dust-fall light, I already understood the shape of it. I had spent my whole life building walls against poison, dose by careful dose, teaching my blood to refuse what the world offered it. And then I had opened those walls all the way down and taken in the oldest poisons there are, and my body — my stubborn, methodical, valley-made body — had done what it was trained to do.

It had refused to die. All of me had refused to die. Including the parts I had swallowed.

They were quiet. That was the thing I could not stop circling, in those first hours, in those first gray days. They were not dead. Dead things have a weightlessness; dead things let go. What lived in me now had weight. When I turned my head, something turned with it, a half-beat behind, like a shadow that had learned patience. When I spoke, I heard my voice twice — once aloud, and once somewhere beneath the floor of myself, an echo arriving late, considering.

And every morning, I had to choose whether to listen.

That was the sentence I wrote in the ledger I started keeping — a real ledger this time, paper and ink, because some inheritances should be written down. *Day one: they are quiet. I did not listen.* It sounded so small, put that way. A daily choice, like choosing which dress to wear, like deciding whether to take sugar in the tea. But I want to be honest, since honesty is the only offering I have left that means anything: the choice was the hardest thing I have ever done, harder than crossing the border stones, harder than singing the Door open, harder than the tasting itself.

Because listening would have been easy. Listening would have been *sweet*. When I let my attention slip — when I was tired, or lonely, or standing in the garden at dusk watching the light go amber on the ruined chapel — they rose toward the surface of me like fish rising to feed, and they brought gifts. Not words, exactly. More like flavors. The taste of being known completely. The taste of four hundred years of accumulated prayer, all those unanswered cries from the valley of Vhalen finally received and held and *understood*. My mother's voice, humming under my ribs. The sensation — and I record it here with shaking hand, because it deserves recording — of never again being alone in my own skull.

I said no. Every day, I said no. Some days it took until noon.

* * *

On the third day, Tomas came to my door carrying a tray.

He stood in the corridor looking like a man who had been pulled out of a river and dried by fire — singed around the edges, moving carefully, as if his joints might not hold. What the gods had done to him during those weeks of answered prayers had left marks I could see and marks I couldn't. His eyes had a new habit: they would fix on a point just past your shoulder, as if listening to someone standing there, and then he'd blink hard and come back.

"Broth," he said. "Cook's orders. She says — " He stopped. Swallowed. "She says a taster who tastes herself needs feeding."

I almost laughed. It came out as something else, a sound like a hinge. "Tell her thank you."

He set the tray down and didn't leave. He stood there working his hands together, and I watched him decide, three separate times, not to say what he wanted to say. On the fourth attempt, he lost.

"Ines. What is it like?"

I looked at him for a long moment. Most of the court had decided what it was like without asking — I had seen it in their faces as I walked the corridors, that particular reverence-terror blend I had first learned to read in the chapel, back when the answered prayers were still novelties. They looked at me the way people look at a reliquary. Nobody asked the reliquary how it felt.

"It's like," I said slowly, "having guests who are very polite and very old and very hungry, sleeping in your house. They don't break anything. They don't demand anything." I paused. "Yet."

"And do they talk to you?"

"They hum." I touched my sternum, where the echo lived. "Like a choir warming up in another room. Sometimes I catch a note and I know exactly which verse it belongs to, and then I make myself think about bread, or laundry, or the price of candles. Anything small. Small things starve them a little, I think. Or maybe they just find small things boring."

Tomas nodded as if this made sense, and maybe to him it did. Then his face did the thing it did now, the flicker past-my-shoulder, the listening. When he came back to himself, his jaw was set.

"The queen," he said. "You should hear it from someone who loves you before you hear it from a herald. Sabine is going to abdicate."

* * *

She did it in the great hall, on the fifth day, and she did it in one sentence.

I was there because Odile insisted I be there — Odile, freed from her cell by an order signed in a hand that shook, freed into a kingdom that no longer quite knew what to do with choirmasters or gods or girls who swallow both. She stood beside me in the crowd of courtiers, her ledger clutched to her chest like a breastplate, thirty years of transcription pressed against her heart. Her knuckles were white around it. Mine were white around nothing at all.

Sabine came down the steps of the dais wearing mourning grey, though no one had died — or rather, everyone had died, in the sense that everything she had built her desperation upon had burned. She had burned her prayer book herself; Tomas told me that too, told me how she fed the pages to the chapel brazier page by page while the child slept in the next room, breathing only when she prayed, until it didn't need her breath anymore. Freed. That was the word everyone used. *Freed.* As if the child had been a hostage and the ransom paid.

The hall went silent in that special way royal halls have, a silence with weight and hierarchy, and Queen Sabine looked out over her court without meeting anyone's eyes — which was, I thought, the most consistent thing she had ever done. She had never met my eyes either, not on the day I swore the taster's oath, not on the day she ordered me to taste a prayer-gift and report its flavor, not once in all the months between. Queens who traffic with gods learn early not to look at what they've spent.

She said: "I am done, and my daughter will be crowned at midwinter, and none of you are to pray in this palace again."

One sentence. The heralds scrambled. The chamberlain dropped his staff of office, and the clatter of it rang through the hall like the last note of a hymn. And Sabine — Sabine walked down through the parting crowd with her chin level and her hands empty, and as she passed me, she stopped.

For one moment, one breath, she looked at me. Directly. The first time. Her eyes were the color of a winter sky over Vhalen, pale and far away and very tired.

"You held what I could not carry," she said quietly. "I will spend whatever is left of my life knowing that."

Then she was gone, through the doors, into whatever unmapped country a queen walks into when she abdicates in a single sentence, and the court began to murmur like a tide coming in, and Odile's hand found my arm and gripped it hard enough to bruise.

* * *

The weeks that followed taught me the shape of my survival, and the shape was hollow.

I want to be precise about this, because the ballads — yes, there are already ballads, Odile has collected three — get it wrong. They say I triumphed. They say I conquered. They describe the tasting at the threshold as a battle won, the gods devoured, the valley saved. And it's true that the thirteenth turn passed. It's true that the moon kept its ordinary appointments afterward, and the unasked gifts stopped appearing on doorsteps, and the healings that took payment stopped taking it. It's true that Vhalen's border stones stand open now, cracked wide, and that cartographers in three kingdoms are arguing about how to draw a valley that officially does not exist.

But survival is not the same as victory, and I had survived the way a house survives a flood: standing, emptied, everything soft ruined.

Some nights I woke and could not remember my mother's face — only her voice, and the voice wasn't hers anymore, it was theirs, worn smooth from use. Some mornings I caught myself humming a note I had never learned and had to press my hand flat against my mouth until the humming stopped. Once, in the market, a fruit-seller offered me a plum and I reached for it and my fingers closed on air six inches short, because some part of me had flinched away from sweetness, from *offering*, from anything that resembled a gift given freely by something that wanted something back.

The immunity held. That was the miracle nobody sang about: I ate the plum, eventually, and it was only a plum. Poison slid off me now like rain off oiled cloth. I could drink from any well, eat from any dish, breathe any miasma, and my body would look at all of it with the vast bored patience of something that had already digested worse. The taster's guild sent a delegation. They wanted to study me. I sent them away with a warning they didn't understand yet: *be careful what you build an immunity to, because one day you may be asked to swallow something that isn't trying to kill you.*

Every day, the choice. Every morning, the ledger. *Day nine: they are quiet. I did not listen. Day ten: they hummed the alto line at dawn, in her voice, and I listened for eleven seconds. Eleven seconds. I am counting.*

Odile found me writing it once, in the choir loft, where I went sometimes because the acoustics of that room had become the only place my own thoughts sounded like mine. She lowered herself onto the bench beside me — her knees were bad, thirty years of standing at a lectern — and read the entry upside down without apology.

"Eleven seconds," she said. "Out of eighty-six thousand minutes a day. Ines, do you know what I would give for eleven seconds of certainty about anything?"

"That's not the point."

"No?" She took off her spectacles and polished them on her sleeve, a gesture I had come to recognize as her version of weeping. "What is the point, then?"

I looked out over the loft rail, down into the chapel, at the workmen repairing the eastern wall, at the long column of light that fell through the patched roof onto the place where the Door had opened in the stone. Where *I* had opened. My body remembered it the way a bell remembers being struck.

"The point," I said, "is that they're patient. Gods are patient the way glaciers are patient. Eleven seconds today. Twelve tomorrow. And one morning I'll wake up and realize I've been listening for years without noticing, and everything I think will be a duet, and I won't be able to find the seam where I end." I closed the ledger. "That's what survives a god, Odile. Not death. Dilution."

She was quiet for a long time. Below us, the repaired choir was running scales — forty voices minus one, minus the alto line that no one dared fill, that sat empty in every arrangement like a missing tooth. Then she said, in the voice she used for announcing difficult keys:

"Then we keep the seam visible. That's what a choir is, girl. Forty seams, all of them showing, holding anyway." She tapped the ledger in my lap. "Write it down. Every day. Even the eleven seconds. Especially the eleven seconds. A lock that knows it's a lock stays shut."

* * *

So I stayed. I took the post they offered — not the old post, not the palace table with its poisoned dishes, but something new, something we invented together in the smoking ruin of the chapel's east wing. A tasting of a different kind. But that is another chapter, and I am getting ahead of myself, because there is one more thing that happened in those hollowed-out weeks, one more thing I must set down before I can set anything else down.

It was evening, near midwinter, the first snow falling past my window in fat unhurried flakes. I was in my chamber, doing nothing — I had gotten good at nothing, at sitting in the amber lamplight with my hands empty, guarding the emptiness — when the humming changed.

It always hummed. I had made peace with the humming the way you make peace with a creaky stair. But this evening the note beneath my ribs shifted key, and I recognized the shift, because I had sung that modulation a hundred times in the loft: it was the turn into the final verse. The alto entrance.

And underneath the hum, faint as a pulse through a wall, I felt something I had not felt since the thirteenth turn ended. Attention. All of it, every one of them, turning toward the same direction like a field of flowers finding the sun.

South. Toward the ruined chapel. Toward the room where a child slept — a child who, the servants whispered, had begun to breathe on her own at last, whose mother knelt beside her cradle day and night, praying to nothing at all.

The gods inside me were not hungry.

They were *listening* — and for the first time since I swallowed them, they were listening to something outside of me.

I put down the ledger. I picked it back up. And I wrote, in a hand that was not entirely steady:

*Day fifty-one: they are quiet. But tonight, they are quiet the way a congregation is quiet. Something is happening in the chapel, and they want to hear it — and I am afraid, for the first time in fifty-one days, that I want to hear it too.*
