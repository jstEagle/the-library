## 37. The Queen's Child Breathes

The child breathed on its own at dawn.

Sabine knew it the way she had come to know everything about her son in these last weeks — not by seeing but by listening, by the terrible arithmetic of a mother's ear. She had been awake for six days and five nights, kneeling at the cradle in the eastern tower, praying without pause because she had learned what happened when she stopped. The room smelled of tallow and cold stone and the sweetish rot of answered prayers. And then, somewhere between one psalm and the next, between one desperate syllable and its successor, the sound changed.

The child's breathing had always been hers. She understood that now, though no one had told her plainly; she had assembled it herself from small horrors, the way one assembles a confession. When she prayed, his chest rose. When her voice frayed and faltered — and it had frayed often, these six days, until her throat was a raw red ruin — his chest stilled, and the wet rattle of almost-silence crept into the room like fog through a bad window, and terror would seize her by the hair and drag her back to prayer. Six days. Her knees had gone numb on the third. Her hands had stopped feeling like hands. But the boy — Alaric, she had named him before any of this, back when names were just names and not leashes — Alaric had lain in his carved cradle like something borrowed, like an ambassador from another country, breathing only when she begged him to.

And now, at dawn, with her voice cracked down to a whisper and her lips split and bleeding, he breathed without her.

She did not dare believe it. Belief was a currency she had spent recklessly for two years, and she had learned what counterfeit coin felt like in the palm. So she knelt there, silent — truly silent for the first time since the gods had delivered him, wrapped in swaddling that smelled faintly of incense and iron — and she counted.

One breath. Two. Three.

Her heart slammed against her ribs so hard she feared the noise alone might wake whatever bargain had made him. Four. Five. Ten. The morning light came through the tower's narrow windows in long gray blades, and dust turned slowly in the light, and the child breathed. Twenty breaths. Thirty. His small fist worked against the blanket. His face, which had always seemed slightly unfinished to her — a sketch of a baby rather than a baby — filled in, somehow, in sleep. Color came into his cheeks that had nothing to do with her voice.

A hundred breaths. She counted them all.

Then Sabine did the only thing left to do. She stood — her legs buckled and she caught herself against the cradle, and the child did not stop breathing — and she walked out of the tower on legs that belonged to someone much older, down the spiral stair, across the frozen garden where the prayer-gifts had been burned in the autumn and their ash still silvered the ground like frost that refused to melt, to the chapel.

* * *

The chapel was ruined. That word had once meant something gentler — worn, beloved, mellowed by centuries of candle smoke — but the truth of the last months had given it teeth again. The east wall still bore the scar of the door-shaped absence that had opened there, a wound in the stone shaped exactly like a doorway and leading nowhere, or everywhere; the masons had tried three times to fill it and three times the mortar had slid out overnight like water finding its level. The pews were scorched where the god-gifts had caught fire. The altar had been stripped bare on Odile's orders — no, not Odile's orders; Odile was in prison, had been in prison since the incident, and it had been Tomas who cleared the altar with shaking hands while the court watched from the doorway like mourners at a funeral they didn't understand.

Sabine stood in the doorway a long time. The light through the broken east window fell in pieces across the floor, colored glass scattered on gray stone: a hand's width of blue here, a smear of gold there, the fragment of a saint's face staring up at nothing.

Two years ago this had been the warmest room in the palace. Courtiers had crowded it at every hour, pressing petitions into the air like flowers into a book. The old gods had begun answering, and answering had felt like spring after four hundred years of winter — the whole dynasty drunk on the novelty of being heard. Sabine remembered the first gift she'd received: a sprig of white blossom in midwinter, appearing on her pillow, fragrant, impossible. She had wept over it. She had pressed it in her prayer book. She had prayed harder.

She had not yet known that the old gods gave gifts the way a fisherman gives bait.

Now the chapel was empty of everyone except her and the dust and the colored light. The court had stopped coming after the kitchen boy died — after the answered prayer that killed him, a gift of honeyed figs that tasted of copper and closed his throat while he smiled. After the thirteenth turn began. After the queen herself stopped attending, because the queen had learned what the gods wanted in exchange, and could not bear to look at the place where she had agreed to pay it.

Sabine crossed the floor. Her joints ached; six days of kneeling had written themselves into her body permanently, she suspected, the way grief writes itself into the spine. At the altar steps she stopped. Then, slowly, with the stiffness of a woman twice her age, she went down onto her knees on the cold stone.

She had come to pray. The habit was that deep, that structural — two years of daily petition, and before that a lifetime of courtly observance, and before that a girlhood in which her nurse had taught her that the old gods listened best at dawn. Her knees found their place. Her hands folded. Her head bowed. And the machinery of prayer engaged itself in her automatically, gears turning, the opening words rising up her ruined throat—

—and she stopped them.

She knelt there with the words caught behind her teeth like a held breath, and she looked at the empty altar, and she thought: *what am I doing?*

What was she praying *to*? That was the question she had never let herself ask, all this time, because the answer was too useful to leave unasked. She had prayed to the old gods and the old gods had answered, and she had told herself it was faith rewarded. But she knew better now. She had known since the night she found the prayer book's margins filling with writing she hadn't put there — instructions, prices, terms — since the child arrived and would not breathe unless she did. The old gods hadn't been listening to her prayers. They had been *farming* them. Every petition a hook, every answered prayer a line, and the dynasty of Vhalen's kings thrashing on it, splendid and doomed, for two years.

And now the gods were quiet. Quiet since the border. Quiet since the girl — the taster, Ines, the valley-woman with the dead mother's surname — had fled the palace with a stolen ledger and done whatever she had done at the stones. Sabine had felt it happen even from here, even half-mad with sleeplessness: a great silence rolling across the kingdom like weather, like the moment after a bell stops ringing when your ears still hold its shape. The prayer book had gone blank. The gifts had stopped arriving. And Alaric —

Alaric breathed.

Sabine knelt in the ruins of her own chapel, in the wreckage of her own bargain, and understood at last that she had nothing left to say to anything that might be listening. Every prayer she owned had already been spoken, and every one of them had been used against her. What remained? Gratitude? To whom? Confession? To what?

So she prayed to nothing at all.

It was not a decision so much as a falling — the way a held breath finally leaves the body. She knelt on the cold stone with her ruined hands open in her lap, and she directed her attention at the vast indifferent dark above the broken window, at the ordinary sky, at the space where gods would be if gods were anywhere, and she said nothing. She asked nothing. For the first time since her wedding day, since the years of empty cradles and the physicians' careful pity and the court's whispered arithmetic about heirs, she wanted nothing from the silence.

And the silence — the silence was a mercy.

That was the thing she had never imagined. She had thought silence would feel like abandonment, like the cold vast indifference the priests warned of, the universe shrugging. Instead it felt like setting down a weight she had forgotten she carried. No voice in the margins. No price accruing. No child whose lungs depended on the endurance of her throat. Just the dust turning in the colored light, and the wind moving through the broken glass with a sound almost like singing, and her own heartbeat, stubborn and unremarkable, keeping time for no one's benefit but her own.

Sabine wept. Not the way she had wept over the blossom on her pillow — that had been hunger dressed as joy. This was simpler. It came up from somewhere below thought, and it shook her shoulders, and it washed out of her some sediment of the last two years, and when it passed she felt scoured, hollowed, lighter than she had any right to be.

She stayed on her knees until the light moved off the saint's shattered face.

* * *

They found her there, eventually. Of course they did; a queen cannot vanish even into her own grief. It was the chamberlain first, then the guards, then a slow accretion of household staff gathering in the chapel doorway the way people gather at accidents, drawn by the wrongness of the sight: their queen kneeling alone in the ruined chapel, dirty, hollow-eyed, smiling.

Smiling. That was what unsettled them, Sabine realized. She could read it in their faces, the way she had learned to read faces in twenty years of statecraft — the flicker of fear under the relief. A weeping queen they knew how to manage. A praying queen they knew how to manage. A queen kneeling in silence, smiling at nothing, was a different animal entirely.

"Your Majesty." The chamberlain approached the way one approaches a wounded animal, or perhaps a holy one; in the current climate the two categories had blurred unpleasantly. "The child — we heard — is he—"

"He breathes." Her voice was a ruin, barely a whisper, and every head in the doorway leaned toward it. "On his own. He has breathed on his own since dawn."

Someone made a sound — half sob, half prayer — and several of the staff dropped to their knees right there in the doorway, and Sabine watched them do it with a clarity that felt almost cruel. They were thanking the gods. Even now. Even after the honeyed figs and the burning choir-loft omen and the thirteenth turn hanging over them like a blade, the reflex of gratitude fired on schedule, aimed at nothing, at everything, at the same hungry dark she had farmed for two years.

"Stand up," she said. They didn't hear her. "Stand *up*." Her ruined voice cracked across the chapel, and they rose, confused, frightened.

She got to her feet. It took longer than it should have. When she spoke again she made each word deliberate, hoarse, unmistakable.

"There are no gods answering anymore. Whatever you felt — whatever you thanked — it is finished. The gifts will not come. The prayers will not be answered. And I want it said plainly, in every hall and every kitchen, so no one kneels in this room again expecting a reward: the things that answered us were not kind, and we are lucky — *lucky* — to have lost them."

Silence. Somewhere in the palace a door closed. A guard in the doorway crossed himself with the old sign, the pre-dynastic one, and Sabine let it go. Faith would take longer to die than gods did. Perhaps that was mercy too.

"The regency council meets at noon," she said. "All of them. And send for—" she paused, tasting the name, deciding. "Send word to the border. To Vhalen. I want to know what happened there. I want to know what the taster did, and what it cost, and whether the stones stand open or shut. Bring me the answer before the moon turns again."

The chamberlain bowed and fled, relieved to have orders. The others dispersed like smoke. And Sabine climbed the tower stairs alone, slowly, one hand on the wall, back to the cradle where her son lay breathing — breathing, breathing, the most ordinary miracle in the world, the only one she had ever actually wanted.

She sat beside him and did not pray. She watched his chest rise and fall and rise, and she practiced, silently, the new discipline of wanting nothing, and she found that watching him breathe required no divine assistance at all. It required only her eyes, and her heart, and the rest of her life.

Outside the window, far to the south, past the frozen gardens and the ash-silvered ground and the hills she had never been permitted to care about, the valley of Vhalen waited — and word was already traveling toward her along the roads, faster than any courier she had sent, carried in the mouths of pilgrims and deserters and choirboys: that the border stones stood cracked wide, that maps were being redrawn, that a woman was walking home through a country that had never existed.

Sabine looked at her sleeping son and understood that the world had changed shape while she knelt, and that whatever came out of that valley next would come asking nothing — and that this, more than any army, was what she should fear.
