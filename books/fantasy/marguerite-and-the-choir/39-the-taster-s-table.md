## 39. The Taster's Table

The table was Odile's idea.

She said it the way she said everything — flatly, as though reporting the weather — standing in the doorway of my grandmother's kitchen with her ledger under one arm and a look on her face that meant she had been turning something over for days and had finally decided to let it fall.

"You were a taster," she said. "Be a taster."

I was kneading bread at the time, because bread was one of the few things my hands still remembered how to do without instruction. Four months since the thirteenth turn. Four months since I had stood at the threshold of myself and swallowed heaven whole, and in all that time my hands had wanted nothing so much as an ordinary task — flour, water, salt, the patient work of making something that would be eaten and gone.

"I'm not hungry," I said, which was not what she meant, and we both knew it.

"They are." She set the ledger on the table, next to the flour I'd scattered, and did not apologize for the mess. Odile had never once apologized for a mess in her life; she considered them evidence of work honestly done. "You told us yourself. They're quiet, but they're not gone. Quiet things starve slower than loud ones, but they still starve." She opened the ledger to a page near the end, where her handwriting marched across the paper like soldiers who had long ago stopped expecting to win. "I've been transcribing the accounts from the last four hundred years. Every answered prayer, every gift that bit, every hunger that outgrew its feeding. There's a pattern, Ines. The gods don't turn cruel when they're denied. They turn cruel when they're *starving*. Everything the court suffered — the kitchen boy, the queen's child, all of it — it was famine wearing a crown."

I wiped my hands and sat down across from her. Through the window behind her I could see the border stones, or where they used to be. We had left them cracked open, my grandmother and I, the morning after the hymn. Not torn down — my grandmother had been very clear about that — but opened, the way you leave a door unlatched in summer so the house can breathe. Magic came through now in small ways: moss that glowed faintly at dusk along the north wall, a well whose water tasted faintly of starlight if you drank it thinking of someone you loved, weather that arrived a day early because it had heard about itself.

And prayers, now, were sometimes answered here. Small ones. A widow's prayer for rain, answered with two days of soft gray drizzle. A child's prayer that his lame goat would walk, answered with a goat that walked with a limp but walked. The valley had spent four hundred years learning how to live unheard, and now it was learning, slowly and clumsily, how to live listened-to.

The problem — and there is always a problem, this I learned at court and have never unlearned — was appetite.

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about carrying gods: they do not speak. I had expected voices, demands, the grand theater of possession. Instead there was only a weight, low in me, beneath the sternum and slightly to the left of the heart — a warmth like a stone pulled from a fire and wrapped in cloth, cooling so slowly it would take a lifetime, maybe several. On good days I forgot it was there. On bad days I could feel it listening.

They had names once. I knew this from Odile's ledger, from the old liturgies she'd recovered from the chapel's ruined archive. Names like *Vessia-of-the-Threshold* and *the Ninefold Shepherd* and one whose name was written only as a gap in the page, a hole in the ink where the scribe's hand had refused to continue. But names require someone outside to call you by them, and the gods had spent four centuries behind the border stones with no one to call them anything. Somewhere in the starving, I think, they had worn their names away like coins passed through too many hands.

What they had left was hunger, and memory, and — this surprised me most — manners.

They never asked for anything directly. That was the first thing I understood, sitting across from Odile while the bread cooled between us. When I let my attention sink down toward that warm stone inside me, I didn't hear *feed us*. What I felt was more like the smell of baking bread drifting through a window to someone who hasn't eaten in days — a leaning, a longing, patient and terrible and polite. They had learned, over four hundred years of imprisonment, that wanting loudly got you nothing, and so they had perfected the art of wanting silently, which is a skill no living creature should ever have to learn.

"Small offerings," Odile was saying. She tapped the ledger. "That's the pattern in reverse. Look — every catastrophe in here began with a large prayer. An heir. A victory. A resurrection. Big prayers are debts, and the gods collect debts the way floods collect houses. But the small ones—" she turned pages, showing me entries in her cramped hand, cross-referenced against prayers recorded in the valley's oral histories, "—a prayer for safe travel. For a good harvest. For a dying woman not to suffer. These were answered cleanly, even before the sealing, even in the worst years. Small honest offerings fed them without binding anyone. The court forgot that. The court thought worship meant magnitude."

"And you want me to sit at a table," I said slowly, working it through, "and eat small prayers."

"I want you to do what you were always going to do anyway," Odile said, "which is taste things carefully and decide whether they're safe. Only now the things are prayers, and the person you're protecting is everyone."

* * *

We built the table in the old meetinghouse, because my grandmother refused to have it in her kitchen. "I spent my life keeping that sort of thing out of this valley," she said, arms crossed in the doorway, watching Odile and Tomas carry in a plain oak table that had once stood in the palace chapel and had, in my opinion, seen enough. "I'll not have it at my own hearth."

"But you're not stopping us," I said.

My grandmother looked at me for a long moment. Her face is a country I have spent my whole life mapping and still find unmapped territory — the deep lines around her mouth, the eyes the color of the slate in the eastern hills. When the priestess line was explained to me, when she told me what our blood was *for*, I had braced for her to be different afterward, revealed, some stranger wearing my grandmother's shawl. She wasn't. She was exactly the same woman, which was somehow more disorienting.

"No," she said finally. "I'm not stopping you." And then, gruffly, because she has never in her life ended a sentence where tenderness wanted it to end: "Someone has to make sure you eat actual food while you're doing it. You'll forget. You always forget."

So the table went into the meetinghouse, and word went out through the valley the way word goes out in Vhalen, which is to say by women talking over fences and children running errands and one extremely reliable deaf shepherd who read lips across entire fields. Once a week — we settled on the evening of the seventh day, because the valley already gathered then and we saw no reason to invent a new habit — people came with their prayers.

Not all of them. Not even most of them, at first. The valley had spent four hundred years praying into silence and had made a virtue of it, the way you make a virtue of any deprivation you cannot escape. My grandmother's generation considered unanswered prayer a form of cleanliness. It took time, and it took trust, and it took — I will admit this — it took me.

Because here is the shape of the work: a person comes to the table and speaks their prayer aloud. Not to me, exactly. To the room, to the air, to whatever habit of hope they carried. And I listen — really listen, the way I was trained to listen for almond extract under the smell of stewed plums, for the bitter green note of foxglove beneath honey — and I taste it. That is the only word. The prayer arrives on my tongue like a flavor, and I can tell things about it. Whether it's honest. Whether it hides a hook. Whether it asks too much.

Most prayers taste like bread. That was the first great discovery of the taster's table, and I think it would have broken the old court's heart: nearly everything people actually want is small and clean. Health for a child. Rain in its season. Forgiveness for a quarrel that has gone on too long. A widow named Sera came the third week and prayed for the courage to plant her late husband's field rather than let it go to scrub, and the prayer tasted — I wrote it in my own ledger, because Odile insisted I keep one alongside hers — *like warm milk and cut grass, clean all the way down.* I gave it passage. I let it sink through me to the warm stone, and the stone warmed, and three days later Sera found her field plowed overnight, furrows straight as psalm lines, and no one in the valley claimed the work.

Some prayers taste wrong. A man came in the sixth week wanting his neighbor's land, dressed up in pious language about stewardship, and underneath the piety it tasted of copper and rot, of the same toxin that had killed the kitchen boy at court. I tasted it and held it in my mouth and did not swallow, and when I told him gently that this prayer would cost more than it paid, he stormed out — but he did not die, and neither did his neighbor, and that, too, is the work. The gods inside me turned over restlessly that night, hungry and unsatisfied, and I lay awake feeling them lean against the walls of me, and I understood that refusing a prayer costs the taster something too. Immunity, I learned long ago, is a debt. So is discernment. Every refusal, the hunger sharpens.

Which is why the honest ones matter. Which is why the table matters.

* * *

Tomas stayed.

He arrived in the valley six weeks after the hymn, walking, carrying nothing but a satchel of seeds and a letter of resignation he had never sent because there was no longer a court to resign from — Sabine had abdicated in a single sentence, as promised, and the regency council that replaced her had neither the power nor the interest to pursue a former steward across a border that officially did not exist. He looked thinner than I remembered, and older, and there was a new stillness in him, the kind that comes from having been somewhere very far away and not entirely coming back.

The gods had worn his face, at the stones. Had asked nicely, in his voice, while the real Tomas was being held somewhere I don't ask about and he doesn't offer. When I look at him now I see both men — the friend and the mask — and I have decided, deliberately, as an act of will renewed each morning, to see the friend first.

He took the cottage nearest the meetinghouse and planted the seeds, and by midsummer there were beans and squash climbing the ruins of the old wall, and he appointed himself keeper of the table. He sweeps it. He sets out the single candle. He keeps the roster of who is coming to pray and checks afterward, quietly, on the ones whose prayers I refused, to make sure refusal didn't drive them somewhere worse. He has a genius for the follow-up, for the human aftermath, that I entirely lack. I can tell a man his prayer tastes of poison. Tomas can sit with him afterward while he weeps about the brother he wanted to spite.

"Why do you stay?" I asked him once, in the autumn, watching him trim the candle wick with the concentration of a man defusing something. "You could go anywhere. The regency would take you back. Any household in the capital would."

His hands paused. Outside, the valley was going gold and rust, and the border stones stood open in the blue distance like a gate left swinging.

"When they had me," he said, not looking up, "they showed me things. What the world looks like from inside a god. And you know what there was? Nothing. Hunger, and the memory of being worshipped, and — this is the part I can't make anyone understand — *loneliness*. Loneliness on a scale that — " He shook his head. "They weren't evil, Ines. They were starving animals in a cage built by a priestess who loved them enough not to kill them. And now there's a girl in a valley who lets them taste bread-prayers once a week, and they've stopped pacing." He trimmed the wick, and the flame steadied. "I stayed because I heard what it sounds like when they're hungry. I want to hear what it sounds like when they're not."

It sounds, for the record, like almost nothing. Like a cat asleep in a sunlit room. Like the hum a cathedral makes when it is empty and content. Some nights, lying in my bed in my grandmother's house, I attend to that hum the way you attend to breathing, and I am not afraid, and I count that among the strangest mercies of my strange life.

* * *

Odile writes it all down.

She has taken a room in the meetinghouse loft, above the table, surrounded by paper, and her ledger has become ledgers — eleven now, bound in whatever leather the valley could spare, her handwriting marching across them in its tireless ranks. She records every prayer offered, every flavor, every verdict, every outcome. She interviews the pray-ers afterward with the gentle ruthlessness of a woman who believes truth is a debt owed to the future. She has begun teaching three of the valley's children their letters specifically so that someday, when she is dust, someone will be able to read what she wrote.

"The court kept secrets," she told me once, dipping her pen, not looking up. "That's why it died. Knowledge hoarded is knowledge halved, and halved again, until what's left is superstition and fear. This—" she touched the ledger, "—is the antidote. One day every valley and every court will know what a prayer weighs, what it costs, what it feeds. Not faith. *Accounting.* The gods deserve better than worship. They deserve bookkeeping."

She says things like that with a completely straight face, and then goes back to writing, and I love her in the way you love a load-bearing wall.

Last week she asked me a question that has been sitting in me since, turning over like a stone in a stream. She was cross-referencing the hymn — the complete hymn, forty verses, sung once at the border stones under a thirteenth moon — against the oldest choir rosters she had recovered from the palace archive. She does this often; the hymn is history now, taught in the meetinghouse as music rather than magic, and Odile documents its provenance the way other scholars document battles.

"Ines," she said, in that flat weather-report voice, "your mother's name appears in the roster from forty years ago. Her choir name, I mean. The name she sang under." She turned the page toward me. "Do you know what it was?"

I looked at the ink. At the small, careful letters. And something moved inside me — not the gods, or not only the gods — something older and quieter, a lullaby stirring in a room it thought was empty.

I did know. I realized I had always known, the way you know a song without knowing you know it, the way the alto verse lived in me for twenty years disguised as a bedtime melody. I just had never seen it written down, never seen it attached to her, never seen it standing at the head of a choir roster like a title.

Like the name of a hymn.

I told Odile I needed a day. She nodded, wrote something in the margin — she is always writing something in the margin — and let me go.

Now it is evening, the seventh day, and the valley is walking toward the meetinghouse through the blue dusk, and the candle on the taster's table is lit, and Tomas is setting out the second chair, the one for the next prayer, the one after that, all the small honest hungers of a valley learning to be heard. The warm stone inside me stirs and leans toward the door, patient, polite, waiting to be fed.

Tonight, after the prayers are tasted and the people have gone home to their bread, I am going to sing the alto line. Freely, for the first time — not as a lock, not as a key, not as a sacrifice, but as a daughter singing her mother's song. And I am going to write the name at the top of my own ledger, in my own careful hand, where Odile can find it, where the valley can find it, where four hundred years of silence can finally read it:

*Marguerite.*

The gods, I think, already know the tune.
