## 40. Marguerite and the Choir

The ledger came apart in my hands the way old things do—not all at once, but in a slow surrender of thread and glue, the spine sighing open like a mouth that had been holding its breath for thirty years.

I was sitting in the choir loft of the rebuilt chapel in Vhalen, because Odile had insisted that if her life's work was going to fall to pieces anywhere, it should be somewhere with good light and better acoustics. The valley's chapel was not grand. It had been a granary before the border stones cracked and the world remembered us, and it still smelled faintly of rye and dust, and I loved it for that. Holiness, I had learned, did not require marble. It required only attention.

Outside, autumn was doing its patient work on the valley. The birches along the Vhalen river had gone the color of candle flame, and the terraced fields below wore their second harvest like a borrowed coat—richer than anything we'd grown when magic couldn't cross our borders, stranger too. A squash the size of a sleeping dog sat in Tomas's garden, refusing to explain itself. The valley had decided, collectively, to be delighted rather than afraid, which I think took more courage than any of us admitted at the time.

"Page forty," Odile said from across the loft, not looking up from her own copying. She had taken to wearing reading spectacles perched on her forehead like a second pair of eyes, and she pointed with her pen as though the pen were a small conductor's baton. "You wanted page forty."

"I wanted the roster," I said. "From the year my mother sang."

"You wanted more than that." Odile's voice was dry as vellum. "You've wanted more than that since the night you sang verse eleven and put a hole in my chapel wall. Go on. Read."

I turned to page forty.

The paper was soft as cloth. Forty years of dark had pressed the ink into it until the letters seemed less written than grown, like lichen on stone. It was a choir roster from the royal chapel—the same chapel where I had sworn an oath I no longer entirely understood to a queen who no longer reigned—and it listed the singers by section, soprano to bass, each name followed by a number and, in some cases, a note in the choirmaster's cramped hand.

I found her in the alto line. Third from the top.

*Alto II: Vasquez, M.*

My breath went strange in my chest. Not caught—caught implies it comes back quickly. It simply changed its mind about what it was doing, the way a river changes course around a fallen tree.

"M," I said aloud.

"Marguerite," Odile said.

I looked up. She had finally set down her pen. In the slanted afternoon light her face was unreadable, which from Odile meant she was feeling something too large to fit through her expression all at once.

"The hymn," she said. "The one you've been asking me about since the day you arrived at the palace, the one no scholar could date, the one whose composer is listed nowhere in any archive in the kingdom." She tapped the roster with one finger. "Look at the heading again. Not the names. The heading."

I looked. Above the list of voices, in the same cramped hand, someone had written the work's title—the title I had seen a hundred times in the archive margins, always abbreviated, always *M. and the Ch.*, and I had assumed, as everyone assumed, that it referred to some long-dead patron or saint whose name history had mislaid.

It didn't say *M. and the Choir*.

It said *Marguerite and the Choir*.

And beneath it, smaller, in ink of a slightly different age, as though added later by a hand that knew what it was writing:

*For the alto who carries the door in her throat.*

---

I want to tell you that I understood everything immediately—that the revelation landed whole, like a bell struck once and ringing true. But understanding, in my experience, arrives the way immunity does: in small deliberate doses, each one tasted, each one survived, until one day you realize the poison has become part of you and you are still standing.

What I understood first was small and stupid and human: my mother had a name I never knew.

She had been Mother. She had been the woman who hummed while kneading bread, who braided my hair with hands that smelled of beeswax, who taught me the alto line as a lullaby and told me, when I asked where she'd learned it, only that it was *an old song*. She had been dead so long that she had begun, in my memory, to feel less like a person than like weather—a presence that shaped everything without ever being seen entire.

And now here was her name, in a stranger's handwriting, in a palace archive four hundred miles and one impossible border away. Marguerite. A flower. A common enough name in the capital, Odile told me later—there had been three Marguerites in the kitchen staff alone—but hearing it, I felt the way I imagine a taster feels when a dish turns out to contain something they've never encountered before: recognition and strangeness in the same swallow.

"She was sent to close the Door," I said. My voice sounded far away to me. "That's what Grandmother told me. She crossed the border, joined the choir, and sang the hymn backward—or tried to—to seal it again. And the court gave her a title in the music. They wrote her into it."

"No," Odile said quietly. "They didn't write her into it. Think, girl. Use that methodical head of yours, the one that catalogs every poison by its aftertaste." She leaned forward, and her spectacles slid down onto her nose, and behind them her eyes were wet, which I had never once seen. "The hymn predates your mother by centuries. Every verse marks a place in Vhalen. Every verse has always marked a place in Vhalen. But the *title*—" she drew a shaking breath— "the title is newer. Added within the last fifty years, by the hand of the last choirmaster of the old line. He knew what she was. He knew what she carried. And when she died sealing the Door with her own body, he did the only thing a musician knows how to do with grief."

"He named the song for her," I said.

"He made sure that anyone who ever sang it, ever, would carry her name in their mouths without knowing it. Four hundred years of dynasties, and the one thing that survived every purge and every war and every attempt to erase your valley from the maps was a woman's name, hidden in plain sight, sung weekly by children who had no idea." Odile laughed—a short, broken sound. "I transcribed this hymn for thirty years, Ines. Thirty years chasing a map. And the whole time, the map was signed."

---

* * *

I walked down from the loft slowly, the way you walk when the ground feels newly made.

The valley was full of evening. Smoke rose from chimneys in straight gray columns, unhurried, unafraid. Somewhere downhill, children were arguing about whose turn it was to feed the goats, and the argument had the particular shrillness of an argument no one actually wanted to win. At the border stones—which were no longer a border, just a ring of cracked granite that the valley had voted to leave standing, as a monument, or a warning, or simply because moving them felt rude—a pair of travelers from the capital stood with their hats off, reading the new inscription the mason had cut into the largest stone. *Here the silence ended.* They'd come to see the place where the gods had been fed. Pilgrims, of a kind. We got a few each week now. Most of them left offerings at the stones: bread, flowers, once a whole wheel of cheese that Tomas ate before anyone could stop him.

My grandmother was on her porch, shelling beans into a bowl that had belonged to her grandmother, who had belonged to hers, back through the long unbroken chain of women who had kept the lock and called it a home.

She saw my face before I reached the steps.

"You found it," she said. Not a question. Grandmothers in the priestess line, I have come to believe, are issued omniscience along with the recipes.

"Her name was Marguerite." I sat down on the step below her, the way I had as a child, when sitting below meant I could lean back against her knees. I was too big for it now. I did it anyway. "The hymn was always titled for her. Everyone who ever sang it was singing her name."

My grandmother's hands went still over the bean bowl. For a long moment there was only the sound of the wind in the birches, and under it—I don't know how else to describe it—a low harmonic, almost subsonic, the way the air itself had hummed ever since the thirteenth turn passed. The valley's accumulated silence, the scholars called it, still resonating out of the stones. I had stopped needing scholars to explain it. Some sounds are just the world remembering.

"We knew her as Lira," my grandmother said at last. "The name she took when she came home. Her choir name stayed behind the border, the way everything of ours used to." She set down the bowl. "But yes. Marguerite. Your grandfather—he wasn't my husband yet then, just a boy who delivered flour to the palace kitchens—he heard the choir rehearse once, through a service door. He said there was one voice in the alto line that made the walls hum. One voice the other singers unconsciously tuned themselves to, the way iron filings find a magnet. He spent two years trying to learn her name. She was already gone by the time he did."

"And no one told me."

"What would we have told you?" My grandmother's voice was gentle, but gentleness in her was never softness; it was the firmness of hands that have held too many dying things. "That your mother was a lock? That the song you fell asleep to was a map to the thing she died closing? You were a child building immunities against poison, Ines. We let you keep one clean thing. One song that was only a song." She touched my hair, briefly, the way she used to check for fever. "We were wrong, probably. We were wrong about most things. But we were wrong out of love, which is the only wrong I know how to forgive."

I leaned my head against her knee.

Inside me—where they lived now, the quiet ones, the thirteen hungry shapes I had swallowed at the threshold of the Door—they stirred. Not hungrily. Not the way they had in those first terrible weeks, when every silence felt like a held breath and I caught myself listening for voices that weren't mine. Just a stirring, the way a sleeping household stirs when someone comes home late: a shifting of weight, a settling closer to warmth.

They had known her name all along, of course. They had known everything all along. Gods are not omniscient, whatever the theologians of the capital now claimed in their hastily written pamphlets—they are simply very, very old, and very patient, and they remember everything they have ever eaten, including prayers, including promises, including a young priestess's daughter singing an alto line in a chapel while the walls hummed.

*Marguerite,* they murmured, in the wordless way they had. Not her voice. Never her voice—I had refused them that bargain, and they had honored the refusal, mostly, in the way that mountains honor requests. But the shape of the name, the feeling of it, offered up inside my ribs like a flower laid on a grave.

"Yes," I said to them, silently. "I know."

---

* * *

We held the singing on the first frost-bright night of winter, because Odile insisted that a hymn deserved cold air—it carried better, she said, and besides, the original had been sung at the turning of the year, and some things you do properly or not at all.

The whole valley came. There was no question of them not coming; the valley had adopted the choir the way a tide adopts a ship, wholly and without discussion. Forty-one voices now, counting me, though I had not sung with them since the night at the stones. I had listened. I had taught. I had stood at the edge of the firelight with my arms crossed like a woman guarding a door, which, in fairness, was what I had been trained for.

But tonight was different. Tonight Odile had asked me—formally, publicly, in front of everyone, with the malicious precision of a woman who had waited thirty years for her moment—whether the alto line might be sung by someone who understood what it was for.

"It was always yours," she said. "By blood, by training, by title. *Marguerite and the Choir.*" She raised her eyebrows. "Unless you'd prefer we change the name. We could call it *Ines and the Choir*. It scans nearly as well."

The valley laughed. I felt forty faces turn toward me, and beyond them the dark hills, and beyond them the whole reopened world, waiting.

Tomas stood near the front with his hands folded. He was changed still—everyone said so, and everyone was right; the gods had worn him for a season during my flight to the border, and what they left behind was a man who moved carefully, spoke softly, and flinched at nothing anymore, because nothing could frighten him worse than what already had. He had stayed in Vhalen. He tended my garden now, and the graves, and he was learning, slowly, to taste food again after years of watching others do it. He nodded at me once. *Go on,* the nod said. *I'll be here.*

Odile lifted her baton.

And I stepped into the middle of the choir, into the space between the second sopranos and the tenors where the alto line lives, and I opened my mouth, and for the first time since the night the moon completed its turn—for the first time since I had taken thirteen starving divinities into my body and held them there the way a taster holds poison, deliberately, professionally, alive—I sang.

Not the verse eleven that opened doors. Not the reversed verses my mother had used to close them. Just the hymn. Whole, in order, all forty voices carrying their pieces of the map, and me carrying the alto line that was not a location at all but the thread that ran through every location, the road itself, the way between.

The sound went up into the frozen air and hung there.

I felt it happen in my body before I heard it with my ears: the hum. The walls of the world humming. The birches along the river catching the harmonic and passing it along, root to root, the way they'd been passing whispers underground all year. The border stones ringing like struck glass. And inside me, the quiet ones rising—not toward my throat, not reaching for my voice, but joining it. Thirteen voices finding the chord. Thirteen ancient, starved, magnificent horrors adding themselves beneath the melody like the lowest notes of an organ, felt more than heard, and the valley felt them, and nobody ran.

A child near the front—Maren the baker's youngest, who had been born after the Door opened and therefore considered gods ordinary—tugged her mother's sleeve and said, in the carrying whisper of the very young, "The song has *more* tonight."

It did. It always would now. I had made my peace with that on a night I hadn't expected to survive, and peace, I had learned, is not a thing you achieve once. It is a thing you taste daily, like prayer, like bread, like the small honest offerings I carried to the quiet ones each morning so they would never grow hungry again: the smell of frost, the argument about goats, my grandmother's laugh, Tomas's terrible jokes, the first note of a hymn sung by people who chose to be there.

When the last verse ended, the silence afterward was enormous and warm, and no one rushed to fill it. Snow began to fall, very lightly, the first of the year, and it caught in the firelight and in the singers' hair, and Odile lowered her baton and did not wipe her eyes, because she had given up pretending she was the sort of person who wouldn't cry at her own life's work being sung correctly at last.

My grandmother found me afterward. She pressed something into my hands: a scrap of cloth, very old, embroidered at the edge with a single marguerite daisy in thread gone gold with age.

"She stitched it the week before she crossed the border," my grandmother said. "She said if she couldn't take her name with her, she'd leave it where it could be found. I never understood what she meant until tonight."

I held the small flower in my palm and felt the gods look at it through my eyes, gently, the way you look at a photograph of someone you loved.

*Sing it again sometime,* they murmured, not demanding. Asking. They were learning to ask. I was learning to answer.

"Next year," I said aloud, and the valley, which had learned to stop being surprised by the woman who talked to herself, simply smiled and started banking the fires.

Above us, past the smoke and the snowfall, the moon rose—whole, ordinary, on its first turn of a new count. And I stood a moment longer in the falling white, one hand on the old embroidery, one hand flat over the quiet thunder in my chest, and I sang the alto line once more, alone, softly, to an audience of one valley and whatever else was listening—

—and every voice I carried, sang it with me.
