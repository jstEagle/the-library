## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The Ledgerhouse kept its accounts in ink that never dried.

Felix Crane had learned this on his first morning, seven years ago, when he'd tipped a fresh page toward the window light and watched the letters shimmer — not wet, exactly, but restless, as though the words themselves were still deciding what they had been spent on. Master Quillon, the senior clerk who had trained him, had caught him staring and rapped his knuckles with a bone stylus.

"Impossible things don't settle, boy," Quillon had said. "That's what makes them impossible. The ink remembers it's lying."

Felix had thought it a joke then. He knew better now. He knew that entry 4,417 — *one winter, sold whole, to the Duchess of Marrow Fen* — had been written in ink that shivered whenever the wind came off the fens, and that entry 6,002 — *the last echo of a dead man's voice, purchased by his widow, condition: fading* — had faded along with it until the page was nearly blank. He knew that some ledgers in the deep vaults were bound in skin that warmed when you touched them and others in covers that wept salt water on anniversaries no one could explain.

He knew all of this because he was very good at his work, and being very good at his work meant knowing everything and appearing to know only what was required.

It was a skill he had been practicing since before he could name it.

On this particular evening — the last of autumn, with frost already feathering the corners of the high windows — Felix sat alone in the Reckoning Room on the third floor of the Ledgerhouse, balancing accounts that would have driven a sane man to drink or prayer. A single lamp burned at his elbow. Before him lay the great quarterly ledger, open to a column of trades that included:

*One shadow, gently used, returned by a man who found living without it lighter than expected.*

*Three hours of yesterday, resold at a loss.*

*A door that opens onto the buyer's own front step, no matter where it stands — provenance disputed.*

And beneath them, in the Master Ledger's careful hand, the entry that had occupied the guild for three weeks now:

*One song, unsung, belonging to a forest that no longer sings. Buyer: private. Price: withheld.*

Felix dipped his pen and made his notation in the margin — *value unverifiable; seller's title unclear* — and paused.

In the walls above him, something scratched.

He did not look up. Looking up was the first mistake. Instead he turned the page with deliberate slowness, let his eyes rest on the figures, and opened the small listening place inside himself the way a man might unbar a door just wide enough to slip a hand through.

The rats began to talk.

They always talked. That was the thing no one at the Ledgerhouse suspected — not Quillon with his bone stylus, not Mistress Hark the auditor with her rings that clicked against her abacus, not even the Ledgermaster himself, Aldous Vane, whose office occupied the top floor like a held breath. They thought Felix Crane was a quiet young man of no particular gifts, meticulous, punctual, forgettable in the way that made him useful for work requiring discretion. They did not know that since childhood he had heard the speech of beasts — not words, exactly, but meaning that arrived dressed in whatever sounds the creature made, so that a dog's bark could carry a sentence and a rat's squeak could carry a grievance long enough to fill an evening.

His grandmother had told him once, when he was small enough to sit on her lap and smell the lavender and pipe smoke in her shawl, that the gift ran in families. She had told him bird stories — long, rambling tales about wrens who carried messages between kingdoms and foxes who kept promises older than roads. He had thought them stories. He had learned, later, to think of them as instructions he hadn't yet learned to read.

Now he listened to rats.

Not by choice, precisely. But a man alone in a city needs voices, and the rats in the Ledgerhouse walls were better company than most clerks. They were gossips, historians, complainers of magnificent stamina. The colony behind the third-floor wainscoting was led by an enormous scarred female Felix privately called the Duchess, and tonight she was holding forth.

"—telling you," she was saying, in the dry rustle of claws on lath, "it isn't hunger. Hunger moves through a place. This *sits*. My cousin's line came down from the north woods two generations back, and they say the forest there has gone quiet. Not empty. Quiet. Like a room after the argument ends."

A younger rat — Felix thought of him as Pip, though he had never named any of them aloud — chittered doubtfully. "Forests go quiet in winter."

"Not like this." The Duchess's voice dropped into the register rats used for serious things, which was almost below hearing, a vibration more felt than heard. "My cousin says the birds left without flying south. Just — went. And the streams still run but nothing drinks from them. And there's a grey in the trees, under the bark, like ash under skin." A pause, claws shifting. "She calls it the hollowing."

"Hollowing," Pip repeated, tasting the word. "Hollow like a tooth."

"Hollow like a house where everyone died but the furniture stayed."

Felix's pen had stopped moving. A drop of ink gathered at the nib and fell, blooming black on the margin beside his neat notation. He blotted it absently, heart ticking faster than the work warranted.

Rats exaggerated. Rats dramatized. A hard winter upcountry, a blight in one stretch of woods — these things happened. He had balanced enough accounts of impossible trades to know that the world leaked strangeness at every seam, and most of it meant nothing, and none of it was his business. His business was columns and margins and the careful fiction that Felix Crane noticed nothing beyond his ledger lines.

But the Duchess did not exaggerate. That was the trouble with her. In seven years of eavesdropping he had found her reports of grain stores, of poisonings, of the comings and goings of cats, to be accurate to a fault. Rats kept better records than the guild did; they simply kept them in their teeth and their bloodlines instead of books.

*The hollowing.* He wrote the word on a scrap of blotting paper, looked at it, and fed the scrap to the lamp flame. Some words were safer as light than as ink.

Above him the scratching resumed, moved along the wall toward the corner where the warm chimney flue passed, and faded. The room settled back into its usual silence — the tick of the cooling stove, the whisper of the never-drying ink, the distant clink of the night porter making his rounds three floors down.

Felix sat for a while with his hands flat on the ledger, feeling the particular loneliness that came over him at this hour. It was a familiar visitor. It arrived every evening around the time the lamps needed trimming, and it always said the same thing: *you live among people who would put you in a ledger if they knew what you were.*

Because that was the truth of the Ledgerhouse, wasn't it? It traded in impossible things. Felix was an impossible thing. Somewhere in those vaults were entries for ears that heard too much, for tongues that spoke to animals, for the second sight and the green sight and the gift of finding lost children in fog. Buyers existed for such things. The guild had sold them before. The only reason Felix remained a clerk rather than an item was that no one had ever caught him being anything other than dull.

So he had built his life the way he built his columns: carefully, invisibly, each number where it belonged. He rose early, worked late, ate the same supper at the same chophouse, walked the same route home past the shuttered milliner's and the chapel with the broken bell. He gave to charity in small, unremarkable amounts. He laughed at Quillon's jokes a half-beat late, as a man does when his mind is properly on his figures. He had made himself a footnote in the story of his own life, and footnotes were safe.

Most evenings, the safety was enough. Most evenings he could sit in this lamplight with the rats murmuring in the walls like distant relatives at a funeral, and feel something adjacent to contentment — wary contentment, contentment with its coat on and one hand on the door. Tonight, though, the word *hollowing* sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

He finished the column. He closed the ledger, and its cover exhaled faintly against his palms, the way the big books did when the day's entries were done. He trimmed the lamp, took up his candle, and made his way down through the sleeping building — past the counting room with its long tables and its high barred windows, past the vault door with its nine locks, past the portrait of Aldous Vane in the entrance hall, painted smiling, which somehow made it worse.

Vane's painted eyes followed him, as they always did. Felix had long ago decided the painter had simply been talented. Mostly he had decided that.

Outside, the city was folding itself away for the night. Frost smoke rose off the cobbles. A cat watched him from a wall with the flat golden patience of its kind, and Felix gave it a small nod — *evening* — and received in return a slow blink that meant, in cat, roughly, *I see you, hearer, and I am not impressed, but I will not sell you either.* Cats were expensive gossip. He appreciated the discretion.

He was halfway down the steps to the street when the Duchess spoke again, one last time, from the gap in the foundation stones beside the door. Her voice was low, and it carried something new in it — not fear, exactly. Rats did not do fear the way men did. It carried urgency.

"Hearer," she said. She had never called him that before. Seven years of pleasant, impersonal squeaking, and now this. "Hearer. There is a thing coming down the road toward your house. Small. Two-legged. Smelling of pine sap and blood and old, old words. My cousin saw it pass the north gate at dusk, walking, walking, walking, and the dogs would not bark at it, and the horses turned to watch it go." A pause. Claws ticked on stone. "The wild is sending something, hearer. Or someone is sending the wild. Either way — it will knock before dawn."

Felix stood very still on the frozen steps, his candle guttering in the wind off the river, and felt the evening tilt beneath him like a deck shifting underfoot.

"That's not my business," he said under his breath, to the rat, to the night, to himself. "I balance accounts. I don't make them."

The wall said nothing. Rats, having delivered their news, considered themselves paid in full.

Felix Crane pulled his coat tight, descended into the frost-smoked street, and walked home the same way he always walked, past the same shuttered windows, telling himself the same comfortable lies at the same steady pace.

Behind him, in the walls of the Ledgerhouse, the ink went on drying without ever drying, and somewhere far to the north, in a forest gone quiet as a held breath, something small and stubborn kept walking.
