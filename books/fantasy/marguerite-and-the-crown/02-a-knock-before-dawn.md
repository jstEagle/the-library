## 2. A Knock Before Dawn

The knock came at the hour Felix thought of as his own — the grey sliver between night and morning when even the Ledgerhouse's lamps burned low and the clerks' dreams were at their thinnest.

He heard it first through the walls, the way he heard everything. Three strikes, uneven, spaced too far apart. Not the fist of a creditor or the boot of a guild courier. This was someone leaning on a door to stay upright.

The rats felt it too. He could feel them pausing in their midnight traffic along the joists above his narrow room, a hundred small hearts going still at once, the way birds go quiet before weather.

*Someone is coming,* said the oldest rat, the one with the torn ear who lived behind the coal cellar and considered herself the building's true proprietor. *Someone wrong-shaped.*

Felix sat up in the dark. He had learned long ago not to argue with the rats about wrongness. They had been reading the Ledgerhouse longer than any clerk.

He dressed quickly, pulling on the plain grey coat that made him invisible among the junior accountants, and went down through the sleeping house — past the counting room with its locked drawers of impossible things, past the assay hall where a jarred thunderclap hummed faintly against its glass, down the stairs worn hollow by two centuries of feet, to the great front door with its iron knocker shaped like an open ledger book.

Through the peephole, by the light of the guttering street lamp, he saw her.

A woman. Or what remained of one. She stood swaying like a reed in current, one hand pressed flat against the door as though it were the only true thing left in the world. Her cloak had been fine once — he could see the ghost of silver thread along the hem, the kind of work that cost more than a clerk earned in a year — but now it was soaked through, torn, crusted with mud and something darker. Her hair had gone white at the temples though her face was young. And she was looking directly at the peephole.

Looking at him, though there was no way she could see through the brass.

"Open," she said. Her voice was a dry leaf. "Before they finish what they started."

Every rule Felix had ever learned told him to wake the night porter, to fetch Master Quillon, to be a small grey cog and let the machine handle it. The Ledgerhouse did not open its doors for half-dead women in ruined cloaks. The Ledgerhouse weighed them first.

His hand lifted the bolt anyway.

*You fool,* said the rat in the walls, without much conviction.

*Yes,* Felix agreed silently, and opened the door.

* * *

Cold poured in with her, river-cold, carrying the smell of wet stone and crushed ferns and something else beneath it — something green and old, like the air inside a forest that has never been cut. She crossed the threshold as if crossing a border, stopped three steps inside, and turned to look back out at the street with an expression Felix would carry for the rest of his life.

Not fear. Vigilance. The look of a soldier holding a wall she knows will fall.

"They're coming," she said again, softer now. "But not yet. Not tonight." Then her eyes found his, and whatever she saw there made her mouth twist — not quite a smile, not quite grief. "You hear them, don't you? The small ones. The ones in the walls."

Felix's blood went to ice water. In nineteen years, no living soul had spoken that truth aloud to him. His grandmother had come closest, once, before the fever took her — *mind the birds, Felix, they're better company than people* — but she had said it the way old women say strange things, wrapped in dementia's plausible deniability.

"I don't know what you mean," he said, and it was the easiest lie he had ever told, because he had rehearsed it all his life.

The woman studied him. Up close he could see the death in her properly now: not a wound, exactly, but a wasting, as if something were being drawn out of her drop by drop, some essential fluid, the way a candle is drawn down by flame. Her eyes were the brown of autumn oak leaves, and they held a patience that frightened him more than her condition did.

"No," she agreed. "Of course you don't."

And then she reached beneath her cloak and lifted out the child.

* * *

Felix had noticed nothing of her until that moment. That was the second impossible thing, though he didn't know it yet — that a woman could carry a child of four or five years against her body and give off no sign, no weight-shift, no small sound, nothing. The girl hung against the woman's hip like something carried so often it had become part of her.

She was small and thin, with dark hair cut raggedly, as if with a knife. Her clothes were strange — not the strange of poverty, which Felix knew intimately, but the strange of elsewhere: a tunic of undyed wool, worked at the collar with patterns that hurt to look at directly, the way a knot hurts if you try to trace it with your eye. Around her neck hung a pendant of grey stone on a leather cord, and the stone was warm. Felix knew it was warm because when the woman set the girl down, the heat of it reached him from a foot away, like a hand held near a banked fire.

The girl looked at Felix. She did not cry. She did not hide behind the woman's legs. She looked at him the way the rats looked at new poison — with total, ancient attention — and then she looked past him, up the stairwell, into the deep of the house, and her lip curled with a distaste so complete it was almost comical.

"She doesn't like it here," the woman said. "Too much iron. Too many numbers." She swayed, caught herself on the doorframe. "Listen to me. I don't have time for the shape of this conversation, only its bones. Her name is Marguerite. Remember it. There may come a day when remembering it is the only thing anyone remembers."

"Mistress," Felix began, "you need a physician, I can wake—"

"You can wake no one." Her voice cracked like ice on a pond. "If you wake them, they'll weigh her. They'll put her in a ledger. They'll write a number beside her name and the number will be the end of everything." She gripped his wrist then, and her fingers were burning hot despite the cold rolling off her cloak, and Felix understood with a lurch that whatever was killing her was doing it fast. "I know what this house is. I've known since before you were born. I know what your masters took. Do you understand me? I know what they *took.*"

He didn't. He nodded anyway.

Outside, far off across the rooftops, a dog began to bark — and then stopped mid-bark, and Felix heard why. Not with his ears. With the other hearing, the one he kept folded away like contraband. Something was moving through the streets toward them. Something that made the dogs go silent and the rats press flat to the floorboards and the moths in the eaves stop beating their wings all at once.

The woman heard it too. He watched her hear it. Her head tilted, birdlike, and her face went grim and calm in the same breath.

"Not tonight," she murmured. "They're circling. Good. Circling means uncertain, and uncertainty buys hours." She crouched, with the care of someone whose body has begun making formal complaints, and brought her face level with the child's. What passed between them then, Felix could not follow. The woman spoke — but not in any tongue he knew. The sounds were soft and layered, vowels stacked inside consonants like seeds inside fruit, and though he understood not one word, some animal part of his mind wept at the sound of it, the way one weeps at a song from a country one has never seen and somehow misses.

The girl answered. One word. Two syllables. She touched the woman's cheek with a small hand.

Then the woman straightened, pushed the girl gently forward, and stepped back out over the threshold into the cold.

"Mistress—" Felix said.

"The door stays shut behind me. That's the bargain. You keep her, you keep her hidden, you keep her *unweighed*, and in exchange I never tell you what I know about your grandmother." She smiled then, fully, and it transformed her ruined face into something luminous and terrible. "Which is cruel of me. But cruelty keeps children alive where kindness doesn't. Ask any fox."

She walked out into the empty street, and the fog took her in stages — boots, hem, cloak, shoulders — and just before the fog closed over her head she paused, tilted her face up to the grey sky, and spoke one word aloud in that other language. A question, by its music. Or perhaps a farewell. Perhaps in that tongue they were the same word.

Then she was gone, and the silence she left behind was worse than any sound.

* * *

Felix shut the door. Bolted it. Stood with his forehead against the cold iron for a long moment while his heart attempted to climb out through his ribs.

Behind him, small bare feet shifted on the flagstones.

He turned. The girl — Marguerite — stood in the middle of the entrance hall, in the pool of shadow beneath the gallery, surrounded on every side by the Ledgerhouse's quiet ostentation: the marble busts of dead Ledgermasters, the framed charter signed by a king two generations gone, the great brass scales in their glass case that could weigh a rumor against a coin. She surveyed it all with the flat contempt of an empress reviewing a provincial garrison.

Then she tipped her head back and looked at the ceiling — at the beams, at the eaves, at the hidden highways of the walls — and she spoke.

It was a question. Felix knew it was a question the way he knew rain was coming, by pressure in the old scars of his mind. And the house *answered.*

Not the way a house answers — with settling timber and wind in chimneys. It answered the way a court answers a herald. From the walls came a rustle of a thousand small movements; from under the floor, a scratching chorus; from the eaves, the papery flutter of roosting things waking; and outside, beyond the shuttered windows, a rising murmur — dogs, cats, rats, crows stirring on the gutters, moths against the glass — a whole hidden city finding its voice at once, and every voice bent toward the small dark-haired girl standing in the hall.

Felix stood in the middle of it, trembling.

Because he could understand them. That was his curse, his secret, the thing he had never told a living person: he heard what beasts said, always had, and had spent his whole life pretending the world was mute. And what they were saying now, in a hundred tongues, in the speech of whiskers and wings and claws on slate, was the same word, over and over, from the coal cellar to the belfry:

*Crown. Crown. Crown-child. The crown has come home.*

Marguerite lowered her gaze from the ceiling and found Felix staring at her. For a moment neither of them moved. Somewhere above, the torn-eared rat said, very distinctly, in the tone of a landlady assessing a tenant:

*Well, boy. Now the trouble starts.*

The girl said something to Felix in the language of seeds and fruit and countries he had never seen. It was, he suspected, not a compliment.

And far away, out in the fog-bound streets, something that circled stopped circling — and began, patiently, to wait.
