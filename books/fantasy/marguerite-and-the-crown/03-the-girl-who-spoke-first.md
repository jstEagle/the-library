## 3. The Girl Who Spoke First

They locked her in the counting room on the second night, and the Ledgerhouse pretended this was a kindness.

Felix heard the argument through the floor of his garret, muffled by oak and plaster but not by enough. The assessors had gathered in Vane's study below — Quillmaster Dree with her dry rustle of papers, the fat appraiser Hobb who could put a price on anything including grief, and two junior clerks whose opinions were worth exactly what they were paid. Their voices rose and fell like water finding cracks.

"A feral child of perhaps ten years," Hobb was saying, in the tone he used for livestock. "No kin, no name she'll answer to, no tongue any clerk can place. She's either worthless or priceless, and I've built forty years on never guessing wrong between those two."

"She bit Marlow," said one of the juniors, aggrieved.

"Marlow should have washed his hands first. Children smell intent." That was Dree, dry as dust. "The question is provenance. Everything has provenance. Where did the mother come from? Where was she going?"

"And where is she now?" Hobb again. "Dead in a ditch, most likely, or gone to ground. Either way, the asset remains."

*The asset.* Felix lay in the dark with his hands behind his head and felt the word settle over the child like a ledger-line drawn through her small body. He had worked for the Ledgerhouse six years. He knew what happened to assets that could not be catalogued: they were liquidated, which was a beautiful word for a terrible thing, or they were archived, which was worse, because archives were cellars and cellars were where the Ledgerhouse kept the things it did not want to remember it owned.

Below him, Aldous Vane spoke for the first time, and every other voice went quiet the way birds go quiet when a hawk crosses the yard.

"We will not price her tonight," the Ledgermaster said. His voice was mild, unhurried, the voice of a man reading a bedtime story to himself. "We will keep her comfortable, observe her, and learn what she is. Comfortable, gentlemen. She is to be fed, warmed, and left undisturbed. The counting room will serve. It has a lock."

"It's full of moths," said the junior clerk.

"Then she will have company."

There was a small laugh at that, quickly smothered. Felix did not laugh. He was listening past the voices, down through the foundations, to the rats in the wall behind the counting room, and the rats were saying something that made his scalp prickle.

*The little loud one,* they chattered, in their quick grey gossip. *The little loud one smells of far away. Smells of under-the-world. The moths go to her. Why do the moths go to her?*

Felix had asked himself the same question since the dawn the woman had collapsed against the guild's door with the child clutched to her chest like a stolen treasure. He had watched from the upstairs window as the clerks carried the girl inside and the woman vanished into the fog — walked, staggered, *melted*, he still wasn't sure — and he had heard, in the same moment, every dog within three streets begin to howl, and then stop all at once, as if corrected.

Since then the beasts of the city had been acting strangely. The pigeons roosted facing the Ledgerhouse. The horses in the stable below stamped and whickered at nothing whenever the child was carried past their doors. And last night, when Felix had gone down for his bread and cheese, a moth had landed on his knuckle and stayed there, trembling, as though it wanted something from him and couldn't ask.

He had spent six years teaching himself that the voices in the walls were weather. Rats gossiped; gulls complained; cats made demands. None of it meant anything. None of it was *for* him. That was the arrangement he had made with his own life: hear everything, acknowledge nothing, want nothing, be nothing remarkable. A man who balanced impossible accounts and kept his head down lived long in the Ledgerhouse. A man who listened too closely to things without ledgers did not.

But the rats kept saying *under-the-world*, and the moths kept gathering, and somewhere below him, behind a locked door, a child was speaking a language older than the guild, older than the kingdom, older maybe than the stones the city was built on — and Felix Crane lay awake and admitted, in the privacy of his own skull, that he was going to go down there.

He told himself it was professional curiosity. He told himself an accurate assessment served the guild, and the guild fed him, and loyalty was simply arithmetic done correctly. He was very good at telling himself things. It was, in its way, the only language he had ever been fluent in besides the beasts'.

It was a lie, and some small animal part of him knew it even then.

* * *

The counting room occupied the corner of the ground floor where the guild once, generations ago, had weighed out coin for kings. It was a handsome room, high-windowed, its walls lined with cabinets of drawers each smaller than a coffin, in which the guild kept samples of everything it had ever traded: feathers that fell upward, seeds that sprouted only in shadow, a thimbleful of river that remembered being the sea. The moths had gotten into the drawers years ago and bred among the impossible inventory until the whole room shimmered faintly at dusk, like the inside of a bell.

Felix came down after midnight with a covered dish of stew, a heel of bread, and a candle stub, moving along the corridor with the particular silence of a man who has practiced being unremarkable until it became a craft. The corridor lamps were turned low. The night porter, old Tam, dozed at his post with a mug of warmed wine breathing beside him; Felix had salted the wine's companion jug earlier, not enough to harm, just enough to make sleep persuasive. Tam snored gently. Felix took the counting room key from the board at his elbow, and his hand shook, and he watched it shake with a kind of distant interest, as though it belonged to someone braver than he felt.

The lock turned with a sound like a swallowed word.

She was sitting in the corner farthest from the door, up on the third shelf of a cabinet, folded into the space above the drawers as if she had been poured there. Her knees were drawn up. Her hair — dark, matted, full of city — hung around a face that was thin as a knife and twice as sharp. Her eyes caught his candlelight and held it, unblinking, the way owls hold light, and Felix understood immediately that she had known he was coming before he had known it himself.

He stopped. He set the dish on the floor, slowly, and stepped back from it, and crouched down to make himself small, which was the first thing his grandmother had ever taught him about frightened animals, back when she'd called her knowledge bird stories and he'd believed them to be nonsense.

"I'm not going to touch you," he said. "Nobody sent me. I brought food because you haven't eaten."

She didn't move. The moths moved. They had risen from the cabinets when the door opened, dozens of them, pale grey-brown wings stirring the candlelight into a slow snowfall, and instead of scattering from him they drifted toward the shelf, toward her, settling on her shoulders and knees and matted hair until she seemed almost clothed in them. They trembled. She didn't.

Felix had grown up hearing animals the way other children grew up hearing lullabies, and he had learned to read the grammar of their attention: the flat wariness of a cat assessing a stranger, the bright arithmetic of a crow counting fingers. What came off the girl now was none of these. It was older and stranger — a stillness like winter water, deep enough that things could drown in it.

"You don't understand me," he said quietly. "Of course you don't. No one does."

And then, because the room was silent and the porter slept and no one living could hear him, he did the thing he had never once done in front of another human being. He spoke the way he spoke to the walls.

"The food is good," he said, in the loose wordless grammar of beast-speech, the speech of scent and posture and small honest sounds, the speech he had never had words for because no human language contained it. "Warm. Not a trick. Eat, little one. No one will know."

The girl's head tilted. One degree. Two.

The moths lifted off her all at once, a soft grey exhalation, and swirled around the candle, and Felix felt the air change the way it changed when a flock decided something. And the girl answered him.

Not in beast-speech. In her own tongue — the one no clerk could place, the one that had come pouring out of her at the gate two days ago like water from a broken jar. But she answered *him*, clearly, deliberately, her eyes fixed on his face, and the sounds she made were unlike anything Felix had ever heard a human mouth make. They were layered. That was the only way he could describe it, later, when he let himself think about it at all: her voice carried more than one sound at a time, the way a chord carries more than one note, so that listening to her was like hearing a girl speak and something vaster speak through her, both at once, both meaning.

He didn't understand a single word. He understood all of it.

His eyes stung. He blinked the sting away, furious at himself, and pushed the dish an inch closer with one finger.

"Eat," he said again, in the beast-grammar, and gestured, and this time she unfolded.

She came down from the cabinet the way water comes down a wall — no wasted motion, no human hesitation, just a flowing descent that ended in a crouch beside the dish, always angled away from him, always ready. She ate with her hands, fast and precise, watching him the entire time, and the moths settled on her again as though they had merely stepped aside to give her room. When the bowl was empty she licked it, considered the bread, and took it, holding it against her chest rather than eating it — saving it, Felix realized, the habit of someone who had gone hungry often enough to know that the next meal is a rumor.

"There's more," he said. "Every night, if you want it. I'll come."

She cocked her head at him. Then she said something — short, questioning, the rising inflection unmistakable in any language — and pointed one grimy finger at his chest.

"My name?" He huffed a laugh, surprised out of him. "Felix. Felix Crane."

"Fff-lix." She tried it, mangling it beautifully, and something in his chest turned over. Six years in the Ledgerhouse and no one had spoken his name with anything except irritation or indifference, and here was a feral child in a locked room pronouncing it like a spell half-remembered.

"And you?" he asked, knowing it was useless. "What's your name?"

She looked at him for a long moment. The moths stirred. And then she drew herself up slightly, there in the candlelight amid the drawers of impossible things, and spoke a single word in her layered, chorded voice — a word that sounded like wind deciding to become a river, like the first syllable of spring pronounced underground — and though he could not translate it, Felix felt it land in him with the weight of a name, a true one, the kind people used to believe could not be given but only found.

"Marguerite," he repeated, because the word his own mouth chose sounded something like that, flower-like, old. "Marguerite."

Her eyes narrowed — not in anger. In *assessment*. As if he had passed some examination he hadn't known he was sitting.

* * *

He came back the next night. And the next.

He told himself each time would be the last, that the risk compounded nightly, that Tam's drugged wine could not hold forever, that Vane's gentle watchfulness missed nothing in his house. All true. He went anyway. There are hungers that have nothing to do with food, and Felix had starved on the other kind for six years without letting himself name the ache, and now here was a creature in a locked room who looked at him as though he were a language she intended to learn.

She learned fast. By the fourth night she had assembled a vocabulary of gesture between them: *food*, *stay*, *go*, *listen*. She taught him three words of her own tongue, tapping objects — the candle, the door, his own hand — and pronounced his corrections with a fierce precision that made him feel like the slow pupil. She would not be touched. When he reached too near she flowed backward onto the cabinets like smoke returning to a chimney, and the moths went with her, and he learned to keep his hands visible and empty, palms out, the way one does with wild things and with gods.

On the fifth night, he heard her speak to the moths.

He had begun to notice that the insects behaved strangely in her presence — not swarming, exactly, but *attending*. On the fifth night she sat cross-legged on the floor, having eaten, and held out one arm, and a dozen moths came to her, walking up her sleeve with their feathery antennae forward, and she bent her head over them and spoke.

It was different from her speech to him. Softer. Slower. The layered quality deepened until it was less like a voice and more like weather given syntax, and the moths turned their heads — moths do not turn their heads, Felix thought wildly, moths do not *listen* — and their wings shivered in unison, all together, like a congregation saying amen.

And Felix, crouched in the shadows by the door with his heart hammering, understood that he was hearing the oldest conversation in the world. The one the guild's founding charters dismissed as superstition. The one his grandmother's bird stories had danced around all his childhood. Speech that was not metaphor, not fancy, not the projection of a lonely child onto indifferent insects — but address, and answer, and covenant.

The girl finished. The moths rose from her arm in a single spiral, circled the candle three times, and streamed up into the gap beneath the eaves, out through some crack Felix had never noticed, gone into the night.

She looked at him then. Directly. With an expression he had not seen on her before — an expression that was almost pity, almost apology, as if she had shown him something without meaning to and was sorry for what it would cost him.

"What did you tell them?" he whispered, in beast-speech, forgetting to be careful.

She considered. Then she tapped the floor — *listen* — and pointed at the wall, at the darkness beyond it, at the whole breathing night outside the Ledgerhouse, and said one word in her chorded voice.

He didn't know it. But the rats did. Behind the wainscoting, in the sudden hush of a hundred small bodies going still, Felix heard the grey gossip turn over the word like a coin bitten for gold:

*Yes. Yes. That is the word for home. The little loud one says the wild remembers us. The little loud one says the wild is dying.*

Felix sat back against the door, cold despite the summer night, and looked at the child in the candlelight — this scrap of a girl, this *asset*, this last speaker of a dead world's tongue — and felt the careful architecture of his hidden life shift beneath him like ice in thaw.

Above, faint through the floors, a door closed in the Ledgermaster's study. Footsteps crossed the hall. Paused, briefly, outside the counting room door.

Moved on.

Felix did not breathe again until they reached the stairs.
