## 4. What the Rats Remember

The Ledgerhouse kept its true accounts below the waterline.

Felix had learned this in his first year, when he was still young enough to be curious and poor enough to be invisible. The counting rooms above dealt in the guild's respectable trade — salt futures, silk contracts, the quiet buying and selling of rare names — but the cellar was where the impossible things were weighed. A jar of last breaths. A key that opened any door that had ever been locked in anger. Feathers from a bird that had not existed since the first kings died. They sat on shelves in the dark like saints' bones, dusted twice a year by clerks who wore gloves and never spoke above a whisper, as though loudness might wake them.

It was also where the rats lived, and the rats were the reason Felix came down at all.

He told himself it was duty. The cellar needed an inventory before quarter-day; someone had to count the jars of breath and note which had gone flat. But his feet knew better than his excuses. He took the lamp down the spiral stair past midnight, when the building above had gone quiet as a held breath, and he set the light on a barrel head and said, to no one, to everyone:

"I'm listening."

The rats came the way they always did — not all at once but in a slow grey tide, out of the gaps between stones, along the shelf-edges, dropping soft as sleet from the joists. There were dozens of them. Perhaps hundreds; they were never all present at once, and Felix suspected they were never entirely separate either, that somewhere beneath their individual whiskered selves ran a single long conversation, like water under ice. The oldest of them lived behind the far wall, in the cavity where the foundation met the river's old course. The others called her Grandmother Teeth, though not in words. In words she was only a rat, scarred and greying, one ear chewed to lace.

*You came back,* she said. Rats did not speak in words, exactly. What Felix heard was meaning wearing the shape of words, the way moonlight wears the shape of a coin. *The tall one with the cold hands. You smell of ink and fear.*

"Fear?"

*You always smell of fear. It is your best smell. Most of your kind reek of nothing at all.* She settled onto a ledger crate, curling her tail around her feet with the composure of a duchess. *But tonight there is something else under it. Something new.*

Felix crouched, elbows on knees, and made himself comfortable the way you make yourself comfortable before bad news. "There's a child upstairs," he said. "A girl. She talks and nobody understands her."

*We know.*

"You know?"

*The moths told the mice. The mice told us. The sparrows heard it through the shutters.* Her whiskers twitched, which in rat-speech was something between a shrug and a sigh. *She speaks the First Tongue.*

The phrase landed in him strangely, like a stone dropped into a well he hadn't known he carried. He waited for the splash and it didn't come. "First Tongue," he repeated. "Meaning what? Before Common? Before Old Imperial?"

*Before,* said Grandmother Teeth, and the word had weight enough to bend the air. *Before the kingdoms. Before the bells. Before your kind decided that naming a thing gave you the right to own it.* She looked at him then, directly, with eyes like two drops of tar, and Felix felt the peculiar vertigo of being seen by something small. *You have heard it your whole life and never known. Every beast speaks it underneath its other speech. It is the speech under speech. And now a child walks into your house of ledgers speaking it whole, and your masters do not know what they are holding.*

"Do you?" he asked. "Know what she is?"

The cellar went quiet. Not silent — quiet, the way a room goes quiet when someone important has entered it. Felix realized the other rats had stopped moving. Dozens of small bodies frozen on shelves and barrels and the cold flagstones, all facing the old rat, waiting.

Grandmother Teeth groomed her paw once, twice, three times, taking her time about it, and Felix understood she was deciding something, and that whatever she decided would matter.

*Come closer,* she said at last. *Not to me. To the wall.*

He rose and crossed to the far wall, the one against the river's buried course, and set his palm against the stone. It was cold in the way deep things are cold, and damp, and under the damp he felt — or thought he felt, or the rats made him feel — a faint rhythm, very slow, like a heartbeat heard through a floor.

*A promise was made here once,* Grandmother Teeth said, coming to sit near his foot. *Before this house was a house. Before it was anything but a hill with stones on top of it. Your kind and ours made a bargain, sealed at the Standing Stones in the north country, and the bargain had a sound, and everything alive could hear it if it listened. We called it the Covenant. Your kind had fancier words for it, written in books your masters keep locked. It said: the wild shall speak and man shall hear, and man shall take and the wild shall be asked, and between the two realms there shall always be a door, and the door shall have a keeper, and the keeper shall have a voice.*

Felix's mouth had gone dry. "And?"

*And the stones went quiet.*

"When?"

The old rat's whiskers drooped. For a moment she looked every day of her age, and rats do not age gracefully; they simply wear down, like coins passed through too many hands.

*Thirty years ago,* she said. *More or less. One season the Covenant's sound was everywhere — in the rivers, in the roots, in the bell-song of the city, faint but there, the way you can hear the sea in a shell. And the next season it was gone. Not faded. Gone, cut, the way a thread is cut. The wild realm began to hollow from that day. The forests went grey at the edges. The beasts forgot their old dreams. And we — * her tail tightened around her feet — *we have been telling each other the story ever since, generation after generation, so that none of us would forget what was taken. My grandmother heard the cutting happen. She was in the walls of this very house.*

"What happened?" Felix asked. "What cut it?"

*That,* said Grandmother Teeth, *is the question, isn't it.*

She let the silence stretch until it hurt. Around the cellar the other rats had begun to move again, slowly, resuming their small occupations, but Felix noticed that several of them had positioned themselves between him and the stair. He chose not to think about what that meant.

*Here is what my grandmother saw,* the old rat went on. *In the year the stones went quiet, men came to this house. Men with ledgers. They worked in the cellar, in the dark, for nine nights, and they carried up boxes that clinked like coins and smelled like blood, and on the ninth night one of them spoke aloud a sentence my grandmother remembered word for word because it was the last time she ever heard the First Tongue in a human mouth.* She paused. *Shall I say it?*

"Yes."

*He said: 'Let the wild keep its silence and we will keep the keys.'*

The lamp flame leaned sideways, though there was no draft. Felix stood with his hand still on the cold wall and felt the sentence go into him like a splinter working toward the heart.

"Who," he said. His voice came out wrong, scraped thin. "Which men. Which ledger."

*My grandmother did not read faces the way you do,* said Grandmother Teeth. *But she remembered the voice, and she remembered that the others called him Master Vane.*

---

He did not sleep. He told himself he would, after he'd checked one thing, and then another, and by the third hour past midnight he was sitting at his narrow desk in the clerks' dormitory with a candle stub guttering, writing on scrap paper in his smallest hand:

*Aldous Vane. Thirty-one years in post. Age — sixty-one? Sixty-three? Thirty years ago he would have been thirty. Young for a master. Too young.*

*Unless he wasn't a master yet. Unless he became one because of it.*

The candle spat. Somewhere above, in the rafters, a mouse moved — a small dry rustle, and beneath the rustle, faint as a thought at the edge of sleep, the mouse's mind touched his: *the tall one is awake, the tall one smells of storm.* He shut it out. He was getting worse at shutting it out, and that frightened him more than the rats' story did, because a man who listens cannot unlisten, and a man who knows cannot unknow, and Felix Crane had built his entire careful life on the principle that knowing too much was how people in the Ledgerhouse ended up in the river.

And yet.

*Let the wild keep its silence and we will keep the keys.*

He turned the phrase over the way he turned over suspect entries in a ledger, looking for the flaw, the place where the sums refused to balance. If the Covenant had been real — and the girl was real, the girl who spoke the speech under speech, the girl the beasts answered through the shutters — then breaking it would have been the largest theft in history. Not of gold. Of sound. Of the whole conversation between the human world and the wild one. And whoever broke it would hold both sides of the silence: the wild unable to demand redress, mankind unable even to hear that anything had been demanded.

And the Ledgerhouse traded in impossible things. That was the whole of its business. Where did impossible things come from, Felix wondered, when the source of all impossibility had been dammed up thirty years ago?

From the reservoir, he thought. From whatever pooled behind the broken covenant. The guild hadn't just committed a crime. It had built its vaults on the floodplain.

He blew out the candle and lay in the dark with his heart going like a trapped bird, and above him, through three floors of timber and plaster, he could just barely hear it: the girl, awake too, in her locked room, murmuring to something. Moths, probably. Or the dark itself. Speaking the language that predated every kingdom, in the house of the men who had helped kill it.

---

Morning brought the ordinary machinery of the Ledgerhouse grinding back to life, and Felix put on his ordinary face and went to work among the ordinary lies.

He was good at his job. That was the bitter joke of it — he was genuinely good, meticulous, quick with figures, tireless at cross-referencing. Senior Clerk Bramblethwaite said Felix Crane could find a mislaid farthing in a burning building. What Bramblethwaite didn't say — what no one said, because no one knew — was that Felix found things because half the building whispered to him. Rats knew where papers were stored; they nested in the warm backs of forgotten files. Moths congregated wherever vellum was sweetest. Even the spiders seemed to spin their webs preferentially across documents someone wanted hidden, as though the building itself had opinions about its secrets.

So when Felix requested access to the historical archives — "for the solstice audit, sir, cross-checking the founding valuations against current holdings" — the request flowed through the morning's channels smooth as oil, and by noon he had a brass token and permission to enter the Record Hall for two hours, supervised, gloves required.

The Record Hall occupied the second floor's east wing, a long room of green glass and iron shelving, where ten thousand ledgers slept in ranked rows like soldiers of some vast and patient army. Dust hung in the slanted light. The supervising clerk, a dried apple of a man named Pell, installed himself at a desk by the door with a newspaper and the particular deafness of the officially present.

Felix began with the founding records, as his pretext required. He worked methodically, loudly, visibly — pulling volumes, making notes, letting Pell see the shape of honest labor. And underneath the honest labor, quietly, he listened.

The hall had its own population. Booklice beyond counting, a colony of martins nesting in the eaves outside the high windows, and one ancient cat — a grey torn named Custodian, formally employed, who spent his days patrolling the shelves against mice and his nights sleeping in the letterpress drawer. Felix had never spoken to Custodian before. Cats were difficult; their minds were all edges and territory, and most of them considered conversation a form of begging. But as Felix passed the end of row twelve, the cat opened one yellow eye and said:

*You're the listener.*

Felix kept walking. *I'm an auditor,* he thought back, carefully neutral.

*You're the listener,* Custodian repeated, with feline contempt for human evasions. *The little one upstairs asks after you. The girl. She asked the sparrows who in this stone pile might be spoken to, and the sparrows said the tall clerk, the one who feeds the wall-things and pretends not to hear.* The cat stretched, claws pricking audibly into the shelf wood. *I told her you were probably useless. Most of your kind are.*

"What did she say to that?"

*She laughed.* Custodian's whiskers angled forward, which in cat was the closest thing to a smile the species permitted. *First time since she came. The whole building heard it. Even Pell looked up, and Pell hasn't looked up since the coronation.* The eye closed again. *Row forty, auditor. Third shelf. The grey bindings with the cracked spines. That's where the founding era sleeps. And if you're going to pull those, pull them slow, because Pell reads newspapers upside down.*

Felix's pulse ticked upward. He finished his legitimate work at row eleven, stretched, complained mildly about his back, and drifted with theatrical casualness to row forty.

The grey bindings were exactly where the cat had said. Founding-era records: the Ledgerhouse's first century, its charters, its early acquisitions. He pulled volume after volume, checking dates against the number Grandmother Teeth had given him — thirty years, more or less — and found nothing, because of course he found nothing; crimes that survived thirty years in a house full of auditors were not recorded in ordinary bindings.

But at the bottom of the third shelf, wedged behind a run of charter copies, his fingers found a book whose spine was blank.

He drew it out slowly. Grey leather, cracked, unmarked — no title, no year, no catalogue stamp. When he opened it, the paper inside was fine and old, ragged at the edges, and the handwriting was cramped and hurried, nothing like the beautiful copperplate of the official ledgers. It was a private record. A working book. Someone's rough accounts.

The earliest entries were dated ninety years back. He flipped forward, page after page, decades sliding under his thumb — trades, prices, notes in shorthand he half recognized from the guild's older cipher systems — and then, thirty-one years ago, the entries changed character. The handwriting grew larger, less controlled. And on a page dated the ninth of the Fading Month, thirty years and one season past, he found a single line, boxed in double rules as though the writer had wanted to fence it off from everything around it:

*The Stones are silent. The exchange is concluded. Let no entry of this be made in any open book. — A.V.*

Felix stared at the initials until they blurred.

A.V. Aldous Vane. Except Vane had been Master for only twenty-six years; before that he had been Second Keeper, deputy to old Master Corvane. Which meant the book was not necessarily Vane's — except the hand, Felix realized, tilting the page to the window light, comparing it against memory, against the marginal corrections Vane made on junior clerks' reports, the distinctive way the man crossed his sevens. It was Vane's hand. Vane had kept a private ledger of the covenant's breaking, dated a full five years before he rose to Mastership.

*Let no entry of this be made in any open book.*

And yet here it was, in the open archive, misfiled or hidden or simply lost — unless it wasn't lost. Unless someone had left it where a certain kind of searcher might find it. Felix looked up sharply, scanning the green-lit ranks of shelves, the dust, the dozing cat, Pell's newspaper. Nothing moved. Nothing ever moved, in this house, except for the things that were always moving whether you saw them or not.

He turned back to the private ledger and read the final pages properly, and the vertigo Grandmother Teeth had promised him arrived on schedule.

Vane had not stumbled into the breaking of the Covenant. He had engineered it, negotiated it, priced it. The last pages were full of terms — payments made to parties unnamed, quantities of something called *first-silver*, a schedule of nine nights' work matching exactly what the rats' grandmother had witnessed in the cellar. And on the final leaf, in fresh ink, in the same cramped hand but written recently, recently enough that the ink still had a faint shine:

*The heir is come. Watch the child. If she reaches the Stones, every debt comes due.*

Felix closed the book. His hands were steady, which surprised him; he had expected them to shake. Instead there was a strange calm in him, cold and clear, like the air after lightning.

For fifteen years he had served this house. It had fed him, sheltered him, left him alone with his secret when any other institution would have caged or bled or sold him. He had told himself the Ledgerhouse was merely hard, the way winter is hard — impersonal, necessary, without malice. He had balanced its books and swallowed its silences and called it loyalty.

But loyalty, he understood now, standing in the dust-green light with a stolen truth in his hands, was a payment made in trust of fair dealing. And the house had been trading in stolen goods for thirty years — stolen sound, stolen speech, a stolen conversation between worlds — and had just posted watch on a child.

"Pell," he said, and his voice sounded almost normal. "I've finished the founding valuations. I'll need to come back tomorrow."

Pell grunted over his newspaper. Felix returned the grey bindings to their shelf, all but one, and walked out of the Record Hall with the private ledger flat against his stomach, inside his coat, over his heart, feeling its weight the way you feel a coal you've picked up barehanded — pain and heat and the absolute certainty that you cannot put it down.

Behind him, unnoticed by any human eye, the martin fledglings in the eaves began to sing, and the sound carried down through the open window, across the yard, up three floors of stone, to a locked room where a girl went still beside her moths, listening, and smiled.
