## 6. Marguerite

The room they put her in was a box of counting.

Marguerite knew boxes. She had slept in the hollow of an oak that was a box of rings, and in a badger's abandoned sett that was a box of earth, and once — for three cold nights — in the ribcage of a whale that had walked out of the sea and died on her grandmother's shore, which was a box of bone and salt-smell. But this box was made of ledgers and lamplight and men's voices behind a door, voices that counted her like they counted everything: *one girl, feral, unpriced; one tongue, unknown, therefore worthless or priceless, pending.*

She sat with her back to the wall where she could see the door and the small barred window both, because a creature that could not see its exits was already half-caught.

Her name came to her the way names come to everyone who speaks the first tongue: not as a sound but as a shape. In the old language she was *Márguerite* — no, that was the near-word, the human-shaped word, the one her mother had given her so that somewhere, someday, a human mouth might say it without breaking a tooth. Her true name was longer. Her true name was *the-small-bird-that-would-not-leave-the-nest-until-the-storm-had-passed*, spoken all at once, the way wind speaks through a whole forest at the same time. But her mother had also taught her the shortening, the kindness of shortening, and so when the moths came tapping at the window glass that first night, she told them:

*I am Marguerite. Daughter of Ysolde-of-the-Riverbank. Last word of the first tongue, if my mother spoke true, and my mother did not lie about important things.*

The moths pressed their soft heads against the glass and said, *poor thing, poor thing, poor thing,* which is what moths always say, because they live only long enough to say it.

*Not poor,* she answered. *Caged. There is a difference. Poor is having nothing. Caged is having everything taken except your skin.*

The moths considered this with their dust-grey wings half-open, and then they said something that surprised her: *the mice under the floor say the man-who-hears is coming again tonight. The one who leaves food. The mice do not fear him. The mice never fail to fear anyone.*

Marguerite went very still.

*Man-who-hears?*

*Hears us,* the moths said, fluttering their agreement at each other. *Or hears something. The rats speak of him too, and rats speak of nothing but themselves unless it is true.*

She turned this over the way she turned over river stones, looking for what lived underneath.

---

Her mother had run for nine days.

Marguerite remembered it in pieces, the way you remember a fire: not the whole burning but the separate bright things. Ysolde's hand around hers, slick with sweat, pulling her through bracken that whipped their faces. The smell of ash behind them — real ash, not hearth-ash, the ash of a burned house, of burned *papers*, though Marguerite had been too young then to know why papers were worth killing for. Her mother's voice saying, over and over, in the first tongue and then, clumsily, in the human words she kept folded away like a dress saved for a wedding that never came: *run now, ask later. Run now, ask later.*

On the ninth day her mother stopped running, because there was blood running out of her side in a way that does not stop, and she knelt down in the mud of a riverbank and held Marguerite's face in both hands.

*Listen to me,* she said, and the first tongue made even dying sound like instruction, like weather. *You are the last. Do you understand? When the stones went quiet, everyone who could speak fell silent one by one, year by year, like lamps going out. I am the last lamp. You are the flame I carried out of the house before it burned.* She laughed then, a small terrible laugh, blood on her teeth. *Your grandmother would have hated that image. She said we are not lamps. She said we are roots. Lamps die when the oil is gone. Roots wait.*

*Mother—*

*No. Listen. If they find you, they will not kill you. That is the worst part. They will keep you, the way they keep everything — in a ledger, in a box, priced and shelved. And a voice in a box is a voice no one hears, and a covenant no one hears is already broken.* She pressed her forehead to Marguerite's. *Find the stones. Find them before the solstice, or find them after, but find them. And trust — * her breath caught, and she spent it carefully, like a woman spending her last coin * — trust the ones the beasts trust. Not the ones who feed the beasts. Anyone can feed a beast. Trust the ones the beasts come to on their own.*

Then the river took her, gently, the way rivers take everything eventually, and Marguerite walked north alone for a season and a winter and another season, sleeping in the shapes animals left for her, eating what the generous world offered, until she smelled smoke and bread and stone-city, and followed the smell to the guild's great door, and knocked, because her mother had also taught her manners.

*Knock before you enter anywhere,* Ysolde used to say. *Even a den. Even a grave. Especially a grave.*

---

They had tried to teach her their language, the clerks. A round woman with ink-stained fingers had come with a slate and written *BREAD* and pointed at bread, and Marguerite had looked at the woman the way she would look at any creature performing a trick, and said nothing, because the human words in her mouth felt like wearing someone else's shoes — technically possible, deeply wrong. The woman wrote *NAME?* and Marguerite had almost laughed. As if a name were a thing you could carry on a slate. As if a name were not a root-system, a history, a promise other creatures made about you when you weren't listening.

But she was hungry, and hunger makes diplomats of us all, so on the third day she pointed at the bread and said, in her careful borrowed shoes: "Bread."

The clerk woman had burst into tears, which confused Marguerite greatly, since she had simply named a thing, and naming things was the gentlest act there was.

That was the day she began to understand these people. They wept when a child spoke. They locked the child who spoke. They argued behind doors about what the speaking was *worth*. They lived inside their counting the way snails lived inside their shells, except a snail's shell grew from its own body, and this counting had been built by dead men and the living were only tenants in it, paying rent in silence.

At night she lay on the counting-room floor — they had brought her a blanket that smelled of camphor and other people — and listened to the building breathe. Every building breathes differently. This one breathed in numbers. She could feel it in the floorboards: the tick of abacuses upstairs, the scratch of pens, the slow arterial pulse of coin being moved from column to column. Underneath it all, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, the wild realm's dying hummed. She had been hearing it all her life, growing quieter each year, a song losing its singers one verse at a time. Here in the city it was nearly inaudible. Nearly. But it was there, and it was grieving, and none of these shell-snails seemed to notice that the ground itself was mourning.

Except perhaps one.

---

The man-who-hears came after dark on the fourth night, and the mice announced him first.

*He comes, he comes, he walks soft, he brings food, he does not look at our holes the way the others look, the others look like they are counting us, he looks like he is apologizing.*

Marguerite rose from her corner and stood in the shadow beside the shelf of ledgers, still as a heron, and watched the door open on a young man with tired eyes and a coat that had been mended more times than it had been bought. He set down a covered plate — real food, bread and cheese and an apple, not the grey gruel the clerks pushed through the door at midday — and he did not step fully into the room. He stood in the doorway like a man standing at the edge of deep water.

And the beasts of the building came to him.

That was the thing. That was the thing that made her breath go strange in her chest. A mouse emerged from the wainscot while he stood there — bold as brass, in front of a human — and sat up on its haunches and looked at him, whiskers twitching, and the man looked back, and between them passed something Marguerite recognized with a shock that went through her like cold river water. Recognition. Conversation. The mouse said something brief and dry, a joke by the shape of it, and the man's mouth twitched at the corner, the way mouths do when they are swallowing a laugh, and the mouse darted away satisfied.

*Oh,* Marguerite thought. *Oh, you're real. My mother said you might be real and I half believed her and now you're standing in a doorway smelling of ink and fear and you're real.*

She stepped out of the shadow.

He startled badly — humans always startled, they were startle-creatures, born halfway to flight — and his hand knocked against the doorframe, and for a moment they regarded each other across the lamplit room: the feral child and the frightened clerk.

"You brought food," she said in the first tongue, knowing he would not understand, watching his face anyway, because faces told truths even when tongues told lies. "You bring food every night. The mice say you apologize to the walls."

His brow furrowed. He didn't understand her — of course he didn't, he was human, humans had traded their birth-tongue for counting centuries ago — but he heard the *shape* of it, and she saw the shape land on him like a bird landing on a branch. He opened his mouth. Closed it. Then, slowly, he crouched down and set the plate on the floor and slid it toward her, and stepped back, giving her the distance a wild thing needs, and that — *that* — was the second proof. He knew to give distance. Either he had learned it from beasts, or he had it in him the way she had the tongue in her, native and involuntary.

A moth came in through the barred window then, spiraling down toward the lamp, and Marguerite, watching the man instead of the moth, saw him glance at it — and saw his eyes *follow* it, briefly, the way one follows a voice calling from another room.

*He hears,* she told the moth later, when the man had gone and she was eating the apple, which was sweet past all sense, a small green miracle in a box of counting. *The mice are right. The rats are right. He hears.*

*Is that good?* the moths asked, because moths have no politics.

Marguerite chewed and considered. Through the wall, the wild realm's grief hummed on, patient as roots. Somewhere in this building were the people who had made it grieve — she did not know how yet, but she knew it in her marrow the way she knew the difference between rain coming and rain leaving. And here was a man inside the walls of the makers, a man the beasts trusted, a man who apologized to mice.

*It is either very good,* she said finally, *or it is the most dangerous kind of trap. The kind baited with exactly what you want.*

The moths said, *poor thing, poor thing,* and settled on the warm glass, and outside the city went on breathing its numbered breath, and the solstice moved toward them through the dark like a slow animal that could not be called back.

---

In the morning, boots in the corridor. Many boots, and among them one pair that walked differently — unhurried, soft-soled, the walk of someone who had never once needed to hurry because the world hurried toward him instead. Marguerite pressed her ear to the door.

The round clerk's voice, nervous: "—she spoke yesterday, sir. A word. 'Bread.' We thought you'd want to know, given the interest from—"

"Given everything," said the soft-soled walker, and his voice was warm the way a snare is smooth. "Yes. I'm always interested in rare stock."

Stock.

Marguerite knew that word. It was one of the human words her mother had taught her, deliberately, bitterly, along with *ledger* and *price* and *property*, saying: *learn these words best of all, little root, because these are the words they will use about you, and a hunted thing must know its hunter's grammar.*

She drew back from the door and stood in the middle of the counting room, in the slant of grey light from the barred window, and she felt the fierce thing rise in her chest that her mother had named with pride and fear in the same breath — *you have your grandmother's temper, may the stones forgive me* — and she looked at the shelves of ledgers surrounding her, hundreds of them, thousands, each one a box within a box within a box, and she understood suddenly and completely that somewhere in one of those books, or soon to be in one of those books, was a page with her on it. Her weight. Her age. Her tongue. Her price.

*Come and write me down, then,* she said to the boots in the corridor, in the first tongue, low and clear, and the mice in the walls went quiet to listen, and even the moths on the glass stilled their wings. *Come and count me. And when the stones wake — when my mother's flame finds its wick — I will make you read every page of what you've done aloud, in a language older than your oldest ledger, and we will see then what your counting is worth.*

The soft-soled boots paused outside her door.

Paused.

And moved on.
