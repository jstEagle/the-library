## 7. The Ledgermaster's Smile

The summons came on a Tuesday, which Felix would remember afterward as the only mercy in it. Tuesdays were quiet days at the Ledgerhouse — the day the ink was changed, the day the clerks trimmed their quills and argued about decimal places, the day even the rats in the walls grew drowsy with boredom and spoke mostly of crumbs.

The note itself was a small thing, cream-colored, sealed not with wax but with a pressed leaf that had been gilded so finely it still showed its veins. It said only: *Mr. Crane. My study. After the second bell. — A.V.*

No reason given. No reason needed. In the Ledgerhouse, a summons from Aldous Vane required no more explanation than winter.

Felix read it three times in the stairwell of the counting wing, where the light came down through a skylight furred with dust and turned everything the color of old teeth. His hands were steady. He made a point of noticing that they were steady, the way a man walking a rooftop ridge makes a point of noticing each shingle under his boot. Steady hands. Steady breath. He was a man who balanced accounts of impossible things; he could balance this.

But his heart had already begun its small, traitorous drumming, and somewhere behind the plaster, a rat stirred in its nest and murmured sleepily, *the tall one is afraid, the tall one smells of fear and bread* — because he had been carrying bread up to the counting room for three nights now, and the whole wall knew it, and walls talked to walls, and who knew how far a whisper traveled in this house of whispers.

*Hush,* Felix thought at it, uselessly. Rats did not hush. Rats were the one creature in the Ledgerhouse that had never learned discretion.

He finished his morning's work first. That mattered, he told himself. A clerk summoned for wrongdoing might flee or bluster, but a clerk summoned for nothing finished his column of figures first, blotted them neatly, squared the ledger's corners, and walked to the third floor with the unhurried tread of an innocent man. He rehearsed innocence all the way up — the four flights, past the map room where the maps of the wild lands were kept under glass and slowly, year by year, redrawn smaller — rehearsed it like lines in a play he'd never auditioned for.

*You found the girl interesting. You fed her because she looked hungry. You have never been in the Undercroft Archive. The archive is a rumor clerks tell each other.*

All true except the last two sentences. He practiced saying the false ones in his head until they stopped tasting like copper.

Aldous Vane's study occupied the tower's crown, and it was famous among the clerks chiefly for being disappointing. They expected a villain's lair — black wood, iron, the skulls of extinct things. Instead there were plants. Ferns in glazed pots, a fig tree grown against the south window, moss gardens under little glass bells, each one labeled in Vane's small, precise hand. The room smelled of loam and green things and, beneath it, faintly, of ink. Sunlight fell through leaded panes onto a desk so tidy it looked less used than maintained, the way a shrine is maintained.

Vane himself stood at the window with his back to the door, watering a pot of something white-flowered with a brass can that had a long, thin spout. He was not what strangers expected either. Not tall, not imposing. A comfortable man, silvering at the temples, with soft hands and reading spectacles pushed up into his hair, dressed in dove-grey wool without a single ornament of rank. He looked like a schoolmaster. He looked like someone's kind uncle. He looked, Felix thought with a lurch, exactly like a man who had torn a page out of a book thirty years ago and slept soundly ever since.

"Mr. Crane." Vane did not turn. "Do you know what this is?"

He tilted the flowerpot slightly so Felix could see it. White flowers, five-petaled, nodding on stems too thin for their heads.

"I — couldn't say, sir."

"Neither can anyone else." Vane set down the watering can and turned, and his smile arrived a half-second before the rest of his expression, like lightning before thunder. "It came through a trade eleven years ago. Seeds, in a paper twist, exchanged for a jar of last words — actual last words, bottled, still warm. The seller wouldn't say where the seeds were from. Nothing in the archive matches them. Nothing blooms anywhere else quite like it. I keep it because it reminds me that the world is still larger than our ledgers." He said it pleasantly, almost fondly, and then: "Sit down, Mr. Crane."

There was one chair before the desk. One. Felix sat in it and understood immediately that the chair was part of the architecture of the room, the way the ferns were, the way the smile was. Everything here had been placed deliberately, including him.

Vane settled across from him, folded his soft hands, and asked about the weather.

That was the terrible thing about it — the gentleness. For ten minutes they discussed the rain, the price of tallow, a dispute between the wool-factors and the guild's assayers that Vane resolved aloud with such easy good humor that Felix caught himself nearly relaxing, nearly laughing, twice catching himself just in time. Vane poured tea from a brown pot. He remembered how Felix took it — barely warm, no sugar — and Felix felt the remembering like a hand closing around the back of his neck. *He knows how I take my tea. He has watched me long enough to know how I take my tea.*

"The child," Vane said, at last, in the tone of a man moving from one item on an agenda to the next. "Marguerite. An odd name to give a feral child, don't you find? Marguerite. A daisy. A pearl. Someone named her once, and recently, I think. Names are rarely lost, Mr. Crane. They're only put down."

"She hasn't spoken to me, sir."

"No?" Vane sipped his tea. "She speaks constantly. To the moths, principally. Also to the mice in the wainscoting, though the mice have sensibly gone elsewhere. Half my night clerks swear the counting room is haunted now." He smiled again, that half-second smile. "And yet the junior clerk assigned to her care reports that she goes quiet as a stone whenever *he* enters. Curious, isn't it? As if she's listening to something the rest of us can't hear. Or as if *he* is."

The tea tasted of nothing. Felix held the cup level through an act of will he would remember for the rest of his life.

"She's frightened," Felix said. "Children are quieter around people who feed them. They watch to see if the food keeps coming."

"Mm." Vane set down his cup. The porcelain clicked against the saucer, a tiny sound, precise as a signature. "Tell me about your grandmother."

The question fell into the room like a coin into a well, and Felix listened for it to hit bottom and heard nothing at all.

"My grandmother, sir?"

"Cranes are not a guild family. Your file says your mother was a laundress, your father unrecorded, and that you were raised by a grandmother in the Marsh End until her death — when was it, nine years ago? You were fourteen." Vane recited it without looking at anything, without notes, and every fact landed softly and precisely, like cards dealt face-up. "A washerwoman with a gift for birdsong, the neighbors called her. Kept pigeons. Talked to them, some said. The neighbors said a great deal about the Marsh End, most of it worthless, but I find the details charming. Tell me about her."

"There's nothing to tell, sir. She was old. She liked birds. She told stories."

"What sort of stories?"

"Bird stories." Felix made himself shrug, made the shrug loose and adolescent and stupid. "Where the wren got its temper. Why the magpie counts. Children's things. I've forgotten most of them."

"You've forgotten most of them," Vane repeated, gently, and let the sentence sit there between them, breathing.

Felix drank his tasteless tea. Somewhere below them, muffled by three floors of stone and paper, the Ledgerhouse went about its morning — the scratch of pens, the cough of the furnace, a cart unloading in the yard. And closer, much closer, in the ivy on the tower's outer wall, a wren was singing its head off, and Felix could not stop himself from hearing it, could not stop the translation arriving unbidden in his mind the way rain arrives unbidden through a leaking roof: *stranger-in-the-nest, stranger-in-the-nest, the grey one listens, the grey one listens, fly, fly, fly.*

*Fly,* agreed a pigeon on the roof-ridge. *Fly now.*

He did not fly. He sat in the single chair and finished his tea like a man innocent of wrens.

"Here is my difficulty, Mr. Crane." Vane rose, wandered to his fig tree, and touched one leaf with one fingertip, absently, the way another man might touch a weapon. "Three nights ago, someone entered the Undercroft Archive. The locks were not forced. The wards were not tripped — or rather, they were tripped, and then persuaded, which takes a key, or a kindness, or a very particular talent. A book was disturbed. *Foundations of Accord*, 1689 edition, the annotated copy. One page removed." He turned. The lamplight through the leaves striped his face green and shadow. "I am not angry, Mr. Crane. Anger is for men who lack information. But I will tell you what I believe, and then you may tell me whether I'm wrong, and we shall both be the wiser.

"I believe the woman who died at our gate brought us something priceless and knew it. I believe the child is not merchandise but provenance — a living deed to something my predecessors sold cheaply and I intend to own outright. I believe someone in this house has been feeding her, listening to her, and asking questions in dark rooms, and I believe that someone began as a boy who talked to pigeons in the Marsh End." He smiled — the full smile this time, warm as a hearth, and somehow the coldest thing in the cold tower room. "Am I wrong?"

Every instinct Felix owned screamed at him to fill the silence. Silence, in the Ledgerhouse, was a debt, and debts accrued interest. He made himself sit in it anyway. Three seconds. Five. Long enough that a guilty man would have cracked, and long enough that an innocent man would have been confused, and he tried to be both at once, confusion laid over guilt like clean linen over a wound.

"I don't know anything about any archive, sir."

"No?"

"No, sir. I balance accounts. Impossible trades, incoming. I've never been below the second cellar. I didn't know there *was* an archive." He heard his own voice do something remarkable: it trembled, just slightly, at exactly the right moment, and he realized with distant horror that the trembling wasn't performed at all. He was that frightened. The lie and the truth had worn the same clothes for so long they'd become indistinguishable even to him. "Someone's feeding her because she's a starving child in a locked room, sir. If that's a crime, half the kitchen staff should be in irons."

Vane studied him for a long moment. The wren outside sang. The fig leaves ticked against the glass in a draft Felix couldn't feel.

"Do you know," Vane said at last, softly, "what I value most in a clerk? Not accuracy — accuracy can be trained. Not discretion — discretion can be bought. Imagination. The capacity to look at an impossible thing and ask not *is it real* but *what is it worth*. Most men flinch from the impossible. Our best men lean toward it." He came around the desk, and Felix's whole body wanted to press back into the chair, and he did not let it. "You lean, Mr. Crane. I've watched you lean for years. The way you linger over the wild-trade entries. The way you read the marginalia in dead men's ledgers when you think no one sees. A man who leans will always be valuable to me — or dangerous. It depends entirely on which side of the ledger he stands on when the account closes."

"I want to stand on the right side, sir."

"I know," said Aldous Vane kindly. "That's what makes this so pleasant."

He returned to the desk, opened a drawer, and took out a slim black volume that Felix recognized with a lurch of the stomach: the duty roster. Vane ran one finger down a column and made a small mark with a pencil no bigger than a finger bone.

"You'll take over the child's care formally. Meals, washing, exercise in the yard — supervised, of course. She responds to you; it would be wasteful to pretend otherwise. Consider it a promotion." The smile again, brief as a knife-flash. "And consider it a trust, Mr. Crane. I promote men whose curiosity serves the house. I retire men whose curiosity serves themselves. The difference, I find, is usually visible within a fortnight."

"Yes, sir."

"One more thing." Vane opened the black volume wider, and Felix saw, upside down, a page of names and figures — and one name freshly written in wet ink, with a number beside it that had too many zeroes for comfort. "Sable Harrow arrives Thursday. You won't have met her; she works abroad, mostly. She'll want to walk the route the dead woman took to our gate. She'll want to speak to everyone who saw the child arrive." Vane closed the book. "Be helpful to her, Mr. Crane. Helpfulness is remembered."

* * *

Felix walked down the four flights with his legs feeling borrowed, past the map room, past the skylight full of dusty light, and out into the yard where the coal smoke hung low and the stable horses shifted in their stalls and said, in their rolling, mournful way, *you smell of the tower, you smell of the tower, what did the grey man give you, what did he take?*

"He gave me the child," Felix said under his breath, to the horses, to no one. "He put her in my hands so that whatever happens to her happens to me."

The oldest mare blew out her lips, unimpressed. *Then don't let anything happen to her,* she said, as if it were simple, as if horses had ever understood the first thing about ledgers.

He stood in the yard a while longer, letting the cold air scour the taste of tea and lies out of his mouth. He had done it. He had lied to Aldous Vane, to his face, over tea, in the tower of the guild that owned his bed and his bread and every lawful thing he had — and Vane had smiled and written his name in a black book beside a number, and Felix could not stop seeing that number, could not stop turning it over, zeroes stacked like coins, like heads.

*A price.* The rats had taught him the word for it weeks ago, gossiping in the walls. *A price on small heads.*

Thursday was two days away. Sable Harrow was coming, and the child in the counting room was singing to her moths, high and sweet and in a tongue older than every kingdom, and Felix Crane climbed the stairs toward her with his heart hammering out the only honest account he had left: *whatever this costs, it has already begun.*
