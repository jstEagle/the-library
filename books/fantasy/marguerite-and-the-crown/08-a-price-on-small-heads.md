## 8. A Price on Small Heads

The Ledgermaster's office smelled of cedar oil and cold iron, and Aldous Vane liked it that way. Cedar for the ledgers, iron for the truth beneath them: that everything, in the end, could be weighed.

He stood at the tall window overlooking the counting floor, watching his clerks move below like patient insects through the morning's trades. A jar of bottled thunder from the Stormreach coast. Three feathers plucked from a bird that had not yet been born. The usual Tuesday traffic of the impossible, each item entered, appraised, and shelved in its proper dark. Order. He had built thirty years of it, grain by grain, and he did not intend to let a feral child with mud in her hair undo it.

"She speaks nothing," said Underclerk Pellin, hovering at his shoulder with a slate. "Not trade-tongue, not high speech, not the river dialects. Master Corvane tried Old Liturgical. She looked at him as if he were furniture."

"And yet she is not silent."

"No, Ledgermaster. The... other thing." Pellin lowered his voice as though the walls might be embarrassed. "The moths. The sparrows in the eaves. She talks to them, or they to her, and Crispin swears the sparrows answered in words. He's asked for a transfer to the salt desk. Says the counting room has gone strange."

Vane turned from the window. In the grey light his face was all planes and patience, a face like a ledger itself — ruled lines, no wasted ink. "Strange," he repeated softly. "Yes. I imagine it would seem so, to a man who has never listened."

He crossed to his desk and drew from a locked drawer a slim volume bound in green leather gone black with age. He did not open it. He simply rested his hand upon it, the way another man might rest a hand on the shoulder of an old enemy.

"Thirty years ago," Vane said, more to the book than to Pellin, "this house made a decision. A necessary one. The wild was dying anyway — slow, untidy, unprofitable. We merely... accelerated the accounting." His thumb traced the cover's cracked spine. "What we did not account for was an heir."

"You believe the child is—"

"I believe," Vane said, "that she is an asset of incalculable value, currently stored in our counting room at no charge, and that every day she remains there is a day some rival house or desperate crown learns she exists." He closed the drawer. The lock spoke with a small, final click. "I also believe that assets which cannot be catalogued must be contained, and assets which cannot be contained..."

He left the sentence unfinished, which was, those who knew him understood, the closest Aldous Vane ever came to cruelty. He preferred his cruelties itemized.

"Draft the notice," he said. "Quiet terms. No broadsheets, no market criers. Word goes out through the usual channels — the pawnbrokers, the night-soil men, the ferrymen. Anyone who moves goods after dark and asks no questions." He paused at the window again, watching a pigeon cross the courtyard below, head bobbing, utterly without language. "A child, alive and undamaged. Deliver her to the undercroft door. Payment negotiable, but generous. And a separate line, Pellin — smaller type."

"Yes, Ledgermaster?"

"'Finder's fee for information regarding any employee who assists her.' We should know which of our own people have soft hands."

Pellin's quill scratched. Somewhere below, faintly, through three floors of stone and cedar, a sparrow sang. Vane did not hear it. He had made very sure, long ago, that he never would.

* * *

Sable Harrow read the contract twice, standing in the doorway of Vane's office while rain needled the glass behind him.

It was written in the guild's plain hand — no flourishes, no legal fog. That alone told her how much he wanted this. Vane dressed his sins in clauses; when he wrote plainly, it meant the sin was too urgent for clothing.

*One child. Alive. Undamaged. Delivery to the undercroft door.*

Below it, in smaller type, the second line about employees. Below that, the figure. Sable read the figure twice as well, not because she miscounted but because she wanted to see whether the number would feel different the second time. It didn't. Numbers never did. They just sat there, fat and indifferent, like well-fed dogs.

"You've read the file," Vane said. It wasn't a question. He was watching her the way he watched everything — as if assessing resale value.

"The woman who brought her," Sable said. "Dead?"

"Drowned, we think. Or walked into the river. The watch pulled a body from the pilings two nights past. No identification." He folded his hands. "You have questions about the child herself."

"One question." Sable kept her voice flat as slate. "Why me? You've got forty blades on retainer and half of them can find their own arses in the dark. Why send for the one enforcer who hasn't worked a live capture in six years?"

Vane smiled. It was a good smile, well-maintained, like everything else about him. "Because you don't hear things, Harrow. The others flinch at shadows and swear the rats are talking. You're deaf to all that superstition. Clean ears. I need someone whose head isn't full of whispering."

Something behind Sable's ribs twisted, old and scarred-over, like a knot in wet wood.

*Clean ears.* Yes. That was one way to describe it. Another way involved a cellar room, a lead apron, and two guild surgeons who had hummed a work-song while they cut, because the humming helped them keep time, and keeping time helped them keep their stomachs. She had been nineteen. She had heard the mice in the infirmary walls complaining about the cold, right up until the moment she heard nothing at all, forever, except the ringing that lived where the listening used to be.

She had signed the consent form herself. That was the part she never told anyone. There had been a form, and a pen, and a sum of money that had seemed, at nineteen, like a fortune. The guild did not steal gifts. It bought them, fair and square, and filed the receipt.

"I'll take the contract," she said.

"Naturally you will." Vane slid the paper across the desk along with a purse that clinked with the particular heavy sound of advance payment. "Begin with the city gates and the river stairs. If she runs, she'll run by water. Children always think water is freedom."

"And if someone's already helping her? An employee, say."

Vane's eyes came up, mild and bottomless. "Then the second line applies. Bring me a name, Harrow, and the fee doubles." He returned to his papers, dismissing her the way he dismissed everything — by becoming slightly more interested in something else. At the door she paused.

"For what it's worth," she said, "the last man who hunted a child for this house ended up in one of your ledgers himself."

"I know," Vane said pleasantly. "I wrote him there."

* * *

In the counting room, Marguerite had learned the architecture of being watched.

There were eyes at the grate in the door — clerks' eyes, coming and going on some schedule she could not parse but could feel, the way she felt weather in her teeth. There were eyes at the high window, slit-thin, barred. And there were eyes that were not eyes at all: the moths that lived in the rafters among the old paper, grey-winged and dusty, who told her things in the fluttering half-speech of their kind.

*Moths see poorly,* she had complained once, early, to their flickering congress above her. *Moths are drunk on light and lie about doors.*

*We do not lie,* the eldest moth had answered, offended, in the way of moths. *We only remember badly. There is a difference, small loud thing.*

But today the moths remembered well enough, because today the men beyond the door had stopped pretending she was a stray to be fed and begun speaking of her in the tones men used for cargo.

She heard them through the grate, two clerks, their voices low but not low enough. One was young, nervous; the other older, with the bored cruelty of a man discussing freight.

"—posted already, I heard it. Quiet channels only. Night-soil men, ferrymen, the pawn-brokers on Ropewalk."

"For a child? What's the number?"

The older clerk named it. The young one made a sound like he'd been struck.

"For *one girl?* She's filthy. She bites. Corvane says she ate a wax seal."

"They're not paying for the girl, you idiot. They're paying for what's in the girl. Like buying a goose for the eggs." A pause, the shuffle of papers. "Alive and undamaged, the notice says. Which means someone wants the goose laying."

Marguerite sat very still on the counting-room floor, her knees drawn up, her back against the great oak table leg. Around her ankles, dust motes drifted in the bar of light from the high window, and the moths came down to sit among them, folding and unfolding their grey wings.

*Goose,* said the eldest moth, tasting the word. *Is goose bad?*

*Goose is a thing kept,* Marguerite said. *A thing kept for what comes out of it.*

*And you are the goose.*

*I am the goose.*

The word settled over her, and she waited for it to hurt the way her mother's death had hurt, the way hunger hurt — a hot, tearing thing. But it did not. It went through her cold instead, straight down, like a stone dropped into deep water, and what rose up in its place was not grief but clarity. Terrible, level, useful clarity.

They had put a price on her. Not on her hands, which could find food anywhere. Not on her courage, which had carried her across a city of stone with her mother's ashes in a pouch at her throat. On the *thing inside her* — the tongue, the listening, whatever sleeping inheritance her mother had died running from and running toward. They wished to buy the egg and keep the goose.

Very well. Then she would learn the shape of the cage while they priced it.

She got up, quietly, and began — for the first time since they'd locked her in — to truly study the room. The grate in the door: iron, riveted, too narrow. The high window: barred, but the bars were set in mortar gone soft with damp; she pressed her thumb into it and the grit crumbled like stale bread. The table: bolted to the floor, but its drawers held chalk, a tally-stick, a knife for cutting quills — small, but a knife. The coal chute in the corner, hidden behind a screen, its hatch rusted but hinged, breathing cold air from somewhere below and beyond.

*Small loud thing,* the eldest moth said from her shoulder, *why do you touch the walls?*

*Because,* Marguerite said, *a goose who knows the shape of her pen is harder to keep than the farmers think.*

Down in the walls, in the dark seams of the old building, the rats had heard the clerks too. News travels fast in a guildhouse, faster in the walls of one, and by evening the whole grey parliament knew: a price had been posted, and a hunter had taken the paper, a woman with a face like a shut door and a walk like falling snow.

That evening, when Felix Crane came down the cellar stairs with his supper crust and his careful, hidden heart, the oldest rat was waiting for him on the third barrel, tail curled, eyes bright as wet garnets.

*Listener,* the rat said, without preamble. *They have weighed her. There is a number now where a child used to be. And the snow-walker has taken the contract — the one who smells of blood and nothing else. You have perhaps three days. Perhaps two.*

Felix stood in the dark with the crust going soft in his hand, and felt the last plank of his quiet life give way beneath him.

* * *

Felix climbed the stairs to the counting room that night carrying bread and a stolen apple, and found Marguerite waiting for him at the grate — not crouched in her corner as usual, but standing, upright and still, with the look of someone who has finished grieving and started planning.

She pushed the folded scrap of chalk-white cloth through the grate before he could speak. On it, in charcoal, in a hand surprisingly precise: a drawing of the coal chute, the courtyard, the river stairs — copied, he realized with a chill, from the moths' bad memory and the rats' good one, stitched together into a map of escape.

Below it, three marks. A number of days. And then a single figure she had clearly pried from the clerks' talk: the price. Drawn as a circle of coins around a small stick-figure of a girl.

Her eyes met his through the grate. She pointed at the figure. Then at herself. Then, slowly, deliberately, at *him* — and tilted her head, asking the question she had no words for in his tongue and he none in hers:

*Which side of the ledger are you on?*

Felix looked at the little chalk girl ringed in coins, and at the bread in his hand, and at the three days the rats had given him, and understood that the question was not whether he would help her.

The question was what it would cost — and whether, when the snow-walker came, he would be brave enough to pay it.

"I'm getting you out," he said, though he knew she couldn't understand the words. "Tomorrow night. The coal chute. Be ready."

She couldn't understand the words. But she watched his face while he said them, the way she watched everything — and whatever she saw there, she nodded once, sharp as a blade going into a sheath, and took the bread.
