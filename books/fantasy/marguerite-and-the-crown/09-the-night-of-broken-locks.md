## 9. The Night of Broken Locks

The rain began at dusk and did not stop.

Felix stood at the window of his narrow room above the counting floor and watched it come down in grey ropes, filling the gutters, drowning the lanterns along Copperlane until the whole city seemed to be dissolving into the dark from which it had been built. Somewhere below, a clerk was still working late — he could hear the scratch of her pen through two floors of timber, a small industrious sound like an insect in a wall. The Ledgerhouse never entirely slept. There was always someone awake to record what could not be recorded anywhere else.

He had made his decision three times that day, and unmade it four times.

That was the arithmetic of cowardice, and Felix Crane was very good at arithmetic. He had spent his whole life balancing accounts that no one else could see: one secret weighed against another, the small warmth of Marguerite's trust against the cold weight of the noose Vane would surely fashion for him if he were caught. He knew how the sums came out. He had known since the moment he'd heard Sable Harrow's boots on the marble stairs that morning — a sound like a door closing — and understood that the bounty had been posted, quiet as everything the guild did, quiet as a knife slid between ribs.

*The girl is merchandise now,* the rats had told him, and rats did not lie about such things. Rats only lied about cheese.

He took down his coat from its hook. It was the good coat, the one with the deep pockets he'd sewn himself, and inside it already waited the things a man needed when he intended to stop being careful: a crowbar wrapped in sacking, a stub of tallow candle, a twist of dried meat he'd been hoarding like a dragon's hoard for reasons he hadn't let himself examine. And folded flat against his breastbone, warm from his body, the ledger with the torn page — the Old Covenant's terms, the guild's signature, the wound in the world written out in a dead clerk's careful hand.

He touched it once, the way a man touches a talisman or a confession, and went down into the dark.

* * *

The counting room where they kept Marguerite was on the second floor, behind a lock older than the building around it. Felix had walked past that door forty times in six days, each time arranging his face into the bland mask of a junior accountant with nowhere to be and nothing to hear. Forty times she had been behind it. He knew because the mice in the wainscoting told him — a chattering, gossipy chorus, *the wild girl, the wild girl, she sings to the moths and the moths come to her like she's a lamp* — and because sometimes, passing, he caught the faintest thread of that language through the wood, syllables like water over stones, old as riverbeds.

Tonight the corridor was empty. The rain helped; rain made clerks drowsy and sent the night watch huddling into the doorway alcoves with their pipes cupped against the wet. Felix moved along the wall the way he'd learned to move as a boy, when listening was a crime and being caught listening was worse than theft.

The lock was the problem. It was always the problem. He knelt before it with the crowbar and found his hands were shaking, and he stopped, and breathed, and made himself look at the thing honestly, because lying to himself was the last luxury he could no longer afford.

*If you do this,* he told himself, *you cannot undo it. There is no ledger entry for going back.*

His grandmother's voice rose in him then, unbidden, from some deep shelf of memory: *A bird in the hand is worth nothing, Felix, if you keep it caged so long it forgets the sky.* He had thought those were just bird stories. He was beginning to understand that nothing his grandmother had ever said was just anything.

The crowbar went into the jamb with a crack that sounded, in the silence, like the end of the world. He froze. Counted ten heartbeats. The rain covered him — the rain, indifferent and generous, hammering the roof, sheeting the windows, drowning every small treacherous sound in its grey roar. He worked the bar, felt the old wood splinter, felt the lock give with a grudging iron sigh, and pushed the door open onto darkness and the smell of dust and, beneath the dust, something green. Something living. She carried the smell of growing things with her the way other people carried perfume.

"Marguerite," he whispered, into the dark. Then, remembering, he said it the way she'd taught him, the first word of the old tongue he owned — her name, but shaped differently, rounded, given breath the way a name should be. *Mar-gwer-ith.* *She who is counted by no one.*

Two eyes opened in the blackness, catching the thin light from the corridor. Not afraid. That was the thing that undid him, every time. She was never afraid of him. Wary, yes, the way a fox is wary, reading him constantly, weighing him — but not afraid. As if fear were a currency she had decided not to carry.

"You came," she said, in her own tongue, and then, in the handful of human words she'd gathered like bright pebbles: "You. Came."

"I said I would." His voice cracked on it. "Can you climb?"

She tilted her head. Behind her, in the corner of the counting room, a moth beat itself softly against the shuttered window, and Felix heard her speak to it without words, a pulse of attention, and the moth went still, waiting, as if it too were part of tonight's conspiracy.

"There is a chute," Felix said. "Coal chute. It runs from this floor down to the cellar yard. It hasn't been used since they converted the furnaces. It's — it's filthy, and it's steep, and I will go first and catch you at the bottom." He heard himself babbling and stopped. "It's the only way. The stairs are watched."

Marguerite looked at him for a long moment. Rain filled the silence. Then she reached out and took a fold of his sleeve between two fingers, examining the cloth, the stitching, as though assessing whether this man was woven strongly enough to bear what she was about to hang on him. Whatever she found there, she nodded once.

They went to the panel behind the third shelf of ledgers — he'd pried it loose two nights ago, sweating in the dark while the rats kept watch and reported the footsteps of the watchmen in a running commentary of squeaks. Cold air breathed up out of the chute, smelling of coal and stone and years. Below, somewhere, was freedom or ruin. There was no longer any meaningful difference between them.

"I'll go first," Felix said, and swung himself in.

* * *

The chute was worse than he'd promised. It dropped away at an angle that scraped his back raw through his coat, and the coal dust of thirty years rose around him in choking clouds, and twice he slid further than he meant to and caught himself on rusted iron brackets that bit his palms. Above him he could hear Marguerite descending with none of his clumsiness — she moved through the dark chute like water finding its course, fluid and silent, a creature for whom enclosed spaces held no terror because no space had ever held her.

He came out the bottom in an explosion of grit, tumbling into the cellar yard behind the stables, and rolled aside just in time for her to land beside him on her feet, light as ash settling. For a moment they crouched together in the black shadow of the stable wall, two smudged figures in the rain, and Felix laughed — a single helpless bark of a laugh, immediately strangled — because they were not free yet, not nearly, and laughter was a madness.

Then the dogs started.

Not real dogs. The guild kept no dogs; dogs were unpredictable, and the Ledgerhouse abhorred the unpredictable. But the alarm bell in the main house had begun to toll, and someone had raised a cry, and boots were hammering on floors above them, and Felix understood with a lurch that the broken lock had been found, or the empty room, or the pried panel. The night had turned against them all at once, the way nights did.

"The yard gate's barred," he breathed. "We can't—"

But Marguerite wasn't listening. Her head had turned toward the stables, toward the long low building where the guild kept its dray horses and the sleek black carriage team that Vane himself rode on state occasions. And Felix, following her gaze, heard what she heard.

The horses were waking.

He had heard horses all his life — their slow cud-chewing contentment, their nightmares, their opinions of particular grooms. But this was different. This was Marguerite reaching into their sleeping minds with that old, patient voice of hers, and the horses answering, and what they answered with was not fear.

It was war.

* * *

The first scream tore the night open.

It came from the big bay gelding, Caesar, who pulled the left side of Vane's carriage and who had hated his harness for eleven years. The scream was pure equine fury, high and shattering, and it was joined a heartbeat later by the mare beside him, and then by the dray horses, all six of them, and the noise they made was unlike anything Felix had ever heard leave a horse — it was the sound of a burning building, of a battlefield, of the sky coming apart. Lights bloomed in the windows of the main house. Men stumbled out into the rain with lanterns, shouting, and every one of them ran toward the stables, because a horse screaming like that meant fire, meant injury, meant money dying, and the Ledgerhouse loved its money more than it loved anything else in the world.

"They're not hurt," Felix whispered, awestruck, appalled, exultant. "They're — oh, they're magnificent. They're lying. They're all lying together."

*Run,* said the horses, in the vast rolling voice of their kind, a voice that shook the ground under Felix's knees. *Run, little speaker, run, and we will hold the door.*

Marguerite grabbed his wrist. Her grip was astonishingly strong, fingers like a hawk's, and she hauled him up and across the yard, through the rain, keeping to the strip of shadow between the stable wall and the far fence. Behind them the stable doors burst open and grooms poured in with blankets and buckets, and Caesar chose that moment to actually rear, a huge black silhouette against the lantern-light, and the whole yard dissolved into beautiful, weaponized chaos.

The yard gate was barred, as Felix had said. Barred with a chain and padlock, because the guild trusted locks the way other men trusted gods.

Marguerite looked at the chain. Then she looked at Felix, and there was something almost like humor in her face, sharp and brief as a kingfisher's flash. She put her mouth close to the padlock and spoke to it — actually spoke, three soft syllables of the first tongue, the same ones she used on moths and mice and frightened children — and Felix would have sworn afterward, and did swear ever after, that the lock hesitated. That some ancient agreement between metal and the wild stirred in its sleep. At the very least, the hasp was old, and the chain was old, and when they both threw their weight against the gate the staple tore out of rotting wood with a screech swallowed whole by the storm.

And then they were through, and the alley beyond was black water and running shadows, and the Ledgerhouse stood behind them with its windows lit and its horses screaming lies into the rain.

Felix did not look back. Looking back was for men who still believed in returning.

* * *

They went down through Copperlane like ghosts, if ghosts were soaked to the skin and leaving wet footprints. Felix led, because he knew the city's veins — the cut-throughs between warehouses, the stair that dropped behind the tannery, the places where the watch lamps had been broken by boys and never mended. Marguerite followed half a step behind and slightly to his left, and he realized, with a strange floating feeling, that she was guarding his flank. That she had appointed herself his protector without discussion, the way she did everything, by simple decree of her own wild nature.

Once, near the tannery, a constable loomed out of a doorway ahead of them, collar up, truncheon in hand, squinting into the rain. Felix's heart stopped. But Marguerite made a small sound — a click, a chirp, exactly the call of a nightjar — and the man glanced up instinctively at the eaves, hunting the bird, and they slipped past him so closely that Felix could have touched his cape, and the constable never saw two children of the covenant walk by an arm's length from his elbow.

"How," Felix gasped, when they dared breathe again, "did you know that call? Nightjars don't even live in the city."

Marguerite gave him a look that said, plainly, *birds talk, even city birds, especially city birds, they know everything*, and he laughed again, breathlessly, and this time didn't strangle it.

They came down at last to the docklands, where the rain met the river and the river met the sea and everything smelled of tar and salt and rope. Here the city loosened its grip. Here were warehouses with doors that asked no questions, and taverns whose lights burned yellow and forgiving, and the great black breathing presence of the water itself. Gulls wheeled overhead even in the rain, ragged and complaining, and Felix heard them clearly for the first time in days — really heard them, their scavenger gossip, their ancient grievances — and one of them banked low over his head and screamed directly into his ear:

*The cat! The gray cat! The one-eyed one says the wild road opens at the drowned stair, but the price is fish, it is always fish, why is it always fish—*

Felix stopped so suddenly that Marguerite bumped into him.

"What is it?" she asked, in her flowing tongue, and he realized he was smiling like a fool, rain running off his chin.

"I know where we're going," he said. "There's a — a guide. Of sorts. A cat." He heard how it sounded and pressed on anyway. "An old cat who knows the wild roads. The gulls say she'll take us to the Green Road, if we pay her in fish."

Marguerite considered this with the gravity of a queen reviewing a treaty. Then she nodded, once, decisively, and pointed east along the waterfront, toward the drowned stair where the old tide-gate had crumbled into the river, and Felix understood that of course she already knew the cat. Everyone wild knew everyone wild. The city's beasts were a parliament, and he had spent his whole life among them without knowing he held a seat.

They were halfway along the quay when he heard it.

Not with his ears. With the other hearing, the inheritance, the gift — a sound like a single wrong note struck inside his chest. Horses. Far behind them, back at the Ledgerhouse, the screaming had stopped, and in its place was a silence with a shape, and the shape was Sable Harrow.

He couldn't have explained how he knew. There were no words in it. Only a coldness moving through the city's beast-minds like a blade through water — the sparrows going quiet on their ledges, the rats pulling their children deep into the walls, the gulls rising in a single ragged cloud off the customs house roof. Something was walking through the docklands that the animals refused to name, and it was walking their way, unhurried, certain, the way winter walks.

Marguerite had gone rigid beside him. She heard it too, whatever it was, in whatever way she heard. Her hand found his sleeve again.

"She's coming," Felix said quietly. The rain drummed on the boards around them. Ahead lay the drowned stair, the gray cat, the fish-price, the Green Road, the Standing Stones, the solstice, all the impossible distance between here and salvation. Behind lay everything he had ever been. "The enforcer. Harrow. We have to move."

Somewhere down the waterfront, a lantern flared behind fogged glass, and a one-eyed cat stepped out of the shadows onto a bollard, tail curled, regarding them both with her single amber eye as though she had been expecting them for years and was mildly annoyed at their lateness.

"Well," said Felix Crane, to the cat, to the girl, to the ruined and unrecognizable man he was becoming. "I suppose we'd better hurry."

Behind them, far off through the rain, a whistle sounded — short, sharp, and answered by another, closer. The hunt had begun.
