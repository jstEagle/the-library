## 10. Out of the City's Teeth

The city did not sleep so much as it ground its teeth.

Felix felt it in his soles as he ran — the cobbles of the Ledger Quarter giving way to the older, looser stones of the river districts, each step a small percussion that said *you are leaving, you are leaving, you are leaving*. Marguerite ran ahead of him, barefoot on the wet stones as if they were warm grass, her ragged dress dark with coal dust from the chute. She had not looked back once. He had looked back eleven times.

Behind them, the Ledgerhouse burned its lamps against the night, and somewhere in that honeycomb of light a missing page and a missing child were being tallied into the same terrible sum.

*Left,* said the rats along the wharf walls as they passed, a chorus of whiskered gossip rippling from drain to drain faster than any runner could carry it. *Gone out. The quiet one, gone out with the wild girl. Tell the harbor. Tell the harbor.*

Word traveled. Word always traveled in this city; it was the only currency the poor were rich in. Felix put his head down and ran.

They came down off the embankment where the fish-market stairs met the tide flats, and the smell hit him like a hand across the face — brine and tar and rotting herring, the great animal breath of the harbor at low water. The docklands at night were a country unto themselves: masts black against a bruised sky, rigging creaking its endless complaint, lanterns strung along the quay like a string of dim orange beads someone had dropped and never bothered to gather up again.

Marguerite stopped at the bottom of the stairs. Her head went back. Her whole body went still in a way that made Felix's skin prickle — the stillness of a creature listening to something beneath hearing.

"What is it?" he whispered.

She didn't answer in words. She never answered in words when there was another way. But above them, out of the dark over the water, came a sound like torn silk — and then the gulls came down.

There were hundreds of them. They poured off the warehouse roofs and the mastheads and the mooring posts, wheeling around the two of them in a slow white storm, and their voices — their voices were not the flat greedy screaming that gulls gave to fishermen. They were speaking. To her. About him.

*Who is the man-thing,* said one voice, old and gravelly, from a bird with a scarred yellow bill. *He smells of paper. Paper and iron boxes.*

*He hears,* said another, younger, brighter. *The rats say he hears. The rats are liars but they are not wrong about hearing.*

"He does hear," Felix said aloud, before he could think better of it, and every gull in the wheeling cloud went silent at once.

Silence from a thousand birds is a loud thing. It pressed on his ears. Marguerite turned and looked at him then for the first time since the coal chute — really looked, with those grey-green eyes that seemed lit from some fire behind them — and something passed between them that had no language either of them owned yet.

*Speak, then, paper-man,* said the scarred-billed gull, settling onto a bollard an arm's length away with the insolent confidence of a customs official. *The night is short and our patience shorter. What do you want of the sky?*

Felix's mouth was dry. All his life he had listened to beasts in secret, eavesdropping on a world that didn't know he existed, and now the world had turned its thousand eyes upon him and demanded terms. It was like being asked to speak after thirty years of muteness.

"We need to leave the city," he said. "By ways the guild doesn't watch. We need — " he glanced at Marguerite, who was watching the gulls with an expression of fierce concentration, drinking in their speech like rain on cracked earth — "we need the wild roads."

A ripple went through the flock. Not alarm exactly. Something older than alarm.

*The wild roads,* repeated the scarred-bill slowly. *The hollowed roads, you mean. The roads that go quiet.* It cocked its head, one gold eye fixed on him. *Nothing walks those ways anymore, paper-man. Nothing that can help it. The green is going out of them like warmth out of a corpse.*

"I know," Felix said. And then, because lying to beasts was apparently no longer among his options, and because some reckless new thing was waking in his chest: "I know what hollowed them. Or I'm beginning to. And I mean to un-hollow them, if I can."

Another silence. Longer this time. Somewhere out on the black water a ship's bell rang the half-hour, indifferent and beautiful.

Then the scarred-bill laughed — a harsh, barking, astonishing sound, and the whole flock took it up, raucous laughter rolling across the quay until stevedores in distant doorways pulled their caps down and muttered about omens.

*A ledger-clerk who wants to mend the sky's debts!* the old gull crowed. *Well. The world has been strange lately. Strange enough that I'll believe almost anything.* It spread its wings, huge and battered and magnificent. *Listen, then. The gates are watched — your guild has bought the watchmen's eyes tonight, we've seen the coin change hands. But the water is not watched, because your guild thinks the water belongs to it too, and the water disagrees.*

It nodded toward a tangle of piers eastward, where derelict hulks leaned against each other like drunkards.

*The old ferry channel. Under the rotting pier with three legs. There's a mudflat path the oyster-catchers use, and past it a culvert, and past the culvert the marshes begin, and past the marshes — * the gull's voice dropped, and all its mockery drained out of it — *past the marshes, the Green Road. If it still exists. If anything still walks it.*

"Will you guide us?" Felix asked.

*We will fly above you and scream if men come,* said the gull. *That much even a coward can do. As for walking with you into the hollow places — * it shook its feathers, and the sound was like dry leaves — *no, paper-man. Even courage has a floor, and mine is the edge of the dying grass. But there is one in the docklands who knows the wild roads better than any bird, because she walks them on four feet and owes nothing to the sky.*

"Who?"

*Find the cat called Grimalkin-of-the-Nine-Lies. One eye. Grey as weather. She sleeps where the tar-barrels keep warm, behind the ropewalk.* The gull lifted off, and the whole flock rose with it, a blizzard of wings. Its last words drifted down through the wheeling white: *And paper-man — tell her the sky remembers what she did at the fish-quay. She pretends otherwise, but she likes to be remembered.*

Then the gulls were gone up into the dark, and the quay was only a quay again, smelling of fish and tar, and Marguerite was tugging at his sleeve with a hand small and strong as a knot.

"Gull-mother says go," she said. Her human words were still strange in her mouth, each one handled carefully, like an egg that might be rotten. "Go now. Men-smell coming."

She was right. On the wind off the river, faint but unmistakable, came the smell of lamp-oil and boot-leather and the particular starched cleanliness of guild enforcers. Felix took her hand without thinking about it, and she let him, and they ran east along the shadow-line of the warehouses while above them, unseen, the gulls wheeled and kept their word.

* * *

Marguerite did not like the city. That was not the truth of it — the truth was larger and stranger than liking or not liking — but it was the closest human-word shape she could press the truth into, and she was learning that human-words were always too small, always leaking at the seams.

The city was a dead thing that thought it was alive. That was nearer. It was built of stone taken from the earth without asking, stacked by hands that sang no songs to it, and under the streets the old water-runs had been buried in pipes and forgotten, and the trees in the little walled gardens grew crooked and sad because no one had ever told them they were trees. Everything here was deaf. Everything here had been made deaf on purpose, cut off from the great conversation the way you might cut the tongue from a singing bird, and the people walked through it all not knowing they lived inside a wound.

But the boy heard. That was the strange thing. The paper-boy with the tired eyes heard the rats and the gulls and the horses, heard them the way her mother had described the covenant-speakers of the old stories, the ones who stood between and translated. Mother had said they were all gone. Mother had been running too hard and too long to know they were not.

Marguerite followed him through the maze of masts and rope and rotting wood, and she listened to the city's few remaining voices. Bats stitched the air overhead, telling each other about midges. In the hold of a grounded barge, mice argued about a crust of cheese with the passionate seriousness of philosophers. And everywhere, threaded through everything, thin as smoke: the wild calling. Faint. Getting fainter. A song sung by singers who were forgetting the words.

Her chest hurt when she listened to it. She listened anyway.

*This way,* she signed to Felix — she had taught him three signs in the coal-dark of the chute, *this way*, *hide*, *quiet*, and he learned them the way he seemed to learn everything, quickly and quietly and as though he were ashamed of how fast he learned. He was strange for a man-thing. He flinched from loud noises. He apologized to doors when he bumped them. When the gull-flock had surrounded them he had not raised a hand or a stick, only stood in the middle of all that screaming white with his mouth open like a man seeing the sun for the first time.

She had judged him by the beasts, as her mother had taught her to judge all two-legs. The rats spoke of him without fear. The horses in the guild stable had screamed their covering-screams for him and lain about it afterward with total calm, which meant they had given it freely. The gulls had bargained with him as an equal. These were good signs, deep-water signs. But her mother had also taught her: *the two-legs who hear us are the most dangerous kind, little word, because they can choose. Choose every day. Choose forever.* And so Marguerite watched him choose, minute by minute, run by run, and waited to see what he would choose when choosing cost him something real.

Behind them, back toward the fish-market, a whistle blew. Then another. Then a bell began to ring in a pattern she had learned to hate in her six days inside the guild-house: not the harbor bells that marked the hours, but the sharp triple-strike of the enforcers' call.

*Hide,* she signed.

They folded themselves into the black gap between a warehouse wall and a stack of salt barrels, and Marguerite pressed her spine to the cold wood and pulled Felix down beside her, and they waited while boots rang on the quay-stones — six pairs, maybe eight, moving with the unhurried confidence of hunters who knew the prey had nowhere to go.

Felix was shaking. She could feel it through his arm. But his breathing stayed slow and even, deliberately slow, deliberately even, and she understood that he was doing it on purpose, holding his fear on a leash the way she held hers, and something in her chest that had been clenched since her mother pushed her out a door before dawn loosened by one small degree.

The boots passed. The bell fell silent. The city exhaled.

*Now,* she signed, and they went on.

* * *

The ropewalk was a cathedral built for a humble god — a long shed as long as a church nave, where hemp was spun into rope in lengths that made Felix's eyes water to imagine. Behind it, between the warm brick of the tar-barrel store and a mountain of discarded net, they found her.

She was exactly as the gull had described: grey as weather, one-eyed, sitting upright in the lee of the barrels with her tail wrapped around her feet and her single amber eye already open and fixed on them, because of course she had heard them coming long before they arrived. Cats heard everything. Cats simply declined, as a matter of principle, to acknowledge most of it.

"You're the cat," Felix said, feeling immediately foolish.

"And you're the paper-man who's set the whole harbor talking." Her voice was dry and smoky, a voice like a struck match. "Sit down before you fall down, clerk. You run like a man who's spent his life sitting."

Felix sat. Marguerite did not sit; she crouched, cat-fashion herself, and stared at the one-eyed cat with open fascination, and the cat stared back, and for a moment the two of them regarded each other across some private frontier of understanding that excluded him entirely.

"The gulls said," Felix began, "that you know the wild roads—"

"The gulls talk too much." The cat licked one paw with elaborate indifference. "The gulls also said they remembered the fish-quay. Did they. Sentimental feathered idiots. I saved one herring-boat from one bad night and they've made a legend of it." She lowered the paw. Her eye, when it fixed on him again, was suddenly and uncomfortably sharp. "So. You're the one who broke the quiet girl out of the counting house. You're the one the rats are singing about. *The listener who chose.* That's what they're calling you down the drains, did you know? They sing it like a ballad. Rats have no sense of dignity whatsoever."

"I need to get her out of the city," Felix said. "To the Standing Stones. Before solstice."

The cat was quiet for a moment. Out in the dark, the harbor groaned and clinked, and the tar-barrels breathed their warm pitch smell, and somewhere very far away the triple-strike bell rang once more, from a different quarter of the city now. They were spreading the net.

"The Stones," the cat said at last. "Of course the Stones. Because breaking a girl out of the Ledgerhouse wasn't ambition enough for one night." She rose, stretched until every vertebra was audible, and looked at Marguerite for a long moment — and whatever passed between them then, Felix saw the cat's ears flatten and her tail-tip flick, and when she spoke again her voice had changed, lost its drawl, gone quiet and serious as a closed book.

"All right," she said. "I'll take you to the Green Road. Not because of you, paper-man. Because of her." She turned and flowed off into the dark between the nets, then paused, looking back with her one burning eye. "But understand the price of my company, both of you. The wild roads aren't hidden from the guild because they're far. They're hidden because everything that travels them pays. The road takes tolls. The road is *hungry* since the hollowing — hungry and getting hungrier, and it doesn't care whether you're innocent. Are we understood?"

Felix thought of the torn page in his coat, pressing against his ribs like a second heartbeat. He thought of the rats' whispers: *when the stones went quiet.* He thought of the fact that he had, twenty minutes ago, committed treason against the most powerful guild in the known world, and that there was no road back from that, only roads forward, each one darker than the last.

"Understood," he said.

"Good." The cat flicked her tail. "Then follow me, and walk soft, and don't step on anything that hums."

They went out through the bones of the docklands — under the three-legged pier where the mud squelched and the oyster-catchers piped their thin warnings overhead, through the culvert whose brick throat dripped and echoed, up onto the sea-wall where the wind came at them sideways carrying the smell of open marsh. Behind them the city glowed against the sky like a banked fire, all its ten thousand lamps burning, all its ledgers balanced, all its secrets sealed.

Felix looked back only once more. High above the harbor, the gulls were still wheeling, still screaming — and he realized with a jolt that they weren't screaming randomly. They were screaming *directions*, false ones, drawing the searchers' lanterns toward the western piers while three small figures climbed east into the dark. Keeping their bargain. Screaming if men came, and screaming louder to lead them astray.

His throat tightened. They had known him for five minutes and they were spending their safety on him.

"That's what the old covenant was," Marguerite said softly beside him, following his gaze. Her English failed her and she reached for the old tongue instead, and the words came out of her like water over stones, and — for the first time — Felix found he understood them without trying, the way you understand a lullaby. "*Not crowns. Not laws. Just this: that the wing helps the foot, and the foot remembers the wing, and neither forgets.*"

Ahead of them the marsh opened wide and silver under a rising moon, and at its far edge, where the reeds grew tall and strange and bent in a wind that touched nothing else, something green and faintly luminous marked the beginning of a road that had not existed an hour ago.

The one-eyed cat stopped at the border of the grass and would go no further.

"There's your road," she said. "And there's your first toll, coming to collect."

Felix followed her eye to the reeds — where, standing hip-deep in the silver water, perfectly still, a figure was waiting. Tall. Wrong. Wearing the marsh like a cloak.

It raised its head, and though it had no eyes, Felix felt himself seen all the way through.

"It knows you're coming," the cat said. "It's been smelling your guilt since the culvert. Whatever you did back in that city, paper-man — the road already knows. And the road charges interest."
