## 12. The Green Road

### Felix

The road began where the city forgot to look.

Behind them, Greyharbour's teeth still showed — the smokestacks, the customs houses, the long grey jaw of the seawall — but here at the edge of the docklands, past the tanneries and the tar-yards, an old stone gatepost leaned out of a hedge like a drunkard against a wall. It bore no sign. It bore only moss, and beneath the moss, when Felix brushed it away with his thumb, a single carved word in letters that seemed to shift when he wasn't looking directly at them.

"That's the first word," Marguerite said.

He looked at her. She was crouched by the post, barefoot despite the cold, her hair full of burrs and gull-feathers from their night among the docks. In the city she had been a wild thing in a cage, all elbows and silence and eyes that watched everything. Out here, three days from the walls, something in her had begun to unfurl — slowly, the way a fern unrolls, not trusting the season yet.

"Which word?" Felix asked. "It's just... marks."

"It's not marks." She touched the carving with one finger, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "It's *open*. Or maybe *begin*. The old tongue doesn't have separate words for those. You say it and the road knows you're asking."

"The road knows."

"Everything knows." She said it without impatience, which was somehow worse than if she'd mocked him. "You just stopped listening. Your people stopped listening so long ago you built whole cities out of not-listening."

Felix had no answer to that. He had spent his whole life listening — to rats in walls, to horses screaming warnings, to moths and mice and once, memorably, a dying salmon — and he had never once thought of it as listening. He had thought of it as a defect. A leak in himself that the Ledgerhouse had found useful enough to keep and careful enough to hide.

He looked down the track beyond the gatepost. It didn't look like much. A cart-rut gone to grass, hedgerows leaning in, birches crowding up pale and thin. But there was something about the way the light fell on it — greener than the light anywhere else, as if the sun made an exception here — and something about the quiet. Not empty quiet. Attended quiet. The feeling of walking into a room where many people have just stopped talking about you.

"Is it safe?" he asked, and immediately hated the question. It was such a clerk's question. Such a Ledgerhouse question. *What is the risk? What is the exposure? What is the downside?*

Marguerite looked at him for a long moment, and then she did something she had never done before.

She laughed.

It was a small laugh, rusty, startled out of her like a bird off a fence — but it was a laugh, aimed at him, and Felix felt it land somewhere under his ribs and stay there.

"No," she said. "Probably not. Come on."

She stepped through the gatepost, and the green light took her, and after a breath that lasted too long, Felix followed.

* * *

The Green Road did not behave like a road.

That was the first thing Felix understood, an hour in. Roads went places. This one *was* places. They would walk through ordinary scrubland — gorse, bracken, a stony stream — and then round a bend and be somewhere else entirely: a hillside covered in bluebells out of season, a stand of oaks so old their trunks had faces in the bark, a meadow where the grass whispered in a wind that touched nothing else. The road moved them the way a river moves leaves, patiently, with its own intentions.

And everywhere, everywhere, the beasts were watching.

Not menacingly. That was the strangest part. Rabbits sat up in the bracken and tracked them with dark mild eyes. A badger crossed the path ahead of them at a stroll, unhurried, like a landlord inspecting new tenants. High overhead, a buzzard circled but did not cry. And through it all ran a low murmur that only Felix could hear — dozens of small voices, layered like wind through wheat:

*The girl. The girl is on the road. Look, the girl.*

*She smells of the old blood. The first blood.*

*Who is the tall one?*

*He hears. Look at his ears — well, not his ears. But he hears.*

"They're saying things about you," Marguerite said. She hadn't turned her head; she spoke to the path ahead, hands in the pockets of the stolen coat they'd dressed her in at the docks, sleeves rolled four times. "The wrens. They think you're strange."

"The wrens can talk?"

"Everyone can talk. Wrens mostly complain." A pause, and then, slyly: "They say you walk like you're sorry to take up space."

Felix opened his mouth to defend his walking, closed it again. Three days ago this child had been cargo in a counting room. Now she was critiquing his posture via songbird intelligence network. He didn't know whether to be proud or unnerved. He settled, as he often did, on both.

"I spent ten years being careful," he said finally. "Being small. It's hard to walk any other way."

Marguerite stopped. She turned and looked up at him with that flat, unblinking appraisal that made him feel like a ledger entry being audited.

"You were a slave," she said. Not a question.

"Not — technically. I was paid. I had a room. I could leave, in theory."

"You couldn't."

"...No. I couldn't."

She nodded slowly, as if confirming something against a list only she could see. Then she turned back to the path and kept walking, and Felix understood that the conversation was over, and that he had, in some way he couldn't name, passed something.

* * *

They made camp that evening in a hollow ringed by hawthorn, and Marguerite taught him his first word of the old tongue.

Not deliberately, at first. She was talking to the fire.

That was the other thing Felix was learning about her: she talked to everything. To the fire as it caught, to the river when they drank, to the moths that came to the flame. Not baby-talk, not madness — conversation. And the things answered, in their fashion. The fire burned steadier when she spoke to it. The moths orbited her shoulders like tiny devoted moons.

"*Aeth,*" she said to the flames, low and warm, and the fire leaned toward her voice like a cat being scratched.

"What does that mean?" Felix asked.

She glanced back at him over her shoulder. "It means *be gentle*. Or *stay*. Fire words are hard. Fire wants to eat everything; you have to ask it nicely to want less." She studied him across the flames, and he saw her make a decision. "Do you want to learn?"

"Yes," Felix said. There was no hesitation in it, and that surprised him. Ten years of hiding what he was, and here he was volunteering for more of it. "Yes. I want to learn."

So she taught him.

She started with the easy ones — or what she called easy, though each word felt like handling something alive. "*Sennu*," she said, "*sennu*, that means *listen-with*, not just listen. Listening with your whole skin. Say it."

"Sennu."

"Wrong."

"How? I said it exactly—"

"You said it with your mouth. The old tongue isn't in the mouth." She pressed her palm flat against the earth beside her. "It's here. And here." She touched her sternum. "The first tongue was made when everything was still one thing, before the world split into speaking and growing. So every word has roots in both. You have to mean it with the part of you that grows."

"That's not how language works."

"That's how *your* language works. Yours is made of fences. Little boxes with little meanings. Ours is made of—" she searched, frowning, and gestured broadly at the darkening wood, the fire, the stars coming out between the branches, "—of weather."

Felix tried *sennu* again. He tried to mean it with the part of him that grew, whatever that was, and to his astonishment the murmur of the beasts around their camp came clearer — not louder, but *nearer*, as if he'd leaned across a table.

*The tall one learns,* said a hedgehog somewhere, wonderingly. *Slowly. Like a stump learning to be a tree. But he learns.*

"He says I learn like a stump," Felix reported.

Marguerite's grin flashed white in the firelight. "Hedgehogs are generous. That was a compliment."

They worked until the moon rose — words for water and stone, for going and staying, for the particular kind of silence that means *I am here with you and asking nothing*. That one, *dova*, she made him say five times, because he kept getting it wrong, kept putting a request inside it, some small plea hidden in the vowels.

"You keep saying *dova-and*," she said. "*Dova* alone. Nothing added. Can you do that? Just be near someone without wanting anything?"

Felix thought about the Ledgerhouse. About ten years of every relationship priced and balanced, every kindness banked against future need, every silence a form of payment. He thought about how he'd slipped food to her in the counting room and told himself it was strategy — keep the asset calm, protect the investment — when even then, even then, he'd known it was none of those things.

"Dova," he said. Just that. Nothing added.

Marguerite held his eyes for a moment. Then she nodded, once, and turned away, and Felix understood that this too was a passing — that in her country, turning away was a kind of trust. You only turn your back on what you no longer fear.

* * *

He woke once in the deep of night to find her sitting up, knees drawn to her chest, staring north.

Felix listened. That was the one thing he was good at. He lay still and let his hearing open the way she'd taught him, *sennu*, listening-with, and he heard the night sounds of the Green Road — owl, fox, the slow breathing of trees — and beneath them, far off, something else.

Nothing.

A patch of nothing, northward, like a hole cut in fabric. Where there should have been voices there was simply absence, vast and clean-edged, and the nearer voices bent around it the way a stream bends around a stone.

"Marguerite," he whispered. "What's north?"

Her face in the starlight was very old for a moment, older than a child's face should ever be.

"The hollow place," she said. "Where the promise broke. Where my mother ran from." She pulled the coat tighter around herself. "We're going toward it, Felix. You know that. Everything we're doing is toward it."

"I know."

"Do you?" She looked at him then, and her eyes were fierce and frightened in equal measure. "The stones went quiet before I was born. My mother said the silence spreads. One day it will reach the Green Road. One day it will reach your city, and your rats will stop talking, and your horses, and then—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Then nobody hears anybody. Ever again."

Felix lay awake a long time after she slept, listening to the hole in the world, and understanding for the first time that this flight was not a flight at all. They were not running from the guild.

They were running *toward* the one thing worse than the guild.

* * *

### Marguerite

The tall one dreamed badly. Marguerite could tell by his breathing, the way it snagged and restarted, snagged and restarted, like a bird hitting a window over and over. She lay in the hawthorn hollow and listened to him not-sleep and thought about names.

Her mother had told her that in the first tongue, you didn't *have* a name. You *were* one. *Marguerite* was a human word, a fence-word, a box — it meant a flower, daisy, silly small thing. Her mother had used it because human words were safe, because the old name could not be spoken where the guild might hear it. The old name was longer. The old name had weather in it.

She had not said it aloud since her mother died. She was saving it. For what, she didn't know. Perhaps for the stones. Perhaps for the moment before the end of everything, when the last word of the first tongue should be the first word again.

But the tall one — Felix, the wrens called him, a hard little clicking name, a pebble of a name — the tall one was trying. Stump-learning, yes, but learning. Last night he had said *dova* correctly, finally, with nothing added, and she had felt it land in her chest like a warm stone. Human words were fences, but sometimes a fence is a shelter. Sometimes a fence keeps the wolf out instead of keeping you in.

Maybe that was why the Covenant had been made with humans at all. Her mother had wondered about it, walking the wild roads, teaching Marguerite the words for root and rain and grief. *Why them? Why the fence-makers?* And her mother had said: *because a promise is a fence too, little one. A promise is a fence you build around yourself on purpose, and stay inside. Only fence-makers understand that.*

The guild had broken the fence. The guild had torn the page out of its own book. She had heard the clerks say it, that day in the counting room, thinking she understood nothing — *the old arrangement, the liability, the unfortunate terms* — and she had understood every word, because merchants' greed sounds the same in every language.

But Felix had come to her in the dark with bread and apples, and the mice had vouched for him, and the moths had liked him, and the horses had screamed for him when he asked. The beasts trusted him the way you trust a wall that has stood a hundred years. Not beautiful. Not clever. Just *there*, when everything else falls.

"Felix," she said quietly, testing it. The pebble-name. It clicked nicely against the teeth.

He stirred. "Mm. What is it? Is something wrong?"

"Wrens are back," she said, because it was true — the dawn chorus was starting, faint and far. "They say the road forks today. Green way and grey way. They say the grey way is faster."

"And the green way?"

The wrens chattered; she listened; her stomach tightened. "Slower. And it goes near the hollow place. Close enough to see."

Felix sat up. His hair was full of hawthorn petals and his eyes were red-rimmed from bad dreams, and he looked, she thought, exactly like what he was: a soft creature from a soft city, walking willingly toward the worst silence in the world for a girl he'd known nine days.

"We'll take the green way," he said.

"You don't even know what's near the hollow place."

"I know what's behind us." He nodded south, toward the distant smudge of Greyharbour, toward the bounty and the enforcers and the life he had burned behind him in a coal chute. "I'll take slow and terrible over fast and caught. Besides." He almost smiled. "If we go near the hollow place, maybe I'll finally hear something you can't."

Marguerite stared at him. Then she laughed — really laughed, loud enough that the wrens scattered — because it was such a human thing to say, such a fence-maker's joke, turning terror into a wager.

"All right, stump," she said, standing, shaking dew from the coat. "Green way. But I'm teaching you the word for *run* first. *Vessa.* Say it."

"Vessa."

"Wrong. Again."

And they set off together down the road that was also places, arguing about pronunciation while the wrens followed above them complaining, and neither of them saw — because it did not wish to be seen — the grey shape at the edge of the green light, standing perfectly still among the hawthorns, watching them go.

Watching, and remembering what it was like to hear.
