## 13. The Hollowing

The forest began to die three days before Felix could see it.

I knew because the road went quiet. Not quiet the way night is quiet, when the moths still sing their small grey songs and the beetles drum beneath the bark. Quiet the way a mouth is quiet after a scream. The birds had stopped naming themselves to one another. The wind moved through the high branches and came out the other side without carrying anything, as if it had walked through an empty house.

*Grey is coming,* said the last of the wrens, from a thornbush that had already forgotten how to be green. *Grey is coming up from under. Grey is coming where the promise used to be.*

Felix could not hear the wren. He walked beside me with his head tipped, listening the way he always listened — his whole body a cupped ear, his breath held like a boy afraid of spilling something — and I watched him slowly understand that there was nothing to spill. His face did a thing I had not seen it do before. It went careful. Careful the way the clerks in the Ledgerhouse were careful, smoothing their faces flat so no one could read what was written underneath.

"What's wrong?" he asked me, in his round human words. "You've stopped walking."

I did not have the human words yet for what was wrong. I had only been learning them for a handful of days, and my mouth was clumsy with them, full of corners. So I answered him the way I answered everything then: I took his sleeve, which was rough and smelled of coal smoke and fear-sweat, and I pulled.

We left the Green Road where it bent around a hill like a hand closing. There was a deer-path there, faint as a scar, and the path knew me — paths always know the heir of the Covenant, even now, even broken — and it let us through. Felix stumbled on the roots. He was always stumbling. Humans walk like they are apologizing to the ground.

"Marguerite," he kept saying, low, the way you speak in a house where someone is sleeping. "Marguerite, wait. What is this place?"

This place. I will tell you what this place was, though telling it costs me every time, the way pulling a thorn costs, the way the wound is cleaner after but the pulling hurts.

* * *

My mother carried me through a forest once, when I was small enough to ride inside her coat, against her heart, listening to it beat its slow two-word sentence: *alive, alive, alive.* That forest had been loud. Loud with good things. The trees spoke to each other across whole valleys in voices slower than seasons, and the moss sang back in voices faster than rain, and between those two songs — the long green song and the quick soft one — everything else lived, everything else had room. Foxes. Owls. Things with too many names to count and some with none. My mother called it the Deep Choir, and she said every voice in it was a note in one enormous agreement, and the agreement had a name older than kingdoms, and she was one of the ones who kept it.

She said: *as long as both sides sing, both sides live.*

She said it the way you say a thing you believe down to your marrow, the way you say *the sun will rise.*

Then we ran. Then the men in iron came with their ledgers and their cold ink, and my mother's coat smelled of smoke, and the forest behind us went quiet in the bad way, the screaming way, and she did not explain to me why. She thought I was too small to understand. She was wrong about many things at the end, my mother, and that was one of them.

Now I stood at the edge of a forest that was neither loud nor screaming. It was simply going out. Like a fire left alone. Like a lamp whose oil has run to nothing while everyone who tended it forgot they were tending anything at all.

"Come," I said to Felix. It was one of my first human words and I said it badly, more breath than voice. "Come and see."

* * *

The first thing was the color.

Or rather, the leaving of it. We stepped beneath the eaves of the trees and the world turned the color of old paper, of ash, of the inside of a shell. Not dead-grey — I have seen death, and death has colors of its own, rich and terrible — but *un*-grey, the grey of things being forgotten. The moss on the north sides of the oaks had gone the color of dust on a windowsill. A fern hung beside the path, perfectly shaped, perfectly whole, and pale as bone. Felix reached out and touched it and snatched his fingers back as if it had bitten him.

"It's not dead," he whispered. "It should be dead. This fern's been cut off from water, from light — it should be brown, rotten, *something*. It's just... paused."

Paused. Yes. That was the word the wren would have used, if the wren had still had words to spend. Everything here was paused, mid-breath, mid-turning-toward-the-sun, and the pause had lasted so long that the pausing itself was beginning to fray.

And the silence. Oh, the silence. Felix stopped walking again, three steps in, and pressed his palm flat against the trunk of an oak as wide as a wagon, and I watched him listen with everything he had. I watched him strain the way a man strains against ropes. His eyes squeezed shut. His jaw worked. A sound came out of him, small and involuntary, like the sound of someone reaching for a stair that isn't there.

"Nothing," he said. "There's *nothing*. Marguerite, there's never nothing. Even in the Ledgerhouse cellar, even in the deepest cellars, even in the city with all its iron — there was always *something*, rats at least, pigeons, fleas, something muttering in the walls. But here—" He opened his eyes and they were wide and wet and frightened, and I understood that I was seeing a kind of blindness happen to him in reverse. All his life he had lived in a world twice as full as everyone else's, and now half of it had been taken, and he stood swaying in the poverty of it like a man missing a limb he'd only just learned he had.

"I hear nothing," he said again, quieter, as if saying it twice might change it. "Nothing at all."

*I do,* I almost told him. *I hear the nothing itself. I hear it eating.*

But I did not say it, because some knowledge is a stone too heavy for the hands of someone still learning to carry. Instead I took his hand — his knuckles were cold — and led him deeper.

* * *

Deeper, the trees grew stranger. They grew at all angles, leaning away from something in the center the way iron filings lean from a wrong-poled magnet. Their rings, where a fallen trunk lay across the path, were visible in the sawn heartwood — no, not sawn; the tree had fallen on its own, given up — and the rings went strange near the middle. Wide and proud for sixty years, seventy, eighty. Then narrower. Then narrow as thread. Then a ring so thin it was less a ring than a memory of a ring, a pencil-line, a held breath. And then nothing. The center of the tree was soft grey pulp, like bread left out for a decade.

Felix crouched over it for a long time. When he looked up at me his face had gone the same color as the ferns.

"How long?" he asked. "How long ago did this start?"

I counted on my fingers, the way my mother taught me, moon by moon. Then I counted years, and ran out of fingers, and made the gesture that means *many*, spreading both hands wide, twice.

"Thirty years?" His voice cracked on it. "That's — Marguerite, that's when the Ledgerhouse changed. That's what the rats were saying, that's what the torn page — the stones went quiet thirty years ago." He stood up too fast and had to put a hand against a trunk, and the trunk gave slightly under his weight, spongy, untrustworthy, a thing that had forgotten how to be wood. "The covenant broke thirty years ago and the wild has been dying ever since, and nobody — *nobody noticed?*"

*Nobody listens,* I could have said. But his human words were still mostly locked boxes to me then, and mine to him, and so I showed him instead.

I knelt at the base of the leaning oak and pressed both palms into the soil, and I did the thing my mother did, the thing I had done perhaps five times in my life because it cost warmth every time, drawing heat up out of your own blood and giving it away. I sang into the ground. Not with my throat. With the oldest part of me, the part that was in the covenant before I was born into it, the part that is a word in a very long sentence.

For a moment — one heartbeat, two — the ground answered.

It came up through my palms and into my arms and I heard Felix make a sound like a man struck. Because the answer was not a voice. It was an echo of a voice. It was the shape of where a voice had been, the way a footprint is the shape of a foot, the way an empty chair holds the shape of the one who sat in it. A great slow syllable, green and deep, trying to rise from miles down, from the roots of roots, from whatever bedrock the Deep Choir once stood upon —

— and failing. Thinning as it rose. Fading before it reached the air, the way a shout fades over distance, except there was no distance, the failure was right here, in the topmost inch of soil, in the space between the buried word and the world.

Gone. The ground went dumb under my hands. Cold, too, colder than the day justified, cold climbing up my wrists like water.

Felix was on his knees beside me without remembering how he got there. I saw his lips moving, shaping questions, and no questions came out. Above us, at the very edge of hearing — at the very edge of *his* hearing, which meant far past the edge of any ordinary man's — one bird, one single stubborn thrush, tried to sing.

It got four notes out. Four thin notes, like coins dropped down a well. Then it stopped, and shook itself, and flew away fast and low, the way creatures fly when they are ashamed.

"That's it," Felix said hoarsely. "That's all that's left. One bird. One echo. Marguerite — if this is what's happening everywhere, if the whole wild realm is going like this—" He was doing the human thing, the terrible human thing, the arithmetic thing, turning living grief into numbers. "How much is left? How long does it have?"

I spread my hands. *Not many.* And then, because I am the heir of the Covenant and lying is not in me the way it is in humans, I made the smaller gesture, the honest one, folding my hands nearly shut. *Not long. Less than the not-many.*

He stared at my folded hands and understood, because by then he was learning to read me the way I was learning to read him, and what crossed his face then was not fear for himself. It was awe — I saw it plainly, awe at the size of the dying, at the patience of it, at how a whole realm could go out like a lamp and the world of men would go on arguing about tariffs and tolls above the silence — and then the awe curdled, exactly as I had known it would, into urgency. Into the tight bright terrible look of a man who has just learned there is a deadline on the world.

"The solstice," he said. "The Stones. We have — how many days?"

I held up fingers. Fewer than two hands' worth.

"Then we can't rest tonight. We can't—" He stopped himself. Looked around at the grey, the leaning trees, the soft-hearted fallen oak. Something in him settled, the way sediment settles in shaken water. "No. We rest. You need sleep and so do I, and walking into the dark exhausted gets us killed before the guild even finds us. But at first light—" He rubbed his face with both hands. When he lowered them he looked older. "At first light we run."

* * *

We made camp in a hollow where a stream should have been. The streambed was there, the smooth stones, the curled dead leaves caught in the bends where water had once pushed them — but the water itself had gone down into the earth or simply ceased, and the dry channel lay like an empty sleeve. Felix built a small fire, feeding it twigs that snapped with a sound too loud for the silence around us, and the fire was the only orange thing in a league of grey, and we both sat close to it as if it were a friend.

He could not stop listening. I watched him try, again and again, tilting his head at every shadow, straining toward every rustle of wind, and finding nothing behind any of it, and each nothing landed on him like a small blow. Once he flinched hard and grabbed my arm — a moth had passed close to his ear, a grey moth, silent even to me, and he had heard *nothing* where his whole life he had heard the small velvet whirr of wings, and the absence had startled him worse than a shout.

"I didn't know," he said finally, staring into the fire. "I mean — I knew. The rats told me the wild was hollowing. I've known since the first night. But knowing a thing in words and standing inside it are different countries." He looked at me across the flames. "Is all of it like this? Everything beyond the Green Road?"

I nodded. Some of it worse. Some of it — I made the gesture for *gone*, both hands opening and emptying, and he closed his eyes.

"And the beasts that are left," he said slowly, "the ones still singing outside this place — the wrens, the foxes, the horses in the city — they're singing on the edge of it. Living next door to it. Hearing it the way you'd hear a neighbor's cough through a wall." He shivered. "That's why they helped us. That's why the gulls bargained and the cat knew the roads. They're not helping two travelers. They're betting on a rescue."

Yes. I touched my chest, where the covenant-word lived, the small warm coal of it that had not gone out even in thirty years of exile. *They bet on me,* I wanted to tell him. *They bet on the last word of the first tongue. Do not let them lose their wager.*

Instead I slept, because the body is a tyrant, and I dreamed of my mother's coat and the two-word sentence of her heart, and somewhere in the dream the sentence lost a word, and I woke before it lost the second.

The fire had burned to a red eye. Felix sat awake beside it, hugging his knees, and he was looking not at the coals but out into the grey dark beyond our hollow, and his expression was one I filed away carefully, the way my mother filed things away, because it mattered: he was not afraid for himself. He was listening to a silence that terrified him, and he was staying anyway. Choosing the terror. Choosing to be the kind of man who walks toward the dying place instead of away from it.

I decided, in that moment, watching him across the embers, that whatever the Stones demanded of me — and I felt, though I could not yet name it, that they would demand something large — I was glad this stumbling, coal-smelling, root-tripping human would be standing beside me when I paid it.

* * *

First light came greyer than the dark had been, if such a thing is possible, a dawn like dishwater, and we broke camp and ran.

We ran the way the deer-path wanted us to run, following its faint scar east and south, toward the Standing Stones, and the forest around us thickened and thinned and thickened, and the grey did not lift. If anything it deepened as we went, as though we were walking not toward the center of the sickness but along its length, the way a wound runs along a limb. Twice we crossed clearings where the grass stood pale as salt. Once we passed a pool of standing water, mirror-still, and in it I saw our reflections and could not tell for a moment which of us was the ghost.

Felix ran better than I expected. He was long-limbed and city-soft but he had a ferocious economy about him, wasting nothing, breathing in rhythm, and when he stumbled — he stumbled — he converted the stumble into speed, rolling up and onward like water finding its level. He did not ask me to slow down. I think he understood that I was running toward something as much as away from anything.

Near midday the trees opened onto a ridge, and from the ridge we saw it: far off, a smudge of true green on the horizon, a band of living color lying across the grey world like a ribbon laid on a shroud. The Green Road's heart. The last strong vein of the wild, running toward the Standing Stones the way blood runs toward a heart.

Between us and it, maybe half a mile down the slope, something moved.

Felix saw it at the same moment I did and dropped flat, hauling me down with him, his hand warm and shaking on my shoulder. We lay in the bracken among the grey ferns and watched.

A fox. A red fox, red as a coal, red as the only other colored thing in the world besides our fire last night, trotting along the edge of the treeline below us with its nose down, tracking something. Tracking *us*, I realized, as the wind shifted and brought its scent up the slope — and not only its scent. On its pelt, tangled in the fur of its ruff, clung smells that did not belong to any fox: lamp-oil, ink, cold iron, the particular sourness of men who have been riding hard for days.

Guild-smells. Hunter-smells.

The fox stopped. Lifted its head. And looked directly up the ridge at us — directly at *me*, its amber eyes finding mine across the distance with the unnerving precision of a creature that has been sent, that carries a message the way a letter carries ink — and it did not run. It sat. It wrapped its tail over its feet. And it waited, patient as a clerk, for us to decide whether to come down.

Felix's breath had gone shallow beside me. "A fox," he breathed. "Out here, in the hollowing, wearing *man-smells* — Marguerite, either that fox is running from them, or it's running *for* them, and I don't know which, and I can't hear it, I can't hear *anything*—"

But I could hear it. Barely. Thin as the thrush's four notes, frayed as the last ring of the fallen oak, one small voice rising out of all that silence, and what it said was this:

*I know your grandmother's name, speaker's-son. I owe her a debt. Come down and let me pay it — before the ones who smell of iron find you first.*

I gripped Felix's arm. Below us, the red fox waited, and far behind it, faint on the wind, came the creak of harness and the jingle of bit-rings, and the baying of dogs that had been taught to hunt children.

"We have to decide," Felix whispered. "Right now. Friend or trap?"

The fox flicked one ear, and waited, and the forest held its breath around us all.
