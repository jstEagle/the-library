## 14. The Fox with a Debt

The Green Road narrowed as it climbed, and by the third morning Felix had learned to read it the way he'd once learned to read ledgers — not by its words, which it had none of, but by its habits. The road liked to double back on itself when it grew bored. It shed its stones like a coat in spring. Where the human world had pressed too close, it thinned to a footpath strangled by bramble; where the wild still remembered itself, it widened into a corridor of birch and hawthorn so green it hurt his eyes, as if the color had been hoarded here for thirty years while everything else went grey.

Marguerite walked ahead of him, barefoot even over ground that bit at his boots. She moved the way water moves downhill — without decision, without hesitation — and the road seemed to arrange itself for her, lifting its roots just enough, parting its nettles like a crowd making room for someone important. She had not spoken much since the hollowed forest. Neither had he. Some silences were the honest kind.

It was she who stopped first.

She didn't stop the way a person stops — planting her feet, turning her head. She simply ceased moving, all at once, the way a held breath ceases, and every hair on Felix's arms stood up in sympathy. Ahead, where the road bent around a fallen oak furred thick with moss, something small and rust-colored sat in the middle of the path as though it had been waiting there since before the oak fell.

A fox.

Not crouched, not wary, not slinking along the verge the way foxes did when they deigned to notice humans at all. Sitting. Tail curled neat around its feet. One ear cocked toward them with an air of mild professional interest, like a clerk who has been expecting two particular customers and is mildly annoyed they're late.

Felix heard it before it spoke — or rather, he felt the shape of its attention, that familiar pressure behind his eyes that meant *beast, listening, aware*. But what came through was not the simple weather of animal thought he knew from rats and horses and gulls: hunger, fear, territory, the warm mud of immediate things. This arrived in language. Old, accented, sardonic language.

"Well," said the fox. "He does hear. I'd begun to think the stories were exaggerating, and I have never once known a story to exaggerate."

Marguerite made a sound Felix had never heard from her — a short, sharp bark of laughter, bright and startling as a branch snapping in still air. She dropped into a crouch, elbows on knees, and answered in the first tongue, rapid and delighted, and the fox answered back, and for a moment the two of them chattered like old friends meeting at a market while Felix stood in the middle of his own life feeling like the foreigner.

Then the fox turned its amber eyes on him and switched, with visible condescension, to something he could follow.

"You'll want to know who I am," it said. "Everyone does. It's the ears. Very expressive. Now — you. You're the grandmother's boy."

Felix's mouth went dry. "My grandmother?"

"Don't repeat everything I say, it's tedious." The fox rose, stretched fore and aft with theatrical thoroughness, and trotted a slow circle around him, nose working at his coat, his hands, the hem where the stolen archive-book still rode hidden against his ribs. "Maren Crane. Maren of the hedgerows. The woman who pulled a thorn the size of my forearm out of my mother's shoulder and never asked a price. Who left saucers of cream on her step through the hard winter and told the neighbors it was for the cat she did not own. Who talked to us — properly talked, not that baby-nonsense humans do — and more to the point, *listened*." The fox paused at his elbow and looked up. "You have her ears. It's indecent, how much you have her ears."

"She died four years ago," Felix said. His voice came out strange.

"Everything dies. That's rather the point of most arrangements." The fox sat again, closer now, and its tail flicked once. "She kept the covenant's small end of things, your grandmother. Not the grand business at the Stones — the daily bread of it. A kindness here. A debt honored there. The wild keeps long accounts, boy, longer than any Ledgerhouse, and we do not forget a credit." Its whiskers twitched. "I am called Russet-under-the-Briar, First-of-My-Name-This-Autumn, and I owe Maren Crane's blood a favor of passage. So. Where are you walking, and how quickly do you need to not be caught?"

Felix glanced at Marguerite. She was watching him with that fierce, weighing look she used — the one that judged him, always, by what the beasts did around him. And the beasts, this beast, had come to *him*. He saw her arrive at the same conclusion, and something in her shoulders loosened half an inch.

"The Standing Stones," Felix said. "Before solstice."

The fox was quiet for exactly as long as a joke would have fit, and no joke came.

"Ah," it said finally. "So it's that errand." It looked up at the sky, at the thin high clouds running east, and sighed the sigh of a creature doing arithmetic it disliked. "Well. Debts are debts. Walk on, then, both of you, and I'll get you there by the quick ways. But I'm obliged to say my piece first, because I'm obliged to say my piece first — covenant law, and worse, politeness."

"Say it," Marguerite said, in his tongue, halting but clear. She had been collecting his words the way she collected everything — carefully, greedily.

The fox fixed her with one amber eye. "The Stones are not a door that opens because you knock, little heir. They are a scale, and scales want weight. Every bargain struck there was paid for there. Blood paid some of them. Years paid others. Names. Voices. The Old Covenant itself — the one your mother ran to keep you out of reach of — cost the last speaker more than anyone wrote down, because the ones who write things down were men with ink, and ink is cheap." Its tail curled tighter. "Whatever you mean to do at those stones, it will take payment. And the wild does not make change."

"I know," Marguerite said.

"Do you?" The fox's voice gentled in a way that was somehow worse than its sarcasm. "You know it the way the young know death — as a word with teeth drawn. I've watched the grey eat the forest for thirty years, child. I buried my den-mates in ground that wouldn't rot their bodies properly. I have seen what an unpaid debt looks like when it comes due with interest." It turned back to Felix. "And you. Ledger-man. You especially should understand: nothing in this world is free, and the things that pretend to be free are the ones that bill you latest and largest. Your guild understood that much, at least. They just never learned what happens when you rob the creditor."

Felix thought of the torn page in the archive-book riding against his ribs. Of Vane's neat hand somewhere in a ledger thirty years gone, signing away a promise. "Then we pay," he said. "Whatever it costs."

"Mm." The fox began trotting up the road, tail high, and threw the words back over its shoulder. "Spoken like a man who hasn't seen the bill. Come along. The quick way goes through the briar-hollow, and if we hurry we can be past the ferry-water by tomorrow night, and past the worst of my jokes by never."

Marguerite fell in beside the fox, and Felix walked behind them both, and the Green Road carried them north.

* * *

Russet-under-the-Briar, it emerged over the following hours, was either the wisest creature Felix had ever met or the most exhausting, and possibly these were the same thing.

The fox narrated. Constantly. It named every tree they passed — not their human names but their dispositions. "*That* alder is a gossip, don't tell it anything. The yew beyond remembers the Romans, or claims to; yews lie about their age the way old men lie about their wars. Avoid the badger set on the left unless you enjoy being lectured about boundaries." It critiqued Felix's walking ("like a man crossing a frozen pond he doesn't trust"), his boots ("an insult to whoever made them, which was probably also you people"), and his habit of flinching when branches cracked ("in the wild, boy, the loud things are almost never the dangerous ones; it's the quiet you should fear, and you're traveling with the quietest thing in three kingdoms").

At this Marguerite smiled — actually smiled, wide and unguarded, the first Felix had seen since the forest of silence — and the sight of it went through him like warm water through cold hands.

They ate at midday beside a stream that ran loud and clean, the first truly living water Felix had seen since leaving the city. Russet showed them which roots to dig (bitter, but filling), which berries were food and which were "a lesson you only need once," and when Marguerite fell asleep in the afternoon sun with her head on her arm, the fox settled beside her like a sentry and lowered its voice for the first time since they'd met.

"A word, grandmother's-boy. While she sleeps."

Felix kept his eyes on the girl. "A word."

"The hunters behind you. Iron-shod, dog-led, and one of them worse than iron." The fox's ears flattened. "I smelled them at dawn, two valleys back. They move like the guild taught them to move — no waste, no song. The dogs are afraid of their handler. Did you know that? Dogs are never afraid of their handlers. Whatever that woman is, she frightens the animals bred to obey her."

Sable Harrow. Felix's stomach tightened. "Can you lose them?"

"For a time. The Green Road helps those it likes, and it likes her poorly — she walks it like a tax collector." The fox licked a paw, unhurried. "But hear me, and hear me plainly, because I like you and it will inconvenience me enormously if you die. There are three kinds of payment the Stones may ask. The kind you can give and go on living. The kind you can give and go on living *differently*. And the kind where the giving and the dying are the same act, distinguished only by bookkeeping." Its amber eyes caught the light, flat and gold and ancient. "Your grandmother paid the second kind, near the end. That's why her hearing was so fine and her body so frail — she'd spent herself into the covenant a coin at a time, all her life, and the wild gave her back its ears in trade. She knew what she was doing. She'd have wanted you to know it too."

Felix looked down at his hands. Hands that had balanced impossible accounts for nine years, that had fed a feral child through a counting-room door, that had never once done anything brave until this month. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because someone owes you honesty, and I'm paying installments on a debt." The fox stood, shook itself, and nodded at the sleeping girl. "And because when the bill comes, boy — and it will come — you'll want to remember that you chose this road knowing the toll. There's no dignity in surprise."

* * *

They walked through the evening and into the blue hour, and the fox's chatter softened into something almost like reverence as the land changed around them. The trees grew older. The moss grew deeper. Once, far off through the trunks, Felix saw something tall and pale moving with unhurried grace, and Russet said quietly, "Don't stare, don't name it, don't thank it," and whatever it was passed on and let them be.

It was full dark when they made camp in the lee of a granite shelf, and Marguerite, wrapped in Felix's coat, asked the question she had clearly been carrying all day.

"The favor," she said, in his tongue, working each word like a stone from the earth. "Your grandmother. What she did. Tell."

Felix hesitated. He knew so little — a saucer of cream, a story about birds, a woman who hummed to the hedge at dusk and called it nothing. "She was kind," he said finally. "To things no one else bothered with. I thought she was just... eccentric. I thought the stories she told were nonsense." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "She used to say the wrens carried messages for her. I was twelve. I told her birds couldn't carry messages. She just looked at me —" his throat closed briefly — "she looked at me like I'd failed a test I didn't know I was taking."

Russet, curled at the camp's edge, made a low sound that might have been laughter or grief. "Oh, she tested everyone. The wrens adored her. Vain little postmen." It rested its chin on its paws. "Sleep, both of you. Tomorrow the road turns toward the river, and rivers in the wild country are not like your rivers. They keep accounts too."

Felix lay back and did not sleep, not really. He listened instead — to the wind working the high branches, to Marguerite's slow breathing, to the vast layered murmur of the living world all around him, louder out here than it had ever been in the city walls, a thousand small voices saying *warm, safe, green, here*. Somewhere south and distant, faint as a scar, he felt the hollowed forest's silence pressing against everything, and beneath it, farther still, the iron-and-paper smell of the hunt that followed them.

Near midnight, the fox spoke once more, softly, whether to him or to itself he couldn't tell.

"Solstice in six days," Russet said. "And the Stones are waking. I can feel it in my teeth. They haven't woken in thirty years, grandmother's-boy, and things that wake after thirty years are always hungry."

In the dark, far off toward the river, something howled — long, and low, and in a voice that was not quite a wolf's.

The fox's ears went flat. "Or," it amended, "perhaps we won't sleep after all."
