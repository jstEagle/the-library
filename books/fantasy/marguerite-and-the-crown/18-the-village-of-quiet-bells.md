## 18. The Village of Quiet Bells

The village announced itself by its silence.

They had been walking since the grey hour before dawn, following the fox along a hedgerow that had gone strange — the hawthorn blooming out of season, white and heavy as snowfall, and the blackberries ripening on the same branch where the flowers still hung. Marguerite walked with her hand trailing through the blossoms the way other children trailed fingers along fence rails, and every so often she would say a word in the old tongue, soft as breath, and the hedge would lean toward her like a plant toward light.

Felix had learned three words in two days. *Ael* meant water, or perhaps meant moving water, or perhaps meant the memory of water — the old tongue did not seem to distinguish between a thing and what it remembered being. *Sennet* was the word for trust, but it also meant the space between two people who had agreed not to run. And *marguerite*, he had discovered with a jolt that first night on the Green Road, was not only her name. It was the word for the small white daisy that opened its face to the sun and closed it again when the light went away.

She is named for a flower that knows how to close itself, he had thought. And then: No. She's named for the flower that opens again.

The fox stopped so abruptly that Felix nearly trod on his brush.

"Here," the fox said. He was a lean red creature with one ear notched and an air of permanent grievance, and he spoke in the way beasts spoke to Felix — not in words exactly, but in meanings that arrived dressed as words because Felix's mind demanded clothing. "Smell it."

Felix smelled woodsmoke, bread, rain on dust. A village smell. He said as much.

"Underneath," the fox insisted, and his nose wrinkled. "Underneath the underneath."

Felix breathed again, deeper, the way he'd learned to breathe when listening to rats in the Ledgerhouse walls — slow, patient, emptying himself. And there it was, beneath the bread-smell and the smoke: a flatness. A nothing. The scent of a place where something had been subtracted and no one had swept up the absence.

"What is it?" Marguerite asked. She had gone still, her hand withdrawn from the hawthorn, her chin lifted the way she lifted it when she listened to things Felix could not hear at all.

"Dreams," she said, before anyone answered her. Her voice was thin. She had been learning human words from him with a frightening speed, hoarding them like coins, but this one came out worn smooth with use. "There are no dreams here. The dream-road is… closed." She searched for the word and found it in his own language, borrowed it back. "Shut. Like a door painted shut."

The fox looked at Felix with his amber eyes and said nothing, which from the fox was a great deal of saying.

"We need shelter," Felix said. It came out more stubborn than he felt. His feet hurt. His coat, with the stolen ledger sewn into its lining, hung off him like a second skin he couldn't shed. Somewhere behind them — a day behind, perhaps two — Sable Harrow was tracking them across the face of the world, and ahead of them lay four days' walk to the Standing Stones, and between those facts Felix needed sleep the way a drowning man needs air. "We need food and we need sleep, and we can't get either sleeping rough with the guild's hounds on our scent."

"The hounds are the least of it," the fox said. "Look at the bells."

Felix looked. The village's church rose above the rooftops ahead, squat and grey, and beside it stood a bell-tower, and in the bell-tower hung three bells, and the bells were green.

Not verdigris-green, though they were that too. Green like growing things. Moss had climbed the bell-ropes and woven itself through the louvres of the tower, and ivy had taken the frame, and one of the bells had a bird's nest built inside its mouth, twigs and wool and a scrap of blue ribbon, and the whole structure stood silent in a way that Felix felt in his teeth. Bells are never truly silent. They hum with the memory of their own voices, a held note, a readiness. These bells had let go of even that. They hung like dead things in a living cage of vine.

"No one has rung them," Marguerite said slowly, "in a long time. Not because they forgot how." She frowned, and the frown made her look suddenly, terribly young. "Because there was nothing left to ring *for*. The dreams went first. Then the…" She pressed her hand flat against her sternum. "Then this. The part of you that rings."

* * *

The village was called Bellharrow, according to the leaning sign at its edge, though the letters had been repainted so many times the name sat on the board like a stranger. They came in along the cart track past a sheep field where the sheep watched them pass with an attention that raised the hair on Felix's neck — not fear, not curiosity, just attention, flat and total, like lamps turning.

The people were worse.

It was market morning, or the hour that should have been market morning. Stalls stood open under awnings. Bread sat on trestle tables under cloth. A woman was setting out eggs in a basket, one by one, with perfect care. And nobody was talking.

Not silently talking, not shyly talking — there was none of the murmur and rustle a crowd makes even when it says nothing. The villagers moved through their morning like figures in a music box, each performing their turn, and when two of them passed they nodded, precise as clockwork, and their eyes never quite met.

"Good morning," Felix tried, to the egg woman.

She looked up. Her face was pleasant. Her face was the problem — it was pleasant the way a painted face is pleasant, all the features correct and arranged, and behind it nothing moved. "Morning," she said, in a voice with no more grain than paper. "You'll be wanting breakfast. The inn does eggs."

"Thank you," Felix said. "We — we've come a long way. From the city."

Something flickered. It was very small, and it was gone almost before it arrived — a contraction around her eyes, a tightening at the mouth's corners, the ghost of an expression that might once have been grief. "The city," she repeated. "That's far." She placed another egg. "You'll be wanting breakfast," she said again, and turned back to her basket, and the flicker was buried.

Marguerite had stopped in the middle of the lane. She was looking at the woman with an expression Felix had never seen on her — not her fierce wariness, not her quick animal assessment, but something older and sadder, the look of someone recognizing a wound they have seen before on a much larger body.

"Felix," she whispered. "Her inside is quiet. All of them. Their insides are—" She pressed both hands over her ears, though there was nothing to hear. "It's like the hollowing. It's like the grey forest, but the forest is *them*."

The fox had vanished, as the fox did whenever anything required courage; he reappeared ten minutes later having scouted the entire village, and reported in a low mutter: "No dogs. Did you notice? A village this size should have dogs. Cats, either. There are birds in the trees but they don't sing, they just sit. Whatever took the dreams took everything that dreams. Everything that *rings*." He licked his paw with elaborate unconcern. "I want it noted that I suggested we go around."

"You didn't suggest anything."

"I am suggesting it now, retroactively. I want credit."

But the inn had beds, and the innkeeper — a broad, slow man whose silence had a different texture than the others', less empty than *held*, like a man holding a door shut against weather — took Felix's coin without counting it and showed them to a room under the eaves with a window looking out on the bell-tower, and for a few hours, in daylight, with the door barred and Marguerite curled on the far bed with the fox a warm weight against her back, Felix slept.

He dreamed.

He woke gasping from a dream of the Ledgerhouse flooded with dark water, ledgers floating open on the surface like lilies, every page blank, and Aldous Vane standing waist-deep in the flood writing on the water with a pen that wrote nothing. He woke and lay listening to his own heart and understood, with a cold sliding feeling, that he had dreamed — that here, in the village of no dreams, he had dreamed anyway.

Beside him, Marguerite was sitting bolt upright, staring at him.

"You dreamed," she said. Not a question.

"I think so. Yes."

She reached out and touched his wrist, lightly, the way she touched things she wasn't sure were real. "The dream-road is shut here. But you walked it anyway." Her eyes were enormous in the dim room. "Felix. The road isn't shut. It's *guarded*. Something is eating the dreams before they can arrive, and it didn't eat yours because—" She stopped. She looked at the fox. The fox looked back.

"Because I'm not from here," Felix finished. "Because my gift—"

"Because you're *covenant*," Marguerite said simply, as if it were obvious, as if it explained everything, and perhaps to her it did. "The wild doesn't stop speaking to its own. Even here. Even now."

* * *

They found the elder in the afternoon.

The fox had heard it first — a thread of true sound in the village's woven silence, thin and wavering, coming from the last house before the fields. Not speech. Singing. Someone in Bellharrow was singing, alone, in a cracked and wandering voice, and the song had no words Felix knew but it had *shape*, it rose and fell like breathing, and the moment it touched his ear every hair on his arms stood up because he recognized the grammar of it.

"That's the old tongue," he said. "Someone here speaks—"

"Not speaks," Marguerite breathed. She was already running.

The house was small and clean and dim, smelling of dried herbs and beeswax, and in a bed by the window lay an old woman so thin she seemed assembled from sticks and parchment, propped against pillows, singing to the ceiling. A younger woman — her daughter, Felix guessed, from the exhausted set of her shoulders — sat beside the bed shelling peas into a bowl, and she looked up at the intrusion with the same flat pleasantness as the egg-woman, but the old woman turned her head, and her eyes found Marguerite, and they *lit*.

The song stopped. The old woman's mouth worked. Tears ran down into the creases of her neck, and she made a sound that was half laugh and half sob, and she said, in a voice like dry leaves, in accented but unmistakable human speech:

"There you are. Oh, thank every stone and star. There you *are*."

"Grandmother—" the daughter began, alarmed.

"Hush, Tilly. Hush and bring the chair. Can't you see what's come through my window?" The old woman struggled up onto one elbow, and Felix moved to help her without thinking, and her hand closed on his arm with shocking strength. She looked at him then, really looked, the way no one in this village had looked at anything since — searching, sharp, alive. "And you. You're the listener. Yes. Yes, I can see it hanging on you like a coat. Sit down, boy, before you fall down, you've got a hunted look and no supper in you."

"How do you know what I—" Felix began.

"I knew your grandmother."

The room went very still. Outside, a wind moved through the silent trees, and the unsung bells did not ring.

"Her name was Wren," the old woman said. "Though that wasn't the name she was born with, and I'd wager she never told you the born-name, because the born-names are for the covenant-folk and she'd have kept it close after—" She stopped herself, and her eyes went to the window, to the green and silent tower. "After the year the bells went quiet. Sit. Tilly, the chair, and put the kettle on if you remember how kettles work, which some days I wonder."

Her name was Maren Ostler, and she was ninety-one years old, and she was dying — she said so herself, cheerfully, the way people say things they have made peace with. She had been born in Bellharrow and would die in it, and in between she had spent seven years as a girl in the hill country to the north, in a household that kept the old ways, where she had learned enough of the first tongue to sing the cradle-songs and not one word more. And there she had known a woman called Wren who talked to wrens, naturally, and to everything else besides, and who had taught her that the songs were not decoration.

"The songs are the stitches," Maren said. She had refused the bed's pillows and sat propped in her chair by the window now, wrapped in a shawl, watching Marguerite with an expression that Felix could only call hunger — a good hunger, a starving-for-beauty hunger. "The covenant isn't a document, boy, whatever the guild thinks it sealed away in its cellars. It's a song sung by two voices. One voice is the wild. The other voice is us. And when one voice stops—" she snapped her thin fingers, a sound like a twig breaking "—the harmony goes. And when the harmony goes long enough, the *music* goes. And when the music goes—" she gestured out the window, at the village, at the flat-faced people in the lanes, at the green bells hanging dumb in their ivy "—you get this. You get a village full of people who wake in the morning and cannot tell you what they lost in the night, because they lost the losing itself. My neighbors aren't sad. Sad would be a mercy. My neighbors are *blank*."

"When did it happen?" Felix asked quietly. "The year the bells went quiet."

"Thirty years ago, come harvest." Maren's eyes were steady on his. "I was sixty-one. I remember it like yesterday, because it was the last day I dreamed. There was a storm that night, away north — everyone said later it was over the Stones, though the guild's men came through the week after and told us it was nothing, a weather, a falling of rocks, and they paid good silver for the telling. Paid silver for us to forget. And we did forget, most of us. That's the horror of it, boy. They didn't take our dreams by force. They bought them, fair and square, entered it in a ledger somewhere, and we sold." Her mouth twisted. "I only kept mine because I was away north that winter, under the hill-people's protection. Their songs held a roof over my sleep."

Marguerite had knelt at the old woman's feet. She knelt the way she did everything — with a fierce, unselfconscious gravity, as if kneeling were a border crossing. She said something in the old tongue, low and questioning, and Maren Ostler's face crumpled and bloomed at once, and she answered, haltingly, in the same tongue, and the two of them spoke together for a while in sounds like water over stones, and Felix sat with his hands useless in his lap, understanding nothing, understanding everything.

At last Maren laughed — a real laugh, rusty, delighted — and wiped her eyes, and said to Felix:

"She wants to know why your grandmother's songs died out, if the listener-line survived. And I told her I didn't know. And she said—" the old woman's voice gentled "—she said that grief makes people stop singing, but shame makes them stop teaching. And she thinks your grandmother stopped teaching. And I think she's right, and I think you know it too, in the place where you keep the things you don't say out loud."

Felix looked at the floorboards. His grandmother's bird stories. Her warnings, wrapped in nonsense. *Never trade what you can't count, Felix. Never sign what you haven't read twice. And if ever a man in a fine coat asks you what you hear in the walls — you hear mice, boy. Only ever mice.* She had taught him concealment instead of covenant. She had handed him survival and called it inheritance, and maybe she'd had no choice, maybe the guild's men had come to her too, maybe shame and fear had sewn her mouth shut — but the result was a boy who could hear the whole wild world and had spent his life pretending it was a curse and a secret and a small dishonest income.

"She was keeping me safe," he said. It came out defensive.

"Yes," Maren said. "And look how well it worked. Hunted through the countryside by a woman with knives, carrying stolen scripture in your coat, in love with a war you can't win alone." She smiled to take the sting out, and failed, and didn't try again. "Safety is a story we tell children. Covenant is the true one. It costs. Everything true costs. But it *pays its debts*, boy, which is more than the guild ever did."

Tilly brought tea, and drank hers in silence, and Felix watched her and saw — now that he knew to look — the flickers. The contractions of the blank face, moments when something moved behind the glass like a fish under ice. Tilly remembered dreaming. Tilly was young enough that thirty years ago she'd been a child, and whatever the guild's silver had bought, it hadn't bought the deep layers. She glanced at Marguerite three times during the tea, and the third time her eyes were wet, and she clearly did not know why.

"Maren," Felix said. "You're dying."

"I'm ninety-one, boy, I'm not dying, I'm *finishing*. There's a difference. A loaf finishes. A fire dies. I intend to finish well." She set down her cup. "Which brings me to why you're really here, and don't pretend otherwise, you've got the Stones in your walk. You're taking her to renew it. Before solstice."

"Yes."

"And you know what it requires."

"Some of it," Felix said carefully. "We know the heir must speak the covenant whole. We know there's a remaking involved. We don't know—" He stopped, because Marguerite had gone very still beside him, and he was not going to say *whether she survives it* in front of her, not yet, not until he'd read the terms again in the stolen ledger and found whether mercy lived anywhere in the old script.

Maren looked at him for a long moment. Whatever she saw in his face, she chose — visibly, deliberately — to let it pass.

"Then let me give you what I have," she said, "while I have hands to give it." She reached beneath her shawl and drew out a cord, and on the cord hung a small object that Felix had taken for a bead: a round grey thing, drilled through, worn smooth. She pressed it into Marguerite's palm and folded the girl's fingers over it. "A pebble from the Standing Stones themselves. My mother carried it home before I was born, back when folk still made pilgrimage, back when the Stones still sang. It's a key of sorts. Or a promise of a key. The hill-people said a stone from the circle remembers the circle, and where it is, the circle knows the way home." Her voice dropped. "It may be nothing. It may be everything. Take it."

Marguerite opened her hand and looked at the pebble, and Felix saw her nostrils flare, saw her whole body orient toward it like a compass needle, and she said one word in the old tongue — soft, wondering — and Maren answered, and translated for Felix with tears standing in her eyes:

"She says it's *singing*. Very quietly. She says it's been singing for thirty years, waiting for someone to hear it." The old woman laughed, wet and helpless. "Thirty years in my pocket and I never once heard a thing. Isn't that the whole of it? The miracle sitting right against your skin, and no ear left to hear it with."

Then Maren Ostler took Marguerite's face in her two thin hands, and blessed her.

Felix had expected Latin, or scripture, or the mumbled formulas of village piety. Instead the old woman spoke in the first tongue — slow, cracked, imperfect, a song half-remembered from a girlhood seventy years gone — and as she spoke, the room changed. The herbs hanging from the rafters stirred though the window was shut. The tea in its cups trembled in rings. And Felix, who had spent his whole life hearing the beasts and nothing else, heard for the first time a third layer beneath the world: a vast, slow, sleeping harmony, like a cathedral organ an ocean away, turning over in its sleep and — for one moment — *noticing*.

The blessing, when Maren reached its end, resolved into human words, plain as bread:

"*Last word of the first tongue. When all other voices fail, yours remains. Speak, and the deaf world will remember its own name.*"

Marguerite was crying without any expression at all, tears simply falling, and she bowed her head into the old woman's hands and stayed there a long time.

* * *

They left before dawn. There was no discussion about it; the fox appeared at their window at the black hour and said only, "Riders on the south road. Guild horses. Moving fast. We leave now or we leave in chains," and that settled it.

But at the edge of the village Felix paused, and looked back.

Bellharrow lay grey under the last stars, shuttered and silent, its green bells hanging in their cage of ivy. And in the window of the last house, a candle burned — the only lit window in the village — and behind the glass, faint and cracked and utterly undaunted, an old woman was singing the cradle-songs to the dark, singing them loud, singing them *for* the village that could no longer hear, stitching at the torn harmony with the only needle she had left, spending her last nights doing the only thing that had ever mattered.

Marguerite stood beside him. In her closed fist, the pebble from the Standing Stones hummed its tiny thirty-year note.

"When the covenant is renewed," she said quietly, in careful human words, "will they dream again? Tilly? The egg-woman?"

"Yes," Felix said. "I think so. I think everything sleeps and waits, and waits and sleeps, and wakes when it's called."

"Then we must not be late." She turned her face toward the north, toward the hills, toward the Stones, and the wind coming down from the high country stirred her hair and carried a smell Felix had learned to dread: iron, and horse sweat, and oiled leather. Far off on the south road, a dog barked — the first dog he'd heard in Bellharrow, and it was not a village dog.

The fox materialized out of the hedgerow, hackles up.

"They've reached the crossroads," he said. "And they're not riding like searchers. They're riding like they already know exactly where we slept."
