## 22. Marguerite's Doubt

The fox had told her in the way foxes tell things — sideways, with a joke folded inside the truth like a thorn inside a berry.

*The Stones take a word and give back a world,* he'd said, licking his paw as though he were discussing nothing more important than the taste of river mud. *But first they must unmake the speaker. That is how it was done before. That is how it will be done again. Your mother's mother's mother stood there once, little heir, and came out changed.*

Marguerite had laughed then, because the fox's jokes were always half-laughable, and because she did not yet understand that *unmade* was not a figure of speech.

Now she understood.

She sat cross-legged on the flat crown of a boulder above the camp where Felix slept, his bedroll a pale smudge in the dark below, the fire burned down to a red eye that watched nothing. Above her the sky was thick with stars — too many stars for the human lands, she thought; these were wild-realm stars, the old ones, the ones her mother had named for her in a voice like water moving under ice. *That one is the Hound That Never Sleeps. That one is the Loom. That one is the Door Left Open.* She had known them all before she could walk. She had thought she would know them all her life.

*Unmade and remade.*

The words from the carved stone would not leave her. Felix had copied them onto a scrap of paper in his careful clerk's hand, translating as best he could with the small stock of the first tongue she had taught him, and even in his clumsy human letters the words had teeth:

*Let the heir come willing. Let her be unmade as the word is unmade from the mouth. Let her be remade as the word is remade in the listening ear. What stands at the Stones at solstice shall not stand away from them unchanged, if it stands at all.*

*If it stands at all.*

She turned the phrase over in the old tongue, where it lived most naturally, and it was worse there. In the first language, *unmade* was the same root as the word for a candle going out. The same root as snow melting from a branch. The same root as a name forgotten by everyone who ever spoke it.

Her mother had never told her this part.

Or perhaps her mother had not known. Perhaps her mother had only run, carrying a child and a dying covenant, hoping to reach the Stones before the guild's knives reached them both, and had never stopped to read the fine print of what she was running toward. Mothers did that, Marguerite was learning. They carried you toward things without telling you the weight of them, because to tell you would be to set the weight on your shoulders early, and mothers wanted their children light a little longer.

Below, the fire popped. Felix stirred in his sleep and murmured something — a number, probably, or a line of ledger-script; even dreaming he was a counting-house creature. She had watched him sleep before. It was a thing she did when she could not sleep herself, the way other children counted sheep. He slept like a man who had spent his whole life practicing smallness: curled slightly inward, hands tucked, taking up as little of the world as the world would allow. And yet he had opened a coal chute for her. He had lied to a ferryman of rivers for her. He had walked into the grey forest where no sound lived, where his gift went blind and screaming, and he had kept walking because she asked him to.

She did not want to die. That was the plain truth underneath all the others, the truth she had been circling for three days like a moth around a lamp it could not touch. She did not want to be unmade. She was eleven years old, or near enough — the seasons said eleven, though the first tongue had no word for birthday — and she had only just learned the taste of bread baked with salt, only just learned that humans could make jokes with their whole faces, only just learned the human words for *friend* and *stay* and *please*. She had spent her whole life being the last of something. Now she was being asked to be the end of something, willingly, and to call it glory.

*Willing*, the stone insisted. The covenant would not take an unwilling heir. That was its oldest law, older than the guild, older than the kingdoms: no bond forged by force would hold. Which meant the Stones themselves would know, would taste her heart the way the river spirit had tasted the ferryman's toll, and if there was fear rotting at the center of her willingness, the whole remaking would fail — or worse, succeed crookedly, and bind the realms together with a knot of terror that would strangle them both someday.

So she could not merely go to the Stones. She had to *want* it.

And she did not want it. She wanted to live. She wanted spring. She wanted to teach Felix the word for *rain-on-warm-stone* and watch him say it wrong forty times and right once. She wanted to see whether the fox ever actually paid his debts or only talked about them beautifully. She wanted —

She wanted her mother.

The grief arrived the way it always did now, without knocking, the way grief does when it has lived in a body long enough to have its own key. Her mother's hands, brown and quick, braiding cattail stems into rings. Her mother's laugh, which sounded like nothing human and everything alive. Her mother turning at the treeline on the last morning, saying — not in words, in the pressure of her whole soul — *run, little word, run and don't stop until the stones can hear you.*

Her mother had known. Marguerite saw it suddenly, clearly, the way you see a shape in smoke only after it has already gone. Her mother had known what the Stones required, and she had sent her daughter anyway, because a daughter remade was better than a world unmade, and because love sometimes has to spend the thing it loves most.

*No,* Marguerite whispered to the night, in the old tongue, and the word came out cracked. *No. You don't get to decide that for me either.*

Because that was the other truth, wasn't it? Everyone had decided things for her. Her mother had decided she would be the heir. The guild had decided she was merchandise. Vane had decided she was a problem to be priced. Sable Harrow had decided, briefly, mercifully, to let her run. And now the dead stone carvers, three thousand years gone, had decided that some future child — her — would stand in a fire and be taken apart like a sentence, and everyone called it holy.

Well. She was the last speaker of the first tongue. If anyone got to decide, it should be her.

*

She considered running.

Not idly. Not the way she had considered it in the counting room, locked among ledgers, running as pure instinct, a trapped animal's single bright idea. This was different. This was deliberate. She lay on the warm boulder with the stars wheeling overhead and she planned it the way Felix planned things, step by step, cost by cost.

South, past the village of quiet bells, there was a road. Roads led to ports. Ports led to ships. Ships crossed water, and water was the one place the beasts' gossip could not follow her, the one place the guild's long arm grew short. She knew the gull-flocks would carry word of her passage — but gulls could be paid. She had seen Felix pay them. She had a pouch of river-pebbles that the ferryman's sister-spirit had given her, pebbles that sang faintly when two of them touched, and singing things were worth much to seabirds.

She could vanish. There were islands. There were places where the wild realm still leaked through in small ways — a spring that never froze, a tree that flowered in winter — and she could live beside those leaks like a beggar beside a rich man's wall, keeping the last of the first tongue alive in her own mouth, alone, until she was old and it died with her anyway.

And the world would hollow. The grey would spread. She had walked through the Hollowing with Felix; she had put her palm against a tree that used to be a talking tree and felt nothing answering, nothing at all, a silence so complete it had texture, like pressing your hand into wet ash. She had seen the deer standing motionless at the forest's edge, not dead but *unlit*, waiting for a word that would never come. She had heard Felix breathe her name in that place and heard how afraid he was, the great listener with nothing left to hear.

If she ran, every green thing between here and the sea would go the way of that tree. Slowly. Then not slowly. The rats in the Ledgerhouse walls had spoken of the hollowing spreading already, meadow by meadow, and the village of quiet bells — the people there had lost their dreams. Lost them. Whole human souls going grey at the edges because a pact made before iron was broken by men with inkstained fingers.

And it would be her fault. Not the breaking — that sin belonged to Aldous Vane and his tidy, terrible ledger — but the refusal to mend it. She would be the girl who could have said the word and didn't. She would carry that the way her mother had carried the flight, the way Felix carried his silences: everywhere, always, woven into everything she ate and said and dreamed.

Unless she didn't dream anymore. Unless the hollowing took that too.

She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes.

There was a third path, of course. The one the fox had hinted at with his sideways smile, the one she kept not letting herself think. *The Stones demand payment,* he had said. *They always do. But they are fair, in the way of very old things. Fair is not the same as kind.* Perhaps there was a way to pay without paying everything. Perhaps a willing heir who offered something else — a memory, a name, a life other than her own — could stand in the fire and walk out the far side smaller but whole. Felix would think of it. Felix was good at finding the seam in a bargain; he had grown up in a house built entirely out of bargains.

But asking him meant telling him she was afraid. Telling him she was afraid meant watching him become afraid for her, and Felix's fear was a loud, clumsy thing despite all his practice at hiding it — she could hear it in his heartbeat whenever she pressed her ear to his chest the way she'd once listened to birds through eggshells. His fear would make him reckless. Reckless Felix got caught. Caught Felix got them both delivered to Vane in an iron cart, and then there would be no choice at all, no Stones, no fire, just a cell and a clerk's pen writing *asset* beside her name.

No. She would carry this alone a while longer. She was good at carrying things alone. It was nearly the only skill her old life had given her.

*

Dawn found her still on the boulder, stiff with cold, having decided nothing.

That was the humiliation of it. All night of planning and she had chosen neither the road nor the fire. She had simply lain there like a stone herself, letting the hours pour over her, and now the sky was doing its slow grey-to-gold trick and the birds were waking in the hedge below with their small blunt human-adjacent thoughts — *worms, worms, the cat, worms* — and none of it had helped.

Felix was awake. She could tell by the change in his breathing, the way sleep's tide goes out of a person all at once. He sat up in his bedroll and looked around for her the way he always did first thing, a quick flick of panic smoothed over so fast a normal person would miss it, and then he saw her on the rock and something in his shoulders let go.

"You didn't sleep," he said. Not a question. He was getting better at reading her; it was both wonderful and inconvenient.

"I slept," she said, in his language, practicing. Her human words were still strange in her mouth, round stones in a net made for fish. "A little."

He built the fire up and set water on. He moved around the camp the way he did everything — quietly, economically, as if existence itself were a ledger to be balanced — and she watched him and felt the terrible warm ache that had been growing in her chest for weeks now, the one shaped like a brother she'd never had, or a father, or simply a *person who stays*. If she ran south, she would lose this. That was the true price of the road, more than the guilt, more than the grey. Everyone she had ever loved had been taken or lost, and here was one she would have to take *herself*, with her own feet, one step at a time, if she chose the sea.

"Felix." Her voice surprised her. "At the Stones. When I am—" She stopped. The human words for it didn't exist, or she didn't own them yet. She switched tongues, and the old language poured out of her, low and fast, the way water finds cracks: *When I am unmade. When the fire takes the word out of my mouth. Tell me truly, you who hear all things — what does it feel like, do you think, to be unwritten? Does it hurt? Or is it like falling asleep in summer, and the world goes on speaking and you simply aren't in it anymore?*

Felix went very still.

He understood more of the first tongue now than he pretended — she had noticed, and said nothing, because it pleased her to have one secret even from him. His face did the thing it did when he was translating behind his eyes, the slight narrowing, the almost-listening. And then his face did another thing, a worse thing. A guilty thing.

It passed in half a breath. A lesser listener would have missed it entirely. But Marguerite had been raised by moths and foxes and a mother whose every mood moved across her like weather, and she knew the anatomy of a hidden thing when she saw one.

"What?" she said. "What is it?"

"Nothing," Felix said. "You need to eat. We have a long day."

*Liar,* said the wren in the hawthorn, cheerfully, in the way of wrens, meaning only *worms*. But Marguerite heard it as judgment anyway.

He handed her the tin cup of hot water with the dried berries steeping in it, and his hand was steady, and his face was kind, and somewhere behind the kindness was a door with a lock on it, and she had seen the shadow of whatever was behind that door pass across his eyes like a bird over water.

She drank. She smiled at him, because she was also practiced at hiding things — she had hidden in plain sight since before she could remember, feral child, mute curiosity, the girl worth a bounty.

And beneath the smile, quiet as a seed under snow, a new resolve took root: she would find out what Felix was not telling her. Because if the Stones were going to unmake her, she would walk into that fire knowing *everything* — every term, every cost, every mercy withheld out of love. She had been lied to by her mother's silence and the guild's ledgers and the whole conspiring kindness of the world.

Never again. Not even by him.

She looked at Felix over the rim of the cup, and he looked back, and between them the fire crackled and the morning birds gossiped about worms, and neither of them knew yet that the truth, when it finally came out — as truths do, at the worst possible hour, beside the worst possible fire — would burn hotter than anything the Standing Stones had ever asked of either of them.
