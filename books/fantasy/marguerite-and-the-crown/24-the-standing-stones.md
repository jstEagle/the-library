## 24. The Standing Stones

*Felix*

They smelled it before they saw it — or rather, Felix smelled the absence of everything else.

For three days they had climbed through country that grew stranger with every mile: hedgerows gone feral and singing, oaks whose roots arched over the road like the ribs of drowned ships, streams that ran silver in the morning and black by dusk. The fox — who had introduced himself, at length and with considerable self-regard, as Master Reynard of the Third Burrow, though Marguerite insisted his true name was a sound like *rain on embers* — had led them along paths that did not exist until he walked them. But on the third evening the fox stopped. He sat down in the middle of the green road, wrapped his tail around his feet, and would not go another step.

"Here," he said, "the road ends being mine."

Felix crouched beside him. "What do you mean?"

"I mean that ahead lies ground no beast may claim and none may refuse." The fox's amber eyes were uncharacteristically still. "The Stones belong to the bargain itself. Walk softly, speaker's-son. That ground remembers every foot that has ever crossed it, and it keeps accounts better than your Ledgerhouse ever could."

Marguerite was already walking. She moved the way she always did when something called to her — not running, but drawn, the way water is drawn downhill, as if resistance had simply become impossible for her. Felix scrambled up and followed, and the fox watched them go with an expression Felix could only describe, absurdly, as a creature taking off its hat.

The hill rose out of the moor like a held breath. There was no trees on its crown, no grass worth the name, only pale wind-combed turf and then — rising against a sky the color of old pewter — the Stones.

There were nine of them, great grey monoliths taller than the guildhall's tower, set in a ring upon the hilltop like the fingers of a hand half-closed around something precious. They were not carved in any way Felix's eye could call deliberate, and yet they were not natural either; their surfaces bore a smoothness that spoke of intention older than chisels, as if the earth itself had been persuaded to grow them into shape. Lichen silvered their flanks. Rain had grooved them in long dark tears. And they were silent.

That was the thing Felix felt most, standing at last within their shadow, his lungs burning from the climb, Marguerite's small cold hand finding his without either of them deciding it should. He had spent his whole life inside a world that whispered. Rats in walls, horses in stables, gulls screaming bargains over the harbor, moths murmuring at candlelight, the low continuous gossip of the living world — he had heard it all since before he could remember, had learned to hold it behind his teeth the way other men hold their breath underwater. Even in the Hollowing, where the forest had gone grey and dumb, there had been silence of a particular kind: the silence of things dying, which still has weight, still has grief in it.

This was different. This was the silence of a bell that has not yet been struck.

"They're waiting," Marguerite said.

She said it in her own tongue first — a phrase like wind moving through winter reeds, syllables that seemed to arrive slightly before her mouth made them — and then, carefully, in the human words she had been learning from him word by word across a hundred miles of road. Her accent turned each vowel into something older. *Waiting.* As if the Stones were travelers who had stopped here ten thousand years ago and simply never resumed their journey.

"How can you tell?" Felix asked.

"Because I have been listening to them since we crossed the river." She looked up at him, and in the flat pewter light her eyes were the exact grey-green of the moor. "They are not asleep, Felix. Sleeping things dream. These are *holding*. Holding a note the way a singer holds a note — you remember? In the village, the woman who sang at the well?" She hummed it: one long unwavering tone. "Like that. For ten thousand years. No wonder they are tired."

He wanted to laugh at the childishness of it — stones getting tired — and found he could not, because standing there he could feel it too, faintly, the way you feel a train coming through the soles of your boots before you hear it. Something beneath the hill, beneath the whole moor, holding one enormous note. Waiting for someone to let it resolve.

The sun was going down behind the western Stones, dragging long shadows across the ring like the spokes of a wheel. Tomorrow was the solstice eve. The day after, the longest day, when — if the fox and the ferryman and the dying elder of the quiet-bells village had told it true — the door between the realms would stand at its widest, and what remained of the wild realm's slow death would begin to spill through.

"We should read the terms," Felix said. "Before anything else. Before we touch anything, or speak anything, or—" He heard himself and stopped. "I've spent my life reading contracts, Marguerite. You don't sign anything until you've read what's written in the small places. Especially when the other party wrote the contract first."

Marguerite tilted her head. "You are afraid of them."

"Yes."

"Good," she said, with such simple approval that he almost did laugh. "My mother said fear is the mind bowing. Bow first. Then look."

* * *

*Marguerite*

The Stones did not speak to her the way beasts spoke. Beasts were voices; they came and went, they argued and teased and grieved, they were many. The Stones were one voice that was also no voice — more like the feeling of standing inside her mother's arms, if her mother's arms had been the size of the sky and had been holding very still for a very long time so as not to wake something.

She walked the ring slowly, laying her palm against each Stone in turn, because that was what the shape of the words in her blood told her to do. Nine Stones, nine greetings. Her mother had taught her the greetings as lullabies, back when Marguerite had thought they were only songs about big grey grandmothers. *Hail, keeper of the east door. Hail, rememberer of names. Hail, you who weigh.* She had not known she was memorizing a key.

At the fourth Stone her hand found something her eyes had missed.

The lichen covered it like a bandage, but beneath, cut deep into the grey flesh of the rock, letters spiraled — not carved downward but somehow *grown* inward, as if the stone had swallowed writing and kept it safe. The characters were nothing like human letters. They coiled. Each one looked like a frozen gesture: a branch bending, a river turning, a bird's wing at the top of its stroke. She knew them the way she knew breathing.

"Felix," she said. "Come see what they are hiding under their blankets."

He came, and stood beside her, and she watched his face do the thing it always did when the world showed him a new room — that careful widening, the scholar's hunger fighting the fugitive's caution. He reached out and touched the letters and pulled his fingers back as if the rock had bitten him.

"It's warm," he said. "Marguerite, the stone is warm. Like skin."

"The words are alive," she said. It was not a strange sentence in her mouth. "Words that are true don't die. My mother said the first tongue isn't a language people made. It's the language things *are*. When you say *river* in the first tongue, you aren't naming the river. You are lending it your voice for a moment, and it says itself through you." She traced one spiral without touching it. "These words have been saying themselves for ten thousand years. That is why the Stones are tired."

"Can you read them?"

"All of it." And then, because she had learned this much of humans, and of him: "But you cannot. And the covenant is between two kinds. So we will read it together, and I will give you the words, and you will make them yours too. That is what a covenant *is*, Felix. Not one voice. Two voices saying the same true thing."

He nodded slowly. Then he did something she had not expected: he took off his coat, folded it, and laid it on the turf, and rolled up his sleeves as though preparing for a night of accounting. Old habits. She was beginning to understand that Felix met even miracles the way clerks met ledgers — sleeves up, pen ready, terrified and precise. She found she loved it about him, though she would not have known how to say so in his language. In hers there was a word for it: the word meant *one who counts the stars so as not to be afraid of the dark.*

"Read," he said.

So she read.

* * *

*Felix*

Her voice changed when she read the first tongue. He had heard her speak it a hundred times on the road — teaching him, scolding the fox, singing fragments of lullaby — and it had always sounded like music from a country he'd never visit. Now, standing with her hand pressed to the Stone and the last light draining out of the sky, her voice became something else. It became the sound the words made when no throat carried them. He felt each syllable land in his chest like a struck tuning fork, and — God help him — he *understood*. Not translated. Understood, the way you understand falling.

And the terms of the Old Covenant poured into him like cold clear water.

*That the wild shall keep the rain and the root and the running thing, and man shall keep the fire and the field and the spoken word. That neither shall enter the other's house uninvited, save at the doors appointed: the Stones, the fords, the thresholds of hearths. That each year at the turning of the light, a voice of each kind shall meet here and renew the promise, speaking it whole, so that the wall between the worlds remains a wall and not a wound.*

*And that the promise shall be kept by speakers bound willingly — unmade and remade at this ring, so that each carries the other kind within them, and neither can forget what they have sworn.*

*And should the promise fail — should either kind break faith — the failing kind owes restoration, and the wronged kind owes mercy, and both owe truth. For a covenant built on anything less is only a cage with good manners.*

Felix stood very still while the words settled into him, and felt the vertigo of discovery come again — the same sick lurch he'd felt in the Undercroft Archive, kneeling over that torn page — except now there was nowhere left for the truth to hide. Here it was, cut in stone older than every kingdom, older than the Ledgerhouse, older than lying itself.

*Unmade and remade.* He glanced at Marguerite. She had gone quiet mid-spiral, her lips still moving, her eyes fixed on some middle distance. She had read those words too. He thought of what she might be feeling and made himself not flinch away from thinking it.

"There's more," she said softly. "Here. The last part. It is smaller. Like handwriting getting tired."

She led him around the ring to the westernmost Stone, the one facing the sunset, and there — lower down, half-swallowed by lichen — the spirals continued in a different register, denser, almost hurried. She read, and Felix listened, and what he heard made the hair rise along his arms.

*Let it be known to whatever kind comes after: the wild does not ask to be loved. It asks to be *answered*. Every gift it gives — the rain, the beast's speech, the green road, the second sight of small things — is a question. Will you answer? Will you answer? Will you answer?*

*And the answer is never a temple. The answer is a person who hears, and does not turn away.*

Felix's throat closed. He thought of his grandmother on her sagging kitchen chair, telling her bird stories to a boy who thought they were nonsense — the wren who carried messages, the robin who paid debts, the sparrow who kept watch at funerals. *Answer them when they talk to you, Felix love,* she'd said once, so casually, peeling apples. *It's rude not to.* His whole life, his hidden shameful gift, his careful double existence in the Ledgerhouse — it had never been an accident, or a curse, or a curiosity to be traded. It had been an inheritance. A post left unmanned, waiting for him to come home to it.

"Felix." Marguerite's hand was on his arm. "Your face is doing the thing like the river spirit's face. Like a man remembering something he paid away."

"I'm all right." And for once it wasn't a lie. "Keep reading. We should know all of it before—"

He didn't finish, because the light died.

Not gradually. The sun dropped below the western Stone and the shadow of the ring swept over them like a closing book, and in the sudden blue darkness Felix heard — felt — the Stones' held note change. Just slightly. The way a sleeping man's breathing changes when someone enters the room.

Marguerite went rigid beside him. Her hand found his and gripped hard enough to hurt.

"They know we're here," she whispered. "Felix — they know *what* we are. The heir. The speaker's son. They have been holding their note for ten thousand years and we are the ones who came to let it rest, and now they are—" She searched for the word and gave up on human speech entirely, pouring out a stream of the first tongue, and the only part Felix caught clearly was a single spiral-word repeated three times, growing louder, echoing off the Stones and off the hillsides and off the underside of the sky:

*Tomorrow. Tomorrow. Tomorrow.*

Then, from far below — down the darkening slopes, back along the green road, from the direction of the city they had fled — a sound that did not belong to the moor at all.

Iron. Many boots. And cutting through the dusk, thin and clear and horribly familiar, the whistle Sable Harrow used to call her hounds.

The fox materialized out of the gloom at the edge of the ring, ears flat, tail low, all his comedy burned away.

"They're early," he said. "And they brought the iron."
