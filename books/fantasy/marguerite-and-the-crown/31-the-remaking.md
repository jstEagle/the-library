## 31. The Remaking

The Stones did not speak in words anymore. They spoke in waiting.

Marguerite stood at their center with the wind pulling at her hair like hands that could not decide whether to hold her back or push her forward, and she listened the way her mother had taught her to listen — not with ears, but with the hollow place behind the breastbone where all true hearing lived.

*Come,* said the Stones. *Come, last word. Come, unfinished sentence.*

The fire was already lit. It had not been lit by any hand she saw. It rose from a ring of black stone at the heart of the circle, and it burned wrong — or right, depending on how you counted such things — burning green-gold and silver both, burning cold on the skin of the world and hot underneath it, where the old promises slept. The flames made no crackle. They made a sound like a mother humming over a cradle, if the cradle held the whole wild world and the mother had been dead thirty years and was singing anyway.

Marguerite was not afraid.

That was the first lie she caught herself telling, there at the edge of everything, and she set it down gently, the way you set down a wounded bird, because lies die quickly in this place. She was afraid. She was afraid the way rain is afraid of falling — knowing it must, knowing it was made for it, grieving the cloud all the same.

She looked for Felix.

He stood at the circle's edge with Sable Harrow beside him, and the fox between his feet with its brush curled over its paws like a comma in a long sentence, and beyond them the beasts of two realms pressed close — wolves with frost still in their coats from the grey country, sparrows thick as a second darkness, the great stag from the Green Road with its antlers hung in lichen and memory. And farther out, in a ragged half-moon, the hunters Vane had brought, their iron and their ledgers, their faces gone uncertain as the fire's light touched them and showed them, Marguerite thought, what they actually were: small creatures with small ledgers, standing at the hem of something that had been counting before counting had a name.

Felix's mouth was moving. He was saying her name. She could not hear it over the fire's humming, but she could see the shape of it — *Mar-gue-rite* — three soft stones dropped into water, and she loved him for the shape of it, and she would remember the shape of it even if remembering turned out to be the one thing fire could not keep.

*I am ready,* she told the Stones.

*You are not,* said the Stones, with terrible gentleness. *No one is. That is why it must be willing.*

* * *

Her mother had run through the dark with her, once, a lifetime ago — which is to say, four years ago, which is to say, at the beginning of the world.

She remembered it now more clearly than she had ever let herself, because the fire was drawing memories up out of her like roots drawing water, and it seemed cruel to refuse it anything. Her mother's hand around hers, too tight, hurting. The smell of wet ash and pine. Behind them, dogs that were not dogs, and men whose voices had been scraped smooth of everything human. And her mother crouching down in the bracken, breath ragged as torn cloth, pressing her forehead to Marguerite's forehead, and saying — in the first tongue, the whole tongue, the tongue that was less a language than a way of being related to everything —

*Little word, listen. If they take me, do not stop running until the trees stop being afraid. When you find the quiet ones, tell them the covenant is not broken. A thing can only break if it was whole, and we kept it whole, we kept it whole, we kept—*

And then the dogs, and then the screaming that was not her mother's but was worse, and then years of running alone, sleeping in the mouths of hills, speaking to moths because moths asked no questions, forgetting human words one by one like coins slipping through a coat with a torn pocket, until the night she woke under the eaves of the Ledgerhouse and could not remember her own name and had to make one up out of the sound the rain made on the slate.

*Marguerite.* Daisy-child. A flower-word. A word for growing in cracks.

She had thought, all these months, that her mother's last words had been cut off mid-sentence. Now, standing in the firelight, she understood. They had not been cut off. They had been *given* to her. *We kept it whole* — and then silence, and the silence was hers to finish. Every step since had been the rest of the sentence.

*We kept it whole,* Marguerite said aloud, in the first tongue, and the words came out of her sounding the way they always sounded when she spoke it — strange and lovely, like water deciding to be music, like light learning to have an opinion. The beasts nearest her bowed their heads. Even the hunters flinched, though they could not have said why, except that some part of every creature remembers the language it was made in.

*Yes,* said the Stones. *Now the rest.*

* * *

The terms were carved into the inner faces of the monoliths, and she had read them in the days before, tracing the worn glyphs with her fingers while Felix watched with his careful frightened eyes. She knew what was written. She had known since the village of quiet bells, since the dying elder put her spotted hand on Marguerite's head and called her *the last word of the first tongue*, and Marguerite had gone into the dark behind the elder's house and been sick with fear among the bean rows.

*A willing heir shall stand in the fire of the making.*

*She shall be unmade — every thread of her drawn out and held.*

*And remade — not as a child of one realm, but as a voice belonging wholly to neither and therefore to both.*

*What she was, she will not be. What she was, she will carry. Nothing is lost that is given. Everything is lost that is kept.*

Nothing is lost that is given. Everything is lost that is kept.

She turned those words over the way she used to turn river stones, looking for the shine underneath. She thought about the ferryman, and the memory he had taken — her mother laughing in a kitchen that smelled of honey-smoke, flour on her knuckles — and how Marguerite had paid it gladly and then grieved it like a limb. Given, not lost. But oh, it had felt like lost. Oh, she had woken nights reaching for a kitchen that was no longer anywhere.

The fire would take more than a kitchen. The fire would take all of it — the moth-nights and the running years, the taste of stolen apples, Felix's face when she first let him hear her speak, the fox's dry laugh, Sable's ruined voice saying *I heard them too, once*. It would draw every thread out of her and hold the threads up in the light, and then it would weave her again into something that could stand between two worlds without tearing either.

And the girl who stepped out of the fire would know what the girl who stepped in had never known: whether it was worth it. Whether *she* was worth it. Whether the child with the made-up name would look at what replaced her and feel robbed, or freed, or both, forever, in the same heartbeat.

*It hurts,* said the Stones, before she could ask. They said it plainly, without apology, the way the wild says all true things. *Not as your kind hurts. Deeper and kinder at once. Like being spoken for the first time.*

Marguerite looked at Felix one more time.

He had stopped trying to say her name. He was just looking at her, and his face was doing the thing it did when he was hearing too much at once — the rats and the horses and the grief of the world pouring through him — jaw tight, eyes bright, a boy built entirely out of doors he was holding shut. She wanted to tell him: *you can let go of the doors now. I am going to become one.* But the first tongue had no word for joke, exactly; the closest it had was *a truth wearing feathers*, and she suspected he would not laugh.

So instead she lifted her hand, the way her mother had lifted hers in the bracken, and pressed two fingers to her own forehead, and then held them out toward him across the firelit air. *From my thought to yours. From my thought to yours.* An old gesture. A keeping-whole gesture.

Felix's hand came up. His fingers shook. He pressed them to his own forehead and held them out to her, and though no words crossed the space between them, the fox made a small sound that was almost a sob, and somewhere in the dark a wolf began to sing, low and steady, a note to walk on.

Marguerite turned to the fire.

* * *

The heat, when she stepped into it, was not heat.

It was attention.

That was the horror of it, at first — that it was not a thing happening *to* her but a thing *looking at* her, wholly, without mercy or cruelty, the way dawn looks at a landscape. She felt herself opened like a book. She felt every page turned. And here was the night in the coal chute with soot in her lungs and Felix's hand slick with fear around her wrist. Here was the counting room, and the moths coming to her whisper through the keyhole, wings like small gray prayers. Here was her mother's heartbeat heard from inside, before birth, before names, the first drumbeat of the only song. Here was hunger, real hunger, winter hunger, the kind that eats words first because words cost the most to keep. Here was the moment at the ford when she had spoken the old tongue into Sable Harrow's ruined ear and watched a woman remember having a soul.

Thread by thread. Drawn out. Held up.

And here — she had not expected this — here was the Ledgerhouse itself, the smell of ink and tallow, the clerks' pens scratching like beetles in wood, the rats in the walls gossiping of a hollowing. Here was the guild that broke the world, seen from inside her own memory, and she understood suddenly that she could not be remade without carrying even this, even the enemy, even the wound's own teeth. A voice between realms must know both realms' languages, and the guild's language was also a language: fear, arithmetic, the desperate belief that if you can count a thing you can control it.

*Do you give it?* asked the fire, and beneath the fire, the Stones, and beneath the Stones, something older still, something that had been waiting since before kingdoms, patient as root-pressure, vast as weather.

Everything in her screamed to hold on. That was the terror, and she let herself feel all of it, every degree of it, because the terms were clear: *willing*. Not brave. Brave was a costume willingness sometimes wore. Willing was this — standing in the open door of yourself, knowing what walks out may not walk back in, and saying *yes* with your whole weight.

*Nothing is lost that is given,* she said.

*Everything is lost that is kept,* answered the fire.

*I give it.*

*All of it?*

She thought of her name. *Marguerite.* The rain-word she had invented for herself on a cold roof, the flower that grows in cracks. The last coin in the coat with the torn pocket.

*All of it,* said Marguerite, and meant it, and the meaning was the match, and the match was struck.

* * *

Being unmade was like this:

It was like the moment between lightning and thunder stretched out into a country you had to walk across.

It was like every moth she had ever spoken to coming home to roost in her ribs.

It was like her mother's hand finally, finally letting go — and Marguerite discovering, in the white space where the grip had been, that she had been holding herself up all along, that the hand had been holding her *together*, not *up*, and that apart was not the same as fallen.

She was threads in a strong light. She was a sentence taken apart to see how it meant. Somewhere outside the fire she knew — distantly, the way you know weather through a wall — that Felix was on his knees, that the fox was keening, that the wolves had risen to their feet in a ring, that Sable Harrow had drawn her blade not against anyone but laid flat across her palms like an offering, like a woman returning something stolen thirty years ago. She knew the hunters were fleeing, or kneeling, or both. None of it reached her as more than weather. She was being read.

And then — the turn. The loom.

The threads came back.

But not in the old pattern. Never again the old pattern. She felt herself rewoven on a wider warp: wool of wolf-song, silk of moth-wing, wire of bell-metal from the village of quiet bells, green fiber cut fresh from the Green Road, one strand of ordinary human wool that smelled of ink and fear and stubbornness and was, she understood without being told, Felix — a single thread of him woven through the whole of her, because nothing is lost that is given, and he had given himself to this the day he opened the coal chute.

And one strand of gold that hummed of honey-smoke kitchens and flour-dusted knuckles, returned to her from somewhere — from the fire's own stores, from the ferryman's far ledger, from her mother's keeping — placed at the center, at the heart, where a voice keeps its first word.

The fire closed over her like water closing over a stone.

The world went quiet.

* * *

Then the world went loud.

Oh — oh, it went *loud*.

Marguerite came back to herself standing in the dead ring of the fire, unburnt, barefoot on warm stone, and the first thing the new-made ears heard was everything. The sap rising in the birches like slow applause. The worms turning the soil in their blind cities. The hawks riding thermals three miles up, complaining beautifully about the wind. The roots of the Standing Stones themselves, going down and down, drinking from a darkness older than dark, murmuring to each other in geological patience. The hunters' hearts. The hunters' fear, which has a sound, which sounds like small bells rung badly. Felix — Felix's blood, Felix's breath, Felix's heart saying her name over and over in the only language hearts know, which is rhythm.

She staggered. The fox caught her — the fox, pressed warm against her legs, laughing and crying in its dry rusted way. *There you are. There you are, little word. Look at you. You're — hm. You're bigger than you were. Not in the body. In the body you're the same. In the everything else—*

*I hear them,* Marguerite said, and her voice came out changed. Not stranger. Clearer. As if all her life she had been speaking through a wall and the wall had been her. *Fox, I hear all of them. All the time. How does Felix bear it? How did he bear it alone, all those years, in that city of stone?*

*He didn't,* said the fox simply. *That's rather the point of him.*

She raised her head. The fire was out. The Stones were singing — actually singing now, a chord too deep for human ears, and she saw Felix clutching his chest and knew he could feel it even if he could not hear it, the way you feel thunder in your teeth. The grey stain on the world — she could see it now with new eyes, the hollowing as a color, a silence-color — was receding from the forest edge like a tide going out, hesitant, disbelieving, testing each foot of ground before it took it back.

Sable Harrow stood with her seal burnt to slag in her fist, and tears cutting clean lines through the grime of battle, and she was whispering — Marguerite heard it, heard every cracked syllable — *I can almost — it's almost — like a word on the tip of the tongue for thirty years—*

And Felix.

Felix was looking at her the way people look at a horizon after a shipwreck — searching it for land, terrified of finding it, terrified of not finding it. And Marguerite, the voice-between, the last word made whole, opened her mouth to speak to him first in the first tongue, to give him back the greeting her mother gave her in the bracken —

—and stopped.

Because beneath the Stones' great chord, beneath the returning birdsong, beneath the whole rejoicing weave of the mended world, her new ears caught something else. Faint. Far. Northward, past the Grey Receding, past the edge of every map the guild ever kept.

A sound like iron shifting in a frame too small for it.

A sound like a door, very old, very patient, beginning — just beginning — to rattle.

The fox's ears went up. The fox heard it too.

*Well,* said the fox, after a long moment. *Isn't that just like a covenant. Fix one lock, find out how many others there are.*

Marguerite closed her hand around the fox's fur, and looked north, and smiled — because she was made now for doors, and she knew it, and the knowing did not frighten her the way it would have frightened the girl who stepped into the fire.

That girl was gone. What remained was her promise, walking around in the world, listening.

"Come," Marguerite said to Felix, in his language, in his soft round human words, giving them back to him one by one like borrowed coins. "Someone should answer it."
