## 32. What Felix Gave

The fire at the center of the Standing Stones did not go out when Marguerite stepped into it.

That was the first thing Felix understood, standing at the circle's edge with ash in his hair and Sable Harrow's blood on his sleeve: the fire had never been a fire at all. It was a door held open, and doors do not burn what passes through them willingly.

She had gone in singing.

He would carry that sound for the rest of his life — a child's voice lifted in the first tongue, whole and unbroken now, each syllable landing on the stones like rain finding its old riverbed. The language that predated every kingdom had come out of her mouth complete, as though it had been waiting thirty years inside her small body for someone brave enough to speak it aloud. And the stones had answered. He had felt them answer through his boots, through his teeth, through the marrow of him, a groan of granite remembering its purpose after three decades of silence.

Then the light took her, and she was gone, and the fire burned on without fuel, without smoke, without heat — only light, patient as dawn.

Felix did not remember falling to his knees. He found himself there anyway, hands pressed flat to the frozen ground, and he became aware, distantly, that he was weeping in front of several hundred people, a guild of hunters, an ex-enforcer with a burned guild seal still smoking in her fist, and every beast within fifty miles.

He could not bring himself to care.

* * *

The silence afterward lasted a long time. Or perhaps no time at all; Felix had lost his grip on the difference. The light swelled until it filled the circle, spilled over the stones like water over a weir, and ran outward across the moor in slow luminous rivers. Where it touched, things changed. He watched frost uncurl from dead bracken and green push up through it. He watched a rowan tree, grey and leafless since before he was born, shudder and burst into berry-red so sudden and violent it looked like a wound. Somewhere behind him, Sable made a sound that might have been a prayer in a religion neither of them knew.

And then, out of the heart of the fire, a voice.

Not Marguerite's voice. Not any voice. It came from everywhere — from the stones, from the grass, from the bell of the sky itself — and it spoke in the first tongue, and Felix understood it the way he understood the rats in the walls of the Ledgerhouse: not by translation but by some older door in him swinging wide.

*THE COVENANT IS SPOKEN. THE COVENANT IS HEARD. BUT A HEARD WORD MUST HAVE A KEEPER.*

The light turned toward him. He felt it the way one feels being looked at across a crowded room — a pressure, a warmth, an unbearable attention.

*YOU WHO LISTEN. YOU WHO KEPT HER WHEN NO ONE WOULD. THE BOND DEMANDS A SPEAKER.*

"I don't—" His voice cracked. He tried again. "I'm not anything. I hear rats. I hear horses. I was never supposed to—"

*YOU WERE ALWAYS SUPPOSED TO.* The voice was not unkind. That was somehow worse. *YOUR GRANDMOTHER'S MOTHER'S MOTHER STOOD WHERE YOU STAND AND GAVE THE SAME ANSWER YOURS TO GIVE. IT IS NOT TAKEN, LITTLE LISTENER. IT IS ONLY EVER GIVEN.*

Felix looked around the circle. Sable stood ten feet away, rigid, her scarred throat working — he realized she couldn't hear any of it, that the voice passed over her like wind over stone, and the look on her face was hunger and grief in equal measure. The fox sat at the circle's edge, singed and limping and grinning its terrible grin, and it nodded once, slowly, the way you nod at someone about to jump into deep water.

Marguerite was somewhere inside that light. He could feel her — not hear her, not yet, but feel her the way you feel the sun through a closed window. She was not afraid. That undid him more than anything else. She had walked into the fire knowing she might be unmade entirely, and she was not afraid, and here he was, kneeling in the frost, terrified of a question.

But that was the whole of his life, wasn't it? Thirty years of listening through walls. Of hearing everything and saying nothing. Of keeping his gift folded up small and hidden like contraband, spending it on gossip and warnings, hoarding it, apologizing for it, lying to himself that it was nothing, that he was nothing, that a man who heard the world could live safely apart from it.

The covenant demanded a speaker's bond. And Felix Crane, who had spent his whole life silent, understood at last that this was the payment he had been saving toward without ever knowing it.

"Yes," he said.

The word came out small. He said it again, louder, standing, ash falling from his shoulders.

"Yes. I give it. Freely." He laughed, and the laugh came out wet and strange. "God help me, I've been giving it my whole life. I just never told anyone."

* * *

It did not hurt.

He had braced for pain — for fire, for the sensation of being opened like a ledger — but what came instead was simply *more*. The gift he had always known, the low murmur of beasts, the gossip of rats, the dream-talk of horses, all of it was still there, and then beneath it another layer opened, and beneath that another, and another, like a well whose bottom kept dropping away.

He heard the worms turning the soil under the moor, thousands upon thousands of them, a slow blind industry like distant surf. He heard the roots of the rowan drinking, a sound like a long satisfied sigh drawn through silver. He heard the lichen growing on the north faces of the stones, which was less a sound than a patience, a green thought repeated so slowly it took a year to finish a sentence. He heard the wind's conversation with the grass, and the grass's answer, and the argument they'd been having, apparently, since before the first kingdom rose. He heard the moor itself — not the things on it but the thing it *was* — a vast drowsy consciousness turning over in its sleep, murmuring place-names in a language older than names.

It was too much.

It was far, far too much.

Felix put his hands over his ears and it did nothing, because none of it was coming through his ears. It was coming through the part of him that had always listened, the hidden room behind his ribs, and that room had just had every wall knocked out at once. He went down again, curled on his side in the frost, gasping, drowning in a sea of voices that were not voices, and somewhere very far away he heard Sable shouting his name and the fox saying, in its dry rust-bucket rasp, *"Don't fight it, lad. You can't swim against the whole sea. Float."*

Float.

He stopped fighting. He let it pour through him — the worm-song and the root-drink and the lichen's year-long sentence and the wind's ancient argument and underneath, underneath everything, a new sound, a sound he had never heard before and knew instantly he would never stop hearing:

The wild realm itself. Breathing.

It breathed where the hollowing had been, and the breath was ragged, wounded, mending — a great grey animal waking from a thirty-year fever, flexing feeling back into limbs gone numb. And threaded through that breathing, small and bright and unmistakable, a voice he knew.

*"Felix?"*

Not spoken. Felt. Carried on the wild's breath like a scent.

*"Felix, I can hear you thinking. Your thoughts are very loud. They're mostly swearing."*

He laughed — sobbed — lay in the frost with tears freezing in his eyebrows and laughed until his ribs ached, because Marguerite was alive, Marguerite was *everywhere*, she was in the stones and the grass and the recovering dark of the forest miles south, and she was teasing him, and he could hear her, and he could hear *everything*, and it was going to kill him, and it was the most beautiful thing that had ever happened to anyone.

"Is it always like this?" he managed aloud, though he didn't need to speak and knew it.

*"Yes,"* said the wild realm, in Marguerite's voice and a thousand others. *"You get used to it. You don't. Both are true. Welcome to the bond, little listener."*

* * *

They gave him the night to learn how to close the doors.

That was the lesson, it turned out — not to shut the gift away, as he'd done all his life, but to close it gently, the way you close a door on a cold morning to keep the warmth in rather than to keep the world out. The fox taught him, or tried to, pacing beside him as he staggered around the perimeter of the Stones like a drunk man learning to walk.

"Think of it as rooms," the fox said. It had introduced itself, finally, with a name that was less a name than a smell — woodsmoke and stolen eggs — but it answered to *Fox* readily enough, with the air of one granting a great favor. "You've lived your whole life in one room with the door bolted. Now you own the house. You don't have to stand in every room at once. Visit. Listen. Leave."

"And if I want quiet?"

The fox looked at him with its amber eyes, and for a moment the comic mask slipped and something old and kind looked out. "Then you come to me," it said, "and I'll teach you the trick my grandmother used. But quiet is a room too, lad. Don't make it the only one you live in. I've seen what that does. It does what it did to the Ledgermaster."

Felix flinched. Vane's end was still bright behind his eyes — the man clutching his ledger as the Stones unmade him, ink and ash scattering on a wind that had finally, after thirty years, been paid. He did not want to think about it. The wild realm, obligingly, carried the memory away downstream, and he understood dimly that this too was part of the bond: the wild did not hold grief the way men did. It composted it.

By full dark he could keep three doors open at once — the near beasts, the land itself, and Marguerite, who was no longer precisely anywhere and therefore was everywhere, and who kept sending him impressions like a child poking a new sibling: wonder, vastness, the taste of sunlight on stone, and once, unexpectedly, a memory of her mother's hands braiding her hair, offered shyly, like a gift.

He sent back one of his own: his grandmother's kitchen, steam on the windows, her voice saying *the wrens are arguing about the roof again, mind you don't tell them I listen.*

Marguerite's laughter rolled across the moor like weather.

* * *

Near midnight, Sable found him sitting against the eastern stone, watching the frost give way to a thin sweet mist that smelled of growing things.

"You can hear me coming," she said. It wasn't a question.

"I could always hear you coming," Felix said. "Your heartbeat's different from other people's. Slower. Like a drum that decided not to hurry for anybody." He paused. "I never told anyone that either. There are a lot of things I never told anyone."

Sable lowered herself to the ground beside him with the careful economy of a woman whose body was a map of old injuries. For a while she said nothing. The mist thickened. Somewhere down the slope, the rowan tree dropped a berry, and Felix heard it land from forty feet away, a soft red syllable in the dark.

"The voice," she said at last. Her hand drifted up, unconsciously, to the pale scar at her throat — the mark the guild's surgeons had left when they cut the hearing out of her, twenty years ago. "When it spoke. I couldn't hear the words. But my scar—" Her jaw worked. "My scar *ached*. Like a tooth remembering it used to be a tooth."

Felix considered lying. It was his old craft, his survival craft, and it rose in him smooth and ready as ever. Then he thought of the river, and the memory he'd bent to spare Marguerite, and the long terrible work of the confession at the fire, and he set the lie down like a heavy bag he no longer had to carry.

"It wasn't aching," he said gently. "It was reaching. Whatever they cut, they didn't cut all of it. You heard the shape of the words through what's left." He hesitated, then opened a door he hadn't dared open before — carefully, politely, the way one knocks. Through the bond he reached toward her, not into her, never into her, just near, the way you hold out a hand to a wild thing.

Sable went rigid. Her breath caught.

"What did you *do*?"

"Nothing. I just... let you feel the edge of it. What it's like on this side of the wall." He withdrew, slowly. "There are others like you, aren't there? Others they cut. The guild wouldn't have invented the procedure for one girl."

"No," Sable said quietly. "There were others." She stared out into the mist, and Felix watched twenty years of discipline fight twenty years of grief and lose, just barely, just around the eyes. "I'll find them," she said. "After. When the city's sorted. Someone should tell them it wasn't their imagination. Someone should tell them it can grow back."

"Can it?"

"I don't know." And then, with the ghost of the smile that had terrified recruits and would now, Felix suspected, terrify entirely different people: "I intend to find out."

* * *

Dawn came up gold.

Felix had seen ten thousand dawns from garrets and cellars and the high grim windows of the Ledgerhouse, and he had never once heard one. Now he lay on the cold stone with his coat buttoned to his chin and listened to the sun arrive: the frost releasing its grip in a chorus of tiny clicks like distant applause; the moor birds waking in a wave that rolled toward him from the horizon, each species joining the song in its appointed order, thrush after lark after wren, a choir that had been waiting thirty years for permission; and beneath it all the deep green hum of the land drinking light, photosynthesis rendered audible, a sound like a billion small bells struck in honey.

Overwhelming. Still overwhelming. He suspected it would be overwhelming for the rest of his life, and he suspected, too, that he would never once wish it otherwise.

Marguerite arrived with the light.

He didn't see her come — she was simply *there*, sitting cross-legged beside him, and she was changed and she was not changed. She was still small. Still sharp-faced, still feral-eyed, still wearing the ruined boy's clothes Felix had stolen for her a lifetime ago in the counting room. But her eyes had depth now, the way wells have depth, and when she looked at him he felt looked at by the forest and the moor and the standing stones and something older than all of them, wearing a child's face because a child's face was what it had.

"You're staring," she said.

"You're staring back."

"I'm allowed. I'm the crown of the wild realm now." She said it lightly, but her fingers were plucking at the frost-killed grass, tearing it into smaller and smaller pieces, and Felix recognized anxiety when he saw it even through a bond. "Felix. When I was in the fire. I saw the terms. All of them. There were clauses at the bottom, in the oldest script, and one of them—" She stopped tearing the grass. "One of them wasn't about me. It was about a door."

The word landed in Felix's chest like a stone in still water.

"A door," he repeated.

"In the north. Sealed by the first covenant. The script said the seal was *woven into* the covenant — that renewing the covenant renewed the seal, but also..." She looked up at him, and the well-depths of her eyes were troubled. "Also woke it. Like turning a key in a lock you forgot you owned. Something felt it turn, Felix. Something on the other side. It's been asleep a long time and it isn't anymore."

The dawn chorus went on around them, golden and oblivious. Felix lay still, listening past it — past the birds, past the worms, past the moor's drowsy turning — straining his new senses northward, toward the cold spine of the world.

And there, at the very edge of what he could hear, faint as a heartbeat through a fortress wall, he heard it.

A rattling.
