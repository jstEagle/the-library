## 36. Marguerite's Name

The house they gave her had a window that opened.

Marguerite discovered this on her third morning in the rebuilt Ledgerhouse, when the frost on the glass had melted into long silver runnels and she pushed at the frame the way she had pushed at everything in this city of stone and rules — half expecting it to refuse her. But the sash lifted, groaning on its cord like an old man rising from a chair, and the cold came in carrying the smell of wet slate and river and, far beneath all of it, the green breath of the wild road still threaded through her hair.

She put her head out into the morning.

Below, the courtyard was busy in the way Felix said courtyards were supposed to be busy: clerks with ink-stained fingers carrying ledgers instead of hiding them, a cart of winter apples, two stable boys arguing over a harness buckle while the horses watched them with the patient amusement of creatures who could now be *heard* if they chose to speak and mostly chose not to, because people were funnier when they didn't know they were being watched. Somewhere a bell rang — not the solstice bell, just a small bell, a door-bell or a tea-bell — and Marguerite flinched anyway, then made herself stay in the window until her shoulders came down from around her ears.

Bells meant bells again now. Not silence wearing a bell's shape.

She breathed the cold until it hurt a little, which was how she liked her cold, honest and clean, and then she sat back on the sill and did the thing she had been doing every morning for eleven days, the thing Felix called *practicing* and she called something else in the first tongue, a word that meant roughly *sharpening my teeth on the world*.

She said her name.

"Marguerite."

It came out of her mouth in the human way, round and soft, four small stones dropped into a well. She knew what it meant here — Felix had shown her the flower pressed flat between two pages of one of the old ledgers, a daisy, white-petaled, common as dirt and stubborn as spring. *Marguerite*, the flower that grew in cracks. That opened its face to the sun without asking anyone's permission.

But that was only what it meant in their tongue. In hers — in the first tongue, the one that lived under all other tongues the way roots live under grass — names were not flowers. Names were debts. Names were doors. Her mother had given her the human word as a cloak to wear over the true thing, the way you wrapped a coal in ash so it would keep through the night.

Her mother had never spoken the true thing aloud where walls might hear.

Marguerite looked at her hands, turned them over, looked at the fine new scars the covenant fire had left on both palms — thin silver lines like the veins of a leaf, like the channels water cuts in stone when it is very patient. The fire had taken her apart down to the last small bone of her and put her back together, and everyone kept asking her, gently, carefully, the way you ask someone who has been ill: *are you all right? Are you still yourself?*

And she kept answering yes, because it was mostly true, and because the fuller answer required words no human mouth had room for.

The truth was that she was still herself the way a river is itself after a flood. The same water. A different country.

* * *

Felix found her at midmorning, the way he always found her, which was to say he didn't look for her at all — he simply walked to wherever she was, drawn by whatever quiet thread the renewed covenant had strung between the two of them. She felt him coming before the stairs creaked: a warmth in the left side of her chest, where the fire had set its deepest mark. He carried two cups of something steaming and a plate of bread with honey, because Felix expressed affection almost exclusively through food, having spent a lifetime being careful with every other kind.

"You're up early," he said, though the sun was well up, though she had watched him sleep past dawn more mornings than she could count. He said things like that the way he did everything — softly, wrongly, kindly.

"The sparrows woke me." She took the honey bread. "They are gossiping."

"About what?"

"About you." She let him suffer for exactly three seconds, watching his ears go pink, before she relented. "About the crumbs you leave on the kitchen sill. They have decided you are a reliable man. This is the highest thing sparrows say about anyone."

"I'll try to deserve it." He sat across from her on the window seat, folding himself down with the careful economy of a man who had learned to take up as little space as possible and was only now, slowly, unlearning it. Outside, the courtyard went on with its honest noise. Inside, the fire ticked in the grate, and the house breathed around them the way old houses do, settling its bones.

"Felix." She said his name deliberately, the way she'd been practicing, and saw him notice — he noticed everything, it was his whole tragedy and his whole gift. "I want to talk about names."

His cup paused halfway to his mouth. "All right."

"In your tongue, I am Marguerite. The flower in the crack." She turned her palms up, showed him the silver lines. "In mine—" She stopped. Started again, in the first tongue, and the words came out strange and layered, each one carrying three meanings the way a river carries silt and light and fish all at once: *the-one-who-stands-between-and-does-not-break*. Then she said, in his language, slowly, tasting each word: "In mine, a name is not what you are called. It is what you promise. My mother gave me the flower-name to hide me. The fire gave me the true one to use me. And now—" she frowned, hunting through his vocabulary for the shape of the thought, "—now I am supposed to choose which one I am. And I find I do not want either one alone."

Felix set his cup down. He had gotten better at listening with his whole body since the Stones — before, he had listened with his gift, hearing beasts through walls and floors, but people had slid off him like rain off oiled cloth. Now he listened to her the way he used to listen to rats in wainscoting: completely, hungrily, as if the smallest word might be the one that mattered.

"Tell me," he said.

So she told him.

* * *

She told him about the moment inside the fire, or as much of it as could be carried in human words, which was like carrying the ocean in a hat. She told him that when the covenant unmade her, it had asked her a question — not in words, but the way winter asks a tree whether it intends to keep living. And the question had been: *what will you be called by?*

Not *what are you called*. What will you be called **by**. By whom. By what voices. Because a name, in the first tongue, was not a possession. It was a rope thrown across a gap, and you had to choose who held the other end.

Her mother had held one end. The wild had held the other. And her mother had died holding on, running through the dark with a child under one arm and a dying language under the other, and Marguerite had spent the whole flight — she remembered it now, remembered it fully, the fire had given her back even the terrible parts — believing that when her mother fell silent forever, the rope would fall with her.

"But it did not," she said. "Because you caught it. You did not even see it, Felix. You caught it with your terrible human ears and your kindness, and you held on, and you never once let go, even when the guild hunted us, even when I was cruel to you, even at the river when—" She stopped. The river was still a tender place. It would be tender for a while yet. "Even then."

Felix's eyes had gone bright. He looked down at his hands, at the faint marks the speaker's bond had left on him too, and said nothing, because he was Felix, and when he was most moved he went quietest.

"So," Marguerite said, and stood, because this next part needed her standing, needed her spine straight and her chin up the way her mother's chin had gone up in the last memory she had of her, turning once to look back before the dark took her. "I have decided what my name means now. I am telling you first, because you are my—" she searched, rejected *friend* as too small, rejected *brother* as wrong-shaped, and settled, with a small fierce smile, on the word she'd heard the fox use, dripping with irony, meaning it herself without any irony at all, "—my pack. And because you are the witness. That is your name in the covenant, did you know? The Stones wrote it. *Witness.* You thought you were just following me around being worried. You were being named the whole time."

"I—" Felix cleared his throat. "Go on."

"My name means this:" She spoke it first in the first tongue, and the room changed — the fire leaned toward her, the frost-runnels on the window gleamed, somewhere far below in the cellar the rats went still and attentive, and outside the sparrows on the sill cocked their heads as one. Then she gave it to him in his own words, one at a time, laying them down like stones across a stream:

"*I am the word that was almost lost, and I am keeping myself.*" 

She let it sit in the air.

"Not the flower in the crack — though I am that too, I like that, flowers in cracks are harder to kill than garden flowers and everyone knows it. And not only the voice-between-realms, though I am that also, and it is heavier than anyone guessed, heavier than *I* guessed, some days I feel both worlds leaning on me like a door leaning on its hinges. But underneath, underneath, the part I choose: I am the one who keeps. My mother ran so the word would survive. I stand so it will be *spoken*. That is my name now. Whoever calls me by it is asking me to keep, and I am answering that I will."

The fire cracked. The sparrows chirruped something that Felix, with his changed hearing, probably caught the whole of and she only caught the edge of — something approving, something that sounded suspiciously like applause.

Felix was looking at her with an expression she had learned to read over these months: wonder fighting terror and losing, gracefully. "That's—" He stopped. Tried again. "When Vane offered to give me a place in the ledger, a lawful name, a line in the book — I refused because it wasn't mine, it was his version of me written in his hand. What you just did is the opposite. You wrote yourself." His voice did the rough thing it did sometimes. "I think it might be the bravest thing I've seen. And I've seen you walk into fire."

"The fire was easier," she said. "The fire did not ask me to be specific."

He laughed — really laughed, the rusty gate of it swinging open — and she counted it as a victory and sat back down and stole the rest of his honey bread, because witnesses should not get attached to their breakfasts.

* * *

They stayed in the window seat while the morning aged. Below, Sable Harrow crossed the courtyard with a traveling pack on her shoulder — heading out again, north first and then who knew where, hunting for others like her, others the guild had cut. She glanced up at their window as she passed, and Marguerite raised a hand, and Sable — Sable, who had burned her seal and stood at the Stones with iron in one hand and mercy in the other — raised hers back, brief and hard, and was gone through the gate.

"She'll come back," Felix said quietly.

"I know. The cats tell me things. Cats know about returning." Marguerite pulled her knees up. For a while she watched the light move on the floorboards, gold sliding inch by inch, and felt the strangeness that had lived in her since the fire settle into something almost like peace: the sense of being *placed*, the way a keystone is placed, held by everything it holds up.

Then she said, "Now you must teach me."

"Teach you?"

"Human words. The way I taught you old ones." She turned to face him fully. "You think I have not noticed. Every night since we came back, you make lists. Little papers. Words with their meanings. *Threshold. Hearth. Promise. Home.* You hide them under your blotter like a boy hiding love letters." His ears went pink again, confirming everything. "I want them. All of them. If I am to be the voice between realms, I cannot speak to your world in borrowed scraps. Your tongue has words mine does not — words for *yesterday* and *maybe* and *forgive*. Mine has words yours does not — words for the smell of rain arriving, for the debt a den owes the forest. A voice between worlds needs both. So we will trade. One of yours for one of mine, every day, until we are both rich."

Felix was quiet a moment. When he spoke, there was something wondering in it. "My grandmother used to say almost the same thing. About trading words. I thought it was just a saying." He shook his head slowly. "She had so many sayings. Bird stories, weather rhymes, little blessings for the doorstep. I memorized them as a child and forgot them as a man, and now—" He stopped, and Marguerite watched something move behind his face, something old and unfinished, a door he kept almost opening.

"Now they mean differently," she finished for him. "Words do that. That is their whole trick."

"Yes." He looked out the window, at the grey-gold sky, and his voice went somewhere else, somewhere further away than the courtyard. "There's a graveyard on the hill above the city. She's buried there. I haven't been since—" He rubbed his thumb along the scar-lines on his palm, unconscious, the way she touched her own. "Since before any of this. I keep thinking I should go. I keep finding reasons it can wait."

Marguerite studied him — her pack, her witness, her first friend in the world of walls — and understood suddenly, with the certainty the fire had left in her, that this was not a small thing he was circling. That the bird stories and the grave and the guilt and the gratitude were all one knot, and knots like that did not loosen on their own.

"Then we will go," she said simply. "Tomorrow, if the weather allows. Today if it doesn't." She smiled, showing teeth, the feral girl's smile softened by everything that had happened since. "You taught me that human people bring flowers to graves. Bring daisies, Felix. Bring marguerites. Let her meet my name."

Felix looked at her for a long moment, and then nodded, once, the nod of a man agreeing to something larger than a walk.

Outside, far to the north, beyond the hills and the receding grey, something old shifted in its sleep — but that was a story for another day, and today the light was gold, and the word-trade had begun, and Marguerite, who was the word that was almost lost, sat in her window with her pack beside her and began, patiently, joyfully, to learn the names of everything.
