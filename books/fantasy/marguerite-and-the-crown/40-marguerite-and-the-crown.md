## 40. Marguerite and the Crown

### Marguerite

The wild realm came to her in the hour before dawn, the way important things always did — quietly, without knocking.

She woke on the hillside above the Standing Stones with frost in her hair and a weight against her chest that had not been there when she slept. She sat up. The weight was a circlet of woven things: birch twigs still green, a raven's feather gone silver at the edge, a thread of river-light that moved when she breathed, and one small grey stone from the hollowed forest, now veined through with living moss.

*It is not finished,* said the fox, who lay curled at her feet with his tail over his nose like a gentleman pretending not to have been watching her sleep. *They argued about it all night. The oaks wanted gold. The rivers wanted water. The moths wanted — well. Moths want everything to be softer.*

"Who made it?" Marguerite asked, though she already knew.

*Everyone,* the fox said. *That is rather the point.*

Below them, the Stones stood in their circle, no longer grey and dumb but humming with a low green light, like embers remembered rather than seen. A year since the fire. A year since she had stepped into it and been unmade and remade, and learned what it was to be a voice between realms — to speak and be heard by wolf and clerk alike, to carry two languages in one mouth and feel neither of them lie.

A year since Felix had stood beside her and given his gift away as if it were nothing, and been given back something so vast he sometimes went quiet for hours just listening to the world breathe.

The crown was warm against her skin. It did not feel like a crown. It felt like a promise that had been waiting a long time for someone to hold it.

"They will ask me today," she said.

*They asked you yesterday. And the day before.* The fox opened one amber eye. *You are very good at making kings wait. It is one of your finest qualities.*

Marguerite looked north, past the circle of stones, past the greening forests, to where the horizon gathered itself into cold blue mountains. Somewhere beyond them, under snow and silence, there was a door. The rats had told Felix of it in the first weeks of peace, in their small gossiping voices: *a door in the far north that the old covenant sealed, and it has begun to rattle.*

She had heard it too, some nights, in the space between one language and the other. Not a sound exactly. More like a word someone was trying very hard not to say.

"Fox," she said. "When I take the crown — what am I?"

He sat up then, and shook himself, and his grin was the old crooked one, the one that had first told her *the Stones demand payment* and later *I owe your friend my life and I intend to pay it in style.*

*That,* he said, *depends entirely on what you decide to be. Crowns are only questions, little queen. The answering is yours.*

### Felix

Felix had never been comfortable at ceremonies, which was unfortunate, because the last year of his life had consisted almost entirely of them.

He stood at the edge of the stone circle in his good coat — the one Sable had bullied him into buying, dark green because "you look like a drowned clerk in black" — and watched the wild realm assemble. Wolves came down from the high passes in their winter coats, sitting in neat rows like merchants at a guild meeting, which delighted him every single time. The river arrived as a woman made of moving water who insisted on being announced properly. Three hundred moths hung in the air above the eastern arch in a shimmering banner that spelled out, when the light hit correctly, nothing at all, because moths had never mastered letters but refused on principle to be left out.

And people. That was the miracle he still hadn't gotten used to. People. Farmers from the village of Quiet Bells, whose bells rang loud again and who had come walking for six days to see this. Clerks from the new Ledgerhouse, wearing sashes of undyed wool instead of guild colors, carrying blank ledgers — blank, because the first page of any honest ledger should start empty. Children, dozens of them, who had begun in the spring to report hearing things in the walls and hedges and hedgerows, and whose parents no longer called it sickness but sent them to Felix on market days to learn how to listen without flinching.

His gift, given back changed, meant he heard all of it at once: the wolves' low contentment, the river's bright impatience, the children's crackling curiosity, the deep slow satisfaction of oaks who had waited three hundred years for this morning. It was overwhelming. It had been overwhelming for a year. He had stopped minding.

"You're doing the thing," Sable said beside him. She stood with her arms crossed, her burned-out guild seal hanging now on a cord around her neck like a warning to anyone foolish enough to wear theirs. "Where you listen to everything and forget to blink."

"I'm witnessing," Felix said. "It's an official capacity."

"It's a Tuesday." But she smiled when she said it, the way she only smiled now, sideways, like someone relearning the motion.

The crowd hushed. The light came up over the hills, green-gold, and Marguerite came down through the grass with the crown against her chest and the fox trotting at her heel like a herald who had appointed himself.

Felix's chest did the thing it always did. A year, and it still did the thing.

She had grown. Not taller, exactly — though yes, that too, her sleeves scandalously short now, and wasn't that a battle they'd had with the village seamstress — but *filled out*, the way a house fills when someone finally lives in it. Her eyes held both realms evenly. When she looked at you, you felt listened to by something older than listening.

She stopped before the central Stone, where the covenant's terms glowed in the first tongue, whole again, unbroken. She turned to face the assembled world — wolf and clerk, river and child — and Felix saw her take one breath to steady herself, the same breath she'd taken before stepping into the fire, and he understood suddenly that she was afraid, and that she was going to do it anyway, and that this was the whole of courage and always had been.

"I am not a queen," Marguerite said, in the old tongue first, and then in the human tongue, so that everyone heard it twice and it was true both times. "A queen rules. I do not rule the wild. The wild does not need ruling. It needs *keeping*."

She lifted the crown.

"So I will not wear this as a ruler wears one. I will wear it as a keeper wears a promise. I am Marguerite, last daughter of the Old Covenant, first voice of the New. I will hear both realms. I will speak both truths. I will keep the door in the north, and every door after it, and I will not let the stones go quiet again — not while I stand, and not while those who stand with me remember."

She set the crown on her own head. No hand lowered it upon her; no one crowned her but herself. The birch twigs took root against her hair — Felix saw it happen, saw the tiny leaves unfurl — and the river-light pulsed once, like a heartbeat, and the grey stone from the hollowed forest bloomed moss all the way through.

The wild realm made a sound Felix had no word for and would spend the rest of his life failing to translate. The people made a sound he knew perfectly, because he joined it.

Beside him, Sable pressed her knuckles briefly to her eyes. "Dust," she said. "Don't."

"Dust," Felix agreed solemnly.

Then Marguerite turned, found him in the crowd as easily as if the whole world were empty except for the two of them, and beckoned.

"Witness," she said. "Get up here. You're late."

### Marguerite

He came and stood at her left side, where he had stood at the Stones, at the ford, at the fire. The right side belonged to whoever came next; she had decided long ago that the right side would stay open until she understood what it was for. Some thrones kept seats empty for war. Hers would keep one empty for whatever came through the northern door.

"Felix Crane," she said formally, for the crowd, in both tongues. "Speaker. Liar-turned-truth-teller. The man who fed a prisoner girl bread through a counting-room window when it could have cost him everything."

"It cost me a very good hiding place," Felix murmured.

"The man who heard us when no one else could." She raised her voice. "The covenant requires a witness. Not a servant. Not a guard. A witness — one who sees what is done and says it aloud, even when saying it costs. I choose him. Does anyone object?"

Three hundred moths descended on Felix's head in a glittering cloud, which the moths intended as the highest honor and which made him look, Marguerite thought fondly, like a man being attacked by a very festive hat.

"No objections," she ruled.

"Thrilling," said Felix, waving moths away. "Truly, the honors of the wild realm are lavish."

But when the laughter settled, he looked at her, and his eyes were wet, and he didn't pretend otherwise, because that was the other thing the year had taught him — that pretending was a currency he'd stopped carrying.

"I'll witness," he said quietly. "Every page. Every promise. Even the ones that cost."

"*Especially* those," Marguerite said. "Those are the only ones worth writing down."

### Felix

Afterward — after the feasting, after the wolves taught the village children a game involving sticks and dignity-destroying trips, after the river-woman got spectacularly drunk on rainwater and had to be poured back into her banks — Felix climbed the hill behind the Stones alone, because there was one conversation he'd been putting off.

The grandmother's grave was not here; it was three hundred miles east, in the churchyard where he'd read her bird stories aloud last spring and finally understood them. But he'd brought a handful of earth from it, wrapped in cloth, and he'd promised himself he'd carry it to every threshold the covenant renewed, so that she could be present at each one. She had kept the covenant in her kitchen, with stories, alone, for forty years. She had earned the view.

He tipped the earth out at the hilltop, into the wind coming down from the north.

"Heard anything?" he asked the wind, because he was a sentimental fool and had stopped apologizing for it.

The wind carried many things — it always did now, everything did, the whole world talked if you had ears for it — but mostly it carried the smell of snow, and beneath the snow, faint and far, that same almost-sound. The word the north was trying not to say.

The door was rattling. It rattled a little more each month. The beasts spoke of it in the way beasts spoke of weather coming: not with fear, exactly, but with the seriousness of creatures who had survived winters before.

Footsteps behind him. Marguerite, crown and all, with two cups of cider and the expression she wore when she'd been looking for him specifically.

"You hear it too," she said. Not a question.

"Every day now." He took the cider. "The rats say the seals on it were old when the Old Covenant was young. Whatever's behind it isn't part of either realm. Or it's part of both. The rats disagree. The rats disagree about everything; it's why I love them."

"The fox wants to come north. He says he has a professional interest in doors."

"The fox has a professional interest in anything that might owe him money."

Marguerite laughed, and the sound went out over the valley, and somewhere below a wolf answered it for the sheer joy of answering. She sat down beside him in the grass, crown slightly crooked, birch leaves catching the last of the light, and for a while neither of them said anything, because they had learned — both of them, slowly, painfully, wonderfully — that silence shared was different from silence hidden.

"We'll go in spring," she said at last. "When the passes open. You and me and the fox and anyone else brave enough or foolish enough — we'll find the door, and we'll listen to it, and we'll decide what it needs. Together. That's the whole lesson, isn't it? Everything I know, it comes back to that."

"Together," Felix agreed.

He looked out at the valley — at the Stones glowing softly, at the fires of the feast, at Sable teaching a farm boy to stand properly while holding a practice sword, at the Ledgerhouse clerks dancing badly with wolves, at the whole improbable, impossible, honest world they had built out of the wreckage of a broken promise — and he thought about the boy he had been, balancing impossible accounts in a cellar, listening to rats because the rats were the only ones who talked to him, telling himself that concealment was safety and safety was enough.

That boy had been wrong about nearly everything. But he had kept listening. And listening, it turned out, had been enough to save the world.

Not bad, Felix thought. Not bad at all.

"Spring," Marguerite said again, and held out her cup, and he touched his to it.

Far to the north, under the snow, under the silence, something ancient shifted against a door that had been shut for a thousand years — and paused, as if listening.

And then, very softly, it knocked.

* * *

*The end — for now.*
