## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The queen's soup arrived at the tasting table at half past four, as it always did, in a lidded silver tureen carried by a boy named Pimm whose hands shook so badly he might have been born in an earthquake. Dominic Sterling watched the tureen come toward him the way other men watched weather: with attention, without alarm.

"Careful," he said mildly, and steadied the lid with two fingers. "You'll slop it on the floor and then we'll both be explaining to Cook why her saffron is decorating the flagstones."

"Yes, Master Sterling. Sorry, Master Sterling."

Pimm was seventeen and terrified of everything: the kitchen, the palace, the Wardens who patrolled the corridors with their ash-grey cloaks, and most of all, inexplicably, of Dominic himself, which suited Dominic fine. Fear kept people from looking too closely. A man nobody feared got noticed; a man everybody vaguely feared got left alone.

He lifted the lid. Steam rose—chicken broth, saffron, leeks cut into coins, a whisper of thyme. Cook had been generous with the saffron today because the queen had slept badly, and Cook believed, with the unshakable faith of a woman who had fed three generations of royalty, that saffron could mend anything short of treason.

Dominic took up the tasting spoon.

This was the part outsiders never understood about his work. They imagined it was all drama—poisons, antidotes, dramatic collapses. In truth, twelve years of tasting had taught him that the great majority of danger came not from malice but from accident. A cook who stored mushrooms near the wrong herbs. A wine cask rinsed with water from a bad well. A kitchen boy with dirty hands and a head cold, sneezing where he shouldn't. The taster's tongue was less a weapon than a lantern, held up to every small darkness before it reached the royal table.

He tasted. Broth first—rich, clean, no bitterness at the back edge where arsenic liked to hide. Then a spoonful of the solids, chewed slowly, letting each flavor report for inspection like a soldier at muster. Saffron, honest. Leek, sweet. Thyme—

Thyme, and underneath the thyme, something else. Faint. A ghost of a note he knew the way you know a voice calling your name across a crowded market.

*Nettle-root and iron-shavings.* The base of a warding draught. Harmless to anyone eating it—more than harmless, actually. Protective. It would sit in the queen's blood for a day and turn aside any hex aimed at her sleep, her heart, her dreams.

Which was fortunate, because Dominic had put it there.

His right hand still remembered the motion: the little twist of the wrist as he'd lifted the lid, the charm—a dried knot of nettle-root wrapped in waxed thread, no bigger than a thumbnail—dropped into the broth while the steam obscured all. Twelve years of practice had made it smooth as breathing. Smoother, because breathing you could hear.

He set down the spoon, waited the required interval—the palace insisted on a full quarter hour between tasting and serving, though everyone pretended this was tradition rather than fear—and pronounced the soup fit.

"Tell them it's good today," he added. "Tell them the taster said so."

Pimm scurried off with the tureen, visibly relieved to have survived another encounter with the mysterious Master Sterling, and Dominic remained alone at the tasting table with his ledger, in which he recorded, in a small careful hand: *Queen's table, evening meal. Broth, saffron. No fault found.*

It was true. He had found no fault. He had, in fact, *added* one, if you counted protection a fault, which the Crown very much did.

Under the Edict of Ash, signed into law on the worst night of Dominic's life, the making of charms was punishable by the loss of the offending hand. The working of greater magic by fire. He had seen the fires. Everyone had seen the fires; they'd burned for eleven days along the harbor road, and the smoke had gotten into everything, into the bread, into the rain, into the backs of throats so that the whole city coughed for a month and children born that autumn were said to have grey eyes.

Dominic had lost things in those fires that did not show up in any ledger. But that was a door he kept shut, and he had gotten very good at keeping doors shut. Smallness was an art, and he was its master. He was forgettable the way furniture is forgettable: useful, present, beneath notice. The palace staff called him Master Sterling and assumed he was some minor guildsman's son with a delicate stomach. The Wardens nodded to him in corridors because nodding to the food-taster was protocol, and protocol was the only scripture they kept. Nobody—not Cook, not the chamberlain, not Captain Vane herself with her cold grey eyes—had ever once looked at Dominic Sterling and thought: *there goes the last Ember-sworn man in Vellamare.*

That was the whole achievement of his life. That was what he had built instead of everything else.

Most days, it was enough.

* * *

After the evening service, he walked home through the Salt Market district, taking the long way past the chandlers' row because the smell of beeswax reminded him of something he refused to think about, and arrived at his shop just as the lamps were being lit.

The shop was called STERLING'S SMALL GOODS, and it sold exactly what the name promised. Thread wound on bone spools. Dried lavender in muslin bags. Little sachets "for restful sleep," which contained chamomile and nothing else, whatever the grandmothers of the district whispered. A shelf of carved wooden buttons, a basket of river stones polished smooth, jars of honey from hives he kept on the roof.

And under the counter, behind a false bottom that had taken him four months to build convincingly, a locked box containing the only truly dangerous inventory in the building: nine charms. Real ones. A knot-charm against drowning. A fever-breaker. Three wards of increasing strength. A charm for safe childbirth that he had made for a midwife on Tanners' Row and never delivered, because she'd been taken by the Wardens the week before, and Dominic had gone home and sat in the dark for a long time and then gotten up the next morning and opened the shop as always.

Smallness, he told himself, was also survival. And survival was not nothing. Survival was the seed of everything else, buried deep where the frost couldn't reach.

He was lighting the lamp over his workbench when the knock came.

Three knocks. Ordinary enough—but the rhythm was wrong. Not the brisk double-rap of a customer, not the flat knock of a Warden. This was hesitant, arrhythmic, the knocking of someone who had perhaps never knocked on a door before, or had forgotten how.

Dominic stood very still, his hand on the lamp key.

Then, through the door, muffled and thin, a voice spoke words in a language that had no business existing anymore. Words that predated the kingdom, that predated the walls of Vellamare themselves. Words the Crown had spent twelve years burning out of every book, every gravestone, every mouth.

Words that meant, roughly: *Taster. Open. Please. It's almost out.*

Almost out. As if whatever carried those words were a candle guttering.

Dominic's hand went to the latch before his mind caught up with it—and stopped. Every instinct he had honed over twelve years screamed the same lesson: *smallness, invisibility, do not open the door.* The Wardens swept this district on Thursdays. Today was Thursday. If there was a child on his step speaking the Ember-tongue, then either it was a trap of exquisite cruelty, or it was the beginning of the end of everything he had built.

He looked at his hands. Steady. They were always steady; that was the point of him.

*Taster,* the voice said again, smaller now. *Please.*

Somewhere down the street, boots. More than one pair. Moving fast.

Dominic Sterling, royal food-taster, master of smallness, keeper of nine forbidden charms and one enormous secret, unlatched his door.
