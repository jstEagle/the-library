## 2. A Knock in Old Tongue

The knock came at the hour when Vellamare's lamps were lit and honest people had already locked their doors against the dark.

Dominic Sterling was elbow-deep in a barrel of pickled herring at the time, which is to say he was having a perfectly ordinary evening. The brine stung a hangnail. A moth bumped against his one good lamp. Somewhere above, his landlady's cat performed its nightly ritual of dragging something dead across the roof tiles with the ceremony of a state funeral.

He almost didn't hear the knocking over the fish.

Three raps. Then—strange thing—a pause, and three more, soft as a bird landing on a sill. Not the fist of a creditor or the boot of a Warden. Those knocked like they owned the door and intended to prove it.

He wiped his hands on his apron, told himself it would be Mrs. Pell wanting her ladder back, and opened the door.

A child stood in the rain.

She could not have been more than eight, though she held herself like something much older and much wilder—a wet sparrow of a girl, caked to the knees in river mud, hair matted into ropes that might once have been black. Her feet were bare. Her feet were bare *in the Salt Market district in the month of Frostmelt*, and she did not shiver, which frightened Dominic more than anything else about her.

"Mrs. Pell," he said, "your ladder's under the stairs."

The child looked up at him. And she spoke.

"*Serra veth an-Embera. Serra domm.*"

The words went through Dominic like a hook through the gill of a fish—one clean pull, and suddenly he was not standing in his doorway at all but twelve years younger, kneeling in ash, holding a woman's hand while the world burned behind them. *Serra veth an-Embera.* Find the taster. *Serra domm.* Be small. Be safe. Be nothing they can see.

Words that predated kingdoms. Words that had been old when Vellamare was a fishing camp. Words that Dominic had sworn, on the only grave he'd ever been able to dig, never to answer again.

"I don't know what you're saying," he said, in flawless, forgettable Common Tongue. "You should go home."

The girl's chin came up. Mud had dried in streaks down her face, and through the cracks her skin showed pale as candle wax. She said, slowly, deliberately, the way one speaks to a fool or a foreigner:

"*Serra. Veth. An-Embera.*"

And then she opened her mouth wider than any child ought to be able to open it, and breathed out light.

It was small—the ember of a dying match, a firefly's worth—but it was *hers*, glowing warm orange in the blue dark of the doorway, and it cast shadows on the wall behind Dominic that moved wrong, that bent toward her like wheat toward sun. He felt it in his teeth. In the old scar along his palm. In the place behind his breastbone where he kept everything he refused to think about.

"Inside," he heard himself say. His voice cracked like a boy's. "Now. *Now.*"

She darted past him, quick as spilled water, and he shut the door and threw the bolt and stood there with his forehead against the wood, breathing like a man who has just seen his own ghost selling chestnuts on a street corner.

*Be small,* the dead woman had told him. Twelve years he had been small. Twelve years of tasting soup and palming charms and laughing at the right jokes and never, ever being memorable. And now the past had come knocking—literally, mud and all—at his door, wearing bare feet in Frostmelt.

"Gods' teeth," he whispered, in the old tongue, because some prayers only worked in their original language.

* * *

Up close, the girl smelled of woodsmoke and river and something underneath both—something green and growing, like the first breath of spring in a forest that hadn't existed for twelve years.

She stood in the middle of his single room, dripping, surveying his life with frank, unnerving eyes. The shelf of harmless charms: knotwork buttons that supposedly prevented blisters, sachets that promised sweet dreams, little clay figures sold as "good luck, no refunds." The copper pots. The bed with its one blanket. The barrel of herring.

She looked at the herring barrel with deep suspicion.

"You're hungry," Dominic said. It wasn't a question. He put bread and cold sausage and the heel of a cheese on the table, and she fell on the food with the manners of a wolf cub, watching him the whole time between bites, the way animals watch when they've learned that kindness can be a trap.

"What's your name?" he asked.

She swallowed. Considered. "Marguerite," she said at last, and the word sat strangely in her mouth, borrowed clothing. Then she touched her own chest and said something else—a short word, round and warm—and he understood that this was her true name, given in the old tongue, and that she was showing it to him the way you show someone a wound.

Dominic did not touch his own chest in reply. He very much wanted to. Instead he said, "Where is your mother? Your—"

"*Mama serra domm,*" the girl said, and her voice did something terrible then; it went flat. "*Ashen men. Fire.*"

Ashen Wardens. Fire.

He sat down across from her, slowly, because his knees had opinions. Outside, rain ticked against the shutters. The moth had gone. Even the cat on the roof seemed to be holding its breath.

"How many?" he asked. "How many Wardens?"

Marguerite held up fingers. Five? Ten? Her hands weren't sure, but her eyes were, and her eyes said: *all of them, everywhere, always.*

* * *

He was still sitting there, doing sums he hated, when the bells began.

Not the chapel bells—the watch bells, up in the Wardenhouse on Candlerig, ringing the pattern every child in Vellamare knew the way they knew their own heartbeat. Three tolls, pause, three tolls. *Sweep in progress. Doors closed. Eyes down.*

Dominic was moving before the third toll finished. He snuffed the lamp. He swept Marguerite's bread crusts off the table into his palm. He pulled back the rug by the hearth, revealing the trapdoor he'd sworn for twelve years he'd never need, and beneath it the crawlspace between his floor joists and his neighbor's cellar—a gap barely wide enough for a thin man, let alone a thin man and a feral child.

"In," he said. "Quiet as dust. If I tap twice—" he demonstrated on the floorboards, *tap-tap* —"you come out. Anything else, you stay. You could be burning alive down there and you stay unless it's two taps. Do you understand?"

She understood. She also grabbed his sleeve as he lowered her down, and said, in careful Common Tongue with an accent like wind through grass:

"You are *serra*. Taster-man. Mama said taster-man keeps the ember-word."

"And your mama," Dominic said gently, "was a fool who talked too much."

But he squeezed her hand before letting go, and she went down into the dark without another sound, which was somehow worse.

He rolled the rug flat. He relit the lamp. He took up the herring barrel—not to hide it, but because salting fish was the most innocent thing in the world, and innocence was armor—and he had exactly four minutes to become nobody before the boots arrived.

* * *

They came down his street like weather.

Through the shutter-slats he watched them: six Ashen Wardens in their grey lacquered coats, ash-white cloaks beaded with rain, moving door to door with the unhurried efficiency of men checking a ledger. Their captain walked last. He carried the long iron rod of his office unlit, which was almost worse—if they'd found anything, it would be lit.

At the house opposite, old Ferro the rope-maker opened his door looking genuinely baffled, which was genuine, because Ferro had never done anything interesting in his life. The Wardens asked their questions. Ferro shook his head. They moved on.

To Dominic's door.

The knock, this time, was exactly what he'd expected: three blows that treated the door as a formality.

"Taster Sterling," said the Warden in front, a young one with rain running off his helm. "Crown business. A fugitive, small, female, on foot since dusk. Seen?"

"Not by me." True. Technically. Dominic had gotten very good at technically true over twelve years. "I've been salting herring since the lamp-lighting. Fish don't leave much attention for the street."

The young Warden glanced past him into the room. Dominic let him look. That was the trick—let them look, let them see poverty and pickled fish and a tired man with brine-reddened hands. Smallness, deployed.

The young Warden hesitated. And then the captain stepped forward, and Dominic's stomach dropped through the floorboards, through the crawlspace, through the earth itself.

Because he knew her. Not well—he'd seen her at palace functions, grey-cloaked among the courtiers, a stillness in a room full of motion. Captain Ilse Vane, they called her, and the kitchen gossip said she'd once tracked a smuggler for nine days through the Mirefen with nothing but a dog and a grudge.

She looked at Dominic the way he imagined a jeweler looks at a stone that might be glass.

"Herring," she said.

"Yes, Captain."

"All evening."

"The barrel doesn't salt itself, Captain."

Something flickered across her face—not suspicion exactly, or not only. Something wearier and older. "There was a fire in the Fenward villages three nights ago," she said. "Before we burned it. Whatever came out of that fire is loose in my city, Master Sterling, and it is *small*, and it is *fast*, and it does not feel the cold." She leaned in, just slightly. Rain dripped from the edge of her hood between them. "If it comes to your door, you will send word. Not because you fear me. Because whatever it carries will burn this city down around everyone you've ever salted fish for. Good night."

She turned. The boots receded. The next door knocked.

Dominic closed his door and stood with his hand on the bolt, shaking so hard the lamp flame wavered—or perhaps that was the draft, or perhaps it was the small warm presence beneath his floor, breathing slow and patient as a banked coal.

*Tap-tap,* he signaled, and lifted the trapdoor, and Marguerite climbed out into the lamplight and looked at him with those old, old eyes.

"Serra," she said. "Taster-man. *An-Embera serra domm.*"

And pressed his scarred palm flat against her chest, where something beneath her ribs answered with heat like a second heartbeat—

—and spoke, in the tongue older than kingdoms, words he had sworn never to answer.

This time, gods help him, he answered.
