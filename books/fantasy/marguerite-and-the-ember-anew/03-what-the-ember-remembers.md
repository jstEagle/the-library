## 3. What the Ember Remembers

Fire first.

Always fire first.

Not the bad fire — the good fire, the hearth-fire, the one that hummed in the old tongue the way a mother hums when she does not know she is humming. *Vaela, vaela, ember-kin.* The woman with hands like bark had sung it over the coals every morning of Marguerite's life, or every morning she could remember, which was not many mornings now. Mornings were slippery. They slid off her like rain off oiled wool.

The woman's name had been Sera. Or Sarai. Or something that started like a struck bell and ended in ash. Marguerite held the shape of it in her mouth sometimes, under her tongue, where she kept the important things: the word for *home*, the word for *stay*, the word for *run*.

*Run* was getting a lot of use lately.

* * *

The village burned on a night that smelled of pine and wrongness.

She remembered it in pieces, because pieces were all that would stay still long enough to be caught. A piece: the door bursting inward and the light behind the men being orange where it should have been blue-black. A piece: Sera's hands pressing something against Marguerite's chest — small, hot, alive, beating like a second heart that did not know fear yet but would learn. A piece: words in the old tongue, spoken slow, the way you speak to someone drowning.

*Find the taster.*

That was all. Not *find your aunt*. Not *find the other folk*. The taster. As if the whole world had narrowed, in Sera's last living moment, to a man who tasted soup for a living.

Marguerite had run. She was good at running. She had run through bracken and bog and over a wall that cut her palms, and the thing in her chest had beaten faster when men in grey followed, and slower when they lost her scent, and once — only once — it had gone warm as fresh bread when she passed a hedge with berries on it, and she had eaten the berries and felt less alone, which made no sense at all, but sense was for people whose villages hadn't burned.

Then mud. Then roads. Then the city, which was a forest that had died and been replaced by stone, and the stone did not sing, and nobody spoke the old tongue, and the thing in her chest grew quieter and quieter, like a bird tucking its head under its wing to wait out winter.

Until the door.

Until the man with forgettable eyes opened the door and heard her say the words, and his face did a thing faces weren't supposed to do. It came apart. Like bread. Like a loaf torn open, all the soft insides showing.

He had said the word back to her. *Vaela.* He had said it badly, rusty, like a hinge that hadn't moved in twelve years. But he had said it.

The ember in her chest had flared so bright she'd thought he must see it through her skin. Maybe he had. His face looked like a man seeing something through skin.

* * *

Now there was a floor under her. A real floor, wooden, swept, smelling of rosemary and lamp oil and underneath it all — faint as a coin at the bottom of a well — of smoke. Old smoke. Smoke kept.

The man — Dominic, he'd said, though the name sat strangely on him, like a coat borrowed from a bigger brother — had given her bread and milk and a blanket that smelled of lavender, and then he had sat down across from her and put his head in his hands, and stayed that way so long she thought maybe he'd fallen asleep sitting up, which was a skill she respected deeply.

Then he looked up, and the ember moved.

It didn't ask permission. It never asked permission. It simply turned toward him the way a flower turns toward light, except flowers are polite about it, and this was not polite. Warmth poured out of her chest and across the little room, and the lamplight bent, and Dominic's eyes went wide and dark and somewhere else entirely.

Marguerite watched him watch.

This was what the ember showed people. She had seen it do it once before, to Sera, on a quiet evening: Sera staring into the middle distance with tears running down into her smile, and afterward she'd said, *it remembers, little wren. It remembers everything we've forgotten, everything they took. That's why they fear it. Not the burning. The remembering.*

So Marguerite sat very still and let it happen, and ate her bread, and waited for the man to come back from wherever the ember was taking him.

* * *

What the ember showed him — she knew this without knowing how she knew, the way you know the stove is hot without touching it — was the old faith whole.

It showed him the Emberfolk in their valley before the valley had a king's road through it: hundreds of hearths, each one lit from the next, flame passed hand to hand at midwinter until the whole hillside glowed like a constellation brought down to earth. It showed him the ember-keepers, women and men with bark-colored hands, singing *vaela* over birthing fires and funeral pyres and the first furrow of spring, because to the Emberfolk all three were the same event: something ending so something else could begin.

It showed him the first king. Not in glory. In shame. A young man kneeling before an ember-keeper, begging warmth for his cold army, and receiving it freely, because warmth was always given freely — and then standing, and taking, and taking more than was given, because he had learned that a flame freely offered can be stolen by the hand that holds the cup.

Marguerite didn't see these things herself. She only felt them pass through her, great slow shapes moving under the surface of a lake, and felt the man across from her feel them, and his breath went ragged, and his hands — his careful, invisible, servant's hands — began to shake.

Good, said some fierce small creature inside her that might have been the ember and might have been her. *Remember too, then. Remember with me. I'm tired of being the only one.*

* * *

When it was over, Dominic sat back and looked at her as if she were a letter he'd been waiting twelve years to receive.

"You saw," she said. Her city-tongue was poor and thick, but she spent it anyway. "You saw the fires."

"I saw—" He stopped. Started again. "I saw my grandmother's kitchen. She used to sing while she cooked. I thought I'd forgotten the song." His voice did something complicated. "I hadn't forgotten it. Someone took it out of me and told me I'd never had it."

"The grey men," Marguerite said. "The ash-smelling ones."

"The Ashen Wardens." He said the name the way you spit out a bad seed. Then, softer, almost to himself: "Twelve years ago they came through the hedgerows and the herb-circles and the hearth-houses, and they collected everything that sang. And everyone who remembered how."

"Your grandmother?"

A long silence. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them, the great dead stone forest settled and groaned like a ship.

"She handed me a charm of forgetting," he said at last, "and told me to be small, and be useful, and live. And I have been so *good* at it, Marguerite. You cannot imagine how good. I am the best at small that this city has ever produced."

The ember pulsed. Marguerite felt it laughing, or grieving, or both — with the ember those were often the same thing, like laughter at a funeral, which the Emberfolk had considered the highest courtesy.

"It doesn't want you small," she said.

"No," he agreed grimly. "I gathered that. It's been shouting at me since you walked in. My hands haven't stopped tingling. Do you know how inconvenient that is? I taste for the queen. My tongue is a precision instrument. And right now it's ringing like a chapel bell."

Despite everything — despite the burned village and the mud and the grey men and the word *taster* rattling around her chest like a stone in a jar — Marguerite found her mouth doing a thing it hadn't done in weeks.

It curved up.

Dominic stared at her crooked smile as if it were another vision. Then, helplessly, his own mouth answered it, just a little, just at the corners, like a candle deciding whether to catch.

"There," the ember seemed to say, settling warm beneath her ribs. "*There* is the man Sera sent me to. Under all that small. Still lit."

* * *

But the warmth could not last, because outside, far off but coming closer, boots were striking cobbles in unison — not marching, exactly; the Wardens never marched, they *swept*, like a broom with opinions — and beneath the boot-sound came the other sound, the one Marguerite's chest knew before her ears did: the low, hungry hum of ash-charms waking up.

The ember went still and thin, a held breath.

Dominic was on his feet before she finished flinching, snuffing the lamp with a pinch of fingers, and in the sudden dark his voice came low and changed — no longer the soft servant's murmur but something older, harder, a voice from before the forgetting.

"They're checking doors on this street. Listen to me, Marguerite. There's a place under my floor, and there are things down there I swore I'd never touch again, and it seems—" a breath, half laugh, half prayer, "—it seems the swearing is over. Get in. Whatever you hear tonight, whatever you *see* — the ember stays hidden, and you stay silent, and if I don't come back for you by dawn—"

"You come back," Marguerite said, in the old tongue, in the voice Sera had used for commands, and the ember flared once, briefly, lighting his face like a struck match.

He looked at her — at the fire in her, at the fire apparently still in him — and nodded slowly.

"Aye," he said. "I suppose I do."

Above them, fists began hammering on doors, one house at a time, each knock closer than the last.
