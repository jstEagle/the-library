## 4. Twelve Years of Small Charms

The morning after the girl arrived, Dominic Sterling did what he always did when the world threatened to become interesting: he made it small again.

He swept the step. He wiped the mud from the threshold with a rag he kept for exactly this purpose, though in twelve years the rag had never once been needed. He opened his shutters to let the smell of yesterday's bread drift out into the lane, because a shop that smelled like nothing was a shop that invited questions, and a shop that smelled like day-old loaf was a shop that smelled like every other shop on Tallow Row.

Then he went inside and looked at the child sleeping in his back room, curled around a hearthstone that was not a hearthstone at all, and felt the smallness crack down the middle.

* * *

Her name was Marguerite. She had told him that much before sleep took her mid-sentence, the way children fall—as though someone had simply pulled a plug somewhere inside her. She spoke perhaps forty words of common speech and several hundred of a language Dominic had sworn, twelve years ago, never to speak again. She had knocked on his door in that language. Three words. He had known them instantly, the way you know your own name shouted by a stranger in a crowd.

*Ember-keeper. Open.*

He had opened. God help him, he had opened.

Now she slept, and the ember—because it was an ember, there was no longer any pretending otherwise—sat against her sternum beneath her filthy shift like a second heart, glowing faintly through the fabric when she dreamed. Dominic had spent the night watching it pulse. Slow, patient, warm as a banked fire. It did not flicker the way ordinary fire flickered. It breathed.

He put the kettle on. He ground his chicory. He performed the entire liturgy of an ordinary morning, and underneath it, quietly, he panicked.

* * *

His shop was called Sterling's Remedies, which was a lie so comfortable it had practically become true. He sold charms for toothache. Charms for colicky babies. Charms for cows that wouldn't come to milk and husbands who wouldn't come home. Every charm was nonsense—knots tied in red thread, salt in blue paper, a rhyme about Saint Aldith's knees—and every charm worked, because half of healing is being believed, and Dominic Sterling was very easy to believe. He had a kind face. He had kind hands. He had spent twelve years cultivating the specific gentleness of a man who has decided never to want anything loudly again.

The Wardens knew about him, in the way the Wardens knew about everyone. Twice a year an inspector came, poked through his jars of chamomile and dried nettle, found nothing but tea, and left with a small purchase for his wife's headaches. Dominic had once heard him describe the shop to a colleague: *"Harmless. The man's practically a herbalist."*

Practically. That word had been the proudest review of Dominic's life.

Because the truth was this: none of it was harmless. The knots were knots his grandmother had taught him, and they held. The rhymes were Ember-sworn litanies worn down to nursery nonsense, and they still carried something, faint as heat off a stone at dusk. Twelve years of small charms, each one deniable, each one real, each one a single coal raked carefully under ash so the fire would not go out entirely—not enough to warm anyone, just enough to keep alive the man who remembered how.

And now a child had walked out of the dark carrying the whole hearth.

* * *

"You're doing the face," said a voice from the doorway.

Dominic didn't jump. He had trained himself out of jumping years ago; a man who jumps is a man who notices things. But he set down the kettle with exaggerated calm and turned to find Tam Broadstairs leaning in his doorframe, eating an apple.

Tam was a smuggler, a fence, an occasional liar, and the closest thing Dominic had to a friend, which told you most of what you needed to know about Dominic's life. They had an arrangement: Tam brought him saffron and information; Dominic brought him poultices and discretion. Tam knew everything about everyone and had correctly guessed almost nothing about Dominic, which was the way Dominic liked it.

"I'm doing no face," Dominic said. "This is my face."

"That's the one." Tam came in uninvited, as he always did, and helped himself to a chair, as he also always did. "You've got your 'nothing is wrong' face on, which historically means everything is wrong. Also, there were Ashen Wardens on Tallow Row at dawn. Grey cloaks. Asking after a child." He took another bite of apple. "Mud on their boots up to the knee. Came from the river gate, which means she came by water, which means somebody put her in a boat, which means—" he gestured with the apple core, encompassing Dominic, the shop, the general catastrophe, "—somebody knows something I don't, and I hate that."

"Tam."

"Yes?"

"How much do I owe you for last month's saffron?"

"Ah," said Tam, with the satisfaction of a man watching a lock turn. "So we're getting to it."

* * *

Here is what Dominic weighed, that morning, while Tam talked and the kettle sang and the ember breathed in the back room:

He weighed his immunity. Twelve years of careful, boring, invisible living had bought him a thing most hedge-witches never had: a clean name. Not a noble name, not a protected name—a forgotten one. The Crown's lists were long, but they were lists of people worth remembering, and Dominic had made himself a man the clerks skimmed past. He could walk through Vellamare like weather. Nobody looked at weather.

He weighed the kitchens. Every sixth day he tasted for the queen's table, and it was the strangest honor of his hidden life—that the palace trusted him, that he moved through its corridors with his tray and his little silver spoon, nodding to guards who thought he was nobody, standing three feet from the throne of the men who had burned his faith and hearing them complain about the price of figs. There was a spite in it that fed him. There was also, if he was honest, a belonging. They knew his name there. *Sterling.* Said without suspicion. Said kindly, even, by the undercook, who saved him the end pieces of the honey cake because she'd noticed he liked them.

Nobody had noticed anything about Dominic Sterling in twelve years except that he liked honey cake ends. And some traitorous, starving corner of him had treasured that.

He weighed the girl. Forty words of common speech. A burn scar down one arm shaped like ivy. Eyes that watched him the way you watch a fire you've been cold too long beside—hungry and afraid of being hungry. Last night she had pressed her small fist against his chest, over his heart, and said something in the old tongue, and he had understood it without wanting to: *You still carry it. Why do you hide it?*

And he weighed the arithmetic, which was the cruelest part, because the arithmetic was simple. If he gave her up, he lost nothing. A child he'd known one night, a language he'd buried, a faith already dead these dozen years. If he kept her, he lost everything—the shop, the kitchens, the honey cake ends, the beautiful forgettable life. Two lives weighed against one, and the one was his.

Any sensible man would hand her over. Any sensible man would tell himself she'd be questioned gently, that the Wardens weren't monsters, that the ember was a rock and the old tongue was a nursery language and the past was the past.

Dominic stood at his own counter, in his own shop, smelling his own bread, and discovered that he was not, and had possibly never been, a sensible man. He had merely been a frightened one with excellent discipline.

* * *

"So here's my offer," Tam said, having eaten the apple down to the stem. "Boat leaves tomorrow night, tide-moon, out of the eel stairs below the Salt Market. Smuggler's run down the coast. Two berths." He held up two fingers. "Yours and hers. You bring nothing, you say nothing, you never come back. In exchange—" and here Tam leaned forward, and his face, usually so pleasantly crooked, went flat and serious, "—the stone stays. Whatever she's carrying, whatever the grey-cloaks think she's carrying, it goes in the harbor before you board. I don't ferry embers. I ferry people. People are illegal; embers are *apocalyptic*. There's a difference in the paperwork."

"And if I don't throw it in the harbor?"

"Then I wish you a long life and a quick death, and I mean both." Tam stood, brushed crumbs from his coat, and paused at the door. "Twelve years I've known you, Dom. You're the only honest man I know, which means you're lying about something enormous, and I've never once wanted to know what. Don't make me want to know now."

He left. The bell over the door gave its small domestic chime, absurdly gentle, like a cat asking to be fed.

* * *

Dominic went to the back room and sat on the floor across from the sleeping child, because his legs had decided sitting was happening whether he approved or not.

Marguerite slept with one hand open, palm up, the way trusting children sleep. The ember glowed through her shift in slow tides. And as he watched, it brightened—just slightly, just once—in a rhythm he recognized, because he had grown up beside a hearth where a woman had tended a flame the same way, coaxing it, greeting it, saying good-morning to it as though it were a relative.

*It knows me,* he thought. *The wretched thing knows me, and it's showing off.*

He should give it to the harbor. He should take the boat. He should take the girl and the boat and the clean slate and never say another word in a dead language as long as he lived, and in thirty years he would be an old herbalist on some gray coast with no name worth remembering, safe, small, finished.

Or.

Or he could do the other thing. The large thing. The thing with fire in it.

Marguerite stirred. Her eyes opened halfway, found him, and did not widen in fear, which somehow was worse. She simply looked at him, the way you look at a question you've already asked.

She raised her open hand and touched the air between them, and said, in the old tongue, one word he had not heard since the night the sky over his village turned orange:

*Choose.*

Down in the lane, boots on cobbles. Grey cloaks, moving door to door, coming up Tallow Row like a tide with a warrant.
