## 5. The Warden's Question

Ilse Vane had learned long ago that kitchens told the truth.

Courtiers lied in marble halls and lied again on marble stairs, but a kitchen was an honest room. Steam stripped pretense from people the way it stripped fat from bones. So when the Ashen Wardens came to the palace at dawn—forty of them, grey-cloaked, quiet as ash falling—she went not to the throne room, not to the queen's chambers, but down, past the sculleries, into the warm belly of the building where the truth rose like bread.

"Line up," she said, and they lined up. Cooks, scullions, undercooks, a boy who turned geese, two laundresses who had wandered in for warmth. They stood in the wet light of the yard behind the ovens, breath steaming, and looked at her boots because no one ever wanted to look at her face.

She understood that. She had spent eleven years making her face into something people didn't want to look at. It had been easier than the alternative.

"I'll be quick," she said. "A child came through Tallow Row last night. Mud to the knees, hair like a crow's nest, carrying something the Crown wants very much. Someone in this city fed her, or hid her, or pointed her somewhere useful." She let the silence do its work. "I don't care about your little sins. The salt you pocket. The wine you water. The cook's boy who's been sharing a bed with the laundress since midwinter—" here the laundress went pink and the goose-boy went pinker, and someone at the back made a sound like a swallowed laugh, which Ilse permitted, because fear alone makes liars of everyone, and she needed these people loose-tongued, not tight. "I care about one thing. Who helped the child."

Silence. Good silence, though. The thinking kind.

"The reward is a pardon," she added. "Full. Whatever name you're carrying, whatever debt, whatever warrant with your description on it in some provincial clerk's bad handwriting—it burns. You walk out of here clean."

Still nothing. But she watched hands. Hands always knew before faces did. A scullion's fingers curled around his rag. An undercook shifted her weight toward the door. And near the end of the line, a thin man with flour on his sleeves stood perfectly still—not frozen-still, not guilty-still, but *settled*-still, the stillness of a man who had decided something years ago and was merely confirming it.

Dominic Sterling. Royal food-taster. The most forgettable man in Vellamare, which was, of course, exactly what made him memorable to anyone paying attention.

* * *

She interviewed them one at a time in the dry-goods room, between sacks of barley and hanging hams, because she liked her interrogations seasoned with ordinary things. It kept both parties honest.

The goose-boy cried. The head cook blustered, then wept about his dead wife, which was either genuine grief or excellent tactics, and Ilse found she didn't much care; he'd told her nothing either way. The laundress confessed to the affair unprompted, in exhaustive detail, apparently relieved to finally tell someone.

And then Dominic Sterling sat down on an upturned flour barrel, folded his hands, and waited politely, as if she were the one being questioned.

"Warden Captain," he said. "Terrible business."

"You know why I'm here."

"A child on Tallow Row. Half the street knows. The other half will by supper." He shrugged. "I taste soup for a living, Captain. My knowledge of city affairs begins and ends with whether the fishmonger waters his eels."

"Mm." She walked the length of the shelf, letting her fingers trail along jars. "Tell me about your work."

"It's soup."

"It's poison detection. Twelve years without a single taster's death in this palace. Do you know how unusual that is? Your predecessor lasted four months. The one before him, seven weeks." She stopped in front of him. "You've survived twelve years tasting for a court where three dukes have died of their dinners in my tenure. Either you are the luckiest man alive, or you are the best there has ever been at your trade. Luck doesn't hold for twelve years, Master Sterling. Skill does. And skill leaves traces."

"What sort of traces?"

"Yours." She held out her hand. "Give me your wrist."

He gave it. That interested her. Guilty men flinched; innocent men bristled; only men with nothing left to lose surrendered their wrists so courteously. His pulse was slow. Annoyingly slow.

She bent her head and breathed in. Under the flour and the kitchen smoke: marjoram, yes, and sage, the honest smells of any cook. But beneath those, faint as a watermark, something green and bitter and old. An herb she had smelled exactly once before, eleven years ago, on the robes of a woman the Wardens burned in Ashmarket Square, an herb that grew wild on hills no cart road reached, an herb that every apothecary in Vellamare would swear did not exist because existing it would be against the law.

Emberleaf. Or its cousin. Something from before.

"You smell of a plant," she said quietly, releasing his wrist, "that isn't sold anywhere in this city."

"Lavender," he offered. "I keep some in my drawer. Keeps moths from the sugar."

"Lavender I know. This isn't lavender."

"Then it must be the soap."

"It isn't the soap, Master Sterling."

They regarded each other across the barrel of flour. He had mild brown eyes, the eyes of a man who apologized to furniture when he bumped it. And yet. And yet there was a door behind those eyes, shut and barred and painted over so many times you'd never know a door was there—unless you had one yourself.

Ilse had one herself. She knew the paint.

"Here's what happens now," she said. "I could arrest you. Detain you. Search that tidy little shop of yours on Tallow Row—the one with the harmless charms, the luck-knots and the anti-moth sachets, all perfectly legal, all perfectly dull. I could pull up your floorboards. I suspect I'd find something interesting under the floorboards."

"And if you didn't?"

"Then I'd have wasted a morning and you'd have a grievance and a story for the tavern." She leaned closer. "Or—and I confess I prefer this—we make a bargain. You tell me where the child is. Today, tonight, whenever she surfaces, wherever she surfaces. You send word to me and no one else. In exchange: your name stays clean. No search. No file. As far as the Wardens are concerned, Dominic Sterling is the invisible man he's worked so hard to become."

Something flickered across his face at *invisible*. Not guilt. Recognition. The look of a man hearing his own obituary read back to him accurately.

"That's quite an offer," he said.

"It's the only one. The next one involves irons."

"And if I refuse? If I say I've seen nothing, know nothing, tasted nothing but soup?"

"Then I search the shop. And the shop next to it. And every shop on Tallow Row until the child falls out of one of them." She straightened. "I should tell you—I'm good at finding things. It's the whole of my talent. People think Wardens burn things. We mostly find things. Burning is just what we do after."

He nodded slowly, and she watched him do arithmetic behind the mild eyes—counting exits, counting hours, counting a small muddy life set against a large grey machine.

"Why do you want her?" he asked suddenly. "The child. Why does the Crown want a mud-covered girl badly enough to turn out forty Wardens at dawn?"

"That's above my station."

"Nothing is above anyone's station. That's how stations work—they're all ceilings over the same room."

For a moment—just a moment—Ilse almost answered honestly. *Because eleven years ago I burned a woman in Ashmarket and she looked at me the way you look at me now, and I have been trying to find out what she knew ever since. Because my brother died in the Night of Cinders and they told me magic did it and I believed them because believing was easier than asking who lit the first fire. Because I have spent half my life hunting a crime I'm no longer sure was a crime.*

What she said was: "It's above my station."

"Of course." He stood, brushed flour from his trousers, and inclined his head with the exact courtesy of a servant excusing himself from a table. "You'll have your answer, Captain. One way or another."

"One way or another," she agreed. "Master Sterling. Your hands."

"My hands?"

"When you took my wrist. Herb-bitter, under everything else. Wash harder." She smiled without warmth. "Or stop washing altogether. It won't matter which, in the end."

* * *

She let him go and stood alone among the hams, doing her own arithmetic.

Eleven years ago, Ilse Vane had been a different creature—younger, certain, armed with a warrant and a righteous fire of her own. She had stood in Ashmarket Square while the woman with emberleaf in her robe refused, to the very end, to give the Wardens what they asked for: the names of the others. *There are no others,* the woman had said, smiling through the smoke. *That's rather the point.* And then she had sung something in a language older than the kingdom, and the fire had taken her, and Ilse had gone home and washed her hands until they bled, and joined the Wardens properly, and risen, and told herself every year that certainty would come back if she just climbed high enough.

It hadn't. Instead she'd found a food-taster who smelled of outlawed hills and surrendered his wrist like a man handing over a receipt.

*There are no others. That's rather the point.*

Unless there were. Unless one of them had been standing in the queen's own kitchen for twelve years, palming charms into broth, keeping a whole court alive one careful spoonful at a time—which meant, if you followed the thought all the way down, that the great poison-plague the Wardens existed to prevent had perhaps never once threatened the palace at all. That the danger had been invented. That the protection had been real, and quiet, and wearing flour on its sleeves.

Ilse pressed her palms flat against a cold ham hook and breathed.

Her captain's oath said report suspicion within the hour. Her report, drafted in her head, read: *Taster cooperative. No evidence. Recommend surveillance.* Which was true, if you squinted. She had gotten very good at squinting. It was practically the same skill as finding things.

Outside, the morning bell rang nine times, and somewhere across the city a child with a coal in her chest was running, and somewhere on Tallow Row a forgettable man was presumably deciding whether to sell her or save her, and Ilse Vane, hunter, finder, burner-after, discovered that she was praying—actually praying, to no god she could name—for him to choose wrong.

Then a runner arrived, mud-spattered, breathless: a sighting. The child, seen at first light, slipping toward the Salt Market with a tall figure in a hooded cloak. And the description of the tall figure—

Ilse closed her eyes.

"—grey cloak, Captain," the runner panted. "Warden's grey. Somebody wearing our colors, walking her right out of the city."

Someone was using her own order as a mask. And the only person she'd spoken to today—the only person who'd heard exactly how the search would run, exactly which streets would close, exactly when—

She was moving before the thought finished, boots ringing on the kitchen flags, shouting for horses, while behind her the honest room steamed on, telling its truths to nobody.
