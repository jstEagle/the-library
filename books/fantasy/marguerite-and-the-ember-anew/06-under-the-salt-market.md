## 6. Under the Salt Market

The entrance to the under-city was behind a fishmonger's stall, which Dominic found fitting. Everything in Vellamare that was worth hiding had always been hidden behind something that smelled worse.

"Keep your head down," he murmured to Marguerite, who had her head down so far she was nearly walking on all fours, which rather defeated the purpose. "And if anyone asks, you're my niece."

She looked up at him with an expression of profound offense.

"All right. My *nephew*."

That seemed acceptable. Children were negotiable about gender; it was lying about their importance they wouldn't stand for.

The fishmonger—a woman named Petra with forearms like ship rope and a face like a closed ledger—looked at Dominic for a long moment when he gave the knock. Three short, one long, then a pause long enough to be insulting.

"You've not come down here in six years," she said.

"I've had no cause."

"And now?"

"Now I have a cause with mud on her feet and Wardens on her scent." He kept his voice pleasant, the voice of a man discussing the price of herring. "We need the salt tunnels. All the way under the market."

Petra's eyes flicked to Marguerite, and something moved behind them—recognition, or its cousin. "There's someone you should meet," she said slowly. "Someone who's been asking after... travelers of a particular kind."

"Petra."

"She came to me two nights ago. Knew things she shouldn't have known." Petra shrugged, a gesture like a landslide deciding against itself. "I don't ask questions down here. Questions are how you end up floating."

Dominic did not like this. Dominic liked very few things, and most of them were soups. But the Wardens had already swept his street twice, and the third sweep would not be a sweep but a taking, and Marguerite was humming under her breath in a language that made the air feel warmer, which was the last thing anyone wanted while standing next to several hundred pounds of flash powder stored by a man named Fennick who had never once considered the fire hazard.

"Fine," he said. "But if this person turns out to be trouble—"

"Then you'll taste it coming," Petra said, with the ghost of a smile. "That's what they say about you, taster. That nothing bitter gets past your tongue."

If only, thought Dominic, leading his niece-nephew into the dark.

* * *

Marguerite did not like the tunnels.

The tunnels were dark in the way of a mouth, and wet in the way of a wound, and they smelled of salt and old smoke and something underneath both that made the ember in her chest sit up and pay attention. It had been paying attention since the fish-woman's door. Since before that, maybe. Since the priestess.

She knew the word now. The tall man—Dominic, Dominic, she turned it over like a river stone—had said it without meaning to, the way people say true things when they are tired. *Priestess.* And Marguerite had felt the ember turn toward the word like a sunflower turning toward a lie.

Because the woman waiting for them at the bottom of the stairs was not what the word meant. Not anymore. Maybe not ever.

She stood in a chamber where salt crusted the walls like frost, wearing gray robes gone white at the seams, and when she saw Marguerite she made a sound Marguerite had only heard once before—from her mother, over the burning.

"Oh," the woman said. "Oh, little flame. You carried it all this way."

Her hands reached out. Her hands were beautiful hands, long and steady, and they reached for Marguerite's chest the way you reach for bread, the way you reach for a thing that is *yours*.

Marguerite stepped back and bit her.

* * *

"Ow," said Sera Voss, former First Keeper of the Ember Rite, disgraced eleven years ago for the crime of surviving when her temple did not, and currently sucking her thumb in the salt-light of a smuggler's lamp. "She bit me."

"She does that," said Dominic, who had been bitten twice since yesterday and was developing opinions about it. "Perhaps don't reach for the child's chest like you're picking fruit."

Sera lowered her hand with the careful dignity of the deeply offended. "I am trying to help her."

"You're trying to *take* from her. There's a difference, and my tongue has spent twelve years learning to tell them apart."

They stood in the antechamber beneath the Salt Market, where the smuggler tunnels branched like roots: west toward the river gates, east toward the old cisterns, north—suicidally—toward the palace foundations. Marguerite sat against the far wall, knees drawn up, watching Sera with the fixed attention of a cat watching a dog that has recently been promoted.

"The ember belongs to the faith," Sera said. "Not to a child. Not to a hedge-taster who's been playing at small charms while the rest of us burned. Do you know what I lost, Sterling? Do you know what I watched go up?"

"I know exactly what you watched go up," Dominic said quietly. "I was there."

That stopped her. For a moment the gray robes looked less like vestments and more like ash, and Sera Voss looked like what she was: a woman who had run out of a burning building carrying nothing, and had spent eleven years telling herself the nothing was a burden she'd been spared.

"I can teach her," Sera said at last, softer. "The rites. The words. She speaks the old tongue like breathing, but she doesn't know what any of it *means*. She needs a keeper. And you—" her eyes went over him, the flour-dusted apron still on under his coat, the smell of thyme and something no apothecary could name, "—you need to go back to being nobody. That's the bargain, taster. Give me the girl and the flame, walk out through the river gate tonight, and by morning you're just a man who makes soup. Safe. Small. Alive."

And there it was. The belonging offered with strings attached—or rather, the belonging offered precisely *because* of the strings, because Sera understood that the only way to make a man let go of a miracle was to promise him back his invisibility.

Dominic looked at Marguerite. Marguerite looked at him. Neither spoke the old tongue, and neither needed to. Some translations happen below language.

"No," he said.

"No?" Sera's voice cracked upward. "You'd keep her? You, who can't even keep yourself? They'll catch you in a day. Two. And when Ilse Vane puts you on the rack, everything you know comes out of you like juice from a grape, and the girl burns with you. Is that your plan? Martyrdom by incompetence?"

"My plan," said Dominic, "is the tunnel north."

Sera stared at him. Then she laughed, and it was not a kind sound. "North? Toward the palace? Toward the Wardens' own front step? You've finally gone mad, taster. Twelve years of tasting poison has pickled whatever was left of your—"

"Sera." He said her name gently, and that gentleness frightened her more than shouting would have. "You asked what I watched go up. I'll tell you what I stayed for. I stayed because the ember was already out of the temple that night. Already hidden. In a boy who worked the kitchens, who swore an oath his elders thought was a formality. The faith didn't die eleven years ago, Sera. It's been *seasoning*."

The silence after that was total. Even the drip of the tunnel water seemed embarrassed.

It was broken by Marguerite, who stood, walked to the middle of the chamber, and pointed—first at herself, then at the ember's warmth glowing faintly through her shirt, then at the northern tunnel. Then, with great clarity, at Sera, she crossed her arms and shook her head in the universal grammar of children everywhere: *no.*

"She votes," said Dominic. "Democracy's restored to the faith."

Sera's face went through several weathers. What it settled on was cold. "You'll regret this, Sterling. Both of you. The Wardens have dogs now. Ash-hounds. They don't follow scent." She smiled thinly. "They follow warmth."

* * *

Marguerite heard the dogs before Dominic did—not with her ears but with her chest, where the ember flinched like a sleeper hearing its name.

She grabbed his hand. He understood immediately, because he was learning her language faster than she was learning his, and they ran.

The northern tunnel was narrow and sloped downward before it rose, and behind them came the sound every hunted thing knows: claws on stone, and breathing that was too even, too patient, and under it a human voice calling orders in the flat tone of someone who has done this many times and been paid well for it.

"They're ahead of us too!" Dominic gasped. Light flickered from a side passage—the smugglers' lamps, being doused or seized. "Petra—"

"Petra sold the location or Petra died refusing to," Marguerite said—in the old tongue, forgetting, and Dominic understood her anyway, which surprised them both so much they nearly stopped running.

The passage forked. Left, the air moved—openings somewhere, maybe the cistern grates. Right, the air was dead and the dark was total, but the ember leaned right, pulled, *yearned*, and Marguerite had learned in her short violent life that when the fire wanted something, the fire usually knew why.

"Left," Dominic panted. "Air means exit—"

But the hounds' voices were already in the left passage, and the ember tugged right like a hand on hers, and Marguerite made the choice the way children make choices: completely.

She pulled him right, into the dark that breathed, and behind them the light of pursuit swept past the fork and away—and ahead, impossibly, faintly, the darkness began to glow. Not the ember. Something older. Carved into the tunnel wall, lines of script waking one by one as she approached, letters in the pre-kingdom tongue lighting like coals under breath:

**THE FLAME REMEMBERS ITS ROAD.**

Dominic read them aloud in a whisper, translating as much for himself as for her, and his voice shook.

"Marguerite," he said. "This tunnel wasn't dug by smugglers."

Ahead, the dark opened into a vastness neither of them had imagined beneath the city—and somewhere inside it, something that had waited eleven years for a warm-blooded visitor began, softly, to sing.
