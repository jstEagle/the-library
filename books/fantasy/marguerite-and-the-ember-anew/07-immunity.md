## 7. Immunity

The cellar beneath the salt merchant's ruined warehouse smelled of brine and old secrets, and Dominic had never loved a room more.

They had been down there three days. Three days of candle stubs and hard bread, of Marguerite chattering in the old tongue to the ember in her chest as though it were a fussy hen, of listening for boots on the floorboards above that never quite came—and then, on the third evening, did.

He knew the tread before he heard it. That was the terrible thing about Vellamare: after twelve years you learned the city's footsteps the way a mother learns her children's breathing. These were Wardens' boots—paired, measured, ash-grey cloaks whispering against stone—and there were too many of them for any honest purpose.

"Marguerite." He kept his voice low and pleasant, the voice he used for suggesting the queen's soup needed another pinch of thyme. "The back wall. Behind the salt casks. There's a gap."

She looked at him with those ember-lit eyes, and for once she didn't argue. Small mercies. The girl had argued about everything else—the bread, the dark, the indignity of hiding while grown men with torches stomped around overhead like the world's angriest dance troupe.

The gap was barely wide enough for a child. It was not wide enough for a man of forty-one who enjoyed his dinners.

"Go," he said. "Straight through, up the steps, left at the broken shrine. Old Sefa's priestess is expecting—" *was* expecting, before the betrayal in the tunnels, before Mother Cerys had tried to pry the ember from Marguerite's chest with hands that shook with wanting. He amended: "Left at the broken shrine. Keep going until you smell woodsmoke and fish. A woman named Odile will feed you. Tell her the taster sends his compliments and his debt."

"You come too." It wasn't a question in her mouth, but it wasn't quite a command either. She was learning the imperial tense of the old tongue, all imperatives and certainty.

"Someone has to convince them the cellar's empty," Dominic said. "It's what I'm for. I'm very convincing when I'm being nobody."

Above them, something heavy struck the trapdoor. Once. Twice. Splinters sang.

Marguerite grabbed his sleeve with both hands, and the ember flared under her skin—not burning her, never burning her, but lighting her from within like a lantern behind parchment. In that light he could see how frightened she was, and how furious, and how much older than eight years old she had decided to be.

"In the old words," she said, "we do not leave our fire-tenders."

"I'm not your fire-tender, little one. I'm a soup enthusiast with a criminal allergy to chains."

The trapdoor burst.

* * *

They came down in a flood of grey and lamplight, six of them, led by a young Warden with a face like a disappointed ledger. Not Captain Vane—Dominic felt a wild, absurd flicker of relief at that, followed immediately by the understanding that Vane's absence meant she was somewhere worse, doing something smarter.

"The girl," said the ledger-faced one. "Step away from the child."

Dominic stepped *toward* him instead, arms spread, wearing the smile he'd polished across twelve years of palace kitchens—the smile of a man whose only crime was an opinion about saffron.

"Gentlemen. Thank the saints. You've found me." He let his knees wobble artistically. "I came down here following a rat. A large rat. Possibly a dog. It stole a wheel of my best cheese, and I intend to have words with it."

The Wardens looked at him the way men look at furniture that has unexpectedly spoken.

"The girl," the ledger repeated.

"What girl?" And here Dominic performed his masterpiece, the role of a lifetime, the part he had rehearsed in every mirror of his mind since the night the edicts fell: the harmless man. The forgettable man. He let his face go pleasantly vacant, his shoulders round, his whole self shrink toward the corners of the room. "I'm Dominic Sterling. Royal food-taster. Third cellar from the palace, if you ever need anything tasted—I'm told I have a remarkable palate."

"He smells of herbs," said a Warden near the stairs, uncertainly. "Strange ones."

"I season my soup," Dominic agreed solemnly. "Revolutionary, I know."

And it might have worked. It should have worked; it had always worked. But then the youngest Warden—a boy really, no older than nineteen, holding his lantern too high—said, "Sir. The casks. There's a gap behind the casks, and it's warm. Feel the air coming through. It's *warm*."

Warm. Of course it was warm. The ember warmed everything it loved, and it loved Marguerite, and she had not yet learned that love leaves footprints.

Ledger-face pushed past Dominic and put his hand flat against the gap in the stones. His expression changed by exactly one degree, which on a face like his constituted open weeping.

"Seal the yard," he said. "She's in the walls."

* * *

Here is what no one in Vellamare knew about Dominic Sterling, because knowing it would have required anyone in Vellamare to think about Dominic Sterling at all:

For twelve years, every meal set before the royal table had passed first through his body. And for twelve years, on every single day of those years, he had taken something small and poisonous into himself—not enough to kill, never enough even to sicken visibly, just enough to teach his blood its lessons. A leaf of foxglove in his morning tea. Three grains of arsenic dissolved in his supper wine. Hemlock on feast days, out of respect for tradition. The palace physicians called him blessed with a cast-iron constitution. The cooks called him lucky. Only Dominic knew that luck was a discipline, that immunity was built the way a wall is built—one small, deliberate brick at a time, laid in secret, mortared with fear.

He had done it so that if someone poisoned the queen, he would taste it and live long enough to say so.

He had never imagined he would use it for this.

But the Wardens were sealing the yard, and Marguerite was in the walls with perhaps ten minutes of head start and the sure instinct of a hunted animal, and the Wardens would follow the warmth, and warmth could be tracked faster than darkness. Unless—

Unless the Wardens were suddenly, urgently, catastrophically needed elsewhere.

Dominic drifted toward the shelf where the salt merchant had once stored his accounts, and behind the ledgers, where he'd cached supplies on their first night, sat the little clay pot he had prepared with hands that shook only slightly: powdered oleander, concentrated past all sense or mercy, scraped and rendered over two sleepless nights from the ornamental shrubs in the warehouse yard. Enough to drop a horse. Enough, if his blood remembered its lessons perfectly, to drop *him*—but slowly, messily, loudly, and for just long enough.

"Sir?" The young Warden again, watching him with lantern-light in his eyes. "You shouldn't touch that. Step back from—"

"Oh, this?" Dominic held up the pot, and gave them all one last look—not the vacant smile, not the furniture-face, but something older, something with fire in it, the face of a man who had once sworn oaths in a language the kingdom pretended was dead. "This is mine. I've been keeping it for twelve years."

He drank it down like the last toast at a friend's wedding.

* * *

Poison, it turns out, is a poor actor. It does not take direction well.

Dominic had planned to collapse gracefully, to convulse with theatrical restraint, to buy his five minutes and fade. What actually happened was that his body took the oleander as a personal insult and began, immediately, to die in earnest. His heart stuttered like a cart on a cobbled hill. The floor came up and introduced itself to his cheek. Somewhere above him, voices cracked into panic—*"the taster's swallowed something, he's swallowing his tongue, get the captain, get a physicker"*—and the seal on the yard broke apart as men ran for help that would not arrive in time, because Dominic had made very sure of that.

Good, he thought, from a great distance, lying in his own spreading shadow. Good. Run along. Every eye on me is an eye off her.

The pain was extraordinary. He would write home about it, if he had a home, or hands, or a pulse worth the name. His heart was trying to decide between galloping and stopping, and it kept changing its mind mid-stride, and each change rang through him like a bell being murdered.

Twelve years, he thought. Twelve years of small poisons, small charms, small surrenders. All of it so that now, at the end, his cowardice could be spent like coin. Let them find the body. Let them waste hours on a dead food-taster while a living ember walked out through the fish-smoke to Odile's kitchen. Let his death be the last charm he ever cast: the charm of being exactly where they were looking.

He was almost proud of himself. He was also, he noticed with detached interest, dying anyway. The tolerance bought minutes, not miracles. The bell in his chest was slowing. The dark was polite, and patient, and coming closer, and somewhere in it a woman's voice from long ago said *you swore, little tendril, you swore by the flame*—

And then the dark caught fire.

Warmth poured into him—not gentle, not at first; it was like being seized by summer itself, like being scolded by the sun. Light bled through his closed eyelids, gold and furious, and a small furious voice was saying old words over him, rapid and ragged, the words Emberfolk mothers said over fevered children, the words he hadn't heard since the night the villages burned.

His eyes opened.

Marguerite knelt over him, both hands pressed flat against his chest, and the ember was *out*—out of hiding, out of her skin, blazing between her palms like a captive sunrise. She was crying and cursing at him in two languages simultaneously, and the curse in the old tongue, he noted hazily, was anatomically inventive.

"You *drank* it," she sobbed. "You drank the bad plant. You *idiot*. Fire-tenders don't get to *leave*."

"I told you," Dominic managed. His voice sounded like gravel learning to sing. "I'm not your fire-tender."

"Then what are you?" she demanded, and the ember flared, and under its light he saw her clearly—saw the truth he'd been dodging since the knock at his door. She wasn't asking what his job was. She was asking what he *was*, in the way the old faith asked it: what burned in you, what were you willing to be spent for.

"A fool," he said. "A very well-seasoned fool. Apparently one who doesn't die when he ought to."

The ember sank back into her chest, and warmth stayed behind in him like a guest who refused to leave, knitting what the oleander had unpicked. His heart found its rhythm—stumbled, caught, held. Above them, boots thundered away toward the yard, toward the story of a poisoned taster, and Marguerite had slipped back through the wall-gap during the chaos and sealed herself beside him, which meant the escape had failed and the plan had failed and everything had gone precisely wrong.

Except that he was alive, which had not been part of any plan at all.

"Why did you come back?" he whispered.

She wiped her nose on her sleeve with tremendous dignity. "In the old words," she said, "we do not leave our fire-tenders." A pause. "You're my fire-tender. I decided."

Somewhere beyond the walls, a horn sounded—three notes, rising, the signal Dominic had dreaded all his life: *found, found, found.* But it wasn't sounding for the warehouse. It was sounding from the direction of the palace, distant and wrong, and beneath it ran a murmur rolling through the city like a tide, voices lifting in the streets, hundreds of them, thousands, speaking words that predated kingdoms.

Marguerite tilted her head, listening, and her eyes went wide and bright.

"They're awake," she breathed. "The words are waking up. And the queen—" She looked at Dominic, and the ember lit her from inside, and she smiled the oldest smile in the world. "The queen wants to see the girl who carries the flame."

Boots returned on the stairs. This time, there was no gap wide enough, and no poison clever enough, and nowhere left in Vellamare to be nobody.

Dominic took the child's hand, and stood up to meet them.
