## 8. The Queen's Table

The cage is not a cage. That is what they keep saying.

*It is a litter,* says the woman who walks beside it. *It is an honor.* The litter has bars woven of silvered ash-wood, and the ash-wood hums the way a struck bell hums after the sound has gone, and Marguerite puts her palm flat against it and feels the wood *remember being a tree*, which no honest cage would do, but she does not say so because her mouth is full of the taste of rope and her hands are bound in silk, which is worse than rope, because rope at least admits what it is.

They carry her up through Vellamare like something fragile and stolen. She sees the city in slices between the bars: gulls, laundry, a boy selling candied chestnuts who stares at her until his mother pulls him away. The ember in her chest leans toward each slice of sky like a cat at a window.

*Dominic,* she thinks, and the ember flickers, and she thinks it again, harder, the way you press a bruise to be sure it is yours: *Dominic Dominic Dominic.*

The ember gives back nothing but warmth and a smell of thyme, which is how she knows he is alive. Dead men do not smell of thyme. She holds that smell in her nose like a coin held in a fist.

* * *

The palace swallows them whole.

She is washed by three silent women who treat her mud-caked skin as though it were a rare fruit to be polished. They comb out knots with fingers gentler than any fingers she has known since the burning, and this makes her angrier than roughness would have, because kindness from captors is a trick with too many teeth. One woman hums while she works — a low, looping tune — and Marguerite goes still as a hare.

It is an Ember tune. A lullaby about the river carrying the coals downstream so the fire could sleep safe under the water. Her mother sang it wrong, on purpose, changing the words to be about soup, and baby-Marguerite had laughed every time.

"What is that song," Marguerite says, and her voice comes out rusty, a door unopened for days.

The woman stops humming. "An old thing," she says. "My grandmother's." And she will not look at Marguerite, and she hums no more, and Marguerite understands two things at once: the song survived, and the woman is afraid of having sung it.

So the old faith did not die in the burning. It went quiet. It hid in grandmothers' mouths and kitchen humming, the way Dominic hides in plainness. The whole city is a banked fire pretending to be cold.

The thought warms her all the way down to where the ember lives.

* * *

They bring her to a small room, not a throne room, which surprises her. She had imagined gold and shouting. Instead there is a round table, a fire (a real one, ordinary, crackling in a hearth like any hearth), and a woman sitting alone with a bowl of soup.

The queen of Vellamare looks tired. That is Marguerite's first thought, and her second, and her third. Tired the way a person is tired after carrying something heavy up many stairs and then discovering there are more stairs. Her crown is a thin band, almost apologetic. Her hands, wrapped around the bowl, are work-worn at the thumbs.

"Sit," the queen says. "Please. I've had them take off the bindings. If you run, you run; my guards are outside the door and very bored, and I am not."

Marguerite does not sit. "Where is the taster?"

"Straight to it. Good." The queen sets down her spoon. "I don't know. My Wardens are not in the habit of telling me things lately. Captain Vane especially. She has developed a habit of looking at me as if I owe her money." A pause. "You care for him. The taster."

"He smells of thyme," Marguerite says, as if this explains everything, and perhaps it does, because the queen nods slowly, the way people nod at true things.

"Sit," the queen says again. "Eat. I know you're hungry. You've been hungry since the village, haven't you? It's in your face. Hunger that's been there so long it stopped complaining."

And that is exactly right, and being seen that exactly right makes Marguerite's eyes sting, so she sits, mostly to hide her face behind a bowl.

The soup is white bean and rosemary, and it is the best thing she has ever eaten, and she hates it a little for that.

* * *

The queen watches her eat for a while. Then she ladles her own bowl, lifts a spoonful, and blows on it, and Marguerite sees it before it happens — sees the steam curl wrong, sees the queen's nostrils flare, sees the woman go still as a pond in winter.

"You carry something," the queen says quietly. "In the food. In the air. In *you*. I can taste—" She sets down the spoon. Her hand is shaking, just slightly. "I have tasted everything served at this table for thirty years, child, and I have never tasted anything like what is standing in my room."

"I don't put anything in your soup," Marguerite says. "That's Dominic's job."

The queen laughs — a short, startled bark, quickly swallowed. "Yes. So I've begun to suspect. Twelve years of soups that never once made me ill, in a palace where three kings died of poisoning in a single century. Twelve years of luck with a face on it." She looks into the fire. "I told myself he was simply careful. I told myself a great many things."

"Are you going to burn me?" Marguerite asks, because the not-knowing is worse than anything, and the ember in her chest flares hot enough that she feels it in her teeth. "The Wardens want to. The tall one with the gray eyes wants to most of all, but even she wants to *ask first*, which is different."

"No one is burning anyone at this table." The queen says it firmly, like a law being passed. Then, softer: "Though I understand why you'd ask. We taught you to expect fire. That was our doing. Ours specifically — the crown's. And I find, now that the truth is sitting across from me eating my soup, that I cannot bear the lie anymore. It is heavy, and I have carried it up many stairs."

Marguerite stops eating. The ember leans forward inside her ribs like a listener at a door.

"The histories say magic was outlawed because the first king feared it," the queen says. "That he built the Ashen Wardens to protect the realm from wildfire and witchcraft and the old blood-sacrifices. Every schoolchild learns it. I learned it. I *taught* it." She turns her bowl slowly, around and around. "But the oldest records — the ones locked below, the ones only the monarch may read — tell it differently. There was no fear, child. There was theft."

The fire in the hearth pops. Outside, a gull cries once and falls silent, as if listening.

"The first king was not an Ember-sworn man," the queen continues. "He was the Ember-sworn man's *brother*. The keeper of the flame in those days kept it for all the people — it belonged to no one, it was tended in common, it answered to every hearth equally. And the brother wanted it. Not its power, at first. Its *permanence*. He watched the flame pass from hand to hand, generation to generation, belonging to everyone and therefore to no one, and he thought: a thing that belongs to everyone can be taken from everyone. He stole it. He sealed it in himself, and when it began to burn him — because it was never meant to live in one body, one bloodline, one *throne* — he outlawed the faith that could have told everyone what he'd done. Burned the songs. Burned the tongues. Burned the people who remembered." Her voice frays at the edges. "Not fear. Shame. We outlawed magic so that no one would ever look at the crown and see the theft still glowing underneath."

Marguerite's hands have gone cold around the warm bowl. "The ember in me—"

"Is the flame's answer," the queen says. "Or its remainder. Or its memory — the scholars argued even then. When the first king died, the flame tore itself free of his line and scattered, and the crown has spent nine generations hunting the pieces, calling it protection, calling it *culling heresy*." She meets Marguerite's eyes at last, and her eyes are wet and furious and ancient. "We called your people monsters, and we were the monster. We wrote ourselves the heroes of a story in which we are the thief. And now the whole of it is sitting in my guest chair, thin as a wren, eating my beans."

Silence. The ordinary fire crackles on, oblivious.

Marguerite thinks of her mother's last command — *find the taster* — and of Dominic palming charms into broth for twelve years, feeding luck to the descendants of thieves without knowing why his hands knew how. She thinks: *the fire didn't scatter. It hid. It hid in kitchens and grandmothers' humming and a food-taster's careful tongue, the same way everything true hides when the powerful are ashamed.*

"You're kin," Marguerite says slowly, testing the word like a step over ice. "The crown. You're Emberfolk too. Bad ones."

The queen huffs — half laugh, half wound. "Bad ones. Yes. The worst branch of the family. The branch that stole the inheritance and then changed the locks." She rises, crosses to Marguerite, and kneels — *kneels*, the queen of Vellamare, on cold stone, crown glinting — and takes Marguerite's silk-bound hands in her worn ones.

"I cannot undo nine generations," she says. "But I can stop the hunting. I can open the doors below and let the true history walk out into the light. I can—" She stops. Her jaw tightens. "If the Wardens allow me to reign long enough to try. Which brings me to the difficulty."

"What difficulty?"

"Captain Vane read the sealed records three nights ago," the queen says. "She came to me afterward looking like a woman who had found out her whole house was built on a grave. And this morning she took a prisoner from my dungeons without my leave — a taster, charged with treason and sorcery — and she has requested an audience at dawn to discuss *the disposition of the ember-bearer*." The queen's grip tightens. "She knows what I know now, child. And Ilse Vane is the most honorable woman I have ever employed, which means she may follow the law instead of the truth, because the law is what she has instead of a heart."

Outside the small room, far below in the stone belly of the palace, a cell door clangs shut, and the sound travels up through the floors like a word spoken in the old tongue.

Marguerite stands. The ember roars up her throat, into her mouth, and when she speaks, her voice comes out doubled — a child's voice and something older beneath it, something that remembers the first hearth:

"Then at dawn," she says, "we go get him."
