## 9. Ashes at the Gate

The Wardens' stronghold had a kitchen.

This was the joke Dominic had been telling himself for three days now, ever since Marguerite was taken and he'd begun, methodically, to plan her rescue with the only weapon he had ever truly mastered: soup. The Ashen Wardens were an arm of the Crown, feared in every alley of Vellamare, and yet they still had to eat. Someone had to taste for them. And there was exactly one man in the city whose tongue they trusted without thinking about it twice, because thinking was precisely what twelve years of careful forgettability had trained out of them.

*Dominic Sterling,* he reminded himself, buttoning his coat before the stronghold's iron gate. *Royal food-taster. Harmless. Forgettable. A man who smells faintly of thyme.*

Except that today his hands smelled of something no apothecary sold, and today he intended to be remembered.

He carried a covered basket on his arm—cheese pastries, honeyed pears, a flask of spiced wine—and a letter bearing the palace seal that he had forged over two sleepless nights with a steadiness that would have made his old master weep with pride or horror. *By order of Her Majesty: refreshment for the prisoner's keepers.* It was even true, in its way. He had heard the queen's own voice through the kitchen door two nights ago, giving orders that the girl be fed well and gently, and something in the way she'd said it had sounded less like custody than like penance.

But penance could wait. First, breakfast.

"Halt," said the gate warden, a young man with a jaw like a shovel and the eyes of someone who had never once been surprised by anything.

"Palace kitchens," Dominic said, holding up the letter. "For the Wardens on night rotation. Captain Vane's orders." This was a lie. Ilse Vane had given no such orders. But Ilse Vane also hadn't slept in three days—he'd seen her through the scullery window of the palace, pacing—and tired captains did not audit pastry deliveries.

The young man read the letter slowly, moving his lips. Dominic waited with the patience of a man who had once stood motionless behind a serving screen for four hours while a Warden searched the pantry he was hiding in.

"Wait here."

He waited. Somewhere above, gulls argued over the harbor. Somewhere below, in a cell he refused to imagine too specifically, a small girl with mud-colored hair was either terrified or furious, and knowing Marguerite, probably both.

The warden returned. "Leave the basket."

"Captain Vane asked me to taste everything personally," Dominic said smoothly. "There was an incident last month. Poisoned figs." There had been no poisoned figs. There had been a Warden with a stomachache and a vivid imagination, but the story had spread through the stronghold like butter on warm bread, and the young man's face confirmed it had reached this gate.

"You'll taste it *here?*"

"I'll taste it here."

And so Dominic Sterling walked through the gates of the Ashen Wardens' stronghold carrying a living ember's chosen guardian in his chest—a warmth behind his ribs that had nothing to do with fear, though there was plenty of that too—and sat down in their guardroom and ate their breakfast one deliberate bite at a time.

* * *

Marguerite was not where he expected her to be.

He had planned for the cells below—the cold ones, the ones that smelled of wet stone. Instead, following the ember's pull like a compass needle, up two staircases and along a corridor lined with confiscated relics in glass cases (he tried not to look; he looked anyway; a child's carved bird, a priestess's cracked chalice), he found her in a sunlit room at the top of the eastern tower, sitting at a table laid with actual linen, eating roast chicken with her hands.

Captain Ilse Vane sat across from her, not eating, watching.

They both turned when the door opened. Marguerite's face did something complicated—relief and fury fighting for the same real estate—and she said a word in the old tongue that Dominic was fairly sure meant *idiot.*

"Probably," he agreed in the same language, and Ilse Vane went very still.

"So it's true," the captain said. She rose from the table. Up close, in daylight, she looked worse than she had through the window—hollow-eyed, gray at the temples in a way he suspected was recent. "The taster. I told myself I was imagining it. Twelve years of reports saying you're nothing, nobody, a man who tastes soup and goes home. And your hands smell of moonwort and ashroot, and no apothecary in Vellamare has stocked ashroot since the Proscription." She drew a slow breath. "I pulled the old guild registers, Sterling. Do you know what I found?"

"A very boring apprenticeship," Dominic offered.

"I found a name struck through. A boy apprenticed to a hedge-witch in the Marsh Quarter, the year of the Burning. The record says the boy died in the fires." Her hand rested on the hilt of her sword—not threateningly, he noticed. Thoughtfully. Like a woman touching an old scar. "Records lie when they're paid to. So does a face, if you practice long enough. I should know. I've practiced."

"Captain—" he began.

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't charm me. Don't make yourself small at me; I've watched you do it to everyone in that palace for years and I am *tired*, Sterling. Tired of standing in rooms where everyone lies politely." She glanced at Marguerite, who had stopped eating and was watching them both with the fierce, unblinking attention of a hawk deciding whether something was prey or weather. "Do you know why I joined the Wardens?"

"No," said Dominic honestly.

"Because when I was nine, my village caught fire, and a woman with burned hands carried me out through the smoke, and by the time I turned around to thank her she was gone, and the magistrates said no such woman existed, because magic doesn't exist, because magic is outlawed, and therefore whatever saved me was *nothing*." Ilse Vane's voice didn't shake. That was somehow worse. "I have spent twenty years hunting the thing that saved my life, because the alternative was admitting the Crown built its law on a lie. Duty is easier than doubt. I recommend it. It's very restful until someone leaves a basket of pastries at your gate and walks into your tower speaking a dead language."

The room was quiet. Below them, the city hummed. Above them, the sky did its ordinary blue.

"The queen knows," Dominic said carefully. "About the first king. About the stolen flame."

"She knows." Ilse laughed, short and bitter. "She's known for a month, since the girl arrived, since the ember—since she tasted whatever it is you people taste. She's been ordering me to feed the prisoner roast chicken and open windows and *be kind*, as if kindness could un-burn a thousand villages." She looked at her own hands. "I have warrants signed in my name, Sterling. For the girl. For whoever harbored her. They expect an execution within the week. The Council wants a spectacle. Fire for the fire-bringers—it plays well in the squares."

"And what do you want?" Marguerite asked suddenly, in accented but clear Common Tongue, and both men turned to look at her. She set down the chicken bone with great ceremony. "You want. What?"

Ilse Vane opened her mouth. Closed it. Dominic watched twenty years of duty grind against one burned-handed memory, and he understood—with the particular clarity of a man standing on a ledge—that whatever happened next would happen in the next ten seconds.

What happened next was the bell.

Not the stronghold's bell. The palace bell. Then another, and another, ringing across Vellamare in the pattern that every citizen knew in their bones and prayed never to hear: *fire in the palace. Fire in the palace.*

Through the tower window, they all saw it together. Smoke rising from the palace spire—but wrong smoke, gold-edged, alive, climbing against the wind like a thing with intentions. And beneath it, spreading outward through the streets of Vellamare, small lights. Candle-flames in windows bending toward the palace. Hearth-fires standing up straight and singing, if fire could sing. Lanterns flaring green-gold in the harbor fog.

The ember's kin, answering.

"It's not burning," Marguerite said, standing, pressing her palm flat against the glass. In her chest, visible now through the fabric of her shirt, a glow like a coal wrapped in silk. "It's *calling*. It's calling everyone who forgot."

"They'll send soldiers," Ilse said automatically. "They'll—"

"They'll panic," Dominic said. "Panic kills more people than fire ever has. Captain. Ilse." He used her first name deliberately, watching her flinch. "You can arrest us both right now and deliver your spectacle, and the Council will burn a child in front of a city full of reawakened hearths, and you know—I think you *know*—what happens then. Or you can walk down those stairs ahead of us, sword drawn, and tell the garrison the prisoner is in your custody, and take us out through the gate under your own authority. Duty, Ilse. You wanted duty. Here it is. Choose which master it serves."

The silence stretched. Down in the courtyard, shouting. The gold smoke climbed higher, beautiful and terrible, and somewhere in the city a church bell began to ring in a rhythm that had been illegal for twelve years and that half the population apparently still remembered by heart.

Marguerite crossed the room and took Ilse Vane's hand—the sword-hand, the killing hand—and pressed it flat against her own small chest, against the warm coal-light glowing through the linen.

The captain made a sound like a woman being forgiven for something she hadn't confessed to yet.

"My village," she whispered. "The woman who carried me. She had light like this. Under her skin."

"She remembers you," Marguerite said simply. "Embers remember everyone they've warmed. She told the others. They've been waiting for you to come home for twenty years, and you kept arresting their grandchildren instead, and they were *very patient*, but honestly, Captain, it was starting to get rude."

A laugh broke out of Ilse Vane—startled, cracked, real—and she wiped her eyes with the back of her sword-hand like a soldier, because that was what she was, and always would be, and perhaps that was all right.

"Gate," she said hoarsely, drawing her sword and pointing it, absurdly, at the door. "Both of you. You're my prisoners. Walk."

They walked. Dominic took Marguerite's hand, and the ember's warmth flowed up his arm and settled behind his ribs where his fear had been living rent-free for twelve years, and evicted it.

At the top of the stairs he paused, one last time, and looked back at the little sunlit room, at the abandoned chicken, at the glass cases full of dead people's treasures. He thought about the boy in the guild register, struck through, declared dead in the fires. He had mourned that boy for twelve years from inside a borrowed name.

Behind him, the palace spire blazed gold against the morning, and the whole city of Vellamare turned its face upward to watch.

"Sterling?" Ilse called from below, impatient.

"That's not my name," Dominic said—to her, to the city, to the boy in the register—and followed the light down.
