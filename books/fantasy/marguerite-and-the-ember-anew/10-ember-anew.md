## 10. Ember Anew

The chains were lighter than Dominic had expected.

That was the first thing he noticed, standing in the ash-strewn courtyard of the Warden stronghold with the palace still smoldering behind him like a candle someone had forgotten to pinch out. Twelve years he had imagined this moment—imagined iron on his wrists, imagined the weight of a name spoken aloud in a place where names were currency and his had always been counterfeit. He had imagined it heavy. It was not heavy at all. It was almost kind.

"Kneel," said the young Warden beside him, who could not have been more than nineteen and whose hands shook as she worked the manacles.

"I've been kneeling my whole life," Dominic said pleasantly. "It's standing up that frightens me."

She blinked at him. Somewhere across the courtyard, fire climbed the eastern spire of the palace in slow golden ribbons—not devouring, not raging, but *climbing*, the way ivy climbs, the way morning climbs a hillside—and half the Wardens had turned to watch it, their faces lit orange, their expressions doing something complicated between horror and homesickness.

Dominic understood that expression. He had worn it for twelve years.

* * *

Marguerite stood at the top of the stronghold steps with the ember cupped in both hands, and for the first time since the burning village, since the woman's last command, since the mud and the running and the door that opened onto a small man with careful eyes—since all of it—she was not afraid.

The ember was not afraid either. She could feel it now, properly feel it, the way you feel your own heartbeat: not as something *in* her but as something *of* her. It had always been hers. That was the thing nobody had told her, the thing the old woman had died trying to say in words too old for kingdoms: it was never a burden to carry. It was a birthright to open.

Below her, they were putting chains on the taster.

She watched him kneel in the courtyard, this small, forgettable man who had hidden her under his floorboards and fed her honeyed porridge and taught her three words of Vellamarese—"bread," "no," and "run"—and she felt something crack open in her chest that was not grief, though grief lived there too, in the room next door, as it always would.

He had told her last night, by the light of the thing in her chest, what he meant to do. He had explained it the way he explained everything: plainly, without drama, while pretending to be very busy with a kettle.

"The ember answers to its own," he'd said. "But it can be *given*. There's a difference between carrying a flame and being handed one. The first is an accident. The second is a covenant." He had looked at her then, really looked, the way he almost never let himself look at anything. "I'm going to give it to you. And then I'm going to stand up in front of them and be seen. For once in my miserable, well-seasoned life."

"Why?" she had asked, in the old tongue, because some questions were too big for new words.

And he had smiled—that dry, crooked smile—and answered in the old tongue too, badly, his accent a crime against her people, and said: "Because a name is just a fire you keep alive by saying it out loud."

* * *

Captain Ilse Vane came down the steps herself.

Dominic heard her boots before he saw her—he had spent twelve years learning to identify people by their footsteps, cooks by their keys, wardens by their weight—and when he looked up, her face was the same face it had been in the kitchen, three days and one lifetime ago, when she had offered him his name back in exchange for a child.

"You could have run," she said.

"Yes."

"You had tunnels. You had contacts. You had twelve years of practice at disappearing." She crouched so they were eye to eye, and up close he could see what he had suspected in the kitchen: the grief under the discipline, worn smooth as river stone from years of handling. "Why didn't you?"

"Because disappearing is how I lost everything the first time," Dominic said. "I got very good at being no one, Captain. And then a child knocked on my door speaking a language I'd sworn off, holding a fire older than your crown, and I realized—I hadn't been safe all these years. I'd been *absent*. There's a difference, and it turns out the difference burns."

Ilse looked at him for a long moment. Behind her, the spire-fire reached its crown and bloomed there, settling into the shape of something almost like a tree, almost like a hearth, almost like a hand held open.

"You know what the law says," she said quietly. "Death for the Ember-sworn. Death for the harboring of them. My own oath says it. I wrote my name under it."

"I know."

"And you know what I found in the archives, after the palace burned? After the queen—" Ilse stopped. Her jaw worked. "After Her Majesty opened the vault beneath the throne room and showed us what the first king stole. You know what I found?"

"Tell me."

"That every record of the outlawry was signed by men who were dead within a year, and every record since has been signed by men too frightened to read the originals." She exhaled slowly. "The law isn't a wall, taster. It's a curtain. And curtains are for hiding things, not for keeping them out."

She rose. She turned to the young Warden with the shaking hands.

"Strike the manacles," she said.

"Captain—the law—"

"The law," Ilse said, "is currently on fire, and good riddance to it. Strike the manacles. Then fetch me ink and paper, because somebody in this city is going to have to write down what actually happened tonight, and apparently it's going to be me." She paused, and something almost like humor crossed her face, brief and dry as a match. "God help the realm. I was always better at reports than mercy."

* * *

They gave him exile instead of death. Exile, and a horse, and a bag of provisions that Dominic strongly suspected contained better cheese than the provisions given to actual free men, because Ilse Vane did nothing halfway except forgiveness, which she did entirely.

At the north gate, where the road left the smoke of Vellamare behind and climbed into country that smelled, blessedly, of rain and green things, Marguerite was waiting.

So were others.

They came out of the hedgerows and the hollows as the sun went down—a dozen at first, then more, faces he knew from wanted-posters and faces he didn't, Emberfolk who had spent twelve years folded into the world's corners exactly as he had, bakers and midwives and one extremely unrepentant smuggler who winked at him from atop a cart. Word traveled faster than fire. Word had always traveled faster than fire; that was the whole trouble with it.

Marguerite stood in the middle of them, and the ember sat above her open palms, steady as a star.

"It wants to go home," she said—in Vellamarese, carefully, the way he had taught her, and then again in the old tongue, easily, the way she had been born to. "All of them want to go home. That's what it's been trying to say. Not to burn the city. To call the scattered ones back."

Dominic dismounted, which took longer than it used to, on account of the chains having recently come off wrists that had forgotten they were wrists.

"Then I suppose," he said, "you'll be needing someone to teach you the words properly. Because I'll tell you frankly, child, my accent is a war crime, but my grammar is impeccable, and grammar is the difference between a prayer and a sneeze."

Marguerite laughed—the bright, startling, wholly unreformed laugh of a feral child who had once bitten a Warden and would absolutely do it again—and held out her free hand.

He took it.

* * *

The hillside was far from Vellamare. Farther than Dominic had ever been, which, considering he had once tasted soup for a living in the most paranoid kitchen in the realm, was saying something. It was green the way places are green when no one has ever thought to outlaw anything there, and in the evenings the wind came up the valley smelling of woodsmoke and wild thyme, and nobody flinched at the smell, which had taken Dominic a full season to stop doing.

There were children now. That was the thing that undid him daily, gently, the way water undoes stone. Children sitting in the grass in a loose ring, cross-legged, solemn, fidgeting, while Marguerite—who was perhaps eleven years old and had the gravity of a duchess and the table manners of a wolf—taught them the old words.

"*Aeth-vaer,*" she said, holding up a handful of dry grass. "Say it. *Aeth-vaer.* It means 'small fire,' but it also means 'the part of you that stays awake when everything else sleeps.' Old tongue is like that. Everything means two things. Sometimes three."

"What's the third?" asked a gap-toothed boy named Perrin.

"Whatever you need it to mean," Marguerite said. "That's the third meaning of everything."

Dominic sat a little apart, as he always did, mending a cloak or shelling beans or pretending to read, close enough to hear, far enough to be forgotten—which was, he had discovered, different from invisible. Invisibility was a wound. Being forgotten on purpose, among people who remembered you the moment you spoke, was a choice. He was still learning the difference. He expected to be learning it for the rest of his life, which was, at last, a life worth the tuition.

"Aeth-vaer," the children chanted, mangling it beautifully.

From the ridge above, Ilse Vane watched—she came every few weeks, ostensibly to "inspect the settlement," actually to eat Dominic's cooking and complain about paperwork and hold her hands near the children's little fires without ever quite warming them, as if permission were a language she was still translating. Today she had brought news: the queen's edict, posted in every city, rescinding the outlawry in the queen's own hand, with a line at the bottom that had made grown Wardens weep in their barracks.

*What was stolen is returned. What was buried is kindled. What was ours is ours again.*

"She signs it 'Maren Ember-daughter,'" Ilse called down. "The whole court thinks she's mad. Half the court is crying anyway. It's very confusing for everyone."

"Tell Her Majesty," Dominic called back, "that the soup recipe she liked is enclosed, and that the secret ingredient was never poison."

"It was love, wasn't it," Ilse said flatly.

"It was saffron. But yes, also that."

Below, Marguerite clapped her hands, and the children fell quiet, and she lifted the ember high above the ring of small upturned faces so that its light fell on all of them equally, the way evening falls, the way rain falls, the way belonging falls on a person who has finally stopped ducking.

"Now," she said, in the old tongue, in the new tongue, in the plain and everlasting tongue of children everywhere, "*all together*—"

And forty voices on a green hillside, none of them perfect, all of them alight, said the word for fire.

And the word, as it always had, and as it always would, answered.
