## 1. The Date That Was Written

The lamp above Edmund Reyes's desk had a habit of guttering at the third hour, and he had long ago stopped flinching when it did. Fifteen years in the temple archive of Vhalen taught a man that most things which seemed like omens were only drafts moving through old stone. He trimmed the wick with the small brass scissors chained to his wrist—everything in the temple was chained, inkwells and censers and scribes—and returned his attention to the ledger before him.

*Grain tithes, district of Marrow's Bend, autumn assessment.* The abbot's hand had grown unsteady in his final years, and someone had to render it legible for the chancery. It was work for hands, not minds, which was precisely why they gave it to Edmund. A man who thought too much about grain tithes was a man with time on his mind, and time was the one commodity the temple had never allowed him to own. His debt bought every hour he sat at this desk. Fourteen thousand marks, compounded quarterly, against the cost of the schooling they'd given him, the roof over his head, the ink that stained his fingers. He had done the arithmetic once, early on, and never done it again. Some sums were better left as fog.

The archive stretched around him like the ribcage of some enormous patient beast. Shelves rose three stories into shadow, ladders on iron rails, the smell of vellum and lamp oil and the particular sweetness of paper that had been loved by too many hands. Somewhere above, another scribe coughed. Somewhere below, the night brothers were singing compline, their voices worn smooth as river stones by centuries of repetition. Edmund knew all the sounds of this place the way a prisoner knows the creak of his cell door: intimately, uselessly.

He copied. *Twelve sacks milled barley. Eight sacks oats, spoiled by damp, deducted.* His pen moved in its practiced rhythm, and the rhythm carried him the way prayer was supposed to carry the faithful, though Edmund had privately concluded years ago that copying ledgers was a form of prayer anyway—one addressed to no one, answered by nothing, performed until the hands could do it dreaming.

It was the fourth hour when the archivist's boy came down the ladder with the misfiled volume.

"Brother Anselm says this came up in the restoration cart," the boy said, setting it down with both hands as if it might bite. "Sealed shelf. He wants you to log it back where it belongs."

Edmund looked at the book and felt, for one unaccountable moment, the way he imagined a man feels when a stranger on the street calls him by name.

It was bound in grey leather gone green at the corners, the kind of binding that meant age without meaning value. No title on the spine. A wax seal—the old seal, the seven-pointed star of the founding era, broken and resealed so many times the wax had built up into a pale crust, like scar tissue. Prophecy books were kept on the sealed shelves because prophecy was dangerous in the way water is dangerous to a drowning man: not because of what it is, but because of what people do to reach it.

"Log number?" Edmund asked.

"Anselm didn't say. He said you'd know."

Anselm, then, was being lazy, or testing him, or both. Edmund sighed, took up the register, and opened the book to find its colophon—every book had one, a signature or date or scribal note that identified it in the catalogue.

The pages fell open on their own. Old bindings did that; the spine remembered where it had been held most often. But the page they opened to was not a colophon.

It was a page of verse, written in a hand so fine it looked printed, the letters tall and narrow as rain:

*When the ember sleeps and the wardens rot,*
*a debt unpaid shall bind the knot.*
*On the day the ledger's line runs true,*
*the fire will come to claim its due.*

Below the verse, occupying the lower half of the page, was a drawing.

Edmund had seen ten thousand drawings in fifteen years of archival work—saints and dragons and genealogical trees and architectural plans for chapels that had burned before they were finished. He knew how faces were drawn in old books: schematic, symbolic, the features of types rather than persons. The Penitent Woman always had downcast eyes. The Warrior-Saint always had the same jaw.

This face was not schematic.

This face had a small scar through the left eyebrow, acquired at nine years old when he had fallen from the wall of the orphanage trying to see whether the temple bells really were cast from melted dragon-bells, as the older boys claimed. This face had the slight asymmetry of the nose that came from the same fall. This face had tired eyes with faint shadows beneath them, drawn with such economy—a few strokes of charcoal, smudged at the edges—that the artist had clearly worked from life, quickly, perhaps secretly.

The face was his own.

The lamp guttered again. This time Edmund flinched.

He sat very still. Outside, the compline hymn ended, and the silence that followed it seemed to lean in through the stone walls and press against his ears. He became aware of his own heartbeat, which was absurd—he had been aware of his heartbeat perhaps five times in fifteen years, and each of those times something had been genuinely wrong.

*Coincidence,* he told himself, and the word had the comfortable weight of a coin he had handled so often it had gone smooth. Fifteen years of careful living will build you a whole theology out of that one word. *Coincidence:* the scar was common enough. The sketch was generic enough, if you wanted to squint. Every tired scribe in Vhalen had shadows under his eyes. The verse mentioned a debt, and everyone in the temple was in debt; that was how the temple worked. The verse mentioned a ledger, and he was sitting in an archive full of them.

He turned the page, telling himself he would simply check the colophon, log the book, return it to the sealed shelf, and go to bed. Tomorrow was a market day. He had charms to sell.

The next page stopped his heart mid-beat.

It was not verse. It was a single line, written larger than the rest, in the same tall narrow hand—but the ink was different. Fresher. Darker. It sat on the surface of the parchment instead of sinking into it, the way ink sits when it has been applied recently, within days, within hours—

The line read: *the morrow after the feast of Saint Aldric's Chains.*

Edmund's eyes went, helplessly, to the almanac hanging beside his desk, because fifteen years of archival discipline dies hard. The feast of Saint Aldric's Chains was tomorrow.

No. Not tomorrow. *Today*, technically—it was past midnight now, past the turning of the watch bell. Which made the day named in the book...

Today.

"No," he said aloud, and his voice sounded strange in the vaulted dark, small and flat. "No, that's—" He laughed, one dry syllable with nothing behind it. "That's a trick. Someone's put you up to this. Anselm. One of the boys. They've inked a fresh line in an old book and—"

But even as he said it, he knew the objections were wrong. The fresh ink was not fresh in the way of a prank; pranks used cheap gall-ink that browned quickly. This was carbon black, expensive, the kind mixed with gum arabic that the scriptorium kept locked away for illumination work. And the hand—the hand matched the rest of the book perfectly, letter for letter, the same peculiar hooked descender on the *y*, the same cramped spacing before punctuation. Whoever wrote the new line had written the old ones. A century ago. Or the book was lying about its own age, which was worse.

And there was still the matter of the face.

Edmund looked at the sketch again, properly this time, and let himself see what he had been refusing to see. The artist had drawn him from slightly above and to the right—the angle of someone standing behind and beside a seated man. The angle of someone standing exactly where the ladder stood. Where the archivist's boy had stood, four minutes ago.

Except the book was a hundred years old. He checked the colophon, finally, and found it: *Written in the year of the Ashfall, completed under seal, to be opened only when the ledger's line runs true.*

The year of the Ashfall. One hundred and two years ago.

His hands were shaking. He noticed it distantly, the way one notices weather. He pressed them flat against the desk and made himself breathe—in for four, hold for seven, out for eight, the little discipline the infirmarian had taught him for the headaches—and when he looked up, the page was glowing.

Not brightly. Not dramatically. If anyone had walked past the end of the aisle, they would have seen nothing but a scribe at his lamp. But the parchment had taken on a warmth of light from within, a gold like the last coals of a banked fire, and the light was moving—flowing along the strokes of the letters the way dawnlight flows along a mountain ridge, filling the tall narrow characters until they seemed less written than molten. The sketch of his face began to glow too, and the glow gathered in the eyes first, two points of ember-gold, and Edmund had the sudden unbearable conviction that the face was looking back at him.

He should have called for the night brothers. That was procedure. Unregistered thaumaturgic manifestation: summon a warden, seal the shelf, file a report in triplicate. Fifteen years of careful living had procedures for everything, and every procedure existed because magic was outlawed, because magic had burned half the world once already, because the war had ended a century ago with an oath and an ashfall and a silence that the empire enforced with chains and pyres.

Instead, Edmund reached out and touched the page.

He would spend the rest of his life—not that he knew it yet—trying to explain to himself why. The closest he ever came was this: the light was warm. After fifteen years in cold stone, copying other men's numbers, the light was warm, and he was so tired of being careful, and some part of him that had never signed any contract leaned toward that warmth the way a man leans toward a window in winter.

His fingertips touched the glowing verse.

The world folded.

* * *

He stood in a city that was burning.

Not the orderly burning of a chimney fire or a torchlit riot—this was burning the way the sea is wet, total and atmospheric, fire falling from the sky in sheets, fire standing in the streets in columns fifty feet high, fire reflected in ten thousand windows so that the whole city seemed made of lanterns about to be dropped. The heat did not touch him. He understood, in the dreamlike grammar of visions, that he was not there—that he was remembering something that had happened before his grandfather's grandfather drew breath.

Dragons crossed the sky.

They came in ragged flights, some wheeling in formation and some tumbling wounded, their scales catching the firelight in colors that had names in no living language. Below them, human soldiers loosed ballistae from the walls, and great bolts trailing chain took dragons from the air like fowlers netting hawks. The sound was beyond sound. He felt it in his teeth, in the architecture of his skull.

And everywhere, underneath the roar, a voice. A woman's voice, speaking words he almost understood—words that felt like the shape of meaning without the meaning itself, dragon-tongue, the oath-tongue, rising and rising toward some culmination—

Then the vision cut, the way a bad memory cuts, and he was standing in a courtyard slick with ash, and the woman was there.

She was kneeling beside a fissure in the earth, a seam of darkness in the scorched ground, and she was young—younger than Edmund was now—with ash in her hair and blood on her mouth, and she was holding something cupped in her two hands. Something small. Something that glowed.

She looked up.

Her eyes were gold. Not amber, not honey—gold, lit from within, the exact color of the light flowing through the prophecy page, and Edmund felt recognition strike him somewhere deeper than memory, deeper than sense, in whatever room of the self is locked even from its owner.

He knew those eyes. He had never seen those eyes. Both things were true at once, and the contradiction broke something loose in his chest, and the woman spoke—not in dragon-tongue now, but in plain trade-speech, and her voice cracked with exhaustion and grief:

*"Someone must remember. Someone must be willing. Promise me the fire won't die forgotten."*

She closed her hands. The light went out. The city went out. The sky went out—

* * *

Edmund woke on the floor of the archive with his cheek against cold flagstone and the taste of smoke in his mouth, though nothing in the room was burning. The lamp had gone out. Grey pre-dawn light seeped through the high clerestory windows, the color of old pewter.

For a moment he lay still and permitted himself the fantasy that he had fallen asleep at his desk, that the whole thing was a dream stitched together from grain tithes and compline hymns. Then he pushed himself upright and saw the book.

It lay closed on the desk, neatly, square with the desk's edge. He had no memory of closing it. Beside it, his pen had been set down parallel to the register, aligned with a precision he would never have managed unconscious. And on the open page of the daily ledger—the page recording today's date, the morrow after the feast of Saint Aldric's Chains, the line running true—someone had written, in his own handwriting, in ink he did not remember dipping:

*She is waking.*

Edmund stared at the words for a long time. Around him the archive breathed its ancient breathing, shelves and shadows and sleeping paper, and far above, the morning bell began to toll, and far below, the gates of the temple grounds groaned open onto the market square, where in a few hours the stalls would rise like mushrooms after rain, and among them, as every week for two years, a charm-seller's table with a striped awning, where a woman with dark hair sometimes bought luck for a copper and never gave her name.

He did not know why he thought of her. He barely knew her—a customer, a nod, copper warmed by her hand. But the thought arrived with the same terrible certainty as the glowing page, and it would not leave.

Edmund looked at the sealed prophecy book, at the broken-and-resealed wax, at the hundred years of patience sitting on his desk like a debt called due.

Then he wrapped it in his coat, tucked it beneath his arm, and did the first careless thing he had done in fifteen years.

He took it to market.
