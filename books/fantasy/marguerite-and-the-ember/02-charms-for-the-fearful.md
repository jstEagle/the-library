## 2. Charms for the Fearful

The market of Vhalen woke the way a wounded animal wakes—slowly, one sense at a time, listening first.

Marguerite had learned to read it that way over seven years in the city: the groan of cart wheels on wet cobbles, the fishwives' voices pitched low until they were sure no soldiers lingered near the harbor gate, the smell of bread and tar and, beneath everything, the faint mineral bite of the river. By midmorning the quarter would be loud enough to lose herself in. But at dawn, when the stalls were still being unshuttered and the lamplighters walked home with their poles over their shoulders like tired spears, the city felt less like a place than like a held breath.

She moved through it the way she had taught herself to move through all things: quietly, with her hands hidden.

Her gloves were good ones this season—kid leather, dyed a brown so dark it read as black in shadow, bought secondhand from a widow on Tanner's Row who hadn't asked why a laundress wanted gloves fine enough for a merchant's daughter. Marguerite wore them even indoors. She wore them to sleep, some nights, when the heat came early. The other washerwomen thought it was vanity, or shame about her hands, which were rough from the washboards either way. Let them think it. Vanity was safe. Vanity was human.

The heat was not.

It had been waking more often lately. That was the word she used for it, privately, because *waking* implied something that had been asleep, and she preferred not to examine what that thing might be or how long it intended to stay awake once roused. It started as warmth in her palms, a pleasant warmth at first, like hands held near a hearth, and then it deepened, gathered, became the kind of heat that made the water in the washing tubs steam around her wrists if she wasn't careful. Twice last winter she'd boiled a kettle without touching the fire beneath it. Once—and she did not let herself think of this often—a candle flame had leaned toward her across a whole table, straining like a dog at its rope.

Her grandmother's voice, remembered through thirty years of forgetting: *When it stirs, girl, you make yourself small. You make yourself cold. You think of snow, of river ice, of anything that is not burning.*

So Marguerite thought of snow. She thought of the winter she was nine, before her grandmother died, before the long walk south with an aunt who never quite met her eyes. She thought of frost ferns on window glass, of breath hanging white and motionless in air too cold to move it. And the heat in her palms subsided, grumbling, the way a large animal settles when it decides, for now, not to rise.

The temple bells rang the third hour, and the market filled.

* * *

Edmund Reyes kept his stall between a knife-grinder and a woman who sold dried herbs, which was, he had told customers with a self-deprecating smile, the correct neighborhood for a charm-seller: between things that cut and things that cured, offering neither.

Marguerite had passed his stall perhaps two hundred times in seven years. She knew him the way one knows any fixture of a street—the narrow man with ink-stained fingers and a scribe's stoop already forming in his shoulders despite his youth, the little table hung with knotted cords and stamped tin medallions and vials of colored sand. Luck charms. Warding knots. Traveler's blessings. The harmless inventory of a man selling comfort by the copper piece.

She had never bought anything from him. Buying meant speaking, and speaking meant being remembered, and being remembered was a debt Marguerite paid down daily and never borrowed against.

But today the market was wrong.

She felt it before she understood it—the same animal attention that read the dawn streets now prickling along her spine. The guards had come early. Temple guards, four of them, walking the row of stalls with the unhurried menace of men whose authority required no demonstration. Their white-and-gold sashes caught the light. At each stall they paused, exchanged a word with the vendor, sometimes turned over a piece of merchandise with gloved fingers. An inspection. Or the theater of one. In Vhalen the difference rarely mattered; both ended with someone's goods confiscated or someone's name written in a ledger, and ledgers, in this city, were how people disappeared politely.

Marguerite's basket of washing was heavy on her hip. She could simply keep walking. She should keep walking.

But the heat rose anyway—not summoned, just *there*, answering the fear the way her heartbeat answered a stair climbed too fast. It pooled in her palms under the kid leather, warm, warmer, and she thought furiously of snow, of ice, of the frozen river at Bellharrow where she'd been born, and the guards were ten paces away and the nearest stall was Edmund Reyes's table of useless, beautiful, harmless charms, and her feet decided for her.

"Good morning," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "How much for the luck knot?"

He looked up.

Later, she would try to reconstruct what she saw in his face in that first instant, and she would fail, because what she saw was too familiar to be described—it was her own face, the one she wore every day, the face of a person performing calm so thoroughly that the performance had become indistinguishable from the skin beneath it. He had gray eyes and a scribe's pallor and a small scar through one eyebrow, and he looked at her the way a drowning man looks at another drowning man floating past: recognition without rescue.

"The red cord or the green?" he asked. His voice was quiet, pitched for her alone beneath the market noise.

"Does it matter?"

"Not to the knot." A ghost of a smile. "The red sells better. People trust red. Green is for travelers, but you don't look like you're going anywhere."

"I'm not," Marguerite said, and heard how much truth sat inside the small sentence, and wondered if he heard it too.

He took up a red cord and began to tie it, and that was when she saw his hands.

They were shaking. Not the fine tremor of a tired craftsman—she knew that tremor intimately, from her own arms after double shifts at the tubs—but a deep, structural shaking, the kind that comes from somewhere behind the ribs. His fingers fumbled the first pass of the knot, a knot that by the look of the hundred finished ones hanging above him he must have tied ten thousand times. He recovered. He tied it again. The shaking continued, patient as weather.

"You're hurt," she said.

"No." Too fast. Then, softer, with the careful honesty of a man rationing truth like bread in a siege: "No, I'm not hurt."

The guards were three stalls away. She could see them in her peripheral vision, white and gold, turning over a fruit-seller's scales, asking questions in the bored cadence of routine. Under her gloves, her palms had gone from warm to hot, and a bead of sweat ran down her temple that had nothing to do with the sun. She thought of snow. She thought of the frozen river. She thought, absurdly, of her grandmother's kitchen in Bellharrow, the iron stove banked for the night, the coals ticking as they cooled.

*You make yourself small. You make yourself cold.*

And then she saw the light.

It leaked from the satchel at Edmund's feet—a seam of glow between the flap and the body of the bag, thin as a knife's edge, gold-white and pulsing very slightly, like light through a shuttered door in a room where someone holds a lamp. It was not sunlight. It was not any light Marguerite had ever seen sold in a market or carried in a bag, and yet some part of her, some part older than her seven years in Vhalen, older than her grief, older than her name, recognized it the way a compass needle recognizes north.

Her heart stopped, started again, wrong.

"That's—" she began, and stopped, because finishing the sentence aloud in front of a temple guard's hearing distance was a death sentence for them both, whatever *that* was.

Edmund followed her gaze. When he saw what she saw, the color left his face entirely, and his hands went flat and still on the table, pressed down as if to hold the world in place. For a moment neither of them breathed.

Then he did something quick and practiced: he shifted his elbow, knocked a spool of cord off the table, and bent to gather it, and in the same motion dragged the satchel back against his shin, out of the light, into shadow. When he straightened, his expression had been rebuilt—apologetic, mild, a tradesman's face.

"Clumsy of me," he said. "Forgive me. The knot's finished."

He set it in her palm. Through the glove, through the leather, she felt nothing of the cord—but her own contained heat seemed to lean toward him, toward the hidden light in his satchel, the way the candle flame had leaned across the table last winter. Straining. Recognizing.

The guards arrived.

* * *

They were close enough now that she could smell the lamp oil they used on their boots, the incense that clung to their sashes. The tallest of them, a man with a jaw like a doorframe, picked up one of Edmund's warding medallions and turned it over with two fingers.

"These are blessed?" he asked.

"Blessed by no one in particular," Edmund said pleasantly. "Stamped tin and good intentions. If the temple ever rules intentions illegal, I'll be ruined."

The guard did not laugh. None of them laughed. The tall one set the medallion down with exaggerated care, the way a man sets down something he suspects might be evidence, and looked at Edmund for a long moment, and then at Marguerite.

"And you? Buying protection from the temple's reach?"

"Buying a pretty cord," Marguerite said. "For luck."

"Luck." He said the word the way a priest says the name of a rival god. Then his gaze dropped—to her gloves, of all things, to her plain washerwoman's dress and her fine kid gloves, and Marguerite watched the small arithmetic happen behind his eyes, the sum that didn't add up, the question beginning to form. Heat crawled up her throat. Somewhere beneath her sternum, the old thing turned over in its sleep and murmured, and she thought of snow, of ice, of the frozen river, *think of anything that is not burning*—

"She's late for the laundry house," Edmund said.

It was said mildly, helpfully, the tone of a neighbor covering for a neighbor, and it landed with strange precision. The guard glanced at him. Something passed between the two men that Marguerite could not read—a flicker, a negotiation conducted entirely in the space of a blink—and then the guard straightened, nodded once, and moved on, and his three companions moved with him, white and gold receding down the row of stalls like a fever breaking.

Marguerite stood very still until they were gone. So did Edmund.

Then, quietly, without looking at each other, they both exhaled.

"Well," she said.

"Yes," he said.

She should leave. Every instinct honed over seven years said *leave now, don't speak again, don't be remembered.* She picked up her basket. She put the red knot inside it, though she hadn't paid, and dug in her apron pocket for the copper.

"Keep it," Edmund said. "Consider it a gift."

"Nothing in this city is a gift."

"No," he agreed, and there was something raw under the mildness now, something exhausted. "But some things are investments. In case I'm wrong about the city."

She looked at him properly then, and he looked back, and the space between the knife-grinder and the herb-woman seemed suddenly very quiet, as though the market noise itself had stepped aside to give them room. His hands had almost stopped shaking. Almost. And the satchel at his feet was dark now, sealed, innocent—but she had seen the light, and she knew light like that. She had been raised on stories of light like that, bedtime stories her grandmother told in a language she'd been forbidden to learn, stories about fires that slept and the women who kept watch over the sleeping.

*Oath-keepers*, her grandmother had called them. *We were oath-keepers once, girl, before keeping oaths became a way to die.*

"What's in the bag?" Marguerite asked.

Edmund's mouth opened. Closed. She watched him weigh lies the way a moneylender weighs coin, testing each for false metal, and she watched him arrive—visibly, wearily—at the conclusion that she had already seen the light, and that a lie would insult them both.

"A book," he said. "An old book. From the archive where I work. It's..." His hands flattened on the table again. "It has my face in it. Drawn in ink a hundred years ago. And tomorrow's date."

The words fell into the morning like stones into a well. Marguerite heard them land, one after another, deeper than they should have gone.

Because her grandmother had owned a book like that. Burned, in the end, on her own instruction, the night before she died—*some pages are doors, girl, and doors open from both sides*—and Marguerite had spent her whole life since pretending she didn't remember the drawings she'd glimpsed in it before the flames: a flame like a seed. A city like Vhalen. A woman with eyes the color of—

She looked up. Edmund was watching her with those gray eyes, and in them was the question he was too careful to ask aloud, the question that had been building in him since she'd flinched at the guards' approach, since she'd seen the light and gone pale instead of curious:

*What are you?*

And she wanted, with a suddenness that frightened her, to answer. To say *I don't know, I've never known, I only know that my hands are hot and my grandmother burned a book and told me to be cold forever.* Seven years of silence pressed against the backs of her teeth.

Instead she said, "Come find me at the laundry house on Tanner's Row. Tomorrow evening. After your archive closes."

His eyebrows rose. "Why tomorrow?"

"Because," Marguerite said, picking up her basket, turning away, her palms burning through the kid leather like twin coals, "if your book is right about the date, we're both going to want to know what we're standing next to."

She was three steps into the crowd before she realized she had never given him her name—and that he had never asked for it, and that somehow this was the most honest transaction either of them had made in years.
