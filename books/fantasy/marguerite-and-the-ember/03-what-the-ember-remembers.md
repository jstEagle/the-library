## 3. What the Ember Remembers

The book did not want to be read.

Edmund had understood this the way one understands a sleeping dog's mood—by the set of its shoulders, so to speak, by the way the leather cover seemed to hold its breath under his fingers. He had carried it home from the archive wrapped in his spare shirt, buried beneath three ledgers of tithe records that no one would ever want to steal, and now it lay on the table in his single rented room above the chandler's shop, glowing faintly at the edges like a coal banked for the night.

He had not opened it since the archive. He had told himself he wouldn't.

But midnight came, as it does, and with it the particular honesty of a man alone with a thing he cannot explain. Tomorrow's date sat inked on a page written before his grandfather's grandfather drew breath, and beside it, rendered in faded sepia strokes that were unmistakably, impossibly his own face: the crooked nose broken by a temple tutor's ring, the thin jaw, the eyes that always looked slightly away from whatever they saw. A scribe's face. A forgettable face. Except that someone a hundred years dead had thought to remember it.

"Coincidence," Edmund said aloud, because saying things aloud made them smaller. It was a trick he'd learned in the dormitories of the temple orphanage, where fear grew fat and shapeless in the dark until you named it and gave it borders. "The date is common. The sketch is flattering to half the men in Vhalen. The glow is—" He stopped. The glow was not anything. Glow was not a category of thing books did.

His hands, he noticed, had already untied the cord.

The cover fell open to the same page, as though the spine had learned it. The prophecy text ran down the left margin in the old liturgical hand they no longer taught—the hand of oaths, the hand of excommunications—and Edmund read it the way he read everything, automatically, translating as he went:

*When the ember wakes, the vessel will be named twice: once in ink, once in blood.*

And beneath that, smaller, in a different script entirely, hurried and human:

*Do not let it look at you.*

Edmund laughed—a short, unhappy sound. Someone, a century ago, had been exactly where he was. Someone had felt this exact cold walk up their spine and had pressed their pen hard enough to tear the page.

*Do not let it look at you.*

The light from the page rose.

Not brightened—rose, the way water rises in a well when you've drawn too fast, climbing out of the paper and up into the air between him and the candle, and the candle guttered though there was no draft, and Edmund had just enough time to think *I should close it* before the room folded inward like a letter being sealed.

---

He stood in a city that was burning.

Not Vhalen. He knew Vhalen the way a scribe knows any city—by its wards and tollhouses, its gutters and its gates. This was some other place, older and grander, its towers crowned with copper that had gone green with age, and every one of those towers was pouring smoke. The sky was the color of a bruise going bad, violet shading to black, and against that sky wings moved. Enormous wings. Dragons, real ones, the kind the temple taught you were metaphors—the kind the temple insisted had never existed at all except as symbols of humanity's old errors—and they wheeled over the burning city in patterns too deliberate to be battle. They were leaving. They were circling the city once, the way mourners circle a grave, and then rising.

Ash fell like gray snow. It gathered on Edmund's sleeves and did not melt, and when he looked down at himself he found he cast no shadow, which meant he was not properly here, which was somehow worse than if he'd simply been standing in a dying city.

A bell tolled somewhere beneath the smoke. Not an alarm. Something slower, heavier—a knell, or a summons.

He followed it.

The streets were empty in the way streets are empty after everyone has either fled or died, doors hanging open, a child's shoe in a doorway with no child attached, a loaf of bread gone to birds on a windowsill. The heat came off the stones in waves. And through it all walked figures that flickered at the edges—people who were not people anymore but memories of people, translucent, repeating small final gestures. A woman setting a table for six. A man sweeping a step that would never be stepped on again. The war had ended here, Edmund understood, and these were the ones the ending had caught.

The bell led him down a stair cut into the living rock, past walls scorched in long horizontal bands, as though something enormous had breathed along them. At the bottom was a door.

Not a grand door. That was what struck him most, later, when he tried to make sense of it all: the door was plain, oak and iron, the kind found on granaries and wine cellars, unremarkable except for the heat shimmering off it and the words carved around its frame in a language that hurt to look at—not painful exactly, but *dense*, each character seeming to contain more than characters should, the way a seed contains a forest.

Before the door stood a woman.

She was young—or she looked young, which Edmund would learn was not always the same thing—with dark hair coming loose from its braid and a travel-stained coat, and she was speaking to someone Edmund could not see. Her voice carried strangely, both intimate and vast, as though the stairwell itself were her throat.

"—and I swear it knowing what swearing costs." She was saying. "Not the fire. Never the fire. But the keeping of it. Let it sleep in me and mine until the world deserves it again, and if the world never does, let it sleep forever."

Something answered her. Edmund heard nothing—no words, only a pressure, a bass note below hearing that made his teeth ache and the ash on his sleeves tremble—but the woman bowed her head as though she'd heard every syllable.

And then she turned, and looked directly at him, and Edmund stopped breathing.

Her eyes were gold. Not amber, not honey—gold, molten, lit from behind, the exact gold he had seen for one terrible instant in the market square that afternoon when a woman buying a luck charm had flinched from the temple guards and her blood had risen visible beneath her skin like sunrise under frost.

"You're the ink," the woman said. Her voice was gentle and very tired. "I wondered when you'd come."

"I don't—" Edmund's voice worked badly here, in this place that wasn't a place. "I'm a scribe. I copy ledgers. I don't know why the book—"

"The book is a door, and doors open toward whoever needs them." She smiled, and it was a kind smile, and it broke his heart a little, because it was the smile of someone carrying something too heavy who has just remembered how to put it down. "You'll want to know what happens next. Everyone does. But the ember doesn't show futures. Only endings. This is the ending. Look at it properly."

She gestured, and the burning city rose around them—not the stairwell anymore but a hill above it, a vantage, the whole catastrophe spread out below like a map made of grief. The dragons were descending now, all of them, folding their wings and lying down in the ash outside the walls, dozens of them, hundreds, great heads lowering to the ground in a posture Edmund recognized from the temple bestiaries even though the bestiaries claimed it was invented: submission. Surrender. The posture of creatures accepting terms.

"They lost," Edmund said. "Or they chose—"

"They chose to lose." The woman's gold eyes reflected the fires. "There's a difference, and the difference is everything. They could have burned the world down to its bones and won. Instead they swore the Oath of Ash: their fire goes into hiding, sealed in one last vessel, kept by oath-keepers of my line, until such time as—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. "Until such time as a vessel comes willing. That part matters. Write that part down, scribe, wherever scribes write things. *Willing.* The ember cannot be taken. Only given. Every horror that follows in your age will come from someone forgetting that."

"And you?" Edmund asked. "What happens to you?"

The woman looked at the door. The plain, cellar-plain door with its unbearable carvings.

"I go in," she said, "and I do not come out. Not as myself. The flame keeps what it takes." She turned back to him, and now her expression shifted into something urgent, almost fierce. "Listen to me. You are reading this a hundred years too late to change it, but not too late to matter. There will be two names. There are always two names—one in ink, one in blood. The ink carries the book. The blood carries the fire. And the Inquisition will come for both, because the throne was built on the ashes of this day, and if the ember ever rises again, the throne falls. Do you understand? They will not hunt you because you're dangerous. They will hunt you because you're *evidence*."

The pressure-sound came again, deeper, closer, and the door began to open—not swinging, but dissolving, its oak going soft and luminous, and beyond it was not a cellar but a light, a warmth, a presence vast and patient and old beyond reckoning, and Edmund felt it turn its attention toward him the way one turns one's head—

*Do not let it look at you.*

—he wrenched his gaze down, down to the ash at his feet, heart slamming, and the woman laughed softly, without mockery.

"Good. Quick. You might survive after all." Her voice was receding now, or he was; the hill, the city, the kneeling dragons were all going soft at the edges like wet ink. "One more thing, ink-bearer. When you wake—and you will wake, it isn't done with you yet—you'll find the blood. The other name. She'll be frightened, and she'll lie to you about what she is, because she has spent her whole life learning that what she is gets people killed. Be patient with her. She never asked for it either."

"What is her name?" Edmund shouted into the dissolving light. "Tell me her—"

The ember looked at him anyway. Just for a moment, just a fraction of its attention sliding across him like a searchlight, and in that moment Edmund understood what it was to be read—to have every page of himself turned at once, fifteen years of careful smallness flipped through by something that had watched empires be kindling—and then the world snapped shut around him like a book closing, and he fell.

---

He woke to the smell of bread and horse, which was wrong, because his room smelled of tallow and old paper.

He woke to daylight, which was worse, because he had sat down with the book at midnight.

He woke to a woman's voice saying, "Breathe. Just breathe. In through the nose. There you are," and opened his eyes to the pale blue rectangle of sky between the market square's awnings, and to Marguerite kneeling over him with her hands hovering an inch above his chest as though afraid to touch him.

The market square. He was lying in the market square, on the flagstones beside his own collapsed stall, his charms scattered around him like fallen leaves—the little tin suns, the knotted red threads, the painted river-stones promising safe passage—and a crowd was gathering at a respectful, curious distance, the way crowds do around anything that might be catching.

"How long," Edmund managed. His mouth tasted of smoke. Actual smoke, woodsmoke and something sweeter underneath, like resin. "How long was I—"

"Since dawn." Marguerite's face was pale under its freckles. "You walked into the square at first light, went to your stall, started setting it up, and then just—stopped. Mid-motion. Like a clock running down. You've been on the ground an hour. I couldn't wake you." Her hands were still hovering. They were trembling. "Your eyes were moving. Under the lids. Like you were watching something."

An hour. He had lived a hundred years in an hour, or a hundred years had lived through him, and the ash-memory of it still clung to his sleeves—he looked, absurdly, half-expecting gray flakes—and the woman's voice was still in his ears: *she'll lie to you about what she is.*

Marguerite reached for his arm to help him sit up, and her sleeve pulled back, and Edmund saw it.

The skin of her forearm was marked—no, not marked, *lit*—a tracery of fine lines glowing beneath the surface like veins of ore struck by lantern-light, branching from her wrist upward and disappearing under her sleeve, and where yesterday a scratch from a market hook had scabbed on her palm, today the scab shone. Gold. Molten, lit-from-behind gold, the exact gold of a dead woman's eyes in a burning city a century gone.

Marguerite followed his gaze. Saw what he saw. And Edmund watched, with a clarity that felt like cruelty, as her face performed the whole sequence in order: recognition, terror, calculation, and then the lie assembling itself behind her eyes like a wall going up brick by brick.

"It's nothing," she said. "A reaction. To the sun. I have—sensitive skin. It runs in my family."

*She'll lie to you about what she is.*

Edmund should have agreed. Fifteen years of survival said agree, smile, gather your charms, go back to your ledgers, let the strange woman with the glowing blood walk out of your life and take the danger with her. The temple taught that curiosity was the first sin and obedience the first virtue, and Edmund had built a whole quiet existence on that foundation, stone by careful stone.

Instead he heard himself say: "In the vision, there was a woman sealing a door. She had your eyes."

Marguerite went absolutely still. Somewhere across the square a fishmonger cried his prices; a cart rattled over cobbles; the ordinary world went on being ordinary, obstinately, around the two of them frozen in the center of it.

"What vision," she said. Flat. Careful. The voice of someone stepping onto ice and testing each footfall.

"The end of the Dragon War. The Oath of Ash. A door that opens only to the willing." He pushed himself upright, and his scattered charms clinked around him, thirty worthless pieces of tin and thread and paint, kindness sold as protection, and he heard the dead woman's voice say *write that part down* and knew, with sick certainty, that he already had—that the words were inked somewhere inside him now, indelible, in the hand of oaths. "The book showed me tomorrow's date, Marguerite. My name is in it. And I think—" He looked at her shining forearm, at the gold light pulsing faintly in rhythm, he realized, with his own heartbeat. "I think yours is too."

For a long moment she said nothing. The crowd had drifted off, disappointed by the dullness of a man merely fainting. The morning sun climbed another degree, indifferent.

Then Marguerite did something unexpected. She glanced at the temple's watchtower—at the black-and-white banner of the Inquisition hanging limp in the windless air—and her hand closed over Edmund's wrist, warm, far warmer than skin should be, and she pulled him to his feet.

"Not here," she whispered. "Not where they can see us talking. Because Edmund—" and her voice cracked on his name, the first time she'd used it, and the crack in it was fifteen years deep, twenty, a lifetime, "—if you saw what I think you saw, then they're going to come for us. Both of us. And we have until sundown, maybe less, before the temple remembers that it owns you."

She was right. Even as she said it, Edmund saw the figure crossing the far side of the square: Brother Anselm, the debt-keeper, ledger under his arm, walking with the unhurried purpose of a man whose errand can wait because its outcome cannot be changed.

Coming straight for the charm-seller's stall.
