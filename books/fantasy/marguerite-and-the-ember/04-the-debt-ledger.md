## 4. The Debt Ledger

The summons came at the hour of second vespers, which was wrong in three ways.

First, temple business was conducted in the morning, when the light through the archive's high windows made honest work of honest ink. Second, the summons arrived not as a written note—standard practice for even the most trivial matters—but as a boy in a novice's gray who would not meet Edmund's eyes and could not leave fast enough. Third, and worst: the summons bore the seal of the Office of Accounts rather than the Office of Records. Edmund had spent fifteen years learning the grammar of the temple the way other men learned the moods of weather, and he knew that when the Accountants came calling, it was never about ink.

He sat for a long moment at his desk after the boy had gone, his stylus hovering above a ledger line he no longer remembered starting. Around him the archive breathed its familiar breaths—the tick of cooling wax, the rustle of Brother Anselm turning pages two alcoves down, the distant groan of the great doors as the evening shift filed out. Everything ordinary. Everything exactly as it had been yesterday, and the day before, and the fifteen hundred days before that.

Except that beneath his desk, wrapped in oilcloth and tucked against the frame where his knee could feel it like a second heartbeat, lay a book of prophecies sealed by order of Archivist Doreth ninety-one years ago. A book with tomorrow's date inked in it. A book with his own face sketched in faded charcoal beside words he could still recite without looking: *the ember wakes; the vessel walks unmarked among the faithful.*

And somewhere in this building, someone knew he had it.

*They don't,* he told himself, the old litany, the one that had kept him alive since he was nineteen years old and stupid enough to sign his name to an indenture contract. *They can't know. You've been careful. Fifteen years careful.*

But the boy hadn't met his eyes. And the seal had been the Accountants'.

Edmund closed the ledger, squared it precisely with the edge of the desk—he was a man of precise habits, habits being the mortar that held a precarious life together—and went to answer a summons he already understood was not about money.

* * *

The Office of Accounts occupied the temple's northwest tower, and Edmund had been inside it exactly once before: the day he signed. He remembered that day with terrible clarity, the way one remembers a fall—one moment on solid ground, the next in air. He'd been nineteen, orphaned twice over, first by fever and then by the charity of an uncle who had discovered that a dead brother's son made poor eating. The temple had offered him paper, pen, and a chair, and Abbot Ferren himself—a younger man then, kind-eyed, smelling of lamp oil and patience—had explained the terms so gently that Edmund had signed before understanding that gentleness was part of the architecture of the trap.

Fifteen years of service against a debt of forty crowns, recalculated annually against room, board, ink, candle-wax, and "administrative overhead." The arithmetic was designed never to resolve. Edmund had known this within three years. He had also known there was nothing to be done about it, and so he had done what survivors do: he had made the cage comfortable, decorated its bars with routine, and called it a life.

The stair to the tower seemed longer tonight. His own footsteps sounded foreign to him, too loud, the footsteps of a man walking toward something rather than merely through.

Abbot Ferren waited for him at the top, alone, seated behind a desk of black oak that had belonged to four abbots before him. The office was spare—a saint's icon with fresh candles, a window overlooking the city's slate roofs going blue with dusk, a single lamp turned low. Ferren had aged into his authority the way trees age into fences, growing around the wire until the wire disappeared. His eyes were still kind. That was the thing about Ferren that had always unsettled Edmund most deeply: the kindness was real. It changed nothing. It was real the way a well-drowned floor is wet.

"Edmund." Ferren gestured to the chair across the desk. "Sit. Wine?"

"No, thank you, Father."

"You look pale."

"It's the archive air, Father. It gets into the blood."

"Mm." Ferren opened a drawer and drew out a ledger—*the* ledger, Edmund's ledger, the one whose columns had grown like ivy over fifteen years, each annual recalculation adding weight. He set it down between them but did not open it. "Do you know why you're here?"

"My debt, Father."

"Your debt," Ferren agreed, and let the word sit there like a stone dropped in still water. Then, gently, terribly: "We're calling it in."

Edmund had imagined this moment, in the way one imagines one's own funeral—vaguely, from a safe distance, always assuming it lay decades off. Now it stood in front of him wearing lamplight, and he found his mind doing absurd things: noticing that Ferren's left thumbnail was split; counting the candle-flames' reflections in the dark window; hearing his own pulse in his ears like a second, faster scribe taking dictation.

"Called in," he repeated. "I—I don't have forty crowns, Father. You know I don't."

"I know exactly what you have." Ferren folded his hands. "I know you have a talent for copying that borders on the miraculous. I know you have fifteen years of faithful service. And I know—" and here the abbot's voice did something subtle, shifted registers the way a key shifts in a lock, "—that three nights ago, you were seen leaving the restricted vault with a volume that has not been catalogued in nearly a century."

The vertigo hit him like cold water poured down the back of the neck. *Seen.* Not suspected. Seen. Which meant a witness, which meant a name given, which meant—

"The Seer's Codex," Ferren said. "Sealed by Doreth's own hand. Do you know how it came to my attention, Edmund? Not through your theft. Through a merchant's report. A woman visited your market stall last week. Bought a luck charm. And the day after, our brothers observed you carrying that same charm's twin in your satchel, along with a certain book, toward the lower quarter." He paused. "They also observed the woman return today. And they observed that when she passed the shrine of Saint Vess, the votive flames *bent toward her.*"

Every instinct Edmund owned screamed at once, a chorus of small reasonable voices: *deny, deflect, confess to something smaller.* He watched them pass like birds and kept his face still, because fifteen years of being owned teaches a man to keep his face still.

"I bought charms," he said. "It's permitted. My stall is licensed."

"Charms are permitted. What I'm asking about is not a charm." Ferren leaned forward, and the lamplight caught the lines of his face and deepened them. "The Codex is a dangerous text, Edmund. Not because of what it says—half of it is gibberish and the other half is heresy—but because of what foolish people believe it says. There is a woman in this city who bends flame. There is a scribe who steals prophecy. And there is an Inquisitor General who, I am told, has already requested copies of our visitor logs." He said the last sentence very quietly, and it landed harder than any shout. "Corvane does not ask twice, Edmund. If he comes here asking who took the Codex, and I cannot give him a clean answer, this temple pays for it. Do you understand? Not you. *Us.* All of us. Brothers who have never touched a forbidden page."

There it was. The architecture revealed—not a trap sprung, but a door held open, with the whole brotherhood standing behind it as collateral. Edmund felt the full weight of it settle onto his shoulders, and beneath the weight, something worse: gratitude. Ferren had arranged it so that refusing would feel like betrayal. That was the temple's oldest craft, older than any scripture. They taught you your chains were everyone else's ropes.

"What is it you want, Father?" Edmund asked. His voice came out level. He was distantly proud of that.

"Simplicity." Ferren slid a document across the desk. Edmund looked down at it and read it twice because the first reading refused to make sense. "Sign this, and your debt is dissolved. Every crown, every year of recalculation, gone. Your indenture ends tonight. You walk out of this tower a free man with a clean record and a letter of recommendation to any scriptorium in Vhalen. All we require are two small things."

Edmund's eyes moved down the page. *The undersigned agrees to surrender the volume known as the Seer's Codex, intact and unmarked, to the custody of the Office of Accounts. The undersigned further attests that he has had contact with an individual exhibiting anomalous characteristics, and agrees to provide said individual's name, description, and place of lodging.*

Name. Description. Place of lodging.

A woman he had spoken to for perhaps ten minutes. A woman who had counted out coppers with heat-shimmer rising faintly from her knuckles, who had flinched when the guards passed, who had looked at him with the particular wariness of someone else who had survived a long time by being careful. She had a scar on her left thumb. She had laughed once, briefly, at his price for the luck charm, and the laugh had sounded rusty, like a hinge unused.

He didn't know where she lodged. He didn't know her name. He knew almost nothing about her—which meant, some cold practical voice pointed out, that lying would be easy. He could describe a fiction. Tall, dark-haired, lodges near the tanneries. Untraceable. Harmless.

But that wasn't what stopped his pen.

What stopped it was simpler and more terrible: he didn't want to. Fifteen years he had kept his head down, sold his harmless charms, paid his impossible arithmetic, and told himself that survival was neutral, that staying alive was its own virtue. And now the temple had laid the bill on the table—freedom, actual freedom, the thing he had stopped dreaming of around year six—and the price was a stranger, and he discovered that some part of him he had thought long dead, some stubborn unprofitable scrap of conscience, had already answered *no.*

"The Codex," Edmund said slowly. "If I surrender it, the matter ends? Corvane loses interest?"

"If the matter is resolved internally, there is nothing for the Inquisitor General to find." Ferren smiled, and it was the smile from nineteen years ago, patient and warm and part of the trap. "You have my word."

*Your word,* Edmund thought, *is worth exactly the paper it's written on, and you wrote the paper.* But aloud he said, "May I have a moment, Father? To consider."

"Of course." Ferren rose, unhurried. "I'll be at my devotions. Take whatever time you need." At the door he paused. "Edmund. You were a boy when you signed. No one will think less of you for choosing freedom now. It's what any sensible man would do."

*Sensible.* The word followed him out of the room's atmosphere even after Ferren's footsteps faded down the stair.

* * *

Edmund sat alone in the abbot's office for a long time.

The lamp burned. The city darkened past the window, roof by roof, window by window, until Vhalen became a scatter of embers on black cloth, and he thought—absurdly, hysterically—*there, that's what she looks like from up here. The whole city looks like her blood must look.* He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes.

Think it through. Be sensible. The options arranged themselves with horrible clarity.

Option one: sign. Surrender the Codex, name a fiction or—no, they'd verify, they had watchers, they'd want the truth—name *her*, and walk free. Free. He turned the word over like a coin he'd forgotten he possessed. Fifteen years. He could rent a room with a window. He could copy what he chose. He could sell his little harmless charms openly, or never sell them again, or learn a trade that didn't involve ink. He could sleep without calculating.

And she would be taken. He didn't know what Corvane's methods were, but he knew the stories, and the stories were told in the lowered voices people used for plagues. Whatever she was—dragon-blooded, cursed, marked, guilty of nothing except having been born wrong according to the current doctrine—they would take her apart to find the answer, and then they would burn what remained, and the temple would file the expense under administrative overhead.

Option two: refuse. Keep the Codex, keep the silence, keep the debt. And lose everything anyway, because Ferren had not come to negotiate; Ferren had come to transfer risk. If Edmund refused, the abbot would simply report the anomaly himself, with Edmund attached as accessory, and the Inquisitor General would collect them both, and the brotherhood would shake its collective head over the tragedy of a promising scribe corrupted.

There was no option three. Except—

Except that there was, and it had been sitting in his chest all evening, growing heavier, and it was this: lie. Not deflect, not misdirect, but stand in front of a man who had known him since he was nineteen and lie, plainly, with a straight face, about something that mattered. He had never done it. Not once in thirty-four years. It was the one possession the temple had never managed to confiscate, and he was about to spend it, and he found his hands were shaking.

Because here was the thing he could not stop seeing: her face when the guards passed. Not fear exactly. *Recognition.* The practiced blankness of someone who had learned to become furniture when authority walked by. He knew that blankness from the inside. He had worn it for fifteen years. And some traitorous, unsensible part of him whispered that in all of Vhalen, in all his careful gray life, this stranger was the first person who had ever looked at him and seen the same thing he saw in the mirror every morning—a survivor, counting exits.

Ferren returned. Edmund heard him on the stair and had one final moment to arrange his face, and he used it, and when the abbot sat down again, Edmund Reyes pushed the document back across the desk unsigned.

"I can't do it, Father."

Ferren's expression didn't change, which was itself an answer. "No?"

"I never took the Codex. I've never seen it. And I don't know any woman who bends flame." He heard the words come out of his mouth and marveled at them, the way a man marvels at his own blood on snow. Steady. Clear. False. "You say I was seen. Someone saw wrong, or someone wants to see wrong. Either way, Father, I won't sign a confession to things I haven't done."

Silence. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, the bells began marking the hour, and Edmund counted the strokes—nine—to keep from counting anything else.

"You understand," Ferren said at last, and there was no kindness in it now, only a tired sort of regret, "that this changes the nature of our conversation. The offer stands until morning. After that, the matter passes beyond my discretion." He rose. "Go home, Edmund. Sleep on it. Sensible men sleep on things."

Edmund stood, bowed—the habit of fifteen years, bowing to the man who owned him—and walked to the door. His legs felt strange, too light, as if the floor had tilted and he was still adjusting.

"Father," he said, without turning around. "One question. My debt. Forty crowns, fifteen years, recalculated every season. Did you ever intend for me to pay it?"

Behind him, a pause long enough to hold an entire career.

"Good night, Edmund."

He walked down the stair and out through the cloister, past the chapel where the evening office murmured, past the brothers who nodded to him as they always did, and no one looked at him twice, and that was somehow the worst of it—that the world could remain so perfectly unchanged on the day he finally broke it.

Outside the temple gates, the night air hit him like a slap, cold and coal-smelling and gloriously unowned. He stood on the steps and breathed it, and his body performed the arithmetic of terror without his permission: *morning. You have until morning. Then Corvane, and watchers, and no more careful life.*

He should run. He should take the Codex and run tonight, alone, sensibly, and never see the woman with the scarred thumb again, because involvement was death and he had spent fifteen years avoiding exactly this.

Instead he found his feet carrying him down toward the lower quarter, toward the market stalls shuttered for the night, toward the narrow lane where a luck-seller had told him—if telling was the right word for a sentence dropped like a coin into his palm—where she might be found after dark.

The Codex, wrapped in oilcloth under his arm, felt warm against his ribs.

He told himself it was just the walk. He was almost able to believe it.
