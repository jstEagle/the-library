## 5. Ash Under Her Skin

The safehouse was a dyer's loft above a shuttered indigo shop in the Tanner's Reach, and it smelled of vinegar and old wool and the particular sourness of fear that had lived too long in one place. Marguerite had chosen it three days ago—before the market square, before Edmund's body had folded like wet paper into her arms, before the gold light had poured out of her own blood in front of a stranger and a crowd—and she had chosen it because the dye vats below still held enough mineral salt to mask a heartbeat. Hers, at least. She had been counting on her own heartbeat being the only one that mattered.

She had not counted on the scribe.

Edmund Reyes sat across the loft from her now, on an upturned crate meant for stretching cloth, his hands wrapped around a cup of barley tea he hadn't touched in an hour. He had not touched it because his hands were still shaking, and Marguerite knew—he *knew*—that he was holding the cup the way a drowning man holds driftwood, less to drink than to have something to grip that wasn't her.

He hadn't asked. That was the thing she kept turning over in her chest like a stone with a sharp edge. He had woken in the market square with her kneeling over him, her veins lit from within like a lantern made of skin, and he had not asked. He had looked at her—at the gold fading out of her wrists, at the heat shimmer rising off her knuckles in the cold morning air—and he had said only, "We should not be here when the guards come," and then he had led her through six streets and two courtyards to this loft, moving with the practiced, unremarkable ease of a man who had spent fifteen years learning exactly how to be invisible in Vhalen.

Now the light was dying through the slanted window, going from amber to rust to the color of old bruises, and the silence between them had grown so heavy it had a texture, like wet felt. Marguerite could feel the ember under her skin the way you feel a splinter you've decided to stop mentioning—present, insistent, warm. Warmer than it had been that morning. Warmer than it had been yesterday. As if the market square, as if the *witnessing*, had fed it.

"You're staring at your hands," Edmund said quietly.

"I'm staring at your tea."

"It's terrible tea."

"It's terrible tea you haven't drunk."

A ghost of a smile crossed his face and vanished, the way a fish surfaces and is gone. He set the cup down on the floorboards with exaggerated care, as if even the small surrender of it might cost him something. "I keep trying to decide," he said, "whether to ask you what you are, or whether to be the kind of man who doesn't ask. I've been both men in the last six hours. I don't like either of them very much."

Marguerite looked out the window. Below, the Tanner's Reach was going about its evening in the muted, watchful way of a district that had heard the temple bells ring the alarm hour and decided, collectively, to pretend it hadn't. A woman was pulling in laundry. Two boys were racing a hoop along the gutter. Somewhere a dog was barking at nothing, or at everything, or at the same wrongness in the air that Marguerite could feel pressing against her skin like a change in weather.

The wrongness was her. She knew that now. She had known it since she was seven years old.

"My grandmother's name was Ilsa," she said.

Edmund went very still. It was the stillness of a man who had learned that sudden movement frightens the truth away, and Marguerite recognized it with a strange, aching familiarity, because it was her own stillness. It was the stillness of every person in Vhalen who had something to hide.

"She came from the Cinderfen," Marguerite went on. "Before it was called that, when it was still just—land. Farmland, river country. She was eleven when the war ended. She never talked about it. Not the war. She talked about *after*, endlessly, in this low voice, like she was telling me a bedtime story even when I was thirty and she was dying. She talked about the oath."

"The oath," Edmund repeated. Not a question. A man testing the weight of a word he'd only ever seen in sealed books.

"You've read it." She turned to look at him. "In the archive. I saw your face when you came out of the vision in the square. You didn't look like a man seeing something new. You looked like a man recognizing something."

"I copied the prophecy book once," he said. "Nine years ago. Part of my debt-work. They had me reproduce it for the Inquisitor's sealed copies, and I wasn't allowed to read it, but—" he gave a small, helpless shrug, "—your hands learn what they copy. The oath-keepers. The sealing of the last flame. A vessel willing and unclaimed." He stopped, and she watched him decide whether to say the rest, and watched him say it anyway, which told her something about him she suspected would matter later. "There was a sketch in the margin. A woman with her hands raised over a door. The annotation said the line of the oath-keepers ends in fire or ends in ash. No third option."

"That's what my grandmother said." Marguerite's voice came out lower than she intended. "Almost word for word. She used to hold my hands when she said it. She'd hold them and look at my palms like she was reading a book written there, and she'd say, *the line ends in fire or ends in ash, little ember, and no one gets to choose which.*" She laughed, and it came out wrong, brittle. "She called me that. Little ember. I thought it was just—affection. Grandmothers have names for their grandchildren. Sweetheart. Sparrow. Little ember."

"When did you know it wasn't?"

She was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the laundry woman had gone inside; the boys' footsteps had faded; the dog had given up. The Tanner's Reach was settling into that particular Vhalen darkness that was never fully dark—the temple's vigil-lamps burned all night in every district, pale and watchful, so that no one could do anything, anywhere, without the light remembering it.

"When I was seven," she said. "I was carrying a pot of stew from the hearth to the table. My mother's house, in the Weavers' Quarter. I tripped on the hem of my own dress—the same dress I'd tripped on a hundred times—and I didn't burn my hand." She held up her right hand, turned it in the dying light, palm out, as if showing it to a magistrate. "The stew went over. The pot went down. My hand went into the coals that had spilled from the hearth, and I stood there with my hand in the fire, and my mother was screaming, and I was *not screaming*. Because it didn't hurt. Because the coals were—" she searched for it, hating how inadequate every word was, how every word she had found in twenty years of searching was inadequate— "they were *listening*. That's the only way I can say it. The fire in the hearth went quiet when I put my hand in it. Like a dog that knows its master's step on the stair."

Edmund had not moved. But something in his face had shifted—the careful blankness of a scribe giving way, layer by layer, to the naked attention of a man hearing a story he had already read the ending of.

"My mother pulled me out and put my hand in the snow and the burn was already gone. Not healed. *Gone.* Like the fire had decided to give it back." Marguerite closed her fist. "My mother never spoke of it. Not once, not ever. But she stopped letting me tend the hearth. She stopped letting me light the lamps. She started flinching when I came near the stove. And she started taking me to my grandmother's house every week, and my grandmother would sit me down and hold my hands and tell me the bedtime story about the oath, and I didn't understand until I was older that they weren't stories. They were instructions. She was training me. All those years, all those visits, she was *training* me, and I thought I was just—being loved."

"Was it not both?"

The question landed somewhere soft and unguarded, and Marguerite had to look away from him, out the window, at the vigil-lamps.

"It was both," she said finally. "That's what I can't forgive. That's what I've never been able to forgive. Because if it had just been duty, I could have refused it cleanly. And if it had just been love, I could have kept it. But it was both, braided together so tight I can't find the seam, and so I spent twenty years trying to be ordinary, and every year the ember got louder, and every year I got better at pretending I couldn't hear it."

"Louder how?"

"Like this." She flexed her fingers, and for a moment—deliberately, for the first time in front of another living soul—she let it rise. Not much. A breath's worth. The gold came up through the skin of her forearms like sunrise under water, and the air around her hands went soft and shimmering, and the cold of the loft retreated three feet in every direction, and somewhere below them, in the dead dye vats, the last residue of mineral salt *sang*—a thin, high, crystalline note that Edmund clearly heard, because his eyes went wide and fixed on her.

She let it go. The light sank. The cold came back in like a tide reclaiming a beach.

"That," she said. "That's on a good day. That's when I'm calm and fed and no one is hunting me. When the temple guards walked past my stall last month, I had to put my hands in my apron pocket and press them against my thighs and count to two hundred, because if I hadn't, the heat would have come through the fabric and every guard on the street would have seen a woman's apron glowing like a paper lantern. When I'm frightened, it rises. When I'm angry, it rises. When I—" she stopped.

"When you what?"

"When someone is kind to me," she said, very quietly. "It rises then, too. That's the worst one. That's the one I can't control at all. My grandmother warned me about it. She said, *the fire answers the heart before it answers the will, little ember, so keep your heart in a locked room.* And I did. I kept it locked for twenty years. And then this morning some shaking-handed charm-seller with a satchel full of glowing paper looked at me like I was a person instead of a problem, and I lit up like a festival lamp in the middle of a market square in a city where magic is punishable by—"

She stopped. Swallowed.

"By fire," she finished. "The irony is not lost on me."

Edmund was quiet for a long time. When he finally spoke, his voice had a roughness in it she hadn't heard before.

"The charms," he said. "The ones you bought from me. The luck charm, the—"

"The hearth-blessing. Yes. I have them."

"Don't use the hearth-blessing."

She stared at him. "They're paper and river-clay, Edmund. I bought them because—" and here she faltered, because the true answer was embarrassing, was intimate, was the kind of thing you didn't say to a man you'd known for two days, but the ember was warm under her skin and the loft was cold and she was so *tired* of the locked room— "because you looked at me like I mattered. Because you wrapped them in blue paper and tied them with string and you said *may your fire be a hearth and not a hunger*, and I thought, this man has no idea what he just said to me, and I wanted to keep the words even if the charm was nothing."

He looked at her as if she had struck him.

"It *is* nothing," she said. "Isn't it? The charms. I've known since the second one. There's no spell in them. I would know—I'm the most qualified woman in Vhalen to know. They're paper and clay and kindness. That's all they are."

"Yes," Edmund said. And then, with the particular honesty of a man who has already lost everything he was afraid to lose: "Yes. They're nothing. They've always been nothing. I've sold fifteen years of nothing to frightened people, and it worked, and I've never decided whether that makes me a fraud or a priest."

And Marguerite laughed. She laughed until her ribs hurt and her eyes stung, laughed the way you laugh when a weight you've carried so long you've forgotten its shape is suddenly named out loud by a stranger, and the terrible thing—the wonderful thing—was that as she laughed, the gold came up through her skin again, unbidden, unfrightened, filling the loft with soft warm light like a hearth in a house where someone is expected home.

Edmund watched the light with an expression she would remember for the rest of her life, whatever length that turned out to be. Not fear. Not awe, exactly. Recognition.

"You're not burning," he said slowly. "When it comes up like that. You're not in pain."

"No."

"It's not consuming you."

"It's *waiting*," she said, and the laughter was gone as suddenly as it had come, and the truth was there, huge and unavoidable, filling the loft the way the light did. "That's what my grandmother's story was actually about, under the bedtime-voice. The ember doesn't want anything. It doesn't hunger. It's not a hunger, it's a *trust*. It's been waiting a hundred years for someone to carry it somewhere, and it's been calling, and I have spent my whole life pretending not to hear the call because I never asked for it. I never asked for any of it. I wanted a stall in the market. I wanted a room with a window over the Weavers' Quarter. I wanted—" her voice cracked, and she hated it, and she kept going anyway— "I wanted to be *ordinary*, Edmund. That's all. That was the whole ambition. One ordinary life, in one ordinary city, with the fire staying asleep in my blood until I died of old age and it died with me, and the oath could go find some other poor girl in some other century. And instead—"

Instead, the date. Instead, the book. Instead, the scribe with his shaking hands and his glowing satchel, instead of the sealed quarter and the bells and the Inquisitor's men who were even now, somewhere in the dark of Vhalen, asking questions with the patience of men paid by the hour.

Instead, this.

She became aware that Edmund had crossed the loft. She didn't know when he had moved—she had been looking at the window, at the vigil-lamps, at the ordinary city that would burn her if it ever truly saw her—and suddenly he was standing an arm's length away, and his hands were shaking again, but he was holding them out anyway, open, palms up, the way you approach a horse you don't know.

"May I?"

She didn't trust her voice. She nodded.

He took her hands. His fingers were cool, ink-stained, trembling slightly—and the moment his skin touched hers, the ember rose to meet him, gold flooding up through her wrists into her forearms, and she braced herself for him to flinch, to pull away, to look at her the way her mother had looked at her over the hearth all those years ago.

He didn't flinch. He held on. And the light settled, the way a dog settles when it finds the one person in the room who isn't afraid of it.

"Twenty years," he said quietly. "You've been alone with this for twenty years."

"Twenty-three."

"Twenty-three years," he said, "and the first time you let it be seen, it's in a market square, in front of the temple, on the worst possible day of your life." His grip tightened a fraction. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry it happened that way. You deserved—" he stopped, and she watched him reject several sentences before settling on the true one, "—you deserved to be seen in a kitchen. With the stew. On a good day."

Marguerite hadn't known she was going to cry until the first tear was already falling, and it hissed—actually *hissed*—where it struck her own glowing wrist, and Edmund looked down at the small curl of steam and made a sound that was half a laugh and half something much older and sadder.

"Even your grief is on fire," he said.

"I never asked for it," she whispered. The words came out of her like a confession extracted under torture, except no one was torturing her, except the twenty-three years, except the locked room, except the whole careful architecture of an ordinary life now collapsed in the space of two days. "I need you to understand that. Whatever the prophecy says I am, whatever the ember thinks I agreed to—I never asked. I only wanted to be ordinary. And I am so ashamed that I wanted that. I am ashamed every single day that some part of me wishes my grandmother had never been born, that the line had ended in ash a hundred years ago and left me free—"

"Don't."

The word came out hard, harder than anything he'd said all day, and his hands tightened on hers, and the light flared once, sharply, and settled.

"Don't be ashamed of that," he said. "I sold fake charms to frightened people for fifteen years because I was too afraid to be anything real, and you kept a sun asleep under your skin for twenty-three years because you wanted one ordinary life. We are the same person, Marguerite. We're the two smallest kinds of survivor there are, and the prophecy picked us anyway, and being picked isn't the same as being guilty." He exhaled. "The temple taught me the opposite lesson for fifteen years. I'm only now learning to say it out loud."

She looked down at their joined hands—at his ink-stained, trembling fingers wrapped around her burning ones, at the gold light pooling between their palms like something shared—and felt the strangest sensation, a loosening in her chest that was almost painful, the sensation of a locked room being opened for the first time in decades and the air inside it, stale and ashamed and lonely, finally moving.

*Seen.* That was the word. Not understood—understanding would take longer, might take years, might never fully arrive. But *seen*. The ember under her skin was warm and waiting, and for the first time in her life, someone was looking directly at it and not away.

"Edmund," she said. "The book. The prophecy. It named today's date, didn't it. That's why you came to the market. That's why you were looking for—"

"It had a sketch of my face," he said simply. "And tomorrow's date is in it too. And the day after. The dates run for six more days, and then the page goes blank." He met her eyes. "Whatever this is, Marguerite—whatever the ember is calling us toward—it has a deadline."

As if the words were a key turning in a lock, the ember surged beneath her skin—once, hard, deliberate, like a fist knocking on a door from the inside—and far below them, through the floorboards of the dyer's loft, through the shuttered indigo shop, through the bones of the Tanner's Reach itself, every vigil-lamp in the district flickered.

Once. Twice.

And did not go out—but leaned, all of them together, all of them at once, toward the north. Toward the Cinderfen. Toward the Ashvault.

Toward the six days that had just become five.
