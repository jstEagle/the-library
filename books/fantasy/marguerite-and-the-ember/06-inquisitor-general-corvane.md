## 6. Inquisitor General Corvane

The candle had burned down to a stub before Inquisitor General Aldous Corvane finished reading the prophecy for the third time.

He did not read it again because he had failed to understand it. He read it again because understanding was a discipline, and discipline was the only prayer he kept. The first reading told him what the words said. The second told him what they meant. The third told him what they would cost—and Corvane had built thirty years of service on the principle that everything, given time and sufficient pressure, would tell you its price.

*When the twin moons cross the ledger's seam,* the old script ran, in a hand that predated the empire's founding by two generations, *the ember shall wake beneath the ash. One shall carry it. One shall bear it. The carrier shall reignite what was sworn dead; the bearer shall let it gutter into silence forever. Between them stands the choice that made the throne, and unmade the dragons.*

He set the page flat on his desk, weighted at the corners with four smooth river stones he had carried since boyhood, and looked at it the way a surgeon looks at a wound he intends to close.

Outside the window of his office in the Bastion of Inquiry, Vhalen lay spread out under a thin gray rain, ten thousand rooftops steaming where the lamplighters were making their rounds. Somewhere in that sprawl—somewhere in the market quarter, if the temple's report could be believed—a scribe had opened a book that should not have been opened, on a date that should not have mattered, and found tomorrow written inside it.

Corvane did not believe in coincidence. He had interrogated too many people to believe that anything happened only once.

* * *

The report from the Temple of the Ledgered Flame sat in a folder stamped with the abbot's personal seal, which meant the temple was frightened, which meant the temple knew more than it had written down. Corvane had learned long ago to read documents the way other men read faces: for what they concealed rather than what they confessed.

*On the evening of the ninth,* the abbot's clerk had written, *a scribe indentured to our archive, one Edmund Reyes, debt-holder number 4471, was discovered in possession of the sealed codex known as the Oath of Ashes. The codex was recovered. The scribe claims he took nothing further. We have reason to believe he was seen in the company of an anomaly.*

An anomaly. The word did such tidy work. It could mean a heretic, a smuggler, a woman with the wrong color blood. It could mean anything the temple did not wish to name aloud, because naming a thing gave it edges, and edges could be defended.

Corvane turned to the second document in the folder: the vigil-lamp registry for Tanner's Reach, compiled by the district's lamp-warden, a nervous little man who reported irregularities with the punctilious terror of someone who had once seen an Inquisitor smile. On the night of the ninth, every vigil-lamp in the quarter had leaned north. Not gone out—leaned. As though drawn by a current no wick should have felt.

North. Toward the Cinderfen. Toward the Ashvault, which was not supposed to exist, which officially had never existed, which appeared in no imperial map and in every imperial secret.

Corvane rose and went to the tall cabinet against the eastern wall. It took him some moments—the key lived on a chain against his sternum, beneath three layers of uniform, and his hands, though steady, were sixty-two years old—to withdraw the slim volume wrapped in oilcloth. He unwrapped it at his desk without hurry. There was no one to see. There was never anyone to see. That was the point of him.

The book was called, in the imperial catalogs, *Foundations of the Concord*, and it was required reading for every child in every schoolroom in Vhalen. What the schoolrooms taught was a fable: brave founders, wicked dragons, a war won by virtue, a peace signed in ash. What the book actually contained—if one read past the verses printed for children, if one counted the marginalia in the oldest surviving copies, as Corvane had spent eleven years doing—was something else entirely.

The empire had not won the Dragon War. The empire had *inherited* it, mid-collapse, when the last oath-keepers had sealed the final dragon-fire—the ember—in a vault in the Cinderfen rather than let either side finish what they had started. And the seal they had used, the oath itself, was not merely a lock. It was a *signature*. The first emperor's legitimacy rested on a single claim: that he stood as witness to the sealing, that the fire slept by consent, that the throne spoke with the authority of those who had put it there.

Which meant, Corvane thought, turning the schoolbook's gilded pages with a fingertip, that whoever held the ember held the empire's founding truth in the palm of their hand. Loose it, and every charter, every coronation, every law signed in the emperor's name became a forgery standing on an open grave. Extinguish it, and the claim died clean—no heir, no pretender, no dragon-blooded remnant rising in a century to say *the fire remembers us*. Either way, the question ended. But only one way ended it *quietly*.

He had lost a great deal to questions that were not ended quietly.

* * *

There came the knock he had been expecting: two soft raps, the interval of a man who had learned never to startle his superior.

"Enter."

Lieutenant Hask entered the way he did everything—efficiently, quietly, and with the air of a man perpetually braced for disappointment. He was forty, gray at the temples, and Corvane trusted him for the same reason he trusted few people: Hask had nothing left to want. A dead wife, a grown daughter safely married into provincial gentry, a career that had plateaued exactly where competence plateaus when ambition is mistaken for disloyalty. Wanting nothing, he could be given everything, and would remain unmoved.

"The temple delegation is waiting below, General," Hask said. "Abbot Ferren and two canon-lawyers. They're—" a fractional pause, "—insistent."

"They are frightened," Corvane corrected mildly, "and frightened men mistake insistence for strength. Show them up. Then have the duty clerks bring me the current patrol charts for the market quarter, the debt registries for every temple holding south of the river, and the last five years of charm-seller licenses." He paused. "And the census rolls for Tanner's Reach. Household by household."

Hask wrote none of this down. He never needed to. "The census will take until morning."

"It has already taken a century, Lieutenant. It can take until morning."

* * *

Abbot Ferren was a broad, pink man built like a wine cask, with the soft hands of a lifetime spent handling vellum instead of work, and he filled Corvane's visitor's chair the way a guilty man fills any chair—as though trying to take up less space than his body required. His two canon-lawyers flanked him like decorative buttresses.

"Inquisitor General." Ferren bowed as far as his waist allowed. "The temple is grateful for your swift attention. This matter is, of course, primarily ecclesiastical—"

"Everything is primarily ecclesiastical, Abbot, until it threatens the state. Then it is primarily mine." Corvane did not offer tea. He never offered tea. Tea was a ritual of equality, and he extended equality to no one in this room. "Your clerk writes that a debt-scribe accessed the Oath of Ashes. I'd like to know how a man bound by indenture, in a locked archive, under canonical supervision, came to be alone with a codex your own seals were supposed to make impossible to open."

Ferren's jowls tightened. "The seals were intact. Whatever the scribe claims—"

"I haven't yet told you what the scribe claims." Corvane let the silence do its work. It always did. "Tell me about the debt."

"The debt?"

"Debt-holder 4471. Edmund Reyes. Fifteen years of service against a principal of—" Corvane consulted the page before him, though he had memorized it an hour ago, "—forty-one crowns, compounded annually at the canonical rate. Your temple has declined to redeem him despite repeated offers from three separate buyers, including, two years ago, an offer from my own office. Why does the temple keep a harmless copyist so carefully?"

The abbot's face performed a small, complicated collapse—the look of a man realizing the trap had been around him all along and he had simply never walked far enough in any direction to touch the bars.

"The debt," Ferren said slowly, "was never... strictly financial."

"No," Corvane agreed. "It rarely is. Debts are how institutions hold people, Abbot, and institutions hold people only when those people are useful. So I will ask you once, plainly, and you will answer plainly, because the alternative is that I begin asking your canon-lawyers, who strike me as men whose loyalty is a matter of arithmetic." He folded his hands. "What was Reyes meant to become?"

And Ferren, sweating, told him: the scribe had been groomed. Chosen at nine years old from the charity rolls, indentured, educated, shaped—a vessel of ordinary blood and extraordinary caution, raised inside the archive so that the prophecy's carrier, when the appointed date arrived, would be someone the temple already owned. Someone whose debt could be called in at the precise hour the stars demanded. The temple had not lost control of the prophecy. The temple had *planted* it, like a farmer planting a field he expected to harvest.

"And the anomaly?" Corvane asked. "The one seen with him?"

"A woman," Ferren whispered. "We don't know her name. But the lamps leaned, General. All of them. And there was—" he swallowed, "—there was gold in her blood. The warden saw it when she knelt over him in the square. Gold, moving under the skin like something swimming."

Dragon-blood. Or near enough. Corvane felt nothing at the confirmation—not triumph, not fear, only the cold settling of a scale finding its level. He had read enough marginalia to know what the old texts said about the oath-keeper lines: that they thinned but never broke, that the fire called to its keepers down through generations like water finding cracks in stone. A woman with gold in her veins, walking beside the temple's planted scribe, on the appointed night.

Either the temple's plan had succeeded beyond its prayers, or it had just slipped its leash. With institutions, Corvane had found, the two conditions were usually indistinguishable.

"You will return to your abbey," he told Ferren, "and you will say nothing of this conversation. You will continue to prepare your scribe for redemption—or whatever the temple intended to do with him at the appointed hour—and you will send me a full accounting of that intention within two days. If the temple's design and the empire's interest align, you will be rewarded. If they diverge even by the width of a hair, I will dissolve your abbey down to its foundation stones and salt the archives. Do you understand me?"

"General, the temple's interests are the empire's—"

"Yes," Corvane said. "That is what concerns me."

* * *

They left. The rain thickened against the windows. Corvane stood for a long time looking out at the city, at the wet glitter of the market quarter in the middle distance, thinking about fires.

His family had died in the Sallow Fever, the winter he was nineteen. The whole street of them—his mother, his father, both sisters, the baby whose name he still could not say aloud after forty-three years. The fever had come out of the fen country, riding the trade barges, and it had burned through Vhalen's poor quarters like wind through wheat while the dragon-oath wards along the marsh roads—wards the old texts promised would keep such plagues penned—had stood silent. Unmaintained. Or abandoned. Or *never functional at all*, depending on which marginal note you believed. Three hundred thousand dead in eighteen months, and the official histories recorded it as a season of misfortune, a paragraph, a bad winter.

He had gone to the oath-keeper shrines afterward, young and hollowed, asking the question every orphan asks: *why didn't you stop it?* And the shrines had been empty, or staffed by the senile, or selling charms. Charms! Little knotted cords and painted stones, sold to the grieving by the handful, promising protection that had failed an entire generation. He remembered standing in one such shrine with his fists clenched, listening to a crone explain that the wards "slept now, dear, the fire sleeps, and sleeping things cannot watch over us," and understanding, with a clarity that had never left him, that the whole apparatus was a lie the living told to avoid burying the truth with the dead.

The fire slept. The fire had always slept. And while it slept, his family had died, and no oath-keeper, no ward, no dragon's ancient promise had lifted one finger—one claw—to stop it.

So let it die forever. Let the last of it gutter out in whatever vault the cowards of the old war had built to hide their failure. Let the empire stand on honest emptiness rather than a borrowed miracle. He did not hate the woman with gold in her blood. Hatred was expenditure, and Corvane wasted nothing. She was simply an obstacle between the world and its cure, and obstacles were removed thoroughly or not at all.

Cruelty, he had told a trembling subordinate once, years ago, was a word invented by people who lacked the stomach for thoroughness. He believed it still. Every hard thing he had ever done had been done completely, cleanly, and in the open record of his own conscience, and the city outside his window had slept soundly every night since—which was, in the end, the only metric of mercy that mattered.

* * *

By the time the duty clerks assembled the materials he had requested, dawn was graying the rain. Corvane worked through the patrol charts first, annotating in his small, exact hand: bridge closures, gate rotations, the two gaps in the Tanner's Reach coverage that he ordered closed not because the fugitives would find them—he assumed they already had—but because a closed gap taught him which routes a hunted pair would *avoid*, and avoided routes could be watched.

The debt registries told him the rest. Forty-one crowns, fifteen years, three refused buyouts—and then, buried in the temple's internal correspondence, a single line that confirmed everything Ferren had confessed and more: *Redemption of 4471 to be executed upon fulfillment of the Ninth-Moon condition. Asset to be presented at the Ashvault approach.*

The temple had not merely planted a scribe. The temple had planned to *deliver* him—contract, soul, and all—to the door of the vault, and claim the ember's stewardship for the Ledgered Flame. Which meant the temple believed it could control the fire. Which meant the temple deserved what was coming to it.

Corvane dipped his pen and wrote the order in full, unhurried strokes, each character as neat as a headstone:

*By authority of the Office of Inquiry, the market quarter of Vhalen—bounded by the River Gate, the Tanner's Reach, the Saltspire Road, and the Old Customhouse Wall—is hereby sealed effective at the changing of the morning watch. No person shall enter or leave save by writ of this office. All vigil-lamps within the quarter are to be extinguished by order, and all charm-sellers, hedge-readers, and licensed wonder-workers are to be detained for examination. The individuals designated the Carrier and the Bearer are to be taken alive. Damage to property is authorized. Damage to the subjects is not. Thoroughness, gentlemen. Nothing less.*

He signed it, blotted it, and rang for Hask.

"Wake the garrison," he said. "Seal the quarter. And Lieutenant—find me everything there is to find about a charm-stall near the fountain square. Its owner, its customers, its neighbors. If a dragon-blooded woman has been walking through that market buying luck, someone sold it to her, and sellers remember their customers."

Hask paused at the door. "And if she's already gone, General? Out through the gaps before we close them?"

Corvane returned to the window. The rain was lifting; the first pale light caught the thousand chimneys of the market quarter, and beneath them, invisible from this height, the streets were beginning to fill—milk carts, fishwives, children running errands, a whole quarter of ordinary lives that did not yet know they had become a net.

"She hasn't gone," he said. "People with secrets run *from* things. People with callings run *toward* them. And every lamp in that quarter leaned north." He turned back to his desk, to the prophecy lying open under its four river stones, to the line he had read a hundred times and would read a hundred more until it yielded its last syllable. "Let her run toward it. I have six days, a legion, and no doubt whatsoever."

He picked up his pen and began, methodically, to write the list of everyone in Tanner's Reach who would need to be questioned first.
