## 7. The Rooftops of Vhalen

*Edmund*

The bells began at dusk.

Not the evening bells—those he knew the way he knew his own heartbeat, the six o'clock toll of Vesper's Rest and the quarter-chimes that followed. These were different. Deeper. Three bells, then silence, then three again, ringing from the tower of the Inquisition itself, a sound Edmund had heard only twice in fifteen years and had hoped never to hear again.

*Sealing of the Quarter.*

He stood in the doorway of Marguerite's rented room above the dyer's shop, watching the street below transform. Lantern-lighters froze mid-task, their poles still raised. A fishwife abandoned her cart where it stood, herring glinting silver in the last light. And from every alley mouth, in ones and twos and then in ranks, came the gray-and-scarlet of the Inquisitorial guard, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who knew the exits were already closed.

"They're not searching yet," Marguerite said behind him. She stood with her back to the wall, arms crossed, and though her voice was steady Edmund could see it in her—the way the air around her hands shimmered faintly, like heat off summer cobblestones. "Searching is loud. First they close the doors."

"Then we go through a window." He turned to her. "The roof."

She looked at him the way people looked at charm-sellers: polite skepticism covering something kinder. "You've done this before?"

"No one has ever done this before," Edmund admitted. "But I've spent fifteen years copying patrol charts for the temple archive. Every rooftop access ladder in the Tanner's Quarter, every watch-post rotation, every gap between patrols. I know this city's bones better than the men who walk its streets." He hesitated, then added, because lying to her had become impossible somewhere between the market square and here: "I've just never climbed any of them myself. Scribes aren't known for their agility."

Something that was almost a smile touched her mouth. It didn't reach her eyes. "Then we'll be slow together. The dyer's roof connects to the fuller's, the fuller's to the warehouse on Cordwainer Lane. After that?"

"After that I stop knowing and start guessing."

"Guessing has gotten us this far." She pulled the shutters open. Evening air poured in, cool and coal-smelling, and below them the sealing continued with terrible patience. "Edmund. If they catch us—"

"They won't."

"You don't know that."

"I know the prophecy book is in my satchel, and I know what Corvane does to people who read forbidden things, and I know—" He stopped. Swallowed. "I know I'd rather fall than be questioned by that man. So let's not fall."

She went through the window first, of course. She moved like water finding its level, up onto the sloped shingles without a sound, and Edmund followed with considerably more noise, palms scraping tar, one boot skidding before he caught the ridgepole and hung there for a mortifying moment like laundry.

From the ridge of the dyer's shop, Vhalen opened beneath them like a second city—a city of angles and shadow, chimney-pots and weathered slate, the gaps between buildings suddenly crossable, the streets reduced to rivers of lantern-light far below. Somewhere to the east the temple spires caught the last red light and burned like candles.

"There," Marguerite breathed.

Edmund followed her gaze. He saw nothing at first—just rooftops descending toward the quarter wall in uneven steps. Then he saw what she was seeing. Faint, almost invisible, a thread of amber light pulsed behind the shingles of the fuller's next door, as if the building itself had a heartbeat.

"The dye vats," he realized. "The furnaces under them. You can feel the fire."

"I can feel everything warm right now." Her voice had gone strange, distant. "Every hearth in the quarter. Every body. The whole city is..." She pressed a hand flat against the shingles. "It's very loud tonight."

"Can you follow it? Warmth to warmth?"

Her eyes came back to him, gold-flecked even in the dark, and she nodded once. "Better than you can follow your charts. Lead when you know the way. I'll lead when you don't."

They went.

* * *

*Marguerite*

The roofs of Vhalen were nothing like the ground. On the ground there were rules—lanterns, watchmen, the thousand small surveillances of a city that feared its own shadow. Up here there was only the wind off the Ashen Sea, cold enough to make her teeth ache, and the astonishing, illicit freedom of empty space.

Marguerite had not felt free since she was nine years old.

She leapt the gap between the fuller's roof and the warehouse beyond, four feet of black air over a drop that would break both their legs, and landed rolling, and laughed—actually laughed, a startled sound she clapped her hand over too late. Behind her Edmund picked his way across like a man crossing a frozen river, testing each slate before trusting it, and she waited for him with her heart doing something complicated in her chest.

"Left at the chimney stack," he whispered when he reached her, breathless, disheveled, alive in a way the archive had clearly never let him be. "There's a patrol change at the Cordwainer post at half past. Ninety seconds when no one watches the north face. My charts say the ladder there is rusted through anyway—they never fixed it, which means they never use it, which means—"

"—which means it's our road." She was already moving. This was the part that frightened her, how much she loved this part. The heat-guidance was effortless now, each warm thing in the city laid out in her mind like stars: the bakery ovens two streets south, the guard's brazier on the corner, the furnace-heart of the fulling mill, and always, threading through all of it, the deep slow pulse of the ember-echo in her own blood, pointing northeast like a compass needle made of longing.

Toward the Ashvault. Toward whatever was calling her.

They crossed Cordwainer Lane's rooftops in the ninety seconds of blind spot, dropping into the shadow of the warehouse wall while a patrol passed below, so close Marguerite could smell the tallow on their torches. One of the guards paused. Looked up. She held her breath and pulled the heat out of herself, down, inward, banking it like a stove dampered for the night, and after a long moment the man shrugged and moved on.

"You did that on purpose," Edmund murmured. "Hiding yourself."

"I've been hiding myself my whole life. Tonight I'm just better at it." She meant it lightly. It came out heavy.

They went on. Over the rope-walk, along the spine of the tannery where the smell rose thick enough to taste, across a courtyard gap on a plank Edmund swore had been left there by roofers years ago and which held, barely, groaning. Twice they froze as search-lanterns swept the streets below—the Inquisition working methodically now, door to door, the sealing tightening like a fist. Once they heard shouting, and a woman's voice protesting, and the sound of a door being broken, and neither of them spoke about it but Marguerite felt Edmund flinch beside her in the dark, and knew he was counting names he couldn't save.

It was past the tannery that it happened.

Later—much later, in the flooded dark of the cisterns, in the burning heart of the Ashvault, in all the long years after—she would replay those thirty seconds and find no version of events where she could have done differently. But later was no comfort at all in the moment when the young guard came around the chimney stack with his lantern raised and his sword half-drawn and shouted the word *halt* in a voice that cracked on the second letter, because he was young, younger than her, hardly more than a boy with an unshaved jaw and terror in his eyes, and his shout brought three more guards swarming up the access stair behind him, and Marguerite's fear—

Her fear flared.

That was the only word for it. It wasn't a decision. It rose out of her like a geyser breaking through crust, fifteen years of banked coals igniting all at once, and the world went gold at the edges. She felt the heat leave her in a wave she could not recall, could not shape, could not stop—and the boy's lantern flared white and burst, burning oil spraying across the rooftop, across his sleeve, across his chest, and he screamed and beat at himself and the flames would not go out because they were not entirely fire anymore, they were hers, they answered her panic instead of his.

"Stop," she gasped—to him, to it, to herself. "*Stop.*"

And it stopped. All at once, like a snuffed candle, leaving darkness and the sweet-horrible smell of scorched wool and the boy on his knees, breathing, alive, but wrong—she could feel it through the soles of her feet, the way warmth drained out of a body, the way his flickered low and lower, guttering like a lamp out of oil.

The other guards reached the roof and found their comrade collapsed and two shadows fleeing across the ridge tiles, and behind them came shouts and whistles, but Marguerite barely heard any of it. She ran because Edmund pulled her running, and she ran because stopping meant dying, and all the while some cold clear part of her mind knelt beside the boy whose name she would never know and watched his warmth gutter, gutter, gutter—

They dropped through a skylight into a chandler's loft and Edmund barred the door behind them with a beam, and only then did she stop, doubled over, hands on her knees, shaking.

"He's dead," she said. She hadn't seen it. She hadn't needed to see it. "Isn't he. I felt him go out."

Edmund didn't answer, which was answer enough.

"I killed him." The words came out of her flat and strange, someone else's voice. "I didn't—I wasn't trying to—it just *came out of me*, Edmund, I couldn't—"

"I know." He took her shoulders. His hands were trembling too, but his voice, when it came, was steady in the way of a man holding himself steady by main force. "I know. Listen to me. Corvane's men came to take us to the question rooms. Whatever happened on that roof, the choice that put him there was Corvane's, and the temple's, and theirs. Not yours."

"That's a comfort people offer," she whispered. "It isn't true."

"It's the truest thing I know how to say." He held her gaze, and in the dark of the chandler's loft, with wax-smell thick around them and the whistles still fading in the streets outside, she saw that he meant it—that this mild, careful, debt-ridden scribe would rather believe the worst of the empire than let her carry it alone. Something in her chest cracked along an old seam.

"We keep moving," she said at last, because grief was a luxury the sealed quarter wouldn't sell them. "Where to?"

Edmund was quiet a moment. When he answered, his voice had changed—flatter, harder, the voice of a man reading from a ledger he wished he'd never opened.

"There's a way down. Old cisterns, under the Tanner's Quarter. They're not on the patrol charts because officially they don't exist anymore. But the temple keeps records of everything, Marguerite. Even the things it pretends to forget." He shouldered the satchel with the prophecy book inside, and the book, absurdly, impossibly, gave off a faint glow through the leather, patient as a lit fuse. "And I think whatever's down there knows we're coming."

* * *

*Edmund*

They descended through the chandler's floor into the cellar, and from the cellar into the dark.

Edmund went first now, because the way down was the kind of knowledge that lived in ledgers rather than instinct: a bricked archway behind stacked tallow barrels, a hairline crack where mortar had failed, a crawl-space that the temple's own surveyors had marked *sealed, 402* and then, in a later hand, *reopened, purpose unknown*. Fifteen years of copying other people's secrets, and here at last was the night they all became useful.

Behind him Marguerite moved silently, but he could feel her silence had changed weight. Grief had gotten into it. He wanted to say something—wanted desperately to say something—but everything he owned felt thin. *It wasn't your fault* was a coin worn smooth from circulation. *I'm sorry* was worse, because sorry implied a debt, and debts were the one language Edmund Reyes had sworn off speaking in.

So instead, in the dark, he told her the truth he had.

"When I was nine," he said, feeling his way along wet stone, "my family sold me to the temple to cover my father's debts. That's what indenture is—you're not bought, you're *owed*, and the owing passes down like blood. For fifteen years I've copied their charts and their censuses and their lists of anomalies, and every night I told myself the same story: *keep your head down, pay your debt, stay harmless, and someday you'll walk out free.*" His fingers found the edge of the old cistern stair, slick with a century of drip. "Tonight I lied to the abbot for the first time in my life. Tomorrow there will be no temple to walk out of. And I find—" he laughed, short and unsteady, "—I find I'm not sorry. That's the part that frightens me. Not the guards. Not Corvane. How *easy* it was."

Marguerite's hand found his shoulder in the dark. Just that. Just her palm, warm as a hearthstone, resting where the weight sat heaviest.

Ahead of them, far below, something answered.

Not sound exactly—a pressure, a warmth rising up the cistern stairs like breath from a lung, and against his chest the prophecy book flared bright enough to leak gold through every seam of the satchel. Marguerite drew a sharp breath beside him.

"She's down there," Marguerite whispered. "Whoever sealed the flame. Whoever's been waiting a hundred years." Her grip tightened on his shoulder. "Edmund—the dead dragons are talking. They're saying *come*."

Edmund looked down the stairs, into the dark where the water waited, and thought of a boy on a rooftop whose light had gone out, and an abbot he'd betrayed, and a date written a century ago that bore tomorrow's ink.

"Then we shouldn't keep her waiting," he said, and stepped down into the dark together with her.
