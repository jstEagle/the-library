## 8. The Charm-Seller's Confession

The cellar smelled of wet stone and old onions, and Edmund had never been so grateful for anything in his life.

They had found it by accident—or Marguerite had found it, by some instinct of hers that he was beginning to suspect had nothing to do with instinct at all. A rusted hatch behind a shuttered dyer's shop in the Tanner's Row, a ladder slick with condensation, and then darkness that swallowed them whole just as the boots of Corvane's sweep-teams began to echo down the lane above. Now they sat in that darkness with a single stub of candle between them, listening to the city breathe its fear through the floorboards.

The sweep had been going for hours. Every quarter hour or so, boots crossed the street above, unhurried, thorough. Corvane's men did not hurry. Hurrying was for people who doubted they would find what they sought. Edmund had learned that much in fifteen years of watching the temple's officers move through the archive like men walking through a garden they owned.

"You're shaking," Marguerite said.

"I'm cold."

"You're not cold."

He looked at her. In the candlelight her face was all planes and shadows, the high cheekbones that had always seemed merely striking now legible as inheritance, the faint gold that still ghosted beneath the skin of her wrists where the blood ran close to the surface. She had pulled her sleeves down over it. She kept pulling them down, he'd noticed, the way a man with a missing tooth keeps touching the gap.

"The cellar's cold," he said again, and then, because the lie sat in his chest like a swallowed stone, "That's the second lie I've told today."

"The second?"

"The temple asked me to name the anomaly I was seen with." He turned the candle stub in his fingers, watching the wax pool. "I told them I'd never seen you before in my life."

Marguerite said nothing. The boots passed overhead again, and they both went still, two animals sharing the same burrow, until the sound faded toward the river.

"First lie in fifteen years," Edmund said. "Fifteen years of copying ledgers and tithing records and confession rolls, and I never once put down a number that wasn't true. Do you know what they pay scribes for? Accuracy. A scribe who lies is worse than a scribe who steals. A stolen coin is one coin. A lie corrupts the whole ledger." He laughed, and it came out wrong, thin and high. "They'll audit my past work now. They'll find nothing, because there's nothing to find, and somehow that will make them more certain I'm hiding something."

"You could have named me," Marguerite said quietly. "You'd be in your cell right now. Warm. Fed. Debt forgiven."

"Yes."

"You don't even know me."

"No," Edmund agreed. "I don't."

And that was the truth of it, or near enough. He knew her the way you know weather, not the way you know a room. He knew the heat that came off her palms when she was frightened. He knew she bought a luck charm from him three days ago and had held it like it might actually work, and that something about that had made his chest ache in a way he hadn't examined. He knew that when the guard died on the rooftops—fell, or was thrown, or was burned, the details still lived in his memory like a splinter he couldn't dig out—she had made a sound he'd never heard a person make, a sound like something tearing.

He didn't know her. But he had chosen her. And the choosing frightened him more than Corvane did.

* * *

The boots stopped coming after a while. Somewhere above, a door slammed, voices argued in the flat cadence of men following orders they didn't understand, and then the street went quiet in the particular way of streets that are being watched. The sweep had moved on. Or it was waiting. With Corvane, Edmund was learning, those were often the same thing.

Marguerite had found an upturned crate to sit on. Edmund sat on the floor across from her, his back against the stone, his satchel in his lap. The satchel. He'd carried it through the rooftops and the gutters and the flooded lane, and now, in the candlelight, he caught himself doing what he'd been doing since the archive: keeping it angled away from her.

She noticed. Of course she noticed. She noticed everything—heat, light, the small evasions of a frightened man.

"What's in the bag, Edmund?"

"Charms."

"I know what's in the bag. I bought one." She tilted her head. "I mean, what's in the bag that you keep turning away from me."

He should have lied again. He was, apparently, capable of lying now; the muscle had been found, and like any new muscle it wanted use. But the cellar was dark and close and smelled of onions, and the candle was guttering, and there is a particular honesty that only comes out of people in cellars, at the end of a day in which everything they were has been taken from them.

He set the satchel down and opened it.

Inside, packed in straw, were the charms of Edmund Reyes. Copper discs stamped with the sunburst of Saint Aldric. Knotted cords in the temple colors. Little glass vials of river water blessed—or not blessed—with a prayer he'd written himself, in his best archival hand. And the prophecy book, wrapped in oilcloth, radiating that faint wrong warmth it had radiated since the page began to glow, like a stone that had been sitting in the sun too long.

He lifted out one of the copper discs and held it up between them.

"Tell me what you see," he said.

Marguerite frowned. "A luck charm."

"Turn it over."

She turned it. On the reverse, so faint you'd only see it if you knew to look, was a single stamped letter. *E.*

"Every charm I've ever sold has my mark on the back," Edmund said. "Not a maker's mark. My mark. Do you know why?"

She shook her head.

"Because I needed to be able to find them again. All of them. Every one I ever sold." He set the disc down on the crate between them, and it rocked slightly, catching the candlelight. "There are four hundred and six of them in Vhalen. I know, because I keep a ledger. Not the temple's ledger. My ledger. Four hundred and six charms, and every one of them is a lie."

The words came out of him now the way water comes through a cracked dam—slowly at first, then all at once, fifteen years of it.

"They don't work, Marguerite. The charms. None of them. There's no blessing in the river water—I filled those vials at my own kitchen tap and said words over them that I invented, words that sound old because I wrote them to sound old. The knots in the cords are just knots. The copper is just copper. I am a scribe indentured to a temple that has outlawed the very thing I sell, and I sell it anyway, and it is—" his voice caught, steadied— "it is nothing. It is kindness shaped like magic. It is a placebo with a prayer stamped on it. I have committed fifteen years of fraud against the most frightened people in this city, and I would do it again tonight, and I don't know what that makes me."

* * *

The silence after was long. The candle flame leaned and straightened. Somewhere in the walls, water dripped with the patience of stone.

Marguerite was looking at the copper disc. She picked it up, turned it in her fingers, and then—Edmund watched this happen and would remember it for the rest of his life—she began to laugh.

It started small, a breath through the nose, a twitch at the corner of her mouth. Then it grew. She pressed her hand over her mouth and her shoulders shook, and the laugh came out around her fingers anyway, and then she wasn't pressing her hand there to stop it, she was pressing it there because her face was coming apart, and the laugh turned into something else, something wetter, and she was laughing and crying at the same time, there in the onion-smelling dark, holding a worthless copper charm like it was the funniest and saddest thing anyone had ever handed her.

"Four hundred and six," she managed. "You *counted* them."

"I keep a ledger," Edmund said, wounded. "I'm a scribe. It's what I do."

"You invented *prayers.*"

"Old-sounding ones. There's a trick to it. You use the archaic second person—*thee* and *thou*—and you never end a sentence the way a sentence wants to end. Fear loves an awkward rhythm. It sounds like it must be ancient, because no one would choose to write that way on purpose."

She laughed harder, and then the laugh cracked down the middle and she was crying, really crying, and Edmund sat there in the wet stone dark not knowing what to do with his hands, this woman who could burn a city, weeping over his fraud.

"Say one," she begged him, wiping her face with her sleeve. "Say one of your fake prayers. Please. I need to hear it."

He should have refused. Some part of him, the part that had survived fifteen years by being small and careful and invisible, said *refuse, deflect, never show the mechanism.* But the mechanism was out. The dam was broken. And there was something in her face—something raw and asking—that made smallness feel like the greater sin.

He took the copper disc from her hand, held it in both palms, and said, in a voice worn smooth by fifteen years of market mornings:

"*Thee who walks in fear, walk on. The dark is only dark because the light is near it. What is carried with a whole heart cannot be dropped.*"

Silence.

"That's it?" Marguerite whispered.

"That's it. I wrote it for a ferryman who was afraid of deep water. He carried that disc for six years. He'd touch it every time the river got rough. He drowned last winter—fever, not the river, he'd have told you the charm kept him safe from the river to his last day—and his daughter came to my stall and asked if she could buy his charm back from me, because she wanted to be buried with it." Edmund looked down at his hands. "I sold it to her for one copper. It was the only honest price I ever charged. And I wrote her a new prayer for the grave, and she thanked me like I'd given her something real, and the worst part—" his voice did break now, and he let it— "the worst part is that I *had.* I'd given her something real. It just wasn't magic."

Marguerite reached across the space between them and took his hand.

Her skin was warm. Not burning, not the terrible heat of the rooftops—just warm, human-warm, or near enough to fool him. She held his hand the way you hold a thing you're afraid will be taken.

"My grandmother's prayers were real," she said. "Did I tell you that? Real words, real fire, the whole inheritance. And do you know what she said about them, near the end? She said the fire never once kept anyone safe. It kept *her* safe. It made her strong and it made her hunted and it made her die alone in a room with the shutters closed, and every prayer she ever spoke with it behind them was worth less to the people she loved than one of your copper lies." She squeezed his hand. "Your fraud kept hope alive better than real spells ever did, Edmund Reyes. I hope that doesn't comfort you. I hope it haunts you. It's the truest thing anyone in this city has done in a hundred years."

* * *

They sat like that for a while, hand in hand in the dark, and Edmund found that the shaking had stopped. Not because the danger had. The sweep would come back. Corvane did not lose things. But something had been set down between them, something heavy that each had been carrying alone, and the cellar felt less like a trap and more like a room.

"Four hundred and seven," Marguerite said at last.

"What?"

"The charm I bought. You said four hundred and six. You forgot yours." She nodded at his satchel, at the oilcloth-wrapped book glowing faintly through its wrapping. "Or does the prophecy book not count as a charm?"

"It counts as a death sentence."

"Then it's the only honest thing you've ever sold." She smiled, and it was a tired smile, a rooftop smile, the smile of someone running out of city. "You sold it to me, you know. Three days ago. You handed me a luck charm and said *may it keep what matters warm,* and then the light started coming out of your bag, and I thought—there it is. The thing I've been running from my whole life. It found me at a charm stall."

"I didn't sell it to you. It chose—"

"Don't." Her voice went quiet and hard. "Don't say it chose me. Everyone says that. My grandmother said it. The dead dragons say it, when they bother to say anything. *Chosen, chosen, chosen.* Like it's a gift." She looked down at their hands, still linked. "It's a debt, Edmund. I know debts. I've been paying one since before I was born. And so have you—just a smaller one, to a crueler god."

Edmund opened his mouth to answer, and that was when the candle went out.

Not guttered. Not drowned in its own wax. *Out*, all at once, snuffed like a pinched wick, and the dark of the cellar became total and pressing and alive. He heard Marguerite's breath catch. Felt the heat bloom off her skin in the blackness, sudden and defensive, gold light beginning to seep up through the sleeves she'd pulled down over her wrists—

And then, from the top of the ladder, through the rusted hatch, a voice.

Calm. Patient. Almost kind.

"Master Reyes," said the voice of the Inquisitor General. "You left your ledger at the temple. I've read it. Four hundred and six—very thorough. But I believe you're holding number four hundred and seven."

The hatch began, very slowly, to open.
