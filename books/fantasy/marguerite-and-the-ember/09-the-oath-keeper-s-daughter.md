## 9. The Oath-Keeper's Daughter

The cisterns beneath Vhalen smelled of drowned centuries.

Marguerite felt them before she saw them—the way she felt all heat now, the ember's call having sharpened her senses into something between a gift and a wound. Below the city's cobbled streets, below the cellars where Edmund had confessed his small honest frauds, the old aqueducts ran black and cold, but beneath the cold ran something else. A warmth. Faint and flickering, like a candle cupped in dying hands.

"She's down there," Marguerite said.

Edmund shifted beside her, his satchel—empty now of charms, heavy instead with the prophecy book—pressed to his chest like a shield. "How do you know?"

"I don't know. I *know*." She touched her own sternum, where the heat had lived since childhood, a coal she'd spent thirty years pretending was only her heart beating too fast. "It's like hearing someone call your name from another room. You don't know the words, but you know they're for you."

The descent was a ladder of rusted iron rungs set into slick stone, and the darkness below swallowed the lantern's light the way the sea swallows a dropped coin. Marguerite went first. She told herself it was because her eyes had grown strange in the past two days—able to find warmth in the dark, to read the world in gradients of heat like a page written in invisible ink. She told herself it wasn't because she wanted Edmund behind her, where she could hear him breathing, where she could pretend she was protecting him rather than the other way around.

The water came to their knees. It was colder than the air, which made the whole flooded hall a map of contradictions: the chill of the water blooming blue-grey in her new sight, the warmth of Edmund's body a gold-edged silhouette beside her, and far ahead—

Far ahead, a light that was not light at all.

* * *

Sera had made a home of the end of the world.

She sat on a dry shelf of stone above the waterline, an island in the flooded hall, and she had arranged her dying the way other people arranged a sitting room. A blanket, threadbare but clean. A clay lamp burning oil that must have cost her everything she'd hoarded. Books—dozens of them, swollen with damp, their spines cracked—piled around her like children gathered close against the cold. And at the center of it all, a brazier of black iron, its coals long dead, though Marguerite could see the ghost of heat still clinging to the metal, weeks old, maybe months.

The old woman looked up when their lantern found her, and Marguerite watched her face do something terrible and gentle at once. Recognition. Not of them—of *her*. Of what she was.

"Oh," Sera said softly. "Oh, you came. And so late, child. We are all so very late."

"You know who I am?" Marguerite's voice came out smaller than she intended.

"I know what you carry. Or what carries you—sometimes it is difficult to say which." Sera's eyes were pale, the blue washed out of them like old linen, but they fixed on Marguerite with an attention that felt almost physical. "Come into the light where I can see you properly. The dark is honest but it is not *kind*."

They waded to her island. Edmund helped Marguerite up onto the stone shelf, and his hand lingered a half-second longer than the climb required, and Marguerite found herself cataloguing that half-second the way she'd begun cataloguing everything about him—hoarding small warm things against the cold she could feel coming.

Sera was dying. There was no polite way around it. Her skin had gone the color of candle wax, and her breathing had the wet, labored hitch of lungs filling slowly with something that was not air. When Marguerite knelt beside her, she could see it in the heat-shapes: the old woman's body was a house with the fires going out one by one, room after room, and only one flame still stubborn in the center of her chest.

"You're the oath-keeper," Edmund said. "The last one. The book said—the prophecy—"

"The book said a great many things. Books usually do. It's their way of getting people to do what needs doing." Sera coughed, and the sound went on too long. When she recovered, she smiled at Edmund with something like affection. "The charm-seller. I know you too. Your little stall on Cordwainer Row. You sold my neighbor's daughter a charm against nightmares, three years ago. Clay and twine and a prayer you made up on the spot."

Edmund went very still. "You knew it was—"

"Child, I have been the last keeper of a dying order for forty years. Do you think I couldn't recognize another person selling hope in small packages?" She laughed, and the laugh cost her; Marguerite saw the flame in her chest gutter. "The difference between you and me is that I had the real thing to sell, and no one wanted it. You had nothing, and they lined up. There's a lesson in that, if either of us lives long enough to learn it."

Marguerite's hands had gone cold in her lap. "The prophecy. It named today. It named *him*. It says one of us is the ember-bearer. I need you to tell me which, and I need you to tell me how to make it not be me."

The old woman was quiet for a long moment. The lamp flame bent in some current of air that had no business existing this far underground, and for an instant Marguerite thought she saw a shape in it—long, sinuous, vast—before it was only flame again.

"Come here," Sera said. "Closer. Let me see your hands."

Marguerite gave her hands. They looked ordinary: work-worn, the nails bitten short from a lifetime of nervous habit, a burn scar on the left palm from a cookpot when she was nine. But when Sera turned them over and held them in her own, the old woman's breath caught, and Marguerite felt the heat in her chest *answer*—rise toward the touch like a cat leaning into a known hand.

"Your grandmother," Sera whispered. "Ilsette."

"You knew her?"

"I trained her. And her mother before her, and hers before that, all the way back to the burning." Sera's grip tightened, surprisingly strong. "The oath-keepers were the dragon-fire's custodians. When the war ended and the last flame was sealed, someone had to hold the key. Not a key of iron, child. A key of *blood*. A line of women sworn to carry the memory of the fire in their bodies, so that it would never be lost, never be found, never be used—until such a time as the world grew wise enough, or foolish enough, to need it again."

"I never asked for this." The words tore out of her before she could make them gentle. "I never wanted—I wanted to be *ordinary*. I wanted a small life. A shop, maybe. A cat. I spent my whole childhood burning my bedsheets and scalding my hands and being told to hide it, hide it, hide it, and now a book in a temple archive says I'm going to either start a war or—"

"Or let the last fire die," Sera finished quietly. "Yes."

"Then let it die!" Marguerite heard her own voice crack against the cistern walls and come back to her, doubled, accusing. "Let it die and let me be nobody. That's what I *want*."

And Sera—dying, ancient, patient as stone—looked at her with such sorrow that Marguerite's anger died in her throat.

"Child," the old woman said, "do you think I chose this? I was a girl in this city when my mother sat me down and told me what our blood was for. I cried for a week. I ran away twice. I came back both times because the fire doesn't ask, Marguerite. It was never a question of wanting." She drew a ragged breath. "But hear me now, because I do not have the luxury of gentle words. The ember must be carried by a willing vessel. That is the first law and the last law. The oath was written that way, in the dragon-tongue, in the oldest tongue there is. Willing, or nothing."

"What happens if it isn't willing?"

Sera closed her eyes. "Then it takes one anyway. Fire does not negotiate. It burns its way into whatever is nearest and strongest—and an unwilling vessel is a lamp with a cracked glass. The flame spills through every fracture. It will hollow you out and wear you like a glove, and everything you were will be ash, and the fire will not care, because fire never cares. It only burns." Her eyes opened, and they were terrible with clarity. "The prophecy gives you a choice. It always did. That is what the prophets never understood about their own books—the future isn't written, it's *offered*. One of you will carry it. The scribe or the blood. But only if one of you says *yes*."

* * *

Edmund had gone to the far edge of the stone shelf, giving them the illusion of privacy, and Marguerite was distantly grateful. She sat with the dying keeper's hands in hers and felt the last flame in the old woman's chest lean toward the coal in her own like a moth toward a mirror.

"Tell me about her," Marguerite said. "My grandmother. Tell me something true."

Sera smiled. "She was afraid her whole life. Just like you. She hid in a fishing village and married a man who never knew, and she was happy, mostly, in the way people are happy who have put something precious in a box and buried it and then listen, every night, for the sound of digging." The old woman's voice was fraying now, words coming slower, each one costing. "But at the end—she came to me, Marguerite. Old and gray and done with hiding. And she said: *I buried it too long. Tell my granddaughter's granddaughter, when she comes to you. Tell her the fire is patient, but it is not kind, and it will not wait for her to be ready.*"

"I'm not ready."

"No one is ready. That is what willing *means*—to step forward without being ready. If you waited until you were ready, it would not be a choice. It would only be arithmetic." Sera's grip was failing now; Marguerite could feel the strength leaving her hands like water through fingers. "Listen. Listen, child. Corvane is coming. He has read the same book you have, and he believes—oh, he *believes*, and belief is the driest tinder in the world. When he comes, you will not have the time to grow into this. You will have only what you have."

"Then tell me what to do. Tell me how to carry it. Tell me the oath."

Something moved across the old woman's face—regret, or relief, or both wearing the same expression.

"The oath is not words you speak," Sera said. "It is words you *become*. It is in the dragon-tongue, and the dragon-tongue is not learned, child. It is *remembered*. And there is only one place left where it can be remembered." Her eyes drifted, unfocused, toward the dark water and the dark beyond it. "The dead remember it. The dead in the Cinderfen, and the dead in the deep places, and the dead who sealed the flame with their own bodies when the oaths of the living failed. They have been waiting a hundred years for someone to ask."

"That's not an answer. That's a riddle."

"It's an inheritance." Sera laughed, wet and thin. "They always arrive too late to refuse gracefully. That's what makes them inheritances." She pulled Marguerite closer, and her wax-pale face was suddenly very near, and her voice dropped to a whisper that was almost not a whisper at all. "One more thing. The ember knows its own. When the time comes, it will test you—not your strength. Fire doesn't care about strength. It will test your *yes*. Whether it is whole. Whether it is true. Whether there is some small corner of you still holding out, still wanting your small life and your cat and your shop." Her pale eyes bored into Marguerite's. "And if there is, it will find it. And it will burn it."

Marguerite wanted to say *then I'll have no corner*. She wanted to say it the way heroes say things in books, clean and certain. What came out instead, in the dark, over the hands of a dying woman, was the truth:

"I'm terrified."

"Good," Sera said, and smiled, and it was the kindest thing anyone had said to her in years. "Fear means you understand the size of it. The ones who aren't afraid are the ones who've made it small in their minds, and those are the ones it eats." She sagged back against her books, breath whistling. "Now go. Both of you. The water will rise tonight—the old flood-channels still answer the moon, and this hall will be under two feet by morning. Take nothing. Leave the lamp."

"The books—" Edmund started.

"Are for the fire to read," Sera said, "when it comes back into the world. It will want to know what it missed." She closed her eyes. "Go. And Marguerite—"

Marguerite stopped at the water's edge.

"Your grandmother's last words to me were a question. She asked: *will she hate us? For what we carried down to her?*" The old woman's eyes stayed closed. "I never had an answer. I have one now. Tell her, when you understand it yourself, whether you hate her. That answer is the last page of the oath. Everything else is only grammar."

* * *

They climbed in silence. The iron rungs were slick and the dark pressed close, and Marguerite's whole body was a struck bell, ringing with everything Sera had said. Behind and below them, the little clay lamp shrank to a spark, then to a memory.

At the top of the ladder, in the cold mouth of the cellar, Edmund caught her arm.

"Marguerite." His voice was rough. "What she said—about one of us saying yes. If it comes to it—" He stopped. Started again. "I've spent fifteen years being careful. Being nothing. And I'm telling you, if the choice comes, I know which of us knows how to survive being nobody. I've had practice."

She looked at him in the dark, this trembling, brave, ridiculous man who sold hope in clay charms, and felt the ember in her chest turn over like something waking.

"Edmund," she said quietly, "the fire doesn't want practice. It wants *yes*." And then, because she could not stop herself, because the dying woman's question was already working in her like a splinter: "But we should hurry. The Cinderfen is three days east—and I think I've just inherited a conversation with the dead."

Far below, faint as a heartbeat, the cistern lamp went out.
