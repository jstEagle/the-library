## 10. What Corvane Wants

The interrogation room beneath the Bastion of the Question had been built by men who understood that cruelty was a craft, and that the finest instruments of the craft were not irons at all.

Inquisitor General Corvane had redesigned the room himself, in his first year of office. He had the racks removed. He had the hooks taken down, the braziers emptied, the walls scrubbed of their old stains. In place of these he installed a table of pale ashwood, two chairs of equal height, a lamp with a steady flame, and a carafe of water that was always fresh, always cool, always full. He had the ceiling raised so that the room did not press down on its occupants but opened above them like a sky, and he had the floor laid with river stone so that every footstep sounded like a footstep and nothing else.

His predecessor had called it a waste of the Question's budget. His predecessor had also believed that pain produced truth, which was the single most persistent error in the history of an institution built on errors. Pain produced whatever the sufferer believed the torturer wanted to hear, delivered at whatever volume made the pain stop. It produced noise. Corvane had no use for noise. He had spent twenty years in the service of the empire learning to distinguish noise from signal, and he had learned that the two sounded almost nothing alike.

Signal, in Corvane's experience, sounded like a tired man asking for water.

"Drink," he said now, pouring for the abbot himself.

Abbot Ossian Veyra had been the pride of the Temple of the Ninefold Dawn for thirty years. He was seventy-one years old, broad through the shoulders even in age, with a voice trained to fill a nave and hands that had signed away more indentures than any man in Vhalen. He had been taken at dawn from his own cell, without struggle, without protest, with only a single question asked in his calm liturgical baritone: *Is this the empire's doing, or the Question's?* Corvane had answered, truthfully, *Both*, and the abbot had nodded as though a small unpleasant theorem had been confirmed.

Now the abbot sat in the equal chair, drank the equal water, and looked at Corvane with the mild patience of a man who had outlasted four Inquisitors General and expected to outlast a fifth.

"You have my scribes' records," Ossian said. "My ledgers. The indenture rolls. I assume you've read the Reyes file."

"I've read everything."

"Then you know the temple's interest in this matter is pecuniary. A debt of four hundred crowns, compounded. The boy is an asset. The woman is an unknown. The temple does not pursue unknowns; it pursues assets." The abbot folded his hands. "You've wasted a morning, Inquisitor. I have matins."

Corvane did not answer at once. He rose, crossed to the room's single window—a slit, high, admitting a blade of grey morning light—and stood in it, letting the cold touch the back of his neck. Below, the market quarter lay sealed. He could see the barricades from here, the long lines of soldiers at the gates, the thin smoke of cookfires from households that had been told, politely and with imperial courtesy, that no one would be leaving until the Question was satisfied. Nine thousand people, give or take. Nine thousand lives paused mid-gesture, like a choir holding a single note.

He had given the order at the fourth bell. He had not hesitated. He never hesitated. Hesitation, in his experience, was simply cruelty delayed until it was less effective.

"You had a scribe named Reyes," Corvane said, still facing the window. "Fifteen years indentured. Harmless, by every report. Sold charms at the Ferrier market. Placebos, we believe—the temple's own assessors found no working in any of them. And yet the temple kept him. Fifteen years of a debt the temple could have sold, discharged, or forgiven at any time. Fifteen years of a man who generated, by my arithmetic, less than the cost of his own ink." He turned. "You don't keep an asset that runs at a loss, Abbot. You keep a *tool*. So I will ask you once, plainly, and then I will ask you in ways you will find less comfortable, and I would prefer the first asking. What does the Temple of the Ninefold Dawn know about the ember of the Ashvault?"

Something moved behind the abbot's eyes. Not fear—Corvane had seen enough fear to know its weather. It was closer to the look of a man who has been playing a game of stones against a worthy opponent and has just realized the board is larger than he thought.

"You have been reading the sealed archives," Ossian said.

"The empire's archives. The temple has merely been borrowing them."

"The prophecy of the Ember-Bearer is apocrypha. It has no canonical standing. It was sealed by—"

"By the Council of Nine in the year of the Ashing, one hundred and four years ago, and sealed not because it was false but because it was *dangerous*, and the temple has kept it locked ever since, and last week a scribe found tomorrow's date written in it beside a sketch of his own face, and the temple's response was to attempt to sell that scribe to me as though he were a sack of grain." Corvane returned to the table and sat. He poured the abbot more water, an old habit of courtesy that he had never managed to break and had never tried. "I know what the prophecy says, Abbot. I have known since I was nineteen years old. What I do not know is what the temple intends to *do* about it. And I have a very low tolerance, these days, for institutions that know more than they say."

The lamp flame stood perfectly still between them. Corvane had chosen the lamp for that reason. A still flame gave a man nothing to watch but the man across the table.

Ossian Veyra was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had lost some of its nave-filling resonance. It was, for the first time, simply the voice of an old man.

"You want to know what the temple intends," he said. "The temple intends what it has always intended. Survival. The Ninefold Dawn has stood in Vhalen for six hundred years because it has never once taken a side it could not abandon. The ember, the dragons, the oath—all of it is older than us and larger than us, and our whole wisdom consists of knowing when to bow and when to look elsewhere. If the ember wakes, we will bless it. If it dies, we will bury it. If an Inquisitor General asks us questions, we will answer them *just* slowly enough to seem thoughtful." He smiled, thin and tired. "You are asking the wrong institution for conviction, Corvane. We are a weather-vane. We point where the wind points."

"Then point," Corvane said, "and tell me where the wind is going."

The abbot studied him. Corvane let himself be studied. He knew what the man saw: a tall, spare figure of fifty-two, grey at the temples, dressed in the unadorned charcoal of the Question's high office, with a face that people described—when they described it at all, which most did not—as *patient*. He had spent a lifetime being described as patient. It was the most useful misreading in the empire. Patience implied waiting. Corvane was not waiting. Corvane had been moving, continuously and without rest, for thirty-three years, and the fact that his movement looked like stillness was simply the finest craft he knew.

"You lost your family," the abbot said quietly. "In the Greyweald plague. The year—"

"Say it."

"The year you entered the seminary. Your father, your mother, your two sisters. Ashfield village. Four hundred and six dead in six weeks." The old man's voice was gentle now, and Corvane hated the gentleness, and let him keep it, because a man who was being gentle was a man who was talking. "The temple's own histories record it. The dragons were petitioned. Three times, by three delegations. They refused to intervene. The last of them was already dying, they said. The fire was already guttering. They could not spare what little remained to save a village of mortals." Ossian spread his hands. "I am sorry. I have read the file, as you say. I am not so old that I have forgotten what it is to lose—"

"Do not," Corvane said, "do that."

He had not raised his voice. He never raised his voice. But the temperature of the room changed, or seemed to, and the abbot's hands came back down to the table.

"You think I am telling you this to wound you," Ossian said. "I am telling you because you asked me where the wind is going, and the wind, Inquisitor, is going wherever grief goes. Yours. Mine. The girl's. The scribe's. The whole wretched prophecy turns on grief—it names an ember-bearer, but what it is really naming is a *mourner* with power enough to make the mourning matter. So yes. I know about your family. Every man in my office knows. It is the first line of your file, and we are not a careless institution, whatever else we are."

Corvane sat very still.

He had built a life on the principle that his grief was private infrastructure—invisible, load-bearing, never to be discussed. He had been nineteen when the Greyweald fever came down out of the hills into Ashfield. He had been in the seminary at Vhalen, three days' ride away, and by the time the news reached him the village had already been burned—burned, the official account said, *as a mercy and a containment measure*, the standard protocol for plague-towns, the empire's answer to a sickness it could not cure. His father had been a wheelwright. His mother had kept bees. His sisters had been nine and thirteen, and the younger one, Liss, had once spent an entire summer teaching a crippled starling to sing, and had succeeded, and the bird had outlived her by four years in the seminary courtyard, and Corvane had fed it every morning of those four years and told no one why, and when it died he had buried it himself at the base of the bell tower and gone inside and taken his vows the same afternoon.

The dragons had been petitioned. Three times. He had read the petitions himself, years later, in the imperial archive—his own grandfather's handwriting on the third one, the ink going shaky toward the signature. *We ask only that you look upon us. We ask only that the fire that watches all things watch us a little.* And the answer had come back in the dragon-tongue, translated by the temple's scholars with what Corvane could only describe as editorial mercy: *The fire is nearly spent. What remains is sworn. We cannot.*

*We cannot.* Not *we will not*. The distinction had tormented him for a decade before he understood it, and when he understood it, it tormented him worse. They could not. The last dragons had bound their remaining fire into an oath, had sealed it in a vault beneath the Cinderfen, had sworn it away to end a war—and in doing so had made themselves unable to save four hundred and six people in a plague village, including a nine-year-old girl who could teach a starling to sing.

That was the arithmetic Corvane had carried for thirty-three years. Not hatred. Arithmetic. The dragons had made a choice, a century before he was born, and the interest on that choice had compounded exactly as the temple's debts compounded, and the debt had come due in Ashfield in the Greyweald fever, and the payment had been collected in full.

And now the prophecy said the fire might wake again. Might be carried. Might be *reignited*—the word the apocrypha used, the word that had been sealed away, the word that had brought him to this room. Reignited. The war un-dead. The oath unsworn. And the empire—which had been founded, as every schoolchild learned, on the peace the dragons' sacrifice had purchased—would discover that its founding legitimacy was a candle that someone, somewhere, might choose to relight.

Or not. That was the other branch of the prophecy, the one the temple's scholars had argued over for a century. The ember could be carried into hiding and *let die*. Allowed to gutter out forever, taking with it the last of the dragon-fire, taking with it the possibility—remote, monstrous, real—of the war's return. And taking with it, also, the possibility of the fire ever again being *asked*. Asked to heal. Asked to look upon a plague village. Asked to watch a little.

Corvane wanted it dead. He wanted it dead the way a man wants a debt cancelled—completely, finally, with the ledger burned and the creditor gone. Not hoarded. Not hidden and preserved and *kept*, the way the temple kept everything, against a rainy day that always seemed to cost someone else. Dead. Let the last fire go out with the last dragon, and let the world learn to live without asking, because asking was the cruelest thing of all. Asking was a nine-year-old girl's voice through a starling's throat. Asking was a shaky signature on a petition that was never answered.

He had never said any of this aloud. He had never needed to. It was not a conviction he carried; it was the ground he stood on.

"The temple will cooperate," Ossian said at last, into the stillness. "You should know that. Whatever you intend—the girl, the scribe, the vault—the Ninefold Dawn will not obstruct the Question. We will open our archives. We will surrender our records. We will even, if you require it, give you the Reyes indenture entire, with the clause the Council added." He paused, and Corvane saw the old man choose, deliberately, to say the next part. "You have read the clause?"

"I have read every word the temple owns."

"Then you know the debt was never four hundred crowns."

"I know," Corvane said, "that the temple has been feeding a boy to a prophecy for fifteen years, the way one feeds a calf for market, and that the Council of Nine wrote a clause into his indenture that only activates if the ember stirs, and that the clause is not about money at all." He leaned forward, and for the first time that morning he let the patience fall away from his face, and watched the abbot understand, with the particular horror of a weather-vane discovering the wind, that the stillness across the table had been movement all along. "I know that your institution sold a child into servitude as a *vessel in reserve*, and I know that when the prophecy woke, you moved to deliver him to whoever would take him, and the only reason he is not in this room instead of you is that a woman with dragon's blood was buying luck charms at his stall when your men came for him."

The abbot had gone grey. "You cannot prove the clause's intent. The Council's deliberations were sealed—"

"Everything is sealed, Abbot. That is the temple's entire genius. You seal the apocrypha, you seal the deliberations, you seal the clause, and then you sit in your nave for six hundred years and call it wisdom." Corvane stood. "I do not need to prove it. I need only the deliberations, and you are going to give me the key to the Council vault before the next bell, and you are going to do it *willingly*, because you have just told me, with your own mouth, that the Ninefold Dawn does not obstruct the Question. I am merely holding you to your weather-vane."

Ossian Veyra looked up at him for a long moment. And then, incredibly, the old man laughed—a dry, cracked, genuine laugh, the laugh of a man who has just been beaten by a better player and can appreciate the game.

"They said you were the most dangerous man in the empire," he said, "because you never raise your voice. They were wrong. You're the most dangerous man in the empire because you *grieve in complete sentences*." He pushed back his chair. "The key is in my cell, in the false bottom of my writing desk, under the psalter. The deliberations are in the black folio. And Inquisitor—" he paused at the door, and his voice dropped, and for a moment he was not a weather-vane at all but something older and wearier, "—the clause names a price. When you read it, you will understand why we kept the boy alive rather than the reverse. And you may find, when you have read it, that you and I are not so differently built after all. You want the fire dead. The Council of Nine wanted something dead too. Read the folio."

The door closed behind him. Corvane stood alone in the pale room with the still lamp and the full carafe and the blade of grey light, and he found that his hands, which never shook, were not entirely steady.

He did not send for the key at once. He went to the window instead and looked down at the sealed quarter, at the smoke of nine thousand paused lives, and somewhere in the maze of rooftops below, two fugitives were moving—he could feel it, the way a man feels weather in an old wound. The scribe and the woman. The vessel in reserve and the blood of the oath-keepers. Running toward the Cinderfen and the vault where the last fire slept, carrying between them the thing he had spent thirty-three years wanting dead.

*Let it die*, he thought, with a clarity that was almost tenderness. *Let it go out. Let no one ever again have to stand in a plague village and ask the sky for help and be told the fire is sworn away. Let the asking end.*

He turned from the window and rang the bell for his clerks.

"Prepare riders," he said. "Fast ones, with imperial writ. They ride for the Cinderfen road, and they ride tonight." He paused, and looked at the black space on his desk where the abbot's folio would soon lie open, and added, quietly, the way a man adds a stone to a cairn:

"And find me everything the temple knows about a scribe named Edmund Reyes. Everything. I want to know what he was *for*."
