## 11. The Road Through Cinderfen

The Cinderfen announced itself a full day before they reached it.

Marguerite smelled it first—a thin, mineral tang on the wind, like a forge left cold and then remembered. Then the light changed. The sun, which had been an ordinary autumn sun over the wheat country behind them, took on a strange filtered quality, as though it were passing through smoke that had nowhere else to go. By late afternoon the horizon ahead had turned the color of old iron, and along its whole width ran a faint ruddy shimmer, the way heat rises off summer cobbles except that this was autumn, and the heat was coming from the ground itself.

"They're still burning," Edmund said.

He had stopped at the crest of the low ridge where the farm road ended and the fen road began, his satchel clutched to his chest the way he always held it when he was frightened—like a scribe's shield, like something that could be surrendered in place of himself. Marguerite watched him look out at the dark plain below and saw him do the thing he did with fear: file it, categorize it, make it small enough to carry.

"A hundred years," she said. "The war ended a hundred years ago."

"Embers don't keep calendars." He unslung the prophecy book from under his coat—the book they had both stopped pretending was anything but what it was—and turned to the pages he'd marked. "There's a verse about this. I copied it out last night while you slept." He read aloud, quietly, the way one reads in a house where someone is dying:

*"Where the fallen lie too great to bury, let them lie burning, that no hand may raise them; let the road pass between, and let none answer if they speak."*

Marguerite felt the words settle into her chest like stones dropped into water.

*Let none answer if they speak.*

Because she could already hear them.

It had started two nights ago, in the inn at Marrow's Ford—not voices exactly, not yet, but a pressure behind her ears, a sense of vast attention turning toward her like sunflowers toward light. She had woken with her palms glowing faintly through her gloves and had sat by the window until dawn, watching the eastern sky, waiting for whatever was calling her to arrive. Now it had arrived. It lay spread across half the horizon, smoldering.

"Edmund," she said. "They're not just burning. They're *waiting*."

He looked at her then—at her hands, at the thin gold light leaking between her fingers—and she watched him decide, again, not to run. She would never stop being surprised by that. Everyone else in her life, upon learning what she was, had found a reason to be elsewhere by morning. This trembling little fraud of a charm-seller simply tightened his grip on his satchel and said, "Then we should know the road before the road knows us," and sat down right there on the ridge to work.

That was how they crossed into the Cinderfen: side by side, at dusk, with a dead man's map and a living woman's dread.

Edmund's POV

The fen road was exactly what the verse promised: a raised causeway of packed ash and broken slag, running straight as a ruled line through a plain of fire-scarred earth. On either side, at distances that varied from twenty paces to half a mile, the war-dead lay where they had fallen.

Edmund had imagined skeletons. What he saw instead was worse and stranger: shapes. Vast shapes, some of them longer than temple ships, their outlines preserved in fused glass and carbonized scale, so that here a ribcage arched out of the ground like the frame of a burned cathedral and there a wing lay folded across a hillside like a collapsed tent, black and brittle and enormous. Between the dragon-falls lay the smaller shapes—the oath-keepers, the soldiers, the ones who had stood with the dragons rather than against them—all of them rendered in the same terrible medium of heat and time. Nothing rotted in the Cinderfen. Nothing was allowed to finish dying.

And everything smoked. Thin gray threads rose from a hundred thousand wounds in the earth, and the air tasted of copper and old grief.

Edmund walked with the prophecy book open in one hand, checking the verse fragments against the terrain the way he'd once checked ledger entries against receipts. It was the only thing keeping his mind from unraveling. Numbers, lines, correspondences—he could live inside those. He had lived inside them for fifteen years.

"The third bridge is gone," he murmured, consulting his notes. "But the verse says *'where the river forgets its name, cross at the standing stone.'* There—" He pointed ahead to a monolith rising from the ash-choked channel, carved over entirely with scales of text worn nearly smooth. "We cross there."

"You're reading a dead language off a burnt rock and navigating by poetry," Marguerite said. Her voice was steady but her face was pale beneath its tan, and her eyes kept flicking to the smoking shapes on either side of the causeway. "In case you're wondering how that sounds."

"It sounds like my entire career," Edmund said. "I sold people protection charms made of string and confidence. At least this poetry has the decency to be about something real."

She laughed—a short, startled sound, quickly swallowed—and for a moment the fen was just a bad road and she was just a woman walking beside him, and Edmund understood why people bought his charms. Not because they believed. Because believing, even falsely, even for an hour, was a place to stand.

Then the nearest shape exhaled.

It wasn't breath, not really. A vent in the ground beside the causeway sighed open, releasing a plume of glowing ash, and the heat of it washed over them like a hand, and Marguerite went rigid—went *lit*, every vein in her forearms shining gold through her sleeves—and Edmund heard, or thought he heard, or felt her hearing, a sound too large for the word *voice*. A groan of cooling metal the size of a mountain. A word, maybe. Or the memory of one.

"What did it say?" he whispered.

Her eyes were wide and wet. "It said—" She pressed her gloved hands over her ears, which did nothing, because the voice wasn't arriving through her ears. "It said *little sister*. It said *you kept the shape we gave you*. Edmund, it knows me. It knows me by name, and I don't—I never learned—"

"*Let none answer if they speak,*" Edmund quoted, and hated the verse, and hated the dead who'd written it, because it was easy to write such a rule when your blood didn't glow.

"We can't answer," Marguerite said. But her voice cracked on it, and Edmund saw the truth underneath: part of her wanted to. Part of her had been wanting to her whole life, in a silence so complete she'd mistaken it for peace.

He did the only thing he knew how to do. He took her hand—through the glove, feeling the banked heat of it like a stone kept near a hearth—and said, "Keep walking. I'll talk. You don't have to say anything at all."

And he talked. Nonsense, mostly—market gossip, the price of tallow candles, the abbot's infamous snoring—filling the air between her and the dead the way he'd once filled charm-bottles with string. It worked badly. It worked enough.

Behind them, far back along the causeway, something moved that was not wind and not smoke.

Neither of them saw it. Yet.

Marguerite's POV

Night in the Cinderfen was a country of embers.

The darkness came down and the ground answered it, ten thousand points of dull red glow marking the places where the war-dead still held their heat, so that walking the causeway after dusk was like walking through a field of buried stars. Marguerite found it beautiful in the way that things are beautiful when they are also unbearable. Every glow was a body. Every body had been someone's—someone's ally, someone's god, someone's whole cosmology—and a century later they were still lying there, still refusing the dignity of ash, because the oath that ended the war had required it. *Lie burning*, the verse said. *That no hand may raise them.* A graveyard designed to be a warning, maintained by fire, forever.

And they spoke to her all night.

Not in words, at first. In pressure, in warmth, in images that arrived behind her eyes like weather: wings wider than rivers; a sky full of fire moving in formation; a city—Vhalen, it must have been Vhalen—young and golden and screaming. She walked with her jaw clenched and her hands fisted and said nothing, said nothing, said nothing, because the verse had told her not to and because some deep animal part of her understood that answering was a door, and doors open in both directions.

But near midnight, exhausted, stumbling, she slipped.

She reached out—without deciding to, without any more intention than a drowning woman has when she grasps a rope—and touched one of the glows with her bare hand.

The world opened.

She was falling through a century. She was a dragon named—for the name arrived whole, vast, untranslatable, but the closest human shape of it was *Vessh-ka-the-Unburned*—she was Vessh, and she was dying, and around her the other dying were making a sound that was not pain but *agreement*, a chorus of consent, because the choice had been made freely: to lie burning rather than rise again, to become a seal rather than a weapon, to give their deaths to the locking of the last flame. And at the center of the agreement, holding the thread of it, a woman with golden blood and Marguerite's own face was speaking words in a language that Marguerite's bones recognized and her mind did not—

"—Marguerite!"

She came back to herself on her knees in the ash, Edmund gripping her shoulders, the glow of her own skin reflected in his terrified eyes. How long? Minutes. The sky hadn't moved.

"Don't touch them," he said. "Whatever it gave you—don't—"

"It showed me the oath." Her voice came out scraped raw. "Edmund, the sealing of the ember—it wasn't a prison. It was a *promise*. All of them, dying on purpose, together, to hold it shut. And the woman—the oath-keeper—she was the hinge. The flame answers her line. It's been calling my family for a hundred years because we're the *lock*, and locks are supposed to—"

She stopped. Because she had almost said it. *Locks are supposed to be opened.*

Edmund heard it anyway. She watched him hear it, watched him go very still, watched the scribe in him reach for the ledger of implications and start writing entries.

"That's why Corvane wants you," he said slowly. "Not the ember. *You.* The ember without an oath-keeper is just fire. But you—" He didn't finish. He didn't have to. Somewhere behind them, riders were following their trail through a field of the dead, and Marguerite finally understood that she was not fleeing toward the Ashvault.

She was being delivered to it—from both directions at once.

Edmund's POV

Dawn found them at the standing stone, and the standing stone found them wanting.

Up close, the monolith was covered in dragon-script, and Edmund—who had spent fifteen years copying texts he wasn't permitted to read, who had learned six dead alphabets the way lonely men learn instruments—discovered to his horror that he could almost parse it. Almost. The grammar eluded him, but the structure didn't, and structure was his native tongue. Names. Dates. A list, descending, generation by generation: the oath-keeper's line, each daughter entered after her mother, the way temples recorded debt-succession.

The last entry was not a name. It was a date.

He checked it three times against his almanac before he let himself believe it. Then he checked the corresponding fragment of prophecy verse, the one he'd been carrying since the archive, the one that had started everything, and the two dates agreed, and the world tilted.

"Marguerite." His voice sounded distant to himself. "The stone lists your line. It ends with tomorrow."

She came to stand beside him. She read it. He felt her go quiet in a way that had nothing calm in it.

"So it's real," she said. "All of it. The vault, the choice, the—" She laughed, a terrible small sound. "I spent my whole life trying to be ordinary. I used to pray to saints I didn't believe in that the heat in my hands was some illness, something a physician could bleed out of me. And the whole time there was a rock in a swamp with my name on it, waiting."

"Not your name." Edmund touched the final entry. "No name. Just the date. Which means—" and here the scribe in him, the man of ledgers and entries, laid it out with terrible neatness— "which means the stone doesn't know *who*. Only *when*. The prophecy names the day, not the person. Corvane thinks you're the ember-bearer. Sera thought you were. But the record only says someone of your blood, on this date, at the vault."

"Or no one," Marguerite said. "Maybe the entry means nothing. Maybe the fire dies unwon and the whole ugly century just... ends."

"Maybe." He looked east, toward the Ashvault, three days' walk through the burning plain. He looked west, back along the causeway, where the thin gray threads of smoke rose from the dead, and where—if the movement he'd glimpsed at dusk had been what he feared—riders were rising with the sun. "Or maybe the stone is a doorframe, and doors don't care which way the wind blows through them."

Marguerite was silent a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had changed—leveled, resolved, the voice of someone setting down a weight she'd carried since childhood and picking up a different one, heavier but at least honestly labeled.

"Then we walk fast," she said, "and we get there first."

They shouldered their packs. Behind them, at the edge of sight, the western horizon had grown a new feature: a smudge of dust, moving.

Ahead, the Cinderfen stretched away into its red-glowing morning, and somewhere in its deepest reaches, under a hundred years of ash, the last flame waited—patient as a debt, warm as a hearth, hungry as a name.

As they stepped onto the causeway, the dead dragons began, very softly, to sing.

Marguerite did not answer.

But she did not cover her ears either.
