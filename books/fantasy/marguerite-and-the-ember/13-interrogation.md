## 13. Interrogation

They did not bind his hands.

That was the first thing Edmund noticed, and the noticing itself was a kind of wound. He had been dragged from the tollhouse in chains—iron at his wrists, iron at his throat, Anselm's hand heavy on his shoulder like a father's blessing—and then, somewhere between the tollhouse and wherever this was, the chains had been removed. He had expected a cell. He had expected the rack, or the pincers, or whatever instruments the Inquisition kept behind its gray doors now that fire was contraband and pain was merely paperwork.

Instead they gave him a chair. A good one, cushioned, angled toward a small iron stove whose warmth was the only warmth in a room built of wet stone. They gave him tea. It was good tea—Vhaleni black with honey, the way he liked it, the way *only someone who had read his file* would know he liked it—and it sat untouched in his hands because his hands would not stop shaking.

And across the desk, reading by lamplight as though the world were an archive and every soul in it a ledger entry, sat the Inquisitor General.

Corvane was not what the market rumors had made him. The rumors said tall; he was merely upright. The rumors said scarred; his face was smooth and unremarkable, a clerk's face, a face you would forget in a queue. He wore no insignia beyond a plain chain of office, and his eyes—gray, patient, terribly awake—moved across a document in front of him with the unhurried attention of a man who had already won and was simply confirming the arithmetic.

"You don't drink," Corvane observed, without looking up.

"I'm not thirsty."

"A lie." Still no heat in it. "You're terrified. Terrified men are always thirsty; fear dries the mouth. But you're afraid the tea is drugged, which is sensible, so I'll drink first." He lifted Edmund's cup, sipped, set it down, and slid it back across the desk with two fingers. "There. Now we've shared something. That's how trust begins."

"I don't want to trust you."

"Of course not. Trust is expensive." Corvane turned the page. "But you'll want to hear what I have to say regardless, because you're a curious man. Fifteen years in the temple archive and you never once requested reassignment. Scribes who hate the work request reassignment. You stayed because the work fed something in you. Curiosity. The same appetite that made you open a sealed prophecy book instead of reporting it." He glanced up at last, and his eyes were almost kind. "Curiosity is not a vice, Master Reyes. It is only dangerous when other people control what there is to be curious about."

Edmund's throat worked. "If you're going to torture me—"

"Torture you?" For the first time, something moved through Corvane's voice—not anger, but a faint, weary distaste, as if Edmund had suggested he settle accounts with fists rather than figures. "Do you know what torture produces? Noise. Screaming shaped into whatever the screamer thinks will end the pain. Lies wearing confession's clothing. I have read ten thousand confessions extracted under pain and I could not tell you one true thing any of them contained." He folded his hands on the desk. "I don't want your noise, Edmund Reyes. I want your *attention*. And then I want you to do exactly what you're going to do anyway."

"Which is?"

"Go back to her." Corvane said it simply, and the simplicity was the horror. "You'll escape me—you may even believe you have. You'll run to the Ashvault, because she's running there and because the prophecy has its hooks in you. I can't stop that. I don't wish to." He opened a drawer, drew out a thin folder bound in gray leather, and laid it on the desk between them without pushing it forward. "I only want you to arrive knowing what you're carrying."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere above, water dripped through stone with a rhythm like a slow pulse. Edmund looked at the folder and did not touch it, and understood, with the sick clarity of a man watching a trap assemble itself out of nothing but paper, that not touching it was already a decision.

"What is it?"

"Records." Corvane leaned back. "From the plague years. Forty-one years ago. The Gray Sweating Sickness, the physicians called it—the fever that took the river districts of Vhalen in a single autumn. You'll remember it differently, of course. You'd have been a child. But you'll remember the shape of it."

Edmund did remember the shape of it. He remembered it in the body before he remembered it in the mind: the smell of vinegar and burning cloth, his mother's hands cracked from washing linens, the cart that came down their street every morning and stopped at different doors each day, and the sound—God, the sound—of his sister coughing in the next room for six days, and then not coughing, which was worse. He had been seven. His father had died the year before in a kiln accident, and the temple had taken him in afterward, an orphan of the plague quarter, indentured against the cost of his keep. Fifteen years of ledgers. Fifteen years of ink. All of it downstream of that autumn.

"The sickness killed four thousand people in Vhalen alone," Corvane said. "Tens of thousands along the coast. And the temples taught that it was a natural visitation, God's inscrutable weather. But natural visitations leave records too, Master Reyes. Trade manifests. Shipping logs. Letters. And when you lay those records side by side—" he touched the gray folder, lightly, the way one touches a sleeping animal "—a pattern emerges. The sickness followed a route. Not randomly. It moved along specific roads, at specific times, always a few days behind a particular traveler. A woman traveling under three names. A woman who paid her innkeepers in old coin. A woman who, in the port town of Sel Maris, was seen standing on the breakwater during the fever's worst night with her arms raised, and the sea-glow rising around her like a second dawn."

The tea had gone cold in Edmund's hands. He set it down because he could no longer feel whether he was holding it steady.

"Her name in the Sel Maris manifest was Aldith Vane," Corvane continued. "In the coastal parish rolls, Aldith Crane. In a letter written by a frightened innkeeper to his bishop—a letter my predecessors filed and forgot—she is called by no name at all, only *the ash-woman*, because when she left a town, the town burned with fever, and when she left a room, the hearth-ash in the grate was found fused to glass." He paused. "You know the word for what she was. You've known it since the market square, since you saw her blood shine gold in the daylight. Oath-keeper. Dragon-blooded. The last living vessel of a line sworn to guard the ember."

"She was a child," Edmund said. His voice came out strange, flat, far away. "Forty-one years ago. Marguerite is—she's young. She wasn't born yet."

"No," Corvane agreed. "She wasn't. Her grandmother was." He let that sit. Then, gently, with the terrible gentleness of a man laying out instruments he does not intend to use: "Aldith Vane had one daughter. The daughter had one child. The child is kneeling beside you in the ruins of my patience, learning dragon-tongue from dead things in flooded cisterns. The line is not large, Master Reyes. It has never been large. It has only ever been *enough*."

"That proves nothing." The words came fast now, tumbling. "Coincidence—trade routes follow trade routes, fever follows ports—"

"Read the folder."

"I won't."

"Then I'll summarize, and you can spend the rest of your life wondering whether I summarized honestly." Corvane's voice remained level, unhurried, a man reading a shipping manifest aloud. "Page one: the Sel Maris manifest, dated the ninth of Harvestmonth, forty-one years past, listing a passenger named Aldith Vane arriving from the south. Page two: the parish death register of Sel Maris, beginning eleven days later. Four hundred names. Page three: the same pattern, repeated at Wrenholt. At Cael Ford. At the ferry crossing at Lowmarsh. Seven towns. Seven arrivals. Seven burnings. The probability of coincidence across seven independent records is a number I had calculated by three of my clerks independently, and none of them enjoyed the result." He tilted his head. "And page four is my favorite. Page four is a letter from Aldith Vane herself—or in her hand, at least—recovered from the effects of a dead priest. Shall I read it to you?"

Edmund said nothing. Corvane read it anyway.

"*'I did not mean for the towns. I swear it by the flame I carry—I was running from the Order, and where I passed, the old blood woke in the ground, and what sleeps in the ground woke angry. If I could cut the ember out of my own chest and bury it in the sea, I would. Tell them I am sorry. Tell them I will hide where no one can find me, and I will never come near a human town again.'*" Corvane lowered the page. "*Never come near a human town again.* She kept that vow for thirty years, Master Reyes. She hid. She raised her daughter in hiding, and her granddaughter after that. And then, this autumn, her granddaughter walked into Vhalen and bought a luck charm from a temple scribe, and the prophecy book opened, and here we all are."

The silence that followed had weight and texture. Edmund sat inside it and felt something small and bright being carefully, precisely, wedged into the soft place between his certainties.

Because that was the cruelty of it—that was the *artistry*—none of it required him to believe. Corvane had not asked him to denounce her, had not demanded a signature, had not even asked a question. He had simply placed documents on a table and stepped back, and now the documents would do their work in the dark, the way rot works in timber, quietly, structurally, from the inside. Every kindness Edmund had seen in Marguerite could be reread as concealment. Every secret she had kept—her palms that ran hot, her flinching from temple guards, the inheritance she had confessed only when cornered—could be reread as evidence. *She never asked for it,* she had told him in the cellar, shame and relief tangled in her voice. *I only wanted to be ordinary.* And he had believed her. He still believed her. But belief, he discovered, was not a wall. It was a door, and Corvane had just shown him that doors opened from both sides.

"Your family lived in the Tanner's Reach district," Corvane said, softer now. "River-side. The sickness entered Vhalen through the river gate on the third of Harvestmonth. Eleven days after Aldith Vane passed through the low country upstream." He watched Edmund with those patient gray eyes. "I am not telling you these things to wound you. I am telling you because you deserve to make your choices with the full ledger open. That is all I have ever wanted—for everyone. Full ledgers."

"And what choice," Edmund heard himself ask, "are you offering?"

"None." Corvane smiled, and it was the most frightening thing yet, because it reached his eyes. "I told you. You'll escape. The guard rotation changes at the third bell; the east drain grate is older than the fortress and rusts on the north hinge; your friend in the cisterns has been waiting since midnight. I could stop all of it and I find I don't care to." He stood, buttoned his plain coat, and came around the desk to stand over Edmund—not looming, never looming, just present, the way a fact is present. "Because here is what I have learned in thirty years of this work, Master Reyes. You cannot argue a man out of love. But you can make sure that when he arrives where love is taking him, he arrives carrying a question he cannot put down. And a man carrying a question holds the ember differently than a man holding certainty. Loosely. Doubtfully. And a doubtful vessel—" he tapped the gray folder once, and left it on the desk, within reach, within memory "—is a vessel that can be reasoned with. Later. When it matters."

He walked to the door. Stopped. Did not turn around.

"My wife and daughter died in the Gray Sweating Sickness," he said, in the tone of a man remarking on the weather. "Third district. Ninth day. I was away buying medicine that arrived too late. So understand me, Edmund: I bear her no hatred. I bear her grandmother no hatred. Hatred is noise, and I don't traffic in noise. I only want the fire put out. Permanently. Before it dreams again." He opened the door onto torchlight and the sound of boots. "The third bell, Master Reyes. You shouldn't keep her waiting."

* * *

They came for the folder an hour later, two guards who would not meet his eyes, and found it unopened, exactly where it had been left, and took it away, and reported—he would learn this later, much later, from a clerk with a guilty conscience—that the prisoner had not touched it.

That was a lie of omission, or perhaps a lie of mercy. Because Edmund had touched it. Once. When the footsteps in the corridor had faded, he had drawn it across the desk with fingers gone numb, and broken the seal, and read page one, and page two, and the four hundred names in the Sel Maris register, and found—on the third page, in the Wrenholt deaths, dated the winter of the sickness—a name he knew from his mother's stories. The midwife who had pulled him into the world. Dead in the eleventh day of the burning.

It proved nothing. Coincidence. Trade routes. Fever follows ports.

He said it to himself as the third bell rang and the rusted grate gave way at his touch. He said it as he waded the cold drain-water out into the night. He said it as he crossed the dark fields southward, toward the smoking horizon where the Cinderfen breathed its long red breath, toward the vault, toward *her*, moving faster now because if he stopped moving the question would catch him.

Somewhere ahead, Marguerite was waiting in the dark, gold-blooded and afraid, trusting him with the only fire she had left.

And somewhere behind his eyes, small and bright and perfectly seated, the splinter began to work.
