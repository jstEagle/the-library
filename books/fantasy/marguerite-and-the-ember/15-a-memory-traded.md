## 15. A Memory, Traded

The dead dragons did not speak the way the living would have. Their voices came up through the stone of the Ashvault like heat through a hearthstone—slow, patient, older than patience itself. They had been waiting here for a hundred years, and they were very good at waiting.

*You wish to learn the first word,* said the one whose name had been Vessarath when it still had a throat to say it in. *Then you must understand what a word is.*

Marguerite knelt on the cold floor of the antechamber, her palms flat against stone that was not entirely stone. The Ashvault's outer door had opened for her at a touch—the door recognized dragon-blood even if it did not recognize her—and now she stood in a corridor of black basalt veined with faint gold, as though the whole mountain had once bled and the blood had cooled into treasure. The air tasted of cinders and something sweeter beneath it, like honey left too long near a flame.

Behind her, miles back through the Cinderfen, Edmund was in Corvane's hands. She had heard the ambush begin. She had run anyway, because Sera's dying instruction had been simple and merciless: *One of you must reach the vault. It does not matter which. But it must be someone willing.*

She had told herself she would come back for him.

She was beginning to understand that coming back might cost more than she had.

*The oath has nine words,* said the second voice, the one called Ilyrenn, who had been the youngest of them when the war ended and was therefore somehow the saddest of the three. *Nine words in the old tongue. Each word is not a sound. It is a thing. To speak a word of the oath, you must become the thing it means.*

"And what does the first word mean?" Marguerite asked. Her voice sounded small in the corridor, swallowed by all that patient dark.

The silence that followed had texture to it. Grief, maybe. Or the particular embarrassment of the ancient being asked to explain themselves.

*It means mother,* said Vessarath.

Marguerite laughed—a short, cracked sound with no humor in it. "That's fitting."

*Nothing about this is fitting,* said Ilyrenn. *Fittingness is a human comfort. We are offering you arithmetic.*

*To speak the first word,* Vessarath continued, *you must give us the memory that makes you a daughter. Not the memory of your mother's face alone—that is only a picture. All of it. The warmth of her hands. The sound of her voice saying your name. The way grief felt when she died, because grief is also hers, given to you. Everything in you that exists because she existed. We will hold it while you speak. When the oath is finished, we will return what remains.*

"*What remains*," Marguerite repeated.

*Some memories burn as fuel,* said Ilyrenn gently. *We cannot promise which.*

She sat back on her heels. In the gold-veined dark, her own reflection looked back at her from the polished floor: a woman with soot to the elbows, hair escaping its braid, eyes that caught the light wrong—too bright, too deep, the dragon showing through the human like a lamp behind paper. Her grandmother's eyes. The oath-keeper's eyes.

She thought about her mother.

Not deliberately—at first it was simply that the memory rose, unbidden, the way it always did in quiet moments. Her mother in the kitchen of their narrow house in the Tanners' Row, flour on her forearms, singing off-key on purpose because Marguerite hated when she sang well. Her mother's hands, rough from lye but gentle at the temples when Marguerite had fever. Her mother's last words—not anything profound, just *shut the door, love, there's a draft*—said three days before the coughing started, said so ordinarily that Marguerite had carried the ordinariness like a stone for eleven years.

If she gave that away, she would still know facts. She knew how facts worked; she'd spent her whole life hiding behind them. She would know that a woman named Liss had borne her, had lived on Tanners' Row, had died in the winter of the gray fever. She could recite it like a ledger entry.

But she would not feel the draft.

"I want to be clear," Marguerite said to the darkness, "about what you're asking. You're asking me to stop being her daughter."

*We are asking you to lend us your daughterhood,* said Vessarath. *And we are warning you honestly that lending, in this place, is a generous word.*

*There is another option,* Ilyrenn added. *There is always another option. You can turn around. You can let the ember die. The world will go on. Most of it, anyway. The magic will gutter out over a generation or two, and the dragons will be truly gone instead of mostly gone, and no one will ever have to make this choice again because there will be nothing left to choose about.*

"That's not an option," Marguerite said. "That's a eulogy."

She pressed her palms harder against the floor. Beneath her, deeper than the voices, she could feel the ember itself—the last flame, sleeping in its vault the way a heart sleeps in a chest, beating whether or not anyone attends to it. It had been calling her since childhood. That was the truth she'd confessed to Edmund on the rooftops, the truth that had made him look at her not with fear but with recognition, two survivors finding each other across the gap. The ember had been calling her since she was six years old, since the first time her palms went hot when she was frightened, since the night her mother died and every candle in the house flared at once and Marguerite had known, with a child's terrible certainty, that she had done it.

She had spent thirty years wanting to be ordinary. She had wanted it the way the drowning want air—reflexively, totally, without any expectation of receiving it.

And now here was the arithmetic: the ordinary life she'd wanted required a world where the ember slept safely forever, hidden, unfound, unweaponized. And the ember could only be hidden by an oath spoken in a tongue that lived in her blood and nowhere else. And the tongue could only be spoken by paying for each word with the very things that made her a person rather than a vessel.

Her grandmother had stood in this corridor once. Sera had described it, dying in the flooded dark of the cisterns, her voice going thin as thread: *She came down carrying the ember's call and she came up carrying less of herself. She never told me what she paid. But afterward she would stand at the window for hours, and when I asked what she was looking at, she said, 'I'm checking.' Checking what? I asked. And she said, 'That everything I loved is still where I put it.'*

Marguerite understood now. Her grandmother had been inventorying her soul. Making sure the payment hadn't taken more than the receipt said.

"All right," Marguerite said. "Teach me the word."

*First,* said Vessarath, *you must open the memory. All the way. Not the shape of it—the substance. Hold nothing back. A word spoken from a guarded heart is a broken word, and a broken word in this place will break the speaker.*

"How do I open it?"

*You already know how. You have been doing it your whole life without meaning to. Every time you let yourself hope. Every time you let yourself grieve. Do that. On purpose. All at once.*

So Marguerite closed her eyes, and she opened the door she had kept shut for thirty years.

It was worse than she remembered. That was the first thing. Memory had sanded the edges off it, and now the edges came back all at once: the smell of the sickroom, camphor and copper; the sound of her mother breathing in that wet, labored rhythm that meant the gray fever had moved into the lungs; the weight of her mother's hand in hers, lighter than a hand should be, as though the woman inside it were already halfway elsewhere. Marguerite had been seventeen. She had held that hand for two days. On the third morning the hand was only a hand.

And underneath the grief, the other thing—the thing she had never told anyone, the thing that had made her certain she was cursed: as her mother breathed her last, every candle in the room had stood up straight and burned blue-white, and the hearth fire had roared once like something waking, and Marguerite's palms had glowed gold through her tears, and some voice older than her own bones had said, inside her, *she is ours now too.*

The dead dragons made a sound when the memory opened fully. It was not quite a sigh. It was the sound a fire makes when it finds dry wood.

*Yes,* said Ilyrenn, and there was something almost tender in it. *That is the substance. Now listen, and repeat after me, and do not stop speaking until the word is finished, no matter what you feel leaving you.*

"Aelirr," said Vessarath.

The word entered Marguerite's mind like a key entering a lock she hadn't known was there. It wasn't a sound—it arrived as a sensation, a warmth in the sternum, a sense of arms around her, of being small and safe and utterly known. *Mother.* The old tongue had built the word out of shelter and source and the first fire, and to say it she would have to *be* those things, and to be them she would have to pour into the word everything her mother had poured into her.

"Aelirr," Marguerite repeated.

The corridor lit up.

Gold light ran along the veins in the basalt like dawn running along a mountain range, and Marguerite felt the memory lift out of her—not torn, not stolen, but *drawn*, the way a midwife draws a child into the world, with terrible gentleness and absolute inevitability. She felt her mother's hands leave her own. She felt the kitchen dissolve. She felt the off-key singing fade to a hum, then to silence, then to a fact: *a woman used to sing.*

She felt the draft close.

"—love, there's a—" The sentence ended and she could not remember how it ended. She knew there had been a sentence. She knew it had been the last one. It was like reaching for a stair in the dark and finding floor.

The light dimmed. The word hung in the air of the corridor, complete, glowing softly above the vault door like a brand cooling into permanence. Somewhere below, impossibly far down, the ember turned over in its sleep, and Marguerite felt it notice her properly for the first time—not the distant call she'd felt all her life, but attention, vast and warm and slightly frightening, the attention of something enormous deciding she might be worth trusting.

*The first word is spoken,* said Vessarath. *Eight remain.*

Marguerite knelt there, shaking, doing the inventory her grandmother had taught her without knowing she was teaching it. Name: still there. Fear: still there. Edmund—

Edmund was still there. His face, his ridiculous careful hands, the way he'd lied to the temple for her after fifteen years of never lying, the laugh he'd tried to hide when she'd teased him about selling hope by the pennyweight. Still there. Still warm.

But her mother was a ledger entry now. *Liss. Tanners' Row. Died in the winter of the gray fever.* She recited it and felt nothing, and the nothing was the worst thing she had ever felt, worse than any grief, because grief at least proved the love had been real.

"Vessarath." Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "The second word. What does it mean?"

A pause. The long, reluctant pause of teachers everywhere who know the lesson is about to get harder.

*It means home,* said Ilyrenn.

Marguerite closed her eyes. Home. The narrow house on Tanners' Row. The market stall with its crooked awning. Edmund's cellar with the damp corner where he kept the good charcoal. The rooftops of Vhalen under her feet with his hand in hers, both of them laughing like idiots while the city burned orange below them and the bells rang alarm.

Home was not one memory. Home was hundreds. Home was the whole woven fabric of belonging, and the second word would take the weave entire.

*You should know the pattern now,* said Vessarath quietly. *You are owed honesty, and we have little enough of that left to give. The nine words are: mother, home, name, beloved, grief, joy, choice, future, self. The old tongue does not waste syllables. By the ninth word, there will be nothing human left in you to speak it with. Only the dragon. Only the fire. You will carry the ember into hiding, and you will keep it safe, and you will not remember why it mattered.*

"You're describing a tomb," Marguerite said. "You're asking me to climb into my own tomb and brick myself in with words."

*We are describing what we did,* said Ilyrenn. *A hundred years ago, three of us climbed into this tomb. We were not bricked in. We were the bricks. That is what an oath-keeper is. That is why there are almost none of us left.*

*You may stop,* said Vessarath. *No one would blame—*

"Don't." The word came out harder than she intended, and the flames in the wall-veins flickered at the heat in it. She breathed. She thought of Edmund, somewhere in Corvane's hands, believing she'd escaped, believing she'd come back for him. She thought of Sera dying in the dark water, passing the burden along like a relay runner who couldn't finish the race. She thought of her grandmother at the window, checking that everything she loved was still where she'd put it.

Everyone she descended from had paid this price or tried to. The inheritance wasn't the fire. The inheritance was the willingness.

Here was the thing Marguerite had learned about sacrifice, kneeling in the dark with eight words left: it only counted if you chose it. A sacrifice forced on you was just cruelty with better manners. A sacrifice sold to you was a transaction. But a sacrifice chosen freely, chosen with open eyes, chosen *because*—that was the only currency the old tongue respected. That was why the vault demanded payment at all. Not because the dragons were cruel. Because a protection bought cheaply could be betrayed cheaply, and the ember could never, ever be betrayed.

"The second word," Marguerite said. "Teach me."

*Dorrennath,* said Ilyrenn.

Marguerite reached for home—all of it, the whole weave—and began to pull it loose.

* * *

She lost the house first. Then the stall. Then the smell of Edmund's charcoal, and the specific rhythm of his footsteps on the stairs, and the rooftop wind, and the bells. She held onto his face the longest—she would realize later that she'd done it deliberately, hoarding one image the way a starving woman hides bread in her sleeve—but the word *dorrennath* was thorough, and thoroughness found even the hidden things, and by the time the second word sealed itself into the door beside the first, Marguerite knew only this: there was a man. He mattered. She could not say why. There was a debt in her chest shaped exactly like his absence.

Two words glowed on the vault door. Seven remained.

Far above, through a hundred fathoms of stone, she felt the Cinderfen shift—and felt, threaded through the ember's slow attention, a spike of heat that was not the ember's. Human-sized. Familiar-shaped. Someone moving fast toward the mountain through country that wanted him dead.

He had come back for her. She didn't remember him, and he had come back for her.

"Third word," Marguerite said, and her voice did not shake, because she had chosen this, and chosen things stand straighter than given ones. "What does it mean?"
