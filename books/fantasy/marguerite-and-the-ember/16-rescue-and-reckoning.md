## 16. Rescue and Reckoning

The cell had no lock worth the name.

Edmund understood this only after three days of staring at it, because Corvane wanted him to understand it late. The Inquisitor General did not make mistakes; he made lessons. The iron band across the door was old, rusted at one rivet, and the guard who brought his bread each evening was a boy of perhaps seventeen who looked at Edmund with the particular pity reserved for men already broken. On the third evening, the boy's keys hung from his belt as he bent to set down the tray, and they hung there a moment too long.

Edmund took them without either of them acknowledging what was happening. Some mercies could only exist if unnamed.

He waited for the changing of the watch—the hour he knew by heart from fifteen years of copying patrol rosters, because even a scribe's prison is built from paperwork—and slipped into corridors that smelled of tallow and wet stone. He walked like a man with somewhere dull to be. That was the whole art of it. Fifteen years in Vhalen had taught him that invisibility was not a talent but a posture: shoulders slightly stooped, eyes down, hands occupied. A scribe carrying an armful of ledgers passed four guards without a single glance. He stole the ledgers from a shelf he'd helped catalog, back when the temple still trusted him, and the irony sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

They let him walk out. Some part of him, the part Corvane had spent three days cultivating, whispered that they *wanted* him to leave—that a man released is a man followed, that every step toward the Ashvault was a step Corvane had already mapped. The whisper had Corvane's voice now. It had been living in his head long enough to pay rent.

*She lied to you,* the voice said, pleasant as a clerk reading accounts. *Or she didn't know. Which is worse?*

Outside, the night over Vhalen was the color of a bruise healing. Edmund turned toward the Cinderfen road and ran.

* * *

Marguerite felt the vault before she saw it—a pressure behind her sternum, like a hand pressing gently on a door her grandmother had once described in a voice gone strange with reverence. Three days alone on the Cinderfen road. Three days of the dead dragons murmuring in her blood, teaching her the oath word by word, taking their payment in memories she could feel thinning like cloth worn at the elbow. She had already lost the smell of her mother's kitchen. She'd noticed its absence the way you notice a missing tooth: constantly, painfully, and with nothing to press against.

She climbed the last ridge at dusk and saw the Ashvault for the first time: a black fist of stone rising from a caldera of glassed sand, unburned in a land where everything else had burned a century ago and never stopped. The door was a slab of dragon-bone pale as moonlight, carved with script that moved when she wasn't looking directly at it.

She was still catching her breath when she heard footsteps on the scree behind her.

She turned with fire already climbing her throat—and stopped. He was gaunt, filthy, limping, his scribe's hands torn from the climb. But it was him. Edmund. Alive, which meant Corvane had let him live, which meant—

"Edmund." His name came out of her before caution could catch it.

"Stop there." He didn't come closer. He stood ten paces off with the wind pulling at his ruined coat, and his face was wrong. Not afraid of her exactly—afraid *of himself*, of what he might be about to believe. "I need to ask you something, and I need you to answer before I lose my nerve."

Her palms were burning. She pressed them flat against her thighs. "Ask."

"The plague." The words came out flat, rehearsed—which meant he'd rehearsed them, which meant Corvane had made him rehearse them, though he no longer knew how to tell the difference between his own thoughts and planted ones. "Forty-one years ago. Ashen Fever. My family died of it. My mother, my sister. I was six." His voice cracked on the number and he hated it. "Corvane showed me records. Temple records, sealed ones. The fever came out of the Cinderfen the year your grandmother broke her oath-path north. The year the dragons' keepers passed through Vhalen province. He showed me shipping manifests, quarantine rolls, a list of the dead that had my family's name on it, Marguerite. *Reyes.* I read my own name in a ledger written before I was born."

"That's what he does," Marguerite said quietly. "That's what he's *for*."

"Is it?" Edmund laughed, and the laugh was terrible because it had no floor under it. "I don't know anymore. That's the truth you deserve before anything else. He didn't torture me. He gave me tea and documents and three days of patience, and now I can't find the seam between what I know and what he sewed into me. So I'm asking you plainly, looking you in the eyes, because it's the only instrument I have left that might still be honest." He stepped closer, one pace, gravel sliding. "Did your people bring the fever? Did the oath-keepers carry it? Yes or no."

The question hung between them, and Marguerite felt it land in her chest like a hook. Because she didn't know. That was the cruelty of it—she genuinely did not know. Her grandmother had died before the telling, Sera had been dying before the explaining, and everything Marguerite knew of her inheritance was fragment, verse, and the dead dragons' biased grief.

"I don't know," she said.

Edmund flinched as if struck.

"But—" She held up a hand, firelight flickering gold between her fingers, and fumbled at the inner lining of her coat. "But I have this. Sera gave it to me in the cisterns. She said, *when someone shows you a ledger, show them letters.*"

She drew out an oilskin packet, cracked with age, tied with cord gone soft as thread. Inside were letters—dozens of them, in a careful clerk's hand Marguerite recognized from the cisterns, Sera's own writing preserved from decades past. Correspondence between the oath-keepers and the temple healers of Vhalen, forty-one years old.

"Read the third one," Marguerite said. "Read it out loud. I want to hear it in your voice, so we both know it's real."

Edmund's hands shook as he unfolded the page. The paper was foxed and brittle, but the ink was dark, and the opening formula was one he knew intimately—*By the light of the Ninefold Flame, to the Honored Physickers of the Temple of Vhalen, greeting*—because he had copied that formula ten thousand times. This was real temple correspondence. He would stake his soul on the hand that wrote it.

He read aloud:

*"We write again concerning the sickness in the low quarters. The oath-keepers have opened their stores to your physickers without condition—you have seen our herbs, our texts, our own sick lying beside yours. We ask again: cease naming us as the source. The fever burns hottest where our people do not go. The first houses stricken kept no contact with any keeper. We grieve your dead. We are not their cause. If the temple continues to publish otherwise, we must conclude it does so for reasons other than truth."*

Edmund lowered the letter. His mouth was dry.

"There's more," Marguerite said softly. "Keep going. The one dated after. The one where the temple thanks them."

He found it. His eyes went down the page and stopped, and stopped again, and Marguerite watched him read the same passage three times:

*"...and we acknowledge receipt of the temple's private assurance that the attribution of the fever to the oath-keeper community was undertaken* **at the direction of the Office of the Inquisitor** *for the preservation of public order, and that the true vector remains under investigation. We accept this assurance with sorrow rather than belief."*

"For the preservation of public order," Edmund repeated. The words tasted like ash and copper. "They needed someone to blame. The city was tearing itself apart, so the Inquisitor's office—" He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious and aimed at himself. "It was Corvane's predecessor. Or Corvane. The office doesn't change, Marguerite. Only the man inside it changes, and even then barely."

"He told you your family died of dragon-work," Marguerite said, "and it was his own office that fed the lie while the fever burned. And he's spent forty years believing his own forgery, because the alternative was knowing what he serves."

Edmund sat down on the scree. Not gracefully—he simply stopped standing, legs giving out, and the letters spread around him in the starlight like fallen leaves. He picked one up and put it down and picked up another, checking dates against dates, formulas against formulas, the scribe in him running audits even as the rest of him came apart.

"It's half-true," he said finally. "That's what makes it work. The fever was real. My family really died. There really were keepers passing through that season. He built the lie out of true stones, and now every time I try to pull one out, the whole wall comes down on me." He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "I read those quarantine rolls and I *grieved*, Marguerite. Fresh grief, forty-one years late, and he stood there watching me grieve and poured tea."

"I know." She knelt beside him—not touching, leaving a careful foot of air between them, because she had learned in three days alone exactly how much a person can bear to be touched. "I know what he did to you. And I'm going to say something now and you're allowed to hate me for it."

He lowered his hands.

"You can't trust your own mind right now. Neither can I trust mine—the dragons have been taking payment for the oath-words, and some nights I can't remember if I ever had a mother or only the idea of one. We are both poisoned, Edmund. Both of us walking around with lies stitched into us. So we can't rely on what we feel or what we remember." She tapped the letters. "We rely on *this*. Ink and dates and hands we can verify. Paper doesn't get interrogated. Paper doesn't get taught new memories. When your head tells you something and the paper disagrees—" she met his eyes, gold flickering in the dark of them, "—believe the paper."

"And when the paper runs out?"

"Then we believe each other. Badly. Imperfectly. Loudly arguing the whole way." A ghost of a smile crossed her face, the first in days. "It's not much of a foundation. But I've seen what certainty builds. I'd rather have doubt with company."

Edmund looked at her—at this woman he had known for mere weeks and would have burned fifteen careful years for without hesitation, except that now he couldn't locate the exact shape of the hesitation Corvane had left in him. Somewhere in his mind was a splinter with the Inquisitor General's fingerprints on it, and it would work deeper with every mile, and they both knew it.

"I saw you die," he said abruptly. "In the vision. The first one, in the market square. A woman with your eyes sealed the flame and the sealing killed her—or changed her—I never learned which. And now you're learning the oath from the same dead who made it, paying in the same coin, and I'm sitting here holding forty-year-old letters thinking: *what will be left of her when the door opens?*"

"Less than there was," Marguerite said simply. "More than nothing, if I'm lucky. That's the trade. I chose it in the cisterns and I choose it again every morning, and choosing it again is the only part I control."

The wind came down off the caldera carrying the smell of hot glass and old grief. Below them, the bone-white door gleamed faintly, patient as a held breath.

Edmund gathered the letters, squared their edges out of pure habit, and handed them back. Then, before caution could reassert itself, he did touch her—one hand closing over hers, her skin warm as a sun-warmed stone, warmer than any human's should be, and he found he didn't care.

"Corvane let me go," he said. "Remember that. Whatever happens next, whatever he offers you, whatever bargain he puts on the table—remember that he opened that door himself. He wants us both here. He wants the ember and the vessel in the same room, because that's the only way anyone can take it."

"Then we give him a room he can't enter." Marguerite rose, pulling him up with her, firelight and starlight mixing on her face. "Come on, charm-seller. You read patrol charts and forged ledgers and you once sold hope by the ounce in a outlawed market. I need all three."

They started down together toward the door that answered only to the dead—and behind them, far across the darkening fen, a line of torches kindled along the ridge road like a wound opening.

Corvane had not hurried. Corvane had never needed to hurry.

The legion was coming, and the vault door had one word left to teach her.
