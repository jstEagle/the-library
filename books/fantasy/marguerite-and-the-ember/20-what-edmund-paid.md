## 20. What Edmund Paid

The fire did not want to kill him. That was the first thing Edmund understood, kneeling in the ruined threshold of the Ashvault with Marguerite's hand burning in his own. Fire wanted only to be held. Everything else—her screaming, the vault walls weeping molten stone, the legion beyond the gates—was the sound of something too large for the vessel it had been given.

*Two vessels,* Sera had said in the flooded dark of the cisterns, her voice already half-ash. *One to carry it. One to steady it. The second must owe nothing and choose everything.*

He owed nothing now. The contract was cinders on the threshold behind him, and the debt that had owned fifteen years of his life had gone up like a moth's wing. He had never felt so light. He had never felt so afraid.

"Edmund." Marguerite's voice came from very far away, or perhaps she was very close and the fire between them had simply swallowed all distance. Her eyes were gold from edge to edge now, no white left at all, and through their joined hands he could feel her being eaten—not flesh first, but *self*, the way water eats a drawing. "You don't have to. There has to be another—"

"There isn't." He was surprised by how calm his voice sounded. Fifteen years of copying ledgers had taught him this much: when the numbers are terrible, say them plainly. "The ember takes what it needs. From you it would take everything human, because you're mostly fire already. From me—" He looked down at their hands, at the light pouring out between his fingers like sand through a failing hourglass. "From me it will take what I give it. That's what willing means. It can't be stolen. Only offered."

"You don't know that."

"I read it," he said, "in a book that knew my face before I ever opened it. I'm choosing to believe the book."

And then, because there was no more time, because her knees were buckling and the gold was climbing her throat like dawn climbing a window, he stopped talking and began.

* * *

The first memory he fed it was small, as one feeds a stove kindling before coal.

The market stall. Rain. A woman he'd never seen before standing under his awning with her hood dripping, buying a luck charm she clearly didn't believe in because her hands were shaking and people with shaking hands will pay for anything that promises tomorrow. He remembered the exact weight of the copper coin she set in his palm—warm, oddly warm, as if she'd been holding it too long—and the way she'd said, *does it work?* and he'd said, *better than anything honest,* and she'd laughed, and the rain had smelled like iron and wet wool.

He gave it away and felt the fire take it, felt the day go soft-edged and gray, a story told about someone else. The ember steadied. Marguerite gasped.

"It's working," she whispered. "Edmund, stop, it's working, you can still—"

"I know." He reached for the next one.

The rooftops. Of course the rooftops. He gave it the whole night entire: the tiles slick under his palms, his terror of heights discovered at forty feet up and immediately made irrelevant, her hand pulling him across a gap he would have sworn no human body could cross. He gave it the moment they'd lain flat behind a chimney stack while a patrol passed below, close enough that he could hear a guard counting under his breath, and Marguerite had pressed her forehead to his shoulder and breathed once, deliberately, and he had understood that she was keeping her fear from flaring, keeping it leashed for his sake, and that this restraint had cost her more than any spell.

He gave it the guard who died anyway. He kept nothing back; the ember took the guilt along with the exhilaration, and somewhere inside the vast warm dark of it, he hoped, both would be kept safe.

That memory went, and with it the knowledge of why his palms were scarred with tile-grit patterns he could no longer explain.

* * *

It is strange, he thought, in whatever part of him remained the accountant, how orderly it was. He had expected tearing. It was not tearing. It was inventory.

The cellar confession: given. The taste of the cheap wine, the way the candle had guttered every time she laughed, the words *they were placebos, all of them, fifteen years of kindness sold as magic* —given, and gone, though some ghost of the shape of the confession remained, an empty room where furniture used to be. He knew he had confessed something. He could no longer remember what, or to whom, or why it had made someone laugh until she cried.

Sera in the cisterns: given. The old woman's hands, black-veined with ash, pressing letters into his. Gone.

The road through Cinderfen: given. The dead dragons speaking in voices like slow rockfalls, Marguerite walking among them like a daughter among uncles. Gone—and with it, strangely, his mother's face, because the ember was thorough and grief was fuel and it burned what was nearest first. He felt that loss arrive and could not name what was lost. A woman had sung to him once. Someone had sung to him. The melody stayed; the singer was ash.

Marguerite was sobbing now, but steadily, the way one sobs at a funeral one has agreed to attend. She had stopped telling him to stop. Perhaps she understood that stopping mid-giving would leave the ember half-fed and wild, that a vessel interrupted is a vessel broken. Or perhaps she understood that he would not stop, and loved him too much to make him spend words on refusal.

He gave her the charm stall entire—the little carved tokens, the false blessings, the ledger of customers he'd kept in his head because writing it down was death. He gave her the temple archive, the dust and the sealed prophecy book, the morning his own face had looked up at him from a century-old page. He gave her the interrogation, Corvane's quiet voice, the splinter of doubt—he felt the doubt go too, felt it burn clean out of him, and understood dimly that this was the one mercy in the whole transaction: the ember did not keep poison. It only kept what was true, and the lie had never been true.

What remained, when he reached the last of it, was duty. Duty was apparently not a memory but a bone; the fire could not digest it. He remembered the temple. He remembered obligation, order, the comfort of a task done properly. He remembered that he had chosen this, tonight, for reasons that mattered enormously.

He could not remember the reasons.

He could not remember her.

* * *

"Her name," he said, and his voice came out sanded smooth, empty of everything but question. "There was a name. I gave you a name."

"You did." Marguerite knelt over him. The gold had drained from her eyes, leaving them ordinary and wet and human, and the ember sat in her now like a banked hearth, quiet, contained, carried. She was holding his face in both hands as if checking a coin for clipping. "Edmund. Your name is Edmund Reyes. You were a scribe. You sold charms that didn't work and saved more lives than any sorcerer alive. You just—" Her voice broke and reassembled itself. "You just paid a debt that was never yours."

"That sounds like me," he said politely. "I'm sorry. Who are you?"

She made a sound he had heard once before, in a cellar, laughing until she cried, except there was no laughter in it now at all.

Beyond the breached gates, boots on stone. Corvane came through the smoke with his legionaries fanned behind him, sword drawn, and stopped.

Edmund watched the Inquisitor General look at the scene—the hollowed man, the woman with a sun sleeping in her chest—and saw understanding move across that scarred face like weather. Corvane had come expecting a weapon. He found instead a hearth. The ember mastered, banked, carried in hiding by a woman who had spent her whole life practicing exactly that.

"She could reignite the war," Corvane said. It was not a threat. It was a man reading aloud the last line of a ledger and finding the sum not what he feared.

"She could also let it die," Marguerite said. "In hiding. In peace. That's the oath, Corvane. That's what it's *for*. Your family deserved better than what happened to them. So did mine. Burning the world won't bring either back."

A long silence. Somewhere in the vault, molten stone ticked as it cooled.

Corvane sheathed his sword. "The empire will declare the Ashvault destroyed," he said. "The prophecy with it. No ember. No bearer. No legitimacy to fight over." His eyes found Edmund, and something almost like respect passed through them, cold and brief as winter light. "Whatever you paid—it was enough. Go."

He turned and walked out, and the legion went with him, and the gates of the Ashvault stood open onto a night that had, against all odds, a future in it.

* * *

They walked south for three days before Marguerite spoke of it. Edmund walked beside her because she asked him to, and because he had nowhere else, and because some part of him that was not memory—some part deeper than memory, older, the part the fire couldn't price—trusted her without knowing why.

On the third evening, camped in the ruins of a tollhouse, she took his hand and set something in it: a small carved token, a luck charm, worn smooth.

"This was yours," she said. "You made it. It doesn't work. It never worked."

He turned it over. His hands, he noticed, knew its weight.

"Tell me about myself," he said. "Start anywhere."

Marguerite smiled through tears, and began.

"Once," she said, "there was a scribe who lied beautifully, and a woman who was mostly fire, and a city that forgot them both—"
