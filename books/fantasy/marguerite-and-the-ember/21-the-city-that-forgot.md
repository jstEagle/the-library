## 21. The City That Forgot

The city had learned to forget the way a body learns to forget a wound—slowly, and never entirely.

Marguerite noticed it first in the small things. The shrine at the corner of Bellows Street where people had once pressed charms against their lips in secret now stood open, its alcove swept clean, a candle burning in daylight without shame. Children chalked spirals on the cobbles—the old dragon-signs that would have earned their mothers a visit from the Inquisition a year ago—and no one washed them away. A fruit-seller had hung a luck-charm above her stall, a crude thing of braided straw and river-glass, and when Marguerite paused to look at it the woman had grinned and said, "My daughter made it. Doesn't work, of course. But it's pretty."

*Doesn't work, of course.* As if magic were a fad that had passed through Vhalen like a fever, leaving only trinkets behind. As if the sky over Cinderfen hadn't glowed for three nights after the vault opened. As if half the eastern quarter hadn't spent a week coughing ash that smelled faintly of cinnamon and lightning.

The empire had decriminalized magic in the spring, by decree of an emperor who had never once been seen in public since. The edicts were read aloud in every square: practitioners licensed, oath-keepers recognized, the Inquisitor's office dissolved into something called the Bureau of Records, which was the same building with new shutters and a great deal fewer chains in its cellars. Corvane himself had vanished—not fled, the rumors said, but *retired*, which was somehow worse. Some claimed he'd walked into the Ashvault's ruins and not come out. Others said he kept a small house near the harbor and paid his taxes on time. Marguerite chose not to know. She had looked into his eyes once across a burning threshold, and she understood that some men do not end; they simply stop being relevant.

She had wanted, in those first weeks, to leave. To take the ember—still sleeping beneath her sternum like a banked coal, warm on cold nights, restless before storms—and carry it somewhere no one had ever heard of dragons. Somewhere she could be ordinary at last, or as close to ordinary as a woman with fire in her blood could ever come.

But Sera was dead, and the other oath-keepers were dead or scattered or too old to climb stairs, and someone had to teach the ones who were coming next. Because they *were* coming next. That was the thing no one had planned for. Lift the ban, and within a season the city discovered it had been full of frightened children all along—girls whose palms heated when they cried, boys who heard voices in the cisterns, an old fishwife who could call eels out of the mud with a hummed note. They came to her door the way the frightened had once come to Edmund's market stall: carrying small hopes, willing to pay in kindness because they had nothing else.

So she stayed. And she built a school.

It wasn't much. A rented hall off Tanner's Row with a sagging roof and windows that whistled in the wind, six desks, seven students, and a blackboard on which she wrote, every morning, the same first lesson:

*A flame is not owned. It is tended.*

She said it aloud each day until the words stopped feeling like prophecy and started feeling like grammar. She taught them breathing first, then listening, then the long slow discipline of holding heat without spending it. She taught them what Sera had taught her in a flooded cistern with death coming up the stairs: that power taken unwillingly burns the taker, but power given freely warms everyone in the room. She taught them the dragon-tongue, word by word, though the dead dragons' voices had gone quiet since the vault—she carried the language now the way one carries a mother's accent, without the mother.

And every afternoon, when the lessons were done and the children had scattered down Tanner's Row like sparks, she closed the shutters, wrapped her scarf against the evening chill she no longer actually felt, and walked to the archive.

To him.

* * *

The archive had been rebuilt on the bones of the old one. The temple had surrendered most of its holdings after the scandal—after the ledgers surfaced showing how long the abbot had sold information to Corvane, how Edmund's debt had been manufactured, inflated, and maintained for fifteen years for exactly this purpose. The new archive belonged to the city, and the city, embarrassed, had made it beautiful. Tall windows. Lamps that burned without oil, tended by a licensed keeper who charged a fair wage. Rows of shelves still smelling of fresh-cut pine.

Edmund worked there.

She had found out by accident, in a way—or told herself it was accident, though she had walked past the building four times before working up the courage to go in. He sat at a copying desk near the east window, because of course he did, because he had always loved the morning light on paper. His hands were steady now. His hair was longer. There was a thin scar along his jaw she didn't remember giving him occasion to earn, and she hated it, and she catalogued it anyway, hoarding every detail the way a miser hoards coins.

He had looked up when she approached the desk, and smiled the smile you give a stranger.

"Good afternoon. Looking for something particular? We've just finished recataloguing the legal folios."

And that was the whole of it. That was everything. Fifteen years of careful living, a prophecy, a war barely averted, rooftops and cellars and a vault door and a sun held between two pairs of hands—and he greeted her like a woman come in from the rain.

The healers had explained it, in their gentle, useless way. The memories weren't destroyed, probably. The ember had *taken* them, and the ember kept what it took—it lived in her now, all of it, every charm and rooftop and confession, folded into the fire like letters sealed in wax. Whether they could be given back was a question no healer could answer. Whether a man could receive back a memory he didn't know he'd lost was a question no philosopher would touch.

So she had decided, standing in that bright reading room with her heart going like a hammer, to stop asking questions and start telling stories.

"Actually," she'd said, "I wondered if you might help me find a book."

* * *

She went every day. She made a ritual of it, because rituals were the only architecture grief respected.

Some days she asked for a book—a real request, researched in advance, so that he would have to walk the shelves with her, close enough that she could smell the ink on his fingers. Some days she brought nothing at all and simply sat at the reading table within his sight, correcting her students' exercises, letting him grow accustomed to her the way one grows accustomed to a clock's ticking.

And some days—her favorite days, her terrible days—she told him a story.

She was careful about it. She had learned early that too much too fast made him withdraw, polite and puzzled, a door closing softly. So she rationed herself. One true story per visit. Small ones first.

"The charm-sellers used to work the fountain square," she said one gray Tuesday, watching him copy a land registry in his neat, patient hand. "Before the reforms. Little stalls, little trinkets. Most of them were frauds, of course."

"Were any of them real?" he asked, not looking up. It was the game they played now—he asked questions, she answered, and neither of them acknowledged what the answers were for.

"One," she said. "One of them was honest about being a fraud."

He glanced up at that, briefly, a flicker of something crossing his face—amusement, maybe, or its ghost. "That sounds like a contradiction."

"It isn't. He sold hope at a copper a piece and told every customer exactly what it was." Her voice caught, just slightly, and she covered it by turning a page of her own book. "People came back anyway. Again and again. Turns out being told the truth is its own kind of protection."

"Hm," he said, and returned to his copying. But that night, she learned later from the archivist, he had stayed late, pulling volumes on the old market quarter, on the fountain square, on the economics of street vending under prohibition. Searching, without knowing why, for a boy with shaking hands and a stall full of kindness.

She collected these moments like the charms he used to sell. The day he laughed—actually laughed—at her story about the guard who fell off the roof of the tollhouse because he was afraid of pigeons, and didn't ask himself why the story felt familiar. The day he frowned at his own hand, mid-stroke, because she'd mentioned that scribes who bite their nails hold their pens differently, and his pen had stilled as if the words had reached some part of him deeper than memory. The day he said, apropos of nothing, "I dreamed about rooftops last night. Red tile, all the way to the river. I don't know why I'm telling you this."

"Because I listen," she said.

"Yes," he agreed, surprised by his own certainty. "You do."

* * *

Winter came, and with it the anniversary, though she kept the date to herself. The school grew to eleven students. One of them, a solemn girl named Petra whose skin shimmered faintly gold when she slept, reminded Marguerite so sharply of herself at that age that she sometimes had to leave the room. She taught Petra the first words of the oath. She did not tell her what the whole oath cost. That lesson, if it ever came, would have to arrive the way hers had—too late to refuse gracefully, which was the only way inheritances ever arrived.

On the short dark days she noticed the ember behaving strangely. Warming when she entered the archive. Settling, like a dog lying down, when Edmund's voice read aloud some dry passage of municipal law. Once, when he brushed past her reaching for a folio, the fire under her ribs had flared so bright she'd gasped, and he'd turned with genuine alarm—"Are you quite well?"—and she'd lied, easily, the way he had once lied for her to the temple. *I'm fine. Just the cold.* Neither of them believed her. Neither of them said so.

The ember remembered him. Of course it did. It had eaten him.

Sometimes, in the worst hours of the night, she let herself hate it. She sat alone in her rooms above the school with her palms open on her knees and spoke to the fire the way you speak to a child who has broken something precious. *Give him back,* she whispered. *You have me. You have all of me. Give him back.* And the ember pulsed, slow and warm and unrepentant, keeping its secrets the way dragons keep hoards.

But hope was a stubborn organ. She had learned that from a charm-seller.

* * *

It happened on a Thursday, near closing, with snow ticking against the tall east windows.

She had come late, after a hard day—Petra had burned her desk lid by accident and wept, and Marguerite had held her and said *a flame is not owned, it is tended* until they both believed it again—and she had no story left in her. She sat at the reading table and put her head in her hand and simply breathed, and the archive settled around her, lamplit and pine-scented and quiet.

She didn't hear him approach. She felt him, the way she always felt him now, a warmth arriving at the edge of her awareness.

"You forgot your glove," Edmund said.

She looked up. He was holding it out—her left glove, kid leather, worn at the fingertips from years of gripping a quill. She must have dropped it by the door. She took it, and their fingers touched, brief and accidental, and the ember rose in her like a tide.

And Edmund went very still.

Not alarmed. Not frightened. Something else—something she had watched for across a hundred visits and never once seen. His eyes had gone distant, unfocused, fixed on the middle distance where visions live. His lips parted. The lamp beside them guttered, though there was no draft.

"Marguerite," he said.

Her whole body stopped. The word hung in the air between them, snow suspended mid-fall.

"I—" He blinked, and the distance drained out of his eyes, and he was looking at her again with the polite puzzlement of a stranger—but shaken now, genuinely shaken, one hand rising unconsciously to his chest as though something in there had knocked. "Forgive me. I don't know why I said that. Do I know you?"

"No," she said, and her voice was steady, because fifteen years of surviving had taught her that, at least. "No, you don't."

"It's just—" He laughed, uncertain, pressing his palm flat against his sternum. "For a moment there was—I had the strangest sensation. Like hearing my own name called from another room. Like a word remembered in the middle of a sentence." He shook his head, embarrassed. "The healers say the fire left... residue. In those of us who were near the vault. Perhaps it's that."

"Perhaps it is," Marguerite said gently.

He nodded, gathered himself, wished her a good evening in that courteous voice she knew better than her own heartbeat, and returned to his desk. She watched him work. She watched the way, twice, three times, his pen slowed and his free hand drifted to his chest, pressing, pressing, as if trying to hold shut a door that had just been knocked upon from the inside.

The ember purred beneath her ribs, smug as a cat.

*Oh,* she thought, walking home through the falling snow with her ungloved hand curled against the warmth in her chest, *you clever, terrible, beautiful thing. You're not keeping him. You're keeping him for me.*

She climbed the stairs to the school, lit the lamp, and pulled a fresh sheet of paper toward her. At the top, in a careful hand, she wrote:

*Tomorrow's story: the rooftops of Vhalen.*

Then, after a moment, she added a second line, and beneath it a third, and by the time the candle burned low she had filled the page—a year of stories, numbered, ordered, each one a key cut to fit a lock she couldn't see. Two hundred and eleven days of true things, ending with the last page of the plan, the one she had written and rewritten a dozen times and never dared commit to until tonight:

*Day 212: tell him everything. Ask him to choose.*

Outside, the snow kept falling on the city that had forgotten, and inside, a woman with a dragon's fire in her blood sharpened her pen, and waited for morning.
