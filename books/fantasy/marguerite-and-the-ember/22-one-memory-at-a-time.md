## 22. One Memory at a Time

The first frost of the year had come to Vhalen overnight, silvering the rooftops where two people had once run for their lives, and Marguerite woke before dawn with the ember humming under her ribs like a cat that knew it was being fed soon.

She dressed in the dark out of old habit—years of hiding her glow had taught her to move through mornings without striking a light—and stood a while at the window of the small room above the school, watching the city she no longer had to fear. Smoke rose from a hundred chimneys in straight, honest columns. Somewhere down in the Lantern Quarter, a temple bell rang the hour, and no one flinched.

One year ago tonight, she had walked into the Ashvault carrying a sun between her shoulder blades, and Edmund Reyes had stepped into the fire beside her because he owed nothing and chose everything.

She touched the window glass. It was cold enough to bite.

"Today," she told the ember, and felt it turn over in her sleep like something dreaming of flight.

* * *

The archive had been rebuilt on the bones of the old one, but the archivists had kept the smell right: dust and lamp oil and vellum, the particular hush of a room full of other people's words. Edmund Reyes worked the morning shift now, cataloguing the recovered ledgers, and he was good at it in the way he had always been good at careful things—his hand steady, his letters even, his mind a well-ordered room with one door left conspicuously unlocked.

He did not know why he left it unlocked. He only knew that locking it felt like a lie.

The woman came every day at the fourth bell. She had come for three hundred and sixty-four days, and today would be the three hundred and sixty-fifth, and some part of him—the part below memory, below thought, in the warm cellar of himself where the ember still lived—had been counting.

She never introduced herself. She never explained. She simply arrived, hung her grey coat on the peg by the reading desk as though she had a key to the building, sat across from him, and told him one true story. Then she left. He had asked her once, early on, who she was, and she had smiled with an expression he could not name—grief wearing patience like a borrowed coat—and said, "Someone who owes you a great many stories."

"And you're paying them back one at a time?"

"One at a time," she had agreed. "It's how I was taught. You don't hand a drowning man the whole sea."

Today she arrived with snow melting in her dark hair and something small wrapped in cloth held in both hands, the way one carries a bird or a coal.

Edmund set down his pen. "You're early."

"I'm exactly on time." She unwrapped the bundle on the desk between them. Inside lay a charm: a disc of pale wood, no bigger than a coin, carved with a spiral that turned inward toward a single point—a hearth-flame rendered in seven strokes of a knife. He knew the design. He knew it the way he knew his own signature, which is to say his hands knew it and could not say from where.

"That's mine," he said slowly. "I mean—I designed that. Didn't I?"

"You did." She turned it so the lamplight caught the grooves. "You sold them in the market for two coppers. Luck charms. Hearth-charms. Charms against bad dreams and bad landlords and bad weather. And here is the story I owe you today, the three hundred and sixty-fifth story, the last one I have."

The archive's hush seemed to lean closer around them.

"They were placebos," she said. "Every one. You weren't a mage, Edmund. You were a scribe with a knife and a kind heart and fifteen years of debt, and you sold frightened people little wooden spirals and told them they worked. And do you know what? They did work. Not because of anything in the wood. Because someone had looked at them—looked *at* them, seen how afraid they were—and taken the trouble to carve them something to hold. A guard squeezed one the night the quarter was sealed and walked past a dragon-blooded girl without seeing her. An old woman held hers while the Inquisitor's men searched her house and her hands never shook. You gave people somewhere to put their courage when they couldn't find their own."

Edmund looked at the little spiral. Something moved in him—not memory, nothing so generous—but a warmth, low in the chest, like a banked fire remembering air.

"I don't remember making them," he said. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "I don't remember any of it. The market. The—" He stopped. There was a shape in his mind, a gap shaped like a person, and he had learned not to press on it because pressing hurt in a way no physician could find.

"I know," she said gently. "That's what I'm here for. That's what all these stories were. I've been handing you back your own life one piece at a time, hoping some of it would stick."

"And has it?"

She was quiet a moment. Outside, the snow ticked against the high windows.

"Tell me," she said, "what happens when you hold it."

He picked up the charm. The wood was warm—impossibly, specifically warm, warm the way a hand is warm—and the warmth climbed his fingers and settled behind his sternum, and the ember that lived there, the ember he carried and did not understand, turned toward it like a face toward a familiar voice.

And then—

Not a memory. Not quite. But a *sensation*, arriving whole and unbidden: wind under his palms, slate rough beneath his boots, the whole of Vhalen spread out below him glittering and gaslit, and laughter—her laughter, high and terrified and alive—somewhere just behind his left shoulder, and the wild soaring certainty that he was falling and flying in the same instant and had never once in his careful life been so free.

His breath caught. "Rooftops," he whispered. "We were on the rooftops. You were laughing. You were afraid, and you were laughing anyway, and I—" He pressed the charm to his chest. "How do I know that?"

Marguerite's eyes were bright and wet. "Because the ember keeps what it's given," she said. "Sera told me once, and I didn't understand until the vault. Fire doesn't destroy everything, Edmund. It transforms. Everything we fed it that night—every memory, every hour—it didn't burn away. It *kept*. It's keeping still. And when you touch something that belongs to those hours—" she tapped the charm, "—it gives a little back."

"A little."

"A little. One memory at a time." She smiled, and it trembled at the edges. "I have three hundred and sixty-four days of practice waiting. I can practice longer. I'd rather not, but I can."

Edmund stared at her—at the woman who had come every day for a year to tell a stranger his own stories—and the warmth in his chest did something it had never done before. It reached. It leaned toward her like a plant toward light, and he understood, with a certainty that bypassed every broken road in his mind, that this was not the first time he had leaned toward her. That leaning toward her might be the oldest habit he had.

"Your name," he said. "Everyone calls you Magistra now, or the Lady Oath-keeper, or—everyone has a title for you. But you have a name. I can feel it. It's right at the edge of—"

"Say it," she whispered. "Please. Even if it's wrong. Say whatever comes."

He closed his eyes. The ember turned. Somewhere in the warm cellar of himself, a door that had been locked for a year swung open onto darkness, and in the darkness something glowed gold, and the gold had a voice, and the voice was saying a word it had said ten thousand times across rooftops and cellars and flooded cisterns and the threshold of the end of the world:

"*Marguerite.*"

He opened his eyes. She had both hands pressed over her mouth, and she was crying the way people cry at weddings, ruinously and without shame, and the lamps in the archive had all flared at once—every flame in the room bending toward her like grass toward wind.

"Marguerite," he said again, testing it, holding it up to the light of himself. It fit. Of course it fit. It fit the way the rooftop fit, the way the laugh fit, the way the gap in his chest had been shaped all along without his knowing. "Marguerite. I said it like—like a word remembered mid-sentence. Like I was saying it all along and only just caught up."

"That's exactly how it works," she managed, laughing through it. "That's exactly—oh, you impossible man, you wonderful fraud, that's exactly how it works."

* * *

They walked home through the snow because she asked him to walk, not to ride, and he was beginning to learn that when she asked for small things she was asking for large ones. The streets of Vhalen were soft with white, the gaslamps haloed, children throwing ice-slivers off the canal bridge with the particular cruelty of the young. Magic was legal now. A hedge-witch sold warming sachets two stalls down from a temple bread-cart, and neither of them burned, and Edmund watched the witch's little flames dance and felt no fear, only a distant professional curiosity about whether her sachets worked.

"Mine didn't," he said. "The charms. You said. Placebos."

"Mm."

"So everything I was—everything I built my life on—was a lie I told kindly."

Marguerite stopped walking. Snow gathered on her shoulders; she let it. "No," she said. "Listen to me, Edmund Reyes, because I have had a year to get this right and I will not fumble it now. A placebo is a lie that makes itself true. You told a frightened woman her charm would keep her safe, and because she believed it, she stood straighter, and because she stood straighter, the soldiers read her as innocent, and because they read her as innocent, she *was* safe. Where, exactly, in that chain, does the lie live?" She took his hand—his scarred scribe's hand, the hand that had signed his own freedom in flame—and pressed the little wooden spiral into it, folding his fingers closed. "You spent fifteen years selling people their own courage at two coppers apiece. The empire called that fraud. I call it the most honest magic in Vhalen."

The ember warmed against his palm. And another piece came back—not a scene this time but a voice, his own voice, younger, tired, gentle, saying *it works as long as you believe it, and believing things is allowed, whatever the temple says*—and Edmund laughed, sudden and startled, a sound that seemed to surprise him more than anyone.

"There he is," Marguerite said softly.

"There's a piece of him," he corrected. "There are pieces everywhere. Three hundred and sixty-four pieces, and I've only found—" he counted the sensations stacked in his chest like coins, the rooftops, the cellar, the cisterns, her grandmother's ring, the taste of burnt coffee in the Cinderfen, "—maybe twenty."

"Then we'll need another year at least." She resumed walking, and he fell into step beside her, and their shoulders touched, and neither of them moved away. "Possibly several. Possibly the rest of our lives, if the ember is feeling generous, and the ember," she added, with the fond exasperation of long acquaintance, "is feeling very generous lately. It likes you. It always liked you. I used to be jealous of how much it liked you."

"It lives in both of us," Edmund said. It was not a question. He had known it the way he knew his own signature. "Since the vault. Since you—"

"Shared the load. Yes." Her breath fogged the air. "Half a sun each. Sera said a vessel alone would burn. She never said what two vessels would be." She glanced at him sideways, snowflakes catching in her lashes, and there was something shy in it, something young, as if a century of inheritance and a year of grief had conspired to make her, in this one moment, simply a woman walking home with a man whose name she had said to herself ten thousand times. "We're finding out as we go."

* * *

The school stood on Ember Lane, because the city had voted on the name and Marguerite had lost the vote and secretly loved losing it. Its windows were lit against the dusk; inside, six students—young oath-keepers, dragon-touched and otherwise—were meant to be studying the Canticle of Sealing and were almost certainly doing nothing of the kind. Marguerite paused at the door with her key in her hand.

"Come in," she said. "For supper. For the evening. For—" She stopped, started again, and he watched her decide, deliberately, to be brave in the ordinary way, the hardest way. "Stay. That's all. Stay."

Edmund looked at the charm in his hand, at the little spiral turning inward toward its single point of flame, and understood suddenly what he had been carving all those years in the market, what the design had always been: not luck, not protection. A map. A spiral drawn from the outside in, from the far cold edge of a life toward the warm center of it. He had been drawing this door since before he knew there was anything behind it.

"I'll stay," he said.

Inside, the students looked up, and one of them—a girl with faint gold threading the veins of her wrists—whispered something to her neighbor, and the neighbor grinned, and Marguerite pretended not to notice, and the pretending was its own kind of tenderness.

Later, much later, when the dishes were done and the students gone to bed and the fire in the hearth had settled to its red and patient work, they sat on either side of it, and Marguerite brought out a battered notebook—three hundred and sixty-five pages, one story to a page, each crossed off in a firm hand except the last.

"This one's yours now," she said, giving it to him. "In case I ever miss a day. In case anything ever—" She didn't finish it. Some doors they had agreed, silently, not to knock on yet.

Edmund turned the pages. His own life, transcribed in her handwriting: *Day 41. The market stall, and how he always gave the first charm of the morning away free, to whoever looked coldest.* *Day 118. The cisterns, and Sera's hands, and the choice.* *Day 200. What he said to Corvane's men when they came for him, and how he was braver than he knew.* *Day 364. Yesterday. He remembered my name for four full seconds and then lost it, and he apologized, and I told him the truth: that I have lost things too, that the dead dragons took my mother's face and my childhood song and my grandmother's kitchen, that we are both collections of returned fragments now, and that I would rather build a person out of fragments with him than have a whole self alone.*

He read that last entry twice. Then he set the book down, and took her hand, and the ember in him and the ember in her—two halves of one patient fire—leaned together across the space between them like hands closing around a single coal.

"Tell me tomorrow's story tonight," he said. "I don't want to wait until morning."

Marguerite laughed—that laugh, the rooftop laugh, the one the ember had already given back to him—and fed another log to the fire, and outside the snow went on falling over Vhalen, over the rebuilt archive and the sealed-and-forgotten Ashvault and the graves of dragons, over a city learning, one law and one lantern and one returned memory at a time, how to stop being afraid of its own light.

"The story of tomorrow," she said, settling against his shoulder as the flames bent toward them both, "begins the same way all the best ones do. With a charm-seller who couldn't do magic, and a woman made of embers, and a fire that keeps what it's given—"

And in the hearth, quite distinctly, the flame laughed.
