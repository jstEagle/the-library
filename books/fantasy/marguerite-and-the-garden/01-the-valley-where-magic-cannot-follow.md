## 1. The Valley Where Magic Cannot Follow

The dead of Hollowmere Valley were a talkative lot, and Ulyana Winterbourne had learned to work while they talked.

She sat at her bench by the eastern window, where the morning light came down gray and thin through the frost on the glass, and turned a knucklebone of old Marta Ellery between her fingers. The bone was sixty years in the churchyard and still warm to the touch — not warm the way living things were warm, but warm the way a stone is warm after a long day of sun. Memory-warm. She held it up to the light and looked for the grain of it, the fine lines where the story lived.

"Mind the third joint," said Marta's voice from the charm hanging at Ulyana's throat. Marta had been dead for six decades and had never once stopped offering advice. "I cracked it falling off the Teller's cart when I was nine. Weakest part of me. Always was."

"I know," Ulyana said. "You've told me."

"And I'll tell you again, because you carve too deep when you're thinking about something else."

Ulyana smiled despite herself and set the burin to the bone. The first cut was always the truest — a single line along the grain, shallow as a breath. Then another beside it, and another, until the lines became a pattern and the pattern became a script that no living language used and no living person could read. That was the point. The script wasn't for the living. It was an address. You carved the name of the dead into their own bone in letters only the dead remembered how to answer, and then — if your hand was steady and your heart was quiet — they answered.

The valley called her work charms. The word was older than the valley's memory of why it was careful, but even here, even after all this time, people were careful with it. They brought her their bones wrapped in linen, carried at arm's length like something that might bite. They paid in eggs and mended fences and, once, in a horse that Ulyana hadn't wanted and couldn't feed. They knocked twice on her door and waited to be let in, because everyone knew you didn't just walk into the bone-carver's house uninvited.

They did not call it magic. Nobody in Hollowmere called anything magic, because magic was the thing that could not follow them over the ridge, and to name a thing was to invite it, and inviting it was the one law the valley kept above all others.

*No magic crosses the ridge.*

Ulyana had heard the law so many times it had worn smooth as a river stone. She'd heard it from the reeve, from the priest, from grandmothers scolding children who'd climbed too high on the western slopes. *No magic crosses the ridge.* It was said the way other places said *don't swim after dark* or *don't take bread from strangers* — a rule so old its reason had rotted out of it, leaving only the shape of the reason behind.

She had been in the valley thirty-one years. Long enough that she no longer flinched at the law. Long enough that she'd stopped, mostly, wondering what lived on the other side of the ridge that needed keeping out.

Mostly.

The burin whispered through the knucklebone. Outside, the last leaves of October clung to the rowan tree by her gate with the desperate grip of things that knew what was coming. The air had that iron smell it got before first snow. Ulyana worked and listened.

"Tell my daughter," Marta's voice said, from the charm at her throat, the same thing it said every time, "that the blue dress was in the cedar chest. Under the winter cloaks. I never told her because she'd have worn it to the fields and ruined it, and I wanted her to have one beautiful thing."

"I know," Ulyana said gently. "I told her. Twenty years ago. She wore it to her own wedding."

A pause, the particular quality of silence a dead voice made when it was remembering. "So I did tell her."

"You did."

"Good." A long exhale that moved no air. "Good. Then we're done, you and I. You can finish me whenever you like, carver. I'm not going anywhere."

They never went anywhere. That was the strange mercy of the valley: the dead stayed close. Not ghosts — Ulyana had seen ghosts, in another life, in a country whose name she did not say, and these were nothing like that. These were more like echoes kept in a jar. When she carved a piece of them into a charm, a sliver of their voice settled into the bone and stayed, patient as seed corn in a cellar, waiting for someone to come and ask a question. Widows asked. Fathers asked. Children too young to have known the dead came asking anyway, because a voice was a voice, and hunger for the dead didn't wait for memory.

Ulyana finished the last line of the address, blew bone dust from the groove, and held the charm up. It caught the gray light and gave back a faint luster, like the inside of a shell.

"There," she said. "Marta Ellery, addressed and answered. Your granddaughter will come Thursday. Try to be kind about the boy she's bringing. He's not right for her, but telling her so won't help."

"I was going to say he has weak ankles," said Marta.

"Don't."

"I'm dead, carver. Honesty is all I've got left."

Ulyana laughed — a real laugh, short and surprised out of her — and hung the new charm on the rack by the window with the others. There were forty-one of them now, each one a small pale moon of bone, each one holding its sliver of voice. On quiet nights, when the fire was low and the wind came down off the ridge with snow in its teeth, she could hear all of them murmuring at once, a sound like a congregation praying very far away. She found it comforting. Most people would have found it unbearable. Most people, she suspected, had never been as lonely as she had been before the voices came, and so most people had no way to know that a room full of the dead could be warmer than a room full of the living.

That was the thought she permitted herself, and only that one. She had learned, over thirty-one years, exactly which thoughts were safe to think and which ones had edges. She kept the edged ones in a locked room at the back of her mind the way she kept the uncarved bones in a locked chest: not forgotten, never forgotten, but not looked at either. Looking at them hurt, and there was no one in the valley to whom it would mean anything, and meaning something to no one was almost the same as not having happened at all.

Almost.

* * *

In the afternoon she walked down to the village with a basket of finished charms, because Mrs. Aldous had died in the spring and her son had finally saved enough coin, and because Ulyana liked the walk. The road followed the stream, and the stream ran fast and cold with meltwater from heights that had no business melting in October, and everywhere she looked the valley was doing what the valley did best: being ordinary, thoroughly, defiantly ordinary. Smoke rising straight from chimneys. Turnips heaped in carts. Two boys throwing stones at a crow with the cheerful incompetence of boys everywhere. A dog asleep in the middle of the road, certain of its rights.

No magic anywhere. Not a flicker, not a whisper, not a wrongness in the corners of the light. Thirty-one years, and the valley had kept its bargain with whatever it had made its bargain with. Things grew here. People were born and married and quarreled and died here, and none of it was touched by anything larger than weather. The priest said God watched over Hollowmere. The reeve said the ridge watched over Hollowmere. Ulyana, who knew more than either of them and less than she pretended, said nothing, and carved bones, and was content.

Content. She tested the word as she walked, the way you test ice at the edge of a pond. Yes. Content was the right size for it. Happiness would have been too large; happiness implied a future you expected things from. Contentment only asked for today, and today was turnips and smoke and a basket of charms and the stream talking to itself the way streams do, saying nothing, meaning nothing, going nowhere but downhill.

At the well in the square, Halvard was drawing water, because Halvard was always drawing water or fixing a fence or carrying someone's hay, in the tireless way of a man who had decided forty years ago that usefulness was the safest thing to be loved for.

"Winterbourne," he said. His voice did the thing it always did around her — dropped half a tone, went careful, as though she were a bird that might startle. "Cold coming. You've wood laid in?"

"Two cords."

"Should be three. I'll bring some up tomorrow."

"Halvard, I can split my own wood."

"I know you can." He set the full bucket down and looked at her, and his face did the thing it always did, which was arrange itself into patience. He was a big man gone gray at the temples, with a reeve's chain of office that sat on him like it had grown there. "But I like the walk up to your place, and you won't invite me for any other reason, so the wood is my excuse. Let a man keep his excuses, Ulyana."

She looked at him for a moment longer than she meant to. There was a whole conversation standing behind his eyes, the same conversation there had been for twenty years, the one they never had because she always stepped around it and he always let her. Someday, she thought, not for the first time, I should either have that conversation or stop letting him carry wood up a hill for it.

"Someday," she said aloud, which was crueler than silence, and saw him flinch the smallest amount, and hated herself for it.

"Thursday, then," he said, recovering, lifting the bucket. "And Ulyana — travelers came through yesterday. Fur traders out of the north pass. Odd-looking pair. Asked questions at the inn."

"What kind of questions?"

He shrugged, too casually. "Roads. Weather. Whether the pass stays open past the feast of Saint Ambrose." A pause. "Whether we get many visitors."

"We don't."

"No," Halvard said. "We don't." He smiled at her, and it was a good smile, and it did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "Probably nothing. Enjoy your walk."

She walked on, and put the travelers out of her mind, because the valley had taught her how to do that too — how to set a thing down and walk away from it, how to leave the edged thoughts in their locked room. Fur traders asked about roads. Everyone asked about weather. And whether the valley got visitors was nobody's business but the valley's, and the valley had kept its secret for longer than anyone could count, and would keep it long after a pair of odd-looking traders had gone wherever traders go.

This was the trick of contentment, she thought, climbing the hill home with the empty basket light against her hip. You didn't need to believe you were safe. You only needed to stop checking.

* * *

The first snow came at dusk.

It came the way first snow always came in Hollowmere — softly, without wind, flakes drifting down through the last gray light like ash from a fire very far away. Ulyana stood in her doorway with a cup of barley tea going cold in her hands and watched it fill the wheel ruts in the road, soften the fence posts, lay a white hush over everything. The rowan tree by her gate held its branches out and let itself be covered. Somewhere down the valley a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

The dead in her workshop had gone quiet.

She noticed it the way you notice a clock stopping — not the silence itself but the shape of the space where the sound had been. Forty-one charms on the rack by the window, and not one murmur among them. Marta Ellery, who had never once in sixty years run out of advice, was silent. Ulyana set down her tea and crossed the room slowly, and stood in front of the rack, and the charms hung pale and still as teeth, and the quiet coming off them was not the quiet of sleep.

It was the quiet of listening.

"Marta?" she said.

Nothing. The fire popped. Snow ticked against the window glass, soft as fingernails.

Ulyana stood there a long time, tea forgotten, watching the snow thicken outside until the world beyond her garden wall dissolved into white. She felt something she had not felt in thirty-one years and could not immediately name, because she had spent three decades teaching herself not to feel it, and the muscle had gone slack. It took her a moment to find the old word for it.

Dread. This was dread. This was the feeling of a door you had nailed shut beginning, very faintly, to rattle.

"No magic crosses the ridge," she said aloud to the empty room, and heard how it sounded — not a law, but a plea.

The snow fell. The dark came down. And somewhere out in all that whiteness, far down the road from the pass, small and almost lost under the whisper of the storm, came the sound of knocking — not at her door, not yet, but at the valley's edge, at the world's edge, patient as a heartbeat.

Ulyana reached for the nearest charm and closed her hand around it, and even that old dead woman's bone felt cold.
