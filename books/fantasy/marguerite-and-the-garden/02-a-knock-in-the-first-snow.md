## 2. A Knock in the First Snow

The first snow came on a Tuesday, which meant nothing to the valley but everything to Ulyana, because Tuesdays were when she carved.

She had a system, forty years deep. Mornings for the small work — buttons, beads, the little finger-bones of sheep that became charms against chilblain and colic. Afternoons for the true work: the human bones, the ones families brought down from the ridge-line graves when they wanted one more word with their dead. And evenings for listening, which was not really carving at all, though she did it with the same knife and the same patience.

The dead did not speak through her. That was the first thing people got wrong about bone-carving, and she had stopped correcting them decades ago. The dead spoke through the bones themselves — through what the bone remembered of the body it had carried, the way a riverbed remembers water. Her knife didn't summon anything. It just cleared away the silt.

The valley's dead were quiet talkers. They said things like *the east field floods before you think it does* and *tell my wife I'm sorry about the horse* and, once, memorably, *the miller's second son is not his son*. Forty years of small voices, small grievances, small wisdoms. She had built a life out of them the way a wren builds a nest out of stolen thread — carefully, secretly, out of other people's leavings.

And every year, on the day of the first snow, she took stock. Not of her stores, though she checked those too. Of the silence.

Because there were two silences in the world, and Ulyana Winterbourne knew the difference better than anyone living.

There was the silence of nothing to hear. An empty room. A still pond. That was most of the valley's quiet, and it was good quiet, honest quiet.

And then there was the silence that had been arranged. The silence of a hand pressed over a mouth. She had not heard that kind since she came over the ridge with nothing but a knife and a name that wasn't hers anymore, and she had spent forty years being grateful for it, and she had spent those same forty years never once asking herself why she was grateful.

That morning, taking stock, she found the silence exactly where she'd left it. Good quiet. Honest quiet. She nodded to it like a neighbor across a fence and went to sharpen her knives.

It was snowing hard by midday — fat, windless flakes, the kind that fall straight down as if the sky has decided something. Ulyana liked snow. Snow meant the road over the ridge would close, and closed roads meant no visitors, and no visitors meant the long white months of uninterrupted work. She had the fire up, the kettle on, a femur clamped in the vise and half-turned into a charm for old Marta's hip, when the knock came.

Three knocks. Small ones. The kind a child makes when he's been told not to bother people.

Ulyana put down her knife.

Nobody knocked at her door. That was not self-pity; it was cartography. Her house sat at the valley's western edge, past the last farm, below the ridge itself, close enough that in certain lights you could see the place where the trees simply stopped growing — not thinned, not dwarfed, just *stopped*, along a line so clean it looked drawn. Everyone in the valley knew the bone-carver lived out there where the land went strange, and everyone in the valley had agreed, without ever discussing it, that you went to her only when the dead required it, and left quickly after.

So nobody knocked. Nobody knocked three small apologetic knocks, and nobody stood outside in the first snow of the year doing it.

She opened the door on a boy.

He was perhaps ten years old, small for ten or starved for eight, and he was blue. Not figuratively — his lips were a bruised violet, his fingers gray-blue, frost furred the collar of a coat that had been fine once, somewhere else, in some other country's fashion. He had dark hair plastered flat by melt, and eyes that were the problem. Eyes should have been frightened. These were not frightened. They were *looking* — at her door, at her lintel, at the carved threshold-stone she'd set there thirty years ago and forgotten — with the weary attention of someone checking an address against instructions.

"Inside," Ulyana said, in Valley speech, the only language worth having here. "Now, before you fall down."

The boy looked at her. And answered.

What he said was not Valley speech. It was not any language Ulyana had ever heard traded in market towns or sung in taverns or cursed by drovers. It had vowels that opened like doors into warm rooms, consonants that fell soft as the snow falling around him. It sounded, absurdly, like something you would say to a garden.

And Ulyana understood it.

Not guessed it. Not pieced it together from context, the way you might worry meaning out of a foreign tongue. She understood it the way you understand your own name — instantly, completely, with a recognition that arrives already finished, needing no work at all.

*Is this the keeper's house?* the boy had said. *I was told the keeper would hear me.*

Her hands went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the weather.

"Get in," she said again, and this time her voice came out wrong, roughened, and the boy — whether from the words or the tone or simple collapse — stepped over her threshold and into forty years of careful silence.

* * *

She worked on him the way she worked on everything: methodically, with hot water and wool blankets and the brisk competence that let her touch strangers without ever being touched back. She rubbed his hands, fed him broth spoonful by spoonful, set him in the chair closest to the fire — her chair, though she didn't note the surrender until later. He shivered for an hour. Somewhere in that hour he stopped shivering all at once, sighed like a small old man, and slept.

Ulyana stood over him and felt dread wearing curiosity's coat, and made no effort to take the coat off, because examining what was underneath would require questions she had spent four decades declining to ask.

*Where did you come from?* was the obvious question, and unanswerable — the ridge pass had been closed by snow for two days, and the boy had frost on his *front*, meaning he'd walked into the wind, meaning he'd come from the ridge itself, from beyond the line where the trees stopped. Nothing lived beyond the line. Everyone knew that. Everyone knew it the way you know the sun rises: without evidence, without memory of anyone checking.

*Who told you about a keeper?* was the second question, and worse, because the boy had said *keeper*, in that impossible language, and keeper was not a word anyone used for a bone-carver. Bone-carver. Charm-woman. The valley's polite names and impolite ones. But *keeper* —

Keeper was a Garden word.

Ulyana turned her back on the sleeping boy and looked at her workshop, at the rows of charms hanging from the beam like pale fruit, each one a voice she had tuned and quieted and kept. And stopped.

Every charm was swinging.

Not one. Not the femur in the vise, disturbed by her passing. All of them — the little sheep-bone beads, the knuckle-charms, the great jawbone of Aldous the shepherd that hung at the row's end and hadn't moved in eleven years — all of them turning slowly on their cords, in air that was not moving, describing slow circles like weathervanes finding a wind.

And beneath the swing, faint as a pulse under a wrist, the dead were stirring. She could feel it the way she felt weather coming in her hip: a pressure, a mutter, a crowd waking in the next room. Forty years of the valley's dead murmuring their small murmurings, and now they were rising toward something, straining the way wheat strains toward sun, and Ulyana stood in the middle of her workshop with her arms wrapped around herself and understood, with terrible clarity, that the silence she had taken stock of that very morning — the good quiet, the honest quiet — was breaking apart like ice in spring.

Because of the boy.

She went back to the fireside and sat where she could watch him. His face had lost its blueness. In sleep he looked ordinary, valley-ordinary, a child any farmwife would claim. Only the coat was wrong, and the eyes behind the lids moving too fast, dreaming hard, and the language — the language that lived in her understanding like a splinter she'd carried so long she'd mistaken it for part of her own flesh.

*I was told the keeper would hear me.*

She should wake him. Question him. March him to Halvard's door in the morning and make him the village's problem, the way she made everything the village's problem except the one thing at the bottom of her own chest.

Instead she sat, and listened to the charms turn on their cords with a sound like dry leaves, and found herself translating, without meaning to, the words the boy had murmured in his sleep:

*Don't let it freeze. Don't let it freeze. I carried it all this way.*

Carried *what*?

Outside, the snow kept falling, straight down, deliberate, as if the sky had decided something indeed. And far off, at the edge of hearing — at the edge of the wards, at the edge of the ridge, at the edge of everything she had arranged and then arranged to forget — Ulyana heard, or imagined she heard, a voice she had buried forty years ago say her name.

Not Winterbourne. Her real one.

The boy slept on. The dead stirred. And Ulyana Winterbourne, who had survived by never asking, sat alone in her workshop and asked the first question anyway:

*How did you get over the ridge?*

No answer came. But every charm in the room swung a little faster, as if the dead, too, were waiting to find out.
