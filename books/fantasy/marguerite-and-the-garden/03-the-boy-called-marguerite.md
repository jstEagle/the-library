## 3. The Boy Called Marguerite

He woke in a room that smelled of fat and smoke and old bone, and for a long time he did not know where he was, only that he was warm, and that warmth was a thing so rare it made him want to weep.

He had not been warm since the frost came to the Garden. He could not remember how many days that had been. The cold had eaten the counting. There had been the last morning of sunlight, when the queen's towers still threw shadows across the terraces, and then there had been snow, and then there had been more snow, and somewhere between one white day and the next, everything he loved had gone quiet under the ice.

Now: firelight on a low ceiling. A blanket of rough grey wool. His hands folded over his chest like two small animals sleeping.

And a woman sitting in the corner, carving something with a knife no bigger than his thumb, watching him with eyes the color of river water over stone.

"You're awake," she said.

She spoke the valley tongue — he knew that much now, having heard it through the door before he knocked, having learned its shape from the mouths of travelers who sometimes wandered too far north and froze there, their voices joining the winter wind. But underneath her words ran another current, faint as a vein of silver in rock, and when she said *you're awake* he heard, beneath it, a word from home.

*Vessha.* Waking. The word gardeners used for seeds.

"Where am I?" he asked. His voice cracked like thin ice.

"The valley of Ashenfell. My house is at the end of it, against the ridge." She set down the knife. "You crossed the ridge. Nobody crosses the ridge."

"I know," he said. "I don't remember doing it."

Something moved behind her face — not surprise exactly. Recognition of a kind of answer she hadn't wanted.

"What do you remember?" she asked.

He closed his eyes and let the memory come, the way you let a shy animal come to your hand.

* * *

There had been a garden. That was the first thing, the truest thing, the thing he would know even if they took everything else. Not a garden like the valley people meant — not beans and cabbages behind a fence — but a country shaped like one, laid out in terraces that climbed for miles, orchards and fountains and hedges tall as cathedrals, all of it alive, all of it listening. In the Garden, everything grew toward everything else. The roses leaned toward the paths because they liked to watch people walk. The fountains sang in the mornings, badly, out of tune, because no one had ever taught them music but they tried anyway.

The queen had ruled it, though *ruled* was the wrong word. She tended it. Her name was Sylvara, and she had hair like late afternoon, and when she walked the terraces the grass grew greener in her footprints for an hour afterward. Marguerite had been one of her foundlings — children the Garden raised itself, grown from cuttings and kindness, or so the older ones said. He didn't know if it was true. He only knew that the queen had carried him on her hip through the peach orchards when he was small enough, and that she had taught him the old tongue, the tongue things were planted in, and that she had once told him:

*"If the winter ever comes, little sprout, you run. You don't fight it. You don't grieve it. You run, and you carry what I give you, and you don't stop until you're through."*

He hadn't understood. No one had understood. The Garden had stood for a thousand years; the world outside had ended its age of magic in blood and iron, the Wardens burning every hedge and hanging every hedge-witch, until the Garden alone remained, hidden, walled, impossible to find — a green secret at the roof of the world.

Then the winter came anyway. Not from outside. From inside. From nowhere. Frost on the peach blossoms in high summer, and then frost that stayed, and then snow that fell upward from the ground as well as downward from the sky, and the fountains stopped singing one by one, and the roses turned their faces away from the paths because there was nothing left to watch.

The queen grew quiet. He remembered that most of all — not her death, which he never saw, but her quietness, the way she stopped humming in the evenings, the way her footprints stopped greening the grass.

And then one night she had pressed something into his chest — into his chest, through the skin, painless as a kiss — and said, *"Run,"* and the gates of the Garden had opened onto snow, and he had run.

After that: whiteness. Cold. A voice calling his name in the dark, maybe, or maybe just the wind practicing. And then this room, this fire, this woman with her knife and her watching eyes.

"I don't remember crossing the ridge," he told her. "I remember running. Then I remember here."

"That's a very convenient hole in your memory," the woman said. But not unkindly. More like someone noting where a wall had crumbled.

"It's not convenient," he said. "It's terrifying. If I can't remember how I got through, I can't remember how to get back."

She looked at him for a long moment. Outside, the wind worried at the eaves.

"Marguerite," he added, because she hadn't asked and he needed her to ask. "My name is Marguerite."

Her knife-hand went still.

"That's a flower," she said.

"It's also me."

* * *

They fed him soup that tasted of turnips and salt, and he ate three bowls, and afterward she wrapped him in a second blanket and sat him by the fire and told him the rules of the house in the flat voice of someone reciting laws she'd carved herself.

"One: you stay inside. Two: if anyone knocks, you go in the back room and you don't breathe loud. Three: you don't touch anything on the shelves." She paused. "Especially not the shelves."

"Why?"

"Because they talk."

He stared at her. She stared back, unblinking, and then, deliberately, lifted a small carved charm from the table beside her — a knuckle of bone, polished smooth, with lines cut into it fine as spider silk — and held it near the fire.

The charm spoke.

Not in the valley tongue. In the old tongue. In *his* tongue. A voice like dry leaves: *—cold, it's cold, why has no one come, it's been so long, tell my daughter the blue jar, the blue jar under the floor—*

Marguerite shot backward off his stool. The blanket tangled around his legs and he went down hard, heart slamming, and the woman caught the charm before it hit the hearth and cupped it in both palms like a bird.

"They talk," she repeated.

"What *is* that?" His voice came out very small. "What did you do to it? Who is in it?"

"No one is in it. It remembers. Bone remembers better than flesh does." She set the charm down gently, facing away from him. "I carve the dead's words into bone so their families can hear them one more time. It's a trade. It pays for turnips."

"But that was—" He couldn't finish. His eyes were stinging. "That was the old tongue. That was a *Garden* voice. That was someone who died in the winter, or—" He stopped, because the arithmetic arrived all at once, terrible and simple. "How do you have a Garden dead? How do you speak the old tongue? *Nobody* outside speaks the old tongue."

The woman was quiet for so long he thought she wouldn't answer.

"My name is Ulyana Winterbourne," she said finally. "And you should sleep. Tomorrow we'll decide what to do with you."

"With me? I'm not a *thing* to do something with—"

"No," she agreed, standing. "But everyone else will think you are. Sleep."

* * *

He slept, and dreamed of the queen, and woke to knocking.

Three raps, spaced evenly, patient. The knock of someone who expected doors to open for them.

Ulyana appeared in the doorway of the back room before he'd finished sitting up, and her face had changed overnight — or perhaps he simply hadn't seen it properly before. It was a face built for emergencies. Calm the way deep water is calm.

"Back room," she said. "Don't breathe loud."

He pressed himself into the gap between a shelf of silent bones and the cold stone wall, and pulled a sack of dried lentils half over his knees, and listened.

The front door opened. Cold air poured through the house, carrying the smell of woodsmoke and horses.

"Mistress Winterbourne." A man's voice, smooth as oiled leather. "Cold morning for callers."

"Most mornings are cold, Master Corren. What do you want?"

"Want? Nothing. Concern, perhaps." Boots on the floorboards — two sets, Marguerite realized. Two men. "There's a boy missing. Small, dark-haired, foreign-looking. Been seen heading up-valley. The reeve asked us to make inquiries."

"A boy." Ulyana's voice gave away nothing. "Half the valley is boys."

"This one's special. Dressed strange. Speaks strange." A pause, and Marguerite felt the pause like a hand on the back of his neck. "Speaks a language nobody knows."

"Plenty of refugees come down out of the high passes this time of year. War in the south, famine in the east. You know this better than I."

"We do know it." The second man spoke for the first time, and his voice was worse than the first man's — quieter, colder, the voice of someone who had done many unpleasant things and found them merely tedious. "We know it very well indeed. We've been following such refugees for a long way, mistress. A very long way. So if one had passed through your valley — if one had knocked on a certain door at the edge of it, on the night of the first snow — well. We'd be grateful to know."

"And if none had?"

"Then we'd be grateful anyway, and sorry to have troubled you." The quiet voice again. "We'd leave a token. For our trouble. Silver buys a lot of remembering, in poor valleys."

A silence. Marguerite realized he had stopped breathing entirely and made himself start again, shallow and slow, the way the queen had taught him to hide in the hedge-mazes during games: *breathe like the plants, little sprout. Plants are never afraid.*

"Nobody knocked on my door," Ulyana said. "I live at the end of the world and nobody comes here, and that's how I like it. Take your silver to the tavern. They'll remember whatever you pay them to."

Boots retreated. The door closed. Horses, muffled by snow, moving away.

Marguerite counted to two hundred before Ulyana opened the back-room door, and when she did, her face was pale and set.

"You heard."

"Yes."

"They'll be back. Men like that always come back, and next time they won't knock politely." She crouched to his level, and up close he saw she was younger than her voice suggested, and tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "So we need to decide, you and I, quickly, whether you trust me."

"Do I have a choice?"

"There's always a choice. That's the whole trouble with choices." She held out her hand, palm up. On it lay a bone charm, uncarved, blank as an egg. "I'm going to hide you. Properly hidden, not lentils-and-a-corner hidden. But hiding magic isn't like hiding bread, and what I'd need to do would use the last of something I've been saving for years. So I want you to say yes. Out loud. To me."

Marguerite looked at her hand. At the blank bone. At the shelf behind her where a hundred small voices waited in the dark, remembering.

In the Garden, you didn't ask strangers for trust. Strangers didn't exist; everyone belonged to the Garden and the Garden belonged to everyone. Trust here was a different animal — thinner, warier, a valley animal with teeth. But the queen had said *run*, and he had run, and running led to places like this, to women like this, who kept the dead in boxes and lied smoothly to hunters and watched him with eyes that were afraid of something he couldn't name.

"Why did you understand me?" he asked. "That first night. Why do you know the old tongue?"

"That's two questions, and I'm only answering one today." Something flickered across her face — grief, or guilt, or both wearing each other's clothes. "Ask me the other one later. When you've decided whether you want the answer."

He thought about the fountains singing out of tune. About the queen's hand pressing warmth into his chest. About the frost coming from nowhere, from *inside*, as though the Garden itself had been betrayed from within — though where that thought came from, he couldn't say. It arrived like a splinter, already sunk too deep to pull out.

"Yes," he said. "I trust you."

Ulyana nodded once, and closed her fingers around the blank charm, and began to carve.

* * *

She hid him that evening in a way he would remember for the rest of his life.

She sat him in the chair by the fire, set the finished charm against his breastbone — right over the place where the queen had planted her gift, right over the ache he'd carried since the snow — and spoke six words in the old tongue. The words went through him like cold water through cloth. The room tilted. For one heartbeat he felt himself become small, and quiet, and *unnoticed*, the way a seed is unnoticed in the palm of a closed hand.

When the feeling passed, Ulyana was looking at him strangely.

"What?" he whispered.

"Nothing." She shook her head. But her hand, he noticed, was trembling as she put the carving knife away. "Nothing at all."

Outside, the snow had begun again — soft, steady, patient. Somewhere up-valley, dogs barked at something moving along the ridgeline, and Ulyana stood at the window for a long time, watching the dark, while behind her the shelf of bones stirred faintly in the still air, whispering among themselves in a language only two living people could understand.

One of them was a boy from a dying garden.

The other, he was beginning to suspect, was the reason it was dying.
