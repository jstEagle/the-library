## 5. The Reeve's Questions

Halvard Ash knew every face that came over the ford, because knowing faces was the work. Twenty-two years as reeve of Thornmere Vale and he could tell you the name of the tinker's mule before he could tell you the tinker's, could tell you which of the Aldous girls was lying about the broken fence by the way she stood with her weight on her heels. It wasn't cleverness. It was just attention, paid out daily like coin, until the whole valley sat in his head like a map he'd drawn himself.

So when the two strangers walked up the ford road on a grey morning in the first week of snow, Halvard saw them from the mill bridge and knew, before they said a word, that they were trouble.

They were wrong for the road in a dozen small ways. Their boots were too good — oiled leather, no patches, the kind of boots you bought in a city where nobody walked unless they wanted to. Their cloaks were grey as the sky but too clean, brushed free of the wet snow that clung to everyone else's shoulders. And they walked together the way soldiers walk, not side by side but slightly staggered, so that neither ever blocked the other's line of sight.

Not merchants. Not pilgrims. Pilgrims came to the vale weeping or singing; these two looked at the valley the way an assessor looks at a field.

Halvard leaned on the bridge rail and let them come to him.

"Good morning," said the taller one. A woman, forty maybe, with a voice like a door closing softly. "We're looking for the reeve."

"You've found him. Halvard Ash."

"Hessa Vane." She didn't offer a hand. Her companion — younger, broad, with the flat patient face of a man who carried things for other people — didn't offer anything either. "We're travelers. We've come a long way, and we've lost something on the road. Perhaps you can help us find it."

"Lose much?" Halvard asked. "Roads are bad this time of year."

"A child," Hessa Vane said.

The word went through Halvard like a fishhook going through water — quick, clean, and then the line pulled tight.

* * *

He made them say it again in his own kitchen, because a reeve's kitchen was where business happened and because he wanted to see them sitting down, off balance, cups in their hands. The big one drank nothing. Hessa Vane held her cup and warmed her hands on it without drinking, which told him she'd been cold a long time and trusted nothing she hadn't checked herself.

"A boy," she said. "Ten years old, perhaps eleven. Dark-haired. He would have been alone. He may have been hurt, or confused — he's not well, we think. He speaks strangely. He wouldn't know how to ask for help properly."

"When did you lose him?"

"Four days ago. Near the high pass."

"The high pass is closed four days ago," Halvard said. "Closed three weeks ago. Snow took it early this year. Nobody's come over it since the first fall."

"Then perhaps he came around," she said, smooth as water finding a crack. "Or perhaps someone found him and brought him down. We only want to ask. If he's here, there are people who love him who are worried sick."

There it was, laid out neat as a market stall: the story, the grief, the reasonable request. And underneath it, plain as a brand under a coat of paint, the wrongness. Halvard had spent twenty-two years listening to people lie about dead livestock and stolen apples and whose ox gored whose pig, and he knew the sound of a rehearsed truth. This woman had said those sentences before. Many times. In many valleys.

"Well," he said, "no boy's come through the ford. No strangers at all since the peddler Marta, and she was alone and complaining about her knees. I'll keep my ears open. You're welcome to warm yourselves and be on your way."

"We'd like to look around a little," the big one said. First words. His voice was softer than his size suggested, which somehow made it worse.

"Around?"

"The village. Outbuildings. People remember things when you talk to them face to face." Hessa Vane smiled, and it was a good smile, practiced, warm at the edges. "You understand. A lost child. We can't leave a stone unturned."

"I understand completely," Halvard said. "And I'll turn every stone myself and send word if I find anything under one. But this is Thornmere Vale, mistress, and we have our ways. Strangers walking through folk's yards asking questions in the first snow — that's how you start a panic. Panics end with somebody hurt. I won't have it. Ask your questions here, in my kitchen, and I'll bring you anyone who wants to come."

He watched her weigh it. He watched her decide, for now, to accept.

"Of course," she said. "We wouldn't want to cause trouble."

But her eyes went past him, through the window, across the frozen yard, up the slope toward the crooked chimney smoke of the house at the valley's edge where the bone-carver lived, and Halvard felt the fishhook set deep.

* * *

After they left — they took a room at the Wayfarer, paying with coins too new and too bright, another wrongness logged away — Halvard stood in his kitchen a long time with his hands flat on the table, thinking.

He thought about Ulyana Winterbourne, who had come down into the valley nineteen years ago with frost still in her hair and a story thin as winter ice: that she was a carver looking for quiet, that she'd heard the vale was peaceful. She'd been right about the peace. What she'd never explained was why a woman with her hands — and they were remarkable hands, everyone said so, even the people who muttered — would choose a valley famous for having nothing worth carving.

He thought about the law. Every child in Thornmere learned it before they learned letters: *nothing crosses the ridge.* No charms, no spells, no tokens of the old world. The valley kept itself plain on purpose. That was the whole reason the vale existed, if you believed the oldest stories — a place where magic couldn't follow, whatever *magic* meant anymore, in an age when the last of it had gone wherever magic goes when it dies. Halvard had always half-believed the stories the way you half-believe the ice will hold. Enough to walk on. Not enough to stand still on it.

And he thought about last night. Because last night, delivering a jar of pickled plums he'd promised Ulyana's household weeks ago — a pretext so flimsy he'd been embarrassed carrying it up the hill — he had seen, through a gap in the workshop shutters, lamplight burning past midnight, and he had seen, for half a heartbeat before the shutter swung, a boy's face. Dark-haired. Ten, maybe eleven. Wrapped in a blanket three sizes too big, watching the snow with an expression Halvard had never seen on a child: the expression of someone listening to a great distance.

He should have knocked. He should have said, *Ulyana, there are strangers on the road, tell me what's in your house.* That was the duty of it, the clean arithmetic of being reeve: strangers hunting a child, a child hidden, a neighbor harboring. Any magistrate in any county would call it simple.

But he stood in the snow outside her fence for a long time instead, and what he felt was not duty. It was fear — not of her, never of her, but *for* her, a fear so old and settled it had worn grooves in him like water wearing stone. Nineteen years of buying her honey at market and pretending he hadn't timed his stall next to hers. Nineteen years of fixing her gate hinge without being asked and leaving before she noticed. Nineteen years of loving a woman quietly enough that even he could almost pretend he didn't, because Ulyana Winterbourne had come to this valley carrying something heavy behind her eyes, and you don't put your hand on a person carrying something heavy. You wait. You keep the path clear. You make sure the firewood gets stacked.

And now two people with city boots and soldier's feet were asking after a child in a valley that had no children missing, and the child was in her house, and the law of the ridge — *nothing crosses* — sat in Halvard's chest like a swallowed stone.

Because here was the thing he had never said aloud, the thing he barely admitted in the privacy of his own skull: he had always known she wasn't what she claimed. Not a carver seeking quiet. Nobody sought this much quiet. She was hiding, and from something large, and the day she'd arrived, nineteen years ago, Halvard — then a young man newly made reeve because his father died and someone had to — had looked at her frost-burned face and made a choice that he had renewed every single day since, the way you renew a roof: silently, annually, without letting yourself count the cost.

He had chosen not to ask.

*Nothing crosses the ridge,* the children chanted. And Halvard had understood, in some wordless farmer's way, that Ulyana herself was the thing that must not cross — that the valley's peace depended on her staying exactly as still as the wards, that her silence was part of the wall. So he'd kept her secret even from her. Especially from her.

Now the wall had a hole in it shaped like a boy, and hunters were drinking tea at the Wayfarer, and the cost of nineteen years of silence was coming due all at once.

* * *

He spent the afternoon doing his rounds, and doing them deliberately loudly.

He stopped at the smithy and mentioned, at volume, that two travelers were asking after a lost boy, poor souls, and wasn't it a shame, and no, nobody matching that description had come through the ford, he'd have sworn it on his father's grave. He stopped at the Wayfarer and told old Sef the same, louder, adding details: the boy was sickly, addled, spoke gibberish, likely wandered off from some caravan. By evening the story had gone around the vale twice, wearing grooves, becoming furniture. *A lost boy. Sad thing. Nothing to do with us.*

It was the oldest trick a reeve had: bury the true thing under a hundred repetitions of the false one, so that when the strangers asked door to door — and they would, whatever they'd agreed to — every answer would already be waiting, bored and consistent. *No boy here. Never was. Reeve asked after it days ago.*

But lies are tools, and tools cut both hands. Because that evening, as he walked home past the meeting-house, he found Tam Aldous waiting for him in the lee of the wall, arms crossed, breath steaming, with the particular patience of a man who has decided to be difficult.

"Heard about the lost boy," Tam said.

"You heard right. Sad business."

"Mm." Tam looked at the snow. Looked back. "Funny thing, though. My Berta was up gathering lichen off the north slope yesterday, and she says she saw smoke from the bone-carver's forge past midnight. Says she heard singing, too. Or chanting. Something in a voice she didn't know." He paused. "Says she thought she heard a child's laugh."

"Berta's seventy," Halvard said. "Her eyes went ten years ago and her ears followed."

"Probably," Tam agreed pleasantly. "Probably that's all it was. Only — you know how folk talk, reeve. And with strangers in the vale asking questions, well. Better the answers come from you than from them, eh?"

There it was. The village, fracturing along its oldest seam — not between families or farms, but between the people who remembered why Ulyana had come and the people who only remembered that she had come from nowhere, alone, and stayed apart, and was therefore owed suspicion the way a stray dog is owed stones. Nineteen years of Halvard standing between her and that seam, patching it, smoothing it, and now the strangers' questions had picked it open like a scab.

"I'll look into it," Halvard said. "You have my word. And Tam — the vale keeps its own affairs. That's how we've survived. Remember whose hands fed you through the fever winter."

Tam's face flickered. Everyone in Thornmere remembered the fever winter, twelve years back, when half the vale lay coughing and it was Ulyana's carved charms — bones, only bones, whatever else folk whispered — that seemed to ease the dying into sleep instead of terror. She'd worked three nights without stopping. Tam's mother had been one of hers.

"Aye," Tam said finally. "All right, reeve. All right." But he walked away looking back twice, and Halvard stood in the cold knowing that *all right* had a shelf life, and that every hour the boy stayed hidden shortened it.

* * *

Night came down blue and early. Halvard took the long way home, the way that passed below Ulyana's hill, and stood at the bottom of the track with his lantern unlit, doing the arithmetic he'd been avoiding all day.

Option one: knock on her door tonight, tell her everything, demand the truth. Duty's choice. Also the fastest way to watch nineteen years of trust die in her eyes — because she would know, then, that he had always known, that the quiet man who fixed her hinges had been keeping accounts all along. And worse: if she refused to give the boy up, he'd have to choose, openly, between his office and his heart, in front of witnesses, with hunters in the vale counting heads.

Option two: say nothing, keep deflecting, pray the strangers moved on. The coward's arithmetic, and it didn't sum. Hessa Vane's boots were too good and her patience was too long. They weren't leaving. And Tam Aldous was already talking, and talk in a small vale is a fire in a hay barn.

Option three.

He turned it over and over, option three, the shape of it, the weight. Option three was walking up that track tonight not as a reeve and not as a suitor and not as a neighbor, but as the one man in Thornmere who had known from the first day and chosen her anyway. Option three was saying: *I don't need you to explain. I need you to let me help. Whatever's in your house, whatever crossed the ridge — the wall is mine too. I've been holding up my side of it for nineteen years without telling you. Let me hold it with you now, while it's breaking.*

His breath hung in front of him. Up the hill, the workshop window glowed, and against the glow, briefly, a shadow moved — two shadows, one small, one bent over work, the lamplight gold and steady and stubborn against all that dark.

Halvard Ash lit his lantern, and started up the track, and told himself it was duty, because a man has to call it something.

Halfway up, through the wind, he heard it — faint, rising from the workshop, threading under the door seams like smoke. Singing. Not Ulyana's voice. A child's voice, high and clear, in a language that made the hairs on his arms rise and the lantern flame lean sideways as though listening.

And beneath the boy's voice, so low he might have imagined it, another sound answered. A dry, whispering chorus, like many small voices speaking through wood and wire and bone.

Every charm in that house, Halvard thought, every carving she'd ever sold, every bone she'd ever touched — awake. Stirring. For the first time in nineteen years.

He stood very still on the snowy track, lantern trembling in his fist, and understood that whatever he chose next, the valley's long silence was already ending — and somewhere down in the dark of the vale, two travelers with city boots were sitting by their fire, waiting for exactly this.
