## 6. Lessons in a Dead Tongue

The first word I teach her is *home*.

Not because it is the most important word in the old tongue — it isn't; the most important words are the ones for snow and door and stay — but because when I woke on her floor with ice in my lashes, the first thing she said to me was not a question or a command but simply, "You're home now," which was wrong, and kind, and made me cry before I could stop myself.

So: *home*. In the old language it is a word you say into your own two hands.

"Like this." I cup my palms together, leaving a hollow between them, as if holding water I don't want to spill. "You have to make the shape first. The word lives in the shape."

Ulyana Winterbourne watches me the way she watches everything — sideways, through her lashes, like she's afraid of being caught looking. Her hands are never still. Even now they're working a piece of rabbit bone against a whetstone, small circles, small circles, the sound of it like a slow heartbeat. She has been carving since dawn. She carves when she's nervous. I've learned that about her in the six days since the snow brought me to her door.

"The shape," she repeats. Not mocking. Testing.

"You hold something that isn't there yet."

She sets down the whetstone. She cups her hands. And she says the word after me — *vessah* — soft and rough at once, a word like a hearth ember wrapped in wool.

The lamp flame stands up straight.

I don't think she notices. But I do. The flame had been leaning all morning, bending toward the draft under the door, and when she spoke, it stood the way a soldier stands when the general walks past. Just for a breath. Then it leaned again, and went on leaning, and Ulyana picked up her whetstone as if nothing in the world had happened.

But the dead in the workshop had noticed too.

They hang from the rafters and line the shelves: her charms, dozens of them, little carved bones strung on sinew, each one holding a voice the way a jar holds smoke. Since I arrived they have been murmuring. Not words I can understand — the valley's dead speak the valley's language, and I only know the old one — but a sound like wind through a keyhole, constant, curious. When Ulyana said *vessah*, every charm on the wall turned on its string. All of them. At once. Facing her hands.

I said nothing. I was six days old in this world and already I had learned its first law: no magic crosses the ridge. Ulyana said it the way other people say *the sun rises* — not a rule, just the shape of reality. And here was her lamp standing at attention, her dead turning their faces toward a single spoken word, and she hadn't seen it, or wouldn't.

I decided, in the cowardly way of children, that it wasn't my secret to tell.

* * *

We fall into a rhythm without deciding to. Mornings she works and I watch, learning the shapes of her trade — the way she listens to a bone before cutting it, pressing it to her ear like a seashell, waiting for permission. Afternoons, while the light goes thin and blue over the ridge, we sit by the stove and I give her my language a spoonful at a time.

It is the only thing I have to give her. That's what I tell myself, anyway. The truth is stranger and worse: teaching her feels like eating after a long hunger. Every word of the old tongue I speak aloud is a word that doesn't die with me. The Garden gave me its whole vocabulary and no one to spend it on, and then the winter came, and now there is this woman with ink-stained fingers and a face like a closed door, and she leans forward when I speak as if I'm handing her bread.

"*Sennu*," I say. *Snow.*

"Sennu." She frowns. "That's too pretty a word for snow. Snow should sound like a slap."

"It does sound like a slap if you say it angry. *SENnu.*"

"That's better." She tries it three more ways, quiet, then laughs — actually laughs, a short startled sound like a bird hitting a window — and outside the window the falling snow stops. Hangs in the air. Every flake suspended, glittering, for the length of one heartbeat, before it remembers itself and comes down.

This time she sees it.

Her laugh dies in her throat. She looks at the window, then at me, then at her own hands, turning them over as if checking whether they still belong to her.

"What did I do?" she asks. Not frightened, exactly. Something older than frightened. The look of someone who has found a door in a wall they've lived against their whole life.

"Nothing," I say. "That's the strange part. You didn't do anything. You just *said* it right." I try to find the words, hunting through my poor stock of hers. "The old tongue isn't — it doesn't describe things. It *calls* them. Snow doesn't know it's snow until someone says its name properly. Then it pays attention."

"Pays attention," she repeats flatly.

"To whoever speaks it." I shrug, because I don't know how else to explain, because I am nine years old and I learned the grammar of creation the way other children learn songs, without knowing what the songs were for. "My mother said the Garden was built out of the language. Not built *with* it. Out of it. Every field, every river, every season — somebody said them, once, in the right shape, with the right hands."

"And your valley?" she asks. Too quickly. Too smoothly. "Did the language build your valley?"

"I don't remember any valley." It's true. My memory is a house with most of the doors painted shut. I remember the garden — *the* Garden, green going back forever, the queen with her crown of living branches — and I remember the winter coming like a tide, and I remember running, and then I remember her floor, her fire, her saying *you're home now*. Everything between is a white page. "Why do you ask?"

"No reason." She picks up her carving. Small circles, small circles. "Eat your soup."

But her hands are shaking, very slightly, and the bone in her grip has gone gray at the edges, the color of frost on a window. I pretend not to see. I am getting good at pretending not to see things in this house.

* * *

On the ninth day I teach her the word for *door*, and that is when the wards flicker.

I don't know the word *ward* yet — I learn it later, from Halvard the reeve, who uses it the way farmers use the word *fence* — but I know what happens. Ulyana has taken me to see it by then, once, at dusk: a walk to the edge of her yard where the snow lies in a perfect unbroken ring around the whole valley, untouched by wind, unmarked by tracks, a seam in the world. "Nothing crosses that," she told me, and her voice had a strange doubled quality, like she was reciting something. "Nothing comes in. Nothing goes out. That's the law. That's the peace."

Now, in the workshop, I give her the word. "*Doruma.*"

She says it. Shapes her hands — I've taught her that much, that every word needs its vessel — and says *doruma*, and the lamp goes out.

Not gutters. Goes out, clean, like pinched by invisible fingers. In the dark I hear her sharp intake of breath, and I hear something else: the charms on the walls all ringing at once, a high clear note like struck glass, and beneath it a sound I have heard only once before, on the worst night of my life, in the Garden, when the winter finally crossed the last hedge —

A long, low groan, deep underground, like a giant turning over in sleep.

Then dark. Then silence. Then Ulyana strikes a match with hands that are not steady at all, and the lamp relights, and everything is ordinary again except for both of us sitting very still in the small gold circle of it.

"What," she says carefully, "was that."

"I don't know." True. "Something heard you."

"Heard me say *door*."

"Heard you say it *properly*." I hug my knees. The stove ticks. Somewhere under the floor, far down, I can still feel it — that vast drowsy attention, like an eye half-opened. "There are doors everywhere, Ulyana. Under things. Behind things. Most of them are shut so hard they've forgotten they're doors. But when someone says the word in the old shape..." I trail off, because I don't know the rest either. I only know that in the Garden, the queen could speak a door into any wall, and that the last thing she did before the winter took her voice was open one for me, small, and low, and cold, and say *run*.

Ulyana is staring at the floorboards. At the space beneath them. Her face has gone the color of the bone dust on her apron.

"This valley has wards," she says, mostly to herself. "Old ones. Older than the village. They've held for — " She stops. Recalculates. Starts again in a different voice, flatter: "They've held."

"They flickered," I say. "When you said *snow*, the snow listened. When you said *door*, the doors listened. Your words are waking things up." I try to smile, to make it a marvel instead of a wound. "That's good, isn't it? That means the language still works here. That means maybe — "

"Don't." The word cracks out of her like a whip. Then, immediately, softer, appalled at herself: "Don't hope out loud. Not in this house. Please."

I don't understand. I want to understand. I sit there with my mouth full of unsaid hope and study her face, and for one moment her whole careful mask slips, and what's underneath is not anger at all.

It's guilt. Old guilt, worn smooth as a river stone, the kind that has been carried so long it has become part of the hand that carries it.

"Teach me another one tomorrow," she says, standing, busying herself with the lamp, with the charms, with anything. "A safe one. A word for bread, or — or sleep. Nothing that *listens*."

"There aren't any words that don't listen," I say quietly. "That's what makes it the old tongue."

She stops moving. For a long moment the only sound is the wind testing the shutters, patient as a creditor.

"Then teach me slowly," she says at last, without turning around. "And Marguerite — if anything ever answers back... you run. Do you understand me? Whatever I say, whatever I do — you run."

* * *

That night I lie on my pallet by the stove and listen to the charms murmur overhead, and I turn the day over in my mind like a stone with a warm side and a cold side.

The warm side: she is learning. Word by word, hour by hour, the woman who took me in is learning the language of my country, and every syllable she gets right is a small resurrection. Today she said *light* — *ilmen* — and held her hands in the vessel-shape, and for three full seconds the whole workshop glowed without any lamp at all, soft and green-gold, the exact color of the Garden at evening, and I cried, and she pretended not to notice, and let the light stay until it faded on its own. She is quick. She is hungry for it. Sometimes when she repeats a word after me she closes her eyes, and she looks the way I imagine people look praying.

The cold side: she already knows some of it.

I didn't understand at first. I thought she was simply gifted, the way some people are gifted at birdsong. But tonight, drifting toward sleep, I replayed it — the way her mouth shaped *vessah* on the very first try, almost perfectly, needing no correction. The way she asked about my valley with that too-smooth voice. The way she said *they've held*, as though she were counting years she shouldn't be able to count. The way she knew the word *wards* before Halvard ever taught it to me.

Nobody learns a dead language that fast unless some room in them has been keeping it warm for a very long time.

I roll onto my side. Above me the charms turn gently on their strings, all facing the stove, all facing me, murmuring their keyhole wind. And I realize, with a child's terrible clarity, that they aren't murmuring at random. They're repeating something. The same phrase, over and over, in the valley's tongue, which I cannot understand — and yet the *shape* of it, the rise and fall, is familiar the way a melody is familiar even in a foreign country.

It sounds like a name.

It sounds like a name being said over and over by the dead, patiently, the way you'd say a name to someone sleeping, trying to wake them.

Outside, the snow has stopped. The whole valley holds its breath under a sky crowded with stars, and somewhere beneath us, deep in the dark where the old doors sleep, something that heard the word *doruma* has not entirely gone back to sleep itself.

Tomorrow I will teach her the word for *name*.

Tonight I lie awake and wonder whose name the dead keep saying, and why, when the charms whisper it, Ulyana stirs in her bed across the room and whispers it back in her dreams — perfectly shaped, perfectly fluent, like a woman answering a question she swore she'd forgotten how to hear.
