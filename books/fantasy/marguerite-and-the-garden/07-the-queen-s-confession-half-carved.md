## 7. The Queen's Confession, Half-Carved

The boy slept in the loft above the workshop, and the dead would not stop talking.

Ulyana sat at her bench with a half-finished charm in her hands — a knucklebone from old Marta Thorne, meant to carry Marta's voice to her daughter on the anniversary of her drowning — and she could not carve. Every time the knife touched bone, she heard it again.

*Ulyana.*

Not Marguerite's word for snow, or his word for door, or any of the fragments he'd been teaching her by firelight. Her name. Spoken in a voice like water over stones, patient and old and unbearably kind.

The queen's voice.

She set down the knife. She wiped her hands on her apron though they were clean. She did these small domestic things because they were what remained when everything else had been stripped away, and then she went to the shelf at the back of the workshop where she kept the charms that were not for sale, and she took down the one she had never once listened to in forty years.

It was made from her own finger bone. The little finger of her left hand, broken in the purge and never set quite right; she'd carved the tip off herself, years later, in this valley, on the first night she arrived. A gift to herself, she'd told the reeve of that time. A reminder.

A confession, she had not told him. Half-carved. Abandoned.

*You could burn it,* said the voice of Marta Thorne, from the charm still warm on her bench. The dead in the valley spoke plainly to their carver; it was one of the few mercies of the place. *Bone burns. Ash keeps no secrets.*

"I know," Ulyana said aloud, because there was no one living to hear her, and because saying it aloud was easier than thinking it. "I've known for forty years."

*Then why do you keep it?*

"Because burning it would be another kind of lie."

Marta's charm said nothing more. The drowned were practical people.

Ulyana carried the little-finger charm to her chair by the cold hearth, and she held it in both hands the way she held every charm before listening, thumbs over the carved faces, and she thought: *not yet.* And then she thought: *the boy is asleep upstairs, and the wards are flickering, and the Wardens are coming, and if I do not remember it now, whole, I will not be able to speak it when speaking is the only thing left.*

She pressed her thumbs into the grooves.

* * *

The Garden had smelled of rain that had nowhere else to be.

That was how Ulyana remembered it first, always — before the towers of white wood, before the terraces climbing the hillside like the steps of some enormous patient animal, before the queen. It was the smell. Forty years in a dry valley where magic could not follow, and still, some mornings, she woke with it in her nose: rain that had fallen and fallen and never once run off, that sank into the roots of things and rose again as sweetness.

She had been nineteen when she came to the Garden, a refugee of the first purges, barefoot, carrying her mother's carving knives wrapped in a shawl. They had let her in because she could hear the dead — because even then, even in a country where magic lived in every hedge and heartbeat, the talent of hearing the dead was rare enough to be treasured. The Garden's dead did not haunt. They *advised*. They sat in the white-wood council hall and argued policy with their grandchildren and great-grandchildren, and the queen herself kept three of them as permanent counselors, and Ulyana's work was to carve the charms that let them speak.

She had been good at it. That was the shame under the shame: she had been *good*. The queen had said so, once, in front of the full court, with her hand resting on the crown of Ulyana's head like a blessing. *This one listens the way rivers listen — all the way down.*

Her name had been Sarelle. Not Queen Sarelle — the Garden had no use for such constructions — just Sarelle, who had been gardener before she was queen, who still planted her own beans, who wore her crown only on the day the autumn leaves turned because she said a crown should mark something, not merely sit. She had been four hundred years old and looked thirty-five, and she had laughed at Ulyana's terrible jokes, and she had taught Ulyana the dead tongue properly, syllable by syllable, in the evenings, in the greenhouse where the last true roses grew.

*Say it again.*

*Sarelle.*

*Slower. You're swallowing the middle of it. The tongue of the dead has no hurry in it. Nothing there does.*

*I'm sorry, my lady—*

*Stop that. Say it slower.*

*Sah-rell-eh.*

*Better. Now ask me something worth the effort.*

And Ulyana, nineteen and homesick and dazzled, had asked: *Will the winter come here too?*

Because even then there were rumors. Even then the world beyond the ridge was going gray — the purges, the Wardens in their iron-gray coats, the villages where the charms went silent one by one. And Sarelle had been quiet a long moment, turning a bean seed between her fingers, and she had said:

*Yes. Eventually. Everything ends, little listener. But the Garden was built to end slowly. We are a country the way a tree is a country. When we fall, we will take a long time about it, and things will live in us the whole way down.*

She had believed her. That was the thing Ulyana could never forgive, could never carve around, could never burn out of the bone: she had believed her, completely, right up until the night the Wardens found the gate.

* * *

In the present, the hearth stayed cold. Ulyana noticed she was shivering anyway.

Upstairs, the boy murmured in his sleep — words in the dead tongue, soft ones, the way children talk to people in dreams. She had put an extra blanket on him and a charm of her grandmother's making under his pillow, a sleep-smoothing thing, and it was working, mostly. Mostly.

The wards flickered whenever he spoke the old language awake. She had watched it happen through the window: the faint silver shimmer along the ridgeline, the valley's long-held breath stuttering. No magic crosses the ridge. That was the law, older than the village, older than her exile. What she had never told anyone — what she was only now, holding her own finger bone, letting herself understand — was that the law was not a law at all.

It was a ward. And she had built it.

* * *

The purge came to the Garden in the autumn of her twenty-third year.

They called it the Quiet War afterward, in the histories the Wardens permitted. There was nothing quiet about it. The Wardens had spent a decade unmaking magic kingdom by kingdom — not conquering, *unmaking*, sealing the wellsprings, salting the ley-grounds, teaching whole populations to forget. Most countries had fallen without a battle, because most countries' magic lived in places, and places can be sealed.

But the Garden's magic lived in its people. In their blood, their speech, their bones. To unmake the Garden, the Wardens would have to find it. And the Garden had hidden itself so well, for so many centuries, that even its own refugees' grandchildren did not know the way back.

Until someone told.

Ulyana had been captured at the ford outside the valley — this valley, the same one, though then it had been a border town called Vesseny, half in the Garden's shadow, half in the gray world's. She had gone there to buy salt and iron needles, ordinary errands, and the Wardens had taken her in the market square, three of them, and put her in a room with no windows and a table and a lamp, and a man with a voice like dry paper had sat across from her and asked her questions for six days.

She had told herself she would not answer. She was twenty-three. She had memorized speeches. She had imagined torture and resolved to bear it, and the terrible comedy of it, the thing that had kept her awake for four decades, was that they never touched her at all.

The man with the paper voice simply talked. He talked about the war. He showed her lists — names of villages already grayed, names of listeners already found and *processed*, which was the word he used, processed, as if they were grain. He showed her a list of names that had not yet been found, and on it, in the middle, was the name of her old teacher, the woman who had taught her the dead tongue in the greenhouse, and beside it a date two weeks away.

And then he said the sentence. She could still recite it. She had recited it to herself in the dark so many times it had worn smooth as river stone:

*"We will find the Garden eventually. It may take us ten years. It may take us fifty. In those years, we will find everyone who has ever passed through its gate, and we will process them one at a time, beginning with the ones easiest to locate. You are the easiest we have located in six years."*

He slid a map across the table. Blank, except for the borderlands.

*"Or,"* he said, *"you can go home to your valley, and grow old, and carve your little bones, and no one will ever trouble you again. We don't want the Garden destroyed, girl. We want it ended. Ending can be gentle. Winter is gentle, if you dress for it."*

Six days. Six days of telling herself *no*, and on the seventh morning she pointed at the map, and her hand did not shake, and that was the worst part, that her hand did not shake. She traced the river. She named the ford. She described the gate — the way it looked like nothing, a gap in a hedgerow, walkable only by invitation — and she explained the invitation, the old words, the ones Sarelle had taught her syllable by syllable in the greenhouse among the last true roses.

The man with the paper voice thanked her. That was the second-worst part. He thanked her, courteously, and had her escorted to the valley border, and she walked back into Vesseny with her salt and her iron needles, and three weeks later the Wardens came up the river road in their gray coats, and the Garden's winter began.

She had fled that same night. North, over the ridge, into the high valley where the refugees of the purges gathered because word had spread that magic could not follow them there — and she had built the ward herself, alone, in one night of desperate carving, using the dead tongue Sarelle had taught her, laying a silence over the whole valley like a sheet over furniture in a house whose owner would never return. *No magic crosses the ridge.* She had told herself it was protection. Shelter for the hunted. A mercy.

It was also, she understood now, sitting in her chair with her own finger bone in her hands, the perfect hiding place for a traitor. A valley where no magic could follow was a valley where the Garden's dead could never reach her. Where Sarelle's voice could never cross the threshold and say her name.

Except now it had. Except now a boy had walked through the first snow carrying a seed and a language older than kingdoms, and the dead in her workshop stirred for the first time in forty years, and somewhere beyond the wards, patient as winter, the queen was waiting to be heard.

* * *

The charm in her hands was half-carved. She turned it in the lamplight.

On one face, in the dead tongue, in her younger self's careful letters: *I, Ulyana, daughter of Vesna, confess—*

And then nothing. Forty years of blank bone.

She had started it the first winter here, when the silence of the valley pressed so hard against her ears that she could think of nothing but filling it. She had broken her own finger — badly, clumsily, a butcher's job — and sat down to carve the truth, and she had gotten five words in before her hands stopped. She had told herself she would finish it in the spring. Then the next spring. Then she had told herself the confession was finished in the way that mattered, finished inside her, known to herself, and wasn't that the substance of honesty?

It was not. She knew it was not. A confession spoken only to yourself is just a story you tell to stay asleep.

*Marta,* she said to the empty workshop. The drowned woman's charm warmed slightly on the bench, listening. *If a person did a thing — one thing, one unforgivable thing — and then spent forty years being decent, does the decency count?*

The dead were silent a moment. When Marta answered, her voice had the flat kindness of the truly drowned.

*Ask the one you wronged.*

"I can't."

*No,* Marta agreed. *You buried her where you couldn't hear her. That was the point of the valley, wasn't it?*

Ulyana closed her hand around the half-carved charm until the edges bit her palm.

Above her, floorboards creaked. Small feet, light, crossing the loft toward the ladder. She had perhaps a minute. She shoved the charm into the drawer of the workbench — the deep drawer, the one with the false bottom, the drawer Halvard had helped her build years ago and never asked about — and she had barely straightened when a tousled head appeared at the top of the ladder, eyes huge in the lamplight, and Marguerite said, in the dead tongue, the words she now knew well enough to feel them in her teeth:

*"The queen is dreaming. I heard her. She says the door remembers your name."*

The lamplight guttered. Outside, far off along the ridgeline, the wards flickered — once, twice — like a candle deciding whether to keep burning.

And down the valley road, faint but unmistakable on the frozen air, came the sound of hooves.
