## 8. Hunters at the Ford

Halvard had been reeve of Wintermere for nineteen years, and in all that time he had learned one rule above every other: trouble always came by the road. Smugglers came by the road. Tax men came by the road. The fever that took his wife had come up the road with a tinker's cart, riding in a bolt of cloth.

So when he saw three riders coming down from the pass on a morning so cold the ford had gone to glass, he did not need anyone to tell him what they were.

Trouble. On horses too fine for the valley, wearing grey cloaks that shed snow like oiled leather sheds water, moving at an even pace that said they were not hurrying because they had never once needed to hurry in their lives.

He was down at the ford anyway, checking the ice stakes before the thaw-swells — there would be no thaw this year, but habits kept a man honest — and so there was nowhere to be, no errand to pretend toward. He straightened, set his mallet in his belt, and waited for them at the near bank like a man greeting cousins.

The lead rider drew rein six feet away. A woman, fifty or so, with a face like a door left open in winter: nothing hostile in it, nothing warm either. Her eyes went past him, up the valley, counting rooftops.

"Good morning," she said. "We're looking for a child."

"Lot of children in Wintermere," Halvard said. "Forty-one, last count."

"A boy. Perhaps ten years old. He would have come over the ridge within the fortnight." She smiled without any of her face joining in. "He may not have seemed entirely — ordinary."

Halvard felt the cold go through his coat and into the place beneath it where he kept the things he actually believed. He thought of Ulyana's face two nights ago at the well, when he'd asked after the boy she'd taken in, and how she had looked at him the way people look at a door they're deciding whether to bar.

"No strangers come over the ridge," he said. "Nothing comes over the ridge. That's rather the point of the ridge."

"Nothing comes over," the woman agreed pleasantly. "And yet here we are."

* * *

They gave their names at the common house, because names cost them nothing. Serel. That was the woman. The two men were Voss and Anthe — brothers, or something shaped like brothers, with the same flat grey eyes and the same way of standing as if the ground had been arranged for their convenience.

Wardens. Halvard knew the word the way you know the word for a disease you've never caught. Every child in the valley learned the shape of it in winter stories: the ones who ended the age of magic, who burned the colleges and salted the wells and hunted the last of the wonder-keepers across three kingdoms until there was nowhere left but places like Wintermere, where magic could not follow, and so neither could they.

Except now they had followed. They sat in his common house drinking his ale, and their horses stood in his stable eating hay that should have fed his own through the lean months, and Serel was telling him, in the tone of a woman discussing weather, that the boy they sought was dangerous.

"Not to you," she added quickly, seeing the faces around the fire. There were a dozen villagers present, drawn by the same gravity that pulls every eye in a small place toward whatever is new. "Not to anyone, perhaps. But he is a remnant. Of the Garden."

The word moved through the room like wind through wheat. Halvard watched it move. Old Marta crossed herself with the wrong hand. Young Piet's mouth opened and shut. And in the corner, Bren the smith — who had been asking questions at the well for two days, loud questions, about strange boys and bone-charms — leaned forward with the eagerness of a man about to be paid.

"The Garden," Serel said, "was a country. Not a metaphor, not a story. A country, with borders and a queen, and everything your grandmothers' songs call magic came out of it the way rivers come out of mountains. It fell a hundred years ago. Its queen died. Its people scattered or worse." She turned her cup slowly on the table. "What remains of it fits inside one child. And we mean to see that what remains does no harm."

"Harm to whom?" Halvard asked.

"To itself," said the one called Voss, and something in his voice made the fire seem smaller. "You've seen what follows that kind. Winters that don't lift. Animals born wrong. Wells that whisper." He shrugged. "We end things cleanly. That's mercy, in the long arithmetic."

"And if we haven't seen your boy?" Halvard said. "If no such child crossed our ford?"

Serel looked at him then, fully, for the first time, and Halvard understood that everything before had been courtesy and this was the actual conversation beginning.

"Then we'll look," she said, "and leave you in peace. We're not the villains of the songs, Reeve. We're tidiers. You'll hardly know we passed through." She smiled her door-in-winter smile. "But I'll tell you what I told the last village. Lying to a Warden isn't a crime. It's just a debt. And debts," she said, setting down her cup, "are the one thing we always collect."

* * *

He walked them out to the ford at dusk because they asked him to, and because refusing would have been its own answer.

The ice had thickened since morning. Their horses crossed it without slipping, which Halvard noted and filed away among the other wrongnesses: the cloaks that wouldn't hold snow, the eyes that never squinted against wind, the way birds went silent along the whole length of the river when the three of them rode past. On the far bank Serel turned in the saddle.

"You have someone here," she called across the ice. "Someone who came to this valley a long time ago and never quite explained why. We remember her kind. Bone-workers. Listeners." She let the words hang in the blue air. "We'll want to speak with her before we go. Nothing more. A courtesy between professionals."

Then they rode north along the far bank, unhurried, and Halvard stood on his side of the frozen river watching them go, and the fear that had been sitting in his chest all day finally finished sharpening itself into something else.

* * *

He climbed to Ulyana's workshop in the dark, taking the switchback path fast enough to make his lungs burn, because some conversations couldn't wait for morning and couldn't be had anywhere with walls that might listen.

She opened the door before he knocked. Behind her, past her shoulder, he saw the boy — Marguerite, though the name still sounded borrowed — sitting cross-legged by the stove with a bowl of barley soup, watching Halvard with eyes the color of new leaves. Watching him the way a deer watches, Halvard thought. Ready to be gone through a window.

"They're Wardens," Halvard said, stepping inside. "Three of them. Grey cloaks. They crossed the ford like it was summer grass."

Ulyana closed the door and put her back against it. For a moment she looked older than he'd ever seen her, and Halvard, who had spent nineteen years not asking her certain questions, felt all those unasked questions stand up at once inside him.

"I know," she said. "I felt them come over the ridge."

"Nothing comes over the ridge."

"I know that too." She laughed, short and joyless. "Apparently the ridge has opinions about who."

"Ulyana." He kept his voice low, for the boy's sake. "They know about you. The woman — Serel — she used your kind's name. Bone-worker. Listener. She wants a conversation. And Bren's been talking at the well, and half the village saw the riders, and by tomorrow everyone will know there are strangers asking after a child, and—" He stopped himself, breathed. Nineteen years of being the man who didn't panic in front of others. "You have to tell me what I'm lying about. All of it. Because I lied to them today, and I'm going to keep lying, and I can't do it blind."

The boy spoke then, from beside the stove, in the valley tongue — accented, careful, learned fast the way children learn everything fast. "She won't tell you. She doesn't tell anyone true things about herself. I've watched."

"Marguerite," Ulyana said sharply.

"It's all right," Halvard said, and surprised himself by meaning it. He crouched down to the boy's level, joints cracking, and looked at him plainly. "Here's what I know, lad. There's a woman in this valley who carves bones so the dead can talk to their grandchildren. She's done it twenty years and charged half of nothing and sat up all night with grieving families more times than I can count. Whatever she was before she came here — and I've always known she was something before she came here — that woman is the best of us." He stood up, knees protesting. "So whatever the truth costs, I'll pay my share of it. Both of you. That's not a question. That's the reeve speaking."

The silence held for a long moment. Snow ticked against the shutters. In the corner of the workshop, on its row of pegs, a carved charm turned slightly on its cord, though there was no draft — and Halvard decided, firmly, not to think about that tonight.

"She betrayed them," Marguerite said quietly, not cruelly, the way a child reports the weather. "The Garden. That's why she understands my language. That's why the ridge let her build her wards. She knows how they're made because she helped make them — for the other side."

Halvard looked at Ulyana. She had gone very still, the stillness of a woman standing on ice and listening to it speak.

"That true?" he asked.

"Yes," she said.

"Good." He nodded once, hard. "Now I know what I'm lying about."

"Halvard—"

"You saved yourself and damned a country. A hundred years ago, or however long ago it was, when you were young and scared and someone had a knife at your throat — I wasn't there. I don't get to weigh it." He pulled his coat back on, because the room had gone cold in a way that had nothing to do with the stove. "But I'll tell you what I told them today, and what I'll tell them tomorrow, and every day after: no such boy ever crossed my ford. And when they stop believing me — and they will, because Serel collects debts — I'd best not be standing alone."

He opened the door onto the dark and the falling snow. Below, the valley lay quiet under its law of silence, forty-one children asleep, the dead in their graves keeping their old peace. Above the ridge, somewhere in the black, three riders were making camp and making plans.

"Get the boy ready to travel," Halvard said. "Not tonight. But soon. Because come spring thaw, one way or another, this valley stops being safe — and I intend to be the one who decides which way he leaves it."

Behind him, faint as breath, one of the bone charms began very softly to sing.
