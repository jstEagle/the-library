## 10. Doors That No Longer Exist

The fire had burned down to its red bones by the time Marguerite found the words.

He sat on the floor of Ulyana's workshop with his back against her chair, because the chair was hers and he still wasn't sure he was allowed to touch it. She worked across the room at her bench, a knife whispering through something small and pale — a knucklebone, maybe, or the joint of a bird's wing. She carved the way other people prayed: steadily, without looking up, as if someone were listening even when no one was.

Someone always was, in this house. He could feel them. The charms hanging from the rafters made a sound like dry leaves when the wind came down the chimney, and sometimes — not often, but sometimes — one of them would hum a note that lived in the back of his throat too.

"Tell me again," Ulyana said, not looking up from her carving. "About the door."

"You've asked me that four times."

"And you've given me four different answers."

"That's because I keep remembering more." He pulled his knees up to his chest. "It's like — you know how ice has layers? When it freezes slow, the top is clear and everything under it gets cloudier? My memory is like that. The top layer is clear. The garden. The queen. The winter coming. But underneath—" He shrugged. "Underneath there's white."

The knife paused. Resumed.

"Start with what's clear," she said.

So he did. He told it the way he'd been telling it to himself all week, in the dark of the loft where she'd made him a bed out of blankets that smelled of tallow and old wool, telling it over and over so it wouldn't melt.

* * *

"There was a road," he said. "Not a road like yours. A road that grew. It went wherever the Garden wanted to go, and when the Garden stopped wanting to go somewhere, the road closed behind it like a hand closing. That's why nobody ever found us. We weren't hiding. We were just never where anyone had already looked."

Ulyana turned the little bone over in her fingers. "And the door?"

"The door was the end of the road. Or the beginning. Both. It stood in a hill that wasn't a hill — it was a hill the way an eggshell is a wall. Thin. Made to be broken from inside or opened from outside, but only ever one way at a time." He frowned, trying to hold the shape of it. "It didn't look like a door. It looked like a tree that had decided to be a door instead. There was a knot where the handle would be, and rings in the wood, and if you put your ear against it you could hear—"

He stopped.

"Hear what?" Ulyana asked quietly.

"Voices," Marguerite said. "Everyone who'd ever gone through. They stayed in the wood. That's how you knew it was real. A false door is silent. A true one is full."

The knife had stopped again. In the rafters, one of the bone charms gave a single soft click, like a tongue against teeth.

"Go on," Ulyana said.

"It only opens for a confession. That's the part I keep forgetting until I remember it, and then it feels like the most important thing in the world. Anyone can knock. Anyone can stand there and want to go home. But the door doesn't care about wanting. It opens for truth — truth said out loud, whole, held nothing back. The Garden has to accept it. And then the rings in the wood turn, and the hill opens, and you're home." He hugged his knees tighter. "I used to think that was a beautiful rule. Now I think it's just honest. You can't come back to a country of truth while carrying a lie. It wouldn't fit through."

Ulyana set down the knife.

For a long moment the only sound was the fire settling and the wind working at the eaves, and Marguerite watched her face do something complicated — a tightening around the eyes, a stillness in the mouth, the look of a woman holding a door shut from the inside.

"What confession did you bring?" she asked. Her voice was level. Too level, the way water is level right before it goes over the edge.

"I don't have one yet. I'm nine." He almost smiled. "The queen said children get in free. She said the door knows we haven't had time to become liars yet." The smile faded. "But she also said — she said it to me the last night, when the frost was already on the inside of the windows — she said if anything happened, if I ended up outside alone, I should find someone who hears the dead. Because the dead don't lie, and a person who listens to them learns the shape of truth even if they've forgotten how to speak it."

The silence that followed had weight. Marguerite felt it press on him like snow on a roof.

"And you found me," Ulyana said at last.

"You heard me before I knocked. I know you did. I was still in the trees and you opened the door."

"I heard the cold," she said. "Nothing else."

But her hands, folded now on the bench, had gone white at the knuckles, and Marguerite — who had spent his whole short life among people who told the truth as easily as breathing, and so had learned to notice the rare times they didn't — saw that she was lying, and did not understand why, and filed it away in the cloudy layer beneath his clear memories, where things waited to be remembered later.

* * *

Over the following days, he taught her the words.

They couldn't do it openly. Neighbors still came — the miller's wife with bread, old Sef with complaints about his knees, once the reeve Halvard with a face like a shut gate and questions he pretended weren't questions. So the lessons happened in the workshop with the shutter latched, Marguerite sitting cross-legged on the floor, Ulyana at her bench with her hands busy so her mouth could be free.

He taught her the word for *door*: **thaveth**, two syllables, the second one falling like a latch dropping into place.

He taught her *truth*: **sel**, which was also the word for *light*, because in the old language those had never been different things.

He taught her *confession*: **vessah** — and here he stumbled, because the word was longer than it looked. "It's not just saying," he explained. "Vess-ah means *un-carrying*. You say the thing and you put it down. But you have to actually put it down. If you say it and keep holding it, the word doesn't finish. It just hangs there."

"Like a charm half-carved," Ulyana said.

"Yes!" He beamed at her. "Exactly like that. How did you know?"

She didn't answer. Above them, faint as breath, three of the hanging charms began to sway though there was no wind.

That was the other thing he noticed, in those days, and tried not to notice, and noticed anyway: every word he taught her changed the room. When he spoke the old language alone, the dead in the bones stirred gently, like sleepers turning over. But when *she* repeated the words — her low voice shaping syllables that had been sleeping for a hundred years — the stirring became something else. The charms knocked together. The fire leaned sideways. Once, the little bell of bone above the door rang once, clearly, though nothing had touched it, and outside the window the snow on the sill shivered and slid off all at once, and for half a heartbeat Marguerite saw the air itself flicker, like a candle flame seen through water.

The wards, he thought. He didn't know the valley's word for them, but he knew what they were; every country had skin, and this valley's skin was scarred thick and old, and his language was finding the seams.

"Does that frighten you?" he asked her once, after the fire had done its leaning trick and both of them had sat very still until it behaved.

"No," Ulyana said.

Which was a lie, probably. But it was a kind lie, the kind you tell a child at night, and he took it the way you take such lies: gratefully, and knowing.

What frightened *him* was not the flickering. What frightened him was that each word she spoke seemed to cost her something. He couldn't name it. It was in the way her jaw set before **sel**, the way she exhaled after **thaveth** like a swimmer surfacing. As if the words were keys, and she recognized every lock they fit, and wished she didn't.

* * *

On the sixth night, the frost came inside.

Marguerite woke in the loft with his breath hanging visible in the dark and ferns of ice climbing the inside of the window glass, delicate and wrong, growing *toward* the warmth instead of away from it. Below, he heard Ulyana moving in the workshop, and the particular sound of her voice — low, urgent, in the valley's tongue — speaking to someone who did not answer in words.

He crept down the ladder. She stood in the middle of the room with a charm cupped in both palms, an old one, its bone gone yellow and soft-edged with handling. The charm was speaking. Not the murmured fragments he'd heard from the others, but whole sentences, in a woman's voice, worn thin as river stone:

*—the rings are turning, the rings are turning backward, whoever holds this, tell them the door remembers, tell them the door waits, tell them—*

The voice frayed out. Ulyana closed her fist around the charm and it went silent, and for a moment she just stood there in the frozen moonlight with her eyes shut, and Marguerite thought she looked like a woman listening to a sound that had stopped years ago and started again tonight.

"The queen," he whispered.

"She doesn't say her name." Ulyana opened her eyes. "But yes. I think so."

"She's getting louder. The closer winter comes, the louder." He wrapped his arms around himself. "In the Garden, when the frost first came, the dead got loud too. Everyone said it was because the border between things gets thin when it's cold. Alive and dead. Inside and outside. True and false." He looked up at her. "Ulyana. The door. Do you know where it is?"

Something crossed her face — fast, gone before he could read it, like a fish under ice.

"Why?"

"Because I dreamed about it. Last night. I dreamed I was walking on a frozen lake and under my feet there was light, green-gold light, like the garden in summer, and the queen's voice came up through the ice and said *beneath, child, beneath.*" His voice shook. "There's a lake near here. Halvard's boys fish it in summer. I saw it when they carried me through the village. Under the ice, Ulyana. I think the door is under the ice."

Ulyana was quiet for a long time. The frost ferns on the window creaked as they grew.

"Lake Vessa," she said finally. "Yes. I think you're right."

The word went through Marguerite like a struck bell. "*Vessah,*" he breathed. "Un-carrying. The lake is named — the lake is named for confessions?"

"The valley named it long ago. Before my time." She turned away, busied herself banking the fire, and her voice came out carefully flat. "Names wear smooth here. Nobody remembers what they meant."

But Marguerite remembered the shape of her shoulders when she'd said it, and the way she hadn't looked at him, and he added it to the cloudy layer, where the white things waited.

* * *

Later — much later, past midnight, when the frost had been beaten back from the windows and the fire built up and both of them should have been asleep — he found her still at the bench, and he climbed onto the stool across from her because some things needed saying face to face.

"When I go home," he said, "you could come."

Her knife stopped mid-stroke.

"The door opens for a confession the Garden accepts. It doesn't say the confession has to be about yourself. Maybe — maybe you could confess for the valley. For the people here. All the things they've done, good and bad, the whole truth of them. Wouldn't the Garden accept that?" He leaned forward, urgent, the plan unfolding in him bright and simple as a paper flower. "You could see it. The terraces. The fountains that run uphill. The queen's court where the candles never burn down. You saved my life. You should get to—"

"Marguerite."

One word. It stopped him the way a hand on the chest stops a boy running downhill.

She set the knife down and looked at him across the bench, and her face in the firelight was the strangest thing he had ever seen on it: hope and grief sitting side by side, keeping perfectly still, like two animals that had agreed to a truce neither trusted.

"The door will open for you," she said. "That's what matters. Nothing else."

"But—"

"Nothing else." She picked the knife back up. "Now sleep. Tomorrow I'll teach you a word, for once. One of ours. *Enough.*"

He went up the ladder. He lay in the dark under the tallow-smelling blankets and listened to the knife whispering below, steady as a pulse, and he told himself the tightness in his chest was just the cold coming back.

But in the cloudy layer, the white things shifted. Somewhere under the ice of his forgetting, a shape moved — a memory not of the Garden but of the purge, of stories told in whispers in the queen's court, of a traitor whose name had been carved out of every record, whose crime had been spoken only once, by the queen herself, on the last night, in the same breath as her instruction: *find someone who hears the dead.*

*Even her,* the queen had said. *Especially her. Forgiveness is a door too, and mine has been locked a long time.*

Marguerite stared at the frost ferns on the window and did not let himself finish the thought. Not yet. The top layer of ice was clear, and he meant to keep it clear a little longer.

Below him, the knife whispered on. And once — just once, so softly he might have dreamed it — he heard Ulyana speak a single word of the old language, alone in the dark, practicing:

"*Vessah.*"

Un-carrying.

Said like a woman testing whether a wound still bleeds.
