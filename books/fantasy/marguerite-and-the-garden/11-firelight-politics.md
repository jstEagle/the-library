## 11. Firelight Politics

The meeting hall had not been this full since the flood year, and Halvard remembered the flood year the way a man remembers a broken bone — by weather in his knee.

He stood at the front with his back to the great hearth because that was where the reeve stood, and because the fire's heat on his spine was the only warmth he trusted tonight. Forty-odd faces looked up at him from benches and barrels and the windowsills of latecomers. The snow outside had stopped an hour ago, which meant the cold would come down hard now, clean and absolute, and every soul in the room knew it.

"Again," said Marta Ostrick from the second row, "for those who came late."

Halvard didn't sigh. A reeve who sighed gave people permission to grumble. "A boy came to Ulyana Winterbourne's door nine days ago. Half-frozen. Speaking no tongue anyone knows. He has been under her roof since. That is all I know for certain, and most of what you've heard beyond it is smoke."

"The smoke is the problem," said Ostrick.

There it was. He'd known it would come from her. Marta Ostrick had lost a husband to the river and a son to the lowlands, and she had made grief into a kind of office she held over the whole valley. She was not cruel. She was thorough, which was worse in winter.

"Say what you mean, Marta," called Torv Anders from the back, and there was laughter, brief and brittle.

"I mean," she said, unbothered, "that strange things have been happening since that boy arrived, and we are fools if we sit here and pretend otherwise. The dead are louder. Ask any house that keeps a charm. My mother's charm hasn't spoken in eleven years, and last week it said her name clear as a bell while I was mending socks. Clear as a bell." She held up her hands as if the words were still caught between them. "I dropped the sock. I want that on the record. I dropped my mending."

More laughter, but thinner. Because half the room had similar stories, and they all knew it. The charms were talking. The dead, so long quiet in this valley — quieter than anywhere else, that was the valley's whole bargain, its whole pride — were stirring like sleepers before a storm.

Halvard let the laughter die on its own. "Charm-talk isn't new. It comes and goes with the seasons."

"Not like this." That was old Sef, the miller, who rarely spoke at meetings and therefore was never interrupted. "My father's charm spoke to me three nights running. It said *the door*. Over and over. *The door, the door.* My father has been dead twenty years and he never once cared about doors." Sef looked around the room. "Something is knocking, is what I'm saying. Something is knocking and we're arguing about whether to answer."

The fire popped. Somewhere near the back a baby fussed and was hushed.

"The boy," Halvard said. "That's what we're deciding tonight. Whether he stays through winter or goes."

"Goes where?" Torv again. "Into the snow? You want us to put a child out into midwinter? What are we, Wardens?"

At the word *Wardens* the room changed temperature. Not warmer or colder — just denser, the way air gets before lightning. Everyone in the valley knew the stories. Everyone's grandmother had told them: the grey-cloaked men who ended the age of magic, who burned the Garden country off the maps, who hunted what remained until nothing remained, and then went home to their stone houses and their long patience. Stories for children, mostly. Scare-tales with a moral: *keep your head down, keep your charms small, thank the ridge that magic cannot follow.*

Two strangers had crossed the ford yesterday asking after a missing child.

Halvard hadn't told the village yet. He'd been holding it like a stone in his fist all day, turning it over, looking for the angle that made it lighter. There wasn't one.

"Before anyone votes anything," he said, "you should know two travelers came through yesterday. Grey cloaks. Asked at the ford inn after a boy of about ten, dark-haired, alone. Said he was kin of theirs. Sick. Wandered off."

"And you told them?" Ostrick asked.

"I told them no such boy had passed."

The silence had a texture to it, woolly and close. Halvard watched faces do arithmetic. *No such boy had passed* — true, technically; the boy had not *passed*, he had *arrived* and stayed. Halvard had spent thirty years learning exactly how much truth a sentence could carry without breaking.

"You lied to them," said a young voice — Bren, the smith's daughter, sixteen and fearless in the way that only the young and the dying ever manage. "Good."

"It wasn't lying," Halvard said. "It was—" He stopped. "It was lying. And I'll answer for it. But I want everyone to understand what we're choosing, because I don't think you do. Those men weren't kin. Kin don't wear travel boots worth more than this hall. Kin don't ask questions in the flat voice of men reading from a list. Whatever that boy is running from, it has money and patience and a very long memory, and when I lied to them, I spent some portion of this village's safety doing it. So think hard before you spend more."

Old Sef spoke again. "What does Ulyana say?"

And there it was — the other stone Halvard had been carrying, the heavier one. Every eye in the room swung toward him with the question underneath the question, the one nobody had asked out loud yet, and he understood with a tired clarity that tonight was never going to be about the boy at all.

"She says he stays," Halvard said. "She says he's half-dead with cold and fear and whatever else, and that no one freezes on her doorstep twice."

"Convenient," said Ostrick softly.

"Marta—"

"No, reeve, let me say it, since we're all thinking it and pretending we aren't, which is how this valley rots, little by little, politeness by politeness." She rose. She was not tall, but she had the gift of standing like a verdict. "Ulyana Winterbourne came here nineteen years ago with no history and no family and a trade nobody taught her, and we took her in because she was useful and because she was sad, and sad people are easy to love from a distance. And in nineteen years she has never once told us where she came from. Never. Not drunk at a wedding, not feverish in childbirth season, not once. And now a child appears at her door speaking a language *she understands* — don't look shocked, half of us have heard her answering him through the window — and the dead wake up, and strangers in fine boots come asking, and we're meant to vote on the boy without asking the obvious question."

"Ask it, then," Halvard said quietly.

"Why is she *here*?" Ostrick's voice didn't rise; it sharpened. "People flee to the ends of the earth for two reasons. Because something chased them, or because something would. Which was it? And did it stop chasing?"

The room breathed. Halvard heard the specific silence of a community agreeing with someone it was ashamed to agree with.

He could end it. He knew things. Nineteen years ago, in his first winter as reeve, a exhausted woman with burned fingertips had stood in this same spot and told him a single sentence — not her story, only its edge — and he had chosen, deliberately, deliberately, to build his reeve-ship partly on the foundation of not repeating it. He could stand up right now and say *I know why she came, and it's none of yours,* and half the room would fold, because they trusted him, and the other half would spend the rest of the winter digging.

Instead he said: "Nineteen years ago I asked her that question. She answered it. I've kept her answer for nineteen years, and I'll keep it nineteen more, because she earned that the day she carved old Wenna's charm so Wenna could tell her son goodbye before the fever took her hearing. Where she came from doesn't matter. What she's done here does. That's my position. Argue with it."

"Your position," said a man near the door — Pell, the tanner, who owed Halvard money and had never forgiven him for it. "Your position, and your lie to the strangers, and your friendship. Funny how it all points one direction, reeve."

"Funny how the truth does that," Halvard said, "when you follow it far enough."

Pell flushed. Someone snorted. The room tilted toward Halvard and he felt the old, sour knowledge of how easily these things tilt — how a crowd is a sled on a hillside and a reeve is just the man steering until the grade changes.

Bren stood up. "I'll say what I think, since we're being honest tonight. When the strangers asked about the boy, did anyone else notice their faces?" She waited. She had the smith's timing. "They weren't worried. Worried people look *around*. These men looked *through* you. Like you were furniture. Like the boy was a thing that had rolled under a cabinet. I've seen that look once before — on the drovers, moving stock they didn't name out loud because naming makes it real." Her chin came up. "They're not looking for a lost nephew. They're looking for something they intend to collect. And I don't care whose nephew he is. Nobody collects children out of my valley."

Applause — scattered, then fuller. Halvard watched Ostrick not join it, watched her sit slowly with her arms folded, unconvinced but outnumbered for the moment, which in valley politics was the same as defeated and twice as dangerous.

* * *

The meeting broke apart an hour later into the kind of resolution that satisfies no one: the boy stays through winter, by a show of hands of roughly two-to-one, with a rider that Ulyana present him to the village at first thaw, and a second rider, added by Ostrick and accepted out of sheer exhaustion, that the reeve personally vouch for any consequences.

*Personally vouch.* As if consequences were a debt he could pay down in installments.

Afterward the hall emptied in twos and threes, breath steaming, lanterns swinging away down the lanes, and Halvard stayed to bank the fire because that was a reeve's job too, the small undignified keeping of things. He was crouched at the hearth with the poker when a shadow resolved into old Sef, who had lingered with the patience of a man who had learned that the important conversations happen after the meeting, in the cooling room, beside the ashes.

"They'll come back," Sef said. "The grey men."

"Yes."

"And next time they won't ask at the inn. They'll ask at doors. One at a time. And someone — Pell, or one of Pell's drinking friends, or some frightened soul at midnight — will tell them about the boy at the bone-carver's house, not out of malice, just out of the ordinary human wish to be left alone." Sef lowered himself onto a bench with a groan. "You know that. I can see you know that."

"I know that."

"So the vote tonight was theater. Nice theater. Good lines. The girl Bren will dine out on that speech for a year. But it settled nothing, because it couldn't." Sef warmed his knuckles at the coals. "Here's what I actually came to say. My father's charm. *The door, the door.* Last night it said something different. It said a name."

Halvard went still. Coals ticked in the silence.

"Whose name?"

Sef looked at him for a long moment, and in that moment Halvard saw that the old man had carried this around all day the way Halvard had carried the grey cloaks — turning it, finding no lightness in it.

"It said *the queen*," Sef said. "In the old way. The way the stories say it. *The queen beneath the snow.* And then it said — and reeve, I'm telling you plain, I checked the carving twice to be sure I wasn't mishearing — it said *she forgives the hand that opened the gate.*"

The poker slipped a fraction in Halvard's grip. He set it down carefully, the way you set down anything when your hands have gone strange.

"Nineteen years ago," Sef said, rising, joints cracking like green wood, "a woman came to this valley with burned fingers and no past, and you asked her one question and kept her answer. I won't ask what it was. But I'll tell you what my father's dead mouth told me, free of charge, and you can do with it what you did with yours." He shuffled toward the door, then paused, silhouetted against the blue dark beyond. "Whatever gate she opened, reeve — somebody's been waiting nineteen years to forgive her for it. And I don't think the waiting is patient anymore."

The door closed behind him. The fire settled. Outside, the cold came down off the ridge like a held breath finally released, and Halvard stood alone in the ash-smelling dark, understanding at last that the stone he'd been carrying all day had never been the strangers at the ford.

It was the sentence he'd kept for nineteen years. And tomorrow, one way or another, he was going to have to give it back.
