## 12. The Warden's Offer

The stranger came on the ninth day of the boy's hiding, when the snow had stopped pretending it would melt and settled in for the season.

Ulyana saw him first from the workshop window: a figure walking up the ridge road with the unhurried gait of a man who had never once been late for anything and never once hurried either. He wore grey — not the grey of wool or weather but a flat, deliberate grey, like stone that had been asked to be nothing else. No cloak clasp. No sigil. The Wardens had learned centuries ago that you didn't need a badge when your face did the work.

She knew that face. Not his specifically — she'd never seen this one before — but the make of it. The stillness around the eyes. She had seen it across a burning courtyard a lifetime ago, on faces that watched her run and made no move to stop her, because stopping her was unnecessary. They already had what they wanted.

Her hands were full of bone dust and goose fat, working a charm for old Sef's hip that ached worse in cold. She set them down carefully, wiped them on her apron, and stood very still until the knock came.

Three knocks. Evenly spaced. The knock of someone who knew the door would open.

* * *

"You're not from the lowlands," she said, before he could speak.

"No." He stood in the doorway with snow melting off his shoulders in small, orderly drops, as if even the weather cooperated with him halfway. "I'm from further than that."

"Everyone's from further than that, this time of year."

A faint smile touched him and withdrew, like a coin shown and pocketed. "Mistress Winterbourne. Or should I say—" and here he paused, delicately, the way a surgeon pauses over the place to cut, "—the name you use now is fine. It's the one I'll use."

The cold that went through her then had nothing to do with the season. She kept her face still. Thirty years of practice at keeping her face still, and it held.

"I don't know you," she said.

"No. But I know your work. The charms are elegant. Crude materials, of course — valley bones, valley dead — but the technique underneath." He tilted his head slightly. "That's Garden work. That's *court* work. Nobody else carves the listening-edge like that. Nobody alive, anyway."

Behind her, in the back room where Marguerite was supposed to be asleep, there was a silence so complete and so effortful that it rang like a struck bell. Ulyana prayed silently to no one in particular that the boy understood, as she had drilled into him every night since Halvard's warning, that this was the moment he absolutely must not exist.

"Come in out of the snow," she said, "or go back down the mountain. Standing in doorways is how villages get ideas."

He stepped inside. Of course he did.

* * *

His name, he offered as he sat at her worktable without being invited, was Corvess. He said it the way men say names they've worn so long the person underneath has died of it. He looked around her workshop with an appraiser's patience — the hanging charms, the little curtains of carved rib and knucklebone that swayed without wind, the mortars, the tallow, the jars of ash labeled in her own hand.

"You listen to them," he said. "The dead here."

"That's the trade. People pay in eggs and firewood."

"And they talk? These valley dead?"

"When they have something to say. Mostly they don't. Mostly it's—" she shrugged, "—weather. A hum. Grief settles faster here than most places. That's why people like the valley."

"Yes," Corvess said softly. "It is quiet here. Remarkably quiet. We mapped it, you know. After the purges. Every place where the old currents died or were killed. This valley lit up on our charts like a wound gone septic and then — nothing. Silence, within a decade. Complete silence." He looked at her. "We never determined why. Magic doesn't die naturally, Mistress Winterbourne. It's killed, or it leaves, or it's *bound*. And binding on that scale requires a hand."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside a crow said something rude and flew off.

"You flatter me," Ulyana said. "I carve trinkets for farmers."

"Mm." He let it lie there between them, unclaimed, like a knife left on a table. Then, briskly, as if changing the subject: "You've had a visitor recently. A child. Small, dark-haired, half-frozen when he arrived. Speaks oddly. The reeve says no such boy exists. The innkeeper says no such boy exists. The miller's wife, who saw him at your window, has suddenly developed a memory like a sieve." He smiled again, and this time it stayed a moment too long. "Your village lies beautifully, Mistress. All of them. At once. Do you know how rare that is? Villages usually crack. Someone always wants the reward, or the revenge, or just the pleasure of watching a neighbor fall. Yours has closed like a fist."

"They're good people."

"They're *her* people," Corvess said. "Yours, I mean. The queen's. Scattered seedlings. We've spent a hundred years pulling them up by the roots, gently, gently, and mostly they don't even remember what garden they came from. But they recognize each other. Isn't that strange? A look across a market square and two strangers who've never met will lie for each other like siblings." He spread his hands. "Loyalty without memory. It's the most stubborn thing we've ever fought."

Ulyana said nothing. Her heart was doing arithmetic it hated: *he knows about the boy, he doesn't know where the boy is, he can't search yet because he isn't certain enough, and everything depends on him leaving this room less certain than he entered it.*

"The boy crossed the ridge," Corvess went on. "In the first snow. On foot. From the far side." He watched her the way she imagined a man watches a trapline. "Nothing crosses the ridge from the far side, Mistress. The far side is the Garden's grave, and the Garden does not export. Except—" and his voice dropped into something almost gentle, almost kind, which was the worst register yet, "—except once. Except one courier. One traitor. Who walked out of the burning court with her life and her hands intact, and whom we let walk, because she gave us what we needed. Because she told us where the queen kept the doors."

The room tilted. Ulyana gripped the edge of the table and found it solid, which felt like a technicality.

"That was a long time ago," she said. Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of it.

"It was," Corvess agreed. "And here's the thing about long-ago crimes, Mistress Winterbourne. We're the only ones who keep the ledger. The valley doesn't know. The boy doesn't know. The dead you carve — do they know? Perhaps. Dead things know everything and hold their tongues; it's their best quality." He leaned forward slightly. "No one living knows what you did. Except us. And we are prepared to forget it too."

* * *

"Forget it," she repeated.

"Erase it. There are records. Sealed ones, in the deep archive — testimony, ledgers, the confession you signed when you were twenty-three years old and shaking so hard the ink blotted. All of it can burn. Officially, the courier who betrayed the Garden died in the purge with everyone else. Unofficially—" he gestured at the workshop, the charms, the whole quiet valley, "—she lived, hid, grew old, and will be buried under a stone with a name that means nothing. That history stands. It's a good history. Warm. Defensible." He folded his hands. "Or it burns tonight, along with the records, and you become simply an old woman who carves bones. No past at all. A woman with nothing behind her and whatever she likes ahead."

"And in exchange."

"In exchange you bring me the boy."

There it was. Laid on the table between the tallow jar and the unfinished charm for Sef's hip, plain as a price tag. Ulyana looked at it. She made herself look at it properly, the way she'd learned to look at anything frightening — straight on, in full light, because things you refused to look at grew teeth in the dark.

"What happens to him?" she asked.

Corvess's expression didn't change, which was itself an answer, but he gave words anyway, smooth as poured wax. "He'll be cared for. He's a child of the Garden — the last one, possibly the last vessel. What he carries can be removed. Extracted. Contained. The winter ends when the last seed dies, Mistress. That's all any of this has ever been. An ending. Tidying up. You of all people understand tidying up."

"Do I."

"You bound a valley's magic into silence to save yourself a second time," Corvess said. "Did you think we hadn't worked that out? The wards on this ridge aren't ruins, they're *knots*. Beautiful knots. Court-work knots. Somebody tied them, and somebody tied them from the inside, and that somebody has lived comfortably in the silence ever since." He shook his head with something that might have been admiration. "You've been protecting yourself here for thirty years with the same skill you used to protect yourself there. I confess I find it restful, meeting a professional."

Outside, the light was going blue with early dusk. In the back room, that ringing silence continued. Ulyana thought of Marguerite lying on the cot she'd given him, listening to every word through three inches of pine plank, a child learning exactly what she was. Let him hear it, some cold honest part of her said. Better he learns it from a wall than from a knife.

"And if I refuse?" she asked.

Corvess sighed, genuinely, and for a moment he looked tired — a man at the end of a very long errand. "Then the ledger stays open. And we do what we've always done. Slowly. Thoroughly. We've waited a hundred years; we can wait out an old woman and a frightened village. But I'd rather not. Truly. The boy will be found eventually — he's a lamp in a cellar, Mistress, he glows through the walls whether you see it or not — and when he's found, everyone who sheltered him will be remembered too. The reeve. The miller's wife. The innkeeper with the sieve-memory. Your good people, entered into the ledger." He rose, brushing snow-water from his grey knees. "Give him up, and none of it touches them. That's the mercy in the offer. Not yours. Theirs."

At the door he paused, hand on the latch, and did not turn around.

"For what it's worth," he said, "the queen is dead. Whatever's left of her is dead. You owe a corpse nothing. Corpses don't forgive and they don't punish — they only get used, by people like me, or people like you." The latch clicked. "Think until midwinter. That's when we come up the valley road, whichever way you choose. Everyone will be awake that night anyway. It's the longest night. It seemed fitting."

Then he was gone, down into the blue snow, his footprints filling behind him as though the world were eager to agree he'd never been there.

* * *

Ulyana stood in the middle of her workshop for a long time.

Then she went to the back room and opened the door, and Marguerite was sitting up on the cot with his knees pulled to his chest, and his eyes were dry and enormous and full of a knowledge no child should carry.

"You sold them," he said in the old tongue. His voice cracked on the second word. "You sold the doors."

"I did." There was no version of denial that survived what he'd heard through the wall. She sat down on the floor beside the cot, because standing felt like a posture and she was done with postures. "When I was younger than you'll believe. When I was afraid. And I've been paying for it ever since, in a currency you're too young to count."

"The lady," he whispered. "The queen. Was she—"

"She was kind to me. That's the worst part. She was kind, and I was afraid, and fear is faster than kindness. Fear always gets its answer first." Ulyana looked at her own hands, the carver's hands, steady as they'd ever been. "He's offering to erase it. Did you hear that part? Thirty years of silence, bought and paid for, and he'll wipe the ledger clean. All I have to do is hand you over."

Marguerite said nothing. In his chest, she knew, the seed sat like a coal in ash, and somewhere far away under the ice a dead country leaned toward this conversation.

"He gives me until midwinter," she said.

Outside, the first flakes of the evening snow had begun to fall — fine, dry, wrong somehow, falling too slowly, as if the sky itself were hesitating. Ulyana watched them through the window and felt, for the first time in thirty years, the wards at the ridge flicker like a guttering lamp.

Midwinter was nine days away. And the longest night was coming faster than any of them knew.
