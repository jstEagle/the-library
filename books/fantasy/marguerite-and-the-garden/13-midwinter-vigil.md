## 13. Midwinter Vigil

**Ulyana**

The longest night began the way all her worst days began: with a knock.

Not on her door this time. On her bones.

She was at the workbench when it happened — a fine tremor running through every charm she had ever carved, the whole row of them rattling against their hooks like teeth in a jaw. She put down her knife. The lamplight guttered though there was no draft.

"Quiet," she told them, and they were not quiet.

The dead of Winterbourne Valley had always spoken in whispers. That was the arrangement. That was the *law* — no magic crosses the ridge — and for thirty years the law had held like ice on a pond, thin in places but holding. A grandmother's charm might murmur a warning about a bad well. A child's bone might sigh its own name once a year on the anniversary of its drowning. Small voices. Contained voices.

Tonight they were shouting.

Ulyana stood in the middle of her workshop with her arms crossed over her chest as if she could hold herself together by force, and listened to forty years of the valley's dead speak all at once. Names she knew. Names she had carved herself, one careful letter at a time. And beneath them, underneath everything, a voice that did not belong to the valley at all — vast and slow and cold, like something turning over in its sleep beneath the floor of the world.

*Winter,* it said, in the old tongue. *I am coming where you hid.*

"No," Ulyana said aloud, to a workshop with no one in it. "You are not."

Her breath fogged. Inside. Her breath fogged inside her own house, and the water bucket by the door had skinned itself with ice an inch thick, and outside the first snow of midwinter was falling straight down, without wind, like ash from a fire too far away to hear.

She thought of the boy asleep upstairs. She thought of the seed she could feel through the floorboards when she stood beneath his room — a faint warmth, a green pulse, the only living thing in the valley that the cold seemed afraid of.

She took down her coat.

* * *

**Marguerite**

Marguerite woke because the dead were singing.

He had heard them before, faintly, since the night he crossed the ridge — a murmur under the floor, a hush in the walls, the way you might hear neighbors through stone. But tonight they were not murmuring. Tonight they filled the house like water filling a hull, and they were singing in the language he had spoken before he had any other, the language of the Garden, and the song was a counting song, the kind the gardeners sang in autumn:

*One for the root, two for the shoot,*
*three for the bud that sleeps—*

He came downstairs barefoot and found Ulyana pulling on her boots by the light of a lamp that burned blue instead of yellow.

"You feel it," she said. Not a question.

"The wards." He had learned the word from her, one of the few valley-words he owned now. "They're — thinning. Like ice before the thaw. Only backward."

"Yes." She looked at him a long moment, and in the blue light her face was older than he had ever seen it, older than faces were meant to be. "Get your coat. And don't touch anything iron tonight. The dead are confused, and confused dead grab at whatever's warm."

They went out into the falling snow.

The village was already awake. Every window on the lane glowed, and people stood in their doorways wrapped in blankets, looking up — not at the sky but at the ridge, where the snow fell into a darkness that did not reflect any of it. The mountains had eaten the stars. Where the wards ran along the high passes, Ulyana had once told him, the air itself remembered an old argument and refused to let certain things cross. Marguerite could see the argument failing. Along the ridgeline, faint as veins under skin, lines of pale light flickered — green, then white, then nothing, then green again — like a heartbeat losing its rhythm.

"It's never done that," said a voice behind them. Halvard stood in the lane, coatless in the cold, which for a reeve was a kind of nakedness. "Thirty years I've lived under those lights. They've never so much as stuttered."

"They're not stuttering," Ulyana said. "They're being *asked.*"

"Asked what?"

"To stop."

Nobody spoke. Somewhere a dog began to howl, and then another, and then all the dogs of the valley at once, a single long note of animal grief that rose and rose until Marguerite pressed his hands over his ears — and even then he could still hear the counting song underneath, *three for the bud that sleeps*, and he understood suddenly, with a child's terrible clarity, that the song was not for him.

It was for the seed. And the winter was learning the words.

* * *

**Halvard**

By midnight, Halvard had done the thing a reeve does when the world breaks: he opened the meetinghouse and told everyone to come inside.

It was not courage. It was arithmetic. Two hundred souls, one building, one great hearth, and a night that felt like it intended to be longer than any night had a right to be. If the cold at the edges of the valley was real — and it was real; Sela Ostrow's well had frozen solid top to bottom, and the millrace had gone silent, locked in ice while the river beneath still ran — then the center would hold longer than the edges, and the center was the meetinghouse, and the meetinghouse needed a fire and a headcount and someone standing at the door with a lantern looking like he knew what he was doing.

So he stood at the door with a lantern, looking like he knew what he was doing, and inside his chest something small and cold kept whispering: *you lied to armed men three weeks ago, and here is the bill.*

The strangers had gone. That was the trouble. Wardens, if that was what they were — gray men with gray eyes who asked about a child and showed him a drawing that made his stomach drop through the floorboards, because the drawing was Marguerite, unmistakably, down to the set of the boy's chin. Halvard had sent them east over the ford and sworn on his office that no such child had passed through the valley. They had thanked him politely. One of them had smiled.

And now the sky over the ridge was flickering like a dying lamp, and every dog in the valley was singing its heart out, and Halvard found himself doing the arithmetic again, differently: *if they come back, and they will come back, they will not be polite twice.*

"Reeve." Old Marta Voss caught his sleeve as she shuffled in, her granddaughter bundled against her like firewood. "You'll want to know. The churchyard stones. They're warm."

"Warm?"

"Warm as bread. All of them. Every grave in the yard." Marta's face in the lantern light was calm the way only very old people can be calm, having already survived everything worth fearing. "My husband's been dead eleven years and his stone is warmer than my hands. You tell me what that means, Halvard Winterbourne's-reeve, because I'm too old to guess."

"I don't know," he said honestly. "Keep everyone inside. Keep them talking. Fear is quieter in a crowd."

Marta snorted and went in. And Halvard stood alone in the falling snow, looking up the dark lane toward the carver's house on the hill, where the lamplight burned blue, and let himself think the thought he had been refusing for three weeks:

*I have known what she is since the day she came down off that ridge with frostbite and a box of bones, and I chose her anyway, and now the choosing has come due.*

He turned up the lane. The reeve's duty was the meetinghouse. But the reeve's heart, apparently, had other paperwork.

* * *

**Ulyana**

At the edge of the churchyard, the dead were waiting for her.

She had known they would be. The warmth of the stones had pulled her like a rope — forty years of graves, and every one of them radiating heat into the freezing night, and above each stone the snow did not land. It hung. It hung in the air in slow spirals, turning, and in each little spiral of hanging snow Ulyana could see a shape gathering, the way breath gathers on glass.

"Carver," said the grave of Tam Ostrow, drowned at nine, whose charm hung on her wall at home. His voice was a boy's voice, bright and bubbling. "Carver, it's coming. We can feel it under us. It's coming up the roots of the world."

"We know," Ulyana said.

"The lady says hold the line."

Ulyana went still. "What lady?"

But she knew. Even as the word left the boy's mouth she knew, and the knowing went through her like a blade finding the gap between ribs. Because there was only one lady the valley's dead would obey, only one queen whose voice could reach into a ward-sealed valley and rally bones that had been silent for decades — and Ulyana had sealed this valley herself, had built the silence brick by brick, had buried the queen's name so deep that even Ulyana had forgotten it until a boy walked out of the snow speaking it.

"She says—" Tam's voice wavered, stretched, became briefly something older and vaster speaking through a nine-year-old's ghost, and the hanging snow around his stone all turned at once to face her. "*You know what you owe, daughter of the carving bench. The door remembers. The door has always remembered. When the night is longest, bring what you buried.*"

The presence withdrew. The snow fell. The stones went cold, all at once, like a held breath released.

Ulyana stood alone in the churchyard with her heart hammering and her hands, her carver's hands, steady as they had ever been — steady the way things are steady when they have stopped pretending.

*Bring what you buried.*

In her workshop, in a box beneath the floor, wrapped in oilcloth, lay a length of antler bone she had never been able to throw away. On it, half-carved, abandoned mid-sentence thirty years ago, was a confession she had never finished.

She had told herself she kept it as evidence against herself. A punishment. A reminder.

Standing in the cold among the warming-dead-cold-again graves, Ulyana finally admitted what it was.

It was a draft. She had been drafting her confession for thirty years, and she had never found the courage to finish the last line.

Behind her, up the lane, a small figure in a too-large coat was picking its way through the snow toward the churchyard — the boy, awake, barefoot under his boots, drawn by the same voices. She watched him come and felt the two halves of her life grind against each other like millstones: the woman who hid him, and the woman who had sold his country.

Both women loved him. Only one of them deserved to.

"Marguerite," she called softly. "Come inside. The vigil's starting."

* * *

**Marguerite**

They kept the vigil the way the valley kept everything: practically, stubbornly, with soup.

The meetinghouse was full and loud and warm, and Marguerite sat between Ulyana and the reeve at the long table near the hearth, and the villagers pretended he was an ordinary child, and he pretended back, and between them they built a small architecture of normalcy strong enough to hold the night at arm's length. There was barley soup. There was a fiddle, until the fiddler's strings snapped all at once, all four, with a sound like small screams, and he laughed shakily and put the fiddle away and nobody asked him to play again.

Every hour, someone went to the door and looked at the ridge and came back and reported, as if reporting weather: *still flickering. Still dark up there. Millrace still frozen.*

And every hour, the cold crept a little further in.

Marguerite felt it the way he felt everything now — through the seed. It sat behind his sternum like a second heart, small and green and frightened, and as midnight passed and the night refused to end, its beating slowed. Not dangerously. Not yet. But slowing, the way a bird slows when a hawk's shadow crosses the grass.

Around him the villagers talked in low voices, and he understood more of it than he used to. Enough to catch the arguments threading under the hymns. *Why here, why now.* Enough to catch an old man mutter, not unkindly but not quietly either, that trouble had come to the valley the same season the carver took in a stray. Enough to see Ulyana's hands go still around her cup, and see the reeve put his own hand flat on the table — not touching hers, just *near* hers, the way men in this valley said things they couldn't say — and say, loudly enough for the room, "Trouble comes when it comes. This valley doesn't turn out its own on the longest night. That's not who we are."

*Its own,* Marguerite thought. *Am I its own?*

He looked at Ulyana. She was watching the fire, and the firelight moved on her face, and for a moment — just a moment — her expression was open in a way he had never seen, grief and guilt and love all tangled together like yarn a cat had played with. He had seen that look before. In the Garden. On the faces of the last gardeners, the ones who remembered what had been lost.

He was still trying to understand it when the bells started.

There were no bells in Winterbourne Valley. Everyone knew that. And yet from the direction of the ridge, clear across the frozen miles, came the sound of a bell — one slow toll, then another, deep and bronze and mournful, a sound Marguerite knew in his bones because it was the bell of the Garden's gate, rung once for each year of mourning, and it had been rung a hundred times the day the winter came.

The whole meetinghouse went silent. Two hundred people listening to a bell that did not exist.

Ulyana rose. So did Halvard. Outside, the dogs had stopped howling all at once, and the silence they left behind was worse than the noise.

"What is it?" Halvard asked quietly. "What's ringing?"

"A door," Ulyana said. Her voice was strange — steady and shattered at once. "Or what's left of one. It's announcing itself." She turned to Marguerite, and in the hearthlight her eyes were wet and fierce. "It's answering the queen. The dead said *bring what you buried.* The door is asking to be found."

"Then we find it," Marguerite said. "That's why I came. That's why I crossed the ridge — I didn't remember how, but I remember *why.*"

"Not tonight." Ulyana's hand closed on his shoulder, gentle and immovable. "Not in the dark, not in the cold, not with Wardens somewhere behind that bell. Tomorrow. When we can see."

But Marguerite barely heard her. Because the bell had stopped ringing, and in the ringing's wake, faint under the wind, he could hear the counting song again — and this time the winter was singing along, and it had learned the third verse, and the third verse was the one about waking.

*Three for the bud that sleeps.*
*Four for the hand that digs it free.*

Somewhere out in the dark, something had begun to dig.

* * *

**Halvard**

Near dawn — if it could be called dawn, the sky merely going from black to bruise — Halvard stepped out of the meetinghouse into a world gone utterly still.

No wind. No snowfall. The cold had stopped creeping and simply arrived, sitting on the valley like a lid. His breath hung in the air and did not disperse. And on the ridge, for the first time all night, the ward-lights had gone out completely — all of them, along every pass, a mountain range wearing blindfolds.

Except one.

Far up the eastern slope, where no pass ran and no path went, a single point of light had appeared. Green. Steady. Small as a candle seen across a lake.

Halvard had lived in this valley his whole life. He knew its mountains the way he knew his own hands.

There was no light up there. There had never been a light up there.

He looked back through the meetinghouse window. Ulyana stood at the hearth with the boy asleep against her shoulder, both of them lit gold, and even from outside Halvard could see that she was staring at the eastern slope too — had been staring, he realized, for some time.

He thought about the gray men and their polite smiles. He thought about a lie told at a ford, and interest accruing on it. He thought about thirty years of a woman keeping the valley's dead, and wondered, with a tired ache, how long she had also been keeping its living — and from what.

The green light on the mountain pulsed once, patient as a heartbeat.

Halvard pulled his collar up and went to wake his deputy. Whatever came next, the valley would need every pair of hands — and he suspected, in the marrow-deep way a man suspects these things, that the hands it would need most belonged to a bone-carver with a past, and that the past had finally finished walking toward them.
