## 14. Betrayal in Bone

Marguerite had learned the shape of Ulyana's days the way he'd once learned the paths of the Garden: by walking them until his feet knew them without asking. She rose before light. She fed the stove first, then the kettle, then herself, in that order, as if warmth were a debt she paid to everything before paying it to her own body. Then she went into the workshop and closed the door, and for an hour or two there was only the small dry sound of her knife against bone.

He was not supposed to go in there alone.

She had never said so in words. It was one of those rules adults made out of silence instead of speech, the kind Marguerite had spent a whole life — a short one, but it felt long now — learning to read. The workshop was hers. The charms hung from the rafters in rows like sleeping birds. The dead of the valley lived in them, murmuring their small grievances and old loves, and Marguerite could hear them even through the door, a sound like wind through wheat that had stopped growing centuries ago.

But this morning Ulyana had gone down to the village with a basket of carved pieces for Halvard's mother's grave-marker, and she had left the stove banked and the bread rising under cloth, and she had said, at the door, *stay inside where it's warm*, which in the grammar of grown people meant *do not touch anything*.

Marguerite lasted until the bread had risen twice and fallen once.

It wasn't curiosity, exactly. Or it was, but not the idle kind. Since midwinter had come on — since the night the dead grew loud and the cold found its way under every door in the valley like water finding a crack — he had been dreaming of the Garden again. Not the green Garden of his earliest memories but the white one, the hushed one, the queen walking the frozen alleys with her hands folded and her face turned away from him. In the dreams she always stopped at the edge of hearing and said something he couldn't catch, and he woke with the word sitting on his tongue like a coin he couldn't spend.

The dead in the valley had begun saying it too. He'd heard it through the charms at night, when the house creaked and the fire settled: a name, or part of one, spoken over and over in the old tongue. *Vasquez. Vasquez.* He hadn't told Ulyana. He didn't know why, except that some words feel like doors you shouldn't open until you're sure what's behind them.

The workshop door wasn't locked. That was the first strange thing. Ulyana locked nothing else — the valley had no thieves worth locking against — but she had always latched the workshop, and today the latch stood open, and later he would understand that the latch had been broken since the night the wards failed, since the dead had pressed against the walls of their little bone houses hard enough to crack one.

He stepped inside.

The smell hit him first: bone dust and linseed oil and something older underneath, something that made the back of his teeth ache, the way the air in the deep cellars of the Garden used to smell. Green things buried. Growing things gone to sleep. His chest tightened around the seed the way it always did when he came near a reminder of home, a warm ache like a second heartbeat.

Charms hung everywhere, hundreds of them, each one tagged with a scrap of paper in Ulyana's cramped hand. *Marta Olsen, d. 41 years past, wants her sister forgiven.* *Old Tam, d. 60 years past, wants to know if the apple trees still stand.* *Halvard's father, d. 12 years past, wants his son to stop apologizing.* Marguerite read them all, slowly, because reading them felt like being trusted with something, even if the trust was accidental.

And then, on the high shelf above the workbench, behind a jar of knives, he saw a charm that had no tag.

It was smaller than the others. Darker bone — not valley bone, not the patient gray of cattle ribs or the ivory of shed antler. This was white, white as the snow outside, white as the Garden's winter, and it hummed. Not loudly. Not the way the other charms murmured. This one hummed the way a struck bell hums after everyone has stopped listening, a note held so long it had become a kind of silence.

He should have put it back. He knew that even then, standing on the stool with his fingers closing around it. Some part of him, the part that had crossed a ridge no one crosses, the part that carried a country in his chest, already knew what it was. Knew it the way you know a face in a crowd before you know the name.

He took it down.

The bone was warm. That was the second strange thing, because nothing in the workshop was warm except near the stove wall, and this shelf was far from it. The carving was different too — not the careful valley script Ulyana used for the dead here, all loops and patience, but the old letters, the letters of the Garden, cut deep and fast, the way you carve when your hands are shaking.

Marguerite could read the old letters. He was the last person alive who could, maybe, except that the dead queen still spoke the language in his dreams, so perhaps not the last after all.

He read it aloud, because the old tongue is meant to be spoken, because words in that language are doors and doors are meant to be opened:

*"I, Ulyana Vasquez, carver of the Queen's own court, confess—"*

The workshop went quiet.

Not silent — quiet, the way a room goes quiet when someone stops breathing. Every charm on every rafter stopped murmuring at once, hundreds of the valley's dead holding their breath together, and in that held breath Marguerite heard the rest. Not the words on the bone. The words under it. The confession-charm had been carved to speak only to its maker, sealed with her blood and her name, but the seal was cracked now — the wards failing, the dead loud, everything in the valley coming loose at its edges — and what was under it poured out like water from a split jug.

He heard a garden burning at its edges. He heard men with iron and salt asking questions in a language that had no please in it. He heard a young woman — younger than Ulyana was now, younger than anyone he could imagine Ulyana having been — weeping while she talked, and talking while she wept, giving up a road, a river, a hidden gate, the names of the Queen's gardeners, the location of the last green country in the world, all of it spilling out of her because they had told her they would let her live.

They had lied. He heard that too. Heard the lie in the flat voices, heard the moment she understood it, heard her run — run through the purge-fire, run over the mountains, run until she fell through the pass into a valley where magic could not follow, and there she had built herself a silence so complete that even she believed it.

*The valley's silence was arranged,* the dead queen had said, or would say, or had been trying to say all along in his dreams. *By her.*

Marguerite stood on the stool with the charm in his fist and understood, all at once and completely, the way children understand things — whole, without mercy, without the softening layers adults wrap around truth to make it survivable.

Ulyana had betrayed the Garden.

Ulyana, who fed the stove before herself. Who had taken him in from the snow without asking what he cost. Who sat up nights carving comfort for the dead of a valley that had exiled her, who taught him the old tongue word by word with tears standing in her eyes that she pretended were from the fire. Whose voice, when she spoke the language of home, made the wards flicker — not because the words were dangerous, but because she had helped build the walls that kept the language out, and the walls remembered her, and flinched.

Every kindness rearranged itself in front of him. That was the worst of it. Not that she had done it — though that was bad enough, that was a wound he would carry longer than he knew — but that the kindness stayed true even after he knew. She had betrayed a whole country and then she had saved him anyway, and both things were real, and he was nine years old, or whatever passed for nine in a boy grown from a garden, and no one had ever taught him how to hold two truths that hated each other.

The charm was still speaking. He hadn't noticed; his own blood was too loud. But it was speaking now, the last of it, the oldest layer, carved deepest, and the voice was not Ulyana's.

It was the queen's.

*"...and I forgive her,"* the dead queen said, in the voice of someone speaking to a room that has already emptied, *"because someone must, and I am dead, and forgiveness is the last duty of the dead. But I do not forget. Carve that somewhere, Ulyana. Carve it in yourself. One day the Garden will need what you took from it, and you will have to choose whether you are the woman who sold it or the woman who gives it back."*

The humming stopped.

The other charms started up again all at once, a rustle like wind returning to wheat, and Marguerite climbed down off the stool with his legs shaking. He put the charm back on the shelf. He straightened the jar of knives in front of it, carefully, exactly as it had been, the way you tidy a room you've robbed.

Then he did the thing he was best at, the thing that had brought him across the ridge in the first place.

He ran.

* * *

Outside, the snow had thickened. Midwinter proper, the villagers called it, the weeks when the sun barely cleared the ridge and the cold got into the wood of the houses and stayed. Marguerite pulled his coat — Ulyana's coat, cut down for him, the sleeves still too long — tight around himself and went out the back, past the woodpile, past the lean-to where the sledges slept under canvas, and up toward the ridge path before he let himself think about any of it.

Thinking was the enemy. If he thought, he would remember the way she'd said *your words make the valley flicker, be careful, little flower* — *little flower*, in the old tongue, the name the gardeners gave to seedlings they meant to keep — and he would remember that she had known what he was from the first knock, had probably known before he did, and he would have to ask himself whether the warmth in her voice had ever been anything but guilt wearing kindness like a coat.

The snow took him at the tree line. It came sideways off the ridge, fine and hard as salt, erasing the path within moments of his feet leaving it. Good, some cold practical corner of him thought. Let it erase me. Let the valley forget me the way it forgot everything else.

But his chest wouldn't let him. The seed burned hotter with every step upward, a coal lodged behind his sternum, and it pulled — not toward the village, not toward warmth, but up, toward the ridge, toward the place where the wards had failed, toward the faint green thread of a scent that shouldn't exist in midwinter. Somewhere beyond the ridge, under the ice, under the years, a door was waiting. He'd told Ulyana about it in the firelit evenings, watching her face go carefully blank. A door that opens only for a confession the Garden accepts.

He hadn't known then what a confession cost. Now he held one in his memory, whole, and it weighed more than the coat, more than the snow, more than the small tired body carrying it uphill.

*Little flower.* He stumbled on a root and caught himself against a pine, bark biting through his mittens. Had she called the gardeners' seedlings that, back when she was theirs? Had she said it to him the way you say a word you've kept in your mouth for forty years, tasting whether it still fits? Was any of it hers, or was all of it penance, and did it matter, and why did it matter so much?

Because — and here was the thing, the true thing, the thing that made him stop in the white roaring middle of the storm and press his back against the tree and cry the way children cry, ugly and total, breath torn out of him in rags —

Because she had been kind to him *anyway*. Knowing what she'd done. Knowing what he was. Holding both truths every single day, feeding the stove before herself, carving names for the dead, teaching him the words of the country she had sold, and never once, not once, asking him to forgive her.

Adults lied. That was the lesson, the whole lesson, the one the storm was writing for him in letters of ice. Adults lied to survive, and then they survived so long the lie became a life, and then a child knocked on the door of the lie and the child got fed and warmed and loved, and none of it was false, and none of it was clean, and there was no one to ask which it was.

He wiped his face with the back of a mitten and the mitten came away wet and freezing and he laughed, a horrible small sound, because the queen had said it too, hadn't she, in the charm's deepest layer. *One day the Garden will need what you took from it.* The Garden needed him. The seed needed a door. And the only person in the world who might know where the door was — the only person who had ever stood on both sides of it — was the woman he had just run from.

The wind shifted. Below him, faint through the roar, he heard dogs — no, not dogs, voices, human voices calling a name into the white. Calling his name. Calling another name after it, over and over, with a rawness that no lie could fake:

*"Marguerite! MARGUERITE!"*

Ulyana's voice, coming up the mountain through the storm, cracking on his name, and the seed in his chest pulled toward it like a plant toward sun, treacherous and green and alive, and Marguerite stood at the tree line with the snow filling his footprints behind him and did not answer.

Above him, further up the ridge, where no living voice should be, something else called back.
