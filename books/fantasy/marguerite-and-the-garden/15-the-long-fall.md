## 15. The Long Fall

The storm did not arrive. It *descended* — as if the sky had been holding the valley's winter in its cupped hands for a hundred years and had finally, deliberately, let go.

Ulyana stood in her doorway and watched the world erase itself. The ridge was gone. The pines were gone. The path to Halvard's house was a white rumor. Somewhere out in that blindness was a boy of perhaps ten years, wearing a coat two sizes too large, running from the woman who had lied to him.

"Marguerite!" she shouted, and the wind took the name and shredded it.

She went back inside only long enough to take down her kit: the awl, the waxed thread, the bundle of blank bone slips she carried against days when the dead needed speaking to urgently. She wrapped her scarf twice around her face, pulled on her snowshoes, and paused at the workshop door.

The charms hanging from the rafters were all swinging. Every one of them. There was no draft that could do that.

"I know," she told them. "I know what I did."

And then she stepped into the white.

* * *

For the first hour she followed logic, because logic was all she had. The boy knew nothing of the valley. He had arrived at her door in the first snow with no map and no sense of direction, which meant he could not have chosen a route now — he would run the way frightened things run: downhill, toward open ground, away from the place where he'd been hurt.

That took him toward the ford.

Her stomach clenched. The ford meant the road. The road meant the Wardens, who had come asking after him in their grey coats with their patient voices, and who had been camped at the far crossing since before midwinter. If Marguerite ran blind into them—

She walked faster. Snowshoes hissing. Breath tearing.

The storm made the world small. Ten steps of visibility, then eight, then five, until she was moving through a whiteness so total it felt less like weather than like being unmade. She tied a cord from her belt to a sapling at the tree line and paid it out behind her, because even a penitent can be lost, and lost was one thing she could not afford to be. Not today. Today she had to be findable — for him.

"Marguerite!"

Nothing. The wind answered with a sound like a door closing.

It was somewhere in that second hour that the voices began.

* * *

She had silenced a great many people in her long exile. That was the work, after all — that was the *gift*, if you wanted to use the word the villagers used. A dying grandmother wanted to leave a message for a son she'd quarreled with forty years ago; Ulyana carved the charm, caught the last words, delivered them clean. A drowned drover's wife wanted to tell someone where the body lay; Ulyana carved, listened, spoke. For thirty years she had been the valley's ear pressed to the ground where its dead lay whispering, and she had told herself it was service. It was. It was also, she understood now, walking through the storm with ice forming in her eyelashes, a kind of hoarding. She kept them. Their words lived in bone in her workshop, in her keeping, in her control — and control had always been the shape her cowardice took.

Now they came back to her, all of them, in the storm.

*Mira of the low fields*, who had died in childbirth and whose charm still hung third from the left, saying over and over, *tell her I named her after the river, tell her I named her after the river.* Ulyana had delivered that message once, twenty years ago, and Mira had never stopped saying it since. Ulyana had heard it every time she entered the workshop. She had learned not to hear it. That was the trick she'd mastered, the true art of her exile: hearing without listening, listening without answering.

*tell her I named her after the river*

"I delivered it," Ulyana said aloud, to no one, to the wind. "I said the words."

But she hadn't stayed. She hadn't sat with the daughter while she wept. She had handed over the message like a tradesman handing over goods and gone home to her quiet house and her ordered bones, and she had called that mercy.

The storm thickened. The voices multiplied.

*Old Tam*, the shepherd, dead nine winters: *the dog knows where the second flock went, tell Halvard the dog knows—*

*The child from the fever year*, too young to have more than a handful of words: *mama mama mama* — and Ulyana had carved that charm with steady hands because steady hands were how you survived, and had never once let herself think about the mother who came every spring to touch the little bone slip and ask, gently, hopefully, *is she still there?*

"She is still there," Ulyana whispered into the storm, and hated herself for the twenty years it had taken to say it.

This was what the Wardens had offered her, she thought, slogging up the rise toward the ford road. Absolution. Erasure. *Give us the boy and your past is wiped clean.* And she had stood there in the cold with the offer in her ears and felt — God forgive her, she had felt the pull of it, the sweet gravity of a life without weight. No confession. No carving of her own guilt into her own bone. Just silence, the good kind, the kind she had built this whole valley out of.

The boy had found the confession-charm. Of course he had. She'd hidden it badly because some part of her had always wanted it found — the way a wound wants air. *I, Ulyana Winterbourne, called Vess of the Garden, did sell the queen's road to the purge-men for the price of my own life.* He had read it in a language older than kingdoms, a language she understood and had spent thirty years refusing to speak above a whisper.

And he had run.

Of course he had run.

* * *

She found the first sign near the ford road: a small dark shape half-buried at the base of a drift. Her heart lurched — then broke differently. It was his mitten. Wool, grey, too big, the kind Halvard's sister had knitted for the reeve himself and Halvard had quietly passed along. It had been pulled off, not dropped. Pulled off by a hand fumbling in panic, or thrown down in fury, or simply lost by fingers too frozen to feel.

She closed her fist around it. The wool was stiff with cold but not bloodied.

"He's alive," she said. "He's alive, he's alive—" and made it a rhythm, made it a rope, hauled herself along it.

Behind her, distantly, something cracked. Not thunder. Not ice. A sound like a branch breaking under deliberate weight, and then another, spaced like footsteps. She didn't turn. Turning cost time, and time was a country the boy was fleeing through faster than she could follow.

The Wardens were in the storm with her. She had known it since the treeline. They moved like that — unhurried, inevitable, the sound of them arriving rather than approaching. Thirty years ago they had moved the same way through the Garden's orchards, and she had heard that sound and hidden in a root cellar and lived, and living had been the whole of her ambition, and everything since had been the interest accruing on that single cowardly loan.

A voice came through the white, not shouting — the Wardens never shouted — pitched to carry the way a blade carries light.

"Mistress Winterbourne. The boy is dying out here. You must know that better than anyone. You of all people know what cold does to small warm things."

She kept walking.

"You could end your searching," the voice said, closer now, or maybe just louder, the storm playing tricks. "You could go home to your quiet house and your quiet bones. We will find him. We always find them. The only question is whether he is found frozen or found whole, and whether you are standing beside him when we arrive. One of those ways, you're a foolish old woman who lost a stray. The other—" A pause, courteous, terrible. "The other is harboring. You remember what we do about harboring."

"I remember," Ulyana said, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "I remember everything. That's my curse and your mistake. You think forgetting is a mercy. You offered me forgetting like it was a gift." She turned, finally, into the blinding white, toward the sound. "I sold a country once because I couldn't bear to remember what was coming. Never again. Do you hear me? Never again."

The storm swallowed whatever the Warden might have answered. When she turned back to the trail, the mitten-print direction, the downhill drag of small snowshoes, she was crying, and the tears froze on her cheeks, and she let them. Let them freeze. Let them mark her. Penance, she was learning, did not begin with the grand gesture at the door beneath the ice. It began here — in motion, in the putting of one foot in front of another toward the thing you feared most, which was not death but reckoning.

* * *

The voices did not stop. She stopped fighting them.

Instead, somewhere in the fourth hour, hollowed out by cold and effort, she began to answer them. Aloud. Into the storm. Like a woman paying debts on a long walk to the gallows.

"Mira — your daughter married the cooper's son. She has two children. She keeps your river-name. I should have told you that years ago instead of once and done. I'm telling you now."

"Tam — Halvard found the second flock. The dog led him straight to it. He never knew how he knew. Now he does."

To the fever-child, the hardest one, she could only say, "Your mother is well. She plants marigolds. She says they were your favorite, though you were too little to have favorites. She decided for you. Mothers do that." Her voice cracked on the last word and she pushed on. "I'm sorry I never let you finish. Whatever you were trying to say, all those years, I'm sorry I kept you at *mama*. You were more than that. Everyone is more than their last word."

The storm seemed — she would never be sure, afterward, whether it was real or only the mind's mercy in extremity — to ease around her, fractionally, the way a room eases when an argument finally gets spoken. The charms in her workshop, miles behind her, would be hanging still. She was certain of it the way she was certain of her own heartbeat. The dead did not want silence. They had never wanted silence. They had wanted *her*, present, answering, accountable — and she had given them craft instead of company for thirty years.

The boy had known her three weeks and had already demanded more truth than she'd given the valley in three decades.

"Marguerite!" She screamed it now, raw, uncaring who heard, Wardens or wind or the dead themselves. "MARGUERITE! I LIED TO YOU AND I'M SORRY AND YOU CAN HATE ME BUT NOT OUT HERE! NOT OUT HERE!"

The storm gave back nothing.

Then — a flicker. At the edge of vision, in the white-on-white, a darkness that was not a tree. Small. Low. Moving.

She lunged. Lost it. Stumbled, fell, got up, fell again, snowshoes tangling, and crawled the last few yards on bare knees to the place where the dark shape had been.

A drift. A shadowed fold of drift, wind-carved, nothing more.

But beside it, half-filled with snow already, small footprints. Fresh. Heading not downhill toward the ford at all — she'd read him wrong, read him like a frightened animal when he had never once been merely an animal — but *up*. Toward the ridge. Toward the high place where the wards had stood, where magic could not follow, where a boy from a falling country might believe no one could reach him.

Toward the place where, she now understood with a clarity that felt like a blade between the ribs, the old door had once been. He wasn't running *from* her. He was running *home* — to a threshold that no longer existed, guided by a seed in his chest that was the last living piece of a dying land, and the winter itself was rising to meet him, and the Wardens were coming up the other slope, and between all of it stood one old woman with a mitten in her fist and thirty years of unanswered voices finally, finally at her back like an army.

Ulyana got her feet under her. Turned uphill. Began to climb.

Halfway through the next gust, through the roar of the storm, she heard — faint, impossible, unmistakable — a second set of snowshoes falling in behind her, and a man's voice, breathless, furious, terrified:

"Ulyana! Wait — you don't have to do this alone—"
