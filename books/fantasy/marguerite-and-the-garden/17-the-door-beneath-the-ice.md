## 17. The Door Beneath the Ice

They walked for two days following a dead woman's voice.

Ulyana had expected it to be like her charms — the carved bones that caught the last warmth of speech and held it, thin as breath on glass. But the queen's voice was not an echo. It was weather. It came from ahead of them and behind them and once, horribly, from inside Marguerite's chest, where the seed sat like a second heart. It did not repeat itself. It waited until they made a mistake, and then it said their names.

*Left,* it said, when Ulyana chose the wrong fork in the frozen riverbed. *You always went left. You never learned.*

"You could just say where," Ulyana muttered.

The voice did not answer. It never answered questions. That was the thing about the dead — she had known this for forty years, had built a whole quiet life on knowing it — they were not oracles. They were witnesses. They told you what they had seen, and only what they had seen, and the queen had seen Ulyana Winterbourne walk away from a burning country with her hands empty and her life intact, and apparently she intended to keep seeing it all the way to the door.

Marguerite walked beside her with his hood up and his chin down, the way he'd walked since the workshop, since the charm he'd found and read and understood in a language no living child should speak. He hadn't accused her. That was worse than accusation. He had simply looked at her the way you look at a house you thought was solid and discovered was built on hollow ground, and then he'd put on his coat and said, *We should go before the Wardens come back,* as if betrayal were a scheduling problem.

"Your feet," Ulyana said now, because silence was a thing she could not afford. Silence let the voice in. "Show me."

He stopped and pulled off one boot without complaint, which was its own kind of terrible — a boy so used to suffering that he treated it as procedure. His toes were white at the tips. She knelt in the snow, unwound her scarf, and worked the wool around his foot with fingers gone clumsy from cold.

"I can walk," he said.

"You can walk on those for another hour and then lose two of them." She tied the scarf off. "I've buried men who lost toes to less. They were stupid men. You're not stupid."

"No," Marguerite agreed quietly. "I'm the opposite of stupid. I'm the last of something."

She looked up at him. Snow gathered on his lashes; beneath the hood his face was sharp and young and older than any face had a right to be. Eleven years old, maybe twelve, carrying a country in his ribs.

"Yes," she said. "You are."

*Both of you are the last of something,* said the queen's voice, from everywhere. *That is why you are still walking.*

---

The valley ended at noon on the second day, though no map would have shown it ending.

One moment there were ridgelines, the familiar gray teeth of the mountains Ulyana had lived under for four decades, the walls that kept magic out and her in. The next moment the ridge was simply — wrong. The rock had a grain to it that ran the wrong direction, like wood cut against itself, and the snow lay on it in shapes that repeated: the same drift, the same curl of wind-carved ice, again and again down the slope, as if the mountain were a sentence someone had written and then, lacking more words, copied out over and over.

"The wards," Marguerite whispered. He had stopped walking. "This is where they end. This is where *she* ended them."

Ulyana's stomach dropped through the ice of forty years.

"I don't know what you mean," she said, and heard how flat her voice came out, how practiced.

The boy turned and looked at her. Not angry. Just looking, the way the dead look, taking witness.

"She stood here," he said. "The queen. Before the winter. She came out of the Garden to the edge of your valley and she unmade the door's far side, so that nothing could cross — not magic, not hunters, not the Garden's own people fleeing. She sealed it from outside because—" He stopped. His throat worked. "Because she knew someone inside had already sold the way in. She just didn't know who."

The wind moved. The repeating snowdrifts glittered, identical, identical, identical.

"And now you know," Ulyana said.

"Now I know," Marguerite said. "And we're both still here, and the seed is still warm, and I think—" He pressed a mittened hand flat against his own chest, and for a moment, through the layers of wool, Ulyana saw light. Faint, green-gold, pulsing slow as a sleeping pulse. "I think she sealed it so that whoever came back would have to come back honestly. No doors left. Only the one underneath."

*Underneath,* the queen agreed, and her voice was almost gentle, which frightened Ulyana more than cruelty ever had.

They crossed the ridge at the place where the repetition broke — a single seam in the rock, narrow as a coffin lid stood on end, that neither of them found so much as arrived at, the way you arrive at the bottom of a staircase in the dark. On the other side the world changed key.

---

Ulyana had spent her whole adult life in a land where magic could not follow, and she had told herself for forty years that this meant the valley was empty of it, a swept room, a silence. She had been wrong the way a deaf woman is wrong about a room being quiet. Crossing the seam was like having cotton pulled out of her ears after a lifetime. The world *roared*. Not loudly — deeply. Under everything. The snow had a sound. The dark had a sound. Her own blood had a sound, and it was singing in a language she had sworn never to speak again, and every word she'd taught herself to forget in Marguerite's firelit lessons came flooding home like water finding its old channel.

Beside her, Marguerite made a small broken noise.

"It's louder," he said. "It's so much louder out here. In the valley it was like hearing through a wall. I thought that was normal. I thought *I* was broken—"

"You were never broken." The words came out of her before she could weigh them, fierce and useless. "Listen to me. Whatever happens past this point, whatever you learn about me — you were never the broken thing. Do you understand?"

He stared at her. Snow filled the pause between them.

"The queen is waiting," he said finally, and turned downhill, toward the sound.

---

The winter began an hour later, and it was not weather.

Ulyana had known cold her whole life. Valley cold, cellar cold, the cold of rivers in thaw and graves in January. This was none of these. This was cold with *intention*. It came up out of the ground ahead of them in long slow exhalations, and each exhalation carried a shape: frost ferns that bloomed across stone in patterns too regular to be chance, spirals, script, the looping characters of the old tongue written in ice across a mile of hillside. As if the winter were practicing its signature. As if it wanted to be recognized.

Marguerite stopped at the top of the first frozen fall and stared.

"It knows we're coming," he said.

"Of course it does." Ulyana came up beside him. Below them the land fell away into a basin, and the basin was full of ice — not a lake frozen over but ice grown upward, terraced, a white ziggurat rising out of the earth's skin, and at its heart, deep down where the terraces converged, something dark. A shape. A rectangle. "It's been waiting a hundred years. What else has it had to do?"

"That's not what I mean." The boy's voice had gone thin. "In the Garden, before, the winter wasn't — it didn't *think*. It was just cold. Cold that wouldn't stop. But this—" He hugged himself, and the light in his chest flickered faster, a heartbeat climbing. "This is patient. Can't you feel it? It's not trying to stop us. It's *letting* us come. Like a trap that isn't sprung yet. Like an animal lying still because it already knows where you'll step."

Ulyana felt it then, fully, and wished she didn't. The cold around them was not attacking. It was *attentive*. Every flake that touched her seemed to pause before landing, considering. Somewhere in the terraces below, the dark rectangle sat in its cradle of ice like a pupil in an eye, and she had the absolute certainty — bone-deep, charm-deep, the certainty she usually only got from the dead — that if she turned and ran now, the winter would let her. Would watch her go. Would save its strength.

For the boy, it would not be so generous.

"We keep going," she said.

"I know." Marguerite wiped his nose with the back of his mitten. "I'm not scared of dying. I'm the last seed; if I die, it's finished either way. I'm scared of—" He struggled. "Of it being *glad*. When I get there. Of feeding it."

Ulyana crouched to his height. Up close, his eyes had flecks of green she'd never noticed in the valley's flat light, green like new leaves, green like a country that no longer existed.

"Then we arrive angry," she said. "Angry things are hard to swallow. Come on."

---

They descended into the terraces as the light failed, and the dead came with them.

Not the queen — she stayed above, her voice thinning with altitude like air — but others. Ulyana felt them the moment they passed the third terrace: presences in the ice, faces under the surface, hundreds of them, thousands, arranged in the frost-ferns and the spiral script. People who had run from the purge and gotten this far. People who had frozen mid-stride a century ago and been kept. Their eyes were open. None of them spoke. They watched the two small figures pick their way down through the galleries of ice, and their watching had weight, like hands resting lightly on her shoulders, like a congregation standing for a bride.

Or for a defendant.

"They followed me," Marguerite said softly. He had stopped at a terrace edge where a family stood frozen beneath six inches of clear ice — a man, a woman, a child between them, all three holding hands, all three facing outward, toward the valley, toward escape. "When I left. The ones who could still move. They put me in the seed-vault and they sang the door shut behind me and I thought — I thought they'd all died safe. Inside. Warm." His voice cracked. "Some of them got this far."

"Some of them got further," Ulyana said. "Come on."

"How do you know?"

"Because I'm the one who told the Wardens which roads they'd take."

She said it and immediately wished the ice would take her too, right there, fold her into a gallery with the others and let her be a face under a surface instead of a mouth above one. Marguerite went very still. Above them the frost-script glimmered.

"You say things like that," he said at last, "like you're trying to make me hate you before the door. So I won't grieve you after."

"Don't be clever with me, boy."

"I'm the last of a country. Cleverness is all I've got." He took her hand — small fingers, cold even through wool, gripping hard. "Keep walking. Say whatever you need to say. I'll hate you and hold your hand at the same time. I'm told that's what family is."

Ulyana Winterbourne, who had not wept in forty years, whose tears had frozen somewhere in the making of her along with everything else soft, blinked hard twice and kept walking.

---

The door was exactly as the queen's voice had promised and nothing like anything Ulyana had imagined.

It sat at the bottom of the basin in a bowl of black ice, and it was not grand. That was the shock of it. After the terraces, the script, the congregations of the frozen dead — after all that architecture of terror — the door was small. Wooden. Oak, silvered with age, iron-banded, the kind of door that belonged on a cottage, on a garden wall, on a place where someone might reasonably expect you to knock. Its handle was worn smooth by hands. Someone had loved this door once. Someone had hung a wreath on it, in some spring a hundred years dead; the ghost of the wreath was still there, a ring of paler wood where the twigs had rested.

Around it, the winter coiled.

Ulyana saw it clearly now, and understanding arrived like a blade between the ribs. The endless winter of the Garden — the cold that had eaten a country season by season, year by year, the catastrophe she had fled and the Wardens had celebrated — it was not a curse laid on the land. It was an animal, and the door was its den, and it had been lying curled around this small wooden threshold for a hundred years, keeping it, guarding it, *waiting*, because the winter knew what Ulyana was only now understanding:

The door was still alive. And living things could open.

Marguerite walked straight up to it. The cold parted around him — actually parted, she saw it happen, the frost-ferns curling away from his footsteps like ferns from fire — and he laid both mittens flat against the oak.

Nothing.

He pushed. Nothing. He knocked, three small knocks with a frozen fist, and the sound fell dead in the ice bowl, and the winter exhaled around them, long and slow and satisfied, and Ulyana realized with horror that it was laughing.

"It won't open for me," Marguerite said. His voice was very small. "It's supposed to open for me. I'm the seed. I'm supposed to go *home*—"

"It opens for a confession," Ulyana said. "You told me. Chapter and verse. A confession the Garden accepts."

"But mine wouldn't — I haven't done anything, I'm a *child*, I—"

"Not yours."

The word came out of her like a tooth coming out of the jaw, and the whole basin heard it. The frozen dead turned in their ice. The frost-script blazed. The winter went utterly, perfectly still — the stillness of an animal that has waited a hundred years and hears, at last, the exact footstep it was waiting for.

Marguerite turned. In the blue light of the ice his face was stripped of everything, hope and fear alike, down to the bare question underneath.

"Ulyana," he said. "What are you going to do?"

She drew her carving knife from her belt. It was a good knife; she had made it herself, from the femur of a horse and a sliver of Warden steel, and it had spoken the valley's dead for forty years. It had never once been pointed at her own flesh.

"What I should have done a hundred years ago," she said, and rolled up her sleeve.
