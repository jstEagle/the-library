## 19. The Queen's Judgment

The door did not open so much as admit them, the way a wound admits a needle — reluctantly, with a sound like the whole world holding its breath.

Marguerite went first because Ulyana's hand was shaking too badly to lead, and because he was, in the end, the seed-bearer, and the threshold knew him. The ice beneath his boots went soft as moss. The dark ahead of them was not empty; it was *waiting*, and it smelled of loam and cut stems and something older than either — the smell of a country remembering itself.

"Stay behind me," Ulyana whispered.

"You can barely stand," he said. Her arm was bound in strips torn from her own coat, and beneath the binding her bone sang a note only he could hear, high and clean and terrible: the confession carved into living marrow, still speaking. He had heard it through the storm for three days. He would hear it for the rest of his life, he suspected, the way you hear a bell after it stops ringing.

"I've been standing in my guilt for forty years," she said. "I can stand a little longer."

They walked into the winter.

It was not the winter outside. Outside had been wind and white blindness, cold as an animal's hunger. This was quiet. This was *architectural*. Frost climbed the air in columns and arches, and every column was carved — roses, thorns, faces, names — the whole history of the Garden rendered in ice so fine that when Marguerite breathed on it, the breath bloomed green for half a heartbeat before dying again.

He knew this place. Not this corridor specifically, but its grammar. He had been born under ceilings like this, in a palace grown rather than built, where the walls listened politely and the floors forgave your weight. His chest ached where the seed sat, and the ache had changed. All his life it had been a stone in him, a weight. Now it pulled. Now it *leaned forward* like a dog at the end of a rope.

"She's here," he said.

"I know." Ulyana's voice came out flat and small. "I felt her the moment we crossed."

"How?"

"Because I have spent forty years not thinking about her," Ulyana said, "and she has just stopped allowing it."

* * *

They found her at the heart of the frost-palace, in a hall where the ceiling had fallen inward to make a bowl of sky, and the sky was the grey of a held breath. She stood at the center with her back to them, and she was not what Marguerite remembered, and she was exactly what he remembered, both at once.

Memory said: a woman tall as a season, crowned in living branches, laughter in her like birdsong in a hedge.

What stood before him was thin. Translucent at the edges, like paper held too long against a window. Her crown was bare twigs. But when she turned — slowly, the way a tree turns toward light over years — her eyes were the same, and her eyes were the worst thing in the world, because they were kind.

"Little door," she said, in the old tongue, and the word for him was *door*, not *boy*, and he understood why. He had carried the way home inside his ribs since the purge began. "You grew."

"Your Majesty." He started to kneel. Her hand stopped him before his knee touched the ground — her fingers passed through his sleeve like cold water, but he felt them anyway.

"Don't kneel to me. Not today. Today I am not a queen. Today I am a dead woman taking attendance." Her gaze moved past him, and everything in the hall moved with it — the frost, the light, the pressure of the winter itself, all of it turning like a lens focusing. "Ulyana."

Ulyana did not kneel. She could not; her legs were done with obedience. She stood swaying in the doorway of the hall with her ruined arm pressed to her chest, and she looked at the queen the way you look at a grave you dug yourself.

"You're dead," Ulyana said. "I watched the Wardens—"

"You watched the beginning of it. You ran before the end." There was no cruelty in it. That was the horror of it. The queen spoke the way winter speaks — without malice, without mercy, simply stating what is. "Yes. I am dead. I have been dead for a hundred years, and in that hundred years, one thing has kept the Garden from finishing its dying." She raised a twig-crowned head toward the frozen sky. "A door that would not open. A door that required a truth, freely told, and received none. Do you know how many came, over the century? Survivors. Remnants. Children carrying seeds smaller than yours. They stood where you are standing and they offered me *anything*. Gold. Service. Other people's confessions, extorted, secondhand. Lies dressed as penance." Her eyes came back down. "The door stayed shut. The winter stayed. And I stayed, and waited, because I had made one promise to myself at the end, and I am a very patient dead woman."

"What promise?" Marguerite asked.

"That if the Garden died, it would die having heard the truth once." The queen stepped closer to Ulyana. Frost spread from her footfalls in fern-patterns. "Not the truth of the Wardens, who never lied about what they wanted. Not the truth of the cowards who fled honestly. *Your* truth, carver. The one you buried so deep you buried it even from yourself. I have been listening to your valley for forty years. Did you think the silence was yours? Every charm you carved, every voice you gave the dead — I heard all of it. I heard you give the dead their words back while refusing your own. It was the most beautiful thing in the whole grey world you made for yourself. And it was not enough. It was never going to be enough. Because the door does not want your grief." She stopped an arm's length away. "It wants your name."

Marguerite looked at Ulyana and saw a woman deciding whether to fall.

* * *

"I was her gardener's daughter," Ulyana said.

The words came out cracked, unused, like a hinge on a door no one had opened. But they came out. Marguerite stood very still and listened, and the seed in his chest listened too, leaning into each word like warmth.

"The queen took me into service when I was fourteen because I could hear the dead through bone, and the Garden needed such ears in the years before the purge — there were already whispers, already kingdoms mapping our borders out of envy. I carved charms for the honored dead. I sat at the queen's left hand at midwinter feasts. She taught me the old tongue herself. She said I had honest hands." Ulyana laughed, one dry note with nothing in it. "Honest hands. And then the Wardens came over the northern ridge with fire that eats magic, and the outer courts burned in a single night, and she summoned me and gave me a task: carry word to the southern refuge, tell them to scatter the seeds, tell them the queen holds the center."

"And you went," Marguerite said softly.

"I went *halfway*." Ulyana's voice steadied as it went, the way a river steadies over rocks. "The Wardens took me at the ford. Three of them. They knew what I was — a servant of the court, a bone-speaker. They offered me the trade every traitor is offered: your life for the location. And I want you both to understand — I did not do it to save anyone else. I did not tell myself some story about surviving to fight. I was twenty-two years old and I wanted to live, and wanting to live was the whole of my reasoning, entire, complete, shameful." She lifted her chin. "I gave them the valley pass. I gave them the queen's position at the center. I told them which corridors were undefended because I had walked them my whole life. Then I ran south, and the refuge scattered, and the seeds went out into the world in children's keeping, and the Wardens went to the center, and I watched the smoke from a hilltop two days' ride away, and I did not go back. I crossed into the valley where magic cannot follow, and I built a life on top of the memory, the way you build a house on a grave, and I called the house contentment."

The hall was silent. Even the frost seemed to lean in.

"Why confess now?" the queen asked. "You confessed at the door. Why?"

Ulyana looked at her arm. "Because I tried every other door first. Because I spent forty years carving other people's truths into bone and calling it penance, and it wasn't, it was *hiding*, and a boy nearly froze to death in my debt before I understood that hiding was the last wall between the Garden and its spring." Her voice broke and she let it. "You asked for my name. Here it is. Ulyana Winterbourne. Traitor. Coward. Gardener's daughter. I betrayed the Garden, I betrayed you, I betrayed every child carrying a seed, and I have no defense, and I am not asking to be forgiven. I am asking to be *heard*. Freely. With no trade attached. That is the only thing I have left that isn't stolen."

She finished, and the silence afterward was enormous.

Marguerite realized he was crying. He didn't remember starting.

* * *

The queen came forward and took Ulyana's face in her hands — hands that passed through flesh and found bone, and Marguerite saw Ulyana shudder as the dead queen's touch met the confession carved there, and the two truths recognized each other like long-separated sisters.

"Witness," the queen said, and the word rang through the hall, through the frost, through the whole buried country. "That is all I ever asked. Not punishment — punishment is the Wardens' language, and I refuse to speak it. Not absolution — absolution given cheap is just another lie. *Witness.* Someone must know what happened here, all of it, and carry the knowing. That is what a garden is, in the end. A place where things are known and tended anyway." Her thumb traced the binding on Ulyana's arm. "You carved your crime into your own living bone and walked three days through a killing storm to say it aloud. Child, I have been offered crowns. You offered me a wound. The door accepts the wound."

Light came up through the floor.

Not the hard white of the winter — green light, deep and slow, the color of the first minute of spring, rising through the ice like sap through a branch. Where it touched, the frost-carvings woke: the roses reddened, the faces smiled, the names glowed in the old tongue, and Marguerite heard them all speaking at once, a hundred years of the Garden's dead saying *yes, yes, this is true, we witness it too.*

The ache in his chest turned over like soil being turned.

"The seed," the queen said, turning to him, and her kindness was still there, but underneath it now was something vast and tired and hopeful, a country looking at its last chance. "It has been waiting a long time to be planted, little door. And the ground is almost ready. But not quite." She glanced at Ulyana, and something complicated moved across her translucent face — not forgiveness exactly, forgiveness was too simple a word for it, more like a debt acknowledged and deliberately left standing. "There is one thing more. The winter at the heart will not break on its own. It must be *outlived*. Something must be planted in the deepest cold and tended through the last of the frost, and the tending cannot be mine — I am dead, and the dead may witness but not work." Her eyes went to Marguerite, then to Ulyana, then back. "And the planting must happen tonight, at the year's darkest hour, or not at all."

"Then we plant it tonight," Marguerite said.

"Both of you?" The queen raised an eyebrow, and for one flickering moment she was the woman from memory, tall as a season, laughter in her like birdsong. "Together? The traitor and the seed?"

Marguerite looked at Ulyana. Ulyana looked back — grey-faced, bloodless, her arm ruined, forty years of lies finally set down — and she nodded once, and it was the most honest thing he had ever seen a person do.

"Together," he said.

The queen smiled, and where she smiled, the frost melted first.

"Then come," she said, turning toward the dark heart of the winter, where the cold waited like a patient animal. "We have a garden to bury. So that it can grow."
