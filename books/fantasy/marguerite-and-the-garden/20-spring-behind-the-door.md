## 20. Spring Behind the Door

The seed did not look like much.

That was the first thing Marguerite thought, standing in the white hall beyond the door with Ulyana's blood still drying on her sleeve and the queen's voice still ringing in the stone. He had carried it in his chest for so long — a warmth, a weight, a second heartbeat that was not his own — that he had imagined something grand. A jewel. An egg of light. Something worthy of a whole country.

Instead it sat in the queen's cupped hands like a plum pit, brown and ordinary, with one hairline crack down its side where the winter had gotten into it.

"It's dying," he said.

"It is starving," the queen corrected. "There is a difference. Dying things cannot be helped. Starving things only need feeding."

She knelt then — the dead queen of the Garden, kneeling on the ice floor of her own buried threshold — and pressed her palms flat against the frozen ground. Where her hands touched, the ice went cloudy, then soft, then dark. Not melted. *Remembered.* The soil came up through the frost like a memory surfacing through sleep, and Marguerite felt his chest ache in a way that had nothing to do with cold.

"Here," the queen said. "This is where the orchard began. Before there were walls. Before there were doors."

Marguerite knelt beside her. Ulyana stood behind them both, her carved arm held against her body, saying nothing. She had not said much since the door opened. He understood why. Some truths, once spoken, took a long time to settle into their new shape.

"Will you do it?" the queen asked him. "Or shall I?"

"I should," he said. His voice cracked on the second word and he didn't try to fix it. "I carried it. It should be my hands."

He dug with his fingers, because there were no tools and because it seemed right that it should hurt a little. The soil was warm. That was the strangest part — after the endless winter of the Garden, after the snow that never stopped falling on the other side of the ridge, this handful of remembered earth was warm as a sleeping animal. He made a hole the length of his forearm and set the seed in it, crack upward, the way you'd lay a wounded bird in a nest.

Then he didn't know what to say. In the old language there were words for planting — he knew them, Ulyana had made him teach her every one — but they were words for ordinary seeds, for barley and beans and the small green things people grew because they must. There were no words for planting the last living piece of a country.

So he spoke plainly, in the valley tongue, the language of the woman behind him.

"Grow," he said. "Please. I walked very far, and it was very cold, and I would like it to have been for something."

For a long moment nothing happened. The hall was silent except for the sound of the ice settling somewhere far above them, deep under the ridge, under the valley, under everything. Marguerite thought: *it didn't work, the crack was too deep, the winter got all the way in,* and he was already composing the apology he would carry back up through the door, back to the workshop that smelled of tallow and bone dust—

The ground shivered.

Not an earthquake. Nothing so violent. It was more like the feeling of a blanket being pulled up over your shoulders by someone who loves you. The dark soil around the seed darkened further, softened, spread — a handspan, then an arm's length, then wider, eating the frost in slow circles. And out of the crack in the seed's brown shell came a single root, pale as bone, that went down instead of up, and Marguerite felt it go *through* him, or he through it, some thread connecting chest to earth, and suddenly he could feel what the seed felt:

Hunger. Enormous, patient, bottomless hunger.

And then, faint as a candle seen through fog — warmth coming back.

"Oh," he breathed. "Oh, it's drinking."

"The land remembers how to feed it," the queen said softly. "It only needed something worth feeding." She rose, and where she had knelt, grass came up through the soil — real grass, green at the tips, each blade trembling like a thing newly born. "Now we learn whether a century of winter can be undone by one small act of faith. Watch the edges, child. That is where spring begins. Always at the edges."

* * *

They watched the edges.

They had time to watch, because the door did not close at once. The queen explained it while they waited, sitting on the first bench any of them had seen in a hundred years — grown, not built, its wood still soft and smelling of sap — while the circle of spring crept outward through the buried hall at the speed of walking.

"A door is a promise," she said. "This one was made to open for truth freely given, and it will stay open while the giving lasts. When the last true thing has been said inside it, it will seal. Forever. No one passes after that — not out, not in. So if there is anything left unsaid between you two—" and here her ancient eyes moved from Marguerite to Ulyana and back again, gentle and terrible at once, "—now is the hour for it."

Marguerite looked at Ulyana.

She sat apart from them, on the icy edge of the growing spring, her boots in the frost and her carved arm cradled like a sleeping cat. She had washed the blood off in a trickle of meltwater without complaint, without flinching, though he had seen her jaw go white. The bones of her forearm showed through the split skin where she had carved her confession, and the marks were already scabbing over in patterns that looked, disturbingly, like script.

"You're not coming," he said. It wasn't a question. He had known since the door opened. Maybe since the workshop. Maybe since the first night, when she'd wrapped him in blankets and fed him soup and never once asked him to stop speaking the old tongue, even when every word made the wards flicker.

"No," Ulyana said.

"You could come. The door doesn't care who—"

"The door cares about truth, and mine is spent." She smiled, and it was the crooked half-smile he knew, the one that meant she was keeping most of the feeling underneath. "Besides. Someone has to keep the valley. Someone has to listen to the dead on both sides of that ridge now, and I'm the only carver either country has left."

"That's not the real reason."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't."

He waited. He had learned that from her — that waiting was a kind of speech, sometimes the loudest kind.

Ulyana turned her carved arm over in her lap and studied it the way she studied bones before cutting them, reading what was written there.

"When I was young," she said, "I thought exile was the worst thing that could happen to a person. Then I learned worse things. But exile taught me one good lesson, and I'll give it to you free, since you're going home to a country full of people who'll want to punish me forever." She looked up. "You can't carve a future out of someone else's past. Whatever they say about me in the Garden — and they'll say plenty, the queen will see to it that it's all true — don't build your life on top of it. Build on the seed. Build forward."

"Everyone I loved in the Garden died in the winter," Marguerite said. "Except you, and you're staying."

The words came out harder than he meant them, and he saw them land, saw her flinch the small flinch she probably thought he couldn't see.

"Yes," she said quietly. "That's the cost. I know what it costs. I've been paying interest on cheaper versions of it for forty years." She reached out — the uncarved hand — and touched his hair, once, briefly, the way she'd done the night he arrived half-frozen at her door. "But you won't be alone. Spring is coming back to your country, Marguerite. Children will be born who've never heard of winter. You'll be the boy who carried the seed. They'll put your name in songs, and the songs will get it wrong, and you'll spend fifty years correcting them."

Despite everything, he laughed. It came out wet.

"And you?" he managed. "What happens to you?"

"I go back up the hill. I bury Halvard properly, under the ridge, where the wards used to stand. I finish teaching myself your language — you left me halfway through the grammar, you know, and I hate leaving things unfinished." Her voice held steady until almost the end. "And I keep carving. Every charm I make from now on, I make honestly. No more silencing. If the dead want to speak, they speak. Even the ones who'd curse my name."

"Especially those," the queen said from the bench, and there was something in her voice that might have been forgiveness, or might have been the memory of it, worn smooth by a century of waiting.

* * *

The spring reached the walls by late afternoon, though *afternoon* was a guess; there was no sky down here, only the vaulted dark and the pale glow rising off the new grass. But Marguerite's body kept valley-time still, and his stomach told him it was evening, and somehow that was comforting. Time still worked. The world still turned.

The change at the edges was faster now. Frost retreated like a tide going out, and where it went, things woke. Moss first, silver-green along the base of the walls. Then the roots of the great trees that had stood here before the burial — dead trunks, he'd thought, but no: buds. Swelling along every black branch, tight as held breath. Somewhere out in the spreading green, water began to move, a sound like whispering, like a country clearing its throat after a hundred years of silence.

And the dead were waking too.

Marguerite heard them before he understood what he was hearing — voices threading up through the soil, thin and wondering, the way voices sound when someone has just been shaken awake. Not the hungry, cold voices of the Garden's long winter. These were confused, then astonished, then — gradually, in ones and twos — joyful.

"They can feel it," he whispered. "The ones who stayed. The ones who froze."

"They can feel it," the queen confirmed. Her own voice had changed since the seed went into the ground. Less echo in it. More woman. "A few of them will return, in time. The land is generous. Most will simply rest, which is all they ever wanted." She looked at Marguerite with eyes that were beginning, very slowly, to lose their frost. "Including me, eventually. Do not grieve that. A queen who cannot die cannot hand anything on."

"Will I see you again? After?"

"After you pass through? No." She said it kindly, the way Ulyana said hard things. "But you will hear me. In the orchards. In the wind through the hedges, if the hedges grow back the way I planted them, and they will, because I was always very stubborn about hedges." For a moment the ancient majesty dropped away entirely and she was just a tired old gardener looking at her work coming back. "Mind the north wall when you replant. Everything wants to lean toward the sun there, and everything falls over."

* * *

The door stood at the far end of the hall, exactly where they'd entered, and it had begun to change.

When they'd come through it, it had been ice — a sheet of frozen waterfall, translucent, showing the grey rock of the threshold tunnel beyond. Now the frost on it was retreating the same way the frost on the land was retreating, and beneath the ice the surface showed wood. Old wood, silvered, carved over every inch with letters in the old tongue. Marguerite walked to it and put his palm flat against it and read, without effort, the way you read your own name.

*A door is a promise. This door promises: that what was lost may be found; that what was broken may be mended; that truth, freely given, opens what force never could.*

Beneath the inscription, smaller, newer-looking, a second line had appeared — the characters still sharp, as if cut yesterday:

*This door closes when the last true thing is spoken within. Let it close in peace.*

"Tonight," the queen said. "Or tomorrow. The land will tell us. The seed drinks fastest at dawn — it always did, even before."

Marguerite nodded. Then he turned around and walked back across the springing grass to where Ulyana sat, because there was one more thing, and he had promised himself he would not leave it unsaid. The door had taught him that much. Unsaid things sealed doors. He was not going to let one seal this one.

"Ulyana."

She looked up. In the growing green light her face looked younger than it had in the valley, or maybe just less guarded, and he realized with a small shock that he had never actually seen her unguarded before tonight. Forty years of exile and she had spent all of them armored, and it had taken a buried kingdom and a dying seed and a confession carved into her own arm to get the armor off.

"I forgive you," he said.

Her breath caught. Actually caught — he heard it, a small hitch like a stitch dropping in knitting.

"You don't have to—"

"I know I don't have to. That's what makes it worth something. You taught me that too, sort of, without meaning to." He sat down next to her on the cold edge of the warm world, their shoulders almost touching. "You betrayed the Garden. And then you spent forty years listening to the dead because you couldn't bear not to hear what you'd silenced. And then a knock came at your door and you opened it, even though opening it ruined you. That's not nothing. That's not even a little bit nothing." He swallowed. "The queen gave you witness. I'm giving you this. Take both. Go home and be the keeper of both countries' dead, and be honest, and grow old, and when you carve charms, carve one for me sometimes and set it somewhere the wind can find it. Because the wind goes over ridges. Wind doesn't need doors."

Ulyana did not cry. He had known her long enough — three seasons, one lifetime — to know she wouldn't, that whatever moved through her would move through silently, the way weather moves through a valley. But she put her good arm around his shoulders and pulled him against her side, and held him, and her chin came to rest on top of his head, and neither of them said anything for a long time, because the last true things had been said, and the silence afterward was also true, and the door was listening to all of it.

When she finally spoke, her voice was rough as bark.

"You were the best thing that ever knocked on my door, Marguerite. And I have had some very strange visitors."

* * *

Dawn, by his body's count. The seed had become a sapling overnight — knee-high, absurd, its leaves unfolding one by one like small green flags of surrender from winter's army. Around it the spring had reached the far walls. The last islands of frost clung only in the corners of the ceiling, melting in slow drips that fell like a clock counting down.

The queen stood by the door. Ulyana stood a little apart, at the border of the green, where the grass met the last grey rime of the old world — one foot in each country, which Marguerite thought was probably where she'd always stood, really, all her life.

"It's time," the queen said. "The land is full. The door is ready to keep its final promise."

Marguerite shouldered the little bundle he'd packed — it wasn't much; a knife Ulyana had given him, a strip of dried venison he refused to eat until he was home, and one bone charm, plain and perfect, carved in her style, that she had made during the night without telling anyone. He'd found it by his bedroll at dawn. It was shaped like a small door, slightly ajar.

He hugged Ulyana first, hard, face buried in the wool of her coat, breathing in tallow and bone dust and cold air, the smell of the only home he'd had outside the Garden. She held on until his ribs ached, and then let go first, briskly, wiping her eyes with the heel of her carved hand as if the gesture were merely practical.

"Grow straight," she told him. "Lean toward the sun. Don't fall over."

"Mind the north wall," he answered, and her laugh broke in the middle, and he pretended not to notice, because that was a kindness too.

Then he turned to the queen, and knelt, and she laid her hand on his head — light as falling snow, warm as new soil — and spoke the blessing of the Garden in the old tongue, the words he'd been born to, the words he'd nearly forgotten, the words he would now carry home to a country learning its own name again.

He stepped through the door.

On the threshold he turned, one last time. The queen was fading gently into the green light, smiling. Ulyana raised her carved arm — not in farewell, he realized, but in the carver's gesture, the one she used when setting a finished charm into the world: *go and speak.*

Beyond her shoulder, through the closing seam of the door, he saw the valley above — the ridge, the first snow, the small crooked house with its chimney smoke — and he saw, standing at the head of the path down from the ridge, figures coming through the storm. Villagers. Torches. And at their front, leaning on two neighbors, pale as bone but alive, a big man with a bandaged shoulder who should by rights be dead.

Halvard had not died. Halvard had walked down off the mountain.

Marguerite had no time to shout, no time to warn, no time even to understand — the door was sealing, wood sliding over wood, the promise completing itself — and the last thing he saw through the narrowing gap was Ulyana turning toward the torchlight, her carved arm lowering, her face going carefully, deliberately blank, as the man she loved came limping home to a secret she had already given away.

The door closed.

Somewhere on the other side, faint as a voice through a wall, he heard the valley begin to shout her name.
