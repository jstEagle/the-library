## 21. The Keeper of Both

The Wardens left on the third morning after the door closed.

Ulyana watched them go from the ridge, the same ridge where she had once stood as a young woman and spoken the words that sealed a valley against everything she had been. There were nine of them now — there had been eleven at the ford — and they moved down the far slope in their grey coats like a line of ink drying on paper, unhurried, unbroken, already becoming something that had happened rather than something that was happening.

They did not look back. That was the thing she noticed most. She had expected hatred, or hunger, or at least the grudging respect soldiers give an enemy who has cost them blood. Instead they simply went, the way a fever breaks and leaves no scar you can point to. One of them — the tall one with the burned hand, the one who had offered her absolution in her own workshop — paused at the tree line and stood for a long moment with his face turned toward the buried threshold beneath the ice. Then he touched two fingers to his brow, some old salute or old farewell, and followed the others down into the low country.

Magic's age was ending. Not in conquest. Not in fire. In a garden going home.

She stayed on the ridge until the grey coats vanished entirely, until the only movement left in the world was snow-smoke lifting off the pines and the slow breath of the valley below her. Her arm ached under its wrappings. She could feel the carved lines when she flexed her fingers, the confession still living in the bone: *I am Ulyana Winterbourne. I sold the Garden to save myself. I built this silence to hide inside it.* The door had accepted it. The door had opened. And now the words were hers forever, written in the one place she could never put down.

Below, the village was waking. Smoke rose from forty chimneys, straight and pale in the windless cold. Somewhere down there Halvard lay where she had carried him, in his own hall, on his own table, because she had refused to let strangers touch him and no one had argued. He had been dead two days and the valley already moved around the absence of him the way water moves around a stone — carefully, without comment, but always aware.

She went down.

* * *

The hall smelled of tallow and pine boughs and the particular clean cold of a room where a body waits. Halvard lay with his hands folded on his chest, and someone — Marta, probably, or one of the ford widows — had combed his beard and dressed him in his good coat, the dark blue one he wore to judgments and weddings and never to funerals, because he always said a reeve should be the second-best-dressed man at any gathering so the grieving could be first.

He looked like a man waiting patiently for a meeting to begin.

Ulyana sat beside him for a long time before she spoke.

"You knew." Her voice came out rough. "All those years. You knew what I was, and you never said."

The dead do not answer through flesh. They answer through bone, and Halvard's bones were not carved, and even if they had been, she had not the heart to take her knife to him. So she said it all anyway, to the silence, because he had spent thirty years listening to her silences and owed her at least one conversation held entirely on her side.

"You asked me once why I chose this valley. Do you remember? Twenty years ago, at the spring fair, after the third cup. You asked, and I told you I liked the quiet." She laughed, and it hurt. "And you said — you said, *No one likes this much quiet, Ulyana. People come here to hide from something.* And then you never asked again. Thirty years, and you never asked again."

The tallow flame leaned. Outside, a crow called twice and thought better of it.

"I found your ledger," she said. "After. In the chest under the judgment seat. Every year since I arrived — a line in your own hand. *The carver keeps her own counsel. Let her.* Signed, dated, witnessed by no one because you showed no one. You wrote yourself permission to trust me and then you filed it away like a boundary stone." She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "You absolute stubborn man. You could have asked. You could have made me say it, all those years ago, when saying it would have cost nothing. You waited until it cost you everything."

That wasn't fair and she knew it. He hadn't waited for anything. He had stepped in front of a Warden's blade because stepping aside had been unthinkable, and he had done it with the same unhurried certainty he brought to everything — to judgments, to ditches, to the long patient work of holding a valley together through winters and feuds and the slow erosion of years. He had died the way he lived: in the way, between danger and other people.

"I'm going to bury you up on the ridge," she told him. "Where the wards were. It's the highest ground we have, and you always said a reeve should be able to see his whole parish." She smoothed the blue coat across his chest, a small useless kindness. "And I'm going to carve you a charm. Don't make that face. I know what you'd say. *The dead should rest, Ulyana.* But you're going to be up there alone on the coldest ground in the valley, and I am not going to spend the rest of my life talking to a grave that can't tell me I'm being a fool. You've earned the right to keep correcting me. Take it."

She stood. Her knees cracked. She was fifty-eight years old and her arm was carved with her own sin and the boy she had saved was behind a door that would never open again, and none of it — none of it — hurt as much as the blue coat.

"Rest today," she said. "We bury you tomorrow."

* * *

They buried him at midday, which was not the custom — graves were dug at dawn, always, so the dead entered the earth with the day still new — but half the valley had turned out and dawn would not have held them all. Halvard's kin came down from the upper farms, grim-faced cousins Ulyana had met only at festivals, and the ford widows came in their black shawls, and Marta the baker closed her ovens for the first time in anyone's memory, and old Sef the ferryman, who had not walked further than his own gate in five years, stood at the foot of the grave leaning on two sticks and refusing all offers of a chair.

The reeve's office fell, by the old law, to whoever the dying reeve named. Halvard had named no one. So the elders gathered that evening in the hall that still smelled of him, and the argument began, and Ulyana did not attend, because she was up on the ridge with a shovel and a knife and a length of antler, doing the work that was actually hers.

The wards were gone. She felt it every time she crossed the old boundary stones — a thinness in the air, like a room after a window has been opened in winter. For thirty years the valley had lived inside a hush she had built, and now the hush was lifted, and the world outside came in plainly: wind, distance, the ordinary vast indifference of things. But the dead — the valley's own dead, the farmers and ferry-men and children lost to fever, generations of them sleeping in the churchyard soil — the dead were quieter than ever. Not silenced. Settled. As if the Garden's going-home had taken the last of the restless cold with it, and what remained was only memory, resting easy.

She dug the grave herself. The ground above the old ward-line was stony and she did not ask for help, though three men offered and she refused them with a look that ended the matter. By the time the pit was deep enough the light was failing and her carved arm was screaming, and she sat on the edge of the grave with her legs hanging into it, which would have horrified Halvard, and ate the bread Marta had pressed on her that morning, and talked to him while she worked.

"The elders are arguing about who takes the reeve's office," she said. "Sef wants his son-in-law. The upper farms want one of their own. Someone will suggest young Aldric, who is nineteen and once fined a man for letting his geese onto the common, which everyone agrees shows promise." She brushed crumbs from her knees. "They'll settle it badly, and it'll take them a month, and the ditches will go unmended, and by thaw they'll be sick of the whole business. Which is when I'm going to tell them what I've decided."

She took out the antler. She had cut it that afternoon from the old stag skull mounted over Halvard's hall door — with his cousins' blessing, given quickly and gratefully, as if they had been waiting for someone to ask. The bone was dense and yellowed and beautiful, and her knife knew it the way her knife knew all bone, the way it had known it since before she had a name anyone in this valley would have recognized.

She carved him a charm. Small, the size of a thumb joint, worked in the old style — the true style, the Garden style, the one she had sworn off for thirty years and found her hands remembered perfectly, stroke for stroke, as if the thirty years had happened to someone else. Into it she put his name, and the date, and one word more, in the dead tongue, the word Marguerite had taught her in the firelit weeks when the valley's wards flickered with every syllable: *witness.*

Not *rest*. Not *sleep*. Witness. Because Halvard had been the valley's witness for thirty years, the one who saw everything and said little, and she would not insult him by asking him to stop now.

When the grave was ready she lowered him in with the cousins' help, and the ford widows sang the low dragging hymn of the valley, and Sef wept openly and dared anyone to remark on it, and the earth went back in shovelful by shovelful until there was only a mound and a marker and the enormous indifferent sky.

Then everyone went down to the hall for the funeral meal, and Ulyana stayed.

* * *

Night came up the valley like water filling a bowl. She sat on the frozen ground beside the fresh grave with the charm in her palm, and when the last light failed she set it on the headstone — plain granite, his name and dates, no titles, because he would have hated titles — and she spoke to it in the dead tongue, and the charm woke.

It did not speak in words, exactly. Charms never did. It gave her what bone gives: a sense of attention, of being heard, of a presence settling in close the way Halvard used to settle onto the bench beside her at market days without ever quite looking at her, present and undemanding and utterly reliable.

"There," she said. "Now you can tell me when I'm being a fool. You'll have plenty of material."

The charm hummed its small bone-hum. If it had opinions, it kept them, which was also like him.

She looked out over the valley. Lights in the windows, forty of them, gold and small. Smoke standing straight up in the still air. Somewhere a dog, somewhere a fiddle badly played, somewhere a child crying and being comforted. The ordinary machinery of the living, running on. For thirty years she had watched this valley from the outside of itself — sheltered by it, fed by it, loved by it in the person of one stubborn man, and never once belonging to it, because belonging required a self she had buried. Now the self was dug up and carved and spent, and what remained was — she turned her hands over, studying them in the starlight — what remained was a woman with a knife who could hear the dead, in a valley full of both.

"They'll ask me to take the office," she said to the grave. "Not tomorrow. In a month, when they're tired. And I'm going to refuse the title and take the work, because you taught me the difference, even if you never said it outright. A reeve holds the living. Fine. I'll hold the rest. The dead, and the ridge, and the door under the ice, and whatever else comes out of the world now that the world's hush is lifted." She smiled, tired and crooked. "Keeper of both. It's not an office anyone voted on. It doesn't need to be. Some jobs you just *are*, once you've stopped lying about what you are."

The wind moved through the pines below the ridge, and in it — faintly, unmistakably — she heard the dead of the valley shift and settle, not stirring, just turning toward her the way a field turns toward the sun. Generations of them. The people whose charms she had carved, whose last words she had carried, whose griefs she had tended for thirty years without ever letting them tend hers.

"Well," she said aloud, standing, brushing the frost from her coat. "Best get started."

She picked up the shovel, and the lantern, and began the walk down toward the lights — and stopped, halfway down the slope, because the charm on Halvard's headstone had begun, very softly, to hum a second note.

She knew that note. She had heard it once before, through six inches of ice, in a voice older than kingdoms, on the night the door opened.

The queen's voice, coming not from beneath the mountain — but from the east. From beyond the ridge. From the direction of the road the Wardens had taken, moving fast, getting closer, carrying something with it that sounded almost like footsteps, and almost like spring.
