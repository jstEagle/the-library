## 22. Marguerite and the Garden

The bone came from a river otter that had died of old age under the willow roots, which was the only kind of bone Marguerite would use now. He had learned that much from her without meaning to: that the dead deserved to be asked first, and that asking took time, and that time spent kneeling in cold water talking to a small dead animal was never wasted.

He knelt there now, in the shallows of the Lisse, the river that ran green through the heart of the Garden, and he held the little bone up to the light the way she used to hold hers.

"I'm going to carve you," he said, in the old tongue, because some things still sounded truer in it. "Not for speaking. For listening. Do you mind?"

The bone said nothing, because bones never did until they were carved, and even then they only said what they had carried in life. But the river moved around his knees, warm as bathwater, and a kingfisher went past like a thrown blue coin, and somewhere behind him the orchards were dropping ripe fruit into the grass with soft, fat sounds, and Marguerite thought: *she would hate how easy this is.* Not really hate. But she'd raise one eyebrow. She'd say something dry about how magic in this country didn't even have the decency to be difficult.

He smiled at the bone. He was twenty-six years old and he still heard her voice in everything, and he had stopped minding that a long time ago.

* * *

The workshop stood where the old seed-house had been, at the edge of the terraces above the queen's garden — *his* garden now, though he kept the title loosely, the way you keep a coat that belonged to someone bigger than you. The Garden had no queen anymore. It had not needed one since the winter broke. What it had instead was a boy who remembered being small and lost in snow, and who had grown into a man who spent his days doing work that would have gotten him exiled from any other country in the world.

Because here was the thing about the Garden, the thing no outsider understood: it was not a place where magic lived. It was a place where magic had *been lived with*, carefully, the way you live with an old animal that could hurt you if you forgot its nature. And when the winter fell — when the Garden began to die — what died first was not the magic but the tending. Everyone had been so busy being powerful that no one had been doing the small daily work of listening.

Ulyana Winterbourne had done that work alone in a valley that hated magic, for thirty years, with nothing but bones and patience.

So when Marguerite came home through the door, and the seed went into the ground, and spring came back like a tide returning to a drained shore, the first thing he built was not a tower or a throne. It was a bench by a south-facing window, and a rack for drying herbs, and a shallow dish for holding carved charms mid-work. A workshop. Her workshop, rebuilt from memory, down to the scratch on the table leg where she'd tested a blade's edge.

The elders had thought it strange. They had expected him to want the palace restored, the high seats refilled, the old names spoken again. Instead their returned prince — they insisted on calling him that, though he had never been anything of the kind — sat cross-legged on the floor teaching children how to hold a graver against bone, and how to ask permission of the dead, and how to listen to the answer.

"She learned it in exile," he told them, when they asked why. "Everything worth keeping, we learned in exile. We should be grateful to the people who taught us."

He did not tell them the rest. That the teacher was gone. That the door had closed behind him forever, sealing with a sound like a long exhale, and that on the other side of it was a valley where the snow lay thin and honest, and a grave beneath a ridge, and a woman who had chosen to stay.

* * *

The door itself still existed. That was the strangest part, the part he had never managed to explain to anyone who hadn't seen it.

It stood in the deep wood below the terraces, where the ground dipped into a hollow that filled with blue shadow even at noon. After the spring came, the ice over it had melted away entirely, and what remained was simply a doorway of grey stone set into the hillside, framed in carvings worn nearly smooth — vines, once, and faces among the leaves. Through its arch there was no valley. There was only stone, seamless, like the inside of an egg. The door had closed forever, just as the queen had promised, and yet it remained, because the Garden did not believe in erasing things. The Garden believed in composting them. In letting what is finished feed what comes next.

The elders wanted it walled up. *A wound in the hillside,* they called it. *A reminder of the sickness.*

Marguerite had refused. He had refused quietly, the way Ulyana would have, which meant he had simply started walking to the door every few days until everyone got used to the idea, and by the time anyone thought to object, the path was worn and it was simply *what he did*, the way some men walked the walls at dusk.

He brought things. That was the ritual. Wildflowers in early spring, when the door's threshold was carpeted in celandine. Ripe plums in late summer, set on the stone sill where they'd ferment sweetly in the sun. Once, memorably, a live hen, which had regarded the sealed doorway with profound disappointment and then followed him home, and had lived out a long and entitled life in the workshop yard under the name Halvard, because Marguerite had a sense of humor and no one else in the Garden shared it.

And every visit, he talked. Out loud, in two languages, about nothing and everything. The harvest. The children learning letters. The new strain of barley that actually tasted like something. The way the northern mountains had gone quiet — truly quiet, no Wardens riding, no white banners, as if the whole apparatus that had ended the age of magic had simply run out of reasons to exist once there was nothing left to hunt. He reported the world to the door the way you'd write letters to someone who couldn't answer.

Except sometimes — rarely, maybe one visit in ten, always when the light was low and he had almost stopped expecting it — the door answered.

Not words. Never words. But a sound, faint, from deep in the seamless stone: a breath, or the ghost of a breath. Or once, unmistakably, the small dry click of a graver against bone, the sound of someone testing an edge, somewhere very far away on the other side of solid rock.

The first time it happened he had pressed both palms flat against the stone and called her name until his voice cracked, and the stone had given him nothing more, and he had walked home in the dark feeling flayed open and inexplicably lighter, the way you feel after crying at a funeral you've been holding off for years.

Now he knew better than to call. Calling made it stop. You had to just talk, and finish your errand, and be turning away, and *then* — sometimes — the sound would come, small and unhurried, like someone in another room acknowledging you without looking up from their work.

She was alive. He was sure of it, in the way you're sure of weather coming. She was old now, or getting there, keeping her valley, keeping both its living and its dead, exactly as she'd said she would. And once in a while, across a door that no longer existed, she paused in her work and listened back.

* * *

Today he finished the charm.

It was a good piece of otter bone, dense and pale, and he had soaked it and dried it and spoken to it for three evenings running, and now he worked it slowly with the finest graver in the rack — the one he never lent to students, the one whose handle was worn to the shape of a grip that wasn't quite his own, because he'd copied it from memory, from watching her hands.

In her style. That was the hard part, and the part that mattered. Ulyana's carving had never been decorative. Every line was functional and every function was a kindness: grooves that gave a voice somewhere to rest, spirals that turned grief so it could drain, a small unbroken circle near the base that he had never understood until he was older and had carved a hundred charms himself and realized what it was. It was a door. A tiny one. Cut into every single charm she ever made, so that whatever spoke through the bone would always know the way back out. So that nothing she worked with would ever be trapped.

She had spent thirty years making sure the dead could leave. That was the whole of her, if you wanted the whole of her. Not the betrayal, not the exile, not the confession carved into her own arm's bone at the threshold. Just that: a woman who could not bear to keep anything captive, not even a soul, not even her own guilt, and who had finally learned to let herself go free by the same method she used on everyone else — by telling the truth out loud and accepting the cost.

Marguerite cut the circle last, as she always had. Then he held the charm up to the window light, and the afternoon came through it golden, and the grain of the bone glowed like a leaf.

"This one isn't for the dead," he told it. "This one's for the living. For a woman on the far side of a wall of stone who probably thinks I've forgotten her." He turned it over. "I haven't. I want you to know that. Whatever you are now, whoever carries you, wherever you end up — I haven't forgotten."

The charm said nothing, being newly made and empty, but it felt warm in his palm, warmer than the room, and he chose to take that as an answer.

* * *

He walked down through the terraces in the long gold light of autumn, past the apple trees bent double with fruit, past the schoolhouse where the evening lesson was letting out and children spilled onto the paths like water. One of them saw him and shouted his name and ran to walk beside him, a girl of eight with grass stains on her knees, and she demanded to know where he was going, and he said, "To visit a friend," and she said, "The door? Can I come?" and he said, "Not today. Today I'm going alone."

"Why do you go alone?"

"Because some conversations are between two people," he said, "even when one of them can't answer."

She considered this with the enormous seriousness of the young, then ran off toward supper, satisfied, and Marguerite went on down through the blue-shadowed wood alone, carrying the charm wrapped in wool in his breast pocket, over his heart, where he'd carried a seed once, a lifetime ago, through snow that never ended.

The hollow was full of evening. Celandine long gone; the threshold now wore a carpet of fallen beech leaves, copper and bright, and he swept them gently from the sill before setting the charm down in the center of the stone, upright, its tiny carved circle facing outward. Facing the valley side. Facing her.

Then he sat down on the mossy bank opposite, as he had done hundreds of times, and began to talk.

He told the door about the barley. About the girl with the grass stains, who was quick with numbers and impatient with the asking-permission part and would either become a great carver or a great nuisance, possibly both. About the last Warden outpost in the north having closed its gates for good, the banner taken down, the riders gone home to farms. About how the world outside had begun, in its slow blind way, to forget that magic had ever been hunted — and how he had decided this was not a tragedy but a mercy, because things that are forgotten cannot be feared, and things that are not feared can someday be welcomed back.

"And when they're ready," he said, "when some child in some gray country finds a bone charm in a market stall and holds it and hears something whisper — I hope whoever carved it left the circle open. I hope they remember the way out."

The light was going. He stood, brushed off his knees, said the closing words she used to say — not a prayer, she'd insisted, just manners — and turned to go.

Behind him, in the seamless stone, faint and clear and utterly impossible:

A breath. Then the small dry click of a graver testing an edge. Then — softer than the leaves settling, quieter than the river far above, but there, unmistakably there, in a voice roughened by decades of smoke and cold air and truth-telling —

*"Marguerite."*

Just his name. Nothing more. The way she'd said it the first night, half-frozen on her doorstep, when the dead in her workshop had stirred for the first time in years.

He did not turn around. Turning made it stop; he knew that now. He stood in the falling dark with his eyes burning and his hand pressed flat over his heart, over the place where a seed had once slept, and he smiled like a fool at the beech trees, and he said, into the gathering night, loud enough to carry through stone and silence and whatever distance remained between one keeper of the dead and another:

"I know. I'm here. Same time tomorrow."

And from behind the door that no longer existed, warm as a banked fire, patient as spring underneath the snow, he heard — or imagined he heard, and had decided long ago that the difference did not matter — the sound of someone laughing softly, and going back to her work.
