## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The king's breakfast arrived at the seventh bell, as it always did, carried up three flights of stairs by a boy who never looked Lior in the eye.

Lior had learned long ago that this was a kindness. The boy's name was Tam, and he had a mother in the Saltrow district and a limp he tried to hide, and if he ever met the taster's gaze for longer than a courtesy, he might start to wonder about the man who ate first from every dish the crown touched. Wondering was dangerous in Vell. Wondering led to questions, and questions, these days, led to lists.

So Tam set the tray on the marble table in the antechamber off the royal kitchens, mumbled something about eggs and smoked eel and honey-cakes, and left. And Lior sat alone with a meal fit for a king and began, as they had every morning for twenty years, to work.

The tongue came first. It always did.

Lior closed their eyes and let the gift rise—not far, just to the surface, like a fish rising to inspect the light before diving back into safe dark. Magic in Vell was a drowned thing; everyone knew it had once swum openly through the city, back when the old guilds stood and the covenant held, but that was grandmother's talk now, the kind of story told in whispers over gutted fish. What remained was small and feral and had to be fed carefully or it would show itself.

Lior's gift showed itself in the mouth.

They pressed two fingers to their tongue and let the taste of the room sharpen into something almost visible: the mineral tang of the well water used to boil the eggs, the ghost of lye soap on the tray's rim, the sweet rot beginning at the edge of the honey-cakes—nothing, that last one, just a baker cutting his sugar with day-old stock, a crime of economy rather than murder. Lior catalogued each flavor the way a clerk catalogues coin, and beneath the cataloguing ran the deeper current, the one no one must ever see: the tasting of intention. Poison carried a flavor all its own when it was meant to kill. Bitterness with an appetite behind it. Lior had tasted that appetite exactly four times in twenty years, and each time had spat, called for the dish to be burned, and watched a kitchen servant hang for it.

Three of those four had been innocent. Lior still knew their names.

*Never glow,* said the first rule, the one carved so deep into Lior that it felt less like memory than bone. *Never heal. Never be seen twice.*

The rules had been taught to them by Old Wenna, the herbwoman who'd found a starving child with a strange tongue and decided, against every instinct of self-preservation, to keep it. *Glowing* was any magic visible enough to draw a crowd. *Healing* was worse—a healed wound left a signature, a smoothness under the skin that a physician could find with his fingers and a watchman could find with a warrant. And *never be seen twice* meant that whatever you did, whoever you helped, you did it once, in passing, wearing a face they wouldn't remember. A charm sold in a market crowd. A fever broken in a doorway while the mother fetched water. Kindness, in Vell, had to look like accident.

Lior chewed the smoked eel slowly, letting the gift read it like a letter. Clean. The eggs were clean. The honey-cakes got a pass with a private note to speak to the baker's supplier—or not; intervening in a baker's fraud was the kind of small visibility that accumulated, and accumulation was how tasters died. Twenty years of discipline said: eat, swallow, nod, live.

Twenty years of loneliness said nothing at all. It had gotten very good at silence.

* * *

The immunity tonics came next, as they did every morning, in three brown bottles lined up on the shelf above the basin. This was the other half of the taster's trade, the half that kept Lior alive through the poisons that slipped past even a gifted tongue—the slow ones, the tasteless ones, the ones brewed by alchemists who charged kings a fortune precisely because they couldn't be detected.

Lior dosed themselves with a dropper's precision. Foxglove-ash for the cardiac agents. Milk-thistle and powdered stag-horn for the liver poisons. And the third bottle, unlabeled, which contained a tonic of Lior's own devising—one part willow-bark, one part something the pharmacists of Vell would have burned them for, stirred clockwise under a waning moon because some things worked better when the working was quiet.

It was not, strictly speaking, illegal. That was the joke Lior never told anyone. Half the medicine in Vell was magic that had learned to wear a coat and carry a ledger. The Registry Act hadn't been posted yet—that was rumor, but rumor in Vell moved like tide, and the tide was coming in—but the fear that would become the Act already lived in every checkpoint and every neighbor's sideways glance. The law always arrived after the fear had done its groundwork, the way lightning arrives after the air has gone strange.

Lior swallowed the third tonic and grimaced. Even after all these years, it tasted like pennies and regret.

Through the antechamber's narrow window, Vell was waking. The harbor fog was burning off in rags, and beyond it the masts of a hundred ships stood up out of the water like a forest of black winter trees. Somewhere down there, in the fish market, the day's luck-charms were about to change hands. Not real charms—never anything a magistrate could point to—but the harmless kind, the kind that worked only as well as belief carried them: knotted red thread, salt in a twist of paper, a word spoken low over a coin. Lior sold them for coppers and made perhaps a silver a week, which was absurd, given that they also tasted for the crown, but the stall was the best disguise Lior owned. Nobody suspects a charm-seller of being a mage. Charm-sellers are the honest frauds of the world, and frauds are safe.

Mostly.

* * *

The fish market smelled the way it always did at midmorning—brine and blood and woodsmoke, gulls screaming their endless litigation overhead—and Lior moved through it in their gray cloak, unremarkable, a man-shaped absence among the crowds. They had gotten very good at being an absence. It was, they sometimes thought, the truest magic they possessed.

The stall was where it always was, between Ostler's rope-shop and the woman who sold eels by the string. Lior laid out the wares: knots and salts and little clay beads fired with a pinch of ash inside, each one "blessed" with a murmured word that was mostly breath. Business was slow, as it was most days. A dockhand bought a knot for his wife's labor. A boy bought salt for his father's nets. An old woman haggled Lior down to two coppers for a bead against her cough, and Lior let her win, and slipped a second bead into her purse when she wasn't looking, because the bead actually worked—it was packed with coltsfoot and honey—and because the old woman reminded Lior, painfully, of Wenna, dead eleven years now, and of the particular ache of caring about someone you can never tell the truth to.

That was the thing about the rules. They kept you alive, and they kept you alone, and after enough years you stopped being able to tell the difference between the keeping and the cage.

"Master Dunmore."

Lior looked up. Brava Tull stood at the edge of the stall with her daughter beside her, and Lior's chest did the small complicated thing it always did around the girl.

Pell Tull was nine years old, gap-toothed, quick-eyed, with her mother's strong jaw and her dead father's laugh. She'd been coming to the stall since she could walk, first riding on Brava's hip, now standing at her side nearly eye-level with Lior across the counter. She asked questions. That was the trouble. She asked why the knots were red, and whether the salt worked on storms or only bad luck, and once—terribly, memorably—she'd asked why Master Dunmore's eyes went silver when he hummed.

Lior had laughed and said it was a trick of the light, and Pell had accepted it the way children accept things, which is to say she filed it away somewhere deep, and Lior had gone home that night and drunk half a bottle of plum wine and hated themselves with great efficiency until sleep took pity.

"Morning, Brava," Lior said. "Morning, Pell."

"I need a charm," Brava said, too loud, too formal—the script they'd agreed on years ago, the fiction that let Lior hand over what they handed over without either of them having to name it. "For my girl. She starts at the chandler's school next week, and I want her to have luck."

"School." Lior nodded gravely. "Dangerous work."

"You've no idea. There's sums."

Lior reached beneath the counter and brought out the charm they'd prepared three nights ago, working by candlelight with the shutters barred: a flat disc of river-clay, glazed dull brown, with a knotted cord through it. To any observer it was worth a copper. Inside the glaze, folded into the clay while it was still soft, was a whisper of working—nothing that would glow, nothing that would heal, nothing that would ever be seen twice. Just warmth. Just a small stubborn warmth that would sit against a child's sternum on cold mornings and cold afternoons and make the world feel, very slightly, like someone was standing behind her.

"One luck-charm," Lior said, "for a scholar." They pressed it into Pell's palm and closed her fingers over it, and let their thumb rest on her knuckles one heartbeat longer than commerce required. "Wear it under your shirt. Don't lose it. Don't tell anyone what it cost."

"A copper," Pell said promptly.

"A copper," Lior agreed solemnly, and took the copper, and watched the girl run her thumb over the disc the way people touch things they already love.

Brava lingered. Her eyes went to the harbor, then to the notice-board at the market's edge, where a new paper had been pasted up that morning among the bills of sale and the missing-persons notices. From here Lior could see only its shape—official, wide, bearing the crown's seal at the top like a black sun.

"They say there's a new law coming," Brava said quietly. "About... certain people."

"People are always saying things," Lior said.

"Lior." She never used the name. The use of it now landed like a dropped glass. "If things get bad. If there's a list. You'd go, wouldn't you? You'd leave?"

Leave. As though Vell weren't the only country Lior had ever known, as though the gift could be packed in a bag along with the tonics and the knotting cord. As though Sefa—

Lior's hands went still on the counter.

Sefa. Sixteen years old, sharp as a fishhook, learning the trade with the kind of hunger that Lior recognized because it had once been their own hunger, and carrying—this was the problem, this had always been going to be the problem—a talent twice the size of Lior's and a tenth of the caution. Last week Sefa had asked why they couldn't just *help people*, openly, the way the old stories said mages used to. Last night Sefa had practiced a warming charm in the cellar and gotten so pleased with it that the candle flames had all leaned toward her like flowers toward sun.

*Never glow,* Lior had told her, and Sefa had nodded, and Lior had seen in her face the exact expression of a person agreeing to a rule they intend to survive by breaking.

"I'd go," Lior said to Brava, which was a lie, and she knew it was a lie, and she took Pell's hand and thanked him for the charm and walked away into the crowd, and Lior watched them go and thought: *I am the loneliest competent man in Vell.*

Then the bells began.

Not the hour-bells. The alarm pattern from the citadel—three strikes, pause, three strikes—the sound the city made when the crown wanted everyone's attention at once. The market stilled around Lior, vendors and buyers alike turning toward the notice-board, toward the watchmen now striding through the fish stalls with hammers and fresh paste and armfuls of paper.

A watchman reached the board nearest Lior's stall. He slapped the new bill up over the missing-persons notices, smoothed it flat with two hard strokes of his hand, and stepped back.

Even at forty feet, Lior could read the heading. Everyone could. It was written in letters tall enough to reach from the harbor to the hills:

**BY ORDER OF THE CROWN: THE REGISTRY ACT.**

*All persons possessing unlawful gifts shall declare themselves within thirty days, upon pain of forfeiture and fire.*

Below it, smaller, the list of penalties. Below that, the seal.

Lior stood very still behind their counter of knots and salt and harmless lies, holding a copper coin warm from a child's hand, and understood that the life they had spent twenty years building—dish by tasted dish, rule by rule, silence by silence—had just been given thirty days to live.
