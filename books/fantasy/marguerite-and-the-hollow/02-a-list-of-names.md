## 2. A List of Names

The proclamation went up on a Tuesday, which Lior would remember afterward as the cruelest detail of all—because Tuesdays were market days, and the whole of Vell was out in the streets to read it.

They had been buying salted cod at Brannick's stall when the criers came through, six of them in royal livery, their voices pitched in that flat, official cadence that made even good news sound like a sentence being passed. *By decree of His Majesty Aldric the Third, Sovereign of Vell and its Territories: the Registry Act is hereby enacted. All persons possessed of unlicensed gift, craft, or art beyond natural means shall declare themselves before the Office of Inquiry within thirty days, upon pain of forfeiture, imprisonment, or death.*

The crowd did not gasp. That was the thing about Vell that outsiders never understood—the city didn't gasp. It went quiet in a particular way, a way Lior had learned to hear the way a sailor hears weather in the rigging. The fishwives stopped haggling. The gulls kept screaming overhead, indifferent. Somewhere near the fountain a child laughed, and the laugh cut off abruptly, as if its owner had been pinched.

Lior paid for the cod with steady hands. They had practiced steady hands for twenty years; they could thread a needle during an earthquake.

"Terrible business," Brannick said, not looking at them, wrapping the fish in brown paper with elaborate care. "My cousin's wife's brother knew a hedge-witch up in Corrow. Nice woman. Cured his gout."

"Mm," said Lior.

"Never did anything wrong, far as anyone knows."

"No," Lior agreed. "They rarely do."

That was the whole of it. That was how it worked now, in the port city of Vell, where magic had been bled out of the law one statute at a time until what remained was this: a list, a deadline, and three punishments arranged in ascending order of enthusiasm. Declare yourself and be registered—marked, taxed, watched, your gift catalogued like a shipment of contraband silk. Refuse, and be hunted. And between those two doors, a third that no one said aloud but everyone understood: keep hiding, and pray the hunt never learns your name.

Lior walked home along the harbor road with the cod under their arm and the proclamation ringing in their skull like a struck bell that wouldn't finish fading. Thirty days. They had survived two decades on three rules—*never glow, never heal, never be seen twice*—and rules like those were built for a world where magic was merely illegal. This was something else. This was the law learning to count.

* * *

The guild ledger lived in a cedar chest beneath the floorboards of Lior's shop, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, because paper remembered things even after ink faded. It had belonged to the Charterhouse Guild of Cunning Folk, dissolved forty years before Lior was born, and it contained, in a clerk's careful copperplate, the names of every gifted person licensed by the old kings to practice openly in Vell. Healers. Charmers. Weather-readers. Tasters.

Lior's grandmother's name was on the fourth page. *Maren Dunmore, taster, licensed by warrant of King Osric II.* Below it, in fresher ink, someone had added Lior's mother, and below that—

Below that, in Lior's own hand, twenty-two years younger and foolish enough to believe a ledger buried under floorboards was safe forever: *Lior Dunmore, taster, apprentice to E. Dunmore.*

They knelt on the cold boards of the shop with the ledger open across their knees and did not touch it for a long time.

The shop above them was quiet in the late-afternoon way, dust motes turning gold in the light from the shutters, jars of dried herbs and bottled charms—harmless charms, luck-charms, sleep-ease, salt for thresholds—glinting on their shelves like a congregation at prayer. From the back room came the small industrious sounds of Sefa sweeping. Sixteen years old, all elbows and appetite, with her mother's dark curls escaping her kerchief and her father's talent for asking exactly the wrong question at exactly the right moment.

"Sefa," Lior called. "Come here."

The sweeping stopped. Footsteps. She appeared in the doorway with the broom still in her hand, and Lior saw her eyes go straight to the open chest, the oilcloth, the ledger—and stay there, hungry, the way she looked at everything Lior tried to keep from her.

"What is it?"

"Sit down."

"I'm standing fine."

"*Sit down*, girl."

Sefa sat. She was learning, slowly, when Lior's voice took on that particular grain. Lior turned the ledger so she could see it, and watched her read, and watched her lips move slightly over the older names, and watched her finger rise toward the page where her own teacher's name sat in faded iron-gall ink.

"You're *on* it," Sefa said. Not a question. Her finger hovered over the letters without touching them, the way you'd hover a hand over a sleeping animal you wanted desperately to pet. "Your name's written down. In a *book*. Where?"

"Under my floor."

"That's—" She caught herself, glanced around the shop as if the walls might have grown ears since morning. "That's not nothing, though. Is it? If they find it."

"If they find it, it's proof." Lior closed the ledger gently, the way you'd close a coffin lid. "A list of every gifted soul in Vell, going back a hundred years. Names, gifts, warrants. Some of these people's grandchildren are alive right now, working charms in cellars, thinking they're ghosts." They tapped the cover. "This book is a hanging offense with a leather binding."

"Then why do you *keep* it?"

Because it was the last piece of a world where Lior's family had been respectable. Because burning it felt like agreeing with the king that the names were shameful. Because grief makes hoarders of us all. Lior considered each answer and discarded it, because Sefa was sixteen and reckless and curious in the specific way that got people burned, and the truest answer was also the most dangerous one:

"Because I thought it was history," Lior said. "And today I found out it's evidence."

* * *

They burned it that night, in the shop's back room, in the iron brazier Lior used for warming charm-blanks, and Lior insisted on doing it alone—or rather, Lior intended to insist, and Sefa simply refused to leave, planting herself on the stool by the door with her arms crossed and her chin set in a way that was going to get her killed someday, Lior thought, or crowned, there was no telling which.

Page by page, the guild died again. Maren Dunmore, taster. Edith Dunmore, taster. Old Hollis Fenwick, who could calm a spooked horse with a hummed note. The Weatherwife of Salt Row, whose name had been struck through already by some earlier frightened hand. Each page curled, blackened, lifted, and came apart into flakes that glowed orange for a heartbeat before going gray. The copperplate handwriting held its shape longest, names legible inside the flame for a moment—*licensed by warrant, sworn and sealed*—and then gone.

Lior had expected to feel relief. What they felt instead was a strange, cold vertigo, like stepping off a stair that wasn't there. Twenty years of careful living, and it had come down to this: a book, a fire, and the discovery that safety had been an illusion with very good posture.

"There," Sefa said softly, when the last page was ash. "Now you're nobody."

"Now I'm nobody," Lior agreed. But they were looking at the girl, not the brazier, and the thought arrived unbidden and unwelcome: *she isn't nobody. She's sixteen, she has the gift, and she isn't in any ledger anywhere—which means if she's ever caught, there'll be no record to burn, no history to erase. Just a girl, and a rope, and thirty days' worth of law behind it.*

"You should go home," Lior said. "It's late."

"It's barely dark."

"*Home*, Sefa."

Sefa went, but slowly, pausing at the door with the night air coming in around her, and she gave Lior a look over her shoulder that Lior would turn over in memory many times in the weeks to come—a look that was half affection and half something sharper, something with edges. Curiosity, yes. But curiosity of the kind that doesn't ask questions because it's already decided to find its own answers.

"Goodnight, Lior," she said.

"Goodnight. Lock your door. And Sefa—" Lior hesitated, hearing their own voice take on the grain it always took for the three rules, the litany, the catechism of survival. "No glow. No healing. Never be seen twice."

"I know." She rolled her eyes, fond and impatient. "You say it every night."

"And you listen every night?"

She grinned, and was gone into the dark of Copper Lane, and Lior stood in the doorway a long while after, listening to her footsteps fade, feeling the ash-ghost of the ledger on their fingers.

* * *

Sleep did not come. Around midnight Lior sat at the workbench with a single candle and did what they always did when the fear got loud: inventory. One jar of luck-charms, harmless. One tin of immunity tonic, harmless. One set of tasting knives, licensed, legitimate, the only magic in Vell a man could admit to at sword-point. One apprentice, gifted, headstrong, currently asleep four streets away behind a locked door that would stop precisely no one who meant business.

Thirty days.

Lior thought about the criers' voices. *Upon pain of forfeiture, imprisonment, or death.* Thought about how the Office of Inquiry had opened its doors that same morning, how clerks had already been taking declarations down at the Custom House, how somewhere in Vell tonight some terrified hedge-witch or herb-healer was weighing thirty years of hidden life against a signature on a crown document, and how some of them—hungry ones, frightened ones, proud ones—would sign. Would walk in and give their names freely, believing registration meant safety.

And then the crown would have a list. A fresh one. Current, accurate, complete.

Lior set down the knife they'd been polishing and stared at the candle flame without seeing it. The old kings had licensed mages and kept ledgers, and those ledgers had rotted under floorboards and done no harm. But this king—Aldric, young and pious and freshly anxious about enemies—this king wasn't building a registry. Registries were for counting. This was for *keeping*. You did not demand that people declare their gifts unless you intended, eventually, to collect them.

The candle guttered. Outside, the harbor bells rang the third watch, and somewhere in the dark of Copper Lane a dog began to bark—not the idle barking of dogs at cats, but the sharp, rhythmic alarm-bark of a dog at strangers.

Lior blew out the candle and listened.

Boots. More than two pairs. Moving up Copper Lane at a pace that had nothing casual in it, and stopping—not passing, *stopping*—somewhere near the corner where Sefa's tenement leaned against the old rope-walk.

They were not looking for Lior. Lior's name was ash. But names were only ever half the hunt, and Lior stood alone in the dark shop with their heart going like a drum and understood, with terrible clarity, that the thirty days had been a courtesy extended to adults—that the crown's men had come for the girl first, tonight, because children declared nothing and testified to everything.

Lior grabbed their coat and ran.
