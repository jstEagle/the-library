## 3. What Sefa Carried

The cold came early that winter, the kind of cold that got into the bones of Vell and stayed there like a tenant who paid no rent. Sefa felt it first in her fingers, which were always the last part of her to warm, even by the brazier in Lior's workroom. She had learned to hide it—tucking her hands into her sleeves, blowing on them like anyone else—but hiding was a skill she was still learning, and at sixteen she suspected she would never be as good at it as Lior was.

Lior could stand in a room full of watchmen and be furniture. Sefa could not.

She thought about this a great deal, usually while grinding dried feverfew or copying charm-sigils onto slips of birchbark in Lior's cramped shop above the chandler's on Tanner's Row. The rules had been drilled into her for three years now, since the day Lior found her trying to sell a luck-charm to a soldier with frostbite in his toes and no coin to pay for it. *Never glow. Never heal. Never be seen twice.* Lior had said it the way other people recited prayers, flat and worn smooth from repetition, and Sefa had nodded along because nodding was easier than arguing.

But there was a fourth rule, one Lior never said aloud, and Sefa had worked it out herself over years of watching: *never let someone suffer when you could stop it.* It wasn't written anywhere. It lived in the pauses between Lior's words, in the way their hands lingered over a tonic they weren't supposed to be able to make, in the extra bread that appeared in the pockets of certain beggars along Cordwainer Street. Lior broke the fourth rule constantly, quietly, and then hated themselves for it.

Sefa was not quiet about anything.

* * *

It happened on a Tuesday, in the week before the Registry Act went up on the courthouse doors, though Sefa wouldn't learn what that meant until later. On the Tuesday itself there was only the cold, and the market, and the errand: two jars of tallow-wax for the chandler below, purchased with coins Lior counted out twice because Lior counted everything twice.

The fish market smelled the way it always did in deep winter—brine and ice and the sweet rot of things the gulls hadn't gotten to. The stalls wore skins of frozen spray. Fishmongers haggled in breath-plumes, and the cobbles were glazed black with ice where the tide-splash had frozen mid-run. Sefa moved through it with her collar up and her hood down, because a hood marked you as someone with something to hide, and she was trying, genuinely trying, to be someone without anything to hide.

That was when she saw the child.

He was curled in the lee of the salt-barrel stall, wedged into the gap between the barrel and the wall where the wind couldn't quite reach him, and he was so still that at first Sefa took him for a bundle of rags someone had abandoned. Then the bundle shivered. A small, terrible shiver, the kind that comes from a body that has stopped being able to shiver properly—the deep, failing tremor of a fire going out.

Sefa knew cold. Everyone in Vell knew cold. But she also knew what cold did when it finished its work, because her mother had died of it before Lior ever found Sefa sleeping in the ash-pit behind a dyer's shop, and something in her chest turned over, hard, at the sight of that small failing shiver.

*Walk past,* said the voice in her head that sounded like Lior. *Get the chandler. Get a blanket. Get help the ordinary way.*

But the ordinary way took time, and time was the one thing the child didn't have. She could see it in his lips, gray-blue under the grime. In the way his eyes had gone glassy and distant, fixed on nothing, on somewhere warm he was already halfway to.

There was no one close. The fishmongers had drawn back from that corner all morning; beggars near the salt-stalls were bad for business, and everyone gave the gap behind the barrels a wide berth, which meant the boy had been dying in public view for hours and the whole market had agreed, wordlessly, not to see it.

Sefa saw it.

Her hand was in her pocket before she decided anything. Her fingers closed around the warming-charm she'd made the week before—a scrap of birchbark no bigger than her thumbnail, sigiled in ink she'd mixed herself, a practice piece she'd kept because it was the cleanest thing she'd ever drawn. It was a trinket. A nothing. The kind of charm half the grandmothers in the Lower City probably carried and no watchman had ever bothered about.

Except it wasn't a nothing, and she knew that too. A warming-charm worked by drawing heat from the air around it and pressing it into whatever it touched, and when it worked well, when it worked *clean*, it did something no trick of tallow and copper wire could do. It glowed. Just barely—a soft amber shimmer under the skin of the world, visible only if you were looking, only if you knew.

*Never glow.*

The boy's breath had stopped fogging the air.

Sefa knelt. She pulled off her mitten with her teeth, pressed the charm flat against his small sternum through the filthy layers of his coat, and whispered the word her mother had used for the hearth-fire, back before, back when words could still mean warm things.

Heat bloomed. She felt it leave the air around them and pour into him like water into cracked ground—the color coming back into his face in a slow tide, the shivering gathering itself into something stronger, something alive. And the charm glowed. Not much. A candleflame's worth of gold, cupped in her bare palm against his chest, in the open air of the fish market at midmorning.

"Hey," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Hey. You're all right. Look at me."

His eyes focused. That was the miracle of it, the small domestic miracle that no one would ever write songs about: a pair of glassy eyes remembering how to see. He looked at her, and then at the light in her hand, and instead of fear there was something worse—something that cracked her open. He smiled.

"You're warm," he said.

"I'm warm," Sefa said, and her eyes were stinging, and she was so busy being furious at herself for crying that she almost missed the silence.

Markets were never silent. But the fishmonger two stalls down had stopped mid-haggle, mouth open, breath steaming. A woman with a basket of cockles stood frozen, staring—not at the boy, but at Sefa's hand. At the light.

And beyond her, at the mouth of Copper Lane, a man in a watchman's oilskin had turned around.

* * *

Afterward, Sefa could never reconstruct those minutes properly. Memory broke them into shards, the way ice breaks: here, the fishmonger's face closing like a door; here, her own hand closing around the charm, killing its light; here, the boy blinking up at her, alive, confused, asking why she'd stopped. Here, her voice saying *run, go, go home, do you have a home?* and him scrambling up on legs that worked again, working, thanks to her, thanks to the thing she'd done, and vanishing into the warren behind the salt-stalls.

Here, the watchman walking toward her, unhurried, which was worse than running.

She left the wax on the chandler's counter without paying—she'd settle it later, she told herself, knowing even then that "later" was a country she might not get to visit—and walked home the long way, doubling back twice, telling herself the man hadn't really seen. Telling herself a glow that small was nothing, that people imagined things, that the Registry Act was just paper, that paper needed proof.

Lior met her at the door with a look that said they already knew. They always knew. It was the most infuriating thing about them.

"The chandler sent a boy," Lior said. "You left without paying."

"I'll pay it back."

"That's not what I'm worried about." Lior closed the shutters. In the sudden gloom their face was all planes and shadow, older than its years, tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "Tell me."

So Sefa told them. All of it, the glow included, and watched Lior's face do the thing it did—nothing, absolutely nothing, a mask so complete it was almost an insult—and then, at the end, the small crack: a breath let out slowly through the nose, the sound of twenty years of careful living taking a blow.

"He'll report it," Lior said. "Not today. Watchmen don't run on the day they see something; they run on the day they're ordered to. But he'll write it down, and writing is how it starts."

"So we run first."

"Where?" Lior asked, and it wasn't cruel, it was just true, and the truth of it sat in Sefa's stomach like a swallowed stone. Run where? Every road out of Vell was watched in winter. Every village had a parish register and a priest with sharp eyes. "We stay. We're careful. We are *very* careful, Sefa, and you—" their voice caught, and for one unguarded moment Sefa saw underneath: not anger. Fear. Raw and specific and shaped exactly like her. "You don't touch another charm in public. Not to save a king. Not to save—"

"Don't," Sefa said. "Don't say I shouldn't have done it."

"I'm saying you should have been smarter about *how*."

"He was dying."

"Yes." Lior closed their eyes. When they opened them, the mask was back, but thinner. "Yes, he was. And now every gifted person on this street is a little more likely to die too, because a watchman saw a light. That's the arithmetic we live by. I hate it. You hate it. But it's the arithmetic."

Sefa looked at the floor. She was not sorry—that was the terrible, stubborn core of her, the part Lior despaired of and, she suspected, secretly loved. She would do it again. She would do it again tomorrow. But she understood, in the way you understand a debt, that the cost hadn't been hers alone to pay.

* * *

They came at dusk.

Sefa heard them first—the boots, four pairs, deliberate, on the stairs to the shop—and her body knew before her mind did, some animal inheritance from the years before Lior, the years of listening for footsteps. She was at the window in three strides.

Four watchmen in the lane below, lanterns lit though the dark was barely settled. And at the head of the stairs, already knocking, a sergeant with a paper in his fist.

"Sefa." Lior's voice was very calm. Calm the way deep water is calm. "Back door. Now."

"But—"

"*Now.*"

She went. Through the workroom, past the shelf of harmless charms, past the hidden panel Lior had never explained, into the yard behind the chandler's where the tallow-vats breathed their greasy steam into the freezing air. Behind her she heard the door open, heard Lior's voice, smooth and bored and perfectly pitched: "Evening, Sergeant. Can I help you? Mind the step, it ices over after sundown—"

And then, cutting through it, the sergeant's voice, flat and official: "*Sefa Ashgrave*. By order of the Crown, under the Registry Act—"

She didn't wait to hear the rest. She went over the yard wall the way she'd gone over a hundred walls, dropped into the alley, and ran.

Not toward the river—they'd watch the river. Toward the market, stupidly, gloriously, because the fish market at dusk was chaos itself: stalls collapsing into carts, lanterns swinging, a thousand bodies and a thousand smells, and every exit a rumor. She hit the crowd at a dead sprint, shoulder-first, apologizing without stopping, and behind her the shout went up—*"There! Stop her!"*—and boots hammered the cobbles.

A fishmonger's barrow swung into her path and she vaulted it. Someone stuck out a foot and she stumbled, caught herself on a tent-pole, and the whole stall swayed—and then, impossibly, held, because a broad-shouldered woman had grabbed the far side and was looking at Sefa with an expression Sefa couldn't read. *Go on, then,* the woman's face said. Or maybe it said *you're mad.* Either way, the stall held.

They knew. Some of them knew. The city was full of people the arithmetic had ground down, and tonight, for no reason she would ever be able to ask about, they were choosing her side.

She burst out the market's far end into Copper Lane and there was Lior—Lior, who should have been upstairs answering questions, standing in the mouth of the alley with a lantern raised high, and as the pursuing watchmen rounded the corner, Lior stepped directly into their path.

"Sergeant!" Lior's voice rang off the walls, indignant, official, *unforgettable*. "Thank the saints—I've been chasing a cutpurse myself, took my strongbox not ten minutes past, went down this lane—did you see—no? Then he's doubled back, the little rat, come on, I'll show you where he climbed—"

And the watchmen, trained to follow the loudest confident voice in any crisis, followed it.

Sefa ran. Down Copper Lane, across the reeking gutter, into the dark throat of the tannery district where she'd spent half her childhood and knew every wall and every shadow. She ran until her lungs burned and the shouts faded and the only sound was her own blood and breath.

Then she stopped, bent double in the doorway of a disused smokehouse, and made herself think.

Lior had bought her the lane. Bought her the night, maybe. But the sergeant had a paper with her name on it, and papers didn't tire, and tomorrow the boots would come again—to the shop, to the chandler's, to every place she'd ever been seen twice.

Which meant Lior was alone in that shop right now, lying to four watchmen with a smile, and Sefa had no way to warn them, no plan, no coin, and nowhere in the city of Vell to be safe.

She wrapped her arms around herself, in the cold she could no longer charm away, and tried to imagine what Lior would do next.

Whatever it was, it would cost something. It always cost something. And for the first time, Sefa understood that this time, the price might be paid in a currency she couldn't earn back.
