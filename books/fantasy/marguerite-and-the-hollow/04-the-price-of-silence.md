## 4. The Price of Silence

They took Sefa at the turn of the tide, when the fish market was emptying and the gulls had grown bold enough to walk among the dropped scales and silvered skins. Lior learned of it from a boy who sold eels off a barrow, who tugged Lior's sleeve with the particular urgency of someone paid by the word.

"The girl with the red scarf," he said. "The watch came for her at the salt-stalls. She didn't run fast enough."

Lior's hands did not shake. Twenty years in Vell had taught them that shaking was a luxury, like weeping, like sleep. They pressed a coin into the boy's palm—more than the information was worth, less than it cost them—and walked home through streets that seemed suddenly made of paper, every shutter a watching eye, every doorway a mouth waiting to speak their name.

The loft above the chandlery looked exactly as Sefa had left it. That was the cruelty of it: the half-carved luck-charm on the workbench, wood shavings curled like question marks around her stool; her cloak still hanging on its peg, because she'd gone out thinking the morning was ordinary; the cup of cold willow tea she'd never finished. Lior stood in the middle of all this evidence of interruption and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that they had taught the girl everything except how to be small enough to survive.

No. That wasn't true either. They had taught her to be small. They had simply not taught her well enough—or she had refused, which amounted to the same thing and hurt considerably more.

On the table lay the summons. Not a rough hand on the shoulder, not a sack over the head—the crown of Vell had grown too elegant for that since the Registry Act. A folded paper, sealed with grey wax, informing *the guardian of one Sefa Ilvane* that the aforementioned had been detained under provisions of said Act pending inquiry, and that the guardian might present themselves at the Wardenhouse between the hours of noon and dusk.

Paperwork. Dread always arrived as paperwork now.

Lior read it twice, burned it in the hearth, and watched the ash settle on the coals like grey moths. Then they put on their good coat—the one that said *harmless functionary* in every stitch—and went to keep their appointment with the machinery of the state.

* * *

Captain Aldous Vey received them in a room with no windows, which Lior supposed saved everyone the pretense of daylight. He was younger than his reputation, broad through the shoulders, with the patient face of a man who had learned that silence extracted more confessions than shouting ever had. A single lamp burned between them on the desk, and beside the lamp, hands folded neatly, sat Sefa.

She looked smaller than Lior had ever seen her—not broken, not yet, but folded up carefully, the way one folds a letter one intends to send somewhere terrible. There were tear-tracks dried on her cheeks. When she saw Lior, her chin came up, and something fierce flickered behind the fear, and Lior loved her so much it was physically painful, a pressure behind the sternum like a swallowed coal.

"Master Dunmore." Captain Vey gestured to a chair. "Thank you for coming so promptly."

"I was given little choice."

"There's always choice." He smiled faintly, as if sharing a private joke with himself. "That's rather the point of this meeting."

He let the silence stretch. Lior let it stretch back. Around them the Wardenhouse murmured—boots on stone, a door closing somewhere, the distant clank of the cells below—and Lior catalogued the sounds out of habit, mapping exits they would never use.

"You've been the king's taster for eleven years," Vey said at last. "Impeccable record. No incidents. No illnesses you couldn't explain." He turned a paper on the desk—a ledger page, Lior saw, an old guild roster, the ink faded. "And yet your name appears here. Guild of Practical Arts, admitted nineteen years ago. Apprentice registered under your name two years past." He tapped the page. "Someone forgot to burn this copy."

Lior kept their breathing even. *Never be seen twice.* The rules ran through their head like a litany, useless now, a prayer to a god who'd already read the ending.

"What do you want, Captain?"

"I want what the crown wants. Order." Vey leaned forward, lamplight pooling in the hollows of his face. "The Registry is a kindness, whatever the pamphleteers say. Gifted persons declared, licensed, supervised—no burnings, no disappearances. But the registry only works if it's complete. And there are people in this city who have hidden very well for a very long time." His eyes, pale as riverwater, settled on Lior with the gentleness of a trap closing. "People like you. People who know other people like you."

Sefa made a small sound. Lior didn't look at her. Looking at her would be surrender.

"You want me to give you names."

"I want you to buy back your apprentice. Six names, Master Dunmore. Six declarations, six licenses, six people who go on living under supervision instead of dying in a granary square. That seems to me a fair exchange for one girl's freedom." He slid a fresh sheet of paper across the desk, and a pen, and set the inkwell down with a soft, deliberate click. "You have until dusk. I'm told you're a reasonable man."

Lior looked at the blank page. It was astonishing, really, how much terror could fit into nothing at all.

Six names. And it would not stop at six—Vey knew that, and Lior knew that, and the captain's patient face knew that Lior knew. Names begot names. The first betrayal opened the door and every subsequent one walked through it more easily, until one day Lior would sit in this windowless room trading strangers' lives like coppers, telling themselves each transaction was the last. They had watched it happen to others, in the early years, before the guild scattered. Taster's tongue, they called the gift—but Lior had always known its true shape: the ability to taste what was hidden in a thing. Poison in wine. Rot beneath sweetness. And underneath Captain Vey's reasonable voice, under the fair exchange and the kind registry, Lior tasted something old and vast and hungry, something that wanted not order but *ownership*, and wore the law as a glove to keep its hands clean.

"No," Lior said.

The word fell into the room and lay there. Sefa's breath caught.

Vey's expression didn't change. "Think carefully."

"I have thought carefully for twenty years, Captain. It's most of what I do." Lior rose, slowly, keeping their hands visible and empty. "I won't give you names. Not six. Not one."

"Then the girl goes to the inquiry. You understand what the inquiries produce."

"I understand exactly what they produce. I've smelled the granary square these past three mornings." The words came out flat and steady, and Lior marveled distantly at the stranger speaking them. "Take my name instead. I'm the guildmaster. I trained her. Whatever she did, she learned the shape of it from me. Put *me* on your list."

Something flickered in Vey's eyes—respect, perhaps, or disappointment, or the pleasure of a fisherman feeling the line go taut. "That's not how the exchange works."

"No," Lior agreed. "It isn't, is it? The exchange only works if I value my life more than my soul. I don't." They turned to Sefa, finally, and let themselves look at her—one long moment, memorizing the set of her jaw, the red scarf gone now, confiscated, her neck bare and vulnerable above her collar. "Keep your chin up, child. However it falls. Do you hear me? However it falls."

Sefa nodded, and her eyes were wet and blazing, and Lior walked out of the windowless room without letting their knees learn the truth about them.

* * *

Treason, it turned out, had a smell. Beeswax and dust and old vellum, concentrated in a narrow shop off Tanner's Row where the sign read *OSTWICK — BOOKS BOUND & MENDED* in letters gone soft with weather.

Maren Ostwick was sixty if she was a day, silver-haired, spectacles pushed up into her hair, mending a folio with thread fine as spider silk. She did not look up when the bell over the door rang.

"We're closed."

"I need to know where the Hollow opens."

Now she looked up. Her eyes were the color of strong tea, and they went through Lior like a probe through a lock—testing tumblers, finding the mechanism. "People who say that sentence in Vell generally end up in one of two places," she said. "The granary square, or the Wardenhouse cells. Sometimes both, in sequence."

"My apprentice is in the cells. I intend to prevent the square." Lior closed the door and shot the bolt, a small decisive sound in the dusty quiet. "They tell me you were court archivist once. Before the fall of the covenant. Before they stripped your rank and your library and left you binding ledgers for fishmongers."

Maren set down her needle with great care, as though it were the last fragile thing in a world of fragile things. "Who tells you things, taster?"

"People who remember. People who whisper that the Hollow isn't a story the priests invented to frighten children. That there's a stair, drowned, beneath the old cisterns, and that the covenant-shard still lies where it cracked a hundred years ago. That the reason the king's hunt feels different from every hunt before it—" Lior's voice caught, steadied, "—is that he isn't hunting mages at all. He's hunting the way *down*. And he needs frightened, desperate, gifted people to lead him to it."

The silence stretched. Somewhere outside, a cart rattled past, and the jars on Maren's shelves shivered faintly, catching the light like a congregation holding its breath.

"You taste truth," Maren said at last. It wasn't a question. "That's the rumor. The taster's tongue."

"I taste what's hidden. In food. In men. In myself, these days, which is the least pleasant application." Lior stepped closer. "I tasted the captain's offer, Mistress Ostwick. Underneath the law there is hunger. Old hunger. The kind that cracked something once, and wants to finish the job."

Maren Ostwick removed her spectacles and polished them on her apron, a long unnecessary ritual, buying time the way Lior bought lives with coins and silences. When she spoke again, her voice had changed—lower, wearier, carrying weight it had been setting down and picking up for decades.

"If I tell you where the stair is, you'll go down. And if you go down, you'll find the shard. And if you find the shard, you'll learn what mending costs." She looked up, and her tea-colored eyes were terribly clear. "It doesn't mend cheaply, taster. Nothing down there does. The Hollow remembers every bargain ever struck beneath this city, and it collects."

"I'm not asking what it costs. I'm asking where the door is."

"Aren't you even going to ask why I'd help you? Why a disgraced archivist would hand treason to a stranger?" A smile touched her mouth, dry as old paper. "Everyone asks that."

"Do you want me to ask?"

"No." She rose, joints cracking like green firewood, and crossed to the back of the shop, where bookshelves gave way to a wall hung with maps—city maps, survey maps, and older things, drawn on skins gone amber with age. Her fingers walked along them and stopped. "I helped break it, you know. The covenant. I was young, and certain, and they told me it was protection—for the realm, for the throne, for magic itself, fenced and guarded like a treasury. I drew the seals myself." Her hand hovered over a map of the old cisterns, and Lior saw it tremble, just slightly. "I've spent forty years learning what my certainty purchased. So no, taster. Don't ask why I'd help you. Ask instead whether you're prepared to learn what I learned—that some doors, once opened, cannot be shut again by anyone."

Lior thought of Sefa's bare throat above her collar. Of the smoke drifting over the granary square each dawn. Of a blank page and a pen and a captain's patient voice saying *six names*, and of the answer already given, irrevocable, the first stone of treason laid with open eyes.

"I'm prepared," Lior said, "to pay attention this time."

Maren Ostwick studied them for a long moment. Then she lifted the oldest map from its hook and unrolled it across the workbench, weighting the corners with jars of mending wax, and her finger came down on the heart of Vell—on the drowned quarter, the collapsed cisterns, a place marked with a symbol like a key broken in half.

"The Drowned Stair opens beneath the Saltgate cistern," she said. "At slack water, when the tide forgets to pull. We go tonight."

"*We*," Lior repeated.

"You didn't imagine I'd draw you a map and let you die alone with my secrets?" Maren was already blowing dust from a lantern, her movements brisk now, decided, a woman stepping back into a role she'd rehearsed for forty years. "Besides—I know the way. And you, taster, know how to taste what's hidden. Between us we may survive the descent." She paused, lantern in hand, and her voice dropped. "But understand what we carry. If the shard can be reached, it can be seized. The king's men are searching too—we're not the only ones who remember the stair. Whatever happens down there must happen before they find it. Or after they fail. Never during."

Outside, the light was failing toward dusk—the same dusk that would close Sefa's inquiry, that would decide whether a sixteen-year-old girl with a warming charm and a stubborn heart lived to see morning. Lior buttoned their coat, took up the lantern Maren offered, and felt the weight of it settle into their palm like a verdict.

"Then we'd best not be late," they said, and followed the disgraced archivist out into the darkening streets of Vell, toward the water, and the door beneath it.
