## 5. Down the Drowned Stair

The cistern mouth opened behind the tannery like a wound that had never learned to close.

Lior stood at its lip while Maren Ostwick wrestled a storm-lantern from her satchel, and tried not to think about how many times in twenty years they had walked past this exact stretch of wall—smelling the lime pits, hearing the hides slap against stone—and never once felt what slept beneath. That was the trick of Vell, Lior supposed. The city kept its secrets the way a miser kept coin: pressed close, counted nightly, shown to no one.

"You're stalling," Maren said. She was sixty if she was a day, with a face like crumpled parchment and hands that shook only when she wanted them to. "I've watched you taste poison for a living. I didn't think you'd flinch at a little water."

"I don't flinch at water." Lior tested the first step with one boot. It was slick as oiled glass, cut into the rock at an angle that suggested whoever built it had expected people to descend on their knees. "I flinch at drowning in the dark under a tannery for a girl who thinks I'm coming."

Maren's lantern hissed alight—a charm of her own making, old law, older craft—and threw their shadows long down the stair. "Then don't drown. Walk."

The stair went down farther than it had any right to. Lior counted two hundred steps before the sound of the city died above them, smothered layer by layer: first the gulls, then the market bells, then even the groan of ships against the harbor pilings, until there was nothing but the drip of water and the small wet percussion of their own breathing. The walls wept. Somewhere below, something vast moved with the slow patience of tide in a cave.

"Tell me again," Lior said, because silence in the dark was a luxury they couldn't afford, "how a court archivist knows the way into a place the crown spent a century pretending doesn't exist."

Maren's voice came back flat and echoless, as though the stone were drinking it. "Because I helped them pretend." A pause. The lantern swung. "There are four hundred years of records in the royal vaults, Dunmore. Charters, censuses, grain ledgers, marriage contracts. And exactly one shelf—sealed, unindexed—that holds everything about the Hollow. I catalogued it when I was young and ambitious and too stupid to read what I was filing. By the time I understood, the king's grandfather had already made reading it treason."

"And you read it anyway."

"I read it anyway." Her boots rang on the stone. "That's why they took my chair. Not for heresy. For *filing errors*."

Lior almost smiled. Almost. Then the stair ended.

* * *

The water came up to their thighs and then their waists, black and warm as bathwater left standing too long, and it did not feel like water at all—it felt like being *regarded*. Lior kept one hand on the wall, which was carved here, though the carvings made no sense to the eye: spirals that resolved into faces if stared at too long, faces that resolved back into spirals. Old bargains, maybe. Or old debts.

"The Hollow isn't under Vell," Maren said quietly. "It's *underneath* Vell. There's a difference. The city is built on the floor of it, like a lid on a well. When the covenant held, the two realms shared a border the way neighbors share a fence. When it broke—" she lifted the lantern, and its light fell short, swallowed, "—the fence came down and everyone pretended the yard was empty."

"How did it break?"

"You'll see. It shows you, if you let it." Maren waded on. "That's the part the records don't say plainly. The Hollow has a memory, and it isn't kind."

The passage widened, and the water shallowed, and then they stepped out onto a shore of black glass that stretched away farther than the lantern could reach, beneath a sky that was not a sky—a ceiling of stone hung with pale root-things that glowed faintly, the color of moonlight through milk. Twilight lived here permanently. Lior's skin prickled with it, with the pressure of magic so thick and so old it had stopped feeling like power and started feeling like weather.

And the bargains—

Gods, the bargains.

They stood across the glass plain in their thousands: pillars of translucent stone, each one shaped differently—a handshake frozen mid-clasp, a pair of linked rings, a mother's hand over a child's head, a sheaf of wheat exchanged for a song. Petrified agreements, each one humming faintly when Lior passed, like struck glasses going still. Some were tall as trees. Some were small enough to hold. All of them were *cracked*. Hairline fractures ran through every pillar Lior looked at, and from the cracks seeped a fine grey dust that drifted nowhere, settling on nothing, simply hanging in the air like the memory of snow.

"They're dying," Lior whispered.

"They've been dying for a hundred years." Maren walked among them without touching, and Lior noticed—for the first time—that she was crying without any change in her expression at all. "Every broken promise between human and fae since the Sundering. They don't vanish. They petrify. And every mage born above us since has been born with a splinter of this in their blood. Your gift, my gift, Sefa's gift—we're all just echoes of bargains that used to be alive."

One pillar stood apart from the rest, near the plain's center, and toward it the ground sloped as though the whole realm leaned in to listen.

* * *

The covenant-shard was the size of a cathedral door and the shape of a broken heart, if a heart could be made of light packed into crystal. It rose from the glass plain on a stump of its own shattered root, and the crack through its center was wide enough to put a fist inside. From the crack poured not dust but *darkness*—a slow exhalation of shadow that curled upward and dispersed, and Lior understood, with a lurch, that this was where the fear came from. This was what the king's hunts fed on. Every pyre in the granary square, every name on the Registry, every terrified mage hiding their glow—the smoke of all that suffering drifted down here somehow, and the shard drank it, and the crack grew.

"It can be mended," Maren said. Her voice had gone strange, formal, the voice of someone reciting. "That's what the sealed shelf says. One keeper, whole and willing, pours their gift into the fracture. The gift becomes the mortar. The keeper becomes—" she stopped.

"The keeper becomes what?"

Maren turned to look at her, and in the shard's pale light her eyes were ancient and terribly sorry. "The seal. Forever. You don't die, Dunmore. That would be mercy. You become the lock. Conscious. Holding. For as long as the covenant stands—which, done properly, is forever."

The plain was silent. Even the whispering stone seemed to lean closer.

Lior approached the shard. Up close it was warm, and it *knew* them—they felt it sort through them the way a customs officer sorts through a traveler's bags, finding the tasting-gift folded small in a corner, the luck-charms, the immunity tonics, the twenty years of careful nothing. It lingered on all of it, gently, the way one touches a thing before taking it.

And Lior understood what Maren was really saying. *Someone must hold the door shut forever.* Someone must stand in the dark beneath the tannery, alone, for centuries, so that a fishmonger's daughter could have her small luck and a freezing child could be warmed. Someone must be the mortar.

They thought of Sefa, sixteen and reckless and rotting in a crown cell because she'd warmed a beggar's hands.

"No," Lior said.

"Dunmore—"

"No." Their voice cracked against the crystal and the crystal rang with it, a low bell-note that traveled out across the plain, and every petrified bargain hummed in answer, thousands of dead promises singing one mournful chord. "You helped break this. Fine. You'll help mend it. But I will not hand my gift—or anyone's—to a single lock, no matter how willing. We tried that. That's how kings get ideas. That's how we got *him*." They laid a palm flat against the shard, and it pulsed beneath their hand like a sleeping animal. "There has to be another way. There's always another way. Find me the page that says there isn't, and I'll read it aloud."

Maren held their gaze for a long moment. Then she reached into her coat and drew out a folio wrapped in oilcloth—brought all this way, Lior realized, carried down two hundred steps and through black water, because she had known, had known since the granary square, what Lior would say.

"There is a page," she said. "But Dunmore—it requires the coronation rite. In nine days, the king means to seize the covenant whole. If we're going to do anything—" she nodded upward, toward the distant weight of Vell, toward the cells, toward Sefa, "—we do it during his ceremony. From inside it."

Above them, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, the shard's darkness breathed out, and breathed in.

And somewhere in the dark beyond the glowing roots, something that had been listening began, very softly, to laugh.
