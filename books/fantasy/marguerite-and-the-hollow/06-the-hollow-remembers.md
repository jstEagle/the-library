## 6. The Hollow Remembers

The shard did not want to be touched.

Maren had known that the way one knows a dog is not safe to pet—not from any sign it gave, but from the quality of the silence around it. The Hollow held its breath. Even the whispering stone, which had murmured ceaselessly since they'd come down the drowned stair, went quiet when she stepped toward the pedestal of fused glass and salt where the covenant-shard hung suspended in nothing at all.

It was smaller than she remembered. That was the first cruelty of it. Sixty years ago, when she had stood in this same chamber with ink still fresh on her court credentials and her whole life arranged before her like a banquet, the shard had seemed vast—a pillar of light half-drowned in the dark, humming with the weight of every bargain ever struck between human hands and fae blood. She had been twenty-three. She had thought herself capable of judging it.

Now it was a splinter. A sliver of pale crystal no longer than her forearm, cracked down its length like ice on a spring pond, turning slowly in air that did not move. The crack wept light. Not brightly—thinly, the way a wound weeps—and each drop of light fell into the dark below the pedestal and did not land anywhere at all.

"Maren." Lior's voice came from behind her, careful, the voice of someone approaching a stranger's horse. "You've gone white as the walls."

"I'm fine."

"You're lying."

"Yes," she said. "I am. It's an old habit. I was good at it, once. It's why they sent me."

She heard Lior shift their weight but not retreat. In the two days since the cistern, since the flooded stair and the petrified bargains—those horrifying statues of glass and stone where old agreements between mages and the fae had frozen mid-handshake, mid-kneel, mid-embrace—the taster had learned when to push and when to wait. They waited now. It was one of the things Maren had begun, against all her instincts, to trust about them.

The chamber around them was a cathedral of failure. Walls of black stone veined with dead silver. Arches that had been carved to hold names and now held only grooves, scraped smooth by some patient erosion or some deliberate erasure. And everywhere, under everything, the whispering—that dry, sibilant susurrus, like pages turning in a library no one tended anymore.

Maren pulled off her right glove. Her hand, beneath it, was an archivist's hand: ink-stained knuckles, a scar across the palm from a bad binding-spill in her thirtieth year. It shook. She let it shake.

"When I was young," she said, to the shard rather than to Lior, because it was easier, "the crown had a problem. There were too many mages, and too many of them answered to something other than the king. Guilds. Families. The fae courts beneath the hill country. Magic in Vell was like water—it pooled wherever it wanted, and everyone drank from everyone else's well. The old covenant kept it civil. Every gifted person carried a spark of the covenant's keeping, and the keeping was shared, and because it was shared, no one could hoard it. No one could *own* it." Her laugh came out wrong, a dry click. "Do you see the flaw yet? I didn't. I was twenty-three and I thought shared meant *unguarded*."

Lior said nothing. Somewhere far above, muffled through a hundred feet of stone and city, Vell went about its morning. Bells. Gulls. The fish market opening its throats.

"The king's advisors—my advisors, by then; I'd risen fast, I was clever, I was *useful*—came to me with a proposal. Consolidation. One vessel instead of ten thousand sparks. One keeper, sworn to the crown, holding the whole of the covenant's power so that no foreign court, no rogue guild, no ambitious hedge-witch could ever twist it. We called it protection." She swallowed. "We called it *safety*. You have to understand how frightened we were. There had been incidents. A guild-mage in Corvess who burned a granary district. A fae-touched child who drowned a village in sleep. Real horrors, real fear—we weren't monsters, Lior, we were *frightened*, and fear is so much worse than malice because it wears reason's clothes."

"You broke it," Lior said quietly. Not accusing. Just placing the stone on the table where it had always sat.

"Six of us. Hand-picked. We came down the stair on the night of the long eclipse, and we performed the Sundering Rite, and it was beautiful." Her voice cracked on the word and she hated it. "That's the thing no one tells you. Ruin is *beautiful* while you're making it. The shard rose up out of the pedestal and sang, and we caught the light in six vessels, and we poured it into the king's keeping-vault, and the covenant cracked—" she gestured at the splinter turning before them, "—like that. Like ice. Like bone. And we walked back up the stair believing we had saved the realm."

"And what happened to the light?"

"It curdled." The whispering stone seemed to lean closer. "Power that is owned does not stay pure. It takes the shape of its container. Within five years the king's vault-mages were doing things the old guilds would never have dared—binding gifts, burning resisters, feeding fear into the keeping until the whole of it turned the color of rust. And the fae—" Maren closed her eyes. "The fae withdrew entirely. Every door shut. Every bargain voided. The petrified ones out there in the galleries? Those aren't monuments. They're *casualties*. Agreements that died mid-word when the covenant broke, freezing everyone who held them."

She opened her eyes and stepped forward, and this time she did touch the shard.

It was cold the way deep water is cold, and it knew her instantly. Light flared along the crack—not pain, exactly, but recognition, the terrible intimacy of being remembered by something you wronged. The chamber vanished.

* * *

She was twenty-three again, standing in this chamber with five companions whose faces she had spent sixty years refusing to recall. Aldous with his careful spectacles. Yeva laughing as she chalked the circle. The others, blurred now, mercifully or not. The shard above them whole and singing, and her own young voice steady as she spoke the opening words of the Sundering Rite: *For the safety of the realm, for the containing of peril, for one hand to hold what many hands might misuse.*

The vision did not linger there. It dragged her forward, through the years, fast and merciless as a current.

The vault beneath the palace, where the six vessels sat in a row, and the young king's eyes reflecting their glow as he learned, slowly, what he now possessed. The first binding-order signed in his name. The first mage dragged from her home for refusing to surrender her gift to crown registration—Maren had drafted that registry herself, her own handwriting marching down the page, name after name after name.

The fae doors closing. She saw the last one: a door of woven hawthorn in the hill country, and a figure on the far side of it—tall, antlered, ancient—looking at her with an expression she had never managed to forget and never managed to describe. Not hatred. Something worse. *Disappointment.* The door shut, and the sound of it was the sound the Hollow had been whispering ever since, over and over, sixty years of one syllable: *no, no, no, no.*

Then the corruption, close-up. Vault-mages feeding on fear the way ticks feed on blood. The keeping-vessel darkening from silver to rust to black. Executions dressed up as justice. And threaded through it all, faint but unmistakable, the covenant's original purpose still trying to function—still trying to distribute itself, still straining toward every gifted soul in Vell like a mother reaching for children carried out of her arms—and failing, failing, cracking a little more with each failure.

The vision released her.

Maren came back to herself on her knees on the black stone, both gloves off now, both palms pressed flat to the floor, gasping. Lior was beside her—one hand hovering near her shoulder, not quite touching, waiting for permission. She gave it with a jerk of her chin, and the hand settled, warm and human and steadying.

"How long?" she rasped.

"Minutes. It felt longer from the outside too—you were talking. To it. Or it was talking through you." Lior's face was pale but composed, the professional calm of a man who tastes poison for a living. "What did it show you?"

"Everything. It keeps accounts." She pushed herself upright, knees aching, and looked at the shard with wet eyes and something new in her chest—not dread, not anymore. Purpose, ground fine and sharp as flour. "Lior, listen to me. The plan we brought down here—mend the crack, restore the covenant, put it back the way it was. I need you to hear what I'm about to say, because I'm the only one alive who knows it's true."

"Say it."

"The covenant as it was can't be restored. Not because the crack is too deep. Because the original design was the flaw. Ten thousand sparks, yes—but all of it *held*, all of it *kept*, a single keeping that anyone strong enough could seize. That's what my king took. That's what your king is trying to take now. If we mend it whole, we're just building a better vault for the next thief." She wiped her face with the back of her wrist, unashamed. "I helped break it open. I think—I think breaking it open was the only part we got right. We just didn't go far enough. We left the keeping intact behind the crack, sitting in the dark, waiting for a hand."

Lior stared at her. She watched understanding arrive in them slowly, the way dawn arrives over water—and watched, too, the fear arrive just behind it, because understanding meant cost.

"You're saying the mending isn't a repair," Lior said. "It's a *redistribution*. Every spark given away. Permanently. To everyone."

"To every gifted soul in Vell. Held by thousands of hearts, owned by none. Ungovernable. Unstealable." Maren laughed again, and this time it almost sounded like laughter. "Sixty years ago I would have called that chaos. Now I know it's the only shape of safety there is. But it will need kindling, Lior. The crack won't widen on its own. Someone will have to feed their own gift into the fracture to split it wide—and no one knows yet what that costs, or whether the one who pays it survives the payment."

Above them—faint, distant, impossible—came a sound neither of them could mistake. Bells. Not the measured peal of morning bells, but the urgent, overlapping clamor of alarm, rolling down through a hundred feet of stone and soil, and beneath it, thinner, a smell that did not belong in the Hollow at all: smoke.

Lior was already on their feet, face turned upward toward the drowned stair. "Sefa," they breathed. "The hunt—something's happening up there. Right now."

Maren rose more slowly, and behind her the shard turned in its nothing, its crack glowing faintly brighter, as if it too had heard the bells—and understood that its hour was coming.
