## 7. Ashes on the Registry

The smoke reached Lior before the news did.

It came threading down through the cistern grates in the old tannery district, a gray ribbon of it, sweetish and wrong—green wood burned wet to make it last. Lior stood at the mouth of the drowned stair with Maren Ostwick's lantern guttering beside them and knew, the way one knows rain coming in the knees, that something above had changed.

They had been three days in the undercity. Three days of whispering stone and petrified bargains, of learning what the covenant-shard demanded and what it would cost, and in three days the world overhead had curdled.

"We go up," Lior said.

Maren looked at them with her archivist's flat gray eyes. "You heard it too."

"Everyone heard it." The Hollow carried sound strangely; grief traveled down here better than light did. All night the stone had murmured with a sound like a crowd, vast and rhythmic, and Lior had told themselves it was water. It was not water. Water did not chant.

* * *

They surfaced into evening, into a city wearing ash like snowfall.

Lior climbed out of the cistern behind the tannery and stood in the alley breathing it—the char-smell of burned thatch and burned hair and burned something else they refused to name—and watched flakes of gray settle on their sleeves. Down the lane, a woman was sweeping her doorstep with the grim efficiency of someone who would have to do it again tomorrow. She did not look up. Nobody in Vell was looking up anymore.

The granary square. Of course it was the granary square. It was the largest open space in the city, built for the weighing and measuring of bread, and there was a bitter rightness in that—a place where hunger had been managed now repurposed for the management of fear. Lior walked toward it the way you walk toward a wound you already know is there, because not looking is its own kind of rot.

The crowd was thinning but not gone. Hundreds still pressed against the barricades, held back by watchmen in the king's new livery—black and gold, the Registry colors—and beyond them, in the center of the square, stood the pyres.

Three of them. Still standing, still smoking, iron stakes black against a bruised sky.

Lior had seen executions before. Vell hanged thieves and beheaded traitors with a certain municipal briskness, an unpleasantness conducted at dawn and swept away by noon. This was different. This had been made to last. The pyres were built low and wide, designed to burn slowly, and the crowd had been kept close enough to see faces. Close enough to hear.

A fishwife near the barricade was telling her neighbor, in the flat recitation of someone who has repeated it too many times: "They didn't scream, the first one. An old man. He just stood there and the fire went up around him and he *sang*. Some kind of old words. And then—" she faltered, then steadied, because the story wanted telling, "—and then the second one screamed, and everyone wished she hadn't, and the third one was a boy. Couldn't have been more than twenty. He called out for his mother at the end."

The neighbor said nothing. The fishwife said nothing more. Around them the crowd wore the particular silence of people memorizing horror so they can recognize it when it comes for them.

Lior stood among them with their hood up and their hands in their pockets, hands that could have—should have—done something, hands that had spent twenty years learning exactly how much power it took to warm a beggar child and exactly how little of it a person could show before the watch came. Twenty years of never glow, never heal, never be seen twice. And all of it, every careful rule, every swallowed gift, had bought nothing but this: a city of ash and memorized screams.

An old man who sang. A woman who screamed. A boy who called for his mother.

Lior turned away before their face could betray anything worth betraying.

* * *

The notice was posted on the granary wall itself, fresh paste still gleaming, and Lior read it twice because the first time their eyes refused the arithmetic.

BY ROYAL DECREE OF THE REGISTRY ACT — the names of the condemned, seven of them, printed in the crown's tidy hand. Seven mages burned in a single afternoon. Below the names, a longer list: the *suspected*, those whose declarations had come too late or whose neighbors' testimony had come too eagerly. That list ran to forty names. Lior scanned it with the numb competence of long practice and found no one they loved on it, which was the only mercy the day offered, and even that felt provisional.

Then, at the bottom, a separate notice, smaller, as if embarrassed:

*Halvard Brine, food-taster to His Majesty's household, executed this day for the crime of sorcerous tampering with the royal table.*

Lior read the name four times.

Halvard. Old Halvard, who had taught Lior the immunity tonics back when Lior was new to the palace kitchens and terrified of getting the dosages wrong. Halvard, who chewed each sample with theatrical slowness and winked at the undercooks. Halvard, who had once covered for Lior when a charm slipped from Lior's sleeve onto the flagstones and glowed faintly blue—had kicked it into the slop pail without breaking his monologue about the proper storage of eel, and had never once mentioned it after. He had known. He had known for fifteen years and had said nothing, because that was the code of the small and the careful: we protect each other by pretending not to see.

And they had burned him in the granary square for sorcery he had never possessed, on evidence Lior could guess at—a tonic mislabeled, a stomach upset in some minor lord, a servant eager to please. Halvard had died wearing Lior's crime, or something shaped like it, and Lior had been underground when it happened, learning how to save everyone except the one man who had saved them first.

Lior's knees did not buckle. They had survived two decades in Vell by knowing how to stand still while everything inside them moved. But something in their chest closed like a fist around a coal.

"You knew him," Maren said quietly. She had come up behind them; her eyes were on the notice, on Halvard's name.

"He covered for me once. Fifteen years ago." Lior's voice came out level, which felt like a betrayal of itself. "He wasn't gifted. He was just kind."

"The crown doesn't distinguish," Maren said. "That's rather the point."

And there it was—the thought Lior had been circling since the cistern, since the chanting stone, since the smoke. They looked again at the square, at the crowd being dispersed with unhurried efficiency, at the watchmen who were not gloating but *documenting*, taking names of the mourners, sketching faces. At the clerks already chalking new lists onto boards.

If the king wanted mages dead, he need only kill them. Quietly, even. A knife in a cellar costs less than a pyre.

But pyres draw crowds. Crowds feel fear. And fear—

Maren followed their gaze and finished the thought aloud, because she had been a court mage once and understood the machinery of power the way Lior understood poison. "Fear has weight," she said. "Down there, in the Hollow, everything has weight. Grief. Terror. The shard drinks it. A century ago the covenant was fed by bargain and trust, freely given, and it held. Break it, starve it, and it will take whatever pours into it instead." Her jaw tightened. "He isn't hunting mages. He's *feeding* something. Every burning, every name on every list, every neighbor turned informer—it all runs downhill, into the dark, into the crack where the covenant broke. He's fattening the Hollow so that when he comes to claim what's left of it, it will know his hand. It will open for him."

Lior stared at her. "You're saying the executions are a harvest."

"I'm saying I helped break that covenant believing I was protecting the realm from exactly this," Maren said, "and watching him do it in three weeks what took us a year—I understand now that we were amateurs. He doesn't want to mend the covenant. He wants to own what remains of it entire, and terror is simply faster than trust."

The coal in Lior's chest stopped being grief and became something with edges.

They thought of Sefa, sixteen and reckless, somewhere in the cells beneath the Registry hall, and whether she was on a list yet, and whether the lists were moving toward pyres on a schedule. They thought of the fishmonger's daughter with her luck-charm, of every marked mage in Vell who had declared out of terror or hidden out of terror, both roads leading to the same square. They thought of Halvard chewing slowly, winking, kicking a glowing charm into the slop pail without a word.

Never glow. Never heal. Never be seen twice. The rules had kept Lior alive, and keeping Lior alive had been the whole of the rules' ambition. They had never once kept anyone else alive. They had never once kept *Vell* alive.

That ended today.

"Maren." Lior pulled the hood lower and turned from the square, from the ash, from the chalked lists. "How fast can we get back down?"

"Why?"

"Because if fear feeds the Hollow, then fear is a weapon pointed at all of us, and I'm done letting other people hold it." Lior's voice stayed quiet—they were, after all, still in the street, still marked by association with a dead man's name—but Maren heard what was underneath it, the thing that had been growing for three days in the dark. "I've spent twenty years tasting the king's food. I know exactly how a man poisons himself slowly and calls it strength. He's doing it to the whole city." A breath. "We go down tonight. Before the next burning. Because I don't think there's time left to be careful, and I am *finished* with surviving."

Maren studied them for a long moment—the disgraced archivist and the outlaw taster, standing in ash, planning treason in front of a wall of the dead. Then she nodded once and turned toward the tannery lanes.

Behind them, the clerks were chalking tomorrow's list onto the boards. Lior did not look back to see if Sefa's name was on it.

They already knew it would be.
