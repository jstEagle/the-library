## 8. The Bargain Refused

The cell smelled of wet stone and old salt, and Sefa had learned, in the nine days since they took her, to count the drips.

There were forty-one between the ceiling crack and the floor puddle before the sound changed—forty-one drips, then a longer silence where the water gathered itself again. She counted them the way Lior had taught her to count breaths when a charm was building: not to hurry it along, but to know exactly where she was inside it. *You are here,* the counting said. *You are still here.*

She was still here. That was the whole of her victory so far, and she wore it like a coat too large for her, because it was all she had.

They had taken her boots at the gate of the watch-house, and her belt with its hidden pockets, and the little tin of ash-paste Lior kept for masking a glow under the skin. They had missed nothing that mattered, though. They could not have known what mattered. The thing that mattered was folded small behind her left ear, no bigger than a grain of barley: a knot of warmth Lior had tied there two winters ago, on a night Sefa had cried herself sick over a dead gull on the pier. *A keeper-knot,* Lior had called it, tying it with two fingers and a wordless pressure Sefa had never been able to name. *It won't do anything grand. It'll just remember you're warm, when you forget.*

It remembered now. In the cold of the cell, in the hour before dawn when the damp came up through the flagstones like something rising from a grave, the knot behind her ear would stir—a faint, stubborn heat, no more than a candle's memory—and Sefa would press her cheek to her own shoulder as if leaning against someone standing beside her, and endure.

Nine days. They had asked her questions for six of them. On the seventh day, the questions stopped, and that frightened her worse than the questions, because in Vell everyone knew what it meant when the asking stopped. It meant they had decided what you were, and were only waiting for the paperwork.

She heard the boots before she saw the light. Two pairs, unhurried, coming down the corridor of cells—the corridor they called the Throat, because of how the sound carried down it, every cough and prayer of every prisoner funneled into one shared misery. Sefa sat up on the plank bed and made herself still. Not calm. Still. There was a difference, and Lior had taught her that too, though she hadn't understood it until now.

The key turned. The door opened onto lantern-light and the captain of the watch, and beside him a woman Sefa had never seen: grey-gloved, grey-cloaked, with a chain of office around her neck worked in the shape of interlocked rings. A registrar's chain. Sefa had seen that shape once before, stamped in wax on the notice-board by the fish market, beneath the words REGISTRY ACT, beneath the list of names that had made Lior burn paper in the dark of their shop while Sefa watched and asked too many questions.

"Sit," the captain said, though she was already sitting.

The registrar did not sit. She stood in the doorway with the lantern held high, and she looked at Sefa the way Lior looked at a charm mid-making—not unkindly, but with an appraiser's patience, weighing what the thing might become.

"Sefa Vellane," the registrar said. "Sixteen years of age. Taken in the fish market at dusk, nine days past, in the act of unlawful working." She said it flatly, a clerk reciting. "The child you warmed lived. The watchmen who saw you are certain of what they saw. You will not be able to talk your way out of it, so I advise you not to try."

Sefa said nothing. Her heart was going like a drum in a festival crowd, but her hands, resting open on her knees, were still. She was almost proud of them.

"You've been quiet for six days of questioning," the registrar went on. "That ends today, because today I'm not here to question you. I'm here to make you an offer." She let the lantern light fall full across Sefa's face, studying her. "Swear your gift to the crown."

The words dropped into the cell like a stone into a well, and Sefa listened for the bottom of them.

"The Registry Act has provisions most people never read past the first page," the registrar continued. "Declaration is one path. But there is another. A gifted person who swears formally—oath, blood-mark, binding rite—becomes a licensed instrument of the realm. Freed. Robed. Fed. Your record struck clean. No granary square. No pyre." She paused, and her voice softened by a degree, which was somehow worse than if it had hardened. "You are sixteen. The crown does not want to burn children. It wants to *use* them. Swear, and by week's end you'll sleep in a warm room with a lock on the *outside* of the door, and everything you can do will finally be worth something."

*Worth something.* Sefa felt the phrase snag on something inside her, sharp as a fishhook.

She thought of the beggar child in the fish market, the girl whose lips had gone blue, whose small fists had stopped gripping. She thought of the warming charm rising off her palms like breath on a winter window, and the look on the child's face—not gratitude, not yet, just the dumb animal astonishment of pain ending. She thought of how the charm had felt in her hands: right. Like a key turning in a lock it had always been meant to turn.

And she thought of what the registrar was actually offering. Not freedom. A longer leash.

"I want to see Lior Dunmore," Sefa said.

"Lior Dunmore is being sought," the registrar said, "on charges of sedition, concealment, and trafficking in outlawed workings. If they come forward, they will be treated according to law." Something flickered behind her eyes—hunger, maybe, or hope. "Is that a yes? Do you know where they are?"

"No," Sefa said. And then, because lying badly was worse than not lying at all: "I don't know where they are. But I know they wouldn't want me to swear."

"They're not here to ask," the registrar said. "You are."

*You are.* And that was the hook catching her properly now, tearing free, and Sefa found—to her own surprise—that she was angry. Not the hot stupid anger that got people hanged. A cold, clean anger, the kind Lior must have carried for twenty years like a stone in the boot.

"When I was ten," Sefa said, "Lior told me why they never healed anyone. Never once, not even the harbormaster's son who would have died without it. I asked them how they could live with that. And Lior said—" she made herself say it exactly, made herself hear it again in Lior's dry, tired voice— "*Because the first time they thank you, you belong to them. And the second time, you belong to whoever they answer to. Magic isn't the danger, Sefa. Ownership is the danger.*"

The registrar was silent a moment. Then: "Pretty words from a woman who sold luck-charms to fishwives and hid like a rat for twenty years."

"Yes," Sefa said. "She hid. So that no one could own her. And now you're offering me a warm room and clean sheets if I let the king own me." She lifted her chin. "I watched a freezing child stop shivering because of my hands. Nobody paid me. Nobody ordered me. Nobody owned that. And I'm not going to trade it for a robe and a leash."

The registrar studied her for a long moment. Whatever she had expected—tears, bargaining, the quick collapse of the young—she had not, apparently, expected this: a sixteen-year-old sitting very straight on a plank bed in a freezing cell, refusing with the same weary steadiness as someone twice her age.

"You understand what refusal means," the registrar said quietly. "Under the Act, an undeclared gifted person who refuses declaration—" She didn't finish it. She didn't need to. Everyone in Vell knew what the granary square was for.

"I understand," Sefa said.

The registrar turned to go. At the door she paused, and without looking back, said: "The Dunmore woman. Or man. Or whatever it is. Did it teach you that speech, or did you come to it yourself?"

"It taught me the words," Sefa said. "I chose them myself."

The door closed. The key turned. The boots receded up the Throat, and Sefa sat in the dark with her heart hammering and her hands shaking now that it was over, shaking the way they always shook after, and she pressed her cheek to her shoulder until the keeper-knot warmed, and she laughed—one short, cracked, astonished laugh—at herself, at all of it.

She had refused. She had actually refused.

* * *

The strange thing about the Throat was that it carried sound both ways.

Sefa had discovered this on her third day, weeping into her knees, when she'd heard—clearly, horribly—a man three cells down sobbing out a name over and over, some wife or mother, and had realized with a lurch that he could probably hear her too. The guards knew it as well; it was part of the punishment, prisoners forced to hear each other's grief with no way to reach each other. But the guards, Sefa had begun to suspect, did not listen closely enough to what else traveled down the Throat.

Words. Whispers. Names.

On her first night back from the registrar's visit, she lay awake replaying her own refusal, wincing at the clumsy parts, and heard, drifting through the wall, a voice so soft she nearly missed it:

"...refused. The Dunmore kid refused them. Swear to the crown, and she told them no."

Another voice, older, cracked: "No. Not the Dunmore kid. She's just the apprentice."

"She's sixteen," the first voice said, wondering. "Sixteen, and she told the registrar no to her face."

Sefa lay very still in the dark, listening to the Throat carry her refusal cell to cell like a current carrying seeds.

By morning, it had changed shape, the way stories do. By the second morning, she heard a woman in a far cell whispering it to a new arrival—that the apprentice of Lior Dunmore had been offered her freedom and had laughed in the registrar's face (she had not laughed; she wished she had). By the third morning, someone had added that Lior themselves had been offered the same bargain years ago, by a different king, and had refused that too—which was not true, as far as Sefa knew, but she found she did not want to correct it. Some lies were load-bearing. Some stories were charms, and the Throat was full of people who needed warming.

On the fourth night, she began to work.

She couldn't work magic; the keeper-knot was all she had, and it kept only warmth. But there were other kinds of making. She had spent two years in Lior's shop learning how charm-sellers talked—how you built belief in small doses, how you gave a frightened person one true thing to hold. And the Throat gave her an audience of the frightened, all of them within whispering distance, all of them hungry for one true thing.

She started with the man three cells down, the one who sobbed his wife's name. His name was Corvin; she learned it through the wall, trading whispers in the drip-counted dark. He was a mender—bone-knitter, hedge-surgeon, the kind of gift that got you burned fastest of all because it was the hardest to hide. He had lasted four days of questioning before breaking, and he had given them three names, and he could not forgive himself, and he was waiting to die.

"Then don't die uselessly," Sefa whispered to the wall. "Listen. When they question you again—and they will, they always come back—you give them names. Wrong ones. Dead ones. Men who moved to Saltmarsh years ago. Women married out of the city. Give them names so confidently they write them down. Every wrong name is a mage walking free tonight."

A long silence. Then, hoarse: "They check the rolls."

"Let them check. A dead man in Saltmarsh costs them a rider's day. A rider's day is a day someone spends running instead of burning." She pressed her palm flat against the cold stone, as if she could pass the warmth of the keeper-knot through it. "Corvin. You broke once because you're human. Break *well* this time."

She heard him breathing on the other side of the wall, fast and shallow, thinking. And then, so quietly she almost lost it in the drip-count: "There's a girl in the end cell. They took her yesterday. Fourteen, maybe. She hasn't stopped crying."

"Tell her," Sefa said, "that a sixteen-year-old refused the registrar to her face and is still alive. Tell her the story travels. Tell her stories are the one charm they haven't figured out how to confiscate."

* * *

It grew the way mold grows in a damp cellar: slowly, invisibly, everywhere at once.

The woman in the far cell, Brava, who could find lost things, began humming when the guards came—little tunes, meaningless-seeming, that were actually signals. Three notes meant questioning tomorrow. A descending scale meant someone had broken. A rising one meant someone had held. The prisoners of the Throat learned the code in two nights, and after that, no one faced the registrar's chair alone, even alone.

Corvin fed the guards wrong names with such sorrowful conviction that they brought him extra bread, believing him cooperative, and somewhere across Vell a bone-mender named Aldsey Row—who had died of fever two winters past, and did not know she was saving lives from beyond it—was reported hiding in the tannery district, and two riders wasted a day finding nothing.

The crying girl in the end cell—Pell, fourteen, a finder-of-water, useless to the crown and therefore doomed—stopped crying on the sixth night. Sefa heard the change through five walls of stone: the silence where the sobbing had been, and then, unbelievably, humming. Brava's rising scale. *Someone held. Someone held.*

And underneath it all, traveling the Throat night after night, growing richer and less true and more necessary with every retelling, ran the story: the apprentice of Lior Dunmore, offered freedom, refused. Lior Dunmore, offered everything, refused. Refusal spreading cell to cell like the warmth behind Sefa's ear—small, stubborn, remembering people that they were warm when they forgot.

On the ninth night, Sefa sat against the wall with her eyes closed, conducting her strange orchestra of whispers, and felt something she had no charm for: not hope exactly, but its skeleton. The sense that courage was not a thing you found in yourself, blazing up like her fish-market charm. It was a thing you caught, the way you caught a fever—from proximity, from story, from the voice of someone in the next cell saying *hold, hold, hold.*

She was still holding. They were all still holding.

And then, on the tenth night, the Throat went silent—not the ordinary silence of exhausted sleep, but a listening silence, dozens of people awake and straining toward the same sound. Boots above. Many boots. The groan of the great gate at the top of the stair, and voices—excited voices, frightened voices—of guards running, and through it all, threading down the Throat like smoke, a single sentence passed mouth to mouth, cell to cell, until it reached Sefa and made her press both hands over her mouth:

*"They've taken the palace. There's fire under the city. The Hollow is open—the Hollow is open, and Dunmore is in it."*
