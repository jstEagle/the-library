## 10. Many Hands

The morning bell in Vell rang differently now. Lior had noticed it first in the weeks after the Shattering Hour—the same bronze, the same cracked note at the third stroke, but the sound carried farther, or perhaps the city simply listened better. Once, that bell had been the crown's voice, telling the fish market when to open and the granary square when to clear for executions. Now it rang because the harbor guild paid for its rope, and anyone could stand beneath it and hear their own name called across the water.

Lior still woke before dawn. Some habits were not chains; they were bones.

The taster's kitchen behind the palace wall smelled of rosemary and woodsmoke, as it had for twenty years. But the door stood unlocked now, and there was a second chair at the table, and pinned above the hearth—not the king's seal, which Lior had burned along with the old guild ledger, but a small wooden plaque carved by Sefa's own hand: *POISON IS A CROWN'S OLDEST RECIPE. TASTE ANYWAY.*

"You're brooding," said Odile from the doorway, carrying a tray of eggs. She had taken over the immunity tonics since the Registry fell, her steady hands measuring valerian and willow bark without a flicker of fear. She was not gifted. She had never needed to be. "You always brood before you taste for him."

"I'm not brooding. I'm remembering."

"Remembering is brooding with better posture." She set down the tray and poured the tea. "Well? Is today the day he poisons himself?"

Lior smiled and took up the silver spoon. The ritual was unchanged: touch tongue to each dish, wait the counted breaths, read the body's quiet report. Bitter almond absent. Rot absent. The mineral tang of the palace cisterns, present as always, harmless as ever. What had changed was everything around the ritual. Once, Lior had tasted alone in a locked room, a secret swallowed twice daily—once the food, once the self. Now any citizen could request the day's tasting record posted at the gatehouse, ink still wet, so that no kitchen, no steward, no king could quietly dispose of an inconvenient appetite.

The king still ate. The king still reigned, diminished and watchful, his coronation-rite shattered into ten thousand sparks that had settled like warm snow over every gifted soul in Vell. He had signed the Repeal with a hand that trembled—not with age, Lior thought, but with the particular tremor of a man who has learned that power hoarded can be taken, and power shared cannot be held hostage. Whether he had learned wisdom or merely arithmetic remained an open question. Lior tasted his food every morning precisely because the question stayed open.

"Harmless," Lior announced, and Odile chalked it on the public slate by the door: *Day 214. All clear.*

* * *

Sefa's school occupied the old salt warehouse on Tanner's Row, and by midmorning its windows glowed.

That was the miracle Lior still could not look at directly, even now, even seven months on: light in the windows, unhidden, unashamed. Children of the fish market sat cross-legged on the floor learning to hold a warming charm steady between their palms while Sefa walked the rows barefoot, sixteen going on seventeen, her braid singed at one end from a lesson that had gone enthusiastically wrong. Through the open door drifted her voice—patient, wry, occasionally exasperated in exactly the way Lior's had once been patient, wry, and exasperated at her.

"No, Pip, you don't *push* the warmth out, you *invite* it. Magic isn't a dog you drag on a leash. It's a dog you feed. Feed it badly and it bites someone. Feed it well and—" she paused, and Lior, lingering in the doorway with a basket of apples for the students, heard the smile enter her voice, "—and it stays because it wants to."

"And where did the spark come from?" asked a small girl with herring scales still stuck in her hair.

"From everyone," Sefa said. "From a woman named Maren who broke something on purpose, and a taster who broke themselves open instead of letting one man swallow the whole sky. You're holding a piece of that. So is your mum. So is the man who sells oysters. Nobody owns it. Everybody keeps it."

Lior set the basket inside the door and slipped away before Sefa could make them stay for the recitation, as she did every week, standing them before thirty children to tell the story of the Hollow. It was not modesty—or not only modesty. It was that the story sounded different in Sefa's mouth. In Lior's mouth it was a confession. In hers it was an inheritance, and inheritances belonged to the children, not to the one who'd carried them through the dark.

Still, walking back along the quay, Lior felt the spark settle warmly under the breastbone, as it did whenever it was acknowledged. Ten thousand sparks, the archivists estimated—one for every gifted person in Vell and half the surrounding coast, plus a surplus that pooled in odd places: a lighthouse lamp that never needed oil, a bakery oven that forgave neglect, a stray cat on Chandler's Lane who glowed faintly when pleased and had become insufferable about it. No ledger recorded them. That was the point. The covenant's keeping lived in bodies now, unwritten, unverifiable, impossible to seize. A king could burn a registry. He could not burn a city's bloodstream.

* * *

Maren Ostwick's archive had no lock.

Lior visited on Thursdays, ostensibly to deliver the week's tasting records for preservation—a citizen's paper trail, Maren called it, the humblest and most radical document in the new Vell—and in truth because the reading room was the only place in the city where silence felt companionable rather than tactical. The old court archive had been a vault: iron doors, chained codices, knowledge doled out by rank and favor. Maren had gutted it. The shelves stood open to the street through tall windows. Anyone could copy anything. Scribes sat free of charge at long tables for those who couldn't write, and a bell hung by the door that anyone might ring to summon a reader for those who couldn't read at all.

"The trouble," Maren said that Thursday, not looking up from her cataloguing, "is that openness attracts both pilgrims and vultures, and they look identical until they open their mouths."

"Has one opened his mouth?"

"A delegation from the capital. Very polite. Very interested in 'standardizing' our collection under royal oversight. For the people's benefit, naturally." She turned a page with deliberate calm. "I told them the archive belongs to the people already, which is why the crown may not have it. They didn't care for the grammar."

Lior lowered themself into the chair across from her. Outside, a fishwife argued cheerfully with a scribe about the spelling of her grandmother's charm-recipes; the scribe was losing. "Do you think he'll try again? Take it back?"

Maren finally looked up. Her face, seamed and tired and lit by the grey window-light, held none of the young court mage's certainty that had once helped shatter the old covenant in the name of protection. It held only the long arithmetic of consequence. "Kings try again. That is what kings are *for*. The question is whether next time he finds a single door to break or ten thousand hands already closed around what he wants." She tapped the cataloguing page. "Which is why I keep asking you, Dunmore, to stop delivering your little papers and start writing the full account. The Hollow. The shard. What it cost. Names, dates, mistakes—especially mine. If we don't write it down truly, someone else will write it down usefully, and useful lies are how crowns grow back."

"I know." Lior turned their cup slowly on the table. "I've started it three times. Every time I get to the part where I refused the captain's trade and Sefa was taken anyway, I stop. Because if I write it honestly, I wrote a child into a cell to keep my hands clean of naming names. And if I write it kindly, I'm lying, and you'll know, and worse, *she'll* know."

"She does know. She was there." Maren's voice gentled. "And she chose to refuse the crown herself, in that cell, before she'd heard half your story. Courage inherited rather than found—you gave her the shape of refusal just by living it where she could see. Write that part too. Especially that part." She slid a fresh quill across the table. "Truth isn't clean, Lior. It's just load-bearing."

* * *

Evening found Lior on the seawall below the palace, as it often did, eating bread and cheese bought openly from a vendor who knew exactly what they were and charged them fairly anyway. The tide came in amber and slow. Across the water, the granary square—where mages had burned, where Odell the fellow-taster had died with a taster's dignity and a commoner's scream—now hosted a night market. Lanterns strung between the old posts. Children darting between stalls. A juggler whose balls trailed soft light, showing off, showing off *legally*, to laughter instead of terror.

Lior watched and let the vigilance rest, the way one rests a muscle that will never fully relax but need not always ache.

It would come back. Vigilance always came back—that was its nature, and Lior had stopped resenting it. Somewhere a clerk copied names too carefully. Somewhere a minister measured the distance between the crown and the ten thousand sparks and wondered about bridges, or sieges. Somewhere the king sat with his trembling hand and his untouched supper and thought his slow thoughts. The work of many hands was never finished; it was only handed on, meal by meal, lesson by lesson, page by page.

But tonight the lanterns held. Tonight Sefa's window glowed until late, and Maren's bell rang twice for readers who could not read, and the tasting record went up at the gatehouse in wet ink for any citizen to check against any lie. Tonight the magic in Lior's chest sat quiet and shared, belonging to everyone, owned by no one, kept like bread among neighbors—broken, passed, and passed again.

Lior finished the bread, brushed the crumbs to the gulls, and walked home through the market with their face turned toward the light, keeping watch simply by being present, which was, they had finally learned, all the keeping any of it had ever required.
