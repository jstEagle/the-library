## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

There are two hundred and eleven days left on my debt, and I know this the way other men know their own names.

I don't count them aloud. Counting aloud is how you get noticed, and being noticed is how a temple scribe named Coral Galloway ends up transferred to the salt mines at Kettering, or worse, promoted. Promotion sounds like a kindness until you understand that in the Order of the Quiet Flame, promotion means visibility, and visibility means accountability, and accountability in this place has teeth. I have watched men rise and watched men fall, and I have concluded that the safest altitude in any hierarchy is the one just below notice.

So every morning I rise before the bell, dress in the grey wool of an indentured scribe, and walk the cloister corridor to the copying hall with my head at precisely the angle of a man thinking about ink.

* * *

The copying hall smells of tallow and vellum and the particular staleness of old men who have made peace with their lives. There are nine of us at the long tables this morning, hunched under windows frosted with the breath of the mountain, each of us bent over our assigned ledgers like penitents. Brother Aldous sits at the head table doing nothing, which is his principal talent and the reason he has held his position for thirty years.

My ledger today is the third of four volumes recording grain disbursements to the alms houses along the southern road. It is the most boring document in the province, which makes it perfect. Boring documents are never audited twice. Boring documents are how a man stays invisible.

"Your margins are crooked," says Brother Fenwick, who occupies the desk beside mine and considers it his sacred duty to comment on everything within his line of sight.

"My margins are consistent," I say.

"They're consistently crooked."

"That's still consistency."

He snorts and returns to his own work, which involves illuminating a capital letter shaped like a flame for a text nobody will read. Fenwick is seventy if he's a day, half-blind, and convinced that I am the finest scribe the Order has produced in a generation. This opinion is based entirely on the fact that I once corrected a transcription error in a hymnal without being asked. He has told the story so many times that younger brothers now look at me with something approaching reverence, which is a problem, because reverence is a form of attention, and attention is the enemy.

The truth about my calligraphy is simpler: I learned it from a smuggler.

Not directly. My mother sold watered wine to smugglers on the Salt Road, and one of them—a wiry, foul-mouthed man called Tetch—used to sit at our table trading stories for stew. He could write, which was rare and useful in his trade, and he taught me letters on the back of a flour sack because, as he put it, "a boy who can read a toll manifest can save your whole cargo." By the time the Order found me—eleven years old, orphaned, indebted for my mother's burial—they were getting a boy whose hand was already better than most brothers'. They thought they'd discovered talent. Really they'd discovered Tetch's homework.

I copy the grain figures. Barley, oats, millet. The numbers flow through me and out the nib without troubling my mind, which is free to do what it does every morning: run the routes.

* * *

Here is what the Order of the Quiet Flame believes, officially: the gods died. All of them, every name in every pantheon, extinguished in some ancient cataclysm the Order calls the Silence. The Flame they tend is not a god but a memorial—a candle lit for the departed divine, tended by the faithful until the end of days. Ours is a religion of mourning, not worship. We keep the vigils. We record the debts. We wait.

Here is what I believe, privately, in the part of myself I don't examine too closely: I don't care. The gods, dead or alive, have done nothing for Coral Galloway lately. What I care about is two hundred and eleven days, after which my debt is discharged and I walk out of this temple a free man with a scribe's skill, a modest purse, and a memory full of roads the Order doesn't know exist.

Because here is the thing about spending your childhood among smugglers: you learn the mountains. Not the official roads—the Order's maps show three passes through Thornfell, all patrolled, all taxed—but the real ones. The goat tracks that become supply paths. The dry riverbeds that flood only twice a year. The cache hollows behind waterfalls where men leave goods and messages and, occasionally, bodies. Tetch drew them for me on that flour sack, over and over, until I could trace them blind. I have spent nine years inside these walls adding detail to those maps from anything I overhear: a brother complaining about a landslide on the eastern trail, a merchant's complaint about a new toll station, a guard drunk enough to describe where the patrols turn back.

Every night, after lights-out, I lie in my cell and walk those roads in my mind. Every pass, every ford, every hollow. It is not escape planning, exactly. Or rather, it is exactly escape planning, but I tell myself it isn't, because a man needs to believe his cage has a door he'll be allowed to use when the time comes.

Two hundred and eleven days. Then the Salt Road south, then the lowlands, then a life where nobody calls me brother or debtor or Galloway-the-quiet-one. A small house. A trade. Invisibility, permanent and chosen.

That's the plan. It's a good plan. I want you to appreciate how good a plan it is, because plans like this deserve appreciation right up until the moment they die.

* * *

At midday the bell rings for the meal, and I file down to the refectory with the others, taking my usual place at the end of the second bench, the seat reserved for men of no consequence. The soup is barley and root vegetables, same as yesterday, same as tomorrow. I eat mechanically and listen.

Listening is the other thing I'm good at. A scribe hears everything, because everyone forgets a scribe is in the room. Today the talk is of the weather—early snows in the high passes—and of a supply run: the temple needs salt, lamp oil, and parchment stock from the lowland markets, and someone must go down the Salt Road to fetch them.

"I'll not go," Fenwick announces to no one and everyone. "My knees have opinions about Thornfell."

"Brother Anselm goes," Aldous says. "And he'll need a scribe to verify quantities at purchase." A pause, the particular pause of a man delegating a chore. "Galloway writes a fair hand. Galloway will go."

There it is. The trap of competence. Be too good at anything in this place and they will use you for it forever; be useless and they leave you alone. I have spent nine years calibrating myself to be just competent enough to avoid punishment and not so competent as to be indispensable, and somehow I have failed at both.

"Yes, Brother," I say, in the tone of a man mildly inconvenienced.

Inside, something in me lifts. The Salt Road. Twice a year, if I'm lucky, the Order sends me into the world, and every time I come back with more than salt and oil. This trip I might confirm whether the rockfall near Miller's Bend has cleared—if it has, there's a route open that the Order's maps mark as impassable, which means it's worth its weight in gold to a certain class of traveler. I might check the hollow behind the third waterfall. I might—

I catch myself smiling into my soup and flatten the expression. Careful, Coral. A man who smiles at soup gets noticed.

* * *

The afternoon passes in figures. At evening vigil, I kneel with the others before the Quiet Flame itself, the great bronze bowl in the heart of the temple where a fire has burned, they say, since the Silence. It gives almost no heat. That's the doctrine of it: a fire that warms no one, lit for gods who need no warmth, tended by men who receive none. Nine years of kneeling before it and I have never felt anything from it except the ache in my knees.

But tonight—I don't know why tonight—I watch the flame a moment longer than usual. It stands perfectly still, unnaturally still, the way a flame burns in a room with no drafts. And for half a heartbeat I have the strangest sensation, the feeling you get when you sense someone standing behind you in an empty room. As though the stillness weren't emptiness but restraint. As though something were holding very, very quiet on purpose.

Then Brother Aldous coughs, and the moment passes, and I tell myself it was the mutton at midday disagreeing with me.

Dead gods don't hold their breath. Sleeping ones might, but the gods are dead. Everyone knows this. The Order has built nine centuries of certainty on knowing it, and I have built two hundred and eleven days of patience on not caring either way.

* * *

Lights-out comes, and I lie in my narrow cell listening to the mountain wind worry the shutters, running the Salt Road in my head from the temple gate to the lowlands. Miller's Bend. The waterfall caches. The fork at Greywater where the smuggler path splits toward the coast. I know them all. In two hundred and eleven days I will walk them for real, and no one will ever find Coral Galloway again unless Coral Galloway wants finding.

I am nearly asleep when I hear it: hoofbeats on the approach road, fast, at an hour when no honest rider travels. More than one horse. I sit up. Through the shutter gap I can see torchlight moving in the courtyard below, and figures—armored figures, wearing the grey-and-ash livery of the Order's own knights, escorting a rider slumped in his saddle like a sack of grain.

Voices carry up through the cold air. I catch fragments. A courier, they say. Found on the road. Wounded—no, dying. Asking for the temple. Asking, of all things, for the scriptorium.

Then another voice, clipped and cold, cutting through the rest like a blade through cloth, and even from my window I feel the weight of it settle over the courtyard:

"Wake the archivist. And no one touches that man's satchel before I see what's in it."

I lie back down slowly, heart tapping against my ribs, and tell myself what I always tell myself: whatever it is, it has nothing to do with me. I am invisible. I am two hundred and eleven days from free.

Outside, the wind shifts, and beneath the hoofbeats and voices I would swear—for just a moment—that the great bronze bowl in the heart of the temple flickered.
