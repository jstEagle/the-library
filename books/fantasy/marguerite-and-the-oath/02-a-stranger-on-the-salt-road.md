## 2. A Stranger on the Salt Road

The trouble with knowing every smuggler's path through Thornfell, Brother Anselm liked to say, was that a man who knew too many ways around a thing was probably going around something. Coral had always assumed the old quartermaster was being clever. It took a dying stranger in a ravine to teach him that Anselm had simply been describing him.

He was three days out from the temple, on the salt run, and for the first time in eleven years the run felt wrong.

Not dangerous. He knew the difference. Dangerous was ice on the Widow's Stair, dangerous was the season's first wolves coming down hungry off the high scree, dangerous was a customs man at Greywater Ford who'd decided to count barrels instead of accepting the customary miscount. Coral had survived all of those and worse, and had done it by being precisely nobody: a thin, stooped scribe with ink-stained fingers and a temple token on a cord, carrying salt down and lamp oil up, boring enough that even bandits waved him past with something like pity.

No, this wrongness was subtler. It started as a smell.

Thornfell in late autumn had a vocabulary of smells, and Coral knew them the way other men knew prayers. Pine and cold stone. The mineral bite of the high passes. Woodsmoke from the charcoal burners' camps, always faintly sweet, always slightly guilty. And underneath it all, the mountain itself—a deep, mineral breath, like a cellar that had been closed for a very long time. The Order said the mountain's breath was the last exhalation of the dead gods, a holy residue. The smugglers said it was sulfur and didn't tip either way.

What Coral smelled on the third morning, crouched over his cookfire above the ravine called Hollowbone, was iron. Fresh iron. Blood, spilled in quantity, cooling in the cold air.

He should have packed up. That was the rule, the first rule, the rule that had kept him alive while cleverer men fed the crows: *you saw nothing, you owe nothing, you are a ledger with legs.* Coral Galloway had spent eleven years being unremarkable with the discipline other men gave to swordsmanship or sainthood. He knew exactly how invisible he was, because invisibility was the only asset he owned and he audited it daily.

But the blood smell was wrong in another way. It was coming from below the trail, down in Hollowbone, where nothing was supposed to be. The smuggler routes didn't cross the ravine floor. The ravine floor was where the mountain kept its old dead things—rockfall, flood timber, one memorable season's worth of frozen mules. Nobody went down there because there was nothing down there to steal.

Which meant whoever was down there hadn't chosen to be.

Coral told himself he was going down to make sure it wasn't one of his own—the fence at Greywater, the boy who ran lamp oil for the burners, anyone whose death would complicate his ledgers. He told himself this with the fluency of long practice, because Coral was very good at lying, provided the person he lied to was Coral.

He left the fire burning, took his knife, and climbed down.

* * *

The man was wedged in the rocks at the bottom like something the mountain had tried to swallow and given up on. He lay half in the shadow of a fallen slab, and at first Coral thought he was dead, because nobody living arranged themselves at that angle.

Then the man's eyes opened, and Coral's quiet life ended—not with a shout, but with the small, terrible sound of a stranger focusing on him with the last of his strength.

The courier—Coral would think of him that way later, because his boots were courier's boots, light and double-stitched, worth more than Coral's entire wardrobe—was maybe thirty, maybe less, and he had been opened up along the ribs in a way that Coral, who had catalogued injuries in the infirmary ledgers for a decade, recognized as a blade wound. A long blade, drawn upward, by someone who knew what they were doing. There was a lot of blood. There was blood on the rocks, blood frozen in the man's hair, blood on his gloved hand where it pressed against his side in a gesture that had long since stopped being useful and become ceremonial.

"No," the man said. His voice came out as a ruin, gravel dragged over gravel. "No temple. No—*no*."

"I'm not—" Coral raised his hands, knife still in one, which was perhaps not the most reassuring configuration. "I'm nobody. Scribe. I was just—"

The man laughed. It cost him; Coral watched the laugh take something out of him that he couldn't afford, watched the man's face go grey around the mouth. "Nobody," the courier said. "Good. Nobodies live."

"I'm leaving, then." Coral took a step back up the scree. "I saw nothing. That's my whole profession, seeing nothing."

"Wait." The hand came off the wound and reached for him, and Coral, to his lasting regret, reached back.

He would replay this moment many times in the months to come, in the way that a man replays the step that broke the ice. There had been a version of the morning where he climbed back up, kicked out his fire, and took the long way around Hollowbone, and by evening the whole thing would have been a strange smell and a story he never told. That version existed. It had been right there, one step away, and Coral had reached back instead.

The courier's grip was astonishingly strong for a man who was mostly dead. He pulled Coral down close, close enough to see that his eyes were brown and sane and absolutely terrified, and close enough to smell the blood and the horse and the faint, sweetish trace of something else—incense, Coral thought, or ash.

"They're dead," the man whispered. "The gods. That's what they tell you. That's what they *made* you write."

"I write salt tallies," Coral said. "Mostly."

"Mostly." The courier's mouth twitched. "Mostly. That's the tragedy of you, scribe. Mostly."

"I don't know what you—"

"They're not dead." The hand tightened. Coral felt something being pressed into his palm, wrapped in oilcloth, warm from the man's body. It was flat, stiff, and heavier than it should have been. "They're *sleeping*. Under this mountain. Under all of them. And the Order knows, and the Court knows, and every soul that's found out has been—"

He coughed then, a wet, catastrophic sound, and when he could speak again the words came slower, rationed out like the last coins of a dying purse.

"Thirteen turns," he said. "Thirteen turns of the moon. That's the count. That's what they're waiting for. When the thirteenth turns full, they wake it, or they bury it deeper, and either way—" His grip failed and recovered. "Either way the world changes and nobody's asked. Nobody's *asked*, scribe. They just write it down after."

"Who's waiting?" Coral heard his own voice from a great distance, thin and reasonable. "Waiting for what? Who's *they*?"

But the courier's eyes had gone past him, up the ravine wall, to the grey sky over the rocks, and whatever he saw there wasn't Coral's problem anymore, because in the next breath the light in him went out the way a lamp goes out—suddenly, completely, with a small settling of the whole body, as if some tension he'd been holding his entire life had finally been set down.

Coral knelt in the frozen blood of a stranger, in a ravine that was supposed to be empty, holding an oilcloth-wrapped thing that was warm as a heartbeat, and listened to the mountain breathe.

* * *

Here is what Coral Galloway did next, and he would like it noted, for the record he was no longer sure existed, that he tried to do the right thing in the correct order.

First: he checked the man's body for identification, found none, and understood that this was itself an identification. Couriers who carried things worth killing for didn't carry names.

Second: he looked up the ravine walls, both of them, for the killers. The wound was fresh—hours, not days—and Hollowbone was a half-day's hard climb from anywhere. The killers had come down and gone back up, or they were still up there, patient as winter. He found nothing, which was worse than finding something. The mountain was very good at holding still.

Third: he considered the oilcloth package, and this was where the correct order collapsed.

He should leave it. He knew he should leave it. He could tuck it under the dead man's arm, climb out, report a body at the temple—no, he couldn't report a body, because reporting a body meant explaining a body, and explaining a body meant explaining what he'd been doing three hundred paces off the salt road at dawn, and Coral's entire life was built on the principle that he was never three hundred paces off anything. He could leave it and say nothing, and let the mountain keep its dead, and let the wrapped thing lie in the frozen blood until the killers came back for it, because killers always came back. That was another thing the ledgers had taught him: nobody paid good money to kill a man and then didn't check the pockets.

And if the killers came back and found it, fine. That was their business. That was blood on other people's hands, and Coral Galloway had kept his own hands clean for eleven years by the simple, disciplined, daily practice of never touching anything that bled.

He stood up. He looked down at the package in his palm.

The trouble was the man's eyes. Sane, terrified, and—this was the part Coral couldn't shake—*entrusted*. The courier hadn't grabbed at him the way a drowning man grabs at anything. He'd looked at Coral—this stooped, ink-fingered, deliberately forgettable man in a temple cloak—and made a decision, in the last seconds of his life, that Coral was the right person. Not a good person. Coral wasn't sure he was that. But the right person: a nobody, a man who saw nothing, a man who knew every smuggler's path through Thornfell and therefore knew exactly how to be unfindable.

*Nobodies live,* the man had said. And then he'd handed a nobody the most dangerous object in the mountains.

"That's not a compliment," Coral told the corpse. "That's a misjudgment."

The corpse, having no further opinion, let the wind answer for him, moving through the ravine with a low sound like a held note. Coral had heard the mountain make that sound a thousand times. The Order called it the gods' last breath. He'd always privately called it the mountain digesting. Standing in Hollowbone with a dead man's blood soaking through his knee, he found, to his horror, that he was listening to it differently. *They're not dead,* the man had said. *They're sleeping.*

It was nonsense. It was heresy, was what it was—the oldest heresy, the one they made novices copy out refutations of until the refutations wore grooves in their souls. The gods were dead. Everyone knew the gods were dead. The Order's whole architecture, the tithes and the oaths and the quiet candles and the debt-ledgers that had owned eleven years of Coral Galloway's life, all of it stood on that single foundation: the gods died at the Fall, and the Quiet Flame was what remained of their light, and the Order kept it. Take the foundation out and—

And Coral was a ledger-keeper. He knew what happened to structures when you removed a load-bearing truth. He'd watched a granary collapse once because a single beam had rotted where nobody thought to look.

"Thirteen turns," he said aloud, and his voice sounded wrong in the ravine, too loud, and he flinched at it like a criminal.

He should burn it. The thought arrived fully formed, sensible, and immediately suspect. Burn the oilcloth and whatever it held, tell no one, go back to salt tallies. The courier was dead either way. The count—whatever count—was being kept by people far better resourced than a debt-scribe with a knife he'd never drawn on anything but cheese.

But you couldn't burn something you hadn't looked at. That was the flaw in the plan, and Coral saw it the way he saw all flaws: instantly, helplessly, with the professional horror of a man whose whole life was records. Because if he burned it unread, then the courier's death went into no ledger anywhere. It would be an entry that no one made, in a book no one kept, and the man would have died for nothing—died holding Coral's hand, having entrusted him with the only thing that made the death mean anything.

Coral Galloway had spent eleven years not mattering. It turned out that when the moment came, he couldn't bear for anyone else not to matter either.

"Damn it," he said, with feeling, and put the package inside his coat, against his chest, where it lay against his heartbeat like a second, slower one.

* * *

He buried the courier. Not well—the ground was iron with frost and he had a knife and a flat rock and no time—but he moved the body off the blood-soaked stone into the shadow of the slab, and he stacked stones over him, and he did the only rite he knew, the one the Order gave its scribes for the anonymous dead: a spoken line, no candle, no incense. *What the Flame lit, the dark cannot unmake.* He said it twice, because the first time his voice cracked on *Flame*, and he wasn't sure anymore what he was promising.

Then he climbed out of Hollowbone and became, with the discipline of eleven years, a scribe on a salt run.

He kicked out his fire's ashes and scattered them. He repacked the mule—he'd left the poor animal tethered two switchbacks up, and she gave him the flat, judgmental look of her kind, as if to say she'd smelled the blood an hour before he had and had opinions about his choices. He took the salt road down toward Greywater, at the correct pace, with the correct stoop, nodding correctly to the charcoal burners' boy when he passed their cold camp.

And all the while the package lay against his chest, and all the while he did not look at it, and all the while it got heavier.

That was the thing nobody told you about secrets, Coral thought, as the pass opened up and the grey light of afternoon came down the valley like water finding its level. They didn't weigh what they were. A map was vellum and ink; it weighed nothing. What it weighed was what it *cost*—and he could feel the cost accruing already, interest compounding by the hour, the way his debt to the Order had compounded for eleven years. Every mile he carried it, the price of being found with it went up, and the price of getting rid of it went up faster, because now there was a body under stones in Hollowbone and a wound on the world that someone would come to read.

He made camp that night in a smuggler's cache—a hollow behind a fallen slab, off every official road, with a view of the salt road below and the stars doing their indifferent, ancient bookkeeping overhead. He ate cold salt pork and did not light a fire. And finally, with the mule dozing and the valley silent, he took the package out and held it in both hands.

Oilcloth, wax-sealed at the fold. The wax was dark—black, almost—and stamped with a seal he didn't recognize: a circle broken by a vertical line, like an eye that had been closed and then, crudely, reopened. He turned it in the starlight. He was a scribe; seals were his trade; he knew the marks of every house, temple, and toll station between here and the coast, and this one belonged to none of them. It belonged to no one, which meant it belonged to someone who could afford not to.

*Thirteen turns,* the dead man had said. Of the moon. A count. A deadline. Somewhere out in the dark, people were counting, and Coral had a sudden, vertiginous certainty that the count had not gotten slower because a courier had died in a ravine. If anything, the killing meant the count was *close*.

He put his thumb under the wax seal.

He stopped.

Because here was the arithmetic of his life, laid out suddenly and completely, the way a ledger balances at year's end with a terrible final click. On one side: eleven years of careful, invisible, half-free living. Four more years until his debt was paid. Four more years and then the coast, a room of his own, ink he owned himself, a small life with no one's blood in it. He had counted those years the way a prisoner counts, and the counting had been the whole of his comfort.

On the other side: a black wax seal, a dead stranger's sanity, and the words *they're not dead* sitting in his chest like a swallowed coal.

He thought about Brother Anselm. *A man who knew too many ways around a thing was probably going around something.* Coral had always thought the old man meant the mountain. Standing in the dark with his thumb under the seal, he understood, finally, that Anselm had meant him.

"Thirteen turns," Coral said to the mule, to the stars, to the dead man under his stones in Hollowbone. "You could have given it to anyone with a sword."

The mule flicked an ear. The mountain breathed its long, deep, mineral breath beneath him, patient as a sleeper.

Coral broke the seal.

The wax parted with a small, clean sound, like a knuckle cracking, like a door he could never again pretend was locked—and inside, in the oilcloth, he found vellum, and on the vellum, ink, and on the ink, in a hand far finer than any salt tally, lines that made no sense at all.

And one line, at the bottom, that did.
