## 5. Thirteen Turns of the Moon

The trouble with knowing every smuggler's path through Thornfell is that you also know every smuggler's friend, and smugglers' friends talk. Not loudly, not honestly, but they talk, the way water talks through a wall—slowly, in the dark, until one day the wall comes down.

I'd spent four days since the ravine doing what I do best: nothing, convincingly. I'd delivered my salt, filed my requisitions, copied forty pages of candle-wax expenditures in a hand so beautiful it made me want to weep with boredom. I'd eaten supper in the refectory and laughed at Brother Aldous's joke about the abbess's cat, which was not funny the first time and had achieved a kind of sublime unfunniness by the eleventh telling. I was invisible again. I was safe.

Except that at night I took the map out of its hiding place beneath a loose flagstone in the scriptorium's undercroft, and I looked at it, and it looked back.

That's the thing nobody tells you about secrets. They don't sit still. A secret is a living thing, and it grows, and it gets hungry, and after four days mine had grown teeth. Every time I passed an inquisitor's empty office I felt it stir. Every time a temple bell rang I counted the hours since the courier died and wondered who else was counting.

So on the fifth day I did something stupid. I went to see Wren.

* * *

Wren Ollivan—no relation to anyone he'd admit to—keeps a shop at the bottom of Salt Row that sells, officially, secondhand boots. Unofficially, Wren sells everything that can be carried, and several things that shouldn't be. He is somewhere north of seventy, built like a bundle of kindling, and has been fencing stolen goods in Thornfell since before the Order finished building its western wall. If the Ashen Court had returned to the mountains, Wren would know. If the Ashen Court had returned wearing dresses and singing hymns, Wren would know that too, and charge you two coppers extra for the detail about the dresses.

Getting to him meant leaving the temple after evening bell, which meant lying to the gatekeeper, which meant my third lie in five days, each one easier than the last. That should have frightened me more than it did. It didn't, because I was busy being frightened of everything else.

Salt Row at night smells of tallow and wet wool and the particular despair of men drinking away wages they haven't earned yet. I kept my hood up and my pace bored. Boredom is armor. Walk like you're going somewhere interesting and every eye follows you; walk like your feet hurt and you could pass through a wedding unnoticed.

Wren's shop had one lamp lit, which meant open, and the door wasn't locked, which meant he wanted customers, or wanted to rob them; with Wren the distinction had always been academic.

He was in his back room, sorting buttons into trays by some taxonomy known only to him and possibly God, though given what I'd learned from a dead man's map lately, I no longer assumed God was paying attention.

"Coral Galloway," he said without looking up. "Temple mouse. You look terrible."

"Thank you. I've been practicing."

"Practicing what?"

"Looking well."

A grunt that might have been laughter. He set down a button—a horn one, cracked—and finally looked at me over spectacles thick as communion wafers. "You want boots."

"I want information."

"Boots are cheaper."

"Wren."

"Information's dear." He leaned back in his chair until it creaked like a ship's rigging. "And you've got the look of a man who can't pay dear. Temple scribe. Indentured. What are you worth, twelve years? Fourteen? Your whole fortune is a cot and a candle allowance."

"Three coppers," I said, and put them on the table. "And this." I put down a silver stylus tip, temple-issue, worth maybe eight coppers to anyone honest and fifteen to anyone who wasn't. Wren's eyes did the arithmetic before his mouth did.

"That's theft from the Order," he said, pocketing both anyway. "You're getting bold, mouse."

"I'm getting tired. Have you heard anything new? Strangers on the roads. People asking questions about the mountains. Anything with ash on it."

The word *ash* landed on the table between us and neither of us touched it for a long moment. Wren's hands went still over his buttons, and I realized I'd never seen Wren's hands go still before. In thirty years of small crimes he had the fidget of a man perpetually mid-transaction. Now he sat like a statue someone had dressed badly.

"Sit down," he said.

"I'd rather—"

"*Sit down*, boy."

I sat. There was a stool by the door that had probably once belonged to a church and now belonged to everyone, which is the way of most holy things.

Wren got up, crossed to the shuttered window, and listened at it for a while, which was theater—he'd have heard anyone coming a street away—but theater meant he needed the time to arrange his face. When he turned around, the arrangement was grim.

"How much do you know about the Ashen Court?" he asked.

"What any scribe knows. Heretics. Exiles. A century ago they tried to—" I stopped, because saying *wake the gods* aloud in a fence's back room felt like shouting in a library. "They were purged. The Order burned their strongholds. The survivors scattered south and were never heard from again."

"Mm. And how often does 'never heard from again' turn out to be true, in your experience?"

"In my experience," I said carefully, "never."

"There it is." He lowered himself back into his chair, and for the first time since I'd known him, Wren Ollivan looked old. Not old like a joke, old like a wound. "They came back three weeks ago. Quietly. First it was strangers buying rope and cold-weather gear in lots too big for pilgrims. Then a man came through asking after guides who knew the high passes—the passes that don't appear on any Order map. Then another man, better dressed, asking different questions. Not where the paths are. Where the *people* are who know the paths."

My mouth had gone dry. "What kind of questions?"

"The kind you don't answer twice." Wren took off his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, a gesture I recognized as his version of wringing hands. "Old Tam Brightwater—you remember Tam? Guided ore wagons over the Shoulder for twenty years. Found him at the bottom of the quarry two weeks back. The Order called it a fall. Tam knew that quarry like his own kitchen. He didn't fall."

The room felt smaller. Rooms have been doing that to me lately.

"And now," Wren continued, "there's a story going round the low taverns, told quiet, told drunk, told by people who don't know they're passing it along. About the Court coming home. About a vault under Thornfell. And about a deadline."

"A deadline."

"Thirteen turns of the moon." He said it flat, like reading off a ledger. "Some counting the exiles keep. The moon that's rising now is the first of them. They mean to find what's sleeping under this mountain and wake it before the thirteenth turn comes full. And everyone who knows the roads, or knows the maps, or knows the *seals*—" his eyes came up and rested on me with terrible gentleness, "—is either useful to them or in their way. Tam was in their way. You understand me, Coral? I'm telling you this because you're the only customer I've ever liked, and liking people is a weakness I can't afford many more of."

I understood him. I understood him so completely that my hands had gone numb.

Thirteen turns. Thirteen months. Some part of my mind, the clerk's part, the part that tallied candle wax and flour sacks, seized on the number and began doing arithmetic with it, because arithmetic was safer than screaming. One moon already risen. Twelve remaining. The courier had died whispering *thirteen turns* like a man reciting a sentence passed on him. Now I knew whose sentence it was. Everyone's.

"Where's the Court staging from?" I asked. My voice came out steadier than I deserved.

"If I knew that, I'd be dead already." Wren resumed sorting buttons, and the small motion seemed to steady us both. "But there's a woman. Descriptions vary—dark hair, grey eyes, speaks like she was educated to command and punished for it. She's been seen at the Wayhouse at Miller's Fork, and once, three days ago, on the salt road itself. Watching travelers. Looking at satchels."

The salt road. My road. The road I'd walked four days ago with a dead man's map pressed against my spine like a second heartbeat.

"Wren," I said. "If a man had something the Court wanted. Something small. Paper. How would they find him?"

"They wouldn't need to find him," Wren said. "They'd need to find everyone, and then subtract. That's how the Order hunts, and the Court learned hunting from the same mother. Papers leave traces, boy. Wax leaves traces. Ink leaves traces. A man carrying a secret walks differently. Carries his shoulders wrong. Sleeps badly. Talks to fences at night and pays in stolen silver." He held up the stylus tip between two fingers and let it catch the lamplight. "You're not hiding, Coral. You're advertising."

I stood up so fast the stool skidded. "Then I'll stop advertising."

"You'll stop *visiting*, is what you'll do, and you won't come back here, and you'll burn whatever paper's making you walk like a man with a knife between his shoulder blades, and you'll thank me someday when you're old and boring." He turned back to his buttons. "Go home, mouse. Go home and be small. Small things survive. It's the ones that carry weight that get buried under it."

* * *

I walked back through Salt Row with the deadline wrapped around my throat like a scarf, and the strange thing—the thing I keep returning to—is how ordinary everything looked. A woman argued with a fishmonger. Two boys rolled a hoop past the chapel. A dog slept in a doorway with the total commitment only dogs achieve. The world went on buying and selling and sleeping, entirely ignorant that it had been given an expiration date, and I envied it with a violence that scared me.

Because that's what a deadline does. It doesn't change the world. It changes *you*. Before the ravine, time had been a corridor I walked down at my own pace—years of ledger lines stretching ahead, each one identical, each one safe. Now time was a fist, closing. Twelve moons. Eleven after that. Somewhere under the mountain something slept, and somewhere above it people were sharpening knives and buying rope, and the count was running whether I moved or stood still, whether I spoke or stayed silent, whether I lived or died. The thirteenth turn would come regardless. The only question—the only question left in the world—was what state the mountain would be in when it arrived.

I reached the temple gate as the midnight bell rang. The gatekeeper nodded me through with the sleepy trust of a man who believed temple scribes came back late only for pious reasons, and I lied to him with my face alone, a smile, and hated myself in the efficient, familiar way I'd recently perfected.

In the undercroft, I lifted the flagstone and took out the map, and this time I didn't unroll it. I just held it, feeling its weight, its wax seal, its patient terrible gravity.

Twelve moons.

And then, from somewhere above me—from the direction of the abbot's tower—a bell began ringing that had no business ringing at midnight. Not the hour bell. The alarm. Three strokes, pause, three strokes. The signal I'd copied a hundred times in the temple's own codebooks without ever once expecting to hear it.

Strangers on the mountain. Riders requesting entry. Inquisitors.

I put the map back beneath the stone and sat in the dark listening to the bell, and thought: *they're early.*

Everyone was early. The Court, the hunters, the end of my small life—all of it arriving ahead of schedule, like debts.

The bell rang on, and somewhere beneath my feet, beneath the flagstone, beneath the mountain's whole patient body, I could almost believe I felt something shift in its sleep.
