## 6. Inquisitor Hale

The bells told me before anyone else did.

Not the usual bells—the dawn call, the noon call, the small cracked bell that Brother Odo rang for meals because the temple couldn't afford a second one that didn't sound like a dying goose. No, this was the *visitor's* bell, the deep bronze one in the gatehouse tower that got pulled maybe four times a year, and each pull of it rolled down through the cloisters like a stone dropped into still water.

I was copying funeral accounts when it rang. Column after column of candle-tithes and burial shrouds, the arithmetic of grief, which is to say: the most honest ledger in the building. I remember thinking, with the particular stupidity of a man whose life is about to change, *someone important has come to pay their respects.*

Then I heard the horses. Six of them, shod heavy, moving at a walk that said *we are not guests.*

I put down my pen. I looked at my ink pot. I did what any guilty man does first: I checked whether my guilt was visible.

It wasn't. The map wasn't on me—it never was, not inside the walls, not since the night I'd wedged it into the smuggler's cache below the Widow's Stair, wrapped in oilcloth behind a loose stone that only I and three dead men knew about. My satchel held nothing but vellum, a knife for scraping errors, and half a honey cake I'd been saving like a dragon hoards gold. My hands were steady. My face, I'd been told by more than one person, was a face you forget while looking directly at it.

So why did my heart go off like a dropped tray of glassware?

Because of the courier. Because of *thirteen turns*, whispered through blood-frothed lips in a ravine two weeks ago. Because dead men have a way of sending visitors.

* * *

I saw them from the scriptorium window before I was summoned down, which is a thing scribes can do—we're furniture with eyes. Six riders dismounting in the forecourt where the flagstones were worn smooth by a century of penitents' knees. Five were Order cavalry, grey cloaks, faces like closed doors.

The sixth was something else.

He was not tall. That was the first surprise. I'd built Inquisitors out of childhood nightmares—towering figures with voices like rockslides—and here was a man of middling height, narrow through the shoulders, with the kind of neatness that looks gentle until you realize it's the neatness of a surgeon's tray. Grey hair cropped close. A face so ordinary that my eyes kept sliding off it, trying to find the interesting parts, and finding none, which is itself interesting. He wore no insignia beyond a small iron flame pinned at his collar, and he stood in our forecourt looking up at our temple the way a man looks at a clock he suspects of running slow.

"That's Hale," whispered Ferren beside me, a junior copyist with a talent for knowing things he shouldn't. "Inquisitor Hale. Third Chair of the Question. They say he's never once raised his voice."

"They say that about everyone terrible," I said.

"No. About him they say it because it's true." Ferren's throat moved. "They say he apologized to a heretic the whole time he worked on him. Politely. Like a physician."

Wonderful. I had been dreading a monster, and instead the gods—or whatever slept under Thornfell pretending to be dead—had sent me an accountant of souls.

* * *

They gathered us in the chapter hall, all forty-one resident scribes and lay clerks, arranged on benches like exhibits. Prior Aldous sat at the front wearing the expression of a man presiding over a funeral he'd only just learned about. The five grey-cloaks stood along the walls. Hale stood at the center, hands folded, and waited for silence the way other men wait for bread to rise—patiently, confidently, as though silence owed him.

"Brothers. Sisters." His voice was exactly as advertised: quiet, level, almost kind. "You will have heard that a courier of this Order died on the salt road some weeks past. You will perhaps have heard it described as misfortune. Bandits. Weather." He paused. "I am here to tell you it was neither."

Nobody breathed. Somewhere a bench creaked.

"Brother Edmun Vail carried documents of the highest sensitivity from the garrison at Cael March to the temple at Vespergate. He never arrived. His body was found in a ravine off the Thornfell trade path—" and here, here is the moment my whole body turned to ice water, "—by a supply clerk of this house, who reported the death and returned with the sad duty done."

Me. He meant me. Of course he meant me; I was the one who'd found the body, who'd reported it, who'd knelt in the ravine mud and lied about what the dead man's hands had been holding. Or rather, hadn't been holding. I had reported a corpse, a broken seal-case, nothing else. The seal-case had gone into the cache along with the map, because I am many things but I am not stupid enough to keep evidence.

"The clerk," Hale continued, "is Coral Galloway. Debt-bound, eleven years served of fourteen." His eyes found me across the hall without hurrying, the way a hand finds the right key in a dark room. "Brother Galloway. You'll speak with me privately. Not now." A small smile, apologetic, surgical. "After the others. I find it's better to let people watch me be reasonable first."

* * *

He interviewed them one at a time in the abbot's parlor, and I sat in the corridor outside and listened to my colleagues walk in frightened and walk out relieved, which was precisely the design. Hale asked each of them the same soft questions—when had they last seen Brother Vail, had he seemed troubled, did he owe anyone money, did he drink—and each of them answered honestly and uselessly, and each came out into the corridor looking lighter, murmuring *he's not so bad, actually,* and I wanted to grab them by the collars and shake them. That's how it works. That's how it has always worked. The Question doesn't break doors down; it lets you hold the door open yourself and thanks you for your trouble.

Ferren came out pale but breathing. "He asked if you were well-liked," she whispered. "I said yes. Was that wrong?"

"It was kind," I said. "Kindness is rarely wrong. It just isn't always useful."

"Coral." She gripped my sleeve. "What did you find? Really?"

"A dead man," I said, and hated myself for how smoothly it came. "Really."

She believed me. That was the worst part. Not that she doubted—I could have survived doubt, doubt is just weather—but that she believed, instantly, completely, because I had a face you forget while looking at it and apparently a voice you trust while listening to it. Eleven years of copying ledgers had taught me to write numbers that balanced even when the truth didn't. Apparently it had taught me other things too.

* * *

When they called my name, the parlor smelled of beeswax and old cedar. Hale sat alone at the table—no grey-cloaks inside, no prior, no witnesses. Two chairs. Tea, already poured, steam rising off it in a thin ribbon.

"Sit, please. Forgive the theater of the public session; institutions require audiences, and I required one less than they did." He pushed the cup toward me. "Willow bark and honey. You look like a man who hasn't slept properly in a fortnight."

"I sleep fine," I said, and sat, and did not touch the tea.

"You found Brother Vail's body." Not a question. An opening move, laid flat on the table like a tile. "Describe it to me. Take your time. Details matter—they always do, though rarely in the way people expect."

So I described it. And here is the thing I will carry to whatever grave I eventually earn: I described it *well*. The angle of the ravine. The position of the horse, which had bolted and broken its leg further down. The blood, already blackened, pooled under the left side of his ribs. The courier's case, its wax seal cracked, its contents missing—which I attributed, with a small shrug I had practiced in a mirror without admitting I was practicing, to whoever attacked him. I gave him the weather. I gave him the smell. I gave him the detail of Vail's boots, one lace snapped, which I invented entirely and which landed beautifully because it was the kind of specific, useless thing only a real witness remembers.

Hale listened with his head slightly tilted, and when I finished he said, "Thank you. That was very clear," and I felt the compliment land like a hook going in clean.

"May I ask what the contents were?" I made my voice curious, not eager. Curious is safe. Eager is a confession.

"You may ask." He smiled faintly. "A ledger. Financial records of the mountain garrisons. Tithes, provisions, payroll. The Order is embarrassed enough about its bookkeeping being robbed without discussing it further." He watched me over the rim of his own cup. "Does that answer satisfy you?"

"It's not mine to be satisfied or unsatisfied," I said. "I'm debt-bound. I copy what I'm given."

"Mm." He set the cup down and turned it slowly, once, aligning the handle with the table edge. "Eleven years of your fourteen. What was the original debt?"

"My mother's. Medical care, winter of the famine years. She signed me over and died anyway. The Order kept the contract. Contracts are sacred here—you'd know that better than I."

Something moved behind his eyes—not sympathy, exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man checking a calculation and finding the number familiar.

"Yes," he said quietly. "Contracts are sacred. That is rather the entire point of everything, isn't it, Brother Galloway. Debts, vows, oaths, seals. Wax and ink and word. Civilization is just a very elaborate way of keeping promises." He leaned back. "Which is why it troubles me—deeply, personally troubles me—when a promise appears to have been broken. Someone opened Vail's case. Someone took what was in it. Someone killed him for it, or killed him after, or—" the pause was feather-light, "—killed him *before*, and took the contents as cover. These are the possibilities. There are others. Possibilities are like debts; they accrue interest the longer they sit."

"And you think one of us—" I gestured vaguely at the wall, at the temple, at forty-one frightened scribes. "We're copyists. We fight with pens."

"So was Brother Vail, once. So am I, in a sense." He rose, crossed to the window, and stood with his hands clasped behind him, looking out at the mountains. When he spoke again his voice had dropped into something almost conversational, which from him I understood was the most dangerous register there was. "Let me tell you a story, since you've told me yours. Years ago I questioned a woman in Cael March. A cook. Unremarkable. She lied to me—badly, at first, then better, and finally so well that I nearly released her. And do you know what betrayed her in the end? Not a contradiction. Not a trembling hand. She became *fluent*. Her lies stopped costing her anything. Truth has friction, Brother Galloway. Lies, practiced long enough, glide." He turned around. "Your account of the ravine glided."

The room went very quiet. My pulse did not, but I've had practice keeping my pulse private.

"Or," I said, "I'm simply a good witness. Clear-minded. You said details matter."

"I did." He came back to the table, and instead of sitting he stood over me, and there it was at last—a flicker of the thing under the neatness, the iron flame at his collar catching light. "And your details were excellent. One lace snapped. Blood pooling left. The horse below. All consistent with the scene I examined myself, two days ago, in that same ravine." He let that settle. "Except for one thing. The seal-case. You said it was cracked open and empty. But the case we recovered had been *burned*. Scorched, deliberately, with lamp oil, after the contents were removed. Fire destroys traces. Whoever took what was inside knew exactly what the Order would look for, and cleaned the vessel the way a careful man cleans a knife." He tilted his head. "Bandits don't burn things neatly. Bandits take and run. This was done by someone patient. Someone literate. Someone who understood what a seal means to an institution like ours."

He straightened. "I mention it only because it interests me. I haven't accused you of anything. I want you to understand that. I am not your enemy, Coral Galloway. I am the Order's memory, and memories are patient." At the door he paused, hand on the latch, and spoke without turning. "I'll be at Vespergate some weeks. The mountains are restless this year—raiders, strange tremors, couriers dying. If you recall anything else, anything at all, however small, my door is open. So is my ear." A beat. "So, regrettably, is my suspicion. Good day."

* * *

I made it back to the scriptorium. I sat down at my desk. I picked up my pen.

And I shook.

Not during—never during, that's the horror of it, during I had been calm as standing water, fluent, frictionless, every lie arriving dressed and punctual like a hired servant. But afterward, alone, with the funeral ledgers waiting and Ferren watching me with worried eyes across the aisle, my hand trembled so badly I blotted three lines of a widow's candle-tithe and had to scrape the vellum back to white.

Because he was right. That was the poison of it. Everything he said was right. The case *had* been burned—by me, that night in the cache, with lamp oil, carefully, because I understood what a seal means, because I am exactly the patient literate man he described and I had burned my fingerprints off the crime the way you'd cauterize a wound. He had reconstructed my sin from its ashes and then thanked me for the tea.

And underneath the fear, worse than the fear, was the other thing. The thing I'd felt in the corridor when Ferren believed me without question. The ease. Eleven years of invisible, obedient, debt-bound honesty, and it turns out all that time I was also learning to be a magnificent liar, the way a man locked in a cellar learns every crack in the ceiling. I had lied to an Inquisitor of the Third Chair, to his face, over tea, and part of me had *enjoyed* it—that cold bright pleasure of a perfect stroke landing, the scribe's satisfaction of a line coming out clean. I hated that part. I hated that I couldn't find its edges, couldn't tell where the terrified man ended and the talented one began.

That evening I went up onto the wall walk above the granary, where nobody goes after compline, and I looked north at Thornfell, a black jag against the last grey light, and I thought about the map sleeping under its stone thirteen miles away. Thirteen turns, the courier had said. The moon overhead was a nail-paring now, thin and sharp. Twelve turns remained after this one, give or take the fence's arithmetic, and somewhere in those turns Hale would either catch me or lose the scent, and somewhere in those turns the Ashen Court would come down out of the high passes looking for what I had hidden.

I pressed my palms against the cold stone and made myself a promise, which is a dangerous thing to do when you've just spent the afternoon demonstrating how cheaply promises can be counterfeited.

I would not become fluent. Whatever I did next—run, hide, burn the map, give it up, God help me, read it again—I would keep the friction. I would stay a man who felt the cost of every false word, because the moment lying stopped hurting was the moment I became the thing the Order built its temples out of.

Below me, in the gatehouse, a horse arrived at a gallop—no bell yet, just hoofbeats hammering the causeway, urgent, unannounced. A rider swung down, staggered, caught himself on the gatepost, and shouted up at the watchman in a voice raw with distance:

"Tremors. Three days, getting worse—the passes at Widow's Stair are *moving*—"

I went cold all over. Widow's Stair. My cache. My stone. My map, buried in a cliff face that had just started to shrug off its skin.

The thirteenth turn, it seemed, intended to collect early.
