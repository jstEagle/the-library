## 7. The Ambush at Widow's Stair

There is a particular quality to silence on a mountain path, and I had learned to read it the way other men read weather. The silence of an empty trail has a texture—wind working through scree, the small industry of pikas, water somewhere below doing its patient work. It is a busy silence. A honest one.

The silence at the mouth of Widow's Stair was not that.

I had come up from Greywater ford before dawn, taking the old smuggler's cut that switchbacked along the cliff face above the river gorge. The path was called Widow's Stair for reasons the name made plain: a stair of stone steps cut into the cliff by hands long dead, narrow enough that a loaded mule had to be walked with its flank to the drop, wide enough for a man if he didn't look down. Widows were made here regularly. The mountain kept its accounts current.

I had walked it perhaps forty times in my years of running salt and contraband for the temple's supply trains. I knew every loose step, every handhold of root and iron peg, every place where the cliff leaned out over nothing like a drunk leaning over a well. Which is why, when the silence told me what it told me, I believed it immediately—and then spent a full minute trying not to.

Because believing it meant stopping. And stopping on Widow's Stair meant standing still on a shelf of stone above a quarter mile of air, while whatever waited ahead of me waited knowing I had stopped.

I did what any sensible man does when his survival instinct speaks plainly. I ignored it and kept walking, because the alternative was standing on a cliff arguing with myself, and because there was no way back anyway—the path behind had gone into shadow, and my feet knew the way forward better than my fear knew anything.

*Thirteen turns,* the courier had whispered, blood bubbling between his teeth. *Thirteen turns.*

The moon overhead was rounding toward its twelfth fullness. I had been counting nights the way a condemned man counts stairs to the scaffold.

Three steps further, the silence resolved itself into a shape.

He stood where the path widened at the first landing—a flat shelf where smugglers traditionally rested and swore at their mules. He was dressed as a pilgrim, grey wool, walking staff, the dust of the road artfully applied. But pilgrims lean on their staffs. This man's staff was held like a spear, butt grounded, point loose in his grip, and he stood with his weight balanced the way soldiers stand, which is to say the way killers stand, which is to say wrong for a pilgrim in every particular that mattered.

"Brother," he said warmly. "You're late."

"I wasn't aware we had an appointment." My voice came out steady. I have always hated how steady my voice comes out. It is the voice of a man copying ledgers—accurate, uninvolved, recording disaster in a neat hand.

"The map," he said. No warmth now. Warmth had been a coat he'd put on for the approach; he took it off the way you'd take off something you didn't want bloodied. "The courier carried it. The courier died. Things pass from hand to hand on this road, brother, and we are simply collecting what falls."

"I'm a salt scribe," I said. "I carry ledgers."

"Then you won't mind showing us your satchel."

*Us.* The word landed a half-second before the sound did—the scrape of a boot above me, on the ledge overhanging the landing, where a second man had been crouched among the boulders watching my back the whole time. I hadn't heard him. That frightened me more than the staff-point did. In my mountains, I hear everything. Or I had, until men came who moved like the Ashen Court moves.

I turned, slowly, keeping my hands visible, the way you do when the arithmetic has already failed. The second man dropped down onto the path behind me—not jumping, flowing, the way water comes over a weir. Younger than the first. A scar through his eyebrow. His eyes went to my satchel with an appetite that had nothing casual in it.

A third figure remained above on the high ledge, half-hidden, holding a sling or a crossbow—I couldn't tell which, and the not-knowing sat in my chest like a swallowed stone.

Three of them. One of me. A path four feet wide, a drop of eight hundred feet, and nowhere to be but exactly where I was.

"Here's what happens now," the first man said conversationally, strolling closer, staff tapping stone. "You give us the satchel. We look inside. If it's ledgers, we apologize for troubling a servant of the Quiet Flame and send you on your way with our blessing." He smiled. "If it isn't ledgers, we thank you for your service to the waking world, and you go on your way regardless. Just—quicker."

There was a version of me, once, who would have handed over the satchel. That version had survived Thornfell for eleven years precisely by understanding that objects are replaceable and flesh is not. Hand them the map. Let them wake their sleeping god or fail at it. Let the Order keep its comfortable lie. None of it was mine. I was a debtor, a copyist, a man whose whole strategy for existence was to matter as little as possible.

But I thought of the courier in the ravine, his hand pressing wax and blood against my chest, spending his last breath on a stranger because a stranger was all that was left. *Thirteen turns.* And I thought of Marguerite Voss's eyes lingering on my satchel at the pass, and understood that she would report the seal, and that Hale—or someone like Hale—was already moving. Everyone wanted this map, and everyone who touched it died, and the only question the mountain was asking me was whose death it would be.

"You should know," I said, "that I'm very good at falling."

The first man laughed. It was a genuine laugh, which somehow made it worse. "Scribe, everyone's good at falling. It's the landing that takes practice."

He reached for the satchel strap.

Later—much later, in the long sleepless arithmetic of what followed—I would try to reconstruct the decision. There wasn't one. That's the truth I finally made peace with. There was no moment where Coral Galloway weighed his options and chose violence. There was only a body that had spent eleven years learning this cliff the way a lover learns a face, and a body that understood, faster than any mind could, that the landing above the third switchback was a slab of granite resting on a lip of shattered limestone, and that the limestone had been failing for a century, and that a man who knew where to put his weight could ask the mountain to do what three armed men could not be outrun past.

The first man's fingers closed on the strap. I let him take the weight of it—let him think the fight was won—and stepped backward off the path.

Not off the cliff. Off the *path*, onto the sloping apron of talus beside the third switchback, the one no sane person used because it funneled straight toward the drop. My heels found the two embedded stones I knew were there the way you find your own doorstep in the dark. And I threw myself sideways against the granite slab.

It moved the way I'd always known, in some buried corner of me, that it could move—a groan, a shudder, a grinding exhalation of stone, and then the lip of limestone beneath it simply gave up a job it had been failing at since before the temple was built.

The mountain came apart.

I have no clean memory of the next ten seconds. Memory, I've learned, edits violence for decency. What remains are fragments: the slab tilting; the first man's laugh dying in his throat; the younger one lunging for me and catching only air; the whole face of the slope liquefying into motion, tons of stone deciding all at once to become a river. I remember clawing across the talus toward the iron pegs of the old smuggler's route, the ones driven into the cliff a hundred years ago by men who'd needed a private way down, and I remember the sound—the sound above all things, a sound like the mountain clearing its throat after a thousand years of politeness.

And I remember faces. That's the part they don put in the songs, if anyone ever makes songs about this. The first man, the warm-voiced one, went over the edge still holding my satchel strap, and his face in the last instant wasn't angry or afraid. It was *surprised*, the pure animal surprise of a creature whose footing has betrayed it, and some part of me that will never be reprimanded noticed that he looked, in that final half-second, almost exactly like a man slipping on ice in a market square—an ordinary accident wearing extraordinary clothes.

The younger one nearly made it. He got both hands onto the path's edge, boots scrabbling against the sliding rock, and for one suspended moment he hung there with the scar through his eyebrow and his eyes fixed on mine, and I want to tell you I saw hatred there. I didn't. I saw a question. *Why?* Why had the ground chosen me and not him? Why had a salt scribe killed him?

I could have reached him. The arithmetic says so. Two feet of intact path between us, my hand extended, his grip already failing. Two feet, and a man's life hanging on whether I spent three seconds hauling him up.

I spent those seconds climbing instead.

His fingers opened. He made no sound at all on the way down, which is worse, somehow, than screaming. The gorge took him quietly, the way the gorge takes everything, and the dust rose up white around me like the mountain exhaling, and then there was only wind again, and my own breathing, ragged and obscene in its continuance.

The third man—the one on the high ledge with the sling or the crossbow—was gone. Fled, presumably, back over the ridge to report to whoever counted these men as assets. I would pay for that too, eventually. Reports travel. Grief travels faster.

I stood on the ruined landing with my hands shaking so badly I had to press them flat against the cliff to make them stop, and I took inventory, because inventory is what scribes do when the world breaks its columns.

One satchel: recovered from the talus, strap torn but holding, map intact. One body: mine, scraped, bleeding at the elbow, otherwise assembled. Two men: dead. Dead by rockslide, yes, dead by geology, dead by the mountain's ancient indifference—but set in motion by my shoulder, aimed by my knowledge, chosen by my weight. I had not pushed them. I had merely known, and acted on knowing, and the distinction felt like the thinnest page in the ledger.

In the temple scriptorium, we had a term for entries that couldn't be reconciled: *discrepant*. You flagged them in red and waited for a superior to resolve them. There was no superior for this. No column in any ledger I'd ever copied accounted for two lives entered on the debit side of my own survival.

I knelt at the path's edge and looked down. The gorge fog had swallowed everything. Somewhere below, under a hundred fathoms of mist, the mountain was already tidying itself, settling the dead into its long archives. Thornfell keeps better records than the Order ever will.

"I'm sorry," I said out loud, to the fog, to the men, to nobody. It was inadequate. It was also true, and I have since learned that those two qualities rarely travel together.

Then I did the thing that survivors do, the thing I would spend months being ashamed of: I checked the sun, calculated my remaining daylight, and started walking.

Because here is what the ambush taught me, in the mountain's blunt pedagogy: hiding was over. The Ashen Court knew the courier's route now, which meant they knew or would soon deduce the caches, the crossings, the fences. The Order would have Marguerite Voss's report of the seal. I was a grain of sand caught between two millstones, and the only thing more dangerous than carrying the map was letting either millstone alone decide what happened next.

Which left a third road, one I'd sworn I'd never take: finding out what the map actually said. Not guessing. Knowing. If men were going to die for it—had died, would die, two of them by my own hand not an hour past—then the least I owed the dead was comprehension.

There was a cache I trusted, three valleys east, an old smuggler's vault behind a waterfall where I could sit with light and time enough to read the thing properly. Three days' walk, if the weather held and no one else was waiting on any path I loved.

I descended the rest of Widow's Stair on legs that belonged to someone older, and at the bottom, where the trail met the river road, I passed a shrine the smugglers maintained to some minor saint of safe passage. Old habit made me touch the stone. New knowledge made me flinch at my own hand.

Two men were dead because of me. The mountain would call it gravity. The Order would call it self-defense. The Ashen Court would call it murder, and collect. And I suspected, with the weary certainty of a man who has copied enough confessions to know their shape, that the truth was all three at once, and that no ledger in the world was large enough to hold the entry.

I walked east. Behind me, the dust was still settling over Widow's Stair, fine and white, like ash from a fire that hadn't started yet.

And far to the north, faint as a heartbeat under the floor of the world, I felt—for the first time, though I would spend chapters refusing to admit it—a tremor. Small. Patient. Almost like something turning over in its sleep.

Almost like something counting moons.
