## 8. Taken by the Order

I had been a knight of the Order of the Quiet Flame for eleven years, and in that time I had learned three things with absolute certainty: that procedure protects the innocent, that instinct is the enemy of discipline, and that I was very good at not thinking about my family.

The road out of Widow's Hollow tested all three before noon.

The scribe—Galloway, Coral Galloway, third ledger-clerk of the Salt Road circuit, debt outstanding fourteen years and change—came down the switchback at midmorning looking like exactly what he was: a tired man carrying salt, ink, and nothing else. I had watched him from the rocks above for an hour while Ser Danel complained about his boots. He walked like a man who had memorized every stone on the path, which he had; the smuggler routes of Thornfell were famous, and the Order's own records noted that Galloway knew them better than any man in the temple's employ. That knowledge alone should have made him suspect. It didn't. Plenty of honest men know dishonest roads.

What made him suspect was the way he held his left shoulder.

A man carrying salt keeps it balanced. A man carrying something precious keeps it close to the body, high, where a blade can't reach it quickly. Galloway's left arm curved inward just slightly, just enough, the way a mother carries an infant or a thief carries a purse. I'd have missed it if I hadn't spent two days staring at a dead courier's hands.

"Ser Voss." He stopped when we stepped onto the road, and there it was—the flicker. Not guilt. Fear doing its best impression of boredom. "Is there trouble?"

"There's always trouble," Ser Danel said cheerfully. He was twenty-three and still believed the world rewarded honesty.

"A courier was found dead in the ravines below Greywater," I said. "Throat cut. Three days ago."

"I'm sorry to hear it." His face did everything right. Concern, appropriate gravity, a small frown of a man calculating whether the murder affected his own safety. Too much, actually. Honest men are stingier with their sympathy; they spend it only where they feel it. Galloway distributed his evenly across the whole conversation, like butter over cheap bread.

"We're questioning travelers," I said. "Standard procedure. You'll understand."

"Of course." He set down his pack without being asked. That, too, was correct. Innocent men cooperate eagerly because they have nothing to fear. Guilty men also cooperate eagerly, because refusing is worse. Procedure, I have found, sorts nothing. It merely gives everyone a script, and the guilty learn their lines faster.

Danel went through the pack. Salt, as declared. A spare tunic. Rations. Ink, three pots. Quills. A ledger of temple accounts, meticulously kept—I glanced at it longer than I needed to, because the handwriting was beautiful in the way that only lonely people's handwriting is beautiful, each letter given more care than it could possibly deserve.

And then, wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom, a wax seal. Broken, but intact enough to read.

I knew the seal. Of course I knew the seal. Every knight who had stood watch over the evidence tent at Greywater knew it: the Quiet Flame inverted, flame-downward, pressed in black wax instead of the Order's red. The courier had carried dispatches under that mark, which meant someone inside the Order had sealed them, which meant the murderer was almost certainly one of us, which meant Inquisitor Hale would arrive within the week and begin asking questions no one wanted answered.

The same mark was now sitting in the bottom of a temple scribe's pack.

"Ser Danel," I said quietly. "Give us the road."

He looked at me, looked at Galloway, shrugged, and wandered off to bother his horse. Bless him. He would make a fine knight someday precisely because he noticed nothing.

* * *

"You want to tell me about this?" I held up the oilcloth bundle.

Galloway's eyes went to it and came back to my face with admirable speed. "It's wrapping. From a shipment of—"

"Coral Galloway." I said his name the way Hale said it, flat and complete, and watched him flinch at the sound. "Fourteen years indentured. No family listed. No debts beyond the temple's. No vices except, according to your supervisor, an excessive fondness for walking at night. You copy ledgers by day and you memorize smuggler roads by dark, and until three days ago, no one in the history of this circuit had reason to look at you twice."

"That sounds like a character reference."

"It's a eulogy," I said. "For the life you were living. Because that seal—" I turned it so the black wax caught the light, "—was on the dispatches of a murdered courier, and it is now in your pack, and you are standing on the last road between Greywater and anywhere."

He was quiet for a moment. Wind moved through the pines above us, that dry mountain whisper that always sounds like someone deciding something.

"If I say I found it," he said finally, "you won't believe me."

"Try me."

"If I say I stole it, you'll arrest me."

"I'm going to arrest you either way," I said. "The question is what I write in the report. There's a difference between a scribe who picked up litter and a scribe who murdered a King's courier for what he carried. One of those men hangs. The other one answers questions."

Something shifted in his face—not confession, not yet, but the exhaustion of a man who has been holding a door shut for three days and feels the hinges going. I recognized it. I had worn that exact expression for most of my adolescence.

"The courier was alive when I found him," Galloway said. "In the ravine past the second ford. He was dying. He put this in my hands and said—" his voice caught, just slightly, "—'thirteen turns.' Then he died. I didn't kill him. I couldn't even save him."

"And you didn't report it."

"To whom?" He laughed, short and bitter. "The Order? The Order whose seal was on his papers? Ser Voss, I am a debtor. My testimony weighs less than the paper it's written on. If I walk into the temple and say 'a courier died and gave me a mystery,' do you know what happens? Nothing good. Either I'm ignored, or I'm implicated, or—inquisitors being what they are—I'm *interesting*. I have spent fourteen years making sure no one finds me interesting."

There it was. Not a confession of guilt but a confession of philosophy, and somehow that was worse, because I understood it completely. I had built my entire knighthood on the principle of never being interesting.

"Show me what's under the wrapping," I said.

"No."

"Galloway—"

"No." His chin came up, and for the first time I saw the spine under all that weariness. "You'll take it if you take it. But I'm not the one who opens it. Whatever's in there, I didn't ask for it, and I'm not handing it to the Order like a good little dog, because the last man who carried it for the Order is lying in a ravine with his throat opened."

We stared at each other. Somewhere downslope, Danel was singing to his horse, badly.

Here is what procedure told me: bind him, take the package, deliver both to the garrison, file the report, let Hale do the rest. Here is what procedure promised: that Galloway would hang or break, that whatever slept inside that oilcloth would pass into the hands of men who might well have killed to keep it buried, and that I would sleep soundly because I had followed every rule correctly.

Here is what instinct told me: this frightened, stubborn, unremarkable man was the only person in this whole affair who hadn't lied to me yet.

"Walk," I said.

His shoulders dropped half an inch. "Ser Voss—"

"You're under arrest. For obstruction, pending inquiry—concealing evidence relating to a capital crime. Not murder. Not yet." I took his arm above the elbow, professional, impersonal, and felt him go rigid under my hand. "You'll come to the garrison at Highmoor. You'll be questioned. And somewhere between here and there, Coral Galloway, you're going to decide whether you trust me enough to tell me the truth, because I promise you—" and I leaned in close enough that only he could hear, "—the man coming to ask these questions next wears an inquisitor's gray, and he does not give chances. This is yours. One chance. Don't waste it on another lie."

* * *

We made camp that night in a hollow off the road, because Galloway's ankle was turning on the scree and because I told myself I wanted to reach Highmoor with my prisoner able to walk. Both things were true. Neither was the whole truth.

Danel took first watch. I sat across the fire from my prisoner and pretended to review my notes, which meant I watched him pretend to sleep.

"You knew the courier," he said at last, without opening his eyes.

"I read the body. That's different."

"You knew the seal."

"Every knight on this circuit knows the seal since Greywater." I fed a stick to the fire. "Black wax, inverted flame. It's not an official mark. It hasn't been used openly in a hundred years. Which means either someone inside the Order is playing games with old symbols, or—" I stopped.

"Or it's not a game," Galloway finished softly. He sat up. Firelight did strange things to his face, hollowing it, aging it. "Ask me what thirteen turns means, Ser Voss."

"Don't."

"Ask me."

"Because you've decided to trust me? After six hours?" I laughed, and it came out harsher than intended. "Scribes don't get to deputize knights, Galloway. I have a report to write. I have a procedure to follow. If I start chasing riddles from dying couriers, I stop being a knight and become—"

"Become what?"

*A person*, said something in the back of my skull, in my mother's voice, in the voice I had sworn at nineteen never to hear again. I stood up abruptly and walked to the edge of the firelight, where the dark came down between the pines like a held breath.

Behind me, Galloway spoke to my back, quiet and even. "You looked at the seal and you didn't call Danel over. You sent him away. You could have searched me on the road with a witness and been done with it. Instead you arrested me alone, on a charge you invented soft, and gave me a chance you didn't have to give." A pause. "Whatever your procedure says, Ser Voss, your feet already decided."

I stood in the dark for a long time, listening to the fire crackle and the mountain breathe, hating him with the particular intensity one reserves for people who are right.

Because here is the thing I have never written in any report: I knew what the inverted flame meant. I had known since I was a child, before I ever swore anything to anyone. My father kept one, a black wax impression, hidden in the lining of his strongbox, and he kept it the way men keep heresies—carefully, lovingly, in secret—and when the Order's investigators came to our house the winter I was twelve, it was the only thing he burned before he opened the door.

They took him anyway. Heresy by association, they called it later, though the association was never named. I swore my knighthood four years after, and the Grandmaster who accepted my oath looked at my family name for a long moment before saying, *Then serve well, Voss, and bury it.*

I had spent eleven years burying it. Burying my father, my name, the black seal, the questions. Procedure was how I buried things. Procedure was a shovel that never got heavy, because you never had to look at what you were digging.

And now a dead courier's seal lay wrapped in oilcloth ten feet behind me, black wax and inverted flame, and a debtor with beautiful handwriting was watching me decide who I was.

When I turned around, he was still awake, watching me with those careful, patient eyes.

"Highmoor by tomorrow night," I said. "After that, it's out of my hands. Hale arrives within the week." I hesitated, and then, because apparently my feet had indeed decided something my mouth hadn't approved: "If you're going to tell me the truth, Galloway, tell me before Highmoor. Tell me while it can still matter."

He nodded slowly. Then, as I lay back and stared up at the wheeling stars through the pine branches, he said one more thing, so quietly I might have dreamed it:

"The gods aren't dead, Ser Voss. Your Order knows it. And the thirteenth moon turns full in five weeks."

I did not sleep that night. I wrote my report instead, by firelight, describing a broken seal and an obstructive scribe, and I left out everything that mattered—and hated my own hand for every careful word.
