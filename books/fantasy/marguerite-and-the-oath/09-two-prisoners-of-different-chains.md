## 9. Two Prisoners of Different Chains

They put me in a cart.

Not a cell, not a wagon with bars—a plain supply cart with a canvas top and a floor that smelled of old salt fish, as if the Order of the Quiet Flame couldn't be bothered to distinguish between a prisoner and a shipment of dried cod. I suppose there was a compliment buried in that somewhere. Cod doesn't lie. Cod doesn't carry maps that could unmake a century of doctrine. Cod is exactly what it appears to be, which is more than I could say for myself by the end of that day.

My wrists were bound with scribe's cord—the soft kind we used to bundle manuscripts, which struck me as either an oversight or a joke played at my expense by someone in the quartermaster's office who knew me. Six years of copying ledgers, and here I was, trussed like a misfiled folio.

Marguerite Voss rode beside the cart on a grey mare that had opinions about mud. She hadn't spoken since the arrest. That silence had a texture to it—not the flat quiet of someone with nothing to say, but the dense, pressurized quiet of someone holding a great deal of something behind her teeth and waiting to see if it would keep.

I tried sleeping. The road up to the garrison switchbacked through pine shadow, and every jolt of the wheels reminded my ribs of the rockslide at Widow's Stair, where two men had died because I'd pulled a lever of stone rather than hand over a piece of vellum. Two men. I kept saying it to myself like a lesson I hadn't learned yet. Two men, Coral. You did that.

"You're counting," Marguerite said, without looking at me.

"I'm resting."

"You're counting your breaths. You do it when you're frightened. You did it on the pass, too."

So she'd been watching me even then, before she knew what I carried. There was a thought to keep a man warm at night, except it wasn't night, and it didn't warm anything.

"Is that a knightly skill?" I asked. "Reading prisoners? Is it in the manual between *How to Sit a Horse Judgmentally* and *Advanced Glaring*?"

Her jaw did something complicated. I would learn, over the following weeks, that this was the closest she came to laughing while on duty—some small civil war fought entirely in the muscles of her face, duty on one side and whatever lived underneath on the other. On the pass she'd let duty win. I was counting on the under-thing eventually staging a coup.

"The garrison has cells," she said instead. "Real ones. Stone floors. You'll miss the cod smell."

"Threatening me with better accommodations. You really are Order-trained."

She turned her horse ahead of the cart and didn't answer, and I lay back against the salt-fish barrels and counted my breaths until the mountain swallowed us.

* * *

The garrison at Highmoor Crag had been built by people who believed fear was a building material. Everything was stone and iron and sightlines—corridors designed so that no one could ever not be watched, windows slitted like suspicious eyes. They gave me a cell with a straw pallet and a bucket, and took my boots, which seemed gratuitous. Where exactly did they think I'd go barefoot?

The hours passed. I catalogued them, because cataloguing is what I do; it's how I survived six years of indenture without going mad or honest. Somewhere past what I judged to be dusk—for the slit window gave me a stripe of sky the color of a bruise—boots stopped outside my door, and the lock turned, and Marguerite Voss came in carrying a lantern and two bowls of stew.

Two bowls. I noted that. A guard bringing one bowl is procedure. Two is a decision.

She set one on the floor near me, out of arm's reach, and sat on the stool by the door with her own, her sword across her knees like a chaperone.

"Eat," she said.

"Is it poisoned?"

"If we wanted you dead you'd have died on the road. It's mutton."

"It's *allegedly* mutton." I ate it anyway. It was allegedly mutton. "So. Interrogation by supper. Is this the part where you befriend me?"

"No." She considered. "This is the part where I ask you a question I should have asked on the pass, and you decide whether to answer it honestly, and depending on your answer, whether you live to see spring."

Charming. At least she was direct. I have been lied to by professionals—inquisitors, debt collectors, the temple itself—and there is a certain courtesy in a threat delivered plainly, like clean water after wine.

"Ask," I said.

"The courier on the salt road. The one whose death started all this." Her eyes were steady in the lantern light. "Did you kill him?"

And there it was. Not *do you have the map*, not *what does the seal mean*—she'd already told me she recognized the courier's wax, which meant she knew more than she'd admitted, which meant she'd been lying too, in her way—but simply: *did you kill him*. The question underneath all her other questions.

"No," I said. "He was dying when I found him. In a ravine off the smuggler's cut below Greywater. He pressed the map into my hands and said—" I hesitated. Saying it aloud made it realer than I wanted. "He said 'thirteen turns.' Then he was gone."

"Thirteen turns of what?"

"The moon. I thought he was delirious. Then I read the map, and I stopped thinking that."

She was quiet for a long moment. The lantern flame leaned and righted itself. When she spoke again, her voice had changed register—lower, careful, the voice of someone stepping onto ice and testing each stride.

"What does the map show, Coral?"

"Not treasure." I set down the bowl. Here was the fork in the road, and I want it recorded that I saw it clearly: I could have lied. I was good at lying; I'd spent the evening proving it to Inquisitor Hale back at the temple, spinning a flawless account of an ordinary supply run while guilt chewed through me like rot through wet timber. Lying to Marguerite would have been easy. That was precisely why I didn't. "It shows a vault beneath Thornfell. Sealed. And the script around the seal—it's liturgical. Old rite, pre-Schism. And it doesn't say *here lies treasure* or *here lies danger*."

I made myself look at her.

"It says *here they sleep*. Plural. The gods the Order burns incense for and mourns and insists are dead, Marguerite—they're not dead. They're asleep. Under the mountain. And somebody thinks thirteen moons is how long they've got."

* * *

You must understand what Coral Galloway does not understand about himself, which is nearly everything, but chiefly this: he tells the truth the way other men gamble—recklessly, compulsively, and always telling himself it will be the last time.

I sat across from him in that cell and listened to him dismantle the foundation of my life, and I could not look away.

Sixteen years I have worn the flame. I took the oath at fourteen, kneeling on cold stone while the Prior intoned the words, and I meant them—I need him to know that, whoever reads this, if anyone does—I *meant* them. The gods are dead; the Order keeps their memory; memory is a sacred trust. That was the whole of my faith, simple as a blade. Dead gods cannot be betrayed. Dead gods make no demands. All that remains is duty, and duty is a thing you can hold.

And now a scribe with ink-stained fingers and a mouth built for trouble was telling me the dead were breathing.

"You've seen this map," I said. My voice came out flatter than I intended. "With your own eyes."

"I copied half of it from memory into the margin of a psalter, which I imagine makes me a heretic twice over." He almost smiled. It didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "The rest I can recite."

"Why?" The question tore out of me before I could dress it properly. "Why tell me? Hale will have you flayed for less. The Ashen Court wants what you know badly enough to kill for it. Every instinct you have—and you have *survivor's* instincts, Galloway, I watched you use them on the pass—you should be lying to me. Instead you hand me the one truth that destroys everything. Why?"

He looked at me for a long time. Lantern light does strange things to a face; his went from boyish to ancient and back again, like a manuscript held near a flame.

"Because you asked the right question," he said finally. "Everyone else asks *where is the map*. You asked *did I kill him*. Nobody's asked me that since Widow's Stair. Not Hale—he knows I didn't, he just wants to see if I'll confess to something he can use. Not the Court—they don't care. You asked it like it mattered." He shrugged, and winced, and I remembered the rockslide report. Two dead. His first blood, though he'd never say so. "A man can only be treated like a thing for so long before he starts handing out truths just to feel like a person again."

That was not the whole reason. I knew it wasn't. But I also knew—knight's instinct, the kind they can't teach—that pressing him would close him like a fist. So I did the harder thing. I answered a question of his own.

"You want to know why I really arrested you," I said. "Not the seal. Not the orders."

"I assumed the seal."

"The seal gave me cause. But I'd already decided." I looked at the lantern rather than at him, because some things are easier said to firelight. "On the pass. You stood there with contraband in your bag and a lie in your mouth, and you were *terrified*, and you lied anyway, and your hands shook, and you—" I stopped. Recast it. "Most smugglers beg. Or run. Or offer bribes they can't afford. You offered me directions to a shrine. You said, *the view's worth the detour.* Who says that to a knight hunting a murderer?"

"A man with nothing left to lose," he said quietly.

"A man trying to give me something. I've thought about it for weeks. Why give a stranger a gift at the worst moment of his life?" I finally looked at him. "I think because you watch people. All those years invisible in the scriptorium—you learned to read faces the way you read script. And you read mine, and you saw—" 

"Saw a woman who hated her job," he said. "Sorry. Saw a knight performing conviction she didn't feel. It was written all over you. Forgive me, but you have the most legible face I've ever copied."

Despite everything—despite the vault and the heresy and the two men dead under the rocks at Widow's Stair—I laughed. It escaped before I could stop it, short and unwilling, and his answering grin was so startled, so genuinely pleased with himself, that I understood I was in considerably more danger than Hale's inquisitors could ever put me in.

* * *

We talked until the lantern burned low. I'll not pretend it was strategy. Some of it was, at first—each of us circling, probing, an interrogation wearing conversation's clothes. But somewhere past midnight the masks got heavy, and what remained was two tired people arguing theology over cold mutton, which is either the definition of folly or of faith, I haven't decided.

"Here's what I can't reconcile," he said. He'd gone from sitting against the wall to sitting forward, animated, gesturing with a crust of bread like a lecturer. "Say the Order knows. Say the elders have known for decades that the gods sleep. Fine. Terrible, but fine—maybe they thought the truth would start a war, maybe they thought worship of sleeping gods would curdle into something worse. I can construct the argument. What I can't forgive is the *debt*. Because that's what it is, isn't it? They indentured me at nineteen for my father's borrowing. Six years of my life priced and sold. And the whole time, the institution holding my paper was built on a lie. My debt is real. Theirs is the fiction. How does that arithmetic survive?"

"Oaths aren't arithmetic," I said.

"No. They're worse. They're *story*." He tossed the crust back into the bowl. "And stories are written by whoever holds the pen. Tell me I'm wrong."

"You're not wrong." I heard my own voice go quiet. "But you're incomplete. An oath freely given isn't a chain, Coral. It's a shape. It's what I chose to be when I didn't trust what I'd be otherwise." I wrapped my hands around my knees, the way I used to during novitiate vigils. "You think I don't know about debts? I owe the Order everything. And I mean that in the way you'd hate."

His eyes sharpened. "Go on."

"And if I tell you the rest, you'll have a weapon. That's what truth is, in the end—it's only neutral until someone picks it up. You talk about truth like it's clean. Truth is the sharpest thing there is. The Order hoards it. The Court bleeds people for it. And you—" I nodded at him. "You handed it to me tonight because you wanted, just once, to be seen accurately. That's not principle. That's hunger."

He was silent a long moment. The lantern guttered, recovered.

"That's the most honest thing anyone's said to me in six years," he said finally, "and I resent every word of it."

"Good. We're even. I resented the psalter margins."

Another laugh, his this time, low and surprised out of him. And I sat there in a stone cell at midnight, guarding a prisoner I was increasingly unsure needed guarding, and caught myself thinking a thought I had no business thinking: that I could listen to this man argue until the thirteenth moon rose and fell and rose again.

I stood, because standing is what you do with thoughts like that.

"Sleep," I said, taking the bowls. "Tomorrow Hale arrives with writs from the temple. Whatever you're going to decide about that map, decide it fast."

"Coral." I paused at the door. He was watching me from the pallet, shadows pooling in the hollows of his face. "Whose side are you on? Honestly. Just for one sentence."

And the terrible thing, the thing I have not yet confessed to anyone, is that the honest answer rose in me fully formed, and I swallowed it, and gave him the safe one instead:

"Mine. Ask me again when yours looks survivable."

I locked the door on his smile—because he was smiling, damn him, like he'd gotten exactly the answer he wanted—and walked the corridor with two empty bowls and the growing, vertiginous certainty that the chains in that cell had not been on the prisoner at all.

Behind me, faint through the stone, I heard him begin to count his breaths.

And beneath us both—though neither of us knew it yet, though the mountain kept its counsel for three more nights—the first tremor moved through the deep rock of Thornfell, slow as a sleeper turning over.
