## 10. The Garrison Burns

I have stood vigil over the dead before. I have knelt in ash after temple fires, recited the names of fallen brothers-in-arms until my voice went hoarse, and once — a story I do not tell at mess — I held a dying man's hand for six hours because he was afraid and no one else would stay.

None of it prepared me for the particular misery of being *responsible* for a prisoner I did not want to like.

Coral Galloway had been in my custody for four days, and in that time he had managed to be polite, useful, funny in a dry way that snuck up on me, and — most damning of all — honest. Not the polished honesty of a man performing innocence for an inquisitor. The tired kind. The kind that costs something.

He had told me about the courier. All of it. The ravine, the blood, the map pressed into his hands with the last strength in a stranger's body. He had shown me the seal — the Ashen Court's mark, a flame inverted, which I recognized from files I had no business recognizing it from — and he had watched my face while he did it, the way a man watches a trap he suspects is about to close.

It should have closed. Procedure said it closed. Sergeant Odo kept looking at me across the garrison yard as if to say, *Knight-Captain, the prisoner confessed to possessing contraband heretical materials, are we marching him to the Order or not?*

And I kept saying, "In the morning."

Four mornings running.

"You know," Coral said on the fourth evening, sitting across the brazier from me in the garrison's small common room, his wrists unbound because I had decided — against every rule I had ever sworn to — that they would remain so, "in the stories, the knight who captures the fugitive doesn't share her supper with him."

"In the stories," I said, "the fugitive isn't helpful about supply routes through Thornfell."

"I'm a very useful criminal."

"You're not a criminal at all. That's rather the problem."

He looked at me over the fire then, and there was something in his face I didn't have a name for yet. Something patient. As though he were waiting for me to catch up to a conclusion he'd reached days ago.

The garrison at Greywatch Pass was not built for comfort. It was built to hold the throat of the mountain road, a squat gray fist of a fort with walls older than the Order itself, and on cold nights the wind came down off Thornfell and made the shutters rattle like teeth. There were thirty-one souls inside it that night. I knew the number. I always knew the number. It was part of what I was — the knight who counted, who checked, who kept the ledger of small duties balanced.

Thirty-one souls. Remember that number. I still do.

* * *

I woke past midnight because the dogs went silent.

Not barking. Silent. Anyone who has served a winter posting knows the difference between a quiet night and a wrong one, and this was the wrong kind. A garrison's dogs bark at wind, at deer, at their own shadows. They go silent only when whatever is outside is worse than anything they know how to announce.

I was out of my bunk with my sword before my mind finished the thought. In the corridor, Odo was already moving, half-armored, his face gray in the lamplight.

"Captain, the east watchtower—"

The arrow took out the lamp beside his head and buried itself in the doorframe where his skull had been a heartbeat earlier. Black-fletched. Ashen Court work; I'd seen their fletching in evidence rooms, catalogued under *historical*, because the Ashen Court was supposed to be a century dead.

The night caught fire all at once.

They came over the east wall in silence — actual silence, which no bandit company achieves and which told me these were not bandits — perhaps twenty of them in dark leathers, and behind them came the fire. Not torches thrown carelessly but fire *placed*, deliberate, jars of burning pitch set against the grain stores and the stables and the barracks doors, and I understood within three heartbeats that this was not a raid.

This was erasure. Witnesses, records, the courier's trail — all of it was supposed to burn, and everyone inside with it.

"TO ARMS!" My voice cracked across the yard. "Formation on the well! Defend the water!"

Because that was the whole battle, right there, in one sentence: they had fire, and we had one well, and everything between those two facts was going to be decided in the next hour.

The garrison woke badly and fought well. Soldiers stumbled from burning barracks into arrow-hail and still formed ranks, because drill lives deeper than sleep. Odo took a squad to the stable to cut loose the horses before the roof came down. Sister Anwen, our garrison healer, was dragging wounded toward the chapel, which was stone and might hold.

And I — Knight-Captain Marguerite Voss, sworn shield of the Quiet Flame, keeper of oaths older than my family name — I stood in the burning yard and thought, with terrible clarity:

*They came for the courier's trail. The courier's trail leads to Coral Galloway.*

Which meant the prisoner block was either already burning or about to be, and some part of me that had nothing to do with duty was moving before the rest of me agreed to it.

* * *

The prisoner block was a converted cellar, two cells, iron-banded door. When I got there the corridor beyond was full of smoke and one Ashen raider was pouring oil under the cell door, methodical as a scribe.

I killed him. I want to say it cleanly, the way the Order teaches you to say it — swiftly, mercifully, without hatred — but the truth is that when I saw the oil jug in his hand and understood what he intended, something in me went cold and efficient and glad, and I have been a long time pretending that part of me doesn't exist.

The door was hot. I wrapped my cloak around my hands and hauled it open anyway, and the smoke rolled out over me like a living thing, and inside—

Inside, Coral Galloway was standing on his cot, holding his blanket over the barred window slit, beating back the flames trying to crawl through it. His eyes found mine through the murk and I will remember, all my life, that his first expression wasn't relief.

It was apology. As if *he* were sorry to have caused me trouble.

"The window's too narrow," he coughed. "I've been trying—"

"Hold your breath and get down."

The lock had half-melted; the key was somewhere in my belt pouch and my hands were shaking. I broke it instead, shoulder against iron, twice, three times, the heat peeling paint off the frame in curling black ribbons, and on the third blow the door gave and I grabbed a handful of his shirt and pulled him out into a corridor that was mostly orange light and roaring.

We made it eight steps before the ceiling beam came down.

I don't remember deciding to push him clear. I remember the sound — a wet, splintering crack — and I remember the weight landing across my shoulders and back, and I remember thinking, with absurd detachment, *that's my sword arm pinned, that's inconvenient,* and then the pain arrived and detached stopped being available.

Fire behaves strangely in a burning building. It has currents, moods. A pocket of clean air sat low along the eastern wall, and Coral — coughing, blind, half my weight in muscle — dragged me into it. Then he put his shoulder under the beam and lifted.

A scribe. A debt-bound copyist who'd spent his life avoiding exactly this kind of trouble, lifting a burning timber off a knight sent to drag him to trial, and he didn't run. That was the thing. The beam came off me, and I rolled clear, choking, and he was still *there*, hauling me up by both arms, saying something I couldn't hear over the fire.

"What?" I rasped.

"I said, *can you walk?*"

"Can *you*?"

"No," he said honestly. "But one of us has to be dramatic about it later, and I nominate you."

I laughed. Gods forgive me, in the middle of a burning garrison with my brothers' screams in the yard, I laughed, and something in my chest that had been rigid for years shifted half an inch and did not shift back.

* * *

The yard was worse than the corridors. Half the east range was gone, the stable roof had fallen and Odo's horse-cutting squad had become a firefight among the paddocks, and the well — our one argument against the fire — was ringed by three Ashen raiders keeping anyone from reaching it.

What happened next happened fast, and I've assembled it afterward from pieces, the way you reconstruct any disaster.

Coral pulled me left, away from the fighting, along the base of the wall toward the postern gate — smuggler's instinct, he'd have called it, knowing how buildings die. And in the light of the burning granary I saw the Ashen commander. Tall, gray-cloaked, standing apart from the slaughter with a drawn blade, watching the prisoner block door I'd broken open. Watching *us*.

He raised a hand. Two archers turned.

There was no time for the arithmetic I usually do — threat assessment, rules of engagement, what the Order permits. There was only the fact that I had a knife in my boot and a body between those archers and a man who had just carried me out of a fire, and my body moved on its own authority.

I threw the knife into the nearer archer's throat. The second loosed — and Coral twisted me aside by the arm, the shaft hissing past my ear close enough to part hair, and then we were through the postern gate and into the rocks above the pass, and the garrison of Greywatch burned behind us like a fallen star.

* * *

We climbed until the firelight was a smudge below us and the cold was real again. Then my legs quit, and I sat down hard on a shelf of granite, and the shaking started — the delayed kind, the kind that comes after, when the body presents its bill.

Coral crouched in front of me. He'd found a soldier's discarded cloak somewhere on the climb; he put it around my shoulders without asking, the way you'd tend someone you'd known for years instead of four days.

"Breathe," he said. "Slowly. Like copying a long line — you can't rush it or you lose your place."

"That's ridiculous."

"It works. I use it for panic." He settled beside me, close enough that the cloak covered us both, and for a while neither of us spoke. Below, the garrison glowed. Somewhere in that glow were people I was responsible for, and I could not make myself look away from it.

"How many?" he asked quietly.

"Thirty-one were alive at sundown." My voice came out flat, which was the only way it could come out. "I'll know the count at dawn or I'll go down there and count them myself."

"They came to kill witnesses. To the courier. To the map." He paused. "To me."

"Yes."

"So if I'd surrendered to you properly — if you'd marched me to the Order when you arrested me—"

"Don't." The word came out sharper than I intended. "Don't you dare take this onto yourself. This is *their* doing. Men who burn sleeping soldiers in their beds don't get to have their motives laundered by the conscience of the man they hunted."

He was quiet a moment. "You didn't march me to the Order, though. Four mornings you said 'in the morning.' Why?"

And there it was. The question I'd been walking around for four days, sitting now in the starlight between us, patient as he was.

I could have given him the procedural answer. *I was awaiting confirmation of the seal's provenance.* I could have given him the strategic answer. *A prisoner is more valuable interrogated slowly.* I am a good liar; I proved that years ago, in a different life, swearing vows I'd been taught to swear.

Instead I heard myself say: "Because I read the courier's file before you ever showed me the seal. Three months ago. And the report of his death didn't add up, and I asked a question about it, and I was told the matter was closed. And I let it be closed, because letting things be closed is what I'm *for*." I stared at the burning fort below. "So when I arrested you, I wasn't hunting a murderer. I was trying to reopen a question I'd already agreed to bury. Everything since has been me discovering how much of my oath is rope tied around a lie."

Coral said nothing for a long time. The wind moved. Below us, a section of the west wall collapsed in a bloom of sparks, and I flinched, and he didn't pretend not to notice, and he also didn't comment, which was its own kindness.

"My debt to the temple," he said finally, "is eleven years old. I used to think if I just kept my head down and copied my pages, the ledger would balance and I'd walk free. Then a dying man handed me a map, and I learned the ledger was never mine alone. Everyone in that temple is a line in someone else's book." He looked at me sideways. "Sounds like you've been carrying a similar page."

"Mine's written in family ink," I said. "Worse."

He almost smiled. "Another chapter, then."

And here is the thing I must set down honestly, because this account may be all that survives of this night: in that moment, on the granite above the burning garrison, I had every reason to take him back. He was injured, exhausted, unarmed. The Order would want him. Duty wanted him. Every vow I had spoken at nineteen, kneeling on cold marble, wanted him delivered in irons.

I said, "The pass forks a mile north. Left goes down to the salt road and the Order's relief column — they'll come at dawn, if there's a column left to come. Right goes into deep Thornfell, on roads only you know."

Coral went very still. "Marguerite."

"You saved my life tonight. Twice — the beam, and the archer. The Order acknowledges debts of blood; it's one of the few laws we keep properly." I pulled the cloak tighter and made myself meet his eyes. "So here is my accounting. I owe you a life. I intend to pay it. After that—" My voice did something I didn't authorize. "After that I don't know what I am. But I know I can't hand you to men who burn garrisons, and I can't hand you to an Order that ordered the matter closed, and I find that I *cannot*, Coral, I physically cannot, watch you hang for carrying a dead man's truth."

The words hung in the cold air between us, and I realized I had just done the one thing a knight must never do: I had said the oath out loud and found it wanting, in front of a witness.

Coral reached over and, very gently, straightened the scorched collar of my armor — such a small, absurd gesture, a scribe tidying a knight — and said, "Then we'd better both figure out what you are. Because the Ashen Court knows your face now, and so does the Order, and I suspect the only people left on our side of any ledger are each other."

Below us, dawn began to consider the mountains, gray and slow, and the garrison of Greywatch smoked like a candle going out.

I did not recapture him. Write that down. Whatever tribunal eventually reads this — whatever inquisitor, whatever court, whatever god waking under the stone — let the record show that Marguerite Voss, Knight-Captain of the Quiet Flame, sat on a rock in the dark beside her prisoner, shared his cloak, and chose.

Gods help me. I chose.

Far to the south, faint as a heartbeat through the granite we sat on, the mountain trembled — once, softly, like something turning over in its sleep.

Coral felt it too. I saw it in his face.

"We need to move," he said. "And Marguerite — before we decide anything else, there's something under Thornfell you need to see. Because if the Order lied to *you*, you deserve to see the proof with your own eyes."

I stood. I buckled my sword belt over the borrowed cloak. And I turned north, into deep Thornfell, away from every road that led home.
