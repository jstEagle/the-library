## 11. Fugitives

There is a particular kind of silence that follows a burning building. I don't mean the absence of sound—there was plenty of sound, the garrison groaning as it settled into its own ashes, the horses screaming in the paddock until they didn't, Marguerite's breathing beside me, ragged and furious and alive. What I mean is the silence inside a person, the moment when every plan you had—every careful, cowardly little plan—burns up with the roof beams, and you're left standing in the cold holding nothing but your own name.

I had spent eleven years building that name into something small enough to hide. Coral Galloway, scribe, debtor, nobody. A man who copied other people's numbers because his own had run out.

And now here I was at midnight, on the wrong side of a burned garrison, running for the treeline with a knight of the Order of the Quiet Flame at my heels—and she wasn't chasing me. She was following me. Which was somehow worse.

"Left," I said, and veered left.

She didn't question it. That frightened me more than anything else that night. Marguerite Voss questioned everything—it was her defining feature, like a blade's edge is its defining feature—but she took my word about the left fork without a flicker of hesitation, because the garrison behind us was still throwing embers at the sky and whatever instinct had kept her alive through ten years of soldiering had apparently decided that the debt-ridden scribe knew something worth knowing.

The smuggler path began where the garrison road ended: a seam in the rock no wider than a goat's opinion, invisible unless you'd been shown it by a man named Dree who'd been paid to show it and paid again to forget it. I slipped through sideways, satchel first, and heard Marguerite exhale behind me in the dark—a short, controlled breath, the sound of a woman cataloguing her humiliations for later review.

"This leads where?" she asked.

"Away."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have tonight." I felt along the wall with my fingertips, counting handholds. "Ask me again when we're not lit up like a festival against that skyline."

She glanced back. The garrison burn painted the clouds orange, and against that glow her face was all angles and ash. "Hale will assume we died in it," she said. "For perhaps two days. Then he'll count the bodies."

"He'll find two raiders and a cook."

"The cook?"

"He ran past me toward the fire. I tried to stop him." I said it flatly, because there was no other way to say it. "He wouldn't listen."

Marguerite was quiet for three steps. Then: "You could have let him be someone else's problem."

"I did. He just didn't agree to be mine."

Another silence, longer. Somewhere above us a stone came loose and clattered down the switchback, and we both froze, pressed into the rock, her shoulder against mine, both of us listening to the mountain decide whether we were worth betraying. It decided we weren't. We moved on.

* * *

We walked through what remained of the night, and I learned things about Marguerite Voss that no ledger would ever record.

I learned that she counted her steps—not from nerves, but from habit, a murmured tally under her breath every hundred paces, a soldier's way of knowing how far she'd come and how far she could get back. I learned that she favored her left side since the burning beam had caught her across the ribs, and that she hid it so well I only caught it when she reached for a root and the breath went out of her like a struck bell. I learned that when I offered to carry her pack she looked at me as though I'd suggested she carry mine while juggling, and then, forty minutes later, handed it over without a word, which from Marguerite Voss was roughly equivalent to a sonnet.

The path climbed through black pine, then dropped into a fold of the mountain where a stream ran loud enough to cover conversation. Dawn found us gray and thin, light leaking through the trees like water through a cracked jug. I called a halt at a place I knew—a hollow under a granite shelf, dry-floored, screened by juniper, one of Dree's caches though the supplies were long picked clean.

"We rest till full dark," I said. "Movement by day is suicide. Hale has riders on the salt road and the Court has eyes I can't even guess at."

"You've thought about this." She lowered herself against the rock with the care of someone negotiating with her own body. "Being hunted."

"It's the family trade." I sat down across from her, close enough to share warmth, far enough to pretend we weren't. "Debtors learn to read pursuit the way priests learn scripture. My father owed the Order eleven crowns and ran for six years before they caught him. I inherited the arithmetic."

"And now you owe them nothing."

"Now I owe them everything else." I pulled off a boot and examined my blistered heel with the detachment of a man reading bad news in a dispatch. "Eleven years of copying their ledgers. Eleven years believing the gods were dead because dead gods make very tidy accounting. And the whole time—" I stopped. The map was against my chest, wrapped in oilcloth, and I became aware that I'd put my hand over it, the way you touch a wound.

Marguerite watched my hand do this. She missed nothing; I'd understood that about her within a day of meeting her on the pass, back when she was hunting me officially and I still believed invisibility was a profession.

"You could still give it to Hale," she said quietly. "Pardon. Testimony. A warm cell and a pension. You know the offer stands."

"I know."

"And instead you're teaching me smuggler paths at midnight."

"And instead." I looked at her. Her face in the dawn-gray was stripped of everything she wore for the Order—the set jaw, the parade-ground stillness—and what was underneath was tired, and young, and more afraid than she would ever admit aloud. "Why are *you* here, Voss? You had your chance at the ford. Hale offered you the same bargain he offered me, just with different currency. Your oath for my head. You could be drinking his bad wine right now, being told what a good soldier you are."

Something moved behind her eyes—fast, like a fish under ice.

"Because I saw the vault seal," she said. "Before the Court breached it. Before the mountain shook. I stood twenty feet from that door and I read the script myself—or tried to—and every line of it contradicted the catechism I've recited since I was seven years old." She flexed her bandaged hand, the one that had cost her blood at the vault. "*The Flame is quenched. The gods are dust.* I've said those words ten thousand times. And the door beneath Thornfell said otherwise, in letters older than the Order, and it was *humming*, Galloway. Doors don't hum. Dust doesn't hum."

"So you broke your oath."

"No." Her voice went very level, the way it did when she was standing on the edge of something. "I haven't broken my oath. That's the problem. An oath isn't a sentence you say once. It's a thing you keep saying, every morning, every choice. And every choice since the vault, I keep saying it and it keeps meaning—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. Started again, smaller. "It keeps meaning something I can't say out loud yet."

I should have let it lie. A wise fugitive lets dangerous things lie. But the mountain was quiet, and the stream was loud, and exhaustion does something to the tongue.

"Say it anyway," I said. "Nobody's listening but me and a juniper bush."

She almost smiled. It was a near thing, gone in a moment, but I saw it land somewhere in me and take up residence.

"When we reach Greywater Cave," she said, "and the hermit tells us what he knows—if he confirms what the door said—then I'll say it. Out loud. To whoever needs to hear it." She closed her eyes. "Until then I'm a knight escorting a prisoner. That's the story. It keeps the oath alive a little longer."

"And if the hermit says the Order was right all along? Gods dead, lie unnecessary?"

"Then you can turn me in yourself. Save everyone the trouble."

I laughed—actually laughed, a rusty, appalling sound in that hushed hollow, and her eyes snapped open and fixed on me with genuine offense.

"Something funny?"

"You," I said. "Me. This. Two people with nothing left, negotiating terms of surrender to each other in a hole in a mountain." I shook my head. "Eleven years I kept my head down and my heart locked, Voss. Best survival strategy there is. Nothing to steal, nothing to grieve. And in the space of one moon I've got inquisitors on one flank, cultists on the other, a dead god humming under my boots, and—" I gestured at her, at the whole improbable fact of her, "—this. Whatever this is. It's terrible tradecraft."

She studied me for a long moment. Outside, the light strengthened by degrees, and I watched her decide something, the way I'd watched her decide things before: visibly, deliberately, like a woman laying stones across a river.

"For the record," she said, "when the beam fell at the garrison, you didn't hesitate either. You came back into a burning building for a woman whose official purpose was dragging you to the stake." A pause. "Also terrible tradecraft."

"I never claimed to be good at this."

"No," she said. "You claimed to be invisible. And look at you now."

* * *

We slept in shifts, and I dreamed badly, and woke to her hand over my mouth.

I came up swinging—which she later described, with relish, as "flailing"—before her grip and her glare informed me that noise was death. She pointed downslope. Through the juniper I saw them: four figures moving along the streambed below our shelf, unhurried, methodical, fanning out the way searchers do when they've done this many times. Not soldiers. Soldiers move in lines. These moved like water finding cracks.

Ashen Court.

They passed within thirty feet of us. Close enough that I could hear one of them speak—a woman's voice, low and pleasant, saying words that turned my blood to well-water:

*"Scent's fresh. The scribe came this way. The knight too—they're together now, which simplifies matters. Two heads, one map."*

A pause. Then another voice, younger, uncertain: *"And if the knight won't come back to us?"*

*"Then the knight is a door we close."* Pleasantly. Like discussing supper.

They moved on downstream. We stayed frozen until the light changed and my leg cramped so badly I nearly sobbed into my own sleeve. When we finally dared breathe, Marguerite rolled onto her back and stared at the granite overhead.

"They knew," she whispered. "Not guessed. *Knew.* 'The scribe came this way.' How?"

I thought about it, and thinking about it made my hands cold.

"Dree," I said finally. "The man who showed me these paths. Someone bought him. Or—" a worse thought arrived, unwelcome and punctual, "—or someone's been buying him for years, and the paths I treasure were never secret at all. Just rented."

"Then these roads are dead to us."

"No." I sat up, wincing, and dug into memory the way you dig into a pocket hoping the coin you remember is still there. "Dree showed me the rented roads. But my father ran these mountains for six years, and he taught me the difference between a road and a rumor. There's a route north from here that isn't on any map, smuggler or otherwise. It's not a path—it's a sequence. Landmarks only, memorized, no trail between them. Scree fields and goat tracks and one stretch along a ridge where you walk exposed to the sky for half a day with nothing to hide behind." I met her eyes. "My father used it twice. Nobody's used it since, because nobody knows it exists except me, and I've never walked it."

"Wonderful," Marguerite said. "An untested escape route known to exactly one terrified scribe, across exposed ground, pursued by people who close doors."

"When you say it like that it sounds bad."

"It *is* bad."

"Yes," I agreed. "But it's bad in a direction they aren't looking."

She held my gaze for a moment, and then—this is the part I keep returning to, the part I'll keep returning to when I'm old, if I'm ever old—she nodded. Once. Complete. As if I'd presented her with a battle plan drawn up by competent staff instead of a desperate scramble invented by a copyist on four hours of nightmare sleep.

"Show me the landmarks," she said. "All of them. If something happens to you, I need to finish the route alone."

The intimacy of that request landed on me harder than any touch could have. She wasn't asking to trust me. She was asking me to make her independent of needing to trust anyone—including me. That was Marguerite entire: love, if it was love, expressed as preparation for loss.

So I taught her. All that day in the hollow, whispering, drawing in the dirt with a stick: the split boulder shaped like a kneeling monk, the cairn with the lightning-split stone, the notch in the eastern ridge that frames the morning star at midsummer. She repeated each one back twice, faultless, and watched me with an attention that had nothing to do with geography, and I pretended not to notice that I was memorizing her the same way—the small scar through her left eyebrow, the way she tucked her hair back with two fingers when concentrating, the exact pitch of her voice when she said *repeat it back* and meant *stay alive.*

By dusk we knew each other's routes by heart. Neither of us said so.

* * *

The ridge crossing was every bit as bad as advertised.

Wind came off the peaks like a debt collector, and the sky was enormous and indifferent, and for half a day we were the only two moving things on the spine of the world, exposed, tiny, utterly visible to anyone with a spyglass and patience. We didn't talk. Talking wastes breath and breath was rationed. But twice—twice—when the wind shoved hard enough to stagger me, a hand caught my arm before I'd even begun to fall, and once, when the same wind found her, my hand was already out. By the third hour we weren't thinking about it anymore. We were just two bodies that had decided, without consultation, to be one body's worth of careful.

Near the ridge's end we found shelter behind a fin of rock, and ate the last of the food—half a heel of bread each, a handful of dried plums I'd hoarded like a dragon—and Marguerite sat looking north at the jagged country beyond, country with no roads in it at all, and said, without turning:

"Galloway. If the hermit confirms it—the lie, the sleeping gods, all of it—what do you do with the truth?"

I chewed my plum pit, which was a habit, not a plan.

"Tell it," I said. "Everywhere. Loudly. In the temple where they indentured me, if it comes to that."

"They'll kill you."

"Probably."

"And that's acceptable?"

"Nothing about it's acceptable." I looked at her profile against the sky, sharp and steady, and found the honesty arriving before I could stop it, the way truth does when you're too tired to guard the gate. "But I spent eleven years being safe, Voss, and safe turned out to be a room with the door bricked shut. I'm done choosing rooms. Whatever's coming out of that vault—whatever the Order's been sitting on for a century—people deserve to meet it awake. Even if waking them up gets me killed. *Especially* then."

She was silent a long time. The wind filled it.

"My order would call that pride," she said at last.

"My order would call it heresy. Between the two of us, we've got the whole catalogue covered."

And then she laughed—really laughed, brief and startled out of her, a sound I suspected few living people had heard—and in that moment, on that ridge, with the whole weight of the world's oldest lie pressing down on us from one side and the world's most patient hunters from the other, I understood that I was lost. Completely, irretrievably, gloriously lost. Not to the Ashen Court, not to Hale, not to the mountain.

To her. To the specific gravity of Marguerite Voss, who prepared for loss the way other people prepare for dinner, and who had just, without saying a word about it, chosen to walk wherever I walked.

We came down off the ridge at dusk into a valley of grey stone and standing water, and there, exactly where my father's sequence of landmarks promised, a cave mouth opened in the hillside above a pool the color of old pewter. Smoke-smell, faint. A thread of woodsmoke, days old or hours old, rising from deep within.

Greywater Cave. The hermit.

Marguerite drew her sword—not at the cave, just out of habit, the way some people touch a charm—and looked at me.

"Whoever's in there," she said, "knew we were coming. That smoke's been tended."

"How can you possibly know that?"

"Because it's banked, not abandoned. Someone's keeping it alive for guests." She started up the slope, then paused, and said, quieter, without looking back: "Whatever he tells us in there—I want you to know I already believe you. About the gods. About the lie. I believed you at the vault door. The hermit's just going to tell me *why* I believe you."

Inside the cave, something shifted in the dark, and an old voice, dry as ledger paper, spoke before we could knock or announce or flee:

"Thirteen turns," it said. "You're late. Come in, children. We have very little time, and I have a great deal of treason to confess."
