## 13. Marguerite's Oath

The hermit's cave smelled of woodsmoke and old paper, which is to say it smelled like every library I had ever loved and every lie I had ever told. I sat by the fire with my sword across my knees—not drawn, not sheathed, just resting there the way a question rests on the tongue—and watched Coral Galloway sleep.

He slept like a man who had learned that sleep was something you did quickly and lightly, one ear always cocked toward the door. Even now, even here, with Brother Anselm's wards chalked across the entrance in symbols so old they predated the Order itself, his fingers twitched every few minutes, as if counting debts in his dreams.

I had arrested that man. I had bound his wrists with my own hands, read him the charges in a voice like a ledger being closed, and delivered him toward a garrison that burned behind us. And somewhere between the fire and the ford and the long cold climb into the deep Thornfell, I had stopped thinking of him as a fugitive and started thinking of him as *Coral*, which was a far more dangerous category of person.

Anselm stirred in his corner. The old hermit had been Order once—before my time, before Hale's time, back when the Quiet Flame still pretended its silence was piety rather than policy. He had looked at me when we arrived, looked at the white-and-gold of my ruined tabard, and said only: "You'll want tea. Confessions go easier with tea."

I hadn't asked for a confession. But he'd made it anyway, and drunk it himself, and now he snored beneath a mountain of patched blankets while the fire popped and the thing beneath the mountain hummed its low, patient note through the stone—a sound I could no longer unhear, any more than I could unhear what Anselm had told us about the vault, about the Order, about the sixty years of careful, deliberate, sanctified lying.

Coral opened his eyes. No transition, no drowsy blinking—one moment asleep, the next fully awake and watching me watch him. It should have been unnerving. Instead it felt like being seen by someone who had already decided not to look away.

"You're doing it again," he said.

"Doing what?"

"Sitting there looking like a woman composing her own indictment." He pushed himself up on one elbow. His hair had gone from merely unkempt to architecturally chaotic over the past week. "For the record, the fire isn't going to confess for you."

"I don't know what you mean."

"You've been rehearsing something since sundown. You keep opening your mouth, closing it, and then adjusting your sword like it's offended you." He sat up properly, wincing where the rockslide bruise met the garrison burn on his ribs. "Marguerite. We've been running for our lives together for six days. Whatever it is, I promise my capacity for judgment is currently occupied entirely by the Ashen Court and Inquisitor Hale."

He was right, which was irritating, because he usually was, and because I had in fact been rehearsing since sundown.

* * *

Here is what people misunderstand about oaths. They think an oath is a wall—you build it once, and it stands, and it keeps things out. They imagine the swearing as a single bright moment: hand on steel or scripture, words spoken, done.

But an oath is not a wall. It is a debt. And I grew up in a house that understood debts better than most temples.

My family name was Voss before it was anything else, and before it was Voss it was something older, something the genealogies had been quietly amended to obscure. My grandmother kept a shrine in the cellar of our house in Corrowmere. Not to the Quiet Flame—to the *Quiet Ones*, plural, the sleeping gods, with candles arranged in the old thirteen-point pattern that the Order had spent two centuries teaching the faithful to call superstition. She prayed to them the way other women prayed for rain. My father sang the old hymns to me as lullabies, hymns whose melodies I would later hear, to my horror, hummed by a chaplain who had no idea what he was carrying in his throat.

We were heretics. Gentle ones. The kind that never hurt anyone, never proselytized, never did anything more radical than light candles in a cellar and remember. And when I was nine years old, the Order found out.

Not through investigation. Through my aunt Sera, who had quarreled with my father over land, and who wrote to the local chapterhouse with the tidy, righteous malice available to anyone with a grievance and a stamp. I remember the inquisitors coming. I remember their boots in our hallway. I remember my parents kneeling in the parlor while a man with a soft voice explained the options: public penance, public trial, or—the third option, offered quietly, almost kindly—*service*. A family that gave its children to the Order, body and name, might find its records... simplified. Its heresy reclassified as misunderstanding. Its shrine permitted to become, officially, a wine cellar.

They took me first because I asked to go. That is the part I have never told anyone. My brother Tomas wept and clung to the doorframe, and I stood there in my good dress and said, "I'll do it," because I was nine and I had already understood, with the terrible clarity of children, that someone in that room was going to have to pay, and I wanted it to be me. I wanted the transaction to be clean. I wanted to be the coin.

The Order took my name into its rolls, dressed me in grey, taught me the Flame's catechism—which is, if you read it closely, a masterpiece of misdirection, every verse pointing away from Thornfell—and when I came of age, they let me swear the knight's oath. Hand on the reliquary. Words in the old cadence. *To guard the truth of the Flame against all darkness.*

I meant it. That's the joke, if you want it. I meant every word, because meaning it was the only way to survive meaning what I'd actually sworn, which was this: that my family's sin would be buried under my obedience. That I would serve the lie so thoroughly, so publicly, so *well*, that no one would ever again look at the name Voss and see candlelight in a cellar. Every heretic I hunted was a payment against my family's account. Every sleeper I woke at dawn for drills, every smuggler I dragged in chains down the salt road, every scribe I caught with contraband in his satchel—

I stopped there, in my own head, because that was the night it all began, wasn't it. A scribe with contraband in his satchel. Brown eyes too tired for his age, standing on the pass with his hands very carefully empty, telling me he was just a courier running salt, and me letting him pass because something in his face made me think of cellars and candlelight and everything I had spent twenty years burying.

I had let him pass. I told myself it was insufficient evidence. I told myself procedure. I told myself a great many things, right up until the garrison burned and I stood in the smoke watching him pull me out of a collapsing armory, and understood that I had been lying to myself with the same fluency the Order lied to everyone, and that the lie had a name, and the name was sitting across the fire from me asking why I kept adjusting my sword.

* * *

"Marguerite," he said again, gentler now. "Whatever it is. Say it or don't. But stop sharpening it first."

I laughed. It came out wrong—too short, too sharp—but it came out. "You know what Anselm told us today. About the Order knowing. About the vault, and the seal, and the sixty years of—" I gestured vaguely at the cave walls, at the mountains, at the entire architecture of my life. "You heard it, and you were angry. I saw it. Angry at them, angry at the lie, angry on behalf of every farmer who ever tithed to a god that the priests knew was sleeping."

"And you weren't?"

"I was relieved." The word fell out of me like a stone off a cliff. "Do you understand? Sixty years. The Order has known for *sixty years*, and I have served them for fifteen, and when Anselm said it, some small vile part of me thought: *oh, thank the Flame, it isn't just me.*"

Coral didn't flinch. He had the scribe's gift, I was learning—patience with difficult texts, and I was becoming, apparently, a difficult text.

"My family were heretics," I said. "Real ones. The kind the Order simplifies out of existence. Sleeping gods, old hymns, candles in a cellar. I was nine when they came. And I bought my family's survival with an oath, and I have spent fifteen years paying it off, and I told myself the payments were penance, and tonight—" My voice did something humiliating. I steadied it. "Tonight I understand that it was never penance. Penance is paid to the injured party. I was paying my jailers."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above us, the mountain hummed its one long note, patient as tide.

"I hunted people like my grandmother," I said. "Did you know that? In Corrowmere, three years ago—there was a circle of old women keeping the thirteen-point vigil. I broke it up myself. Took their candles. One of them looked at me, and she had my grandmother's hands, and she said, 'Child, who taught you to be ashamed?' and I reported her for obstruction and had her fined." I looked at my palms, at the sword-calluses, at the hands that had done it. "That's my oath. That's what it's built on. Not faith. Shame, purchased wholesale, applied retail."

Coral was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had none of the wit I'd come to brace for. Just care, laid flat and plain.

"My debt is four hundred crowns," he said. "Or it was. Eleven years copying ledgers at eight coppers a day against a sum my dead father borrowed to keep us fed. I used to tell myself the same story you did—that serving it was honorable, that a debt honestly worked was a kind of virtue. And then a dying man put a map in my hands and I learned that the temple holding my debt has known for sixty years that everything it teaches is a managed lie, and I realized my debt wasn't owed to a church. It was owed to the lie." He shrugged, small and tired. "So. Two prisoners of different chains, we called it. Turns out the chains are the same make."

"They are," I said. "Mine just came with a sword."

"Mine came with excellent penmanship." And there it was—the flicker of humor, offered carefully, like a hand extended across a gap. I took it. I found I was crying, quietly, without drama, the way you bleed from a wound you'd forgotten was deep, and he didn't make a production of it. He just moved around the fire and sat beside me, close enough that his shoulder touched mine, and let the silence hold what neither of us could say yet.

"I swore to guard the truth of the Flame," I said finally. "And the truth of the Flame is that there is no Flame. There's a vault, and a seal, and something underneath it that the men in white have been sitting on like dragons on gold. Every prayer I ever led was a diversion. Every oath I administered to a new knight was a fresh coat of paint on the same old door." I turned to look at him. His face was firelit and very still. "If I keep the oath, I keep the lie. If I break the oath, I lose the only shape my life has had since I was nine years old. Do you see? It isn't duty versus love, whatever you're politely not saying. It's duty versus *duty*. The oath I swore and the truth I owe. And I cannot pay both."

"No," he agreed softly. "You can't. Nobody can. That's the whole rotten design of it. That's how the Order survives—not by making people evil, but by making them honest in ways that serve the lie." He hesitated. "When you arrested me. On the pass. Why did you let me go?"

"Insufficient evidence."

"Coral-proof answer. Try again."

I exhaled. "Because you looked at me the way my grandmother looked at those candles. Like something true was worth the risk." I said it before I could stop myself, and having said it, discovered I could not unsay it, and did not particularly want to. "I've regretted it approximately four hundred times since. It's been a very educational week."

He smiled—not the deflector smile, the real one, rare as honest coin. "For what it's worth," he said, "when you had me in irons and read me the charges, your voice shook on the fourth one. 'Possession of contraband doctrine.' You stumbled over *doctrine*. I've been turning that over for days, wondering what kind of knight trips on a word."

"The kind who was raised on it," I said. "In a cellar. By candlelight."

He nodded slowly, and I watched him decide something, the way I'd watched him decide things all week—quickly, completely, with the whole self. "Then here's what I think," he said. "You didn't swear your oath to the Order. You swore it to a nine-year-old girl in a parlor who needed the transaction to be clean. And she's been waiting fifteen years for someone to tell her the debt was never hers." He looked at the fire. "It was theirs. All of it. Every crown of it."

Something in my chest that had been clenched since Corrowmere, since the parlor, since the boots in the hallway—something loosened. Not broke. Loosened, like a knot remembering it was once a rope that could be used for climbing instead of hanging.

"If I renounce it," I said, "there's no going back. Hale will brand me traitor before the echo fades. Everything I am gets rewritten in their ledgers. Heretic's daughter. Oathbreaker. They'll come for my family again—they'll *dust off* my family—"

"Or," Coral said, "you write it first. You've spent fifteen years learning exactly how the Order tells its stories. You know every trick in their ink." He turned to face me fully. "When the time comes—and it's coming, Marguerite, the moon doesn't care about our schedules—don't let them author you. Stand up in front of whoever's listening and tell the truth in your own voice, in your own order, before they can tell it in theirs. An oath-breaker is whatever the accuser says she is. But a woman who stands in the light and says *here is what I was, here is what I was asked to protect, and here is what it actually was*—" he spread his hands, scribe's hands, ink-stained even after a week in the wilds, "—that's testimony. Testimony is harder to burn."

"You sound like a man planning to testify himself."

The smile faded from his face, and what replaced it frightened me more than any inquisitor: resolve. "I've been thinking about that," he said. "About the temple. About walking in the front door."

"Coral—"

"Not tonight," he said. "Tonight I'm going to sleep, and tomorrow we figure out what Anselm knows about the seal, and the day after that we stay ahead of Hale. But eventually I'm going to stop running. It's the only road left that goes anywhere." He glanced at me sideways. "Question is whether you'll be standing next to me when I do. Or across the field. With the sword."

I looked at my sword, leaning against the cave wall where I'd finally stopped fidgeting with it. Fifteen years of weight. And yet, when I imagined setting it down—not losing it, *setting it down*, choosing to—it did not feel like falling. It felt like exhaling.

"Next to you," I said. "Apparently. Against all training and doctrine."

"Against all doctrine," he murmured, "the knight sits down beside the heretic and shares his blanket, because the mountain nights are freezing and sentiment is no excuse for hypothermia."

"That is not in any catechism."

"It's in mine. I'm writing a new one. Very short. Mostly footnotes."

I laughed—really laughed, for the first time since the garrison burned—and the sound went up through the cave and out into the dark, and for a few hours, wrapped in a threadbare blanket with a heretic's heartbeat steady against my shoulder, I let myself believe that debts could be renegotiated, that oaths could be outgrown, that the future was a text we might still edit.

Then, near dawn, Anselm's wards at the cave mouth flared white and died.

All thirteen points, extinguished at once, in order—from the outside in.

Someone had walked up the mountain who knew the old script well enough to unmake it. Someone patient. Someone thorough.

Someone carrying a lantern with the Quiet Flame's seal, whose light I could already see moving through the pines below, climbing steadily, unhurried, the way a man climbs when he is certain the door at the top will open.

Hale had found us.
