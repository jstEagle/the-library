## 14. Hale Closes In

The river at Greywater Ford ran shallow and quick over pale stones, the kind of water that looked innocent and would take your ankles out from under you without apology. We came down to it near dusk, Marguerite limping—her knee had been complaining since the scree slope above Widow's Stair, though she'd deny it under torture, possibly literally—and me doing my best impression of a man who was not being hunted by two separate organizations with a combined body count I'd personally contributed three corpses to.

I want it noted that I did not choose this ford. The ford chose us. Every other crossing for twenty miles was watched—I knew because I'd spent half my life learning which roads were watched, back when knowing was a hobby instead of a survival strategy. Greywater Ford was the last quiet place. Which meant, of course, that it wasn't.

"He's here," Marguerite said.

She didn't explain how she knew. She just said it the way you'd say *it's going to rain*, and started checking her sword's draw before we'd even reached the tree line.

"How can you possibly—"

"Because he wants us to cross. If he wanted us dead, there'd be crossbows in those alders." She nodded at the stand of trees on the far bank, dark and still in the way that trees full of men pretending to be trees are never still. "Hale doesn't kill what he can use. He'll want to talk."

"That's worse," I said. "Crossbows are honest."

Marguerite almost smiled. It was a small thing, barely a crack in her, but I'd learned to treasure those cracks the way a miser treasures coins. Three weeks ago she'd been dragging me toward the garrison in irons. Now she was standing between me and an Inquisitor of the Quiet Flame, technically a traitor, actually something more complicated than that, and neither of us had said aloud the thing we both knew, because saying it would make it real, and real things could be taken away.

We crossed anyway. There was nowhere else to go, which is the traditional reason people do stupid things at river fords.

* * *

He was waiting on a flat stone shelf above the water, and I'll give him this: Inquisitor Hale understood theater. He sat alone by a small fire with a kettle suspended over it, as if he'd been expecting us for tea rather than cornering us at the edge of the known world. His gray robes were immaculate despite the wilderness. His face was the kind of face you forget immediately and then spend years trying to remember—a clerk's face, a neighbor's face, the face of a man who might ask to borrow your ladder.

Two figures stood back among the rocks. Not crossbowmen. Just watchers. Somehow that was worse too.

"Coral Galloway," Hale said warmly, as if greeting a nephew. "And Ser Voss. Sit. The water's boiling."

"We're fine standing," Marguerite said.

"You're fine bleeding, from what I hear. The knee." He poured tea into two clay cups anyway and set them on the stone between us, an offering or a trap, and with Inquisitors those are usually the same object. "Sit down, Coral. This will go easier sitting."

Everything goes easier sitting. That's how they get you into the chair.

I sat. Marguerite remained standing behind me, one hand not quite resting on her hilt, and I felt her there like a wall between me and the fire's warmth, which was strange, because three weeks ago she'd have been the one holding the irons.

"Do you know why I'm here alone?" Hale asked.

"Because your men are in the alders," I said.

"Because I've read your file," he continued, as if I hadn't spoken, which was his way. "Eleven years indentured to the Order of the Quiet Flame. Debt inherited from your mother's apothecary failures—she was treating miners' lungs with willow bark and prayer, poor woman. You copied ledgers. Ten thousand pages, they estimate. You never once filed a complaint, requested a transfer, or attempted escape. Your conduct reviews describe you as 'unremarkable'—" he smiled, "—which, in my experience, means remarkable in ways the reviewer lacked the imagination to see. And then, in a single season, you acquire a document worth killing for, evade the Order's pursuit across Thornfell, survive an ambush by the Ashen Court, and abscond with one of my knights."

"I didn't abscond with her," I said. "She absconded herself. Ask her."

"I'm asking you." He leaned forward, and the firelight moved in his eyes like something swimming. "Here is what interests me about you, Coral. You are not a believer. Your file makes that clear—you attended services the way a man attends a debtors' meeting. You don't love the gods. You don't hate them. So why are you dying on this hill? Why does a scribe who spent eleven years perfecting invisibility suddenly decide to carry a burning scroll through a country full of people who want him ash?"

"Maybe I got tired of being invisible."

"No." He said it gently, almost kindly, and that gentleness was the most frightening thing I'd heard in weeks. "People don't change that much. They just stop hiding what they were. You were always this—the man who memorizes smuggler routes while copying hymnals. The man who sees everything and writes nothing down where anyone can find it. You didn't become brave, Coral. You became *found*. And I'm here to offer you the chance to be invisible again."

The kettle whistled. He ignored it.

"The Order will pardon you," he said. "Fully. Your debt—eleven years of it, every copper—annulled by writ. You walk away clean. No inquisitors, no Ashen Court hunting you—we have an arrangement with certain parties there that predates either of our careers, and it extends to cover you. A new name, papers, passage south past the mountains to anywhere salt water touches. You could open a shop. Copy contracts. Marry someone dull and grow old in a town where nobody has ever heard of Thornfell."

It sounded wonderful. That's the thing I need you to understand—it sounded genuinely, achingly wonderful, the way the lie always does when it's tailored to your exact measurements. Eleven years I had counted days. Four hundred and some remaining, and here was a man offering to erase them with a word. My whole self had been built around that ledger: so many pages copied, so many left, freedom at the end of the arithmetic. And he was offering to burn the ledger.

"And all I have to do," I said, "is give you the map."

"And testify," Hale agreed, "that Ser Marguerite Voss broke her oath willingly, knowingly, and with malice—that she conspired with you from the moment of your arrest, that the garrison fire was negligence on her part, that she seduced a prisoner into treason."

Behind me, Marguerite went very still. Not angry-still. Worse. Receiving-still. The stillness of a woman taking a blow she'd been expecting since the day she broke her vows.

"She didn't seduce me," I said. "I'm not that lucky."

"Coral." Hale's voice dropped into something softer, confessional. "Let me tell you what happens otherwise. The Order cannot permit the map to circulate—not because the elders believe the gods are dead, but because they know they aren't, and belief is load-bearing architecture. Pull that stone out and the temple falls on ten thousand people whose entire lives are built on the floor it provides. You've seen the tremors. You know what sleeps down there. Do you imagine the Ashen Court waking it gently? They are exiles, Coral. Exiles don't want to heal the world. They want to watch it admit it wronged them."

"And the Order lying for a century heals people?"

"The Order *feeding* people," Hale said. "Every orphan fed from a temple kitchen, every miner's fever treated by a Flame-blessed healer, every frightened soul given a ritual to hold in the dark—that is purchased with the lie. You want to spend truth like coin. Fine. But look at the exchange rate. One map, told aloud, and you buy: panic, schism, the collapse of the healing houses, and a door opened for the Ashen Court to walk through wearing honesty like a coat. Is that the trade? Because I've done the sums, scribe. I do them every night."

God help me, the sums were good. That's what nobody tells you about temptation—it rarely arrives looking like a devil. It arrives looking like an accountant, and its numbers check out.

I looked at the tea steaming between us. I looked at the alders on the far bank, where men were pretending very hard to be trees. I looked, finally, at Marguerite—or rather, I felt her, because I couldn't make myself turn around. Standing between me and the fire. Between me and the pardon. Having heard every word of what testifying against her would purchase, and saying nothing, because she would not beg, and she would not ask me to damn myself for her either, and so she simply stood there and let me choose, which was the cruelest kindness anyone has ever done me.

"When you arrested me," I said to her, not turning, "you gave me one chance to tell the truth. You remember?"

"I remember," she said quietly.

"This is mine, then." I turned back to Hale. "You're giving me one chance to tell a lie. Same shape. Different direction."

"It isn't the same at all," Hale said. "One keeps you alive."

"One keeps *me* alive," I agreed. "That's the whole difference. That's the entire sum. Everything else—the debt, the shop, the dull wife, the salt water—all of it is just me, alive, having sold a woman who stood between me and a fire." I picked up one of the clay cups. He watched me do it, and I saw in his forgettable face a flicker of something—hope, maybe, that I was reaching for the offer. I drank the tea. It was good. Chamomile and something bitter underneath, like all mercies. Then I set it down and said: "No."

Hale closed his eyes briefly, the way a man does when a horse he liked comes up lame.

"I gave you the chance," he said, and there was no warmth in it now, only a tired, procedural sorrow. "It goes in the record. You refused pardon. You refused to spare Ser Voss. Whatever follows, you authored."

"I know how ledgers work," I said. "I copied enough of them."

He rose. The two figures among the rocks stepped forward, and now I could see they weren't soldiers—they were Ashen Court, ash-gray scarves at their throats, and the arrangement between Hale and the Court became suddenly, horribly concrete. Heretics and Inquisitors, sharing a riverbank, united by the oldest law there is: the institution comes first.

"The map," Hale said, holding out his hand. "Or the alders. Those are the options, Coral. I regret them. But I don't choose them any more than you chose to pick up that courier's burden."

Marguerite's sword cleared its sheath with a sound like a struck match.

"Hale," she said, "step back from him."

"Ser Voss. You are a branded traitor standing on Order business with a drawn blade. Every crime they say you committed, you are currently committing in front of witnesses." He sighed. "I truly did hope to spare you the tribunal."

"There won't be a tribunal," she said.

And then the mountain answered her.

Not a tremor. A *voice*—a long, rolling groan from deep beneath Thornfell, deep enough that the river shivered into rings against the stones, deep enough that the tea in the abandoned cup trembled and the fire flattened as if ducking. The alders erupted with movement—men no longer pretending—and somewhere upstream a whole slope of pines cracked and slid, and through the roar I heard the thing I'd been hearing in my sleep for a week:

A rhythm. Slow. Patient. Like a heartbeat that had been counting the turns of thirteen moons and had nearly finished.

Hale stood very still, watching the water shiver, and for one unguarded moment his forgettable face forgot itself completely, and I saw what lived underneath the accountant: fear. Old fear. The fear of a man who had done the sums his whole life and had never once included this variable.

"The thirteenth moon," I said. "Four nights from full. You can't pardon me from what's coming, Hale. Nobody can. The only question left is who's standing next to whom when it wakes."

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he took something from his robe—not a weapon, but a folded paper, sealed with the Quiet Flame—and set it on the stone where the tea had been.

"My offer stands until the moon fills," he said. "After that, there are no offers. Only survivors." He turned to go, then paused. "For what it's worth, Coral—your file said *unremarkable*. I intend to correct it."

They melted into the dark along the riverbank, Ashen Court and Inquisitor walking side by side, and the mountain groaned again overhead, and Marguerite put her hand on my shoulder—steadying me, or steadying herself, I couldn't tell.

"Four nights," she said.

"Less, if we're slow." I picked up the sealed paper. I should have thrown it in the river. Instead I tucked it inside my coat, next to the map, because I am the man who memorizes routes and burns nothing, and some habits are load-bearing too.

"We move tonight," Marguerite said, already turning upriver, toward the vault, toward the sound of the heartbeat under the world. "While the ford is empty."

The water rang against the stones like a struck bell that wouldn't finish fading, and we walked into the dark with four nights left, carrying a map, a pardon we didn't want, and each other—which was, when I did the sums honestly, the only asset on the ledger that had never once been a lie.
