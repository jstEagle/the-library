## 15. The Refusal

Hale stood in the shallows of the ford with the patience of a man who had never once been late for anything, and I understood, watching him, that this was the moment my whole life had been walking toward without telling me.

The river ran fast and gray with snowmelt, loud enough that we had to raise our voices to speak across it. Behind Hale, six of his men sat their horses along the bank like a row of black nails driven into the green. Behind me—upstream, where the water came down out of the high passes—lay three days of smuggler road and the vault beneath Thornfell, and Marguerite Voss, who was watching me the way a person watches a lit fuse.

"Brother Galloway." Hale's voice carried over the water effortlessly, the voice of a man accustomed to being heard in large silent rooms. "You look tired."

"I've been traveling," I said.

"With stolen property."

"With borrowed property," I said. "I intend to return it."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile. Inquisitors are not permitted smiles; they are permitted the memory of them, worn like an old scar. "That is what I hoped you'd say. Then let us speak plainly, scribe to scribe. You were indentured to the Order of the Quiet Flame for eleven years. You copied ten thousand pages of their ledgers. You know better than most how the arithmetic of debt works." He spread his hands, palms up, an oddly humble gesture for a man standing in armor. "So here is mine. Surrender the map. Testify before the elders that Knight-Commander Voss aided your flight—that she broke her oath knowingly, willfully, with malice. Do this, and every page you owe is struck from the ledger. Your freedom, granted in full. No pursuit. No questions. You could be in Lowmark by winter, drinking wine you didn't steal, writing whatever nonsense pleases you."

I felt the offer settle over me like a warm coat. That was the thing about Hale—he didn't threaten first. He *gifted* first. He made survival feel like temptation rather than cowardice, so that when you refused him, the refusal would look like madness instead of principle.

Behind me, Marguerite said nothing. She had dismounted and stood with her hand resting on her sword hilt—not drawing it, just touching it, the way you touch a doorframe in an earthquake. Waiting to see which way the ground would move.

"And if I refuse?" I asked.

Hale sighed, genuinely sorrowful, or genuinely skilled at seeming so. "Then the arithmetic changes. The Order does not forgive twice, Brother Galloway. And I am afraid the testimony would no longer be needed—the charge becomes flight, heresy, and theft of temple property, and the penalty for all three is the same fire." He tilted his head. "I confess I don't understand you. You are not a brave man. I've read your ledgers. Your marginalia is the marginalia of a man who has spent his whole life making himself small. Why die for a map?"

And there it was—the question I'd been avoiding since the ravine, since the courier's blood soaked into my sleeve, since thirteen turns became the only calendar that mattered.

"Why live for a lie?" I said.

Hale's expression didn't change, but something behind it did—a recalculation, quick and cold. "The gods are dead," he said. "This is doctrine. This is the foundation on which eleven centuries of order rest. What you carry is not truth, scribe. It is a stone to throw through a window."

"Maybe," I said. "But the window was painted shut, and everyone inside is suffocating, and somebody has to break the glass before they all stop noticing they can't breathe."

For a moment—for one long, ringing moment—Hale looked almost pleased. As though he'd wanted me to refuse. As though a man who refuses is easier to condemn than a man who wavers.

"So be it," he said quietly. "Archers."

* * *

I have thought, since, about how quickly a decision becomes a body in motion. One word—*archers*—and six men who had been sitting still as furniture became a machine.

Marguerite moved first. Of course she did. She was already turning, already shouting "Down!" with her shoulder slamming into my chest, and then we were both off the bank and into the river, and the world became noise and cold.

The water took us like a debt collector takes a debtor—thoroughly, personally, without negotiation. I went under once, twice, came up gasping with my satchel strap cutting into my throat because I had knotted it double out of habit and habit, for once, was saving my life. Arrows hit the water around us with flat slapping sounds, absurdly gentle, like rain on a pond. One skipped past my ear close enough to part my hair.

"Left!" Marguerite's voice, raw against the roar. "The bend—there's a channel—"

I didn't ask how she knew. Later I would learn she'd ridden this river on patrol for four years, that she knew every rock in it the way I knew every smuggler's path, and that this symmetry—one more thing we shared without meaning to—would keep arriving like a bill we couldn't stop paying.

We came around the bend and the channel narrowed between two shelves of granite, and the horses couldn't follow, and the arrows stopped, and for perhaps half a minute I believed we had won.

Then I heard her cry out.

Not loudly. That was the terrible part—it wasn't a scream, it was a short, bitten-off sound, the sound of a woman refusing to make noise about something that absolutely deserved noise. An arrow had come off the high bank at an angle I hadn't even thought to fear, and it had taken her below the left shoulder blade, angling in, and she went sideways into the current and I went after her because the alternative was unthinkable and also, frankly, unavoidable—I'd already decided, somewhere below thought, that I was not doing any of this without her.

I caught her by the collar of her mail shirt. Mail, thank every sleeping god, which had turned the shaft enough that it hadn't gone deep—but enough is a relative term, and the head was in, and the barbs were in, and the river was dragging us both toward a chute of white water that would finish what Hale started.

There was a fallen pine wedged against the far bank, its roots torn up in some old flood, forming a black lattice above the waterline. I got a hand into it. Then the other. Then I got her, barely, and hauled, screaming things I don't remember saying, until we were both out on the stones with the river roaring past like it resented our escape.

She was conscious. That seemed important. She was pale as the granite and shivering so hard her teeth clicked, but her eyes were open and furious.

"Don't," she said.

"Don't what?"

"Don't pull it out. Don't touch it. Don't apologize yet—you always apologize—and don't look at me like that."

"Like what?"

"Like I'm dying."

"You're not dying."

"Good," she said, and passed out.

* * *

I got her upstream to a place I knew—a smuggler's cache cut into the hillside above the ford, a low cave behind a curtain of hanging roots, dry-floored, with an old iron brazier and a stack of wood that some dead smuggler had laid in against exactly this kind of night. I built a fire small enough not to show. I cut her mail shirt off her with a scribe's knife, which took forever and ruined the knife, and then I looked at the arrow and understood why she hadn't wanted me to pull it out.

The head was lodged against the shoulder blade. Not through. If I pulled it straight, the barbs would open her like a ledger. It had to go deeper and out the front, or not at all.

I am a man who copies words for a living. I have steady hands because ink punishes tremors. I told myself this, several times, while my hands shook anyway.

"I need to push it through," I said, when her eyes opened again.

"I know."

"It's going to hurt."

"I've been stabbed before, Coral."

"Not by me."

"No," she agreed, and almost smiled. "By everything else. Go on."

I broke the fletching, pushed the shaft through, snapped the head off, and drew the stub out the front while she made that same bitten-off sound and gripped my wrist hard enough to leave bruises I wore for a week like jewelry. Then I packed the wound with the yarrow-and-salt paste I'd learned from a herbwife in Thornfell who charged me nothing and asked fewer questions, bound it tight, and sat back on my heels feeling like a man who has just defaced something holy.

She lay with her eyes closed, breathing carefully, the firelight moving on her face. There was blood on my hands up to the wrists. Her blood. Somewhere downstream, Hale was regrouping, and somewhere above us the Ashen Court was doing whatever the Ashen Court did, and the thirteenth moon was swelling toward fullness like a bruise coming up under the skin of the sky.

"Coral," she said.

"Yes."

"You should have taken his offer."

"I know."

"You could be free. Actually free. Eleven years of copying other people's numbers, and he handed you the key, and you threw it in the river."

"The river keeps things," I said. "It'll give it back someday."

She opened her eyes then and looked at me, and there was something in her face I hadn't seen before—or maybe had seen and refused to name, the way I'd refused to name a great many things, because naming makes them real and real things can be lost.

"Why?" she said. "Why refuse? Tell me truly. Not the speech you gave Hale. The true one."

And here is the thing about being asked a true question by someone bleeding for you: you cannot answer it with rhetoric. Rhetoric is for men standing safely on riverbanks.

"Because of you," I said.

Her breath caught. Carefully, because of the wound, but it caught.

"Hale offered me survival," I said. "Survival is what I've done my whole life. Keep the head down, copy the ledgers, learn the smuggler roads, count the years. And it worked. Right up until a dying man put a map in my hands and I discovered that surviving isn't the same as living, and right up until a knight with an oath like a millstone around her neck kept choosing, over and over, to do the harder thing than her oath required." I wiped my hands on my knees, uselessly; the blood had dried into the creases. "I refused him because every version of the future he offered me didn't have you in it. A cell in Lowmark, wine I didn't steal, pages nobody reads—and no Marguerite Voss arguing theology at me across a fire, telling me I'm a coward with good handwriting. I've had the quiet life. I had it for thirty years. It turns out it was just a smaller kind of drowning."

She was silent a long time. The fire popped. Outside, the river went on being the river.

"That's a terrible confession," she said finally. "You've doomed us both for the sake of a wounded woman with poor prospects."

"I've been told I make poor choices."

"You make *slow* ones," she said. "You circle the truth for days like a hawk that won't land. I've watched you do it since the garrison." Her voice was going rougher, softer. "Say the rest of it, Coral. You've started; finish it. I may be delirious tomorrow and I want it on record."

I laughed—actually laughed, kneeling in a smuggler's cache with her blood under my fingernails—and then I stopped laughing, because she was right, and because I was tired, so tired, of circling.

"I love you," I said.

The words came out smaller than I meant them to. True things often do; they don't need volume, they need only to be said once, plainly, the way you enter a figure in a ledger that cannot be corrected afterward.

"I love you," I said again, steadier. "Not despite the oath. Not despite any of it. I've watched you break yourself in half trying to honor a lie, and I've watched you choose truth anyway, every single time it cost you something, and I don't think there's another person alive I'd follow into a river full of arrows. So there it is. Entered in the ledger. No erasures."

She reached up—slowly, guarding the wound—and took my bloody hand in hers.

"Marguerite," I said. "That's the rest of it. I love *you*, not the knight. The woman who knows every rock in this river. The woman who let a fugitive go because burning a garrison full of people was worse than breaking her vow. Say something, or I'm going to die of it right here, and Hale won't even need his archers."

"Coral Galloway," she said, and her voice cracked in the middle of my name, which I will remember until I die, "you are the worst liar I have ever arrested, and I have loved you since the firelight at the garrison, and I have hated myself for it every day since, because loving you felt like the last piece of myself the oath hadn't taken." Her grip tightened. "Well. The oath is nearly gone anyway. It seems fitting that what's left of me goes to you."

Outside, far downstream, I thought I heard a horn—once, thin, swallowed by the water's noise. Or maybe I imagined it. Maybe I wanted to imagine it, so that this moment would have edges, so that it couldn't stretch out forever into something soft enough to be broken.

She slept, eventually, her hand still closed around mine, her breathing shallow but even. I fed the fire and kept watch and did the arithmetic I always did, the counting that had governed my whole life: three days to the vault. Nine nights until the moon stood full. One wounded knight. One scribe with a knife that no longer had an edge and a map that could end the world or save it.

And downstream, closing slow but certain as a debt called due, the sound of horns.

I looked at Marguerite's face in the firelight—at the lines pain had carved around her mouth, at the peace underneath them now that the circling was done—and I understood that whatever waited for us beneath Thornfell, we would meet it awake, together, and that this was worth more than every quiet year I had ever bought with my silence.

The horn sounded again. Closer this time. No imagining it now.

I banked the fire, shouldered our gear, and began, gently, to wake her.
