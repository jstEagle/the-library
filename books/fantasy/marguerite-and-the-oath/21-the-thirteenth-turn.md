## 21. The Thirteenth Turn

*Coral*

There is a particular kind of silence that comes just before a mountain decides whether to kill you or let you live. I had heard it twice before in my life—once in a rockslide at Widow's Stair, once the night the garrison burned—and I heard it again now, standing on the ridge above Thornfell with Marguerite's hand cold in mine, waiting for the thirteenth moon to finish rising.

The vault held.

I want to write that sentence larger than the page allows, because for three days we had not known if it would. Three days of tremors rolling through the valley like a giant turning over in his sleep, of refugees streaming down the smuggler roads I'd spent ten years memorizing, of the Order's bells ringing panic instead of vespers. And then, as the moon cleared the eastern peaks—fat and white and full for the thirteenth time since that dying courier pressed his map into my bleeding hands—the shaking stopped.

Not faded. *Stopped.* The way a man stops mid-sentence when he hears something in another room.

"Is that good?" Marguerite asked quietly. "Or is that worse?"

"I genuinely don't know," I said. "I've been a scribe, a fugitive, and a heretic this year. Nobody warned me I'd need to become a theologian too."

She didn't laugh. She hadn't laughed properly since the judgment hall, since she'd stood before the assembled Order and renounced every vow she'd ever spoken, her voice steady while the elders' faces went through grief and fury in equal measure. She'd saved my life with those words. She'd also burned down everything she was, and I think some mornings she woke up reaching for the ashes out of habit.

But tonight, watching the moon rise whole over a mountain that had decided—for now—to keep breathing, something in her shoulders loosened by a degree. Maybe two.

"It held," she said again, as if saying it twice would make it true.

"For now." I pulled the folded copy of the map from inside my coat—the third one we'd made, this one on good rag paper in ink mixed with ash so it couldn't be washed away—and looked at it without opening it. I didn't need to anymore. I could draw it from memory in the dark, which was fortunate, because we were about to spend a great deal of time in the dark. "Hale will have copies within the month. The Ashen Court already has theirs. Half the Order's scribes copied it before the elders thought to confiscate it, and you can't uncopy a thing, no matter how many inquisitors you send."

"So everyone knows."

"Everyone who matters knows the map exists. Knowing what it *means*—" I shrugged. "That's the war that hasn't started yet."

Below us, the valley was a scatter of cookfires. Refugees, mostly—people who'd fled the tremors and hadn't gone home, because home sat on top of a sleeping god and they'd only just learned that. Some of them had started calling us things. *Truth-bearers.* *Map-walkers.* One old woman had pressed a charm into my hand at the ford two days ago and called me blessed, which would have been funnier if Hale weren't currently offering a hundred crowns for my head and five hundred for Marguerite's.

"They're building something down there," Marguerite said. "In the camp. I heard them last night. They're talking about staying. About learning the roads. About—" she paused, choosing the word carefully, "—*waking up*."

"The way we did."

"The way we did," she agreed.

We stood there a while longer, two outlaws watching the moon complete its turn, and I found I couldn't summon the fear I probably ought to have felt. It wasn't courage. It was more like the strange lightness I'd felt the day I laid the original map on the temple floor and told four hundred people the truth with my voice shaking and my knees threatening to resign. After you've done the thing you were most afraid of, everything else is just weather.

"We should go," Marguerite said finally. "Before the moon gets high enough for Hale's scouts to see us against the snowline."

"One moment." I took out my stylus—not the temple-issue bronze, that had been confiscated along with everything else I owned, but a cheap iron one I'd bought from a tinker—and scratched a small mark into the granite of the ridge: a circle, thirteen points around its rim, the last point filled in. A scribe's tally. A debt paid.

"What's that?" she asked.

"A record," I said. "For whoever comes after. That's how it starts, you know. Not with armies. With someone writing something down where the next person can find it."

Marguerite Voss, knight of an order that no longer claimed her, traitor to an institution built on a lie, looked at my little scratched circle for a long moment. Then she drew her dagger and added a second mark beside it: a flame, small and clean, the sigil of the Quiet Flame crossed out with a single stroke.

"There," she said. "Now it's a conversation."

* * *

*Marguerite*

I had been a knight for eleven years, and in all that time I had never once walked anywhere without knowing whose authority put me there. Marching orders, patrol routes, a chain of command that ran from my captain to the commander to the elders to the Flame itself—or to whatever the Flame actually was, which turned out to be a story told by men who knew better. Every step of my adult life had been authorized.

Tonight I walked unauthorized, carrying stolen maps into mountains I'd been trained to purge, beside a debtor-scribe-turned-heretic whom I loved with a ferocity that still frightened me when I let myself look at it directly.

It should have felt like falling. Instead it felt like the first honest breath I'd taken in a decade.

Coral moved ahead of me on the switchback trail with the easy certainty of a man walking his own memory. He'd promised these roads were safe from the Order's patrols, and he'd been right so far, though "safe" was a relative term that apparently included loose scree, a frozen waterfall we had to cross sideways, and a cave whose previous occupant—a bear, judging by the evidence—had left in a hurry and might return with opinions.

"You're smiling," he said over his shoulder. "That worries me. Smiling on this trail usually precedes disaster."

"I was thinking about the bear."

"The bear is a legitimate concern."

"I was thinking I've fought duels, sieges, and one very memorable riot in Vell's Landing, and none of it scared me like that bear's den did."

"That's because the bear has no oath," Coral said solemnly. "Bears are entirely honest creatures. They tell you exactly what they intend, at volume, from a distance. Institutions could learn from bears."

I laughed—actually laughed, the sound startling both of us—and he grinned back at me, and for perhaps thirty seconds on that freezing switchback, with the thirteenth moon silvering the snow below us, I forgot that we were fugitives with prices on our heads and a war germinating under our feet.

Then we reached the overlook above Greywater Pass, and I stopped forgetting.

Fires in the pass. Not refugee fires—these were laid in a pattern, disciplined, spaced. Military. I put my hand on Coral's arm and pulled him down behind a shelf of rock, and we lay side by side on the cold stone while I counted.

"Twelve, maybe fifteen," I murmured. "Disciplined camp. Sentries posted on the high ground. That's not the Order—the Order doesn't post sentries above their own camps, arrogance being cheaper than vigilance."

"Ashen Court?"

"Or Hale's hunters wearing Ashen colors. He's done it before." I risked a look. The banners were furled, which told me nothing except that whoever commanded down there had learned caution. "They're sitting between us and the northern route."

Coral was quiet a moment. When I glanced at him, he had that expression I'd learned to read during our weeks in the deep mountains—the one that meant his mind had gone somewhere unpleasant and come back with a plan he hated.

"There's another road," he said. "Older. My mentor called it the Weepers' Path because the cliff face above it seeps groundwater year-round, and in winter it freezes into a wall of ice fangs that occasionally decide to fall on travelers. It's not on any map the Order has. It's barely on any map *I* have."

"How bad?"

"Three people have died on it that I know of. Two of them were my friends." He met my eyes. "But nobody's watching it, because nobody sane uses it."

"And we are not sane."

"We are spectacularly not sane. It's our defining quality."

I wanted to argue. I was trained to argue—assess the position, weigh the ground, choose the defensible option. But eleven years of defensible options had led me to standing in a judgment hall renouncing my vows while an inquisitor I'd once respected called me a traitor in front of God and everybody. The defensible options were all spoken for.

"Weepers' Path," I said. "At first light. Tonight we rest and I take first watch, because you've been copying maps by firelight for three nights and you're starting to squint like my old fencing master."

"I do not squint."

"Marguerite," I said, using his name deliberately, the way he used mine—small gifts we gave each other in the dark, reminders that we were people and not just roles in someone else's catastrophe, "you squint like a man reading scripture by candlelight and disagreeing with every line."

He opened his mouth to protest, considered, and closed it again. "That's fair," he admitted. "I do disagree with most scripture lately."

* * *

*Coral*

We made the Weepers' Path at dawn, and I will spare future historians the details except to record that the path earned its name, that Marguerite crossed the worst of the ice-fang stretch without a single wasted movement while I clung to the rock like a barnacle with regrets, and that neither of us died, which by the standards of that road constitutes a triumph worth a ballad.

By midday we were through, and by late afternoon we reached the hollow where we'd cached supplies two weeks earlier—a smuggler's cache my mentor had dug into a hillside forty years ago and which had outlasted him, the Order's attention, and one avalanche. Inside: hard bread, smoked goat, a jar of honey gone crystalline, and the thing I'd come for. Our copies. Six of them, wrapped in oilcloth, each drawn in my own hand.

Marguerite watched me count them with an expression caught between affection and exasperation. "Six."

"Six."

"When I asked why six, you said redundancy."

"I stand by redundancy."

"Coral. There are two of us."

"There are two of us *now*. But think about what happens next. These maps aren't for us—they're seeds. One goes to the refugee camp, to whoever's organizing there. One goes south, to a printer in Marrow's Bend who owes me a favor and prints things the Order disapproves of. One stays buried here as insurance against the other five being destroyed. One—" I hesitated, because this was the part I'd turned over a hundred times in the night watches. "One goes back to the temple. Left anonymously in the scriptorium, in a fair hand, where some young scribe finds it the way I found the ledger discrepancies that started me asking questions. Someone has to be on the inside. Someone always has to be on the inside."

"And the sixth?"

"The sixth is ours. Because if we die somewhere stupid, I want whoever buries us to find it and understand what we were doing."

She was quiet for a long moment. Then she took one of the oilcloth bundles, weighed it in her hands like something living, and tucked it inside her coat against her heart, which was either practical or sentimental and with Marguerite was reliably both.

"You know what I keep thinking about?" she said. "The judgment hall. When the mountain shook and the elders' whole beautiful lie came down around their ears. Hale was screaming for your execution and the floor was cracking and the senior elder—Elder Corvane, the one who taught me canon law—he looked at me. Just looked. And I realized he *knew*. He'd always known. Decades of knowing, and he'd built his life on top of it the way they built the temple on top of the vault." She shook her head slowly. "I thought breaking my oath would feel like losing myself. It didn't. It felt like putting down something heavy I'd forgotten I was carrying."

"Do you regret it? Any of it?"

"No." No hesitation, which was its own kind of answer. "Do you? You had a quiet life. Ledgers and salt runs and counting down your debt. You could have burned that courier's map and gone back to it."

I thought about it honestly, because she deserved honesty and because lying to Marguerite had never once worked anyway.

"Some nights I miss it," I said. "The smallness of it. Copying figures by lamplight, knowing exactly what tomorrow held. It was a cage but it was a *clean* cage." I looked up at the sky, where the thirteenth moon wouldn't rise again for another month, and then at the peaks beyond, where something vast slept with one eye half-open. "But the cage was always going to burn, Marguerite. The god was always going to stir. The only choice anyone ever had was whether to be awake when it happened. I'd rather be awake. Even out here. Even hunted." I smiled wryly. "Even on the Weepers' Path."

"Especially on the Weepers' Path," she said. "That path nearly killed us twice."

"Once."

"The ice bridge counted."

"The ice bridge was character-building."

She cuffed the back of my head, gently, the way she did, and we broke camp as the light failed, moving north along a ridgeline only smugglers and goats remembered.

And that was when I saw it—far off, across the valley, on the flank of Thornfell itself. A light on the mountain. Not a campfire. Too steady, too white, too high, in a place where nothing human should be. It hung above the hidden entrance to the vault like a star nailed to the stone, and as I watched, it pulsed. Once. Twice. In rhythm.

Like a heartbeat.

Marguerite saw it too. Her hand found mine in the dark and gripped hard.

"Coral," she said. "Tell me that's a reflection."

I opened my mouth to lie to her, gently, the way you comfort someone—and then the light pulsed again, faster, and somewhere beneath our feet, so deep it was more felt than heard, the mountain hummed a single low note that traveled up through our boots and into our bones.

The thirteenth turn was complete. And something below had noticed.

"We need to move," Marguerite breathed. "*Now.*"

We ran north into the dark, six maps between us and the coming war, carrying the truth like a lit torch through a dry field.
