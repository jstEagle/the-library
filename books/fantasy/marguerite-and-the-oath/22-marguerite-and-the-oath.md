## 22. Marguerite and the Oath

The road we walk now doesn't appear on any map, which is fitting, because I'm the one who drew the maps.

It's a smuggler's path, one of the old ones, cut into the eastern flank of Thornfell where the mountain drops away into pine and fog. My father walked it before me, and his father before him, back when the Galloways were moving untaxed brandy instead of untaxed people. There's a certain poetry in that, I suppose. Three generations of men who learned every crack in the world so they could slip through it, and now here I am, using all of it to carry farmers and millers and two very confused nuns toward a valley the Order hasn't thought to watch yet.

"Keep to the left of the cairn," I call back over my shoulder, for the dozenth time this morning. "Left. The right side is a scree slope that eats ankles and dignity in equal measure."

An old miller named Harrow mutters something about smart-mouthed guides. I let him. A guide who isn't grumbled about is a guide nobody trusts, and trust is the only currency out here that doesn't devalue.

Marguerite walks at the rear, as she always does, because she says rearguard is where the danger comes from and because—though she'd never admit it—she likes watching me work. I can feel her eyes between my shoulder blades like sunlight through a gap in shutters. Six months ago those eyes were cataloguing my satchel for contraband. Now they're just... watching. It's better. It's also more terrifying, if I'm honest, but I've decided to stop being honest with myself about small things. I save it all up for the big ones now.

* * *

We make camp that night in a hollow beneath an overhang that some long-dead smuggler carved steps into, back when the Order's reach was shorter and its appetite smaller. The refugees settle into exhausted silence—the particular silence of people who have walked away from everything and are only now realizing their legs still work. Harrow builds a fire small enough to hide from the sky. One of the nuns, Sister Anselm, produces a lump of honeycomb from somewhere in her habit and distributes it like communion.

I count heads. Forty-one tonight. Last week it was thirty-three. The word spreads the way words always spread: not by proclamation but by whisper, by a name passed hand to hand along roads that don't exist. *Galloway. He knows the ways through. Ask for Galloway.*

And Voss. Always they say both names, like a single word with two syllables. *Galloway-and-Voss.*

Which is funny, because we're not anything official. We're not an order or a company or a cause. We're a scribe who committed truth in public and a knight who committed treason in front of witnesses, and apparently that combination is more convincing than any sermon ever preached at the Quiet Flame.

"They're asking again," Marguerite says, settling beside me at the edge of the firelight. She says it quietly, so the refugees won't hear. "About the tremors."

"What did you tell them?"

"The truth." She turns her hands toward the coals. There's a new scar across her palm, thin and white—a souvenir from the vault, from the day she chose me over everything she'd built her life on. "That the mountain is shifting because something under it is turning in its sleep. That no one knows when it wakes. That we're walking them toward higher ground anyway."

"How did they take it?"

"One of the nuns started praying. Harrow asked if there was anything he could hit." A pause. The corner of her mouth does the thing it does—not quite a smile, but the ground where smiles are planted. "They took it well, actually. Better than the elders did."

The elders. Right. I think about the temple sometimes, the great hall where I stood with the bloodstained map held above my head like a lantern, my voice shaking and then, remarkably, not shaking. I think about Inquisitor Hale's face going through three distinct kinds of fury before settling on the cold administrative kind. I think about the moment the floor bucked under four hundred pairs of feet and every doctrine the Quiet Flame had ever printed became suddenly, catastrophically negotiable.

Hale called for my execution anyway. Of course he did. Men like Hale don't update their beliefs; they update their targets. But the mountain had voted first, and the elders—who had spent forty years signing orders they knew were lies—found that their spines worked after all, provided the alternative was being buried under the evidence.

So: vindication. Shadowed by loss, as promised. The temple split down its seams. Half the scribes walked out behind us. Half stayed, and half of those inform for Hale now, and the other half copy our maps in secret and send them east in flour sacks. The Order didn't die. Orders don't. They just shed skin and pretend the old one never fit.

"You're doing the thing," Marguerite says.

"What thing?"

"The thing where your face goes somewhere else and you narrate the whole conversation to yourself without telling me."

"I would never."

"You're doing it right now."

* * *

Later, when the camp has gone quiet except for wind and the occasional snore of profound millerly depth, I climb the twenty steps to the overlook. This is my ritual now. Every night, I look north, toward Thornfell's mass against the stars, and I check.

Tonight the mountain is dark. No glow. No sound. Just stone and snow and the memory of a seal I read by torchlight while the world tried to kill me for knowing how.

But the ground remembers. Twice this month the tremors came—one short, one long—and both times the refugees went silent in a way that told me they'd stopped thinking of them as weather. You can hear the difference in a crowd. Fear of storms sounds like complaint. Fear of gods sounds like prayer.

Sister Anselm finds me up there. She does that—appears beside me without footsteps, which I maintain is witchcraft and she maintains is "just walking softly, child," which is exactly what a witch would say.

"You count them every night," she says. "The heartbeats of the mountain."

"That's morbid."

"That's faithful." She settles onto the cold stone beside me with a grace that should be illegal for a woman her age. "You know, in the old days, before the Flame, we had a word for what you're doing. *Vigil.* Keeping watch over a sleeping thing, not to wake it, but to be awake when it does."

"I'm a scribe, Sister. I keep ledgers."

"Mm. And what is a vigil but a ledger of the sky?" She pats my knee with a hand like a dried leaf. "The girl down there. The knight. She watches you count."

"I know."

"Do you know why?"

"Because if I fall off the cliff she'll have to fill out paperwork."

Anselm laughs, a dry rustle, and stands. At the top of the steps she pauses. "When the thirteenth moon waned and the vault held, everyone said the danger had passed. Everyone was wrong, weren't they."

It wasn't a question, so I didn't answer it. But she nodded as if I had, and went down the stairs, and left me alone with the mountain and the arithmetic I do every night: the tremors are getting longer. The intervals are getting shorter. Whatever sleeps below is not sleeping deeper.

It's surfacing. Slowly. The way a swimmer rises toward light.

* * *

Morning brings a stranger, which is how most of our problems arrive these days.

She comes up the smuggler's path from the south, alone, on foot, wearing the grey travel-stained robes of a Flame scribe—or what used to be Flame scribe, before the schism made every grey robe either a traitor or a spy depending on who's counting. She's young. Twenty, maybe. Ink-stained fingers. The desperate, furious eyes of someone who has recently discovered that the institution that raised them has been lying since before they were born.

She stops ten feet from our fire, sees Marguerite's sword, and visibly decides she doesn't care.

"Coral Galloway?"

"Depends who's asking."

"My name is Wren. I copied maps for the eastern archive." She pulls a folded paper from her robe and holds it out with a hand that shakes only a little, which given the sword is impressive. "This is yours. Or—it's a copy of yours. We've been copying them for months. Sending them east. But the archive was raided three nights ago. Hale's people. They took everything, and they took Brother Callow too, and—" Her voice cracks, and she hates it, I can see her hating it. "They knew where to look. Someone gave them the routes. And the interrogations have started, and I couldn't—I wouldn't sign the confession, so I ran, and everyone said find Galloway-and-Voss, so I found you."

I take the paper. It's my own cartography staring back at me, rendered in a careful young hand, every cache and switchback labeled in a neat ecclesiastical script. Strange feeling, seeing your survival translated into someone else's handwriting.

"Hale has Callow," Marguerite says quietly. She's moved to stand at my shoulder, reading over my arm. "Callow taught half the eastern archive to read old script."

"He taught *me*," Wren says. "He's seventy years old. He signs his name with a flourish because he thinks it's funny. And they have him."

Silence around the fire. Even Harrow has stopped pretending not to listen.

Here's the thing about the life I've got now: six months ago, a stranger in trouble would have been someone else's problem. I had a debt, a desk, and a philosophy of strategic blindness that had kept me alive through things that killed braver men. My entire moral architecture was load-bearing cowardice, and it worked, and I hated it, and I mistook the hatred for conscience so I could sleep at night.

Then a dying man pressed a map into my hands and said *thirteen turns*, and the architecture came down, and I learned what I'd suspected all along: that underneath the cowardice there was a person who, when shown a truth worth dying for, would rather die than bury it. I didn't choose that person. I just stopped outrunning him.

"We can't storm an interrogation cell," Marguerite is saying, practical as ever. "Where was he taken?"

"The garrison at Saltmere. Rebuilt. Hale commands it himself now."

"Hale." Marguerite's jaw does something complicated. Hale, who offered me pardon for betrayal. Hale, whose face I last saw through smoke as the garrison burned. "Of course he does."

Wren looks between us, and I watch her do the arithmetic every refugee does in their first hour with us: *these two are famous, these two are outlaws, these two are my last hope, and one of them keeps looking at the other one like—*

Yes, child. Like that. Get used to it. Half the order we're accidentally founding was built by people who looked at each other like that and decided the world was worth the trouble.

* * *

That evening I sit with the copies—all of them, the ones Wren carried and the ones sent in flour sacks and the master sheets Marguerite and I have been refining since the night we fled the temple with the original pressed flat between boards on my back. Maps of the hidden roads. Maps of the caches. And, in a locked case that travels against my ribs, the thing itself: the bloodstained original, the courier's last argument, the reason for all of it.

A new order is forming around us whether we will it or not. It has no name yet. Wren calls it "the Vigil" because of something Sister Anselm said, and Anselm pretends to be embarrassed, and the name sticks anyway. Its sacraments are ink and honesty. Its saints are a dead courier nobody can identify and a seventy-year-old man with a flourishy signature sitting in a cell at Saltmere. Its doctrine fits on a page: *the gods are sleeping, the lie is ending, be awake when it does.*

Forty-one refugees become sixty. Sixty become a caravan. Somewhere east, other cells are forming around other copies, scribes and knights and millers and nuns, all of them holding the same dangerous document and the same dangerous idea: that the truth belongs to everyone it will kill.

Marguerite finds me at dusk, packing for the road south. She looks at the case against my ribs, then at my face.

"You're going after Callow."

"We're going after Callow," I correct. "Unless you plan to stay here and guard refugees, in which case I'll go alone and be insufferable about it later."

"You'd be dead by Saltmere."

"Probably. Hence the insufferability being posthumous."

She almost smiles. Then she doesn't, because she's looking past me, south, where the horizon has gone strange—the kind of amber light that isn't sunset, sitting low over the mountains like a bruise seen through skin. As we watch, the ground gives a long, slow shudder. Not the sharp jolt of the recent months. Something deeper. Slower. Rhythmic.

Like breathing.

Around the camp, the refugees have gone still. Nobody screams anymore; we've passed that lesson. They just hold still and wait it out, faces turned toward Thornfell, and when it passes they resume packing with the grim efficiency of people who have accepted the terms.

"Intervals are down to four days," Marguerite says. "Three, if the pattern holds."

"It won't hold. Patterns never do. That's the whole problem with patterns." I close the map case. "Hale wants the truth buried and the witnesses with it. The Ashen Court wants the seal broken and the god woken. The mountain wants to wake up. And we want—" I stop, because I've never actually said it out loud, what we want.

Marguerite waits. She's good at that. Six months of watching me talk my way through terror has taught her which silences need filling and which ones need keeping.

"We want people standing in the open when it happens," I finish. "Awake. Knowing. Not kneeling in a temple that lied to them, not dancing around a fire for exiles with a resurrection fantasy. Just—awake. However it ends."

"However it ends," she repeats.

"And in the meantime, there's a seventy-year-old man in a cell who taught a generation of children to read, and I owe him a debt, and I keep my debts now. All of them. Even the stupid ones." I shoulder my pack. "Especially the stupid ones."

She falls into step beside me, sword at her hip, oath broken and remade into something neither of us has a name for yet—duty, maybe, but duty chosen instead of sworn, which is the only kind that holds when the mountain shakes. Behind us the caravan stirs, and Wren catches up with ink-stained fingers and furious eyes, and Harrow grumbles about southern roads, and Sister Anselm appears at the rear without footsteps, keeping her vigil.

South, then. Toward Saltmere. Toward Hale. Toward whatever the thirteenth turn failed to finish.

The mountain breathed again as we walked, long and slow, and this time I swear I felt it lean, faintly, toward the waking world.

We intend to meet it awake.
