## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The temple of Vael kept its accounts the way avalanches kept their schedules: precisely, and without mercy.

I had copied eleven hundred contracts in my nine years there, and I could tell you the exact number because I counted everything. That was the first thing they taught a debt-scribe—not letters, not law, but arithmetic. A man who cannot count cannot be cheated, said Brother Ossian, which was true as far as it went, and also a lie, because the men who taught me counting were the same men doing the cheating, just with better handwriting.

My desk sat in the scriptorium's coldest corner, beneath a window that framed the mountain like a tombstone. Ink froze in the well by midwinter; I learned to warm it against my ribs. The contracts came up from the low country on mule-back—smugglers who'd been caught, laborers who'd borrowed against next year's wages, widows who'd signed away their daughters' labor for a winter's coal—and I copied each one fair and true into the temple's great ledger, *Liber Debitum*, the Book of What Is Owed.

Fair and true. That phrase does a lot of work if you let it.

Here is what "fair and true" meant: I copied the interest rates exactly as written, even when the rate on the page differed from the rate spoken aloud in the signing room below. I recorded the collateral exactly as written—the mule, the boat, the daughter—even when the collateral had already been seized before the ink dried. I wrote the names of the debtors in a fine clear hand, because a beautiful ledger is a comfort to the powerful, and the powerful paid for beautiful things.

And then, at night, when the brothers were asleep and the temple's one lamp-oil ration had burned down to a blue bead in its dish, I took out my own book.

It was nothing grand. A sewn sheaf of rag paper, small enough to hide in the false bottom of my ink chest, wrapped in oilcloth against damp. On its pages I kept a second accounting, the one no one had commissioned: every name that passed through my hands, every rate actually charged versus the rate written down, every seizure, every "voluntary" indenture of a child whose father owed four crowns and eight coppers. Nine years of it. If you stacked the temple's ledgers beside mine, mine would look like a hymnal next to a boulder. But it was complete. That was the point. Somewhere in Thornfell, someone would eventually want the truth in a form they could carry, and I intended to be the man holding it.

I told myself this made me brave. Mostly it made me tired, and occasionally terrified, and very good at pretending to be dull. Dullness is a craft like any other. I had refined mine to a lacquer finish. Brother Ossian believed I was the most boring scribe in the mountain, and I worked hard to keep his faith.

Sister Anselm knew, though. Of course she did. You cannot hide anything from the woman who taught you your letters, mostly because she taught you the letter for lying while looking honest, though she'd have denied it under torture.

Anselm ran the infirmary and the alms-cellar and, unofficially, the conscience of the entire temple, an organ the rest of the building had amputated centuries ago. She was seventy-one years old, built like a doorframe, and she treated debt-fever and frostbite and broken bones for anyone the temple's own contracts hadn't yet killed. Every few weeks she'd come stand behind my chair while I worked, read three lines over my shoulder, and say something like, *"You've spelled 'indenture' wrong again, Coral."*

I never had. It was her way of telling me she saw me, and my way of surviving was not to turn around.

* * *

The night the stars went out, I was copying a contract for a smuggler named Dree Voss, who had been caught with forty pounds of untaxed salt in the false keel of his boat and had agreed to five years' labor in the mines below the temple in exchange for not being hanged. Salt. Everything in this province came back to salt eventually—the white roads, the white scars on the mountainside where the mines opened their mouths, the white crust on the lips of miners who came up coughing. The temple owned the mines. The mines fed the wards. That much I knew, the way you know the shape of a thing you've never looked at directly: the border wards of Thornfell, the great invisible lattice that kept out whatever the founders had been afraid of, were renewed each year in a rite at the capital, and the rite required salt, and our salt was considered the purest in the kingdom.

Dree Voss's contract ran to six pages. I was on page four, warming the ink against my ribs, when the light changed.

Not dimmed. *Changed.* The lamp flame stood up straight and went the color of old teeth, and the shadows in the scriptorium leaned all one direction, like grass under wind, except there was no wind. My quill dragged a long gray streak across Dree Voss's collateral clause.

Then the sound came. I don't know how else to write it—a shudder, but felt in the teeth rather than the ears, as if the mountain itself had been struck a blow somewhere below the level of hearing. Dust sifted from the rafters. In the reliquary chapel across the courtyard, the great bell rang once, unstruck, and stopped itself.

I sat very still, the way you do when the floor might be a liar.

Then I did the only thing my nature allowed. I finished page four.

* * *

They found Sister Anselm at dawn in the alms-cellar, kneeling among the charity bread as if she'd paused mid-prayer. Except she wasn't praying. She was dead, and her eyes—

Her eyes were crusted with salt. Not wept salt, not the dried tracks grief leaves. Packed. Filled. As though her tears had run backward, or as though something had reached in through them and left a deposit, the way frost fills a window's corners. Her face was otherwise peaceful. That was the worst part. She looked like a woman who had seen something so beautiful she'd had to close her eyes against it, and the salt had simply moved in while they were shut.

Brother Ossian pronounced it apoplexy. He said it loudly, twice, in the courtyard, in the voice he used for things that must not be discussed further. The novices nodded. The older brothers looked at the sky and said nothing at all, which told me more than any sermon.

Because the sky was wrong.

I stepped out into the dawn yard and looked up, and the stars were gone. Not clouded—there wasn't a cloud from horizon to horizon, just a clean, hard, empty blue-black fading into morning. And I understood, in the way you understand a sum is wrong before you find the error, that the stars had not merely failed to appear. They had been *taken*. The whole vault of night above Vael was blank the way a page is blank after a debt has been scraped away and rewritten.

Around me the temple went about its morning. Bells for prime. Porridge in the refectory. Two brothers arguing about a mule. And underneath it all, a silence with a shape, the silence of men who have all noticed the same impossible thing and agreed, wordlessly, to keep copying.

I went back to my desk. My hands were steady, which surprised me. I took out Dree Voss's contract, blotted the gray streak, and recopied the page fair and true.

Then I unlocked the false bottom of my ink chest, unwrapped the oilcloth, and opened my own ledger to a fresh page. At the top, in my smallest hand, I wrote:

*Night of the vanished stars. Sister Anselm, dead. Salt in the eyes. Sky empty. Cause unknown. Entered here because it will be entered nowhere else.*

I looked at those words for a long time. Nine years of recording other people's losses had taught me one thing above all: a record is a wager. You write it down because you believe someone, someday, will come asking. But sitting there with the mountain silent around me and the sky scraped bare, I felt the floor of my understanding give way—the quiet, certain knowledge that the world I had catalogued so carefully, the world of rates and seizures and small cruelties, had been a floor laid over a deeper dark, and the dark had just sent up its first bill.

That evening, Brother Ossian summoned me to his study. He looked older than he had at breakfast, which I hadn't thought possible, and on his desk lay a document bearing the seal of the capital.

"Coral," he said, in a voice I'd never heard him use—careful, almost gentle. "How much do you know about the mines?"

"Only what the contracts say, Brother."

He studied me for a moment too long. "The temple has received instructions. All records pertaining to the ward-salt are to be sealed and forwarded to the crown. Immediately. You'll prepare the copies yourself. No one else is to see them."

"And the debtors' names in those records?"

"What of them?"

"Once they're sealed, no one can audit them. No one can prove what was taken or owed."

Ossian's eyes flicked to my chest—to the ink chest, the one with the false bottom—and then away, so quickly I might have imagined it. "Coral," he said, "the stars are out. Audit is a luxury of lit skies."

I bowed and withdrew, and in the corridor outside his door I stopped, pressed my back against the cold stone, and did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since dawn. The wards fed on salt. Our salt fed the wards. The king's renewal rite had failed—or been made to fail. And now the crown wanted every record of that salt locked away where no scribe with a conscience and a hidden ledger could ever compare the columns.

Someone had balanced these books before I ever got the chance to open them.

And Sister Anselm, who saw everything, had died with her eyes full of the evidence.

That night I packed my ledger deep in my kit, along with dry bread, a knife, and every copper I owned—which is to say, thirty-two. I didn't know yet where I was going. I only knew that a man who keeps accounts cannot stay in a house whose books are being burned, and that somewhere below the mountain, in the salt-dark of the mines, the first entry in a new ledger was waiting to be read.

I blew out my lamp and listened to the temple breathe around me, and for the first time in nine years, I was afraid of the dark for reasons that had nothing to do with being caught.
