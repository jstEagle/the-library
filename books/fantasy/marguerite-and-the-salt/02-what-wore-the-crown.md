## 2. What Wore the Crown

There are three ways into the royal archive of Thornfell, and I had built two of them myself, back when I still believed the crown would reward loyalty with honesty.

The first is the servants' stair behind the tapestry room—sealed now, since my pardon was revoked and my face went up on the checkpoint boards. The second is the coal chute that feeds the archive's own hearth, which the archivists never use because they prefer to be cold in a dignified way. The third is the front door, which requires papers I no longer possess and a name I can no longer say aloud in this city without someone spitting.

I went in by the coal chute. It seemed fitting. Everything I'd built lately had been out of filth and desperation, and at least the chute didn't pretend otherwise.

I dropped the last six feet onto ash that hadn't been swept since my disgrace, which I chose to take as a personal courtesy from whatever god looks after fallen ward-keepers. The hearth room smelled of cold iron and older secrets. Above me, through the floor, lay forty years of the kingdom's memory: treaties, censuses, ward-registers, rite-records. Everything the crown knew. Everything the crown preferred not to know, filed separately under lock.

I had come for the second kind.

My hands were steady. I want that noted, by whoever keeps the ledger of my life, if anyone does. My hands were steady all night, even when they shouldn't have been. Fear came later, wearing better clothes.

* * *

The renewal rite had failed eleven days ago. The whole capital knew it, though nobody said it plainly—the way nobody says plainly that the milk has turned or that a friend is dying. You feel it instead. The wards along the Processional had gone grey as dishwater. The temple bells rang the hour and the sound fell flat, like coins on wool. And King Aldric, who had greeted his people every morning of his reign from the balcony with that tired, decent smile of his, had stopped appearing altogether. The court said illness. The court says illness the way the sea says weather: as cover for something enormous moving underneath.

I had designed the renewal wards twenty years ago. Not alone—I was junior then, hungry, flattered to be included—but my hand was on them. I knew their grammar the way you know your mother's voice. And when I read the reports smuggled out to me by a clerk who owed me more than he could safely pay, I understood within an evening what had happened, because there was only one thing in the world that could make those wards fail so quietly, so completely, without a single alarm bell.

Bad salt.

Not stolen salt. Not missing salt. Corrupted salt—mined wrong, blessed wrong, or worse, mined by people whose suffering had soaked into it. Salt remembers. Every apprentice learns that before they learn anything else. Salt is the kingdom's oldest witness; it holds what is pressed into it. Feed a ward salt pressed full of debt and despair and the slow grinding misery of ten thousand unfree hands, and the ward doesn't shatter. It opens. Politely. Like a door held for a guest.

I needed to see the rite-record. I needed to know what the salt had opened *onto*.

* * *

The archive's inner vault took me the better part of two hours, because whoever had come before me had been thorough in ways that made my professional pride ache. The lock on the restricted shelf was new—Temple work, silver-cored, the kind that tastes your blood while it decides about you. I have old skills and fewer scruples than I did at thirty, and I got through it with a burned thumb and a great deal of muttered profanity, and then I stood in the dark among the crown's worst truths and lit one small hooded lamp.

The renewal records were exactly where protocol demanded, which told me everything. When the crown hides a document, it doesn't remove it—that leaves a gap, and gaps invite questions. It buries it in order. Bureaucracy is the best crypt in the world.

I found the assay first. The salt delivered for the king's renewal had been certified pure by three separate temple assessors, which was two too many; purity certifications travel in threes only when someone is very frightened. The mine of origin was listed as Vael. Of course it was. Half the salt in Thornfell comes out of Vael, out of the mountain where the temple keeps its debt-ledgers and its laborers and its polite, papered-over slavery. I had signed off on Vael salt for years. We all had. It was clean, the assessments said. It was always clean.

But the assayers' marks were wrong. Subtly wrong—a rotated glyph, a stroke weight off by a hair. Forged, and forged recently, and forged by someone with access to genuine temple seals. I sat back on my heels in the dust and felt the cold start at the base of my skull and work its way down.

Because I knew those wards. I knew what corrupted salt did to them. And I knew—had always known, in the place where ward-keepers keep the things they don't say at dinner—that the renewal rite wasn't a blessing. It was a maintenance ritual. A feeding. The wards around the king's person weren't there to protect Aldric from the world.

They were there to keep something *in*.

Every ward-keeper of my generation learned the shape of it without learning its name. The coronation renewal, the border seals, the great lattice over Thornfell—all of it drew from one deep working laid down centuries ago, before the temple had its current face, when the founders had imprisoned something in the mountain salt. Something hungry. They called it nothing, officially. Children called it the Salt King, in the way children will find the true name of a thing without ever being taught it.

The rite had failed. The door had been held open. And the guest had walked in wearing the only body in the kingdom warded against everything except the salt itself.

I found the founder's record an hour before dawn, in a folio so old the leather had gone soft as skin.

It was a binding contract—not metaphorically, literally, ink on vellum, the original working set down by hands that wrote in a script half gone from the world. I read it twice because the first time my eyes refused the obvious. The binding required three components: salt from the deep vein, a vessel of royal line to hold the hunger's attention, and—

*A seal of Hartwell blood, freely given, to close the working.*

Hartwell. I knew the name the way every ward-keeper knows it: from the extinction notice. House Hartwell, hereditary seal-bearers, line ended some ninety years past—died out, scattered, erased. There was a formal record of it, dry as bone, filed with all the other houses that had burned through history's fingers. Officially extinct. Officially finished. Which meant, in the language of archives, that someone had once paid good money to make it official.

I sat in the dark with the lamp between my knees and made myself think it through, because terror wants you to leap to conclusions and terror is usually lying.

If the line was truly dead, the founders' contract was a dead letter, and the Salt King's prison had been maintained all these centuries on ritual habit and royal blood alone. Possible. But the founders had not been fools—they had built redundancies the way I built wards, layered and hidden. And if the line was not dead—if someone had buried the name rather than end it—then somewhere in Thornfell there was a person walking around with the one blood in the world that could either re-seal the hunger forever or unmake the binding entirely, depending on how it was given. Freely offered, it closed the door. Taken by force, it—

No. The contract was specific. *Freely given.* That was the hinge of the whole ancient working, the flaw and the lock both. The Salt King could not take a Hartwell. It could only be *given* one.

Which meant that if such a person existed, the hunger in the king's body had spent the last eleven days doing the only thing it could do: looking.

And I thought about the failed rite. About the corrupted salt from Vael. About why, of all the mines in the kingdom, the hunger's servants had reached into Vael specifically—and I remembered, absurdly, a piece of temple gossip from years ago, a clerk's bored complaint about the mountain's ledgers: *some scribe down there copies contracts all day and knows more names than the abbot.*

A scribe. In Vael. With access to every debt-record in the temple's keeping—and therefore, if he had any curiosity at all, and scribes always do, access to the genealogies underneath the debts. To the families the temple had ground down and renamed and scattered until their histories fit in a ledger line.

I dug through the census folios with hands that had finally, finally begun to shake. Ninety years is long enough to lose a name in, but debts leave trails—marriage settlements, surety bonds, inherited obligations, the whole sad sediment of a family sinking. And there, at the bottom of a Vael surety register, forty years old, a collateral notation in a minor clerk's hand:

*Hartwell descent claimed by debtor's kin; unverifiable; noted for interest only.*

Interest only. Filed away. Forgotten by everyone except, perhaps, the family itself, who would have kept the knowledge the way poor people keep everything precious: quietly, uselessly, close.

I closed the folio. I put out the lamp. And in the dark of the crown's own memory, Marguerite Vane—disgraced, pardoned-and-unpardoned, architect of the failing wards, coward by several documented measures—sat perfectly still and let the truth arrive the way winter arrives: not all at once, but completely.

The wards were failing because I had helped build them and the salt had been poisoned and the king was worn like a coat. The binding could be remade or undone by a single drop of Hartwell blood, freely given. And the only living Hartwell was a debt-scribe in a mountain temple, copying other people's servitude in his own careful hand, with no idea that he was the last key in the kingdom—or that everything with teeth had already begun turning toward Vael.

I had perhaps days. Perhaps less. The hunger had the crown, the temple's forged seals, and infinite patience. I had a coal chute, a burned thumb, and a horse I'd stolen from a man who deserved it.

I laughed once, quietly, because the alternative was screaming, and screaming echoes in archives.

Then I folded the founder's contract into my coat, next to my heart, where I keep the things that are going to kill me, and started climbing back up toward the ash and the cold and the long road into the mountains—to find a scribe named Hartwell, and tell him what his blood was worth, and pray to every star still burning overhead that I reached him before the salt did.
