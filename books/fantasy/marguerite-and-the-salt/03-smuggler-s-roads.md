## 3. Smuggler's Roads

They came for me at the hour of terce, which I have always considered poor manners on the inquisitors' part, since it interrupted the only peaceful stretch of my day—the copying of contracts for men who could not read them and would not admit it.

There were four of them, which seemed excessive. I was a scribe. My most dangerous weapon was a well-sharpened quill, and even that I mostly used against my own fingers when the ink froze.

"Coral Hartwell," said the tall one, whose name I later learned was Brother Ossian, though at the time he was simply a wall of grey wool with a face like a closed ledger.

"That's me," I said. "Though if this is about Brother Fenwick's ink requisitions, I want it noted that I flagged the discrepancy twice."

"Search his cell."

They searched my cell. They found the ledger.

I had kept it for six years, hidden inside the false spine of a hymnal nobody sang from—*Hymns of the Mountain Vigil*, a book so dull even the moths had given up on it. In it I recorded every debt the temple of Vael held: every laborer bound to the salt mines below, every smuggler who'd paid his passage in years of service, every widow whose husband's contract had been quietly transferred to her sons. Names, sums, terms, dates. Six hundred and eleven entries. I told myself it was an act of witness. Some nights I suspected it was just the one rebellion available to a man with ink-stained hands and no courage to speak of.

Brother Ossian turned the pages with two fingers, as though the names might stain him.

"Heresy," he said.

"It's bookkeeping," I said.

"The temple's debts are temple property. To copy them is theft." He looked up, and there was something almost gentle in his face, which frightened me more than cruelty would have. "To *keep* them—" He closed the book. "Where did you get the idea no one owned these names but God?"

I didn't answer. There was no answer that wouldn't end with more chains.

They took me down through the mountain in a cage-cart, past the mine entrances where the debt-bound watched us pass with the flat patience of people who had learned that watching anything too closely was itself a debt. The salt glittered in the torchlight. It had always glittered. I had never once wondered why the temple sat on top of it like a hen on eggs.

That was the week the stars went out, you understand. The whole sky above Vael was a smooth black nothing, and everyone pretended it was weather.

* * *

The transport to the lowland tribunal took nine days by the pilgrim road. We made it four before the road stopped existing.

I mean that literally. One moment there was the switchback, the pines, the drop into cloud; the next, our escort—six soldiers and Brother Ossian—were staring at a hillside where no road had ever been, and the cart horse was screaming.

"There was a road," Ossian said, to the hillside, to God, to me.

"There was," I agreed.

Because I knew what had happened. I knew because I had grown up in these mountains, before the temple bought my father's debts and then, with the effortless appetite of institutions, my own. And I knew something the temple maps did not show: the smugglers' roads. Paths that existed only if you already believed in them, cut by generations of men moving salt and untaxed brandy under the wards' blind eye. The wards had fallen. The stars were gone. And the old paths, which had always bent around authority, had apparently decided the temple no longer counted as authority.

The hillside swallowed our cart. Not dramatically—no flash, no thunder. The road simply became a sheep track, then a suggestion, then a steep green nothing, and we spent a humiliating afternoon hauling the horse back uphill while I memorized, with a scribe's precision, exactly which way the mountain had lied to us.

On the fifth night they made camp in a hollow, and I lay in the cage listening to the guards argue about whether the starless sky meant the end of days or merely the end of the tax year, and I thought: *someone is going to come for me out of that dark, and it will not be rescue.*

I was half right.

* * *

She came at the change of watch, when the fire was down to coals and the sentry was doing that thing sentries do, which is standing very still while thinking about anything but sentry work.

I heard her before I saw her—a whisper of movement, no more—and then a hand closed over my mouth through the bars and a voice said, close to my ear: "Scribe. If you scream, I'll have to be rude, and neither of us has time for rude. Nod if you're Coral Hartwell."

I nodded, mostly because her hand smelled of cold iron and I valued my lips.

"Good. Keep nodding or shaking. Are you the one who wrote the ledger?"

Shake. Well—I hesitated first, and she felt the hesitation.

"You are. Don't insult us both. Do you want to leave this cage?"

Nod. Obviously nod.

"Then be quiet and small."

What followed took perhaps ninety seconds and was, I confess, the most exciting thing to happen to me in six years of copying contracts. She did not fight the guards; she *unmade* them, in a manner of speaking. Her fingers traced signs in the air and the fire guttered blue, and the sentry sat down where he stood like a puppet with cut strings, snoring. The other three followed. Even Brother Ossian, rising blearily with a knife already in his fist, folded mid-lunge and slept in the dirt with an expression of profound disappointment.

"Ward-craft," she said, working the lock. "Old craft. Come on."

"Who are you?" I whispered.

"Marguerite Vane. Formerly keeper of the western border wards, currently a traitor with excellent timing. Walk."

We walked. The mountain received us the way it receives its own—sheep track, scree, a path that appeared under our feet only when she stopped trying to lead and let me choose. That interested her. I could feel her interest like weather.

"You know these roads," she said.

"I grew up here. Smugglers' roads. They don't like being mapped, so nobody maps them. You have to *ask* them."

"And they answered you."

"They've always answered me. I assumed they were just badly maintained."

She made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. We climbed until the camp fires were embers below and the starless sky pressed down like a lid, and then she stopped in a hollow ringed by stones—old ward-stones, I realized, their carvings worn to ghosts—and turned to face me.

"Coral Hartwell," she said. "Last of the Hartwell line."

"I'm a scribe."

"You're a Hartwell. Your grandfather was Sealer Hartwell of Vael, and his mother before him, back eleven generations to the woman who wrote the original binding. The temple burned the genealogies eighty years ago and declared your blood extinct. They were thorough. They were not thorough enough, because debt records are also records, and someone in your family kept paying what the temple said they owed, generation after generation, signing their name in the margins." She paused. "You sign your full name on everything. I've seen the ledgers. You can't help yourself."

"I like things to be accurate," I said stiffly.

"So do I. That's rather the problem." She crouched, drew a line in the frost with one finger, and spoke a word I didn't know but my bones did. The line flared white, then black, then ate itself. "Ward-craft responds to intent and to blood. Watch."

She pricked her thumb on a knife and let one drop fall on the dead ward-line. Nothing. Frost, stone, silence.

"Now you."

"No."

"Coral."

"That's a *ward-line*. You said yourself the wards are broken, fallen, corrupted—whatever word we're using. If I touch it and something happens, I'd rather not know what."

"If you touch it and something happens, we'll both know what, and knowing is the entire reason I climbed a mountain to break you out of a cage." She held out the knife, handle first. "Please. I've walked a long way on a guess. Let me stop guessing."

And here is the thing I have had a great deal of time to think about since: I took the knife. Not because I believed her. Because some scribe-shaped flaw in me could not bear an unverified claim. Give me a rumor and a blank margin and I will test the rumor every time.

One drop of blood fell on the ward-line.

The line did not flare. It *bloomed*—white light running outward through the frost in branching veins, the whole hollow suddenly lit like noon, the old carvings on the stones waking one after another with a sound like distant bells underwater. For three heartbeats the mountain remembered what it was supposed to be. Then it all collapsed inward, the light drinking itself down into the point where my blood had fallen, and the darkness that rushed back in was darker than before, and colder, and somewhere far beneath us—deep, deep down, below the mines, below the salt—something turned over in its sleep and paid attention.

Marguerite exhaled slowly. "Unbinding blood," she said. "Or completing blood. Both. It depends entirely on how it's used."

"Used for *what?*" My voice came out smaller than I wanted. "What binding? What are you talking about?"

So she told me. There in the dark, with the starless sky overhead and the taste of my own blood in my mouth, she told me about the Salt King—the hunger the temple hadn't imprisoned so much as *fed*, all these centuries, with the salt from its own mines, mined by its own debt-bound, hauled up in barrels stamped with the temple seal. She told me the border wards weren't walls. They were a diet plan. A regulated feeding schedule, calibrated by ward-keepers like her, designed to keep the thing below dreaming instead of waking. She told me the king's renewal rite had failed because someone had fed it corrupted salt, and that King Aldric was no longer entirely King Aldric, and that in nineteen days, at the coronation renewal, whatever wore the crown would wear it permanently.

"And your blood," she finished, "is the original seal. Given freely, it can rebind the Salt King for another age. Taken unwillingly—or given wrongly—it completes the opposite. It finishes the job the corruption started. Either way, Coral, the rite needs a Hartwell. Which means either we reach the capital and use you properly, or *it* uses you however it likes."

The silence afterward had texture. I remember that. It felt like wet wool.

"No," I said.

"Coral—"

"No. No, absolutely not." I was backing away from her, from the dead ward-line, from the whole conversation, my hands up as if I could ward off information. "You've got the wrong scribe. I copy contracts. I am *nobody*. Nobody is the entire point of me—that's what the temple built, that's what the debts built, a man so small he doesn't matter to anyone. And now you're telling me I'm the keystone of some eleven-hundred-year-old—no. Find another Hartwell."

"There are no other Hartwells. That's what 'last of the line' means."

"Then find a different blood! You're ward-keepers, you're clever, you rewrote the wards once, rewrite them again!"

"With what? The craft is dying, the wards are falling, and the one blood that answers—" She stopped herself. Took a breath. When she spoke again her voice was quieter, and worse for it. "I'm not asking you to decide tonight. I'm asking you to walk downhill with me and stay alive long enough to decide at all."

But I wasn't hearing her. All I could hear was the arithmetic of my own life collapsing: six hundred and eleven names in a hidden ledger, and mine among them at last, except mine wasn't a debt anymore, it was a *price*. I had spent thirty years learning how to be insignificant, and she stood there asking me to be load-bearing.

"I didn't ask for this," I said.

"No," she agreed. "Nobody asks. That's rather the definition."

"Well, I decline. Formally. Consider it declined in writing." I was already turning, already climbing, because above us—not toward the capital, not downhill, but *up*, toward the high passes where the world got simpler and thinner—there was a route I'd copied once from a dead smuggler's map, a route over the shoulder of the mountain that crossed no wards, touched no salt, and asked nothing of anyone.

"Coral!" Her voice cracked across the scree. "You'll die up there alone!"

"Better than being *useful*!" I shouted back, and immediately hated how it sounded, and kept climbing anyway, because the alternative was to stand still and become a person things depended on.

Behind me, faintly, I heard her say something else—too quiet to catch, or maybe I chose not to catch it. The mountain took my side. The smugglers' road opened ahead of me, narrow and stubborn and free of meaning, and I walked it into the starless dark, alone, telling myself the lie I was best at:

*I am nobody. Nobody is safe.*

Three ridges later, I found the first of the salt-eaten villages, and learned precisely how wrong a scribe can be.
