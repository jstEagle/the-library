## 4. The Starless Vale

I had never realized how much of the world was stars until they were gone.

You don't count them, you know. You don't stand in a temple courtyard at midnight and think, *there go another four hundred lights I'm taking for granted.* You just assume they'll be there, the way you assume the roof will hold and the bread will rise and the temple will keep its word about anything. Then one night the sky is a black lid screwed down over the world, and every assumption you've ever made feels like a contract written by someone who never intended to honor it.

Which, given my profession, was a familiar feeling.

Three days I'd been running from Vael, and I use "running" generously. Mostly I walked, because running requires a destination and hope, and I had neither. I had a stolen satchel, a secret ledger full of names the temple owned, and a growing conviction that Marguerite Vane—the woman who'd cut my transport loose on the smuggler's road like she was pruning a hedge—had been telling me something true.

*Your blood can unbind the Salt King.*

Or complete its freedom. She'd said that part too, with the cheerful precision of someone delivering a bill. I hadn't stayed for the itemized costs. I'd done what any sensible man does when told he's the last of a bloodline meant to feed an ancient hunger: I'd run into the mountains where nobody could find me, including, ideally, myself.

The trouble with the mountains was that they'd gone wrong.

I noticed it first as silence. Mountain nights are supposed to be loud—wind, owls, the creak and groan of stone settling like an old man in a chair. Instead there was nothing. Not quiet, which is a living thing, but absence, which is not. The kind of silence that makes you check whether your own breathing still works.

Then I came over the shoulder of the ridge above Harrowmere, and I saw the village.

It looked fine. That's what I want to be clear about. From the ridge, in the flat grey light of a starless moon—because apparently we still got the moon, some bureaucratic mercy—Harrowmere looked exactly like a village should. Smoke from chimneys. Lamplight in windows. People in the lanes.

But the smoke rose straight up. No wind anywhere in the valley, not a blade of grass bending, and the smoke climbed like it was being pulled on strings.

And the people were smiling.

I watched them from the rocks for the better part of an hour, because watching is what I do; it's the one skill my life has actually equipped me for. A woman carried water from the well and smiled. A man split wood and smiled between strokes, the axe rising and falling with the regularity of a clock. Two children played a game with a knotted rag, laughing, and their laughter came out even and identical, like copies of copies of copies.

None of them spoke to each other. That was the thing. A village runs on talk—gossip, complaint, argument about whose goat ate whose cabbage. These people moved through their tasks in perfect parallel, each one alone inside their own smiling, and when two of them passed in the lane they didn't look at each other. They looked *through*, the way I look through a contract clause I've copied four hundred times without ever reading.

There was frost on everything. In high summer. It glittered along the fence rails and crusted the well stones white, and where it touched the wood, the wood had gone grey and porous, eaten thin as old bone. Salt. The whole valley wore a skin of salt, and under that skin, everything was being slowly dissolved.

I thought of my mentor, Sera Ilvane, dead in her chair with salt crusted in her eyes, her face frozen in something that might have been a smile if smiles could be crimes.

I sat down hard on a rock and was quietly, thoroughly sick behind it.

Here is what I understood, sitting there with my hands shaking: the wards hadn't just fallen. Wards falling would mean *invasion*. Monsters pouring through gates, fire, screaming—things a person could fight or flee. This was worse. This was the wards' *job* being done by something else. Something patient. The Salt King hadn't broken into the world. It had moved in, hung its pictures, and started rearranging the furniture, and the furniture was people, and the people were grateful.

Because here's the detail I haven't mentioned, the one that took me longest to admit: they looked happy. Genuinely happy. No debts on their faces. No ledgers behind their eyes. Every laborer I'd ever copied a contract for wore a particular expression—I knew it better than my own handwriting, because I'd put it there, stroke by stroke—and these people didn't have it. Whatever had them had taken the weight off.

That's how it buys you, I thought. Not with chains. With relief.

I made myself look away from Harrowmere, and I made myself a rule, standing there in the dark: don't go down. Don't go near the villages. Don't look at the faces. Keep to the high paths, keep moving south toward the capital only if it suited me, and mostly just keep moving, because a man alone in a starless country is already halfway to being no one, and no one, I was beginning to suspect, was the safest thing left to be.

I kept that rule for almost two days.

* * *

The rule broke in a place called Cinderbeck, though I only learned the name later, from a signpost half-eaten to lace by salt.

I'd come down low to find water—a stream, any stream, my throat had gone to parchment—and the stream ran through the edge of the village, and that was my excuse. I wasn't going in. I was just drinking. The fact that the village was there was geography's fault.

Cinderbeck was smaller than Harrowmere and further along. The salt had gotten into the walls themselves; houses sagged like wet paper, and the lanes crunched underfoot even before I stepped on them. And the people—there were fewer of them, maybe thirty, and they'd stopped pretending to work. They stood in the square in loose clusters, smiling up at the empty sky. Waiting, I think. For something to finish arriving.

I kept to the shadow of the wall, drank at the stream with my belly to the mud, and told myself to leave.

Then I heard the crying.

Not the even, copied laughter from Harrowmere. Real crying—ragged, hiccupping, human—the sound of someone who hadn't been gotten to yet. It came from a shed behind the farthest house, and I swear to you I argued with myself. I gave a genuinely persuasive speech about survival, about one child against whatever this was, about how heroes die in the first act precisely because they climb out of the mud to investigate a noise.

Then I climbed out of the mud and investigated the noise.

She was small—six, seven years old—with dark hair full of burrs and a smudge of ash across her cheek, wedged into the gap between a rain barrel and the shed wall. When my hand closed on her arm she fought like a wildcat, all elbows and teeth, until I did the only thing that has ever worked on frightened creatures of any species: I went completely still and waited.

"They're still," she whispered finally. "Everyone's still. My mam went out to see and she came back smiling and she won't stop and she tried to get me to smile too." Her fingers found my sleeve and gripped it with a strength that hurt. "Don't let her make me smile."

"I'm not going to let anyone make you do anything," I said, which was a lie, because everyone in Thornfell was owned by somebody, and I had spent ten years writing down exactly who. But it seemed like the right lie for the moment.

Getting her out was the easy part. Getting out of Cinderbeck was not, because as I lifted her over the wall, the people in the square turned their heads.

All of them. At once. Smoothly, silently, like a field of flowers following the sun—except there was no sun. They turned toward us and smiled wider, and began, without hurry, without breaking formation, to walk.

I ran. I'm not proud of my pace or my form. I ran with a child on my hip through salt-crusted lanes while thirty smiling people converged with the unhurried certainty of tide coming in, and I learned something important in those hundred yards: they weren't fast. They didn't need to be. The salt was everywhere underfoot, and wherever I stepped, it *knew*. The crust reached for my boots like frost reaching up a windowpane, slowing me, tasting me—

—and flinching.

Where my blood dripped—from a scrape on my palm I hadn't even noticed—the salt recoiled. Blackened. Hissed.

I didn't have time to think about that. There was a gap in the wall, and beyond it the slope, and beyond the slope a figure coming *down* the slope toward me with a drawn warding-rod and the expression of a woman who has traveled three days to say *I told you so*.

Marguerite Vane planted herself between me and the villagers, drove the rod into the earth, and spoke a word I felt in my back teeth. Light snapped out in a ring. The smiling people stopped at its edge—not repelled, exactly. *Polite.* They stood at the boundary of her light and smiled in at us, dozens of faces, patient as creditors.

"Up the slope," she said. "Now. Slowly. Don't run—they follow motion."

We went up the slope. Slowly. The child had her face buried in my neck. Marguerite backed after us, rod raised, holding that ring of light around us like a woman carrying water in her hands, and I could see it costing her—sweat cutting lines through the dust on her face, her breath going ragged—but she held it, step by step, all the way to the ridgeline, where the villagers finally stopped as if they'd reached the end of their parish.

They stood there smiling up at us for a long time.

Then, together, they turned and walked home.

* * *

She built a fire small enough to hide in a cupped hand, and we sat around it: me, her, and the girl, who had introduced herself as Pip and had not let go of my sleeve since the wall. I'd stopped trying to detach it. Some contracts you just accept.

"You bled on it," Marguerite said. Not a question. She'd seen the blackened salt below the wall; I'd watched her looking at it.

"Scraped my hand on the stones."

"Show me."

I showed her. The scrape was already closing, faster than it should, the edges pink and clean. Marguerite looked at it the way I look at a ledger entry that doesn't balance—hungry and appalled at once.

"Hartwell blood," she said softly. "It doesn't just resist the salt. It *hates* it."

"Wonderful. Can it hate quietly? From a great distance?"

"Coral." She said my name like setting down something heavy. "That village back there. That's what's coming for everyone. Not armies. *Relief.* It takes the debt out of their faces and puts a smile there instead, and it will take years, decades, but it will get every last—" She stopped. Looked at Pip, asleep now against my side, her grip finally slack. Lowered her voice. "You've seen the ledgers. You know better than anyone what people will trade for the weight to be gone."

"That's why I ran from you."

"I know."

"And I'm still not saying yes. To the rite. To any of it. I'm a scribe, Marguerite. I copy things. I am not chosen for things. Chosen is a word powerful people use when they're about to spend someone."

Something crossed her face at that—guilt, old and worn smooth from handling. "Fair," she said. "I'll give you fair. I'm not asking you to save the king tonight. I'm asking you to survive tomorrow, and I can't do that alone, and you can't either. That ring I held? Ten minutes more and it would have taken me under. You need me, and—" her mouth twisted, wry, reluctant, "—apparently you need someone to carry the child while you trip over salt."

"I didn't trip."

"You absolutely tripped."

Pip stirred against my side and murmured something in her sleep, and both of us went quiet, listening to her breathe, this one small unowned thing in a valley full of smiling hollows.

"Not the mission," I said at last. "I'm not agreeing to your rite, or the capital, or the king, or any of it. Just—you. For now. Because you held the light."

Marguerite nodded slowly, and for the first time since the smuggler's road, she looked tired in an honest way instead of a performed one. "For now," she agreed.

She was reaching for the teapot when she froze, head tilting, listening. Down the valley, faint but carrying in the dead air, came a sound I hadn't heard in three days and had hoped never to hear again: bells. Many bells, ringing in unison, from the direction of the capital.

"The coronation road is opening," Marguerite said. "Every pilgrim in Thornfell will be walking to Aldric's renewal. Thousands of them. And the Salt King wants his Hartwell delivered with the crowds." She looked at me across the tiny fire. "We have three days to decide whether we're walking there—or being walked."
