## 5. Salt in the Blood

The bathhouse at Ferrow's Crossing was the kind of place that charged by the hour and asked no questions, which made it the most honest establishment in Thornfell. Steam, sulfur water, and a proprietor named Doss who had once been a ward-keeper himself before the crown decided his particular talents were better left unlicensed. He took one look at Marguerite Vane walking in with a fugitive scribe and a satchel of contraband chalk, sighed like a man whose bath had gone permanently cold, and gave us the back room.

"Still owe me for the Ashford job," he said.

"You'll be paid in gratitude," I told him. "It appreciates in value."

"It does not," said Doss, and shut the door.

* * *

The back room was all tile and echo, a sunken pool fed by the hot spring beneath the town, and shelves along one wall where Doss kept his old tools: bone styluses, jars of ground quartz, a bundle of ward-chalk gone soft with age. Coral stood at the edge of the pool holding his satchel against his chest like a shield, looking around at the steam and the sigils scored into the tile with the expression of a man who had expected a hideout and found a classroom.

"This is where you teach me?" he said. "Ward-craft? In a bathhouse?"

"Where else?" I rolled up my sleeves. "Salt lives in water. Water remembers what it carries. Every proper ward is just a conversation with water that you've taught to hold its shape." I fished a stylus from the shelf and tossed it to him; he caught it badly, against his knuckles. "You've been copying contracts for how long?"

"Six years."

"Then you already know the hardest part. Precision. A ward-line is a contract too—terms, conditions, penalties for breach. The difference is that when you breach a contract, someone sues you. When you breach a ward, something from outside the world comes through the gap and wears your face like a coat." I stepped down into the pool, boots and all, because dignity was a luxury I'd stopped affording years ago. "Sit. Watch. Then do it wrong, and I'll correct you, and then you'll do it less wrong, and in about four days you'll be adequate."

He sat on the tile edge, legs dangling above the water, and watched me draw the first line with more attention than he'd given anything since I'd dragged him off that smuggler's road. That was the thing about scribes—they learned the way stone learns rain. Slowly, and then all at once, and then they were a different shape entirely.

I drew the old forms. Not the temple's sanctioned geometry, all right angles and obedience, but the older curves, the ones that predated the church of Vael, the ones the first ward-keepers had scratched into cave walls before anyone thought to charge admission to salvation. A circle that wasn't quite closed. A spiral that turned inward instead of out. The seven-fold knot that meant *hold*.

"What's that one called?" Coral asked, when I'd finished the knot.

"It doesn't have a name anymore. The temple burned the naming-books. We call it the hold-knot now, which is like calling a wolf 'the dog-shaped problem.'" I nodded at the stylus. "Your turn. Copy it."

He did. It was terrible. The second line wavered where his hand remembered it was afraid, and the fourth angle was two degrees shy of true, and any temple examiner would have failed him on the spot. But the shape of it—the intention underneath—was sound. More than sound. When he lifted the stylus away, the lines held a faint shimmer, like heat over a road, and the water in the pool went briefly, perfectly still.

I hadn't managed that until my third year.

"Well," I said, and heard something in my own voice I didn't love. "You're not hopeless."

"I'm a copyist," he said, staring at the shimmer. "Copying is the whole of my talent."

"Coral Hartwell, if copying were the whole of your talent, that line would have faded by now. Lines fade when the drawer doesn't believe them. Yours believes itself." I climbed out of the pool and wrung out my sleeves. "Which brings us to the part you're going to hate. Hold out your hand."

* * *

Here is what I knew about blood-tests, and why I'd been putting this off for two days of hard travel: they are not subtle. There is no gentle diagnostic, no discreet inquiry. You take salt—clean salt, blessed salt, salt that has never touched a debt-contract—and you introduce it to the blood, and the blood tells you what it is. The temple used to run these tests on every child born within a day's ride of the old seal-sites. They stopped running them about ninety years ago, officially because the practice was barbaric, and unofficially because they'd found what they were looking for and buried it.

I took the jar from my pack. Fine white salt, ground smooth as flour. Coral watched me open it with the wariness of a man who had learned, over six years of copying debt contracts, exactly what small white granules cost people.

"What is that for?"

"A test. For your bloodline." I kept my voice level, clerk-flat, the tone I'd used for delivering verdicts back when I still delivered them. "Hartwell isn't just a name. It's an office. Your family were the original seal-keepers—the ones who bound the Salt King under the mountain, before there was a temple, before there was a crown. The binding runs in the blood. Which means the unbinding might run there too."

"And if it does?"

"Then we'll know," I said, which was true, and also a lie by omission so large it could have rented rooms.

He held out his hand. Palm up. Steady. Scribes' hands are steady; it's the first thing the work teaches you, because a trembling line invalidates a contract and gets people sold. He watched me pour a measure of salt into my palm, and he didn't flinch, and I hated him a little for making it easy, because nothing about it was easy.

"Marguerite," he said, as I raised my hand over his. "What happens if it burns?"

"Then we'll know," I said again, and tipped the salt into his palm.

For a heartbeat, nothing. The grains lay against his skin like snow on a road, ordinary, inert. I let out a breath I'd been holding since the smuggler's road—

And then the salt caught fire.

Not flame. Worse. The grains glowed, white-hot, and sank into his skin like sparks into snow, and Coral made a sound low in his throat—not a scream, he was too stubborn for a scream—and snatched his hand back, and where the salt had been there was a mark: a perfect circle, seared pale into his palm, ringed by a hair-fine spiral. The hold-knot. Branded into him from the inside, as if his own blood had recognized the design and signed for it.

The pool water shivered. Every jar on Doss's shelves rattled once, softly, like teeth.

Coral stared at his hand. I stared at Coral. Somewhere beyond the door, Doss was pretending very hard not to exist.

"So," Coral said finally, in the voice of a man reading his own foreclosure notice. "That's a yes."

"That's a yes." I crouched beside him and took his wrist, turned the mark toward the lamplight. The skin around it was unbroken. The burn had gone somewhere else—downward, inward, into whatever current ran under his blood. "Ninety years ago the temple tested every child in the borderlands and found one family whose blood answered the salt. They didn't celebrate. They erased them. Marriage records falsified, deaths invented, the whole lineage filed under extinct. Because a living Hartwell isn't just a key to the binding, Coral. A living Hartwell is the binding. And keys get used."

He pulled his hand back slowly, cradling it. "Used how?"

I should have lied. I want that on the record, wherever records go after they burn: I considered lying, and the lie was right there, warm and ready, the way lies always are when you need them. But I'd spent three days watching this man flinch every time I mentioned his name, watching him treat his own bloodline like a debt collection notice, and I was tired—tired in the marrow, tired in the part of me that used to believe competence could outrun consequence.

So I told him the truth instead, and the truth was mine to give.

"The unbinding rite requires a Hartwell," I said. "Blood, freely given, into the seal itself. That's how the first binding was sealed—a Hartwell gave themselves, willingly, and the Salt King went under the mountain. To renew it, or to break it, the price is the same. A life. Given, not taken." I met his eyes. "Given willingly. That's the term. That's always been the term."

The silence in the back room had texture. Steam, sulfur, the tick of cooling tile.

"No," Coral said.

"Coral—"

"No." He stood up, and the mark on his palm caught the light, and he looked at it the way a man looks at a brand he hasn't agreed to wear. "You brought me here to teach me wards. That's what you said on the road. Teach me enough to survive, enough to—" He stopped. Recalculated. I watched him do the arithmetic, because of course he did; he was a ledger-keeper, arithmetic was his native tongue. "You've known since the archive. Since before the road. You didn't intercept my transport to save me. You intercepted it to deliver me."

"Yes," I said. There was no version of denial that survived contact with his eyes. "Both things can be true. I saved you and I spent you. I've done worse—I'll tell you the worst of it, since we're being honest tonight."

He didn't sit back down. Good. I hadn't earned his sitting.

"Eight years ago," I said, "the crown came to me. I was young, I was brilliant, I was broke, and I had a record—one arrest, one pardon pending, the whole machinery of the law resting on my neck like a boot. They offered me a trade. Design the new border wards, the ones feeding the renewal rite, and walk free. Full pardon. Clean name. Marguerite Vane, restored." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "They didn't tell me what the wards would eat. Or maybe they did tell me, in the way institutions tell you things—in a footnote, in a clause, in language designed to be skimmed. Corrupted salt. Mined salt. Debt-salt. Blood and sweat worked into the crystals before they ever reach the mountain. I built the finest feeding-system in Thornfell's history and wired it to a slave mine, and I signed the drawings, and I walked away clean."

Coral had gone very still. It was the stillness of his ledgers, I realized. The stillness of a man recording.

"Every laborer in those mines," I said, "every debt-bound worker who ever swung a pick at the salt seams—what happened to them happened through my designs. And when the wards failed last month, when the stars went out, the corruption that came through the gap came through architecture I drew. So no, Coral, I didn't bring you here to sacrifice you. I brought you here because you're the only person alive who can fix what I broke, and because I would rather carry the truth of that into the capital than carry another mile of not saying it." I spread my hands. "There. Guilt for guilt. You wanted to be nobody. I wanted to be forgiven. Neither of us got it. Now stop running and start building, because the coronation is in eleven days, and the king is wearing something that used to be a man, and I am done being complicit by silence."

* * *

He didn't answer right away. He walked to the pool's edge and looked down at the water, at the faint reflection of the mark on his palm, and I let him look, because some decisions have to be made alone even in a room with two people in it.

"My ledger," he said at last. "The one they arrested me for. Everyone the temple owns. Four hundred and twelve names."

"What about it?"

"If we do this—if I do this—it can't be for the king, or the crown, or the stars. Stars don't read ledgers." He turned around, and something had settled in his face, the way sediment settles in water that's finally stopped being shaken. "It'll be for the names. All of them. Mine included, if it comes to that. Nobody gets erased while I'm holding the pen."

I believed him. Gods help me, I believed him completely, and belief is the most dangerous material a ward-keeper ever handles.

"Then we leave for the capital tomorrow," I said. "Tonight, you sleep. Tomorrow, you learn the hold-knot properly, because in eleven days you're going to need it."

He nodded, and turned toward the cot in the corner—and that was when the door opened without a knock, and Doss stood in it, gray-faced, holding a scrap of paper as if it had bitten him.

"Proclamation came in with the evening riders," he said. "New edict from the capital. Ward-craft is outlawed. Practitioners are traitors." His eyes flicked to me, then to Coral, then to the chalk dust still on the tiles. "And there's a bounty posted. Two hundred crowns for a disgraced keeper named Vane." He swallowed. "And five hundred for her companion. They're calling him the Last Hartwell."

"They know," I said quietly. "They've known since the archive. This whole road—we haven't been running ahead of them. We've been walking their corridor."

Outside, far off across the starless dark, something began to sing.
