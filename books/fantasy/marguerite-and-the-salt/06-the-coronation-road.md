## 6. The Coronation Road

The caravan master's name was Doss, and he had the eyes of a man who had stopped counting his sins around the same time he started counting his coin. He charged us four silver each to walk with the pilgrims to Coronation Road's end, and he did not ask our names, which in Thornfell these days was less a courtesy than a survival trait.

"Keep your heads down at the checkpoints," he told us, spitting into the dust. "The king's men aren't looking for faces anymore. They're looking for *marks*. Salt-scars. Ward-sigils inked on skin. You so much as sneeze in cipher and they'll put you on a cart."

Marguerite, beside me in the guise of a widow named Anselma, murmured her thanks and looked appropriately bereaved. She was very good at looking bereaved. I suspected she'd had practice.

I was Brother Tam of some minor monastic house that had burned down, conveniently, before anyone could check. A scribe's hands are hard to disguise — ink in the knuckle-creases, callus on the middle finger like a second thumb — but I'd rubbed ash into them and let Doss assume I was a lay brother fleeing a fire rather than a debt-scribe fleeing an arrest warrant. It is remarkable how much of identity is just what people can't be bothered to verify.

So we joined three hundred pilgrims walking east under a sky with no stars in it, toward a capital where a king who was not entirely a king would renew a crown that was not entirely a crown, and I tried very hard to feel like a man taking sensible precautions rather than a man walking into the mouth of the thing that wanted him.

I was mostly successful during daylight. Nights were harder. Without stars, the dark had no ceiling. It just went up and up, indifferent as a ledger with no last page, and I found myself doing sums to fall asleep — how many days' rations, how many miles, how many checkpoints between us and the palace. Numbers have always been my prayers.

* * *

The first checkpoint was nothing: bored soldiers, a glance at Doss's caravan seal, a wave through. The second was worse only because it rained, and rain makes everyone crueler.

It was the third one I will not manage to forget, though God knows I've tried, because forgetting is what I was built for. Erasure was practically my trade.

The checkpoint stood where the road narrowed through a cut in the hills, timber barricades and a gallows frame that had been erected recently enough that the wood was still pale. A sergeant of the king's new order — white salt-crowns stitched on grey tabards, the sigil I'd seen on edicts nailed to every temple door since the wards fell — walked the pilgrim line with a list.

"Ward-touched," he kept saying, to no one and everyone. "Ward-keepers are traitors to the crown. Harborers of ward-keepers are traitors to the crown. The king in his mercy offers confession and cleansing." His voice had the flat recitation of a man reading a contract aloud, which is perhaps why it snagged something in me. I know a read-aloud voice. I've been one for eleven years.

They pulled a girl from the line four families ahead of us.

She couldn't have been more than ten. Her mother made a sound I have no word for and was held back by two soldiers who did not look at her, because looking is the first step toward disobeying, and these men had clearly trained themselves out of it. The girl wasn't crying. That was the worst part, or one of the worst parts. She held her hands out — palms up, fingers spread — the way ward-keepers do when they're showing their sigils, except hers weren't inked. They were *born* in her, faint silver traceries across her palms like frost ferns, the mark of a child touched by the old craft before anyone decided the old craft was treason.

"She's never done anything," her mother was saying. "She's never kept a ward in her life. She draws them in the dirt, that's all, she draws them in the dirt because they're pretty—"

The sergeant consulted his list as if her name might be on it and her guilt thereby confirmed. Then he looked at the girl's palms, and for one moment — I want to be honest about this, because honesty is the whole point of what follows — for one moment something moved behind his face. Doubt, maybe. Or its coward cousin, discomfort.

Then he read the edict anyway.

I want to say I surged forward. I want to tell you that Marguerite and I fought our way through three hundred pilgrims and a dozen soldiers and died gloriously saving a child, and that this chapter ends differently than it does. But here is what actually happened: I stood in a crowd of three hundred people, every one of us suddenly fascinated by our own boots, and I watched soldiers hang a ten-year-old girl from a gallows frame while her mother screamed herself hoarse, and I did nothing.

Except.

Here is the thing about being made of paper and ink. When there is nothing else you can do, you do the only thing you know. My hand was in my coat before I understood I'd moved it. I had a stub of pencil and the back of a ration chit, and while the sergeant read the charges — *ward-touched, unregistered, enemy of the renewal* — I wrote them down. Her name, when her mother gasped it: *Lisset*. Her age. The date. The sergeant's name, which I read off his tabard with the cold thoroughness of a man copying a debtor's signature. The number of soldiers. The color of the rope. The fact that the wood of the gallows was still pale, still new, still wet enough to take a nail without splitting.

Three hundred people looked away. I wrote it down.

It is not much. I know it is not much. A dead girl does not rise because a stranger noted the date. But somewhere in the middle of writing — somewhere between *Lisset, aged 10* and *hanged at the third mile-stone past Harrow Cut* — something shifted in me with an almost audible click, like a lock turning, like a ledger opening to a fresh page.

All my life I had kept records to hide people *from* power. The temple's debts, the smugglers' contracts, the quiet arithmetic of who owned whom — I'd copied it all faithfully, and then, in secret, I'd copied it again into my own forbidden book, because someone ought to know, even if knowing changed nothing. I thought that was the shape of resistance: a hidden ledger, a hidden self, a small man keeping small accounts in the margins of other people's cruelty.

But power had stopped hiding what it did. It was nailing edicts to doors. It was hanging children by the roadside in broad daylight, in front of witnesses, *daring* someone to write it down. And I realized, standing in that crowd with graphite dust on my fingers and Lisset's name already smudging, that I had spent eleven years recording the wrong crimes. Not the hidden ones. The loud ones. The ones committed in full view by men whose names were stitched on their chests, certain that no one would ever keep count.

Someone should keep count.

That is not heroism. I want to be clear about that, because I suspect that later — if there is a later — people will want to make it heroic, and it wasn't. It was bookkeeping. It was the flat, stubborn, unglamorous conviction that a thing written down is a thing that happened, and a thing that happened cannot be wholly erased, and a regime that hangs a child has purchased itself a permanent entry in a book it does not control. The Salt King eats names. Very well. Then names are ammunition, and I am, as it happens, a munitions factory with bad posture.

By the time the caravan rolled on, I had filled both sides of the ration chit and started on the margin of Marguerite's map. She watched me write for a long time without saying anything. When I finally looked up, her expression was strange — not pity, exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a woman watching someone else arrive late at a place she's lived for years.

"You could have looked away," she said quietly. "Everyone else did."

"I've spent my whole life looking away," I said. "At ledgers. At contracts. At the part of the page where the interest compounds and the laborer signs himself into a grave. I'm very good at it. It nearly got me killed twice this month." I smoothed the chit flat against my knee. "I think I'm finished."

"Finished with what?"

"With pretending the record doesn't matter. With being nobody on purpose." I laughed, and it came out wrong, too close to the edge of something. "Do you know what's funny? The Salt King wants me for my blood. The temple wants me for my ledger. The crown would want me for my silence, if it knew I had any left to sell. Everyone keeps trying to collect on me, and I keep trying to be worthless stock." I tapped the chit. "I'm done depreciating myself. From here to the capital, I'm keeping a different kind of book."

Marguerite studied me for a long moment. Then she reached into her pack and handed me something I hadn't seen since Vael: a proper quill, goose-feathered, well-cut, and a slim bound notebook with a cracked spine that had clearly survived more journeys than most men do.

"I stole it from the archive," she said. "Along with everything else. It seemed wasteful to leave good paper behind." A pause. "Fill it."

I filled it.

* * *

We passed two more checkpoints in the following days. At the fourth, they turned back a family whose grandmother had a salt-scar on her wrist from some old mine work, and I recorded it: names, ages, the sergeant's face, the direction they were sent walking — south, toward the salt flats, where nobody comes back from smiling. At the fifth, they posted a new edict on the toll-house wall, and I copied it verbatim, every clause, including the one that declared all records of the old wards contraband and their destruction a sacrament.

They are burning the archives. Of course they are. The first act of every new ownership is destroying the previous owner's books.

On the sixth night, camped in a fold of hills with the starless dark pressing down like a lid, Marguerite sat beside my fire and said, without preamble: "There's a gate in the capital's east wall that the pilgrims won't use. Ward-door. Sealed since before Aldric's grandfather. I can open it, but it'll cost us the caravan cover, and once we're inside the palace district there's no crowd to hide in."

"And the coronation?"

"Four days." She fed a stick to the fire. "Coral. Whatever you're writing in that book — when we go through that door, understand what we're walking into. The thing wearing the king knows you're coming. I've felt it pulling at the wards since Harrow Cut, like a fisherman testing a line. Every mile closer, the pull gets stronger." She looked at me across the flames, and her face was older than it had been at the start of the road. "If it's cultivated you — if this whole road has been its hook — then the moment we enter the palace, we're not infiltrating. We're arriving. On schedule. As invited."

I looked down at the notebook in my lap. Lisset's name was on the first page. Behind it, forty pages of the regime's arithmetic: the taken, the hanged, the marched south, the edicts, the burnings. A record of everything the powerful hoped no one was counting.

"Then let's not keep it waiting," I said, and turned the page.

Far off to the east, past the hills, past the dark, the capital's watch-fires burned in a ring — and I would swear, then and after, that the fires flickered in unison, once, like something exhaling.
