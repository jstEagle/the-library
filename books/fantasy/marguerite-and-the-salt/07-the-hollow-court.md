## 7. The Hollow Court

There are three ways into a palace: the front gate, the servant's stair, and the lie. We took the lie, because it was the only one that didn't require a bribe I couldn't pay.

Marguerite Vane, disgraced ward-keeper, returned to the capital in the grey robes of a salt-tithe auditor — a profession so dull that even the guards at the Gate of Bells waved us through with their eyes half-closed. Coral walked two paces behind me, hood up, hands folded like a man carrying his own funeral urn. He'd been practicing looking invisible for six days. He was getting better at it. It broke my heart a little every time he succeeded.

"Stop fidgeting," I murmured as we crossed the outer court.

"I'm not fidgeting."

"You're counting things."

"I'm *inventorying*," he said, with the particular dignity of a scribe caught doing the one thing he knows how to do. "It calms me. Fourteen guards on the wall, three braziers unlit, one fountain full of brine instead of water."

That last one stopped me. I looked. The Fountain of the Nine Maidens — I'd helped inscribe its purification wards when I was twenty-two and still believed the crown deserved my best work — ran thick and white-rimmed, crusted halfway up the maidens' throats. Salt doesn't belong in moving water. Moving water is the one thing it can't sit still inside.

"It's spreading," I said.

"You think I don't know that?" Coral's voice had an edge I hadn't heard before. Six days of smuggler roads and hollow villages will do that to a man. "Every well we passed since the vale. Every stream. Marguerite, the rain tasted wrong last night."

I hadn't told him I'd tasted it too. Small mercies; we were saving our confessions for after the world ended, if there was an after.

* * *

The palace itself was worse than the fountain. Palaces are supposed to breathe — kitchens and laundry-steam and the noise of a hundred small corruptions that keep a throne honest. This one held its breath. Servants moved along the walls like fish in a tank, silent, eyes down, and when one of them brushed past me in the east corridor I felt her flinch away from my hand as though ward-craft were contagious.

Which, I suppose, under the new edicts, it was. Ward-keepers: enemies of the crown. Traitors to the renewal. I'd read my own name on the proclamation board at the last checkpoint, printed in the king's own hand — or the hand that wore the king. *Marguerite Vane, architect of the failing wards.* They'd gotten that part right, at least. I had built them. I had built them badly, on purpose, to buy my pardon, and now they were failing on nobody's purpose at all, and the whole kingdom was paying out my debt in salt.

Coral touched my elbow. "You've gone somewhere."

"I'm here." I checked the corridor. Empty. "The king's private chapel is through the next arch. Aldric keeps — kept — a hour of prayer before dawn court. If any part of him is still in there, that's where it will be."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then we run, and you finally get your wish of being nobody, somewhere far from me."

He didn't laugh. Neither did I. We went through the arch.

* * *

The chapel smelled of old cedar and new salt.

King Aldric sat alone before the altar, and for one absurd moment he looked exactly like the coins — broad shoulders, silver-streaked beard, the heavy jaw they'd been stamping on currency for thirty years. Then he turned, and I saw what thirty years of coinage had never shown anyone: a man looking out of his own face through bars.

"Coral Hartwell," he said. His voice was gravel dragged uphill. "And Marguerite Vane. Two ghosts. Good. Ghosts are the only ones left who'll speak plainly to me."

"Your Majesty—" Coral started.

"Don't." Aldric raised a hand, and I saw the tremor in it, saw the fine white crust along the knuckles like frost that wouldn't melt. "Don't kneel, don't call me Majesty. I have perhaps an hour. Maybe less. Some mornings the thing that wears me sleeps late, and some mornings it watches through my eyes while I scream behind them, and I have learned to tell the difference by the taste in my mouth. Today it tastes of cedar. Today I am, for one stolen hour, myself." He laughed, and it was the worst sound I'd heard in weeks, and I had recently heard a village full of smiling people hum in unison. "So let us be quick and plain. You came to save me."

"We came to end it," I said.

"Same thing." He stood, steadying himself on the altar rail. "Listen to me, both of you, because I don't know how many hours like this I have left. Whatever rite you're planning — do it. Don't weigh it. Don't look for another way. I have looked. There isn't one." His eyes found mine, and they were wet, and furious, and entirely human. "I would rather die a king than live as a glove."

"The rite may not kill you," I said, because lying to kings is a habit even disgraced ward-keepers can't fully break.

"Then you're a worse liar than the priestess, and she's very good." He came down the altar steps slowly, one hand trailing the rail like a man walking a ship's deck in a storm. "She comes to me at night. The high priestess. She sits where you're standing and tells me how honored I should feel. She says the Salt King chose me because I was strong enough to hold it. She says when the renewal is sealed at coronation, I won't feel anything ever again, and do you know—" his voice cracked, and he mastered it, the way kings are taught to master everything except themselves— "do you know the truly obscene part? Some nights, I'm tired enough to want that. To want the quiet. That's how it wins, Vane. Not with hunger. With *rest*."

Coral spoke quietly. "Why us? Why now? If the binding is old, if it's held for centuries—"

"Because it wasn't holding. It was *feeding*." Aldric's mouth twisted. "The wards, the tithe-salt, the mines, the debt-bonds — you thought all that was greed? Greed is simple. This was agriculture. The temple has been farming something for three hundred years, and this year the harvest came due, and the thing in the ground reached up and took the reaper first." He looked at Coral then, long and strange. "Hartwell blood. I read the archive before it read me. Your family built the original seal. Did the woman tell you what that means?"

"She told me it means I can unbind it," Coral said carefully.

Aldric smiled, and it was kind, and it was terrible. "Everything true is also a trap, boy. Ask her. She knows about traps. She built one or two herself."

* * *

I felt her before I heard her — every ward-keeper knows the pressure-change of a working this large, the way the air goes taut as a drumhead — and by the time the chapel doors opened without a sound, I already had Coral's wrist in my grip and my other hand full of a ward-frame I couldn't finish, because finishing it would brand us both as traitors in front of forty witnesses, and not finishing it meant standing empty-handed before the high priestess of the Salt King.

She came in alone. That was the worst of it. No guards, no acolytes. She didn't need them. She wore temple white gone grey at the hems, and her smile was the smile of a woman who has already read the last page of your letter and is being very patient about letting you finish writing it.

"Lady Vane," she said warmly. "And the last Hartwell. You've brought him right to us. How thoughtful. We've been *cultivating* him for years."

Coral went rigid under my hand. "What does that mean?"

"It means, child, that nothing about you has been accident." She walked toward us unhurried, and the salt-crusted fountain outside seemed to whisper through the walls. "Who do you think arranged the debts that bound a Hartwell orphan to the mountain temple? Who do you think kept the ledger-masters ignorant of your bloodline, so you'd grow up believing yourself nobody? Who do you think let Vane discover the archive trail — let her *think* she was clever — knowing guilt makes the best leash?" Her eyes flicked to me, and I felt three decades of my life rearrange themselves around that look like iron filings around a lodestone. "You didn't rescue him, Lady Vane. You *delivered* him. Willingly. Freely. Just as required."

"The rite requires willingness," I said. My voice came out flat. I was proud of that. "It always has. The seal was a free gift. So is the unbinding."

"Yes." She beamed. "And there is the beauty of it. We could not take him. Taking voids the rite — the old Hartwells wrote that law themselves, cunning people, generous people. He must *choose*. He must stand at the renewal and give himself to the salt with an open heart, or the binding holds and our king stays... crowded." She spread her hands. "So we made a life for him with no door out of it but ours. Debt. Obscurity. A hidden ledger and a death sentence waiting on it. And then fear, and a guide, and a road, and a dying king begging sweetly for mercy — every step of it arranged so that by the time he understood the choice, refusing would feel like murder and accepting would feel like sacrifice. Either way, he walks to the altar. The only question is which story he tells himself on the way."

The silence afterward had weight. I could hear Coral breathing beside me, shallow and fast, and I could hear Aldric saying, low and broken, "*No. No, I warned them, I tried—*" and I understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that even the king's lucid hour might be part of the design — mercy as bait, honesty as hook.

Coral pulled his wrist free of my grip.

"Coral," I said. "Don't listen—"

"No," he said. "No, I'm going to talk, because everyone in this room keeps deciding things for me and I'm apparently the only person in Thornfell who hasn't." He faced the priestess, and his voice shook, and he didn't stop. "You want willingness. Fine. Here's mine: I refuse. I refuse your rite, your road, your little story about doors. You spent years building a maze around one man? Then you should have checked whether the man was a *scribe*." He smiled, and it was the first real smile I'd seen on him since the stars went out. "Because I keep records, priestess. Everything you just confessed — every arrangement, every debt, every leash — I remember all of it, word for word, and I write it down twice. You've just given testimony in front of a king and a ward-keeper, and neither of us forgets."

Something crossed the priestess's face then. Not anger. Something older and more careful — the look of a farmer watching weather turn against the forecast.

"Oh, child," she said softly. "You think refusal costs nothing. The renewal is in four days. The wards fail a little more each night. Every village between here and the vale grows quieter, and kinder, and emptier. Refuse, and all of it simply... continues." She turned to go, robes whispering against the salt-dusted stone. "You were always going to choose, Hartwell. We merely made sure that whichever way you chose, you'd believe it was yours."

The doors closed behind her. The pressure lifted. In the ringing quiet, King Aldric sank back onto the altar steps, an old man again, and said:

"Now you understand why I begged you not to weigh it."

Coral stood very still. Then he reached into his coat and drew out his forbidden ledger — the little book of owned names he'd risked his neck over — and held it in both hands like a man testing the weight of a stone he intended to throw.

"Four days," he said.

Neither of us answered. Outside, faintly, through the chapel walls, we could hear the fountain running, and beneath the water, if you listened the way ward-keepers learn to listen, something patient and vast was humming — and the song it hummed, I realized with a cold that started in my spine and finished in my soul, was a lullaby. The same one Coral had been murmuring in his sleep for the past three nights.

It had been teaching him the words.
