## 8. Everything the Stars Owed

They gave me a cell with a window, which was the first insult. A cell without a window says: you are nothing. A cell *with* a window says: we want you to see what you're giving up. The Salt King's people had learned persuasion in a seminary somewhere, and it showed.

The window looked out over the capital's salt-yards, where the white heaps glowed under a sky that still had no stars in it. Nine days now. I counted the way I count everything—compulsively, in columns. Nine days of dark, and every night the darkness seemed less like an absence and more like a held breath.

The second insult came at dusk, carried on a silver tray by a priestess whose smile had too many teeth in it, like a ledger with pages added after the audit.

"Coral Hartwell," she said, setting down wine I would not drink and bread I probably should have. "Or should I say, Coral *Hartwell*, restored to your name, your debts dissolved, your freedom guaranteed by royal decree." She laid a parchment beside the tray. It bore the king's seal—the real one, wax the color of dried blood. "All you must do is decline."

"Decline what?"

"You know what. Tomorrow, at the renewal, when they call for the Hartwell blood, you simply say no. Walk away. The rite fails, the wards stay fallen, and His Eternal Majesty continues his gracious rule until the sun itself gets thirsty." She said *His Eternal Majesty* the way my temple mentors used to say *the gods willing*—automatically, with the soul entirely elsewhere. "You'd be the richest scribe in Thornfell by morning. We might even give you back your little book."

That landed harder than it should have. My ledger. The forbidden one, with every name the temple of Vael owned, copied in my own cramped hand over eleven years. They'd taken it off me at the checkpoint along with my pens, and I had been mourning it like a limb.

"They've read it," I said.

"His Majesty found it *illuminating*."

I thought about that. Somewhere above us, wearing a dead-eyed king like a coat, an ancient hunger had sat down and read my accounting of eleven thousand debt-bonded souls—and its response was to offer me a salary.

"I'll consider it," I said, because considering things is free, and she left satisfied, which told me everything about how well she understood scribes. We are the only profession that can be bought with stationery.

* * *

Marguerite came at moonrise, which is to say she came at nothing, because there was no moon either. She came through the wall, or what I mistook for a wall until her hand closed around my wrist from inside a shadow that smelled of chalk and old rain.

"Don't shout," she whispered.

"I never shout. Shouting is for people who haven't learned to write things down."

She pressed something into my palm: a stylus. Bone-handled, worn smooth, hers. Then a bundle of paper—rough, smuggled, worth more than the wine upstairs. Then her hand again, gripping hard enough to leave marks.

"There's a gap in the ward-lattice below the east stair. I held it open getting in; I can hold it getting out. Twenty heartbeats, no more. We run tonight, we reach the coast, we find a ship, we—" Her voice did the thing it only did when she talked about running, which was fray at the edges like cheap thread. "Coral. You don't owe them your death. You don't owe anyone anything. That's the whole point of what you wrote. Nobody owes their life as payment."

"That's not what the ledger says."

"The ledger says what *they* took. Not what you owe."

"It says both, actually. Page four hundred and twelve. Debts run both directions; that's what makes them debts and not thefts." I was stalling and she knew it. I turned the stylus over in my fingers. "Marguerite. If I run, tomorrow they do the renewal anyway. Corrupted salt, failed binding, whatever's left of Aldric gets eaten to the rind, and the thing wearing him starts looking for the next vessel with a longer shelf life. And everyone in my ledger stays exactly where they are. Eleven thousand names, all still owned."

"And if you stay?"

"Then someone finally pays what's owed, and the account closes clean."

"That's not bookkeeping, that's martyrdom with good penmanship."

I laughed. I hadn't expected to; it came out cracked, like a hinge unused for years. "Everything is bookkeeping. That's the secret nobody tells you. Empires, temples, marriages—it's all just entries and erasures. The powerful win by controlling the record. By deciding which names get written and which get blotted out." I looked at the window, at the starless dark. "You know what they offered me? Freedom and my debts erased. Do you understand what that means, coming from them? It means they think a person can be settled. Paid off. Closed like an account." I shook my head. "My mother was sold for three crowns and a mule. There's a line in the Vael registry saying so, in some clerk's hand, and that clerk probably went home to supper feeling efficient. When I'm gone, the only record that I was ever owed anything will be the one I make myself. So I'm going to make it."

She was quiet for a long time. In the dark her face was mostly eyes and fury.

"I helped build those failing wards," she said finally. "I've told you. I traded my craft for my pardon and I've been running from it ever since, and running has accomplished precisely nothing except keeping me alive to feel worse." Her grip tightened. "So here is my counteroffer, scribe. You write your ledger. I hold the door. And when the moment comes tomorrow—if it comes—you remember that the door was open the whole time. That you *chose*. Because the salt wants you to believe you were always going to end up in its mouth. It's been cultivating you since before you could write. Don't let it have the ending written in advance."

"That's rather a lot of metaphor before dawn."

"I'm a ward-keeper. Metaphor is load-bearing."

* * *

I worked through the night.

Not the way poets work through nights—all sighing and candlelight. The way scribes do: back straight, wrist loose, ink refreshed at intervals so the line weight stays honest. They had left me my cell and my window and, after the priestess's visit, a guard who had apparently been instructed to be kind to me, which he achieved mainly by falling asleep.

The paper Marguerite brought wouldn't hold eleven years of records. So I didn't copy the records. I distilled them.

Every name. That was the discipline—to resist the archivist's itch, the urge to annotate, contextualize, justify. Just the names, in two columns: *owned by the temple*, and *owned by the crown*. Eleven thousand, three hundred and six souls, and I knew them the way you know a psalm, because I had written each entry originally, and a scribe's memory is the one vault no auditor can breach.

Tamsin Vale, sold at nine for a weaving debt her grandmother incurred. Odo Merrick, blinded in the salt mines of Vael, still carried on the rolls as *productive*. The whole Hartwell line, my own family, entered generation by generation until the final entry: *line presumed extinct*—written, I noted with professional disgust, in the same year I was born. Somebody had blotted us out while I was learning to walk. Sloppy. Erasure should at least be thorough.

Somewhere past the middle hours my hand began to shake, and I let it, because the shaking was honest too. These weren't abstractions. Tamsin Vale had once stolen me half an apple in the temple yard and split it with the solemnity of a treaty. Odo Merrick had taught me which stones sang hollow. I was not writing a list. I was writing the only eulogy any of them would get, and I meant it to be legible.

Near dawn I added the last column, the one I had promised myself I wouldn't add because it felt like vanity, and then added anyway, because vanity is just honesty that makes you flinch. At the bottom of the final page, beneath eleven thousand names, I wrote:

*Coral Hartwell, scribe, son of Lira Hartwell, sold for three crowns and a mule. Owned by no one. Owed: everything.*

Then I signed it. Properly. With the flourish they teach you for documents that matter.

* * *

Marguerite returned as the sky went the grey of old dishwater. She took the finished ledger, wrapped it in oilcloth against her ribs, and stood there holding it like it might detonate.

"Freed prisoners carry it out," she said. "After. Whatever happens after. The ones in the palace cells, the ones the unbinding releases—they'll scatter, and each of them takes a page. You can't burn a book that's become eleven thousand separate fires." She swallowed. "It's good, Coral. It's the best thing I've ever seen anyone do, and I once watched a woman ward a plague-town with her bare hands."

"It's adequate. The margins are cramped."

"Coral."

"Don't. If you say something kind right now I'll start thinking about my mother, and then I'll be useless, and I need to be useful for approximately six more hours." I straightened my cuffs, which were ruined, and my spine, which was not. "Is the door still open? Your twenty heartbeats?"

"It's open. It'll be open during the ceremony too, right up until it isn't. I need you to promise me you'll look at it. Really look."

"I promise to look at doors. It's one of my hobbies."

She laughed wetly and did the thing I'd never seen her do: she touched my face, briefly, like checking a temperature. "For what it's worth, whoever blotted your line out of the record was wrong. Extinct. Ha. The temple couldn't even count correctly."

"No," I agreed. "But I can."

Bells began then—deep, wrong bells, bells that sounded like they were being rung underwater, and the light through my window turned faintly white, the color of the salt-yards waking up. Down in the streets, the procession was forming. Somewhere a king who was not a king was being dressed in robes for his own renewal, and an ancient hunger was smoothing its borrowed face in a mirror, expecting me.

Marguerite slipped toward the shadow in the wall, the ledger safe against her heart, and paused there, half-dissolved into dark.

"When it offers you eternity," she said quietly, "and it will offer—it offered me something once, in the archive, in the dark—I want to know what you'll answer with."

I picked up her stylus, weighed it in my hand, and gave her the truest sentence I owned.

"Eleven thousand names," I said. "And mine at the bottom, freely given. Let's see the salt try to erase *that*."

The bells rang louder. The door of my cell unlocked itself, politely, from the outside—and the priestess with too many teeth stood in the opening, smiling, and said, "His Majesty will see you now."
