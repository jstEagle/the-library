## 9. The Unbinding

*Coral*

There is a particular kind of silence in a room where ten thousand people are waiting for you to die.

I know this now the way I know the weight of an inkwell or the smell of old vellum—intimately, professionally, in the way of a man who has spent his whole life taking inventory of things he'd rather not own.

The Hall of Renewal had been built to impress, and it did. Vaulted ceiling painted with constellations that hadn't existed in the sky for three months. Pillars of white marble veined with grey, like salt frozen mid-weeping. Below us, the court of Thornfell stood in rows, dressed in their grief-colored finery, watching the dais where King Aldric sat on the Salt Throne with his hands folded and his eyes fixed on nothing at all.

He was lucid when they brought me in. That was the worst part. I'd expected hollow, smiling, gone. Instead his gaze found mine across forty feet of marble, and it was a man's gaze, tired and terrified, and his lips moved around words too quiet to carry: *I'm sorry. I'm so sorry.*

"Kneel," said the high priestess.

Her name was Seraphine Vole, though she'd stopped being Seraphine Vole somewhere around the time her eyes went the color of skimmed milk. She wore white robes crusted at the hem with crystals, and when she smiled—and she smiled often, generously, the way a fisherman smiles at a worm—I could see salt glittering between her teeth.

I knelt. My knees found the stone with more grace than I expected, given that my hands were shaking hard enough to rattle my own bones.

Marguerite was somewhere in the crowd. I couldn't look for her without looking for her, if that makes sense, and looking for her would get us both killed before the rite even started. So I stared at the floor instead, at the great seal of Thornfell inlaid in silver beneath the throne, and I thought about bookkeeping.

That's what Marguerite had told me, two nights ago, through the bars of a cell that smelled of brine: *You're good at one thing, Coral Hartwell. Be good at it now.* And she was right. I had spent eleven years copying contracts in a mountain temple, recording other people's debts in other people's ledgers while keeping my own forbidden book of every name the temple owned. Eleven years of small, careful, treasonous arithmetic.

Tonight the arithmetic came due.

"The renewal rite," Seraphine announced to the hall, her voice carrying the way voices carry in dreams, "renews the wards that guard our borders. The wards are fed by salt. The salt is fed by labor. And the labor—" her milk-white eyes slid down to me, kneeling, "—is fed by devotion."

*By blood*, she meant. By Hartwell blood, the last of a line the crown had declared extinct two hundred years ago because extinct lines can't object to how they're used.

A priest approached with a basin. Another with a knife of black glass. Somewhere above us, in the painted constellations, a chip of plaster fell and no one noticed.

I thought about the ledger hidden in the lining of my coat—the last one, the true one, every name the temple and crown had ever owned, copied out over three sleepless nights in a cell while guards who thought I was praying actually watched me write. It was already gone. Marguerite had smuggled it out with the freed prisoners at dawn, folded into a bread delivery, distributed across six hands headed six directions. Whatever happened in this room, those names were loose in the world now, multiplying like spilled grain.

You cannot unspill grain. I'd learned that from experience, actually, in the temple pantry, but the principle held.

The knife touched my palm.

And the doors of the Hall of Renewal exploded inward.

* * *

*Marguerite*

I have held collapsing wards before. Let me tell you what nobody tells you: it does not feel like holding something up. It feels like holding something *down*—like pressing your palms against a door with a hurricane behind it, except the hurricane is made of hunger and it knows your name and it is very, very patient.

The plan had been simple, which is to say it was terrible, which is to say it was ours. Coral would let them start the rite. The rite required a willing Hartwell, and the Salt King's whole long game—decades of corrupted salt, a hollowed king, a priestess with milk in her eyes—had been aimed at getting exactly one terrified scribe onto that dais willingly. What the Salt King had not planned for was that willingness is a door that swings both ways.

Coral would give his blood. But he would give it *his* way—anchored, named, claimed—instead of the way the rite demanded, which was open-mouthed and empty.

My job was the wards. The old ones, the true binding, the lattice the temple had built two centuries ago and then quietly starved. They ran under the Hall of Renewal like roots under a garden, and they were dying, and I had helped design their replacements, and I will carry that to whatever grave I'm owed. But dying things can still be *held*. For a while. For long enough.

I'd come up through the crypts with salt-burned hands and a stolen acolyte's robe, and I'd found the root-lattice in the dark beneath the throne room, and I'd put my bleeding palms against it, and I'd said the words my grandmother taught me before the temple took her name away too.

The lattice woke up angry.

It knew it was being used again after two hundred years of neglect, and it expressed its opinion by pouring itself up through the marble floor of the Hall of Renewal in veins of white fire, cracking the silver seal, splitting the dais, and generally ruining what I understand was a very expensive ceremony.

Screaming. A great deal of screaming. Courtiers are not brave; I say this with affection, having been one.

Seraphine Vole turned from the kneeling scribe, and her face did something I'll be thinking about for the rest of my life, however long that turns out to be. It came apart. Not like a mask dropping—like a mask *dissolving*, the human face going soft and running, and underneath there was no face at all, only pale crystal and a darkness behind it that was older than the kingdom, older than the temple, older maybe than the stars it had eaten.

"*Keeper,*" it said, in a voice like a landslide remembering how to speak. "*You held these wards once. You sold them. Come hold them again.*"

"I know what I sold," I said, and pressed harder, and the white fire sang. "I'm buying it back."

It laughed, and the laughter fell from the ceiling as salt.

* * *

*Coral*

Here is what I remember of the next few minutes, in the order my mind filed them, which is the order of a clerk, because that is what I am:

Item: the throne cracked down the middle, and King Aldric screamed—a human scream, a man's scream—and something vast began pulling itself out of him like a hand out of a glove.

Item: Marguerite rose out of the shattered floor with light all over her arms, holding the world's oldest door shut with her bare hands, shouting words I didn't know she knew.

Item: the Salt King—because it had a name now, or rather it had finally stopped pretending it didn't—turned its attention on me, and everything else in the hall became very small and far away.

Item: it offered me eternity.

I want to be honest about this, since honesty is apparently the theme of my death: the offer was beautiful. That's the thing nobody tells you about ancient hungers—they don't tempt you with ugliness. It showed me a library that went on forever, every name ever erased restored to its page, every debt forgiven, every record kept perfectly, eternally, by me, safe, permanent, *needed*. It showed me myself without the shaking hands. It showed me a universe with no hidden floors.

"You will never be nobody again," it said, and its voice was kind, and that was the most frightening thing in the entire hall. "You will be the one who remembers. Forever."

And here is the thing. Some part of me—the part that had spent eleven years hiding a forbidden ledger under a floorboard, the part that wanted so badly to matter that it committed quiet treason just to keep names alive—some part of me wanted to say yes.

So I did what I always do with accounts that don't balance.

I opened my books.

"You want to remember?" I said. My voice cracked, and I let it. "Then remember *this*."

I didn't have the ledger anymore. I didn't need it. I had spent three nights in a cell copying it, and before that eleven years keeping it, and every name had gone into me the way ink goes into vellum—not on the surface, but through. I began to recite.

"Tomasin Grell, quarry-hand, debt of nine crowns, owned since age fourteen. His daughter is called Wren. Wren has a scar on her left eyebrow from a fall she took laughing."

The Salt King flinched.

"Marta Ostrevan, salt-walker, third shift, twenty-two years bound. She sings off-key on purpose so the overseers won't ask her to lead prayers."

It reached for me—I felt it reach, cold and enormous and suddenly *hurried*—and I kept reading.

"Denna Hartwell, my grandmother, who was not extinct, only inconvenient. She taught me letters on flour-dusted boards and told me that a name written down is a name that can't be stolen."

The white fire under Marguerite's hands roared higher. The Salt King was pouring out of Aldric now entirely, a shape like a glacier given ambition, and it was *thinner* than it had been a moment ago, because I was feeding it the one thing it could not digest.

Names freely given are not debts. That was the flaw in it, the error in its ancient arithmetic—it had built itself on ownership, on salt taken and labor taken and lives taken, and I stood there giving names away, handing them out like bread, anchoring myself not in permanence but in *people*, and people do not belong to hunger. People belong to each other.

"Coral Hartwell," I said, and my voice was steady now, and my hand—the cut one, the bleeding one—was raised, and the blood fell on the broken seal and burned like a struck match. "Scribe. Debtor. Son of a line the crown declared extinct and the salt declared useful. I give my name to no king, no god, and no hunger. I give it to them."

I gestured—at the crowd, at the prisoners' families smuggled in among the courtiers, at Marguerite blazing on the broken floor, at Aldric weeping in the ruins of his throne.

"To everyone who carries the ledger. Write me down wherever you like. Or don't." I smiled, and it hurt, and it was worth it. "A name in every hand doesn't need a grave."

The Salt King made a sound then that I hope never to describe in any ledger, living or dead, and came apart—not violently, not like the doors had, but the way frost comes apart on a warm window. Unmaking itself into ordinary water. Into nothing. Into, I choose to believe, a faint warmth on a windowsill somewhere, glimmering.

The last thing I saw clearly was the ceiling, where the painted constellations were peeling away like old varnish—and behind them, coming through, the real ones.

They'd waited. All this time, they'd waited.

Good stars. Patient stars.

* * *

*Marguerite*

The wards closed gently, the way a hand closes over a candle to carry it. I felt them go—felt the lattice I'd betrayed and the binding I'd rebuilt settle back into the bones of the mountain, fed now not by salt and debt but by something freely given, which is the only currency the old craft ever truly accepted, and the one we'd been too greedy to pay.

King Aldric lay in the wreckage of his throne, breathing, weeping, whispering thank you to a scribe who was no longer there to hear it. The hall was full of people on their knees, and for once none of them were kneeling for a king.

In my pocket was the copy of the final ledger—the first of thousands, if the freed prisoners did their work. On its last page, in Coral's small, stubborn, beautiful hand, was a single line I hadn't seen until that morning:

*If you're reading this, the account is settled. Spend the rest well.*

Outside, through the shattered doors, the night sky was filling up with stars like a ledger filling up with names.

I stood up. My hands were burned. There was work to do.
