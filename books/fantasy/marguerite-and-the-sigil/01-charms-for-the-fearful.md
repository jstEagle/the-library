## 1. Charms for the Fearful

The market of Greyharbor opened before dawn the way a wound opens: slowly, and in places nobody wanted to look at too closely.

Lena Amberdale was there when the first tarp went up, because she was always there. She had her stall between a woman who sold eels out of a rain barrel and a man whose entire inventory was one crate of misprinted prayer-cards, and she had learned long ago that this was the best real estate in the smuggler's market—not the good spots near the fish gate, which drew wardens like gulls draw fish guts, but the shabby middle, where people came to buy things they didn't want anyone to know they needed.

She laid out her goods with the care of a surgeon and the speed of a thief. Luck-knots first, three-strand cords of grey harbor wool, each one knotted nine times with her fingers moving through patterns that looked, to any watcher, like idle fidgeting. Then the fever-charms: little sewn pouches of salt and dried yarrow and one pinch of something else, something that had no honest name, stitched shut with red thread and a word said backward under the breath. Then the smaller stock, the things that kept her fed more than they kept her customers lucky—hangover cures that were mostly willow bark and mostly worked, love tokens that did nothing at all but made the buyer feel brave enough to speak, and a jar of "blessed" lamp oil that was just lamp oil, sold at a markup to people who wanted to believe their houses were watched over.

None of it was big magic. That was the first rule.

*Never big magic.* Big magic left marks. Big magic made the air taste like copper for an hour afterward and made dogs howl two streets away and made wardens come with their iron-sensing rods and their ledgers full of names. Big magic was what had gotten the craft outlawed in the first place—or so the proclamations said, nailed up on every gate in Vellamark, though Lena privately suspected the outlawry had less to do with danger and more to do with the fact that a witch who could ward a house didn't need to pay rent, and a witch who could charm a fever didn't need to buy medicine, and free people were bad for business.

Her magic was small on purpose. A knot tied right could hold a little luck the way a cup holds water—a mouthful, not a river. A fever-charm could take the edge off a burning child's brow, could turn three bad nights into one bad night, could mean the difference sometimes, if you were lucky about it, but never certainly. Certainty was big magic. Certainty got you remembered.

That was the second rule. *Never twice for the same customer.* A woman who bought a luck-knot before her husband's fishing run and then came back before her daughter's birthing was a thread someone could follow. Two data points made a pattern; three made a name. So Lena sold to a face once, and if the same face drifted toward her stall again—and faces always drifted back, because small magic worked just well enough to be addictive—she'd shake her head, say her stall was closed, say she was only minding it for her cousin, say anything, and watch the disappointment settle on them like ash.

And the third rule, the rule underneath the other two, the rule that was really all three rules wearing a coat: *never be remembered.*

Lena Amberdale had been very good at not being remembered for fifteen years.

"Oi." The eel-woman leaned across the gap between stalls, her forearms slick to the elbow. "You got anything for joints? My knees are singing the morning song."

Lena looked at her. Marta. Third time this season, which meant twice already, which meant—

"You've got the wrong stall," Lena said. "I mind this for my cousin. I couldn't say what he sells."

Marta squinted at her. "Your cousin. Right. Your cousin with your face."

"Cousins look alike," Lena said, and turned back to arranging knots she had already arranged, and after a moment Marta grunted and went back to her eels, and Lena let out a breath she hadn't known she was holding.

This was the shape of her days. This was the whole shape: lay out the goods, sell to strangers, refuse the familiar, pack up before the wardens' midmorning walk, count coins in a room with no name attached to it, sleep, do it again. Fifteen years of it. It was a life built like one of her own luck-knots—small, tight, holding nothing much, but holding.

She told herself she didn't mind.

She told herself that most mornings, actually, usually while her hands were busy and her mind was allowed to go quiet. Hands busy was the trick. A witch's hands remembered things her head tried to forget, and as long as the fingers were moving—knotting, stitching, folding—the rest of her could pretend this was enough. The market noise was enough. The smell of brine and tar and frying dough from the cook-cart was enough. The gray light coming off the water was enough.

It was enough. It was enough. It was—

"Luck-knot?" said a voice, small and hopeful, and Lena blinked down at a boy of maybe ten holding out two coppers, his other hand clutching a folded paper that looked like a letter or a summons. "For my da. He's going up-country for work and Mam says the roads are bad since the wards started flickering."

Lena's hands paused over the wool.

"The wards," she said. "Flickering?"

"You haven't seen? Night before last, out past the boundary stones—like the lights along the wall couldn't decide whether to stay lit. Da says it's nothing. Da says everything's nothing." The boy shrugged with the practiced fatalism of children raised on adults' reassurances. "But Mam cried, so. Luck-knot?"

Wards didn't flicker. That was the whole point of wards. They were the oldest working in Vellamark, older than the city walls, older than the crown, fed—everyone knew this the way everyone knows things without knowing how they know them—by the fire under Mount Sarn, steady as a heartbeat, constant as the stars. If the wards flickered, it would mean the fire was failing, and the fire couldn't fail, because if the fire failed then the border stood open, and everyone knew what the border was for.

Everyone knew. Nobody asked.

"Here." Lena took the coppers and pressed a knot into the boy's hand, tying the ninth loop as she did it, quick, clean, a mouthful of luck and no more. "Tell him to walk at dawn and dusk, not noon. Roads are quieter then."

"That's not magic advice."

"No," Lena agreed. "It's free."

He grinned and ran, and Lena watched him go and felt the flicker-word lodge under her ribs like a fishbone. Wards didn't flicker. But she had felt something two nights back, hadn't she—a wrongness in the weave, faint as a draft under a door, there and gone while she'd been counting coins. She'd told herself it was tiredness. She was good at telling herself things.

*Never big magic,* she reminded her hands, and her hands went back to work.

* * *

Midmorning brought the crowd, and the crowd brought the usual parade of fear dressed up as shopping.

A dockhand wanting protection from a foreman's grudge—she sold him a knot and told him honestly that knots couldn't stop fists, only soften aim, and watched him decide to believe her anyway. An old man wanting a charm against nightmares, which was really a charm against missing his dead wife, which was really nothing Lena could stitch, though she gave him willow bark for his sleeplessness and charged him for the willow bark and let him call it blessed. A young wife with shadowed eyes buying her third fever-charm of the winter for a sick child, and Lena refusing her—*never twice*—and then standing there hating herself while the woman pleaded, until finally Lena said, "There's a hedge-healer up the coast road, Maren, by the old tollhouse. Tell her the eel-seller sent you," which was a lie wrapped around a kindness, and the woman left almost happy.

By the time the light turned silver-gray toward noon, Lena's throat was raw from talking and her stock was half gone and her rules had been bent exactly once, which was once too often, and she was beginning to think about packing up when the last customer of the day arrived.

He wasn't remarkable. That was the thing she'd remember afterward—that he wasn't remarkable at all. Middle-aged, seaman's roll to his walk, oilskin cape beaded with spray. He browsed her stall the way men browse when they're killing time, and then he picked up a fever-charm, turned it over, and said, "My daughter. Croup. The physick wants four silvers to bleed her and I've heard bleeding's rubbish."

"It is rubbish," Lena said. "Two coppers. Keep the pouch dry, hang it at the head of her bed, and steam the room—boil water, sit with her in it. Steam does more than my thread does, truth told."

"And the thread?"

"The thread helps the steamer believe," Lena said. "Believing helps the child. It all works together. Don't ask me to explain the order of it."

He smiled—tired, grateful, ordinary—and dug in his purse, and counted out coppers, and then added one more coin from the bottom of the purse, an old one, dark with handling, saying, "Keep it, I've got a pocketful of these antiques, worthless at the mint—"

Lena took it without looking. Then she looked.

The coin was old silver, worn nearly smooth, but the stamp survived in the high places: a circle broken by a vertical line, and within the break, a small descending spiral. Three turns of the spiral. Always three.

Lena knew that mark the way you know your own handwriting. She knew it better than her own handwriting, because her own handwriting she'd changed twice in fifteen years, and this mark she had never once been able to change, because it lived in her skin.

It was branded into the base of her left palm.

The market noise went distant, the way sound does underwater. Somewhere an eel bucket sloshed. Somewhere Marta was arguing with the cook-cart woman about the price of vinegar. And Lena stood behind her stall of small safe harmless charms, holding a coin stamped with Odile's sigil—the sigil of a woman who had been dead or fled or erased for fifteen years, the sigil that had been burned into Lena's hand at her apprenticeship, the sigil that meant *bound to the craft, bound to the mistress, bound forever*—and her hands began to shake.

Not a tremble. A shake. The kind that comes up from somewhere below the wrists, from somewhere below the ribs, from the locked cellar where she kept everything she refused to look at.

"Miss?" The seaman frowned. "You all right? It's only an old coin—"

"Where," Lena said. Her voice came out strange, flat, far away. "Where did you get this."

"Eh? Been in the family. Grandfather's purse, passed down. Why, is it worth something—"

"Where did your grandfather live."

"Nowhere. Here. Greyharbor, all his life—" The man stepped back, and Lena saw herself through his eyes suddenly: a charm-seller gone rigid, gripping a worthless coin, asking questions with a face gone white as gull-bone. "Look, forget the charm, I'll pay the coppers—"

"Take the charm." She pushed it at him. "Take it. Take the coin back."

"I don't want—"

"*Take it.*"

He took it. He took the charm and put the coin back in her palm—her shaking palm, closing her fingers over it himself like she was a beggar and he was being kind—and he left, quickly, glancing back once, and Lena stood alone behind her stall in the middle of the loudest market in Greyharbor and felt the loneliness arrive the way it always arrived: not gradually, but all at once, like a tide turning.

Because here was the thing about rules. Rules kept you alive. Rules kept you small and safe and unremembered. But rules were also a kind of wall, and she had built hers so carefully, stone by stone, for fifteen years, and now she understood—with the coin biting cold into her palm, with Odile's spiral sitting there under her thumb like an eye opening—that the wall had another name, and the other name was *alone*, and she had done it to herself, and she had done it gladly, because the alternative had been fire and ledgers and a mistress's voice saying *you'll thank me someday, girl*, and she had run from that alternative so hard and so fast that she had run clean out of her own life and into this gray daily nothing and called the nothing freedom.

Odile's sigil. In Greyharbor. In a sailor's grandfather's purse.

Either it meant nothing—an old coin, a common mark, a symbol some forgotten guild had used and abandoned, coincidence, coincidence, *coincidence*—

Or it meant the past had found the street she lived on.

Or it meant Odile, or Odile's work, or Odile's debts, had been here all along, threaded through the town like red thread through a seam, and Lena had simply never pulled.

"Closing early," she heard herself tell Marta, already folding the tarp, her hands steadying themselves the way hands do when they're given a job. "Sick. Family thing."

"Since when have you got family?" Marta said, and laughed at her own joke, and Lena laughed too, because that was easier than answering, and packed her luck-knots away one by one, nine loops each, small magic, small life, and walked home through streets that suddenly seemed full of watchers.

That night she set the coin on her table under her one lamp and did not touch it for a long time.

Then she touched it. Turned it over. And froze.

Because on the reverse, where a mint-mark should have been, there was instead a single line of script, fine as spider silk, in a hand she recognized the way you recognize a voice in a dream:

*Girl. They're coming. Burn everything.*

*—O.*

Outside her window, far off past the rooftops, past the boundary stones, past the whole sleeping width of the border, something flickered.

Lena reached for her matches.
