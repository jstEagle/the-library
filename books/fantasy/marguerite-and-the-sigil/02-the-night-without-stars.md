## 2. The Night Without Stars

Lena Amberdale had three rules, and she was breaking none of them when the sky went out.

She was in her room above the dye-shop on Tanner's Row, counting the day's take by candlelight because she didn't trust herself to do sums after dark anymore—her eyes were thirty-four years old and they lied to her about copper the way they'd begun to lie about everything. Eleven coppers, two bits of silver, and the strange old coin from the man with the boat-hook scar. She'd set that one apart. It sat on the table like a spider she couldn't bring herself to kill.

Midnight came the way it always did in Greyharbor: bells from the Saltchurch tower, a drunk singing three streets over, the smell of the tannery vats turning sour in the cold. Lena blew out the candle out of habit—you didn't leave a flame burning unwatched, not if you were who she was—and lay back on her cot in the dark.

That was when the stars went out.

Not clouded. Not faded. *Out*, all at once, like a hand passing over a hundred candles, and the darkness that came down through her window was so total that for a moment she thought she'd gone blind. She heard herself make a sound, small and stupid, a child's sound. Her hands went to her face. Her face was still there. The dark was outside her, and it was *wrong*.

Then the horizon shuddered.

She felt it before she understood it—a long rolling tremor through the floorboards, up through the cot, into her teeth, as if the whole rim of the world had been struck with a hammer. Somewhere far to the east, past the salt marshes and the border stones she had spent fifteen years not thinking about, something enormous had either fallen or been let go.

Lena sat up in the dark and did the arithmetic she'd been refusing to do for fifteen years.

The wards ran on fire. Old fire. Deep fire. Fire from under Mount Sarn, though no warden would say so aloud and no charm-seller was supposed to know it at all. Odile had told her once, late, drunk on plum brandy, tracing the sigil on Lena's palm with one cracked fingernail: *Everything they build is a wall around a secret, girl, and every wall eats fuel.* And the stars—the stars were part of it too, weren't they? The wardens' boundary stones drank starlight the way their own little charms drank candleflame, and the two systems leaned against each other like drunks, and if one fell—

If one fell, the other would follow.

Lena got up. Her legs were steadier than she deserved. She dressed in the dark without thinking about it, the way you dress when you've rehearsed a thing for fifteen years without ever believing you'd need it: wool first, then leather, then the grey cloak that made her one more shadow among Greyharbor's ten thousand shadows. She took the coin off the table. She didn't know why. It felt important the way a tooth feels important right before it has to come out.

Downstairs, the city was waking up badly.

* * *

By the time she reached the street, people were pouring out of their doors in nightclothes, faces tipped up to a sky with nothing in it. A woman knelt in the middle of Tanner's Row weeping at the emptiness overhead. Two boys were throwing stones at the dark as if they could knock the stars back into place. From the direction of the Saltchurch tower someone was ringing the alarm bell wrong—all crashes, no pattern, the bell-ringer's hands shaking or gone.

"The wards," someone said, and the word moved through the crowd like fire through dry grass. "The wards have fallen." "The wards are down." "We're open. Gods help us, we're *open*."

Lena pulled her hood low and walked, fast but not running, because running made you memorable. That was rule three and she clung to it now like a rail in a flood: *never be remembered*. Fifteen years of small charms and smaller fears, fifteen years of being nobody from nowhere, and it could all be undone by one neighbor saying *I saw her run.*

She was halfway to the harbor stairs when the wardens came.

They came in files of six, lanterns hooded, halberds shouldered, moving through the panicked streets with the terrible calm of men following orders written before tonight. She heard their sergeant's voice, flat and carrying: *"Curfew. All persons to shelter. Witches and charm-workers will present themselves for questioning. Harbor's closed. No one leaves Greyharbor."*

No one leaves.

Lena turned down an alley between a chandler's and a cooperage, heart slamming, mind doing what it always did—splitting, calculating, the cold half of her taking notes while the frightened half screamed. They wouldn't find witches tonight. They'd find *someone*. Wardens who had just lost the wards needed a neck to wring, and there was no neck more convenient than an unlicensed one. Every hedge-witch, every luck-peddler, every fool who'd ever sold a fever-knot in a smuggler's market was going to hang for a crime committed a hundred miles underground by something none of them had ever seen.

And among them, when the wardens started matching symbols on the fallen boundary stones against the marks on known practitioners' hands—

Her palm. The sigil. Branded at her apprenticeship, faded to a pale silver ghost, but there. Always there. She'd worn gloves for years, told people she had a burn scar, kept her hands in her pockets like a girl ashamed of them. If any warden looked closely at Lena Amberdale's palm tonight, she would not be questioned. She would be *answered for*.

She cut through the chandler's yard, over a fence slick with wax-drippings, and came out on Rope Walk with the harbor breathing below her—black water, black masts, black sky above it all, no lamp reflected anywhere because the lighthouse itself had gone dark. Even the lighthouse. Whatever had fallen, it had fallen everywhere at once.

A patrol crossed the head of the street. Lena pressed herself into a doorway and watched six lanterns pass, and in the light of the nearest she saw the warden's gauntlet, and stamped into the gauntlet's steel plate, the warden's badge: a circle broken by seven strokes.

She knew those strokes. She'd drawn them a thousand times in a ledger she was about to burn.

* * *

Her room smelled of tallow and old paper, and it took her less than a quarter hour to make it smell of smoke instead.

Fifteen years of records. That was what she burned, kneeling at her cold hearth with the strange coin in her pocket and the window cracked just enough to pull the draft. The ledger from her first year in Greyharbor, when her hand had still shaken writing down prices. The ledger from her tenth year, neat as a moneylender's. The loose pages: customer names coded into flower-names, remedies coded into recipes, *one knot of return-luck for Marigold, paid in kind; one fever-charm for Foxglove's daughter, twice now, break the rule or refuse her next time*. Fifteen years of proof that a witch lived on Tanner's Row and worked small magics steadily, carefully, illegally, for neighbors who would sell her name to the first warden who asked.

She fed it all to the hearth page by page and watched the flower-names curl and go.

There was a moment—there was always going to be a moment—when she came to the bottom of the second chest and found the thing she'd buried under the ledgers: a child's practice-book, its pages soft with handling, full of a young girl's careful copies of sigils. *Lena, aged eleven*, in a round uncertain hand. Odile's marginal notes in red ink: *Better. Again. Your lines wander when you're proud of yourself.* On the last page, the mistress's own mark, drawn full-size for copying: the circle broken by seven strokes, the same sign that rode the wardens' badges, the same sign stamped on the coin in Lena's pocket, the same sign branded into her palm at fourteen when Odile had held her wrist over the brazier and said, *This is how I keep you safe, and this is how I keep you mine.*

Lena held the page over the flame and her hand wouldn't move.

The paper caught anyway—fire is patient, fire doesn't need your permission—and she watched eleven-year-old Lena's careful lines blacken and lift, and she thought: *she marked me so no one else could claim me, and now the marking is the thing that will get me taken.* Fifteen years of hiding inside the very brand that was supposed to protect her. The joke was old enough to drink. It wasn't funny yet. Maybe in another fifteen years.

Smoke stung her eyes. Or that was the excuse she used.

She left the practice-book burning, took the coin, took her knife and her kit—the good kit, the one packed for exactly this night since the first winter she'd spent in Greyharbor—and went out through the dye-shop's loft window onto the rooftops.

* * *

Greyharbor from above was a city holding its breath.

Roof after roof, slate and shingle silvered with frost, and between them the streets full of moving lights—warden lanterns, dozens of them now, converging on the market district, on the temple, on the harbor stairs. Bells arguing across the rooftops. Dogs going mad in yards. And everywhere people standing in doorways looking up at nothing, because human beings cannot stop looking up at a sky that has betrayed them.

Lena ran the roofs the way she had as a girl, before the outlawry, before the rules—chimney to ridgepole to gutter, boots finding the sure footing without consulting her. There was a version of her that had loved this. She tried to remember what that girl had been running *toward* and couldn't, only what she'd been running from, and the memory arrived with its usual timing, which was the worst possible:

Odile's kitchen, fifteen years ago. Plum brandy and woodsmoke. Her mistress's face in the firelight, older suddenly, frightened in a way Lena had never seen and never seen since. *They're coming for you, girl. What you are. What I made you. Pack nothing you love.* And then the back door, and the marsh road, and the last thing Odile ever said to her, thrown after her like a stone: *Don't look back and don't send word. If you're smart, I was never your mother and you were never anything of mine.*

Lena had been smart. Fifteen years smart. And tonight the bill had come due anyway, sliding under the door of the whole world at midnight, putting out the stars to get her attention.

Because that was the thought she couldn't shake, the one walking beside her along every ridgepole: *this is about me.* Not the wards, not the dragon-fire, not whatever vast machine had failed out east—but her. The coin with Odile's sigil surfacing in a smuggler's market after fifteen years, the same week the wards fell. Coincidences were just debts wearing masks. Something had been looking for her, patiently, and tonight it had stopped being patient, and it had put out every star in the sky to say so.

Ridiculous. She knew it was ridiculous. One charm-seller in a city of forty thousand, one faded brand on one palm. The wards hadn't fallen for her.

But her feet carried her east along the rooftops anyway, toward the high ground above the tanneries where she could see the horizon, and she told herself it was to check the roads, to plan her route out before the harbor closed for good, and she almost believed it.

From the tannery roof, Greyharbor spread below her like a spilled jewelry box with all the jewels stolen. Eastward, past the marshes, the land ran flat and black to the line of the old border hills—and along that line, faint as breath on glass, something glowed. Not firelight. Not dawn, which was hours away. A greenish shimmer, low and steady, marking the whole eastern sky like a seam of luminous rot, and even at this distance Lena's palm began to itch.

She looked down at her hand. In the starless dark, the sigil stood out on her skin like a scar lit from beneath—pale silver, unmistakable, glowing.

Glowing brighter, she realized with a calm that terrified her more than panic would have, the longer she looked at the green seam on the horizon. As if it recognized what it saw. As if, across fifty miles of dead wards and darkened country, something on the other side of that light had turned its attention toward her, and her own skin was answering.

Below, on Tanner's Row, a warden's lantern stopped in front of the dye-shop. Then another. Boots in the street. A fist on a door, and the muffled voice of her landlady, frightened, protesting, and then the flat warden's question that Lena was too high and too far to hear but had no trouble imagining:

*"Where does the witch sleep?"*

Lena Amberdale stood on the rooftop with her burning city at her back and her traitor hand shining like a beacon, and understood, finally and completely, that the past had not come collecting.

It had already collected. Everything after this was just delivery.
