## 3. The Warden Who Knocked

Dain Corvess had been a warden for eleven years and a disgrace for one night, and he was learning quickly that the second job was harder.

The first thing they took was his badge. The second was his horse. The third, by way of the magistrate's clerk with his ink-stained fingers and his voice like wet paper, was his name — struck from the rolls of the Vellamark Border Company with the same dry scratch of a pen that had once written it in. *Corvess, Dain. Warden, Third Rank. Dismissed for dereliction of duty in the matter of the western wards.* As if the wards had failed because Dain had been asleep at his post. As if anyone could have stood watch against a sky that went out like a snuffed candle.

He kept the coat, though. They hadn't asked for it back, and he hadn't offered. Grey wool, company grey, with the border-stitch at the cuffs that wardens sewed themselves because the Company never paid for thread. His sister had stitched his. He'd stitched hers. He wore it now in a city that spat at wardens on principle, and he let them spit, because the coat was the last true thing he owned.

Three days after the Night Without Stars, Dain Corvess walked into Greyharbor with a dead man's warrant in his pocket and a piece of broken boundary stone wrapped in oilcloth inside his coat, pressed against his ribs like a second heart.

* * *

The stone had come from the western line, from the marker at Cold Ford where the wards had stood for two hundred years without so much as a flicker. When the stars died, every boundary stone along three hundred miles of border had cracked down its center — not shattered, not toppled, but split, neat as a loaf, as if something inside each one had finally stopped holding it together. The Company's masons couldn't explain it. The Company's hedge-scholars couldn't either, though they'd charged the Crown a handsome fee for trying.

Dain had gone out to Cold Ford alone, before they took his badge, before the word *derelict* finished attaching itself to him like a burr. He'd knelt in the frost-mud between the halves of the stone and looked at what was written inside.

Not carved. Not painted. *Grown*, if anything — lines that ran through the rock the way veins run through a leaf, silver-pale, in patterns no mason's chisel could have made. And threaded through those patterns, repeated over and over like a signature at the bottom of a very long letter, was a mark he recognized.

A circle, open at the top. A line through it, slanting left. Three small ticks beneath, like claw-marks or bird-tracks.

He'd seen that mark before. Not on stones. On skin.

His sister Sera had borne its twin on the back of her right hand, small as a coin, inked there by a woman she'd sworn Dain never to mention to anyone. Sera had been careful the way clever people are careless — she'd hidden the witch perfectly by never speaking of her, which meant the only person who knew the witch existed was the brother who loved her enough to keep quiet. Sera was four years dead now. Fever, the healer said, though Dain had always privately believed you could die of a secret the same as any sickness, worn through from the inside.

And now the same sigil that lived on Sera's grave-marker hand was grown into the bones of the fallen wards.

So Dain did the one thing a disgraced warden was still qualified to do. He worked a case. Unofficially. Illegally, probably, though no one had gotten around to outlawing stubbornness yet.

It took him two days in Greyharbor's smuggler's market, trading on the last shreds of his reputation — *Corvess, he'll find what you lost, no questions* — before a fence with a cleft lip told him about the charm-seller. Luck-knots. Fever-charms. Never the same customer twice. Works the fish-row end of the market, third stall past the tallow-renderer's, comes late, leaves early, face like a woman who's practiced forgetting her own name.

"Never be remembered," the fence said, and laughed through the gap in his lip. "That's the whole trade, friend. You want to find someone whose entire craft is not being found?"

Yes, Dain thought. I do. Because I've spent my life guarding a wall built by whatever made that mark, and the wall just fell, and I need to know if the maker is the wound or the knife.

* * *

She wasn't at the fish-row stall. Of course she wasn't. But the tallow-renderer remembered a woman who'd bought candle-ends in bulk, and the candle-seller remembered a woman who'd asked once, quietly, whether wax held a scent longer than paper, and the paper-seller —

Dain followed the trail the way he'd once followed smugglers through the marsh country: not looking for footprints but for absences. Places where a person had been careful. A market stall dismantled too thoroughly, its boards scrubbed. A rooming-house where the landlady swore up and down she'd never rented to any such woman while her eyes flicked, twice, toward the stairs. A chimney that had been swept the morning after the stars went dark, though the house's other chimneys wore a year of soot.

Someone had burned fifteen years of paper in a hurry, Dain thought, standing in that swept little room with its bare hooks and its scrubbed floor. Someone had known the sky falling would come for her specifically.

Which meant she knew what the wards were fed on. Which meant she mattered.

He found her on the fourth day, at dusk, on the rooftops.

He'd given up on the streets and taken to the high ways himself — old warden habit, the patrol routes above the smuggling lanes that the Company pretended didn't exist. Greyharbor's roofs were a country unto themselves: slate ridges and tarred flats, clotheslines strung between chimneys like bunting, the whole city's secrets drying in the open air. He was crossing above Cooper's Lane when he saw her, three roofs over, moving fast and low with a bundle on her back, silhouetted against the last bruised light of a starless evening.

Even at that distance he knew her by her hands. She moved across the ridgeline the way wardens crossed ice — weight centered, every step committed, no wasted motion. A woman who had done this many times before. A woman running.

Dain should have called out. Should have signaled the nearest watch-post, earned back a sliver of his name by handing them a witch on the worst week in living memory to be one. The whole city was howling for someone to blame, and the wardens were sweeping the low streets with nets and iron, and here was exactly what they wanted, delivered on a roof.

Instead he followed her. Quietly. The way you follow a wounded animal you haven't decided about yet.

She led him a mile across the rooftops, down through a gap in a warehouse roof into a loft full of rope and salt-smell, and Dain nearly lost her twice in the dark, and then she simply — stopped. Stood still in the middle of the loft with her bundle hugged to her chest and said, to the empty air:

"You walk like a warden."

Dain froze in the doorway, one hand on the frame.

"Heavier on the left," she went on, not turning around. She had a low voice, level, tired in the bones rather than the throat. "Company drill favors the right side coming off a ladder. You favor the left. Old injury?" Now she turned, and the light from the loft's one salt-glazed window caught her face: mid-thirties maybe, sharp-boned, brown hair cut short and practical, eyes the color of strong tea. Eyes that were doing arithmetic on him. "Or you're not really a warden, and you learned to walk like one from a book."

"I'm not really a warden," Dain said. It came out easier than he expected. True things usually did, once you'd lost everything worth lying for. "I'm a former warden. Which is worse."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. An acknowledgment, maybe, of a joke made in a language she understood. "Former," she repeated. "They strike your name off the rolls?"

"This morning." He kept his hands visible and loose at his sides. Eleven years of de-escalation training, and it all came down, in the end, to showing your palms. "Dain Corvess. Western line, Cold Ford station. Until three nights ago."

Her expression didn't change, but her grip on the bundle shifted — a small tightening, the kind of motion a body makes when a name lands somewhere it wasn't braced for. "Cold Ford," she said. "You were there. When the stones split."

"I was there two days before, checking the marker line. I was in Greyharbor when they went." He took one step into the loft, slow, deliberate, the way you'd approach a trap you suspected was armed. "I've been asking myself ever since whether that makes me the luckiest man in the Vellamark or the most useless."

"The second," she said. "No offense."

"None taken." Another step. The salt smell was stronger here, and under it something else — beeswax, dried herbs, a faint resinous tang he couldn't place but his sister's coat pockets had once carried home. "I'm not here with the watch. I'm not here for the watch. I'm here because of this."

He drew the oilcloth bundle from inside his coat and unwrapped it, kneeling, setting the two halves of the boundary stone on the floorboards between them with the care owed to something holy or something dangerous. In the window-light the inner faces showed their silver veining, and threaded through it, unmistakable even in the gloom, the open circle, the slanting line, the three small ticks.

The charm-seller went very still.

Dain had interrogated smugglers, deserters, one genuinely frightening ash-witch out of the Karst, and he knew every species of stillness a human body could produce. This was none of the guilty kinds. This was the stillness of a woman looking at her own handwriting on a document she never signed.

"Where did you get this," she said. Her voice had dropped half a tone.

"Cold Ford. The western marker. There are forty more like it along the line, all split the same way, all grown through with the same mark." He stayed on his knees, hands flat and open on the floorboards beside the stone. "I know what that mark is. My sister bore its twin. Inked, not branded — a charm-witch did it for her, years ago, a woman she trusted. Sera never told me the witch's name. She only ever called her—"

He stopped. Because the charm-seller had extended her right hand into the window-light, palm up, fingers spread.

And there, in the center of her palm, was the sigil.

Not inked. Not drawn. *Branded* — an old scar, white and smooth-edged, the tissue grown around the mark the way bark grows around a nail driven deep. The open circle. The slanting line. The three ticks. It sat in her palm like a door set into flesh, and as the last light of the starless dusk touched it, the scar-lines flushed faintly warm, a dull ember-glow, as if something behind her skin had noticed the stone on the floor and turned toward it.

Dain forgot how to breathe.

Sera's hand. Sera's laugh. Sera at nineteen, hiding her marked hand in her sleeve at supper, and their mother pretending not to see, and the whole family keeping the secret the way you keep a coal alive in ashes — cupped, guarded, half-starved. Sera dead at twenty-nine with the mark still on her, and Dain at the graveside thinking: *someone gave her that. Someone marked my sister and then vanished, and I will never know if it saved her life or shortened it.*

Now here was the someone. Here was the hand.

"That's not ink," he heard himself say. His voice sounded far away. "That's a brand. That's apprentice-marking. That's—" He stopped again, because the next word was *ownership*, and saying it felt like putting his boot through a door he'd only just been invited to knock on.

"It's a brand," Lena Amberdale said — and he didn't know her name yet, wouldn't learn it until later, but he would remember forever that her first act upon being cornered by a stranger was not to defend herself but to answer his question honestly. "Given when I was fourteen. By the woman who raised me. And yes, before you ask — it's the same mark that's in your stones. Every ward on the Vellamark border carries it. It's the signature of the fire that fed them."

"Whose fire?"

She closed her hand slowly, and the ember-glow went out, and for a moment she looked less like a fugitive than like a woman standing at a grave.

"A dragon's," she said. "The last dragon. Under Mount Sarn. And Dain Corvess, former warden, I am going to tell you something I have spent fifteen years making sure no one could torture out of me, because you showed me a dead woman's handwriting instead of a warrant, and because you flinched when you saw my palm like you'd seen it before." Her tea-colored eyes fixed on his, unblinking. "But first you're going to tell me about your sister. Because you said *bore*. Past tense. And I need to know if the woman who marked her is the reason Sera is dead."

The loft was silent except for the salt wind worrying the eaves and, far below, the watch-drums beating their sweep-pattern through the low streets — *come out, come out, we know you're here* — a rhythm Dain had marched to for eleven years and was hearing, for the first time, from the wrong side of a locked door.

"Sera Corvess," he said. "Dead four years. Fever, they said." He swallowed. "She got the mark when she was dying anyway, or near enough — some wasting sickness the healers couldn't touch. The witch's charm didn't cure her. Sera told me, at the end, that it wasn't meant to. That it was meant to *hide* her. From something. She never said what." He looked down at the split stone, at the silver veins and the small fierce signature threaded through them. "She said the witch paid more for that mark than Sera could ever repay. She said it cost the witch something permanent."

Lena Amberdale sat down on a coil of rope, abruptly, like a puppet with a cut string. She stared at her own branded palm as if it belonged to a stranger.

"Fifteen years," she said softly. "Fifteen years I thought the price was mine. That she sold something of *mine* to buy my passage out. And now you tell me she was marking other people too — hiding other people — and every mark was another debt, and every debt has been sitting in the dark under Mount Sarn gathering interest, and three nights ago the creditor came to collect." She looked up at him, and Dain saw that her eyes were wet and furious and, underneath both, terrified in the specific way of a person who has just realized the bill is larger than everything she owns. "Your sister isn't why the wards fell. But she might be one thread of the same rope. And I don't know yet if that makes me the girl who escaped or the girl who helped tie the knot."

Outside, the watch-drums changed pattern — the sharp triple-beat that meant *sighting, close, converge* — and boots sounded on the warehouse stairs below, more than one pair, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who believed the building was already theirs.

Dain rose, and did the last thing a loyal warden would ever do, and the first thing a loyal brother always would.

He put his shoulder against the loft's back shutter, the one facing the harbor-dark, and looked at her.

"Roofline's clear to the tannery," he said. "I can hold the stairs for maybe ninety seconds. After that, you'll want to be gone." A beat. "Or — I know a road east. Out of the city, past the sweep-lines. Toward Mount Sarn." He heard himself say it and marveled, distantly, at the sound. "If you're going where I think you're going, witch, you shouldn't walk it alone. And neither should I."

Below, the first boot reached the landing. A fist hit the loft door, once, twice — the polite knock that precedes the breaking.

Lena Amberdale was already moving, bundle on her back, toward the shutter — but she paused beside him, one breath, and pressed her branded palm briefly against the back of his hand, ember-warm, like a seal pressed into wax.

"Ninety seconds," she said. "Make them count, warden."
