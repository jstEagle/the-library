## 4. What the Wards Were For

### Dain

The cellar under the burned chandlery smelled of tallow and old smoke, and it was the first place in Greyharbor Dain had felt even half safe since the sky went dark.

He'd chosen it for practical reasons: one entrance, thick stone, a drain wide enough to crawl through if wardens came over the roof. He had not chosen it because the witch would look at him like that—like he was something she was still deciding whether to run from—and because her hands, wrapped around the stub of candle she refused to light, hadn't stopped trembling since the boundary stone.

He set the stone fragment on the floor between them and turned it with his boot until the carved face caught the grey light leaking down the stairwell.

"Look at it properly," he said. "Not at me. At it."

Lena Amberdale looked at the stone. She had been looking at everything except him since he'd caught her on the rooftops, which he understood. He'd spent three days being looked at the same way by every warden in the city who remembered his name.

The fragment was a piece of the Vellamark boundary, one of hundreds that had stood along the northern ridge for as long as anyone could remember—a knee-high pillar of grey granite, carved with interlocking lines that everyone assumed were decoration. Farmers leaned on them. Children played at knucklebones on their flat tops. Wardens like Dain had walked their circuit for generations, checking for cracks, reporting chips, doing nothing else, because there was nothing else to do. The wards held. That was the whole job.

Except last night they hadn't.

"This is the sigil," Lena said quietly. Her finger hovered above the carving without touching it. "Or close to it."

"It's the sigil." He pulled his glove off and turned his own palm up. The brand there was older, faded to a pale silver scar, but the lines matched: the same interlocking knot, the same small door-shape at its center. "Every warden who ever served the mark carried this. We thought it was an oath-mark. A badge." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "We were told it meant we were *trusted*."

"And it doesn't?"

"It means we were *keyed*." He crouched beside the stone. "You don't put keys on a wall, Amberdale. You put them on people."

She went very still. It was a particular kind of stillness, and Dain recognized it because his sister had done the same thing—the stillness of someone listening to a sentence finish a thought they'd been refusing to have for years.

"The wards didn't keep anything out," he said. "That's the lie. That's what they told us at the academy, what the histories say, what the songs say—Vellamark stands against the dark, the wards hold the line, brave the wardens, et cetera." He tapped the stone. "But you can't build a wall out of nothing. Walls cost. And nobody in two hundred years ever asked what paid for this one."

"What paid for it?" Lena asked. Her voice was careful, level, the voice of a woman who had learned to sound calm the way other people learned to swim—badly, and only because drowning was worse.

Dain took a breath. This was the part he'd rehearsed on the walk over, and the part that sounded insane no matter how he ordered the words.

"Fire," he said. "Dragon-fire. Fed up through the roots of Mount Sarn, through channels cut before the city existed, into every boundary stone on the mark. The wards aren't a wall. They're a *circulation*. The mountain breathes, the stones carry it, the border holds. One system. One heart."

Lena's candle rolled out of her fingers and clattered on the flagstones.

"My father served thirty years on the ridge," Dain went on, quieter now. "He used to say the stones were warm in winter. Everyone said it. Warm stones, lucky stones. We thought it was geothermal. Some trick of the deep rock." He shook his head. "It wasn't the rock. It was a heartbeat. And three nights ago, on my watch, it started skipping."

"You said the wards fell all at once."

"They fell all at once because they didn't fall at all. They *starved*. Slowly, for years—maybe decades. The fire's been failing since before I was posted. My father noticed it too, near the end. Said the stones weren't as warm as they used to be. Old man's talk, we called it." His jaw tightened. "Then whatever was left of the flame got pulled out entirely, all at once, in a single night, and every star the wards were woven from went dark, and here we are."

"In a single night," Lena repeated. "Something took it? Or—" She stopped.

"Or someone sold it," Dain finished. "Yes. That's the other thing I have to tell you, and I'll tell you now because you're going to figure it out anyway and I'd rather you heard it from me than worked it out alone in the dark at three in the morning, which is when I worked it out, and it's a bad hour for it."

He reached into his coat and drew out a folded square of oilcloth. Inside was a rubbing—he'd made it at dawn, charcoal on butcher's paper, pressed against the fallen boundary stone where the carving had split open. Under the sigil, revealed only now that the stone's outer face had cracked away, was a second layer of marks. Older. Deeper. Cut into the rock before the wardens' badge was ever designed.

A ledger-line. A transaction record, written in stone.

"I've seen script like this once before," Dain said. "In the warden archives, on the founding charters. It's contract-language. Binding-script. The old courts used it—before the outlawry, before magic went underground, back when deals were made in blood and witnessed in granite." He smoothed the paper flat between them. "This isn't a prayer or a warning. It's a receipt."

Lena leaned over it. In the grey light her face was all angles and shadow, and Dain watched her lips move slightly as she read—not reading the words, he realized, but tracing the *hand* of them. The way a scribe knows another scribe. The way a forger knows a forgery.

And then he watched the color leave her face.

"No," she said.

"You recognize it."

"I said no." But her hand had come up to hover over the charcoal line again, the same hovering, not-touching gesture she'd used on the sigil, and her fingers were shaking so hard now that she pressed them flat against her thigh to stop them. "This is—no. This is a coincidence. Script like this has conventions. Any trained—"

"Amberdale." He kept his voice low. "Do you know whose work this is?"

The silence stretched. Somewhere above them, boots crossed the street—wardens on patrol, four men at least, moving fast. Neither of them breathed until the sound faded.

"Yes," Lena said finally, and it came out like something being pulled out of her with hooks. "I know whose work it is."

### Lena

Her mistress's handwriting. Fifteen years, and she still knew it the way she knew her own pulse—the long downward strokes, the little hook she always put on terminal letters, like a door swinging shut. Odile Vane had taught Lena her letters with that hand. Had written out charm-instructions in it, neat as scripture. Had signed, in it, the false papers that got a fourteen-year-old girl out of a burning apprenticeship and into a life of hiding.

And apparently had also written, in binding-script older than the city, a contract of sale.

"Tell me what it says," Dain said. "You read it better than I do. I get maybe one word in five."

"That's about my rate too." Lena laughed, and hated how thin it came out. "Binding-script isn't really read. It's more... weighed. You feel the shape of the bargain." She traced the air above the rubbing without touching it. "There's a party of the first part—that's the mountain side, the wardens' founders, whoever cut these stones. There's a party of the second part." Her finger stopped. "That column's been deliberately effaced. Scratched out. But not recently. Recently enough that the scratches are fresh."

"When?"

"Within the last few years. Maybe fifteen." She swallowed. "Maybe less."

"And the terms?"

Lena closed her eyes. She let the shapes sink in, the way Odile had taught her to let a charm-sink before judging it—don't fight it, just feel its weight. The contract was simple, as great bargains usually are. Terribly simple.

*"Flame perpetual,"* she recited, "from the heart of the sleeper beneath Sarn, to the stones of the mark, unbroken, in exchange for"—her voice caught—"for passage. For safe conduct. For one soul's passage out of reach of the Court, guaranteed in perpetuity, the debt transferred to the fire itself."

The cellar was silent. The candle lay where it had fallen. Dain didn't move.

"One soul," he said.

"One soul," Lena confirmed. "Sold below. To the Undercourt. And the price of the sale was—" She opened her eyes. "The price was the wards. Don't you see? It's not that someone stole the fire. Someone *spent* it. The whole border, two hundred years of protection, millions of people inside it—it was all collateral on one transaction. One person's escape. And when the debt came due, the Court collected."

"Whose escape?"

She couldn't answer. Her mouth formed the word and nothing came out.

Because there was only one transaction of that size in her entire world. Only one escape bought with impossible currency. Only one person who would have known how to write binding-script, who had access to the old forms, who had spent years teaching a frightened apprentice that the safest magic was no magic at all while all along—

"All my life," Lena said, "she told me never to be big. Never to be seen. Never to matter. I thought it was fear. I thought she was a coward, and I resented her for it, and then I thanked her for it, because the fear kept me alive." Her voice cracked down the middle. "It wasn't fear. It was *accounting*. She knew exactly how much I was worth. Because she'd already sold something to buy me, and she knew the bill would come due someday, and she built her whole life—built *my* whole life—around making sure I'd be small enough, hidden enough, forgettable enough that the collectors would never find me."

"Find *you*?" Dain leaned forward. "Amberdale. Whose soul was sold?"

"I don't know." It was almost true. It was true in the way that a locked door is closed. "Fifteen years ago Odile got me out of the apprenticeship. Out of a bad situation. I never asked how—I was fourteen and I was running and I didn't ask. And now there's a contract in the foundation of the world with her handwriting on it, and the payment was one soul, and the goods protected were 'the girl,' and I am the girl, Dain. I'm the only girl she ever—"

She stopped. Pressed both hands over her mouth. Breathed.

The horror wasn't the sale. She could survive the sale; she'd survived worse things done to her by people who claimed to love her. The horror was the arithmetic. Every warm meal she'd eaten in fifteen years of hiding. Every market day, every luck-knot sold, every night she'd slept unseen and unfound. All of it purchased. All of it *financed*. And the interest had come due last night, and the payment had been the stars, and the sky over every farm and fishing village on the Vellamark had gone dark so that one girl could slip out of one door fifteen years ago.

Somebody's soul. Somebody's whole self, burning down below in whatever the Undercourt did with the things it bought, keeping a dragon's heart lit—or failing to—and she had never once, not in fifteen years of careful smallness, paid a single coin toward the debt.

"I never wanted this," she whispered.

"I know."

"I never *asked* for—she should have told me. She should have let me choose. You don't get to buy someone's freedom with someone else's *self*, that's not rescue, that's just—" Her hands dropped. "That's just the Court with kinder handwriting."

Dain was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had changed—rougher, and gentler underneath the roughness.

"My sister bore your sigil," he said. "Its twin. She died eight years ago, in the purge after the outlawry, and I have spent eight years trying to work out whether the mark killed her or failed to save her, and I have never once managed to make the question come out clean." He picked up the candle stub and set it upright between them, an offering of small light neither of them lit. "So I won't tell you it wasn't your fault, because you wouldn't believe me yet, and because some of it probably is, the way some of mine is mine. What I'll tell you instead is this: debts like this don't care whose fault it is. They just collect. And right now the collection is a dying dragon under Mount Sarn and a border that's open to everything outside it, and you are the only living person who can read the contract."

Lena looked at the rubbing. At the hooked terminal strokes. At the door-shaped knot at the center of the sigil, the one that matched the scar on her palm, the one that matched the scar on a dead woman's palm, the one that had apparently been a *key* all along.

"She's dead," Lena said. "Odile. Three years now. I went back to the cottage once, after. I couldn't make myself go inside." She looked up. "The cottage is still standing. On the moor road, past Harrow Ford. Nobody's touched it—people said it was unlucky."

Understanding moved across Dain's face slowly, like weather.

"You want to see the rest of the ledger."

"I want to know what we owe," Lena said. "All of it. Down to the last line." She picked up the candle stub and finally, with a word so small it was barely a whisper, lit it. The flame steadied. It was a tiny thing, a hedge-witch's trick, the kind of magic that had gotten her craft outlawed—and after the night the sky had gone dark, it felt obscene and necessary at once, like singing at a funeral.

"If the fire under Sarn was spent buying my passage," she said, "then somewhere in that house is the rest of the account. What she sold. What it cost. Who paid." She stood, knees stiff, fifteen years of running settled suddenly into her bones like cold rain. "And if the answer is what I think it is—"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to. Dain had seen the same shape in the contract's effaced column that she had: a name scratched out, but the scratch-marks fresh, recent, deliberate. Someone had tried to erase the debtor's identity from the stone itself.

Someone had failed.

"Get your coat," Dain said, rising. "Harrow Ford is two days' walk, and the roads out of this city are about to be full of wardens looking for a charm-seller and a disgraced warden." He paused at the foot of the stairs, the grey light catching the faded sigil on his palm. "And Amberdale—if there's a ledger in that house, and if your name is in it—"

"It's in it," Lena said. "It's been in it since before I could write."

* * *

They left Greyharbor before dawn through the drainage culvert, and the city behind them was a smear of torchlight and shouting, wardens sweeping street by street for someone to blame. Ahead, past the last farms, the moor rose black under a starless sky, and far to the north—so faint it might have been imagination—a dull red glow pulsed above the shoulder of Mount Sarn.

Like a heartbeat. Like a heartbeat skipping.

Lena walked with her branded fist closed tight against her chest, and did not look back.
