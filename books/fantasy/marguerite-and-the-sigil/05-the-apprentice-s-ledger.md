## 5. The Apprentice's Ledger

The cottage was exactly where memory said it would be, which was the first wrong thing.

Fifteen years of weather should have taken something—a roof tile, a shutter, a corner of the chimney. But Odile Vasse's house stood at the end of the goat-track above Thistlefen as if it had been waiting, slate roof whole, garden gone to seed in an orderly way, as though even the weeds had been instructed not to make a mess. The door was unlocked. Of course it was. Odile had never locked anything in her life; she said locks were a confession that you feared what you deserved.

Lena stood on the threshold for a long time, one hand flat against the frame, and let herself remember the last morning she'd stood here.

* * *

She was nineteen and furious, and fury made her brave in the way it always does—the way it makes you mistake momentum for courage.

"You can't keep me here," she'd said. "You can't keep any of us. You split Tamsin's shadow to keep her quiet when she wanted to leave, and now she doesn't want anything at all. She just sits by the fire and smiles at nothing."

Odile hadn't looked up from her work. She was grinding something in the stone mortar—iron shavings and dried rue, the smell sharp enough to sting the eyes—and her hands moved with the terrible calm they always had, like water finding its level. "Tamsin is safer," she said. "Wanting things is how the Court finds you. Wanting is a scent trail, girl. I've cut yours short, and you should thank me."

"I don't want to be safe. I want to be *real*."

That got her attention. Odile set down the mortar and looked at her apprentice across the worktable, and for a moment—one long, unguarded moment—there was something in her face that wasn't coldness at all. It looked almost like grief wearing coldness as a coat.

"Real costs more than you have," Odile said quietly. "Go to bed, Lena. In the morning we'll speak about your future."

In the morning there had been a purse of coin on the kitchen table, a road-pass written in a hand too fine to be Odile's, and a single instruction: *Walk south until someone asks your name. Then lie.*

Lena had walked south. She had lied beautifully for fifteen years.

* * *

Now she pushed the door open and stepped into the smell of dust and dried herbs and old smoke, and the past closed over her head like river water.

The main room was untouched. The worktable still bore the grooves where Odile had carved sigils into wood for practice, hundreds of them, overlapping until the surface was less table than palimpsest. The hearth was swept clean. On the mantel sat the little clay figure Lena had made at eleven years old—a lopsided woman with no face—and beside it, a jar of buttons, and a candle burned down to a puddle.

One candle. Burned out. Not snuffed.

Someone had sat in this room after Lena left, working late by candlelight, and then simply stopped. The wax had pooled and hardened around the wick like a small grey heart.

"Don't touch anything yet," Lena told herself aloud, because saying it made it a rule, and rules were how she kept her hands steady. "Look first. Touch second. That's how she taught you."

So she looked.

The cottage had two rooms downstairs and a loft. The bedroom was bare—bed stripped, wardrobe open, drawers pulled out and left hanging like tongues. Odile had left in a hurry, or been taken, or simply decided one day to stop existing in this place. But the workroom behind the kitchen was different. The workroom had been *tidied*. Things squared away. Books shelved spine-out in the old order: histories, herbals, then the thin dangerous volumes wrapped in oilcloth at the back where a child's reach wouldn't find them.

Lena had found those books at thirteen. She had read them all. She had never told Odile, and Odile had never mentioned the faint disturbance in their wrapping, and that mutual silence was, looking back, the truest conversation they ever had.

She started with the books, because books were comfortable, and comfort was a way of delaying. She checked each shelf for hollows, false backs, loose stones. Nothing. The hearth next—ash sifted through her fingers, cold for years. Under the floorboards beneath the worktable, where she'd once hidden a stolen orange and been beaten for it—not beaten, corrected, Odile didn't beat, Odile did worse things with words—but under those boards there was only earth and one very indignant beetle.

It was the loft, then. It was always going to be the loft. She'd known since she climbed the ladder, but knowing hadn't made her feet move any faster.

* * *

The loft had been hers. Narrow cot, narrow window, a shelf of treasures accumulated the way children accumulate: a bird skull, three river stones with holes through them, a ribbon won at a fair she wasn't supposed to attend. All of it still there, furred with dust, arranged as if its owner might come back and need it.

And on the desk beneath the window, square and patient as a cat, the ledger.

Lena knew that ledger. Every apprentice knew it. It was the great black book with the brass corners in which Odile recorded every transaction of her craft—who paid what, in coin or favor or stranger currency, and what was given in return. As a child Lena had been forbidden to read it, which meant she had read it, cover to cover, twice. It was full of small cruelties rendered in beautiful script. *To the miller's wife, a charm against miscarriage; received, silence regarding the visit.* *To the reeve of Thistlefen, a forgetting; received, his discretion and his wife's ring.* Page after page of a woman buying safety with other people's needs.

But fifteen years ago, the ledger had ended mid-page. Lena remembered that. The last entry she'd ever seen was dated the spring before she ran, and it was mundane: *To the cooper, mending of a quarrel; received, two barrels, nails.* After that, blank pages. She had assumed the book was simply finished, retired, replaced by some newer record she'd never seen.

The ledger on the desk was open to pages she had never seen.

Her hands were shaking before she touched it. She pressed them flat against her thighs and counted—Odile's trick, four counts in, four counts out—and then she picked up the book and carried it to the window, because the light was better there, and because she needed to do something ordinary with her body while her mind went somewhere terrible.

The new entries began the week after Lena fled.

*Sold: passage south for the girl. Received: —*

No. That wasn't right either. She read it again, and again, and the words refused to rearrange themselves into anything kinder.

*Received from the Undercourt, by broker's hand: passage for the girl, unmarked roads, unwitnessed. Price tendered below.*

Below. Below was a word with weight in Odile's vocabulary. Below was the mountain's roots, the green-glass doors, the court that predated the wards and would outlast them. Below was the place mothers invoked to frighten children and witches invoked to frighten each other, except Odile had never frightened easily, so what had she been doing writing *below* in her careful hand?

Lena turned the page.

There was a second entry, dated three days later, and it was shorter, and her eyes snagged on it the way a thumb snags on a knife-edge.

*Paid to the Undercourt, in settlement of the above: one soul, whole and entire. Source: —*

Source. The word hung there with a blank after it, and the blank was the worst thing Lena had ever seen, because Odile recorded everything, always, down to the ring off a reeve's wife's finger, and here, on the single most important transaction of her life, she had left a space empty. Either she hadn't known whose soul she was selling.

Or she had known, and couldn't write it.

Or—Lena's mind supplied the third option with the gentle precision of a surgeon—she had known, and had written it elsewhere, and destroyed that page, and left this one blank on purpose, so that anyone who came looking later would understand everything except the one thing that mattered.

Lena sat down on the narrow cot. The dust rose around her in a soft grey bloom, and she breathed it, and it smelled like being thirteen, like the bird skull and the holed stones, like a childhood spent learning magic from a woman who loved her in the way that a dam loves a valley—by holding it, and by drowning it.

"One soul," she said to the empty loft. "Whole and entire."

Whose?

Not Odile's own. A witch's soul was her working capital; Odile would sooner sell her hands. Not Tamsin's—Tamsin had still been smiling at fires years afterward, hollowed but present, present in the way that mattered to the ledger's arithmetic. Some other soul, then. Some stranger bought cheap. Some criminal or dying man or—

Or the girl.

The thought arrived without permission, the way the worst thoughts do, fully dressed and holding out its hand. *Price of passage for 'the girl.' Paid: one soul.* What if the entries weren't two lines of one bargain? What if they were a loop? What if Odile had sold passage for the girl *paid by* the girl—had taken the payment out of the same account she was spending it from, a sleight of hand so elegant it was almost beautiful, splitting her apprentice's soul the way she'd split Tamsin's shadow, except this time she'd sold half of it below to buy the other half's freedom?

Half a soul could pass the Court's roads without challenge. Half a soul cast no full shadow for the wardens' hounds to read. Half a soul could hide in Greyharbor for fifteen years selling luck-knots and fever-charms and never once be remembered, because there was less of her to remember.

Lena looked down at her palm.

The sigil sat there in its old brown scar-tissue, the brand Odile had pressed into her at apprenticeship, the mark she'd spent fifteen years hiding under wraps and gloves and lies. She had always thought of it as a leash. A claim. *This girl is mine,* it said, in the grammar of the old craft.

But leashes run both ways. And brands are how you mark property that's about to change hands.

"Oh," Lena said. Very quietly. To the bird skull, to the dust, to the dead candle on the mantel below. "Oh, you *bitch*. You brilliant, cowardly, magnificent bitch."

She was crying. She noticed it the way you notice rain—distantly, as a fact about the weather. Fifteen years of freedom, and it had been purchased. Every market day, every narrow escape, every night she'd slept sound in a rented room because no one was hunting her—that had all been bought with a piece of herself she'd never missed, because how do you miss the part of you that's gone? You don't. You just feel lighter than everyone else, and call it luck.

She thought of the customers she'd turned away. The woman with the sick child she'd refused because the child's case was too big, too memorable. The old soldier who'd begged for a charm for his nightmares and gotten nothing but a warning to see a physician in Vellamark. All those years of *never big magic, never twice, never be remembered*, and she had told herself it was discipline. Craft. Wisdom.

It was rationing. She had been rationing herself, because some deep animal part of her had always known there wasn't as much of her as there should be.

* * *

She didn't know how long she sat there. The light through the loft window moved from gold to copper, and the dust settled, and eventually the crying stopped the way weather stops, not because anything resolved but because the sky ran out.

Then she got up, because grief was a luxury and the stars had gone out two nights ago, and whatever else the ledger meant, it meant Odile had dealt with the Undercourt, and the Undercourt was the reason the horizon had shuddered like a struck bell.

She took the ledger. She took the road-pass with its too-fine handwriting—she'd left it behind at nineteen in a gesture of independence, and Odile had kept it, of course she had, Odile kept everything—and she took the clay figure with no face, because some childish, unforgivable part of her needed to.

At the top of the ladder she paused. One last look.

The cot. The window. The shelf of treasures. And there—she hadn't seen it before, or hadn't let herself—tucked behind the bird skull, a folded scrap of paper gone soft with age. She opened it with fingers that had finally stopped shaking.

It was in Odile's hand, and it was five words long:

*If you're reading this: below.*

Below. The mountain. The green doors. Whatever waited under Mount Sarn with a debt fifteen years past due.

Lena put the note in her pocket next to the ledger, climbed down, and walked out of the cottage without closing the door. Let the weather take it now. Let the weeds make a mess. There was no one left to hide from, and no one left to hide.

Behind her, far to the north, Mount Sarn breathed its slow smoke into a starless sky, and somewhere between the mountain and the sea, a disgraced warden was walking toward Thistlefen with questions that had her name at the bottom of them.

She felt him coming the way you feel weather. And for the first time in fifteen years, Lena Amberdale did not start planning how to disappear.

She started planning what she would say.
