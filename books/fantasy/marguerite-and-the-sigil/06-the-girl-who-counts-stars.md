## 6. The Girl Who Counts Stars

The child had been following Lena for three days before I worked up the nerve to speak to her.

I'd been watching from my spot under the collapsed awning of what used to be a chandler's shop, back when Greyharbor had shops instead of ruins and chandlers instead of ash. Three days. The child moved like smoke — always at the edge of vision, never quite committing to being seen. But I saw her. I saw everything now that there was nothing left to see up in the dark.

"The Swan," she whispered, counting on filthy fingers. "The Swan, the Shepherd's Crook, the Nine Sisters, the—"

She stopped. Her mouth kept moving but no sound came out, because everyone knew the next one. Everyone had known it since they were small enough to be carried to a window and pointed at the sky.

*The Lantern-Bearer.* Gone. All of them gone, but that one worst of all, because my mother used to say the Lantern-Bearer was the star that watched over children who couldn't sleep, and I was eight years old when it went out, and I hadn't slept properly since.

My name is Wren. I'm ten years old, or maybe eleven — it's hard to keep count when the years all look like each other. And three days ago, when the sky went black and the whole city started screaming, I made myself a promise: I would find out why. And then the woman with the burned records came walking through the market ruins like she knew exactly where she was going, and I thought: *her*. She knows. She has to know. Nobody walks that steady through an ending unless they helped build it.

So I followed her.

* * *

Here is what I remember about magic, and you can believe me or not, most people don't:

Before the outlawry, magic wasn't a crime. It was a chore. My mother did charms the way other mothers did mending — sitting by the fire of an evening, knotting thread for luck, murmuring fevers down out of crying babies. The neighbors brought her eggs and bread for it. Once, a warden came to our door, and I hid under the table because I thought we were in trouble, and my mother laughed and gave him a charm for his rheumatic knee and he paid her in copper and tipped his hat on the way out.

That was before. Before the Edict, before the burning years, before they took my mother away in a cart with her hands tied and told me she'd been sent somewhere for retraining. I waited by the window for two years. Nobody came back.

So when I say I remember magic, I don't mean I remember spells. I mean I remember a world where a woman could be kind with her hands and nobody called it witchcraft. That world went dark the same night the stars did, and I want it back, and I will follow anyone who might know the road.

Even a stranger who breaks every rule I've ever seen her follow.

* * *

On the third day, the woman — Lena, though I didn't know her name yet — stopped at the mouth of the old fish-market lane and stood very still, the way a deer stands when it knows something is behind it.

"You've been following me," she said, without turning around. "Three days."

My heart slammed against my ribs. I'd been careful. I'd been so careful, downwind, in shadow, matching her pace.

"I count," I said, because it was the truest thing in me. "You stop at corners. You check sightlines. You sleep sitting up, never lying down. You're running from something."

Now she turned. She was thinner than I'd guessed, with shadows under her eyes deep enough to drown in, and her right hand stayed closed like it was holding something precious or terrible. She looked at me the way people look at strays — with pity first, and then calculation, and then something worse: recognition.

"Where are your parents?"

"Where are yours?" I shot back. It was rude. Mother would have boxed my ears. But I'd learned that adults ask questions like that to make you feel small, and I refused to feel small ever again.

Something flickered across her face. Not anger. Something more tired than anger.

"It isn't safe to follow strangers," she said. "Especially now. Especially me."

"Why especially you?"

She didn't answer. She walked on. I counted to fifty and followed.

* * *

That evening she made camp in the shell of a granary, high enough up that you could see the roads approaching, and I settled into my own hiding place thirty feet below and ate the heel of bread I'd been saving. It was stale enough to hurt my teeth. I made it last as long as I could, which wasn't long, because I hadn't eaten properly since the wards fell and the markets scattered and the world turned upside down.

And then, because I was hungry and angry and tired of being invisible, I did the thing I'd been doing every night since the stars went out. I lay on my back in the broken doorway and I recited.

"The Swan. The Shepherd's Crook. The Nine Sisters. The Miller's Wheel. The Lantern-Bearer. The—" my voice cracked, and I hated it, and I pushed through anyway, "—the Drowned King. The Seven Bells. The Long Road Home."

Silence above me. Then footsteps, quick ones, and suddenly she was standing over me in the dying light, and her face was doing something complicated.

"Where did you learn those?"

"My mother." I sat up. "Before."

"Before," she repeated, flat. "How old are you, child?"

"Wren. And old enough."

"The outlawry was fifteen years ago. You can't be more than—"

"Ten. Maybe eleven." I lifted my chin. "But I remember. I remember my mother knotting luck-threads. I remember the warden who bought a charm for his knee and thanked her for it. I remember what it was like when magic was just a thing people did, like baking, like mending. Do you?" My voice shook and I let it. "Do you remember what it was like before?"

For a long moment she just looked at me, and I watched her face do battle with itself — the part that wanted to send me away fighting the part that remembered too. I'd seen that look before, on other survivors, in the early years. It was the look of someone deciding whether hope was worth the cost of carrying it.

"You shouldn't recite them," she said finally. "The constellations. Not here. Not now. People hear a child naming the old stars and they think *witch-touched*, and witch-touched children disappear."

"They already disappeared," I said. "All of them. Up there." I pointed at the empty black. "What else can they take?"

She flinched. Actually flinched, like I'd struck her. And I understood, in the way children understand things adults think they're hiding, that I'd said something true and terrible, that the stars weren't just gone but *taken*, and that this woman knew how, or knew who, or knew why.

"You're going after them," I said. "Aren't you. Whatever took them. You're going after it."

"That's none of your business."

"Make it my business."

"*No.*"

The word cracked out of her like a whip, louder than she meant it to be, loud enough to echo off the granary walls. Then she pressed her lips together and looked away, and when she spoke again her voice was low and rough.

"You don't know what you're asking. You don't know what it costs. Go home, Wren. Find someone to take you in. Forget you ever saw me."

"I don't have a home," I said. "I have a window I sat at for two years waiting for my mother to come back. I have a city full of people who'd turn me in for a loaf of bread if they knew I remembered the old names. And I have—" my voice broke again, and this time I let it break all the way, because if I couldn't be honest with the one person who might understand, then honesty was dead in this world, "—I have nothing. So either you take me with you, or you leave me here to starve, but I am not going to forget. Forgetting is the only thing I refuse to do."

* * *

She stood there for a long time. The wind moved through the granary, stirring ash. Somewhere far off, a warden's horn sounded, and we both went still until it faded.

Then she crouched down to my level, which grown-ups almost never do, and studied my face like she was memorizing it. Her right hand opened slightly, and I saw — just for a second — a mark on her palm. A sigil, old and strange, faintly warm-looking even in the cold light.

"Where did you get that?" I breathed.

"Nowhere you need to know about." The hand closed again. "Wren. If I feed you tonight — one meal, that's all — will you go? Will you find the relief houses, the wardens' charity line, anywhere with walls and adults?"

"No."

"If I feed you, will you at least stop following me?"

"No."

She exhaled through her nose. Almost, almost a laugh. "You're the most stubborn creature I've met in fifteen years, and I once haggled with a smuggler over a cursed ring for six hours."

"He sounds like he lost."

"He did." She looked at me a long moment more, and then she reached into her pack and pulled out bread, real bread, soft, and hard cheese, and an apple that made my mouth flood just looking at it. She set them down between us on the dusty floor. "One meal. Eat slowly or you'll make yourself sick."

I tried to eat slowly. I failed. The apple I saved, cradling it in both hands like the treasure it was, because apples were a memory-food, an before-food, and some part of me wanted to hold onto it forever.

She watched me eat with an expression I couldn't read — hunger of a different kind, maybe, the kind that comes from watching someone else be fed. When I finished, she didn't tell me to leave. She just sat down beside me, stiffly, like her body had forgotten how, and stared up at the empty sky through the broken roof.

"The Lantern-Bearer," she said quietly. "My mistress used to point it out. Said it was the first star witches learned to find, because it never lied about where north was."

"Mistress?" I turned to look at her. "You had a teacher? A real one?"

"A long time ago." Her jaw tightened. "She's dead now. Or she may as well be."

"What was her name?"

A pause so long I thought she wouldn't answer.

"Odile," she said at last, and the name came out of her like a splinter coming out of skin — painful, and necessary, and leaving something cleaner behind.

"Odile," I repeated, tasting it. And then, because I was full of bread and cheese and courage: "Teach me something. One small thing. A knot, a word, anything. Please. I'll keep it secret, I swear it, I'll never use it where anyone can see, I just — I can't carry nothing anymore. Nothing is too heavy."

She looked at me sharply. "*Nothing* is heavy?"

"Carrying nothing. Yes. It's the heaviest thing there is."

For a moment I thought she'd refuse. I braced for it — the sensible refusal, the safe refusal, the refusal that would leave me alone in the dark with an apple and a memory. She was already turning away, already closing herself back up into whatever shape she'd built to survive.

But then she paused. And her voice, when it came, was so quiet I had to lean close to hear it.

"Hold out your hands."

I held them out. She took a length of plain twine from her pocket — no charm-thread, just ordinary string — and looped it once, twice, crossing the ends.

"This isn't magic," she said. "Not really. It's older than magic. It's remembering. You tie it like this—" her fingers moved, quick and sure, "—and while you tie it, you think of someone you want kept safe. That's all. The knot holds the thinking. Sailors did it for their wives. Mothers did it for their sons at war. No law ever banned it, because no law could see it."

"And it works?"

"It works because believing it works keeps the person in your mind, and people who are kept in mind are harder to lose." She tied the final loop and pressed the little knot into my palm, folding my fingers over it. "There. Now you're carrying something."

I looked down at the knot. It was nothing. Twine and fingers and a thought. It was everything.

"For my mother," I whispered.

"For your mother," she agreed softly.

We sat there in the ruined granary while the wind moaned through the broken roof, and the empty sky hung over us like a wound that wouldn't close, and I held my knot and she held her closed hand with its secret mark, and neither of us said anything for a long time.

Then, far off across the rooftops, a bell began to toll — not the warden's alarm, but the deep old bell from the harbor tower that nobody had rung in years. Once. Twice. Three times.

Lena went rigid beside me.

"That's not possible," she whispered. "That bell's been silent since the outlawry."

"What does three tolls mean?" I asked. "In the old days?"

She was already on her feet, packing, moving fast, and when she answered me her voice had gone cold and certain, the voice of someone whose past had just come over the horizon.

"In the old days," she said, slinging her pack across her shoulder, "it meant the border wards had fallen. Somebody's ringing it because they've only just realized what happened five nights ago — or because something's coming that they think the wards should have stopped." She looked down at me, and I saw her make a decision, and I saw her hate herself for making it. "Can you run, Wren?"

I was on my feet before she finished asking.

"I can run," I said.

"Then stay behind me, keep your mouth shut about the stars, and don't let go of that knot." She was already moving toward the stair, toward the dark, toward whatever was coming. "Because I have a feeling, little bird, that the rules just changed for both of us."

I tucked the knot into my shirt, against my heart, and ran after her into the night.
