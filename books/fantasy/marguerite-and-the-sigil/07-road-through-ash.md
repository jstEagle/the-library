## 7. Road Through Ash

The border road had been dead for fifteen years, and it smelled like it.

Lena felt the smell before she saw the road itself—a sweetness under the rot, like fruit left too long in a closed room. Old magic going bad. The wards that had once run along this stretch of the Vellamark frontier had been fed on dragon-fire, and now that the fire was failing, whatever they'd held in check was leaking through the seams.

"Keep to the center," Dain said behind her. "The stones at the edges are boundary markers. Things gather there."

"What kind of things?"

"The kind we don't stop to look at."

Wren walked between them, small hand hooked into the strap of Lena's pack like she'd been doing it all her life instead of two days. The girl hadn't spoken since dawn except to count stars that weren't there—*the Weaver's Loom, the Seven Sisters of the Ford, the Long Antler*—reciting constellations into a sky gone blank as a scrubbed slate. It should have been eerie. Instead it was the only sound keeping Lena's thoughts from spiraling.

She checked over her shoulder for the hundredth time. Greyharbor was a smudge of smoke behind them. No wardens visible yet. That meant nothing. Wardens traveled quiet when they wanted to.

"How far?" she asked.

Dain didn't break stride. He was built for this road, she was realizing—all long bones and weathered patience, his disgraced warden's coat dyed black so the old silver trim wouldn't catch light. "Three days to Mount Sarn if the road holds. Four if it doesn't."

"And if the things at the edges find us?"

"Then we'll wish it were wardens."

* * *

They made camp that first night in the shell of a waystation, one of the old relay posts where wardens had once kept the beacon-fires burning along the frontier. The roof was half-gone. Someone had lived here recently—there were ash rings where cookfires had been and a child's carved wooden bird on the windowsill, left behind like an apology.

Lena built the smallest fire she could, screened by stones, and rationed out dried plums and hard bread. Wren ate like a small animal, hunched over her portion, watching both of them between bites.

"You can slow down," Lena said. "No one's taking it."

Wren didn't slow down. But after a while she said, "You eat last."

"I'm not hungry."

"That's a lie." The girl said it without accusation, the way she said everything, like reporting weather. "You gave me your share yesterday too. And the day before."

Lena looked at Dain for rescue. He was cleaning his boots with a strip of rag and pretending very hard not to exist.

"Eat your plums," Lena told Wren.

Wren ate her plums. Then, wiping her hands on her knees, she asked the question Lena had been dreading. "Why are we going to the mountain?"

Because my dead mistress sold a soul to buy my escape, and I think the soul might be mine, and the whole world is coming apart because of what Odile did before I could even write my own name properly. Because I've spent fifteen years being small and careful and it bought nothing but more running.

"Someone has to fix what's broken," Lena said.

"Is it you?"

"It might be."

Wren considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Good," she said finally. "I'm tired of the dark."

She curled up against Lena's pack and was asleep within minutes, starved body surrendering the moment it felt safe. Lena sat looking at her—at the hollows under her eyes, the chapped lips, the constellation-names still moving faintly behind her eyelids—and felt something in her chest shift like a joist settling.

*Don't*, she warned herself. *You know how this goes. You keep people at arm's length so that when you run again, you leave nothing behind.*

But she was already leaving things behind. She'd been leaving them since the night the stars went out.

* * *

On the second day, Dain began teaching them signs.

He did it without announcing it, which Lena would learn was his way. They were walking a stretch where the road narrowed between leaning marker-stones, and he simply raised one hand—two fingers folded, thumb across the palm—and held it there until they noticed.

"When a warden shows you this," he said, "it means *hold*. Stop moving, stop talking. Movement draws them; sound draws them faster."

"Draws what?" Lena asked.

"Anything hunting. Show me."

She copied the sign. Her fingers were wrong twice—he corrected her grip with a brief, impersonal touch, the way you'd correct a recruit—and then he nodded once and moved on, as if she'd passed some examination he hadn't officially given.

Through the morning he taught them others. Fist against sternum: *danger behind*. Flat hand slicing down: *down, now*. Two fingers walking across the opposite palm: *count your people*. That one he made Wren practice five times, because the first four she did it laughing, and the fifth time she did it right, and something in Dain's face eased that hadn't been easy since Lena met him.

"Why teach us this?" Lena asked during a water break. "You're not a warden anymore. They took your coat, your rank, your—" she gestured vaguely at everything, "—your life."

"They took the title." He screwed the cap back onto his waterskin. "The training stays. And the training says: a warden alone is a corpse with a schedule. A warden with people who know the signs is a unit."

"We're not wardens."

"No." He looked at Wren, asleep sitting up against a stone, then at Lena. "We're worse. We're three people nobody's coming to save. So we save each other or we don't get saved."

It wasn't sentiment. It was arithmetic. Lena found she liked him better for that.

* * *

The hollow-eyed things came at dusk on the second day.

Lena heard them before she saw them—or rather, she heard the absence of them. The wind died. The insects that had been rasping in the dry grass along the road cut off all at once, like a door closing on a noisy room. Dain's hand came up: *hold*. Then flat, slicing: *down*.

They dropped into the ditch beside the road. Lena pulled Wren beneath her, pressing the girl's face into her shoulder, and looked up through the tangle of dead bramble.

They came along the tops of the boundary stones. That was the worst part—not that they moved wrong, though they did, flowing like poured shadow—but that they used the old ward-stones as a road. Things that had been locked outside the wards for centuries, now walking the very architecture that had excluded them, because the architecture no longer meant anything.

There were seven of them. Maybe eight. Tall, thin, wrong-jointed, with faces like unfinished sculptures—features suggested rather than committed to. Their eyes were holes. Not dark sockets. Holes, as if someone had pushed a finger through wet clay.

"They're crossing the ford stones," Dain breathed. "That puts them on our side of the river."

One of them stopped. Turned its head—the motion smooth as oil—in their direction.

And Lena understood, with cold certainty, what it was listening for. Not sound. Not movement.

Her.

The sigil on her palm had been warm all day. She'd been telling herself it was the sun. Now it pulsed once, unmistakable, a heartbeat in her skin, and the creature's head tilted like a dog catching scent.

"Warden Corvess," it said.

Its voice was like gravel poured slowly. Dain went rigid beside her.

"It knows your name," Lena whispered.

"They remember everyone who ever held the line." His jaw was tight. "I held it eleven years. Apparently that buys recognition."

The thing took a step toward the ditch. Another joined it. Then another.

And Wren lifted her head from Lena's shoulder, looked at the creatures with her starless-sky eyes, and said, clearly and calmly:

"Hollow-men. You're hollow-men. You lost your names when the fire went out."

The creatures stopped.

All of them. Mid-stride, mid-lean, frozen like puppets with cut strings. The nearest one's hole-eyes fixed on the small girl in the ditch, and its unfinished mouth worked, opening and closing, as if the words she'd given it were rattling around inside looking for somewhere to land.

"Hollow-men," it repeated. Its voice cracked on the second word. "Hollow... men."

"What did you do?" Dain breathed.

"Named them." Wren shrugged against Lena's arm, casual as anything. "Mama taught me. Before. You name a thing before it names itself, and it can't be worse than its name. Hollow-men aren't so bad. Hollow-men just want their insides back."

The creatures stood swaying in the dying light, murmuring their new name to each other, passing it along like a cup of water. *Hollow-men. Hollow-men.* And then, one by one, they turned away from the ditch and flowed onward along the stones, westward, away from Mount Sarn, murmuring.

Lena lay in the bramble with her heart slamming and stared at the child in her arms.

"Your mother taught you that."

"Mm." Wren settled back down, unbothered, already halfway to sleep. "She said it was older than the wards. Older than the dragons, maybe. Naming's the first magic. Everything else is just naming louder."

Dain caught Lena's eye across the ditch. In the last grey light she saw something in his face she hadn't seen before—not fear, not calculation. Wonder. The look of a man whose maps had just been redrawn.

"Where's your mother now?" Lena asked quietly.

Wren was silent long enough that Lena thought she'd fallen asleep.

"Don't know," the girl said at last. "She went to the mountain. Said somebody had to remember the old words where the fire lives." A pause. "That was before the stars went out."

Lena lay awake long after the child slept, watching the blank sky, turning that over. Somebody had to remember the old words where the fire lives. Somewhere under Mount Sarn, maybe, a woman who named monsters was waiting—or had waited, or had failed. The world was full of mothers who went toward the fire. Hers had sold a soul and run the other way.

Or so the ledger said. Ledgers lied. People lied. Fifteen years of hiding had taught her that much.

* * *

On the third day, the wardens found them.

Not with swords—with a horn. One long note rolling down the valley behind them, answered by another ahead, and Lena's stomach dropped because the answers were converging. They'd been funneled. All day, she realized, the road had been narrowing them, bending them east, and she'd thought it was the landscape.

"Off the road," Dain snapped, all warden now, voice stripped to command. "Tree line, now. Wren—sign."

Wren's fist hit her sternum: *danger behind*. She'd learned them all overnight, practicing in the dirt with a stick while the adults slept. Lena grabbed the girl's hand and ran.

The trees were black pine, close and dry, and they crashed through them while the horns spoke to each other across the valley. Behind them, on the road, hoofbeats. Ahead—

Ahead, at the edge of her vision, between the trunks: movement. Tall, thin, wrong-jointed.

More hollow-men. Dozens. Standing among the pines like a congregation, all facing the same direction. Facing the mountain. Not hunting. Waiting. Migrating, maybe, the way birds migrate, drawn by whatever call the broken wards had loosed into the world.

Except the ones nearest them turned. Slowly. Hole-eyes finding the warmth of living bodies.

"The sigil," Dain gritted, back to back with her, knife out. "It's ringing like a dinner bell."

"I know." Lena's palm burned. "I know."

The horns were closer. The hollow-men were closer. Trapped between the law and the lawless, and both of them hungry for her.

Then Wren stepped out in front of them both, planted her feet in the pine needles, and threw her arms wide.

"Hollow-men!" she shouted, in a voice far too big for her body, a voice that rang against the trees like a struck bell. "Your name is hollow-men! Your name is hollow-men! Your name is hollow-men!"

The word rolled out through the forest, and the forest answered. Every standing shape shuddered. The murmur started at the front of the congregation and rippled backward, outward, dozens of ruined voices picking up the name and passing it hand to hand like a flame being shared—

*Hollow-men. Hollow-men. Hollow-men.*

—and the line broke. Not fleeing. Turning. Flowing past them on both sides, close enough to touch, close enough to smell—cold stone and old rain—ignoring them utterly now, named creatures with somewhere better to be. The pressure of them washed over Lena like a river around a stone.

Through the gaps in the black shapes she saw the wardens burst onto the scene—six riders, silver-trimmed coats, crossbows leveled—and stop dead, because there was nothing left to shoot. Just a disgraced warden, a charm-seller, and a small girl standing in a clearing full of departing shadows.

For one suspended moment, everyone stared at everyone.

Then the lead rider pointed at Lena and shouted, "That's her! The branded witch—that's the one the Regent's warrant wants alive!" and the moment shattered, and they ran.

* * *

They ran until dark, until the horns faded behind them, until Wren stumbled twice and Lena carried her the third mile on her back, the girl's cheek against her shoulder, whispering constellation-names like a prayer. When they finally stopped—in a ravine where a stream cut down toward the plains, water loud enough to drown the sound of pursuit—Lena set the child down and put her hands on her knees and shook.

Dain crouched at the stream's edge, washing blood from a scrape on his forearm, and looked up at her.

"You understand what happened back there," he said. "The wardens will report it. A witch who commands the hollow-things with a word. The Regent's warrant wants you alive, which means someone below the mountain knows exactly what you are and has told the wardens what you're worth." He wrung out the rag. "You're not running from the law anymore, Lena. You're running from a bargain someone made before you could walk."

The sigil on her palm pulsed in the dark, warm as a coal, pointing east like a compass needle toward Mount Sarn—toward the green glass doors she'd glimpsed from the ridge, opening in the rock like eyes waking.

Toward whatever had sent men with warrants instead of knives.

"Then we'd better get there first," Lena said.

Above the ravine, far to the east, the mountain breathed its slow column of smoke against the starless sky—and for the first time since the night the stars went out, Lena thought she saw a flicker deep inside the smoke. Orange. Brief. Like something down in the roots of the world striking a match.
