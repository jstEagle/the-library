## 8. The Tollhouse of Small Mercies

Dain Corvess had walked the border road in daylight and in dark, on duty and in disgrace, and he had never once seen it the way it looked now: empty. Not quiet—empty. The way a house is empty after a funeral, when everyone has gone home and taken the living with them.

The ash came down fine as flour, sifting out of a sky that had no stars to hold it up. It grayed the shoulders of his coat, dusted Wren's wild hair until the child looked like something grown from a grave, and settled in the creases of Lena's face where she was frowning at the road ahead as if she could scowl it into behaving.

"Two miles," Dain said. "Maybe three."

"You said two miles an hour ago."

"And it was true then." He shifted the strap of his pack where it had worn a groove into his shoulder. "The tollhouse sits where the old boundary stone used to stand. Before the wards, before the warden corps, there was a toll. Travelers paid a small mercy—a coin, a prayer, a kindness—and passed on. The house stayed because houses remember being useful even when roads forget them."

Wren trotted ahead three steps, then back two, orbiting them like a moth that had decided they were her lamp. "Who lives there?"

"A woman named Maren." He heard how his voice changed saying the name and hated it. "Hedge-healer. She patched me up after—" He stopped. After Vellamark's western line failed and forty wardens died and one survived, and the corps needed someone to blame who wasn't already dead. "After a bad night."

Lena glanced at him. She had a way of looking at people sideways, like reading a charm for its true working. "You've been here before."

"Once. Seven years ago."

"And you're sure she'll take us in? Strangers, on the road, the week the world ended?"

"She took me in when I was bleeding and lying about why." Dain kept his eyes on the road. "She'll take you in because she's the kind of woman who can't help it. But I'll warn you now—she'll know what you are before you're through the door. Hedge-healers smell craft the way hounds smell blood. So don't lie to her. She's had a long life of other people's lies and no patience left for them."

"I don't lie," Lena said.

"You sell luck-knots."

"That's commerce."

He almost smiled. Almost. His face had gotten out of the habit.

* * *

The tollhouse appeared out of the ash-fall like a memory surfacing: low, stone-hipped, roofed in shingles gone soft with moss, a chimney breathing a thin thread of smoke that was the only honest thing on the whole horizon. A sign hung by the door, its paint flaked to ghosts of letters. Dain could still read them underneath. *Small mercies given here. Large ones sold elsewhere.*

A garden clung to the south wall despite everything—feverfew, comfrey, a row of brown bottles sunk to their necks in the earth like buried soldiers. And over the door, nailed up with the confidence of someone who meant it, a horseshoe turned the wrong way round, spilling its luck outward to any passerby. Dain had always liked that. Generosity as architecture.

He knocked the way wardens knocked—three, pause, two—and heard Lena breathe a small laugh beside him.

"What?"

"Nothing. It's just—you knock like the law."

"The law knocks worse."

Footsteps. The door opened on a chain, then all the way, and Maren stood in the gap: broad, gray-braided, sleeves rolled over forearms mapped with old burn scars, a ladle in one hand as though soup were a weapon she was prepared to deploy. Her eyes went from Dain to Lena to Wren and back again, quick as a card sharp's.

"Well," she said. "Corvess. You look terrible."

"Good to see you too."

"You look worse than terrible. You look like a man carrying news he hasn't said out loud yet." Her gaze snagged on Lena and stayed. Dain watched it happen—the small stillness, the nostrils flare, the healer reading air. Maren's mouth thinned. "And you'd be the charm-seller of Greyharbor. The one whose sigil is on every fallen boundary stone between here and the sea."

Lena went very still. "I never put it there."

"No," Maren said. "But somebody did, wearing your hand." She unhooked the chain the rest of the way and stood aside. "In. All of you. Soup first, questions after. Questions keep better than soup."

* * *

The inside of the tollhouse was warm in the specific way that only very old houses are warm—not fire-warm but lived-in-warm, warmth soaked into the stones by decades of ordinary evenings. Bundles of herbs hung from every beam. A shelf of labeled jars caught the lamplight: *knucklebone salve, widow's ease, night-cough syrup, truth (mild)*. Wren made a sound like a small animal entering paradise and had to be physically steered away from the shelf.

Maren fed them at a table scarred by generations of knives. Barley soup with smoked fish in it, bread with a crust like armor, tea brewed from something bitter and piney that Dain remembered tasting through split lips seven years ago. Wren ate like the famine was personal. Lena ate slowly, watching Maren over the rim of her bowl, and Dain recognized the posture of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like.

Finally Maren sat, folded her scarred hands, and looked at Lena the way healers look at wounds they already know are bad.

"Odile Marchetti trained you," she said.

It wasn't a question, but Lena answered it like one. "Yes."

"For how long?"

"Four years. Then I ran."

"Why?"

Lena's jaw worked. "She wanted to take me somewhere. Below. She said it was time to meet the people we owed. I didn't want to go. I ran that night and never stopped running."

Maren nodded slowly, as if this confirmed a ledger only she could see. "I knew Odile thirty years. She was my sister in craft before the outlawry, back when hedge-witches and court-trained mages argued over the same hedgerows and mostly shared the tea. She was brilliant. She was kind. And she was frightened of exactly one thing in this world, which was debt." Maren turned her cup a quarter-turn on the table. "When the outlawry came, Odile didn't run like the rest of us scattered. She negotiated. That should have told us everything. You don't negotiate with the Undercourt unless you intend to pay."

Dain felt Lena stop breathing beside him.

"You know what she sold," Lena said. It came out flat, stripped of question.

"I know what she told me, near the end, the last time she passed through—must be twelve years ago now. She was older. Frightened in a new way, like a woman who'd signed something and only then read the fine print." Maren's voice gentled without slowing. "She said she'd sold a soul to buy passage. Not her own. She couldn't give her own—it was already promised elsewhere, that's what she said, promised elsewhere, whatever that means. She gave a shadow instead. Split from a living girl. A shadow carries most of what a soul does, to hear the Court tell it. Enough to count. Enough to satisfy."

The fire ticked. Somewhere in the walls a beam settled with a groan like an old man changing position.

"Whose shadow?" Lena asked. Her voice was perfectly level, which was how Dain knew she was in the worst danger of the night.

Maren met her eyes and didn't flinch, because Maren had spent a lifetime not flinching at things. "She wouldn't say the name. But she said the girl was her apprentice. And she said—" the old woman's hands tightened around her cup— "she said the worst part was that the girl would never know. That she'd grow up whole-feeling and light, and never miss what was gone, because you can't miss a weight you never carried. Those were her words. *You can't miss a weight you never carried.*"

Lena set down her spoon with exaggerated care, the way you do when the alternative is throwing it.

Then she stood up so fast her stool went over backward, and crossed to the hearth, and stood there with her back to all of them, and Dain watched her right hand close into a fist at her side—the palm with the sigil pressed hard against her thigh as if she could grind the brand off against her own body.

"Fifteen years," she said to the fire. "Fifteen years I thought—I *knew*—she saved me. Out of love. Out of—she was the only person who ever—" Her breath hitched, caught, tore loose. "She didn't save me. She *paid* for me. With me. I'm not her apprentice, I'm her *coin*. I've been walking around for fifteen years spending myself and calling it freedom—"

"Lena." Dain rose halfway.

"Don't." She held up a hand without turning. "Don't tell me it isn't fair to be angry at a dead woman. Don't tell me she did it to protect me. I have spent fifteen years building a life out of *never be remembered*, and it turns out the reason nobody came looking is that the bill was already settled. My whole careful little life was a receipt." Her voice cracked down the middle. "She taught me my letters. She taught me knots. She sang to me when I had fever. And the entire time she was—she was keeping me the way you keep a purse. Counting me."

Wren had gone still over her soup, eyes enormous, watching Lena the way children watch storms—frightened and unable to look away.

Maren got up, slow, joints cracking, and stood beside Lena at the hearth without touching her. "Do you want the rest?" she asked quietly. "Or have you had enough for one night?"

"There's more?"

"There's always more. That's what makes it a debt and not a price." Maren stared into the coals. "The last thing she told me was a warning. She said: *if the wards ever fail, the Court will come collecting, and it will come for the girl, because a shadow remembers its body the way a river remembers the sea. Tell her—if you ever find her—that she mustn't run. Running is the one payment they accept forever.*"

Lena laughed. It was the worst sound Dain had heard in years—dry, cracked, nothing in it but exhaustion. "Don't run. She tells me this now. Fifteen years of running and the advice is *stop*."

"She wasn't giving you advice," Maren said. "She was giving you a map. If running feeds them, then standing still starves them. There's a door in that, girl. Doors open both ways."

Lena was silent a long moment. When she spoke again her voice had gone quiet and dangerous, the voice Dain imagined she used on customers who tried to haggle. "Where is the shadow now?"

"Below. Where souls go when they're sold. Mount Sarn's roots, if half of what the old stories say is true." Maren finally touched her—two fingers, light, on the shoulder. "Which means if you want it back, you don't run from the mountain. You walk in."

* * *

Later, when Wren had been bundled into a cot by the window and had fallen asleep mid-sentence in the middle of reciting constellations that no longer existed, and Lena lay rigid on the pallet by the hearth pretending to sleep, Dain stepped out into the cold to check the perimeter—an old habit, and also an excuse.

Maren followed him onto the stoop. The ash fell between them, fine and patient.

"She's going to go down there," Dain said. "Isn't she."

"Oh, certainly."

"And it'll kill her."

"Probably not." Maren pulled her shawl tighter. "People go below expecting to fight the Court. That's what kills them. But she's not going to fight. Look at her—she's going to *argue*. She's her mistress's student after all, and Odile could argue the interest off a loan." The old woman smiled faintly, without much humor. "Besides. She won't be alone. She has a warden who owes a debt of his own, and a child who remembers magic, and a healer who knows where the keeper's road starts."

Dain looked at her. "The keeper's road."

"Sorrel's road. Up through the mountain's glass tunnels to where the last dragon lies dying." Maren's eyes were steady on him. "You didn't think I kept this tollhouse on a dead road out of sentiment, did you? Somebody has to feed the ones who pass through. And somebody, Corvess, has to point them at the mountain before the Court finishes waking up."

Behind them, through the window, Dain could see the hearth-light fall across Lena's pallet. She wasn't sleeping. She was sitting up, staring at her own open palm, turning it in the firelight as if the answer were written there—which, Dain was beginning to understand, it was.

Far to the east, beneath the ash-cloud, Mount Sarn breathed. Once. Slowly. Like something in its sleep rolling over.

Like something beginning to wake.
