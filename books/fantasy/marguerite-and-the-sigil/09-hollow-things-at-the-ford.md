## 9. Hollow Things at the Ford

The river came out of the hills the color of old pewter, running fast and cold with snowmelt from Sarn's shoulders, and Lena hated it on sight.

Hated it because it meant they were close. Hated it because fords were where roads narrowed and choices ran out, and because Dain had gone quiet in that particular way he went quiet when he was doing arithmetic he didn't like. He stood at the water's edge now, boots planted on the shingle, reading the crossing the way other men read letters.

"Too fast to swim," he said. "Water's waist-deep at the stones, maybe chest-deep mid-channel. Doable. But we'd be slow, and slow is a word I've stopped liking."

"Then we go around," Wren said.

"There is no around." Dain crouched and touched the water, then wiped his fingers on his coat. "The road ends here. The bridge at Marrowfell came down when the wards did—I felt it go from thirty miles off, like a tooth breaking. This ford or nothing."

Lena looked upstream, downstream. Grey light, grey water, banks stitched with alder and dead bracken. The ash-fall had thinned since morning, which should have been a mercy, but instead it just meant she could see farther, and seeing farther meant seeing how empty everything was. No birds. No smoke from the crofts on the far hills. A whole valley holding its breath.

"Something's wrong with the quiet," Wren said.

Wren had been saying versions of that all day, and every version had been right. The child counted things when she was nervous—stars, steps, crows—and today she'd counted silence. *Four hours without a bird. Nine miles without a living thing.* Lena had stopped telling her to hush. That was the trouble with found family, if that's what this was: you started believing them.

"It's the sigil," Lena said. She didn't look at her palm. She hadn't looked at it all day; looking felt like feeding it. "It's me. Whatever those things are, they're not hunting generally. They're hunting specifically."

Dain straightened. "You're sure."

"I've been sure since the second night, when they passed us by to circle back toward my side of the fire. They don't want food. Hollow things don't eat, do they? Not meat." She flexed her fingers inside her gloves. "They want what's missing. And something about me smells like what's missing."

"Your shadow," Wren said simply, the way children say terrible things.

"My shadow," Lena agreed, because there was no point pretending anymore, not after Maren's tollhouse, not after the ledger and the words *a shadow split from a living girl* written in Odile's cramped, careful hand. Fifteen years of small charms and smaller fears, and the whole time the thing she'd lost had been out there leaving a scent-trail only monsters could follow.

"So we split up," Dain said. "I take Wren across—"

"No," said Wren.

"—and you cross downstream alone, draw anything that's following you away from—"

"I said no." Wren's voice cracked on it, and then steadied, which was worse. She planted herself between them, thin arms crossed, chin up, all six years of her and however many years of whatever else she was arranged like a wall. "Nobody goes alone. That's the rule now. We made it at the tollhouse."

"We made no such rule," Dain began.

"You said *we watch each other's backs*, and Maren said *that's the whole of warden law left, so mind it*, and I heard." Wren's eyes were wet and furious. "I count things. Nobody's left yet. Nobody's going to leave."

The river filled the pause, cold and indifferent.

Lena looked at the two of them—the disgraced warden with his broken rank and his dead sister's symbol burned into memory, the starless child who remembered magic from before the outlawry—and felt the loneliness under her ribs shift, painfully, like a bone setting wrong. For fifteen years her rules had kept her alive by keeping her alone. Never big magic. Never twice for the same customer. Never be remembered. Clean rules. Simple rules. Rules that had never once required anyone to stand in front of her and refuse to be left behind.

"Fine," Lena said. "Together. All three, mid-channel, same time. If something comes, we don't fight it—we move. Water slows them. I'm betting on that."

"What are they, exactly?" Dain asked. "You named one last night before it named itself. How?"

"They name themselves when they come for you. It's old defense-lore—pre-outlawry, older than the wards. My mistress taught me a fragment of it and swore me never to use it." Lena stepped onto the first of the crossing-stones, testing it. Wet, slick, solid. "A hollow thing is a shape with the middle scooped out. When it hunts, it has to announce itself—it fills its mouth with your name, or a name close enough, and it can't strike until it finishes saying it. If you know its true emptiness—if you call it what it is before it calls you—you unmake the announcement. You make it a stranger to itself for a breath. One breath is enough to run."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then it finishes saying your name," Lena said, "and takes what it came for."

* * *

They were halfway across when the birds came back.

That was the worst part, later—that the world announced it. A single crow burst from the alders on the south bank, screaming, followed by a dozen more, and then every bird in the valley rose at once like a torn sheet thrown into the air, and beneath their crying came a sound Lena felt in her teeth before she heard it: a long, wet, dragging slowness, like something heavy being pulled through bracken that did not want to let it go.

"Don't stop," Dain said. "Don't look. Move."

She moved. Stone to stone, the water grabbing at her calves, cold as a hand. Wren ahead of her, Dain behind, his warden's staff held crosswise. Twenty feet of channel left. Fifteen.

On the south bank, the alders stopped moving.

Not stilled—stopped. The wind went elsewhere. The bracken lay down flat in a spreading ring, the way grass does under a sleeping animal, except nothing slept, and out of the flat place came three shapes walking without hurry, because hurry was for things with heartbeats.

They were man-shaped the way a glove is hand-shaped. Grey as the river, greyer, with surfaces that didn't decide between skin and smoke, and where faces should have been there was smoothness, and in the middle of the smoothness a slow opening, like an eye learning to exist. The nearest one stopped at the waterline. The water did not ripple around its feet. The water seemed to lean away.

"Lena Amberdale," it said.

Her name in a voice like a draft under a door. Her whole body went to ice that had nothing to do with the river.

"Keep moving," Dain breathed. "It has to finish the—"

"*Lena Amberdale,*" the hollow said again, longer, savoring, and the second hollow tilted its headless head toward Wren, and the third toward Dain, and Lena understood with a lurch that the first one wasn't announcing a hunt. It was introducing a pack.

"Wren," she said. "Behind me. Now."

Wren didn't move. Wren was staring at the second hollow, and the second hollow had begun, softly, horribly, to speak—not Lena's name but a smaller one, a half-name, a name like a door left ajar:

"*Little—*"

"*No.*" Wren's voice came out tiny. Then she did the bravest and most foolish thing Lena had ever seen: she answered it. "You're not a name. You're a hole wearing a name. I know what you are. You're a *hollowing*. You're the space where a—"

The hollow lunged.

It didn't walk the water. It poured across it, fast as spilled ink, and its arm-length of grey reached out and closed around Wren's shoulder, and where it touched her coat the color went out of the wool like breath off glass. Wren screamed—not in pain, Lena realized, in *loss*, in the specific horror of feeling something being taken that she couldn't see, some warm middle-of-her beginning to be scooped—

Everything in Lena went very still and very clear.

Later she would try to reconstruct the choice and fail. There was no deliberation. There was no weighing of rules. There was a child's scream and fifteen years of discipline, and the discipline lost so completely and so quickly that it hardly qualified as a fight.

Lena tore off her glove.

The sigil on her palm woke like a struck bell. Heat flooded up her arm, and she reached—not toward Wren, but past her, into the space between them, into the cold where the hollow lived—and she took hold of the only thing she had ever refused to take hold of. Everything. The raw stuff underneath charms, underneath knots and fever-salves and luck tied in red thread. The big magic. The kind that gets you remembered.

"*I know what you are,*" she said, and her voice came out doubled, hers and something older speaking through the brand. "*You are the Unmanned. You are the Scooped. You are hunger with the eating removed. You have no name of your own and you never will.*"

The hollow froze. Its whole grey surface shivered like a struck puddle.

"*And I am not afraid of you,*" Lena said, which was the truest spell she knew, "*because I have already lost what you would take.*"

She closed her fist.

Fire left her palm.

Not charm-fire, not candle-flame, not the polite warmth of a luck-knot. This was will given a body, white-gold and roaring, and it went through the hollow like dawn through fog. The creature didn't burn so much as *remember burning*—it arched, it opened that learning eye wide, and for one instant Lena saw into the scoop of it and saw nothing, endless nothing, a hunger shaped like a person, and she almost pitied it, and then it was gone. All of it. Ash on the wind, and the ash blew apart before it landed, as if even its remains couldn't agree to exist.

The silence afterward rang.

Wren sat down in the shallows, gasping, clutching her own shoulder where the wool had gone pale. Dain had his staff raised against the remaining two hollows—but they weren't advancing. They stood at the waterline, and slowly, slowly, both of them turned their faceless heads toward Lena, and then, without a sound, they walked backward into the bracken and were not there anymore.

"They'll report," Lena said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "That's what they do now. They saw."

"Saw what?" Wren whispered.

Lena looked at her own hand. The sigil was still glowing, faintly, gold fading to ember-red, and above the treeline to the north—too far to reach, too near to ignore—a light answered. A warden-beacon, kindled on some ridge, flaring once, twice, three times. The old signal grammar Dain had taught them on the road. Lena didn't know the words, but she knew the rhythm of attention when she saw it.

*We see you. We are coming.*

"That," Dain said quietly. "They saw that. Every warden on this stretch of the border just saw a witch work raw craft in the open, unlicensed, unhidden, bright as a bonfire." He turned to look at her, and there was no blame in his face, which somehow made it worse. There was something worse than blame. There was recognition. "Fifteen years, Lena. Fifteen years of never being remembered. And now every ward-post from here to Sarn knows your name."

Lena closed her fist over the fading brand. The cost of it hit her then, delayed, the way wounds do—all the small safe years of it, the market stall and the false names and the luck-knots sold to strangers who'd forget her by morning. Spent. All of it spent, in one breath, for one child, and she found, examining herself for regret, that there wasn't any. Only a vast, vertiginous lightness, like a door swinging open in a wall she'd thought was load-bearing.

"Good," she said.

Dain blinked. "Good?"

"Let them know my name." She picked Wren up out of the shallows—the child was shaking, but her grip on Lena's neck was fierce and unbroken—and settled her against her hip like she'd been doing it for years. "Let them write it down. Because the wardens aren't the only ones watching, and the things below the mountain need to know it too. Let them all come find Lena Amberdale."

She turned up the far bank, toward the black shoulder of Mount Sarn breathing its slow smoke against the sky.

"She's got a debt to collect."

* * *

They made four miles before dusk, and camped in the shell of a shepherd's cot whose roof had half fallen in, and it was Wren who broke the silence, as the stars—still absent, still a blind black ceiling—should have been coming out.

"You said you already lost what they take," Wren said. She was wrapped in Dain's cloak, a coal of a girl in the firelight. "When you burned it. You said *I have already lost what you would take.* What did you mean?"

Lena fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch.

"I meant," she said, "that somewhere under that mountain there's a piece of me that's been waiting fifteen years in the dark. And tomorrow we're going to go and get her back."

Above the cot's broken roofline, far to the north, another beacon flared. Then a second. Then a third, closer than the last.

Coming, all of them. Coming for her.

Lena found she was smiling.
