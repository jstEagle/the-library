## 10. The Court Beneath the Mountain

* * *

**Lena**

The mountain was breathing.

Lena had heard that phrase before—old women in Greyharbor said it about every peak with a plume of smoke, the way sailors said a sea was angry or a wind had moods. It was a figure of speech. Mountains did not breathe. Mountains were stone and patience and nothing else.

She stood on the ridge above the dead border road and watched Mount Sarn breathe, and understood that she had been lied to her whole life by people who meant well.

The smoke came in long, slow exhalations. It rose from vents along the mountain's flanks—not one column but dozens, like the chimneys of some vast buried house—and each exhalation shuddered the air above it, so that the clouds tore and reformed around the summit as if the sky itself could not decide whether to look away. Between breaths there was a pause. A held silence. Then the whole slope seemed to draw inward, the smoke thinning, the vents dimming, and Lena felt the pull of it in her own chest, an answering contraction, as though something under the rock had reached up through fifteen miles of stone and taken hold of her lungs.

"That's not a dying fire," Dain said beside her. His voice was flat, warden-flat, the voice he used when feeling would only slow him down. But his knuckles were white around the strap of his pack. "A dying fire gutters. This is—" He stopped. Started again. "This is labored."

"Like a man drowning," Lena said.

"Like a man drowning slowly," Dain agreed, "and knowing it."

Below them the border road ran out into a plain of grey ash—the old flood-meadows of the Sarnwater, they'd learned, before the river died with the wards—and beyond the ash the mountain's roots rose up black and sheer. The road itself ended at a broken archway half-swallowed by slag: the last boundary stone, the one Dain had called the Anchor, the point where all the wards of Vellamark had once been tied off like the end of a knot. Even from the ridge Lena could see the sigils carved down its face, and she could see, too, how they had been cut—not weathered, not defaced, but *cut*, cleanly, the way you'd sever a rope.

Someone had untied the border on purpose.

Wren crouched at the edge of the ridge with her chin on her knees, counting under her breath. She counted everything now—the stars that weren't there, the birds that didn't come, the hours since the ford. Lena had stopped trying to make her stop. The counting was how Wren held the world together, and lately the world needed holding.

"Forty-one doors," Wren said suddenly.

Lena looked where the girl was looking, and her stomach dropped through the ridge, through the ash, through the bones of the world.

Far below, at the mountain's base where the black rock met the grey plain, light was opening in the stone. Doors. There was no other word for them—tall rectangles of green glass standing in the mountainside where no door should be, glowing from within like lanterns sunk in deep water. As Lena watched, another kindled into being, then another, each one sliding open with a slowness that was somehow worse than speed, the way a wound opens worse than it cuts.

They hadn't seen them from the last ridge. An hour ago the mountain's face had been only rock.

"They're waking up," Wren said. She wasn't afraid, exactly. That was the terrible part. She sounded like someone reading a list she'd memorized long ago. "Green glass means collection. My grandmother said—you don't go to the Undercourt, the Undercourt comes to *you*, and when it does, the doors are green, because green is the color of things owed." She frowned, counting again. "Forty-two now."

Dain swore softly and thoroughly, in the manner of a man who had sworn an oath against swearing and was breaking it deliberately, one word at a time.

"The wards fell four days ago," he said. "Four days, and the Court's already got forty-odd doors open. That's not an army mobilizing. That's a ledger being settled." He turned to Lena, and in the flat grey light of the starless noon his eyes looked bruised. "Your mistress sold something down there. Fifteen years ago. And now the wards that kept the sellers out are gone, and every debt in Vellamark has come due at once."

"Not every debt," Lena said. Her mouth was dry. "*Our* debt. Mine." She flexed her right hand inside its glove. The sigil beneath the leather had been warm for two days now, ever since the ford, ever since she'd burned a hollow thing out of the world with raw will in front of God and three wardens. Warm, and getting warmer, like a coal wrapped in cloth. Like something being *summoned*. "Whatever Odile paid, whatever she promised—the bill has my name on it. I can feel it."

Wren looked up at her. "Then why are we walking toward it?"

Because there was no one else. Because the dragon was dying and the stars were dark and a child who remembered magic was counting doors on a ridge in the cold. Because fifteen years ago a woman she'd loved had done something monstrous to keep her safe, and the interest on that monstrosity had compounded every single day Lena had spent selling luck-knots and pretending to be small.

"Because," Lena said, "I'm tired of running from a house I've never seen."

* * *

**Dain**

Dain had stood watch on the border for eleven years, and in eleven years he had learned that fear came in flavors.

There was the fear of the empty road at night, which was clean and simple and passed. There was the fear of command, of sending men out and waiting to count them back, which sat in the stomach like swallowed stone. And there was this—a new flavor, one he had no name for—standing on a ridge while a mountain breathed and green doors opened in the dark, knowing that the thing below was older than the kingdom, older than the wards, older maybe than the idea of borders, and that it had opened its doors not in rage but in *routine*. Collection day. The end of a fiscal quarter in hell.

That was what curdled him. Not the size of it. The *bookkeeping* of it.

He checked the treeline behind them out of habit. Nothing moved in it, which meant nothing yet. Since the ford, the hollow things had stopped following openly—they'd learned what Lena's fire could do—but Dain had been a warden too long to mistake absence for safety. They were pacing them. Something out in the grey country was keeping pace with their little band of three, patient as interest accruing.

"Sorrel said the keeper's road comes down the north face," he said, unrolling the crude map they'd copied from the tollhouse wall. "Through the old fire-tunnels. If we can reach it before full dark—"

"Look," Wren said.

Not at the mountain. At the plain.

One of the green doors had finished opening, and something was coming out of it. At this distance it was no bigger than a finger held at arm's length, but Dain's eyes were warden's eyes, trained on eleven years of night horizons, and he saw it clearly: a procession. Tall figures in a line, moving with the unhurried gait of bailiffs walking a familiar route, and behind them—this was the part that made his skin crawl—behind them walked others. Small ones. Bent ones. Dozens of shapes drifting out of the door like smoke drawn backward into a chimney, and every one of them moved toward the mountain's shadow without hesitation, without resistance, the way water moves downhill.

People. Those were people. Or they had been.

"Hollows?" Lena asked quietly.

"No." Dain rolled the map closed because his hands wanted something to crush. "Hollows are empty. Those are—" He searched for the word and hated the one that arrived. "*Debtors.* People who owe. Look at the way they walk. Nobody's driving them. They know where they're going."

Wren watched the procession with her head tilted, and for a moment she looked much older than her years, or much younger, Dain couldn't decide which. "My grandmother said the Court never takes," she recited. "It only collects. You always owed it something. You just didn't know what yet." She paused. "Everyone owes it something."

"Cheerful child," Dain muttered.

But she was right, and he knew it, and the knowing sat in him like cold iron. He thought of his sister, Ilse, dead nine years, and the twin sigil she'd borne on her palm—the same brand Lena carried, the mark that had made him lower his sword on a rooftop in Greyharbor instead of taking the witch in. Ilse had never told him where the brand came from. He'd assumed it was witch-work, apprentice-marking, some hedge-craft tradition. Now, watching green doors open in the roots of a breathing mountain, he wondered for the first time whether it was something else entirely. Whether the sigil wasn't a mark of belonging to a craft at all.

Whether it was a mark of belonging, period. The way cattle are marked. The way ledgers are marked.

"Lena," he said. "Show me your hand."

She went still. Then, slowly, she pulled off the glove.

The sigil glowed. Not brightly—not the blaze of the ford—but with a low, steady light, like a window seen from a dark street, and Dain felt his breath go strange in his throat, because he had seen that exact glow once before. On a sickbed. Nine years ago. In the last hour of his sister's life, her palm had lit up like that, soft and green-gold, and she had smiled at the ceiling and said, *Oh. They're coming for me after all,* and he had thought it was fever-talk.

It had not been fever-talk.

"It's pulling," Lena said. Her voice was steady; her hand was not. "Since the ford. It's been pulling, like a compass needle. Toward the mountain." She curled her fingers shut over the light. "Ilse had the twin. Dain—who marked her? Who gave her the sigil?"

"I don't know." His voice came out rougher than he intended. "She wouldn't say. She only ever said—" He stopped. Eleven years a warden, and here was the sentence he'd never spoken aloud. "She said we'd all be called home eventually, and I shouldn't wait up."

Above them, the sky did its starless noon-nothing, grey and blind. Below them, the mountain exhaled, and the doors burned greener, and somewhere in the plain between, the procession of debtors walked steadily down into the dark.

"We camp short tonight," Dain said, because someone had to say something practical or he was going to sit down in the ash and not get up. "Cold camp, no fire, watches in pairs. We hit the keeper's road at first light." He looked at Lena's closed fist, at the light leaking between her fingers. "And tomorrow we stop asking whether we're walking into a trap. We already know we are. From here on we just ask how fast."

* * *

**Wren**

Wren did not sleep, because sleeping meant dreaming, and dreaming meant the dark between stars, and the dark between stars had started talking back.

So she sat at the edge of the ridge with her knees pulled up and counted the doors instead. Forty-seven. Forty-eight. They opened slowly, one every few minutes, and each time one opened she felt it in her teeth, a hum like a struck bell heard through a wall. Grandmother would have known what to do. Grandmother would have drawn a circle in the ash and put a sprig of rowan at each quarter and sung the old names until the doors got embarrassed and shut again. But Grandmother was ten years gone, and the songs were mostly gone, and all Wren had left was the counting and the remembering, and the remembering was starting to feel less like a gift and more like a job nobody had trained her for.

Except—that wasn't quite true either. Someone had trained her. She just couldn't see the shape of the training yet.

She heard Lena come and sit beside her. Lena moved quietly for a grown person, quieter than Dain, quiet as a cat deciding whether to trust you. Wren didn't look over. Looking over meant talking, and talking meant admitting she was scared, and she had decided she wasn't going to be scared in front of them until they'd seen her be brave at least once more.

"Forty-nine," she said instead.

"You should sleep."

"I'm keeping watch."

"You're eight years old."

"I'm *nine*," Wren said with dignity, "and I'm the only one here who remembers what the stars were called, which makes me the most qualified person on this ridge to notice if anything else goes missing." She pointed up at the blind grey sky. "The Swan is gone. The Sisters. the Old Plowman. The Lantern-Bearer. I used to fall asleep naming them, all twenty-two, in order, oldest first. Now when I close my eyes there's just—" She hunted for the words. "Just the holes where they were. And the holes have edges. And the edges glow a little. Green-ish."

Lena was quiet for a moment. When she spoke, her voice had changed—softer, careful, the voice of someone handling something breakable. "Wren. When you look at the holes in the sky. And when you look at the doors on the mountain."

"They're the same shape," Wren said. "I know. I counted."

"Do you know why?"

Wren shrugged, which was a lie, because she had a guess, and the guess lived in her chest like a swallowed coal. *Because the sky is a ward too,* she wanted to say. *Because somebody sold the fire that hung the stars, the same as somebody sold the fire that fed the border, and everything is one big ledger, and everybody in it is a debtor, even the sky.* But saying it aloud would make it real, and real things had to be dealt with, and she was nine, and she was so tired.

Instead she said: "Fifty."

Down on the plain, the fiftieth door slid open, and this time what came out was different. Not a procession. Not debtors. A single figure, tall and pale even at this distance, who stood at the mouth of the green glass and did not move, and did not move, and did not move—and then, slowly, deliberately, raised one arm and pointed.

Not at the mountain. Not at the plain.

At the ridge. At them.

Wren's whole body went cold and then hot. Beside her she heard Lena's breath catch, and across the little camp Dain came up off his bedroll with his hand on his knife, and none of them moved, because there was nowhere to move *to*, and the pale figure stood pointing at them across five miles of ash with the patience of a thing that had all the time in the world.

"It sees us," Wren whispered. "How can it see us? We're miles away. It's dark. Everything's dark."

Lena looked down at her own right hand.

The glove was off still. The sigil was burning now—really burning, green-gold light spilling between her fingers, bright enough to throw shadows on the rocks—and the light pulsed, once, twice, in rhythm with the far-off doors, like two hearts learning to beat together.

"It's not seeing us," Lena said. Her face in the sigil-light looked years older and years younger all at once, and very afraid, and very certain. "It's seeing *this*. I'm a lit candle on a dark hill, and I've been lit since the ford." She closed her fist, and the light dimmed but did not die. "It knows I'm coming. It's been waiting for me to come."

"Fifty-one," Wren said, because the counting was all she had, and because someone had to keep track of how many doors stood open between here and the end of the world.

The pale figure lowered its arm. Turned. Walked back through the green glass, unhurried, and the door stayed open behind it like a held breath.

Dain said, "First light. Keeper's road. We move fast and we move quiet, and we assume everything down there already knows our names."

Wren hugged her knees and watched the doors burn and did not tell them the other thing she'd noticed, the small thing, the thing that had been true since the ford and was truer every hour: that the hollow things pacing them through the grey country had stopped pacing an hour ago. All of them. Everywhere.

They hadn't given up.

They had been *called in*—gathered up like tools after work, like coins after market—and Wren was almost sure that was worse. Because you only call your tools in when the work is about to begin.

Above the breathing mountain, in the hole where the Lantern-Bearer used to hang, something at the edge of the dark glowed faintly green.

Wren started counting again, and did not stop until dawn.
