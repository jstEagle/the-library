## 11. Bargains at the Green Door

The herald was waiting for them at the tree line, and it had brought a chair.

That was the detail Lena's mind snagged on, the one she'd carry into sleep for years afterward if she got any sleep again: not the green glass door standing alone in the mountainside with no wall around it, not the smoke of Mount Sarn staining the sky the color of a bruise, but the chair. A high-backed thing of black wood and silver wire, placed precisely on the moss as though the forest were a parlor. The herald sat in it with its ankles crossed, hands folded on one knee, wearing a body that was almost right.

Almost. The proportions were a half-step off, like a coat cut for someone slightly taller. Its face was smooth and pleasant and had clearly been copied from a living man—the jaw too generous, the eyes a shade too wet—and when it smiled, the smile arrived a moment after the mouth moved, as if traveling a long distance to get there.

"Good morning," it said. "Or near enough."

Dain's hand went to his knife. Wren, who had been walking ahead picking her way through the bracken, stopped dead, and Lena watched the child do the thing she did when something old and dangerous crossed her path: she went still and began to count under her breath. Stars, probably. Constellations that no longer existed. An old defense, Sorrel had called it, and Lena had seen it work once already against the hollow things at the ford—but this wasn't a hollow thing. This was worse. This was polite.

"Don't," Lena said quietly, to all of them. "Don't run, don't draw, don't count. It wants a reaction. Reactions are currency down there." She stepped forward, putting herself between the herald and the others, and held up her right hand, palm out, sigil bare. "You know what I am. Let's not waste each other's time."

The herald's smile finished arriving.

"The Amberdale girl." Its voice was warm, unhurried, the voice of someone reading terms aloud from a document they'd written themselves. "Fifteen years we wondered where Odile had hidden you. She was clever. Clever women make poor clients and excellent legends." It rose from the chair without appearing to use its legs, simply being upright between one blink and the next. "You've come a long way to knock on our door. Allow me to spare you the queue."

"There's a queue?"

"There is always a queue. The mountain is dying, the wards are down, and every debt in Vellamark has come due at once. Do you know what that does to a court's business?" It gestured behind it, at the green glass door set into bare rock, glowing faintly from within like a bottle held up to sunlight. Through the glass, Lena thought she could see movement—shapes descending a stair that went down much further than the mountain was deep. "Everyone wants passage. Everyone wants a renegotiation. And here you are, walking straight to us, saving us the trouble of sending anyone up."

"I'm not here to deal."

"No one who says that ever means it." The herald clasped its hands. "But let's assume you're sincere. You want to go down—I can see it in your palm; the sigil pulls toward us like a compass needle, did you know?—and you want to go down *safely*, which is where I come in. The roads below are not kind to the unescorted. Hollow things in the tunnels. Creditors in the galleries. The Regent's own collectors, who have no sense of humor and less patience. With my mark on you, you walk through every door below unchallenged. No tolls. No hunts. No one lays a hand—or anything else—on the Amberdale girl."

Lena felt Dain come up on her left, close enough that his shoulder nearly touched hers. He didn't speak. Good. She could feel him deciding to trust her, and it sat strangely in her chest, heavier than fear.

"And the price," she said.

"You're learning." The herald beamed. "The price is simple. Almost elegant. We don't want your soul—that's already spoken for, and the Court honors prior claims, mostly. We don't want your magic; hedge-craft barely registers down there, no offense intended. What we want—" and here its wet eyes slid past her shoulder, deliberate, unmistakable, "—is the child."

* * *

Wren didn't flinch. That was the worst part. Lena heard the small sharp intake of breath behind her, heard Dain say her name low and warning, and then heard nothing at all, because Wren had gone quiet in the way she went quiet around danger: counting, holding, waiting to see what the world would ask of her.

"Her blood predates the outlawry," the herald continued, conversational now, a merchant describing stock. "Do you understand how rare that is? Magic before the Purification ran in families, in *lines*, thick as sap. The outlawry burned most of it out or drove it underground until it thinned to your little knots and fevers. But she remembers. You've noticed. She names things before they name themselves. She counts stars that were extinguished and they answer her. That isn't learned, witch. That's inheritance. The Court has been collecting pre-outlawry blood for two hundred years, and we have perhaps a dozen vials of it, and none of them *count stars*."

"She's a child," Lena said.

"She's an asset. So are you, frankly, but you're depreciated goods—you're marked, hunted, halved, and sentimental. She's appreciating." The herald spread its almost-hands. "Passage down for the three of you. Safe conduct through the Court entire. Protection from the hunt that is, I should mention, already authorized for you regardless of this conversation. In exchange: the girl walks through the green door alone, and we never bother any of you again. Everyone profits. Isn't that how you people say it? In your market?"

Lena became aware that her hands had curled into fists, and that the sigil on her palm had begun to ache—a slow hot pulse, like a tooth, like the brand remembered fire. Fifteen years of rules rose up in her, the whole litany she'd lived by: never big magic, never twice for the same customer, never be remembered. And beneath them, older and quieter, the rule she'd never written down because she'd never needed to: *you don't sell people.* She'd built her entire life inside that one. Every luck-knot, every fever-charm, every careful anonymous kindness—it had all been load-bearing walls around that single foundation stone.

She thought about saying something grand. She was tempted to. But she'd haggled too long in smuggler's markets to mistake theater for leverage, so instead she said, flatly:

"No."

The herald tilted its head. "No counteroffer? No negotiation? You haven't even asked what she'd get out of it. Comfort. Education. Longevity, potentially—pre-outlawry blood ages beautifully down below. She'd want for nothing."

"She'd want for everything," Lena said. "Everything except the one thing you're buying. Ask her yourself if you doubt me—no. Don't. Don't even look at her again."

"Witch—"

"No." Louder now, and the sigil flared, and she let it. "I'm going to say this once, so pass it down to whoever keeps your ledgers. I am going to walk through that door or find another way down, and I am going to do it with both of them beside me, and if anyone below touches that child, I will burn whatever passes for your accounting house to the ground. I've spent fifteen years selling charms to frightened people. I know exactly what fear is worth at retail. Yours is about to become very expensive."

Silence. Even the mountain's smoke seemed to hang still.

Then the herald laughed—not cruelly, which somehow made it worse. It laughed like a clerk enjoying a joke he'd heard many times and never tired of.

"Oh, Odile trained you well after all. All that fire, and nowhere to put it." It reached into its coat—an ordinary gesture rendered obscene by the wrongness of the body doing it—and drew out a sliver of green glass, thin as a fingernail, glowing softly. "Then we proceed to the standard arrangement. Refusal carries a fee. Everything does; that's rather the point of a court. Hold out your hand."

"Absolutely not."

"Then I'll save us both the unpleasantness." It didn't move. It didn't need to. The sliver of glass lifted from its fingers and hung in the air between them, and Lena felt the pull before she understood it—the sigil on her palm heating, yearning toward the glass like iron to a lodestone, because of course, of course, the Court had helped write the sigil, the Court's fingerprints were all over her from the age of nine, and her own skin would betray her faster than any collector could chase her.

Dain moved first. He caught her wrist—not gripping the branded palm, careful even now, careful of the wound of her—and pulled her back a step. "Run," he said.

"We can't outrun a mark."

"Then we make it expensive to collect."

But the glass had already found her. It drifted forward, gentle as a moth, and pressed itself against her palm, and Lena had one heartbeat to brace before it sank in—not through the skin, but *into* the sigil, into the lines of the brand itself, and cold spread along every stroke of it like frost climbing a windowpane. She gasped. Her knees buckled. Dain caught her around the shoulders.

When she could see straight again, the herald was regarding its own open palm, where a copy of her sigil now glimmered faintly, mirrored, inverted—a hunter's copy of a key.

"There," it said, satisfied. "Marked for the Court's hunt. Every collector, every creditor, every hungry thing between here and the deep galleries will feel you coming and be *entitled*. Not required—entitled. There's a difference, and you'll learn it. Some will ignore you. Most won't." It retrieved its chair from nowhere, or the chair had always been there, or such distinctions no longer mattered. "For what it's worth—and it's worth nothing, I know—I do admire the refusal. We see so few clean ones. Odile negotiated. Odile always negotiated. You just said no and meant it, and that's rarer than pre-outlawry blood, honestly."

It settled back into the seat, ankles crossing again, the picture of patience.

"You'll come down eventually. They always do. The mountain is dying, your dragon is guttering, and the only door is ours. When you arrive—and you will arrive—we'll waive the hunt as a courtesy. Consider it an investment in a long relationship." The smile arrived late again. "Travel safely, Amberdale girl. Well. As safely as one can, marked."

The green glass door dimmed. The chair stood empty. Where the herald had been there was only moss, and a faint smell like coins held too long in a closed fist, and the sense of having been weighed, appraised, and entered into a ledger under *assets, pending*.

* * *

They walked until the light changed, which took longer than it should have, because the smoke had eaten the sun and turned noon into a bruised amber dusk. Then they climbed above the tree line to a shelf of granite where they could see the road behind them—empty, for now—and Lena sat down hard on the stone and looked at her palm.

The sigil was still there. That was the first horror: it hadn't been erased or overwritten. It had been *annotated*. Fine threads of green now laced through the old scar-lines, delicate as veins in a leaf, and when she flexed her fingers the green threads pulsed, slow and patient, like something breathing in time with a clock far underground.

"It doesn't hurt," she said, before either of them could ask. "That's what I keep waiting for. It should hurt. It just... sits there. Like a debt I agreed to."

"You didn't agree," Dain said. He crouched across from her, forearms on his knees, and studied her palm with the flat professional attention he gave to boundary stones and wounds. "It forced entry. There's warden law about marks laid without consent—there used to be, anyway, before everyone decided warden law was a joke." His mouth twisted. "A mark without consent can be contested. Contested by whom, in this case, I genuinely don't know."

"Can it be removed?"

"Not by me. Possibly not by anyone who isn't holding the original ledger." He paused. "Which is below."

"Of course it is." Lena laughed, and it came out cracked. "Everything's below. The soul, the ledger, the cure for the mark. It's like the whole world is a funnel and I'm standing at the wide end."

Wren had been silent through all of this, sitting a little apart with her knees drawn up, watching Lena's hand the way she watched the night sky: intently, hungrily, as if it might teach her something. Now she scooted closer, hesitated, and then reached out and put her small grimy fingers directly on the sigil.

Lena started to pull away—habit, reflex, fifteen years of nobody touching the brand—and stopped herself.

"It's cold," Wren said. "Down there, everything's cold. But yours is warm underneath. Under the green." She frowned, concentrating. "It's like a hearth with snow on it. Somebody built a fire in you and then somebody else threw a blanket over it and the blanket is heavy but the fire's still there." She looked up, and her eyes were fierce and certain in her thin face. "Blankets come off."

Dain made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. "The child has spoken. Blankets come off."

"That's not a plan, Wren."

"It's better than a plan. Plans are what people make when they've forgotten how fires work." Wren folded her arms, deeply satisfied with herself, and Lena felt something in her chest crack slightly along an old seam—the seam she'd welded shut over fifteen years of never being remembered, never being needed, never letting anyone close enough to matter. This child had known her eleven days and was already talking about the fire in her like it was common knowledge.

She looked at Dain. "How far to the keeper's road?"

"Two days, if the wardens' patrols stay on the low routes. Three if we have to climb." He rubbed the back of his neck. "And that's assuming the mark doesn't call anything to us tonight. Entitled things travel fast."

"Then we walk tonight. Rest in daylight, move in dark—they'll be looking for campfires." She flexed her hand again, watching the green threads pulse, and made herself look at both of them, really look, because there was a thing she had to say and she'd spent fifteen years not saying things and it was going to kill her faster than any collector. "Before we move. I need you both to hear this once, plainly, because I may not get another chance."

Wren went still. Dain's eyebrows rose.

"What that thing offered, I refused. Not because I'm brave. Because selling Wren would have cost me the only thing I have left that's actually mine, which is the line I won't cross. But understand what it means. I'm marked now. Every mile from here to the mountain, we're walking with a bell tied to my hand that rings for anything with a grudge and a claim. The smart move—the move I would have made a month ago—is to leave me. Take the keeper's road yourselves, find Sorrel, get to the dragon. I'm slower now. I'm louder. I'm worth coin to anything that catches us."

"You're done?" Dain said. "Because I was blamed for the wards falling, my name's mud in every garrison from here to Sarn, and I've been running since before I met you. Loud is my native language." He shrugged. "Besides. Someone has to teach you warden signs properly. You butchered the sign for *water* yesterday."

"You signed *soup*."

"Ambiguous soup. The point stands." But his eyes weren't joking, quite. "Corvess men don't leave people at fords. My sister taught me that. She'd have liked you. Stubborn, bad at accepting help, brand on her hand." His voice caught, just slightly, on the last word, and he looked away at the smoking mountain.

Wren said nothing at all. She simply leaned sideways until her shoulder rested against Lena's arm, a small point of pressure and warmth, and stayed there, and that was answer enough, and it undid Lena more thoroughly than the herald's mark had.

"Fine," Lena managed, after a moment. "Fine. Then we move. Dain, take point. Wren, you're with me in the middle, and if anything green glows anywhere near us, you grab my left hand, not the right. The right one's spoken for."

They gathered their gear in the failing amber light. And as Lena rose, rolling her shoulders, flexing the annotated brand, she saw it: far below at the base of the ridge, at the edge of the ash-grey road, a light had appeared. Small. Green. Patient. Then another, further east. Then a third, west, where the ford lay.

Not chasing. Not yet. Just... keeping pace. Lanterns in the dark, spaced like stations on a route, marking the road ahead of them all the way to the mountain.

"They're not hunting us," she said quietly. "They're *herding* us. Every road leads down."

Dain followed her gaze and swore softly. "Then we stop taking roads." He pointed—not at the paths, not at the lantern-lit ways, but up and left, where the land broke into scree and shadow and the flank of Mount Sarn itself, black against the bruised sky. "There's no road up there. Which means no lanterns. Which means the herald's map ends at the treeline." He glanced at Wren, at Lena's marked hand, at the three green lights patiently blooming below. "It also means climbing a dying mountain in the dark with a mark on you that rings like a dinner bell. Last chance to vote for the roads."

Lena looked at the lights. At the mountain. At the two people who had, impossibly, voted to stay.

"Up," she said. "We go up. If the Court wants to herd me somewhere, I'd rather arrive tired, filthy, and unscheduled."

And they turned from the road—from the patient green lanterns and the funnel that led down—and began to climb.
