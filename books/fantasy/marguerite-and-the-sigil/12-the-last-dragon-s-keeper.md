## 12. The Last Dragon's Keeper

Dain Corvess had spent eleven years learning to read the mountain, and the mountain was telling him it was dying.

He'd felt it first as a wrongness in his boots—a tremor too slow for an earthquake, too deep for anything made by men. Then the air itself changed as they climbed the last switchbacks of the keeper's road: warmer, thick with a smell like hot iron and old copper coins, the smell of a forge banked down to embers. Wren kept stopping to press her palm flat against the rock.

"It's breathing," she said. "Slow. Like when my grandmother got sick."

"Dragons don't get sick," Dain said, because it was true in the way all the things he'd been taught were true—technically accurate and useless now.

Lena said nothing. She'd said little since the ford, since the light she'd thrown had burned a hollow thing out of existence and painted her name across half the wardens' reports in Vellamark. She walked with her branded hand curled into a fist at her side, and every hundred paces she glanced back the way they'd come, counting pursuers who weren't there yet.

Dain knew that walk. He'd worn it himself for eleven years—the walk of a person waiting for the past to catch up.

The road narrowed where the mountain's roots shouldered up through the ashfall. Old warden work, this stretch—he recognized the survey stones, the cut of the drainage channels, work done before the outlawry when keepers and wardens still spoke to each other. Someone had maintained it until recently. The gravel was raked. The cairns marking the path were fresh-stacked.

"Someone lives here," Lena said.

"Someone did," Dain corrected, nodding at the third cairn, which had been kicked apart—not by weather, not by animals. By a boot. Deliberately. "Or someone wants us to think nobody does."

Wren crouched over the scattered stones and picked something from between them: a small brass fitting, green with age, shaped like a dragon curled around its own tail. She held it up without a word.

A keeper's badge. Dain hadn't seen one outside a museum since he was a boy.

They found the door an hour later, or rather they found where a door should have been: a seam in the cliff face so precisely fitted that Dain walked past it twice before Wren stopped, frowned, and said, "It's warm here," and pressed her hands against stone that answered with a shudder of heat.

There was no handle. There was no knocker. There was only a shallow depression in the rock, worn smooth, roughly the size of a hand.

Lena looked at it. Looked at her own palm. Dain watched her jaw tighten.

"No," she said. "No, I'm not knocking on doors with this thing anymore. Every time I do, something finds me."

"The sigil opens roads," Dain said. "That's what you told me. That's why the hollows hunt you."

"I know what I told you." Her voice was flat and tired. "I'm saying I'm tired of being a key."

Wren reached out and touched the depression herself, small fingers fitting into the worn hollow, and nothing happened—and then everything happened, because somewhere behind the rock a bell rang once, low and bronze, like a struck heart.

The seam split. Green light spilled out, thin as a blade.

Standing in it was a woman built like a doorframe—broad, weathered, sixty if she was a day, with grey hair cropped short and burn scars laddering both forearms like old rope. She held a fire-iron in one hand, not raised but ready, the way a soldier holds a weapon they've decided not to use yet.

"You rang my bell," she said. "With a child's hand. Clever. Cowardly. Come in before the ash gets in, and if either of you is a warden, know that I've killed two already this year and I remember their faces fondly."

Dain raised his hands slowly. "Ex-warden."

"They're all ex-wardens now. The wards are down." But she stepped aside anyway, and her eyes—pale, furnace-lit—went to Lena's fist. To the brand showing through the dirt and healing burns. Something moved across her face like a cloud crossing the sun.

"Well," she said softly. "Fifteen years, and Odile's girl comes home after all."

* * *

Her name was Sorrel, and her home was a cathedral of heat.

The tunnel opened into a vast chamber where the rock itself glowed dull orange along veins running through the walls like the circulatory system of some buried god. Chains hung from the ceiling, enormous, each link the size of a man's torso—but the chains were slack, trailing into darkness below a ledge, and at the far end of the chamber, past a lip of black glass, there was light.

Not firelight. Not lamplight. A slow pulse of gold, rising and falling, rising and falling, with a rhythm Dain realized with a lurch was familiar because it matched the tremor in the road, the warmth in the stone, the breath of the mountain itself.

"Heart-flame," Sorrel said, following his gaze. She'd set the fire-iron down and put water on to boil, moving through the motions of hospitality with the automatic grace of someone who'd been alone too long and was performing normalcy. "You can see it from here on a good night. Used to be you could see it from Greyharbor, if the tales are true. Now—" she shrugged. "Now it gutters. Like a candle in a doorway."

"That's what's killing the wards," Dain said. It wasn't a question. He'd suspected since the boundary stones; now, standing in the warm dark, he *knew*, the way you know a wound you've dressed a thousand times. "The wards were never fed by warden-magic. We were just the hands. The fire came from below."

"From him." Sorrel nodded toward the gold pulse. "Vaskarath. Last of his line, last of anything, probably. The wardens drew off his flame through the old conduits—gently, the way you milk a goat, not the way you bleed it. And in exchange the keepers tended him. Fed him. Kept him company." Her mouth twisted. "Company matters more than you'd think, for a creature that lives a thousand years. Loneliness is a kind of sickness."

"And now?" Lena asked. Her voice was careful. Controlled. Dain had learned to hear the fear underneath it.

"Now the wards are down, the conduits are cracked, and he's dying. Slowly. A dragon doesn't die fast—it would be a mercy if he did. He'll gutter for years, maybe decades, and the world above will go starless and cold and full of holes while he does it." She poured three cups of something that smelled like pine and bitterness and pushed them across the table. Wren wrapped both hands around hers immediately, and Sorrel's hard face softened a fraction. "Unless something changes."

"What kind of thing?" Dain asked.

Sorrel didn't answer right away. She looked at Lena instead—at the fist, at the brand—and Dain saw her decide something, weighing it the way you weigh a bridge before crossing.

"His flame is halved," she said finally. "That's the truth no warden ever learned, because we keepers kept it. A dragon's fire isn't one thing. It's two. The bright half and the shadow-half, the burning and the *reason* for burning, and they have to stay joined or the fire eats itself. Vaskarath's shadow-half was sold. Long ago. Before your outlawry, before mine. Sold to the Undercourt, and the Court has kept it ever since, down in the dark, hanging in a cage, and he's been burning on half a heart for four hundred years."

The chamber was very quiet. Somewhere far below, the gold light pulsed. Rose. Fell.

"Halved," Lena repeated. Her cup was untouched. Her fist had opened without her seeming to notice, the sigil bare in the green-gold light, and Dain saw Sorrel look at it and look away, fast, like a woman refusing a gift she couldn't afford.

"Halved," Sorrel agreed. "And here's the part that will interest you, witch, though I suspect you've already guessed. The Court doesn't buy souls for nothing. Everything down there is a transaction. And a creature that sells half its own heart—" she shook her head. "No. A creature doesn't sell its own heart. Someone sells it *for* them. Takes the price, takes the passage, leaves the wound."

Lena stood up so fast her chair scraped. "My mistress sold a soul. Fifteen years ago. One soul, to the Undercourt, price of passage—for me." The words came out of her like something she'd been holding behind her teeth for days. "I read it in her ledger. I thought—I hoped—it was some stranger. Some nameless—"

"It wasn't a stranger," Sorrel said quietly.

"No," Lena said. "It wasn't. It was me. Part of me. My shadow, Maren said. Split from me and sold so I could slip the Court's roads unseen." She laughed, and it was the worst sound Dain had heard from her yet—dry, cracked, a laugh with nothing alive in it. "So the dragon and I match. Half a heart each. His sold four hundred years ago, mine fifteen. Both of us burning on what's left."

"Not matching," Sorrel said. "Mirroring. There's a difference, and it matters." She rose, crossed to the chamber's edge, and stood looking down toward the distant gold pulse. "Come here. Both of you. You especially, girl—you need to see what you're carrying."

Dain went. So did Wren, small hand finding his without being asked, and he let her, because his own hands weren't steady enough to trust.

From the ledge, the chamber fell away into a vastness the green-gold light barely touched. And there, coiled in the roots of Mount Sarn like a shipwreck coiled in a harbor, lay the dragon.

Dain had seen dragons in engravings. In warden training charts, wings spread, jaws open, rendered in confident ink by men who'd never stood within a mile of one. Nothing had prepared him for the reality: the sheer *mass* of the sleeping thing, scales gone dull as old pewter, ribs showing beneath the skin of his flank like the timbers of a starving horse. His wings were folded tight, and they were the worst part—torn, threadbare, membrane thin as parchment, wings that had once shadowed valleys and now couldn't lift him off the stone.

And at the center of him, visible through the translucent skin of his chest, the heart-flame pulsed. Gold. Flickering. Rising—

—and guttering. Dain could see it now, this close. The flame wasn't steady. It flared and sank, flared and sank, and each time it sank a little lower than before, and each time it took longer to rise.

"He knows we're here," Sorrel said. "He always knows. He just hasn't the strength to care."

As if the words summoned it, the great head lifted. Slowly, with the terrible economy of the very weak, Vaskarath turned toward the ledge. His eyes opened—eyes like deep wells with a coal glowing at the bottom of each—and they swept across Dain without interest, across Wren with something almost like surprise, and stopped on Lena.

Stopped. Held.

The coal in those eyes brightened.

"*Little half-one,*" the dragon said.

His voice was not a voice. It was a landslide given language, a vibration that came up through the ledge and through Dain's boots and through the bones of his chest, and Wren gasped and gripped his arm, and Sorrel closed her eyes as if she'd been waiting years to hear him speak again.

Lena had gone white. Her branded hand rose halfway to her mouth and stopped there.

"You know me," she whispered.

"I know the shape of the missing thing." The coal-bright gaze didn't waver. "I have looked at my own emptiness for four hundred years, little half-one. I know it better than I know my scales. And you carry its twin." The great head lowered again, wearily, to the stone. "Odile's work. Yes. I smelled her signature on you the moment you crossed my threshold. She stood where you stand, once. Long ago. She asked me questions too."

"What did you tell her?" Lena's voice shook.

"I told her the truth." The flame in his chest flared, sank. "That halves do not heal alone. That a soul caged is a soul screaming, even across four hundred years, even across the dark. That she did not listen. They never listen, the ones who come down here whole and leave halved. They think a wound you cannot see is a wound you do not have."

The pulse of gold light dimmed. The dragon's eyes drifted shut, the coals banking down to embers, and the vast body settled with a sound like a hillside sliding.

"He'll sleep now," Sorrel said. "Speaking costs him. Days, sometimes, after." She turned, and in the strange light her burn-scarred face was unreadable. "But he gave you more than he gives most. He named you, girl. *Half-one.* That's keeper's speech. That means he counts you among the wounded he keeps watch for."

Lena stared down at the dying light of the heart-flame, and Dain watched her do the arithmetic he'd already done—the same wound, the same thief, the same court, the same fix. Her shadow down there in the dark, caged and screaming. The dragon's shadow down there too, maybe, in some deeper vault. Two halves of two hearts, locked in the Undercourt's ledgers.

And one road down. One sigil. One key.

"We go tomorrow," Lena said at last. Her voice was steady now, and that steadiness frightened Dain more than any shaking had. "Down the keeper's road. To the Court."

"You don't even know what you'll bargain with," Dain said.

"Yes, I do." She turned, and the sigil on her palm caught the guttering gold light and threw it back, and for one moment it looked less like a brand and more like a door standing open. "I'll bargain with the only thing I have left. Myself."

Behind them, deep in the roots of the mountain, the heart-flame pulsed once—rose, and fell—and in its falling, Dain thought he heard, faint and far away, something that might have been a scream.

Or might have been an answer.
