## 13. Fifteen Years of Running

The dragon's warmth was not like a hearth. A hearth gives its heat away and asks nothing back. Vaskarath's warmth was a held breath, a banked coal, the heat of something that had once been enormous and was now spending itself in careful rations, the way a starving man spends his last coin.

They sat in the curve of her flank, the three of them, and Sorrel had gone up to tend the glass tunnels, and for a long while nobody spoke. The mountain hummed around them, low and slow, like a song remembered from childhood.

It was Wren who broke it. She was lying against the dragon's side with her ear pressed flat, counting something under her breath.

"She's got two hearts," Wren said. "One of them's wrong. It counts too fast."

Lena looked at her hands. The sigil on her palm had been glowing faintly ever since they'd come down through the keeper's road, a pale gold thread in the lines of her skin, and she'd been hiding it inside her fist the whole way because she couldn't bear Dain looking at it.

"Tell us," Dain said. Not a demand. He'd learned, somewhere in the last week of ash roads and hollow things, how to ask without demanding. "You've heard ours. The wards, my sister, all of it. You know everything about us and we know nothing about you except that you're afraid of being remembered."

Lena almost laughed. Almost. What came out instead was a sound like a cough, and then she found herself saying, "I'm not afraid of being remembered. I'm afraid of what remembering costs."

Vaskarath's eye slid open. One vast amber disc, filmed and dim, but watching. Lena had the sudden, absurd feeling of being a story told to an audience that had earned the right to hear it.

So she began.

* * *

"I was seven when Odile bought me," she said. "Or won me. I never knew which. There was a famine year in the river valleys—maybe you remember it, Dain, you'd have been a soldier's son by then—and children were currency the way grain was. My mother traded me for a sack of barley and didn't look back down the road."

She'd expected pity. She'd braced for it, the way you brace for cold water. But Dain just nodded slowly, the way men nod at weather they've also walked through, and Wren reached over and took a corner of Lena's sleeve between her fingers and held on, and that was somehow worse and better than anything.

"Odile wasn't kind," Lena said. "I want that understood first. Everyone who tells this story later will want to make her a tragic figure, a woman broken by the outlawry, and she wasn't. She was brilliant and she was cruel and she loved me the way a jeweler loves a stone—as material." She flexed her branded hand. "But she was honest about one thing: she told me exactly what I was for. 'You have a door in you, girl,' she said, the first night. 'The Court can't walk through a closed door, but they'll always find an open one. My job is to teach you to be shut.'"

"The Court," Dain said quietly. "The Undercourt. Even then?"

"Even before the outlawry. Before the wards, even. Odile dealt with them—small things at first, passage-tolls, safe-conducts. That's where the sigil comes from. It isn't a witch-mark, whatever the wardens' manuals say. It's a *road-pass*. Branded into the palm of anyone the Court has agreed to let walk its ways unchallenged." Her mouth twisted. "Odile paid dearly for mine. And then spent fifteen years making sure I'd never need it."

Wren had gone very still. Children always went still when adults started talking about doors.

"She taught me charms the way other mistresses taught stitches. Fever-knots. Luck-threads. Small magics, small magics, always small. If I so much as shaped a cantrip bigger than a cupped hand, she'd burn my practice-cord and make me start over. I thought she was stingy. I thought she was frightened of the wardens." Lena shook her head. "She wasn't frightened of the wardens. Wardens were weather to her. She was frightened of *them*, below, because she knew what they'd want if they ever saw me work big magic. They'd want the door opened."

* * *

The dragon shifted beneath them—a long tectonic sigh—and Lena waited until it settled.

"I ran when I was seventeen," she said. "Ten years in that cottage above Greyharbor, learning to be shut, and I ran. Because I'd found out what she was doing with me, or half of it, and I couldn't live under it anymore."

"What half?" Dain asked.

"The half I could see." She stared into the dim glow of the dragon's hide, where veins of fading fire moved like slow lightning under skin. "There was another girl in the cottage. Not a servant, not a sister—I never saw her clearly, she was always at the edge of rooms, always turning away. Odile called her Marguerite. Said she was a cousin's daughter, said she was simple, said keep away from her. And I did keep away, because Odile's rules were the only walls I had. But I'd catch her sometimes, standing in my doorway at night, wearing—" Lena's voice caught. "Wearing my face. My face, but grey, like a photograph of me, like me seen through dirty water. And she'd look at me with such *longing*. Such terrible, patient longing. Like a dog tied outside in winter."

Dain had stopped breathing. She could feel him not breathing beside her.

"And one night I asked Odile straight out. Who is she. And Odile looked at me for a long time and said, 'She is the part of you that would have been loud.' And then she said the thing that made me run. She said, 'Be grateful I took her. Without her, you would be *visible*.'"

* * *

Wren sat up. "That's not right," she said, with the flat certainty of a child correcting arithmetic. "You can't take somebody's loudness out. That's not a thing you can do."

"It is," Lena said. "If you're Odile. If you're willing to go to the Court and pay their price. She split my shadow, Wren. Cut it off me whole—the shadow-self, the unhidden self, the girl I would have been if I'd never needed to be quiet—and sold it into the Undercourt, and kept it there, on account, like a lien against my name. And with it gone, I became nothing. Unmemorable. A charm-seller who could stand in a market square and be forgotten between one customer and the next. Fifteen years of small charms and smaller fears, and every year I thought: how clever I am, how careful, how well I hide. And every year I was just—" she gestured at herself, at all of her, "—*less*."

The word came out cracked. She hadn't meant it to. Around the dragon's warmth, in the hush of the mountain's roots, there was nowhere to put a crack except outward.

"You were a child," Dain said. "You didn't sell anything. *She* did."

"I took the bargain's benefit for fifteen years."

"You survived," he said, with the blunt finality of a man stating troop strength. "There's no guilt in surviving. There's only guilt in what you do after, and everything you've done since I've known you has been feeding a child and breaking your own rules to do it."

Lena turned her face away, because her eyes were doing something she didn't permit them in front of customers.

* * *

"The night I ran," she said, when she could speak evenly again, "I took nothing but my kit and the clothes I stood in. Odile didn't chase me. That's the part I never understood until this week. I waited three days in the smuggler's market for her wardens—for *her*—and nothing came. I thought I'd finally become invisible even to her." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "She wasn't chasing me because she'd already paid. Passage for one girl, out of the life, out of the ledger, unfindable. She'd bought my escape the same way she'd bought my silence. Everything about my freedom was a transaction, and I was the only one at the table who didn't know the price."

"And the price," Dain said slowly, "was Marguerite."

"Was me. Is me. Is hanging in some bazaar under this mountain right now, fifteen years old forever, still waiting in doorways." Lena pressed her branded palm flat against the dragon's warm hide, as if the sigil might ground her. "And the worst of it—the truly unforgivable part—is that some nights, in Greyharbor, I was *happy*. Small happinesses. A customer's fever breaking. Wren asleep with a full stomach. And I'd think: this is enough, this smallness is enough. When all along my other half was in the dark, and the wards were falling, and the whole world was going starless because of a debt signed in my name before I was old enough to hold a pen."

Vaskarath made a sound then. Not speech—dragons don't speak, Sorrel had told them, not in words—but a resonance, deep in the chest, that moved through the rock and through Lena's bones. Grief-shaped. Or recognition-shaped. She couldn't tell which, and suspected it was both.

"She lost her shadow-half too," Wren whispered. She had her ear against the dragon again. "That's why her heart counts wrong. Somebody sold hers a long, long time ago."

"Yes," said a voice from the tunnel mouth.

Sorrel stood there, lantern in hand, soot to the elbows, and her face was old in a way it hadn't been an hour ago.

"And now you know why I brought you down," she said. "Dragon and girl. Same wound. Same seller, if the ledgers run true. The Court holds a flame-half and a soul-half, and it means to spend them both—and it cannot be bargained with, only *paid*, and it accepts only one coin." She looked at Lena's glowing palm. "So rest, all of you. Eat. Tomorrow we go down to the green doors, and you'll want your wits about you, charm-seller, because the Court has been waiting fifteen years for you to walk in."

Lena looked around the circle of them—the disgraced warden, the star-child, the keeper, the dying dragon whose warmth held them all like a hand—and felt something she had no rule for.

Not safety. She'd never once been safe.

Belonging. Which was worse, and better, and worth any price she might yet have to pay.

She slept, for the first time in fifteen years, without checking the exits twice.

And far below, behind doors of green glass, something ancient turned a page in a ledger and wrote, in ink the color of deep water: *The girl comes willingly.*
