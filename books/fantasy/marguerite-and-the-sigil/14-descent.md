## 14. Descent

The keeper's road began where the mountain's skin ended.

Sorrel led them to it at first light, if you could call it light—the sky over Mount Sarn had gone the color of old pewter, and the smoke rising from the peak spread out flat against it like a lid on a pot. She stopped at a seam in the rock, barely wider than a door, half-hidden behind a curtain of dead ivy that had once been green and was now the same grey as everything else since the stars went out.

"This is as far as I go," she said. "The road remembers keepers. It doesn't always remember visitors."

"Then we'll introduce ourselves politely," Dain said. He had his arm bound from the hollow-thing's teeth at the ford, and he'd refused Maren's stronger salves in favor of the weak ones—"I need to feel it," he'd said, which Lena understood better than she wanted to. Pain kept you honest. Pain told you which parts of you were still yours.

Wren stood at Sorrel's side with her arms crossed tight, the way she did when she was holding something in by force. She hadn't slept. None of them had, really, but Wren wore sleeplessness differently—a child's version of it, all edges and glaring eyes, as though exhaustion were an insult someone had paid her.

"You're coming back," Wren said. Not a question. A demand wearing a question's clothes.

"We're going to try," Lena said.

"No." Wren stepped forward and grabbed Lena's sleeve, her small fingers digging in with surprising strength. "You're *coming back*. That's different. Trying is what people say when they've already decided they won't."

Lena crouched so they were eye to eye. Up close, the girl smelled of woodsmoke and river water and the particular dust of roads no child should walk alone. Fifteen years ago, another girl had stood at the mouth of a dark place, being told to wait while an adult went ahead into danger. That girl had waited exactly as long as it took the adult to disappear, and then she had run the other way, and it had saved her life, and she had hated herself for it every day since.

"Wren," she said. "If this goes wrong—if the Court takes me—there's a charm in my left boot. The blue thread one. You remember how it works?"

Wren's jaw tightened. "It finds the person who tied the knot."

"It finds the person who tied the knot. And if the knot goes cold, it means—" She stopped. Saying it aloud made it real, and some cowardly part of her flinched from that. But Wren was watching her with those starless-sky eyes, waiting for honesty, and Lena had promised herself, somewhere between the ford and here, that she would stop lying to children. "It means I'm gone. Really gone. And then you take Dain home, and you let Sorrel feed you until you're round as a dumpling, and you grow up, and you don't spend your whole life looking for me. Promise me that part especially."

"I promise nothing," Wren said.

"That's not how promises work."

"Then I'm doing it right." But her grip on Lena's sleeve loosened, finger by finger, until she was only touching her, not holding her. "Marguerite wouldn't leave."

Lena went still. Of all the things the girl could have said. Marguerite—the name Odile had given the shadow, the name that lived now in a cage beneath a mountain, or worse, in the belly of a dying dragon's flame. Wren had never met her. Wren knew her only from Lena's confession around the dragon's warmth, three nights of telling the whole ugly story while Vaskarath's breath rattled like wind through a broken house. And still the girl had built a loyalty to her out of nothing but words.

"Marguerite didn't get a choice," Lena said gently. "That was the whole crime of it."

"Then make your choice be the opposite." Wren released her sleeve and stepped back, chin up, and for a moment she looked so much like a small grey-eyed warden that Lena almost laughed. Almost. "Go down there and choose everything."

* * *

Sorrel pressed a lantern into Dain's hands before they went in. It was an odd thing, keeper-made: no candle inside, just a lump of dull stone that gave off a slow amber glow, warm rather than bright, like a coal remembering fire.

"Dragon-glass core," she said. "It burns on whatever heat's nearest. Down there, that'll mostly be you." She looked at Lena. "Both of you. Your palm most of all. Don't fight it. The road will want to see where you're going, and honestly, so would I."

Lena turned her hand over. The sigil sat in her palm as it had for fifteen years—the coiled mark Odile had branded into her skin at her apprenticeship, the mark that had made her invisible to wardens and legible to courts, the mark that was, she now knew, less a brand than a lock. A door-shaped thing. A keyhole thing.

It had started glowing two days ago, faintly, the way a hearthstone stays warm after the fire dies. Now, standing at the mouth of the descent, it shone through her glove.

She took the glove off. No point pretending anymore.

"All right," she said to the dark seam in the rock. Her voice came out steadier than she felt, which was its own kind of magic, the oldest kind, the kind that needed no sigil at all. "Let's go meet what we ran from."

* * *

The first mile was ordinary rock, and then it wasn't.

The tunnel opened without warning into a vein of glass—not glass laid by any craftsman but glass *grown*, walls smooth and curved and faintly green, as though the mountain had swallowed a piece of the sea and kept it. Their footsteps rang oddly. The lantern's amber light slid along the surfaces and came back changed, doubled, tripled, so that walking through those tunnels felt like walking inside a bottle with a hundred other versions of yourself, all slightly delayed, all slightly wrong.

Dain walked half a step behind her, the way he'd taken to doing since the ford. Guarding her back. Or maybe just unable to look away from the sigil-light spilling between her fingers.

"It's brighter," he said, for the third time in an hour.

"I know."

"Should it be?"

"I think it's counting distance." She flexed her fingers. The light pulsed, slow and patient, like a heartbeat that wasn't hers—or maybe it was hers, the one she'd left behind, answering across fifteen years and a mile of rock. "Odile used to say the Court's roads only open for what they've marked. I used to think that meant the mark was a leash." She watched the green light run up her wrist. "Now I think it might be a homing signal. Something down there is calling its own."

"And you're answering anyway."

"I'm answering anyway."

He was quiet for a few steps. In the doubled light his face became several faces, all of them tired, all of them stubborn. "My sister used to say something like that," he said finally. "About fear. She said fear is just a compass that points at what matters." A pause. "She got it from a book. Wardens aren't supposed to read books, so officially I'm telling you she made it up."

"Officially noted."

They walked on. The air grew warmer, which should have been comforting and instead felt like walking deeper into a fever. Somewhere far below—too deep for ears, arriving instead through the soles of the feet, through the bones—something vast exhaled.

Vaskarath was above them, in the roots of the mountain, tended by Sorrel. But this breath came from further down. This was the other lung. The Undercourt, breathing in its sleep, or pretending to sleep, the way creditors pretend to sleep until the debtor walks past the door.

Lena's palm flared. She gasped.

"What is it?"

"It's not counting anymore." She stared at her hand. The sigil had stopped pulsing. It burned steady now, a small green sun cupped in her flesh, throwing shadows up her arm—and the shadows were wrong, or rather they were *right* in a way that frightened her, because among them, cast on the glass wall beside her own shadow, was a second shape. Girl-shaped. Her height. Her stance. Standing exactly where a companion would stand.

Dain saw it too. His hand went to his knife out of pure warden reflex, and then stopped, because you can't cut a shadow.

"It's not her," Lena said quickly. "It can't be. She's below—in the bazaar, in the cage, wherever they keep sold things. This is just—" She searched for the word and hated every candidate. "An echo. The road showing me what's at the end of it."

"The road," Dain said, "has a cruel sense of hospitality."

They descended. The glass tunnels spiraled, sometimes narrowing to single-file squeezes where the walls pressed close and warm as a throat, sometimes opening into chambers tall as cathedrals where their lantern was a spark and the sigil was the only true light. And everywhere, the doubling. Every surface reflected them, and every reflection carried that extra figure, closer each time—or so it seemed. Lena stopped measuring. Some knowledge you carry better unmeasured.

* * *

They ate at what might have been noon, sitting on a ledge above a tunnel that dropped away into green-black depth. Hard bread, dried plums, water from Sorrel's skins that tasted faintly of mineral and dragon-warmth. Dain chewed mechanically, watching the dark below. Lena found she couldn't eat at all, and after a while she set the bread down and admitted why.

"When I ran from Odile," she said, "I ran for three days. Through the fen country, in autumn, with nothing. And every night I told myself the same story: whatever she did, whatever she sold, it wasn't mine to fix. I was fifteen. I didn't sign anything. The debt was hers." She turned the bread over in her hands without eating it. "And then I spent fifteen years building a life on top of that story, and the story held, because I never let anyone close enough to ask questions. Never twice for the same customer. Never be remembered. You know the rules."

"I know the rules," Dain said. "I arrested four people for breaking them."

"And now look at us." She almost smiled. "Here's what I couldn't know at fifteen, Dain. The debt doesn't care whose name is on it. It collects from whoever's nearest. Odile bought my escape with Marguerite, and the bill has traveled fifteen years to find me, and it found Wren first, and it nearly found her at the ford, and it will keep finding people I—" She stopped. The word *love* sat in her throat like a bone. She was out of practice saying it. "People I keep," she finished. "So no. I'm not walking into this because it's mine. I'm walking in because it's the last place it can follow me from."

Dain was quiet a moment. Then he said, "You know that's the same speech wardens give before they do something stupid and brave. Word for word. We train on it."

"Do you survive it?"

"Some of us." He looked at her, and in the green light his eyes were very clear. "Corvess means raven. Did I ever tell you? My mother thought it was funny, naming a border family after a bird that eats carrion. She said ravens are the only ones who go down to the battlefield and come back with something useful."

"Is that what you're doing?" Lena asked softly. "Coming back with something useful?"

"I'm coming back with *you*," he said. "Useful remains to be seen."

* * *

The tunnels changed again near the bottom, and this time the change was not in the rock.

It was in the sound. Or rather, in the arrival of sound where there had been none: a murmur, distant and layered, like a market heard through several walls. Voices. Many voices. Some speaking, some singing, some making sounds that were neither. The Undercourt, audible now, the way you hear the sea before you see it.

And the sigil answered.

Lena had to press her hand against her chest to bear it. The light poured between her fingers, green as the glass, green as the doors they'd seen opening in the mountainside from the ridge, and along the tunnel walls the reflections multiplied and multiplied until there were hundreds of her, hundreds of Dains, and beside every image of her walked the girl-shaped shadow, no longer behind, no longer beside, but *ahead*—leading. Walking the way she walked. Turning her head the way she turned it, to check on the people behind her.

"She's leading us down," Lena whispered.

"Or the road is," Dain said. But he didn't sound sure either.

The last stretch of tunnel sloped steeply, and the glass underfoot grew slick, and the murmur swelled to a hum, and the hum resolved, horribly, into individual voices—snatches of bargains, fragments of prices, a woman's voice saying *one soul, passage for two, agreed and witnessed* in cadences Lena recognized with a sickness that went all the way through her. Odile's voice. Recorded in the road itself, in the glass, fifteen years old and perfectly preserved. The mountain remembered transactions. The mountain had been listening the whole time.

Lena pressed her palm flat against the wall, and the voice stopped, stung into silence by the sigil's flare. But she had heard enough. One soul. Passage for two. Agreed and witnessed.

Passage for two. For Odile and the girl. For the escape.

Her legs nearly failed her on the last turning. Dain caught her elbow and held it, silently, and did not ask, and she loved him a little for the silence, and hated that love too because it was one more thing the Court could price.

* * *

The tunnel ended at a threshold.

There was no door—that was the first strange thing. Just a place where the green glass gave way to something older, black stone veined with silver, worn smooth by feet over centuries. The murmur beyond it was loud now, unmistakably a city: a market, a court, a civilization of the sold and the sellers. Warm air rose from it, smelling of incense and iron and something sweet underneath, like fruit going soft.

The sigil in Lena's palm burned white-green, steady, complete. Whatever it had been counting, the count was done.

Beyond the threshold, the dark opened into vastness, and lights moved in it—lanterns, or things shaped like lanterns, hanging and drifting and descending in slow spirals, and Lena understood with a lurch that some of those lights were souls. The bazaar. The market of sold things. They were looking straight down into it from the lip of the keeper's road, like thieves at a treasure house, like debtors at the creditor's window.

Dain set the lantern down and checked his knife, his bindings, the warden's kit he'd carried since Greyharbor and never explained. He moved like a man packing for a job, not a miracle.

"How do we play it?" he asked.

"We don't have a plan. Plans are for places that follow rules." Lena looked at her burning palm, then past it, down into the drifting lights. Somewhere down there hung a grey girl-shape wearing her face, and somewhere down there sat a Regent who collected debts the way other creatures collected shells, and somewhere in between was a bargain she intended to break. "We walk in like we belong. The sigil says I belong. Let's find out what else it says."

She stepped over the threshold.

Behind her, in the glass, a hundred reflections stepped with her—and this time, when she glanced back, the girl-shaped shadow among them had stopped at the line of black stone. Waiting. Watching. And as the light of the bazaar rose around them and the glass dimmed behind, Lena could have sworn—would swear later, to Wren, to anyone who asked—that the shadow raised one hand.

Not waving.

*Pointing.*

Down. Deeper. Toward the heart of the market, toward a light that burned a different color than all the rest—grey, flickering, familiar as her own face in a mirror.

Toward Marguerite.
