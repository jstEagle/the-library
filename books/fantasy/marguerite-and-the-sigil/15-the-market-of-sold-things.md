## 15. The Market of Sold Things

The road down was made of glass.

Not glass like a window—glass like a throat. The keeper's tunnel curved and narrowed as it descended, its walls smoothed by centuries of dragon-breath into something translucent and faintly green, so that when Dain's lantern swung, the light moved through the stone itself instead of along it. It was like walking inside a vein. It was like being swallowed slowly and politely.

Lena kept her palm open at her side, the way Sorrel had told her to.

"Let it lead," the old keeper had said, pressing a stub of tallow candle into Wren's hands and refusing to look at any of them for longer than a breath. "The sigil remembers the way home. All brands do."

*Home.* Lena turned the word over as she walked and found it had no bottom. Fifteen years she had run from this direction, and now her own hand glowed like a coal in a draft, brighter with every hundred steps, pulling her down toward the thing that had been sold in her name.

Dain walked half a pace behind her, the way he always did—warden's habit, guarding the rear even when there was no rear left to guard. His arm was bound where the hollow guard had opened it in the bazaar above, three days ago now, or four; time ran strangely under the mountain. He had not complained once. He had not spoken much at all since they'd passed the last of the keeper's road-markers and entered territory that no warden's oath covered.

"You're doing the thing," he said finally.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you count your breathing like it's a spell." She could hear the shrug in his voice. "My sister did that. Before exams. Before executions."

Lena almost smiled. Almost. "And did it work?"

"It worked until it didn't." A pause. The lantern light bent green through the walls. "Then it worked again, later, when she needed it to. That's the trick nobody tells you about fear. It comes back around."

They walked on. Somewhere far below—farther than far, deeper than deep—something vast exhaled, and the whole tunnel shivered, and a fine rain of warm dust came down like flour through light.

Vaskarath. Breathing. Guttering.

Lena pressed her hand flat against the glass wall and felt the heat of the mountain's grief come through it, and her sigil flared so bright it threw their shadows up ahead of them, running toward the dark.

* * *

The Undercourt announced itself first by smell.

Not rot—she had expected rot, had braced for it the way you brace for a blow. It was worse than rot. It was the smell of a house closed for winter: cold ash, dried flowers, wax, and underneath it all something sweet and wrong, like fruit going over in a bowl no one will admit to owning.

Then sound. A murmur, vast and layered, like a city heard through a wall. Voices. Hundreds of voices. Thousands.

Then light—not the green glow of the tunnels but a gold so pale it looked like the memory of gold—and the tunnel simply ended, opening like a mouth onto—

Lena stopped walking.

She had imagined a court. Thrones, maybe. Pillars. Something out of the old woodcuts in Odile's forbidden books, all spires and shadows and elegant cruelty. What she saw instead was a market.

It filled a cavern so large the ceiling was a rumor—a darkness overhead studded with drifting motes of pale fire that might have been lamps or might have been watching. Terraces of black stone stepped away below them, tier on tier, and every tier was stalls. Canopies of woven shadow. Counters of bone-white rock. And moving through it all, crowds: figures tall and thin as herons, figures squat and wide as doors, figures that were mostly suggestion, mostly smoke, wearing shapes the way other people wore borrowed coats. The herald's people. The Court's people. Buyers and sellers and things that were both.

And above every stall, hanging from hooks of dark iron or floating in cages of green glass or simply tethered by nothing at all—

Lights.

Little lights. Flickering, wavering, each one a different color if you looked long enough, though most were grey. They hung in rows like lanterns at a festival. Like drying herbs. Like laundry.

Lena's stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

"They're souls," she said. Her voice came out flat and small, a coin dropped on stone. "Dain. Those are *souls*. They're selling—"

"I see them." Dain's voice had gone very quiet, very level—the voice he used when the alternative was shouting. His knuckles were white around the lantern handle. "Warden training says observe before engaging. Warden training has never once prepared me for anything actually worth observing."

A soul near the nearest stall drifted against its glass cage, and for half a heartbeat it had a face. An old man's face, puzzled, patient, pressing at the wall of his prison the way a sleeper presses at a dream. Then it subsided. The stall's proprietor—a heron-thin figure in robes the color of pond water—turned it idly with one long finger, the way a shopkeeper turns a bolt of cloth to catch the light.

Lena looked away and immediately hated herself for looking away.

"We find mine," she said. "We find mine and we get out. In and out. No stopping, no looking, no—" Her voice cracked on the word and she rebuilt it. "No bargaining. Whatever we see. Whatever they offer. We are nobody here. We're just two more customers."

"Lena." Dain touched her shoulder, brief and hard. "Your hand."

She looked down. The sigil was blazing now, gold bleeding through her fingers, throwing light across the black stone like a spilled lamp. Every figure within twenty paces had turned to look at it. A ripple went through the crowd—not alarm, not yet. Interest. The interest of appraisers.

"Charming," Lena whispered. "Fifteen years invisible, and the moment I walk in, I'm the loudest thing in the room."

"Can you cover it?"

"With what? I don't have a glove, Dain, I have a *soul-brand*." But she folded her fingers over her palm anyway, and the light leaked between them like water through a fist, and they started down the stairs together, two small figures descending into a market full of hung lights, one of them glowing.

* * *

The bazaar had districts. Lena learned them the way she'd once learned Greyharbor's smuggler rows—by what was for sale and how the sellers watched you pass.

There was a row of memories: shallow dishes of silver liquid, each holding a scene that played when you leaned close. A wedding. A drowning. A child's first snow. A woman paid three teeth for a dish holding an afternoon in an orchard, and she carried it away cradled against her chest like an infant, and Lena had to keep walking because her legs wanted to stop and her rules said *never be remembered, never be noticed, never stop moving.*

There was a row of names. Actual names, bottled, labeled in a script that hurt to read sideways. A squat door-shaped creature bought a name off a hook and drank it, and afterward stood a little straighter, a little more *somebody*, and Lena understood with a lurch that somewhere a person had just become slightly harder to remember.

There was a row of futures, a row of griefs, a row of first loves still warm in their jars.

And everywhere, everywhere, the lantern-souls. Grey ones mostly. A few gold. One, impossibly, a soft green, and when Lena passed beneath it she heard—faintly, unmistakably—humming. A tune Wren might know. A tune from before the outlawry.

"Don't," Dain murmured.

"I'm not doing anything."

"You're memorizing the layout. You're counting exits. You're building a plan to buy all of them, and we both know it, and neither of us has anything they take as payment." He steered her gently by the elbow, warden-hands, steady even wounded. "One soul. Yours. That's the mission. Everything else is after."

"And if mine isn't here?"

He didn't answer, which was its own answer, and then the crowd thinned, and the terraces rose around them, and Lena felt the sigil pull—actually *pull*, a gentle insistent tug behind her sternum, like a hooked fish testing the line—and she knew.

"This way," she said, and stopped needing Dain's guidance, because her hand knew the market better than she did. It led her past the memory-row, past the name-row, up two terraces into a quieter quarter where the stalls were fewer and grander and the goods were displayed not on counters but in cases of green glass taller than a man. The light here was older. The silence here had weight.

The tug grew stronger. Stronger. Then—

It stopped.

Lena stood in front of a case at the quarter's heart, alone among the grand stalls, and looked at what hung inside it, and the world went very simple and very loud.

It was a girl.

Small. Made of grey light, flickering like a candle in a doorway, like a reflection in disturbed water. She hung from no hook and sat in no cage; she simply floated at the center of the case, turning slowly, and she was wearing Lena's face.

Younger. Fifteen years younger—the face from before, from the cottage, from the ledger, from the night Odile had knelt with a knife of moonlight and a bowl of shadow and told her *hold still, love, this is the only way anyone lets you live.* The girl-shape's eyes were closed. Her hands were cupped in front of her chest, the way children cup moths. Around her wrists, thin rings of green fire turned without touching her, patient as debt.

"That's me," Lena said. She hadn't meant to say it aloud. "That's—that's *me*, that's my—"

"Marguerite."

The voice came from beside the case, and Lena spun, sigil flaring, Dain's knife already whispering free of its sheath.

The seller was old. Old the way mountains are old, old the way the smell of the place was old—robed in layered greys that might have been silk or might have been smoke, face a smooth oval with features arranged like an afterthought. It inclined its head with terrible courtesy.

"Forgive me. Lot forty-one. Marguerite. We do grow attached to the inventory." Its oval face tilted. "You would be the original. How rare. Most originals never come to visit."

Lena's mouth was dry. "I want to buy her back."

"Of course you do." The seller gestured, and the case's green glass seemed to deepen, and within it the girl—Marguerite, *her*, the missing half of her, the piece that had been paying rent in hell for fifteen years while Lena ate stolen bread and slept in rafters—drifted another slow turn. "Though I wonder. Do you know what lot forty-one is *for*?"

"She's for me. She was taken for passage. My mistress sold—"

"Your mistress sold, yes, and the Court bought, and the Court is not sentimental." The seller's voice never changed pitch. It was like listening to a ledger read aloud. "Souls are fuel, little witch. Fuel burns. And there is one fire in this world older than our ledgers, one flame too proud to gutter quietly, and our Regent has grown tired of waiting for it to die on its own schedule."

The cold arrived before understanding did. It climbed Lena's spine vertebra by vertebra.

"The dragon," she said.

"Vaskarath." The seller pronounced the name with relish, savoring it. "His heart-flame gutters because his shadow-half was sold to us an age ago—we hold it still, a fine vintage. But a shadow alone won't reignite him. It takes a *whole* soul to seed a dying flame. And lot forty-one"—a long grey finger indicated the case, the girl, the face, the face, *her face*—"is a whole soul. Split, aged, ripened in our keeping. When the wards fell, the Regent's design completed itself. Your dragon dies. We feed him your girl. He lives forever—bound, banked, enslaved to the flame we lit in him. The mountain burns eternally. The Court holds the match."

Somewhere very far away, Dain was saying her name. Lena didn't hear it properly. She was looking at the girl in the glass—at the cupped hands, at the closed eyes, at the small mouth that was her own mouth—and doing arithmetic she couldn't stop doing.

Fifteen years. Fifteen years of luck-knots and fever-charms and never twice for the same customer, fifteen years of being no one, of burning her records, of running—all of it, every careful rule, every lonely year, had been the *aging process*. Odile hadn't saved her. Odile had *cellared* her. Kept her hidden and halved and growing, like wine laid down for a buyer who hadn't yet been born.

And the buyer was here. And the price of the dragon's chains was her.

"No," Lena said.

The seller waited.

"No. You don't get to—you don't get to make her a *lamp*." Her voice shook and she let it. "You don't get to chain a living thing to a fire with pieces of me. I came here to take her back. Name your price. Anything I have. The sigil, the years, whatever's left—"

"Oh, the sigil." The seller's oval face did something that was almost a smile. "The Regent will be delighted to discuss the sigil. Everyone wishes to discuss the sigil." It spread its grey hands toward the stair at the terrace's end, where the dark gathered thick as velvet, and where—Lena saw it now, had been refusing to see it—pale gold light burned around a doorway tall as a cathedral door, and something within it was already standing. Already waiting. Had perhaps always been waiting.

"But first," the seller said, "the Regent asks that you understand exactly what you've come to collect. Look closely, little witch. Look at what fifteen years of your freedom cost."

Lena looked.

And in the case, in the grey light, in the cage of patient green fire—the girl with her face opened her eyes.

They were Lena's eyes. And they were afraid.

* * *

Behind them, unnoticed at the terrace's edge, a small figure in a keeper's oversized coat slipped out of the crowd's shadow, tallow candle still clutched in one fist, and stared up at the case with eyes gone huge and furious.

"*No,*" Wren said, very quietly, to no one who was listening. "*Nobody gets sold. Not her. Not anybody.*"

And the candle in her hand lit itself.
