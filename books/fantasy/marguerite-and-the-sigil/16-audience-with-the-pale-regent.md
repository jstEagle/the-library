## 16. Audience with the Pale Regent

They came for her at what might have been dawn, though dawn was a rumor down here. Two heralds of green glass stood at the door of her cell and did not knock, because doors in the Undercourt were not things you knocked on; they were things that opened whether you wanted them to or not.

Lena had slept badly on a bench of black stone that was warm in the wrong places, like something recently alive. She'd spent the hours mapping the room by touch: one door, no windows, walls that hummed faintly when she pressed her palm against them—the sigil answering, always answering, like a dog hearing its name called from another room.

She rose before they could make her.

"Lead on," she said, and hated how steady her voice sounded. Steady voices were a kind of lie she'd gotten very good at.

The corridors of the Court were roads, actually—she could feel it now, the old warden-signs Dain had taught her flickering under her feet like half-remembered letters. These weren't tunnels. They were *streets*, ancient ones, worn smooth by feet that had walked them when the world above still had stars. Green glass lanterns burned without flame. Somewhere far off, water moved with a sound too slow for water.

The heralds flanked her without touching her. They didn't need to. The mark on her palm itched with every step, pulling her forward like a hook through soft flesh, and she understood that this was how the Court led people: not by force but by the thing they carried.

*You could turn around,* she told herself. *You could run.*

She thought of Marguerite—the grey girl-shape wearing her face, hanging in that terrible bazaar like a lamp made of everything Lena had cut away—and kept walking.

* * *

The throne room was smaller than she expected.

That was the first cruelty of it, she decided later. Everything about the Undercourt was built to unbalance you: vast bazaars where souls hung like fruit, caverns deep enough to swallow mountains, and then this—a modest hall, low-ceilinged, almost domestic, lit by a single hearth of pale fire that gave no heat. A hearth. Someone had *designed* this to feel like a parlor, like a place where reasonable people sat and discussed terms over tea.

The Pale Regent sat beside the hearth in a chair of woven bone, and it looked up when she entered with an expression of mild, patient interest, the way Odile used to look up from her ledger when Lena came in muddy from the garden.

Lena stopped three paces in. Close enough to seem unafraid. Far enough to run.

"You are the girl Odile Vane apprenticed," the Regent said. Its voice was quiet and dry, like pages turning. "Fifteen years ago. You are the passage she bought."

"I'm the girl she stole out the back door," Lena said. "Whatever she paid, I never saw the coin."

"No." The Regent folded its hands—long grey hands, nails like polished slate. "You were the coin."

The words landed exactly where they were meant to. Lena let the silence stretch rather than fill it, because filling silences was what frightened people did, and she refused, absolutely refused, to be frightened in front of this creature. Her heart was doing its best to betray her anyway.

"Sit," said the Regent, indicating a second chair across the hearth from its own. Matching chairs. Facing each other. A conversation between equals—that was the staging, and Lena had sold enough charms to know that nothing offered freely was free.

She sat.

* * *

"You want your shadow back," the Regent said. "Of course you do. I have watched you since you entered my market. You looked at her—" the grey hands opened slightly, encompassing everything, the bazaar, the hanging lights, the whole economy of stolen selves— "the way starving people look at bread."

"She's not a thing," Lena said. "She's me."

"She is a debt," the Regent corrected gently. "A debt owed to me, held by me, and—this is important, so attend—a debt I am prepared to forgive."

The hearth-fire flickered. In its light the Regent's face was difficult to hold onto, features sliding like reflections in moving water, and Lena realized it was doing it deliberately, letting her see hints of other faces. For one vertiginous moment she saw Odile there—younger, sharp-eyed, laughing at some joke fifteen years dead—and yanked her gaze to the floor.

"Don't do that," she said.

"Do what?"

"Wear her."

Something that might have been amusement crossed the shifting face. "Your mistress wore many faces herself. Did you know that? Toward the end, she wore mine."

Lena's head came up despite herself.

"Sit comfortably," the Regent said. "This is a longer story than yours, and I tell it well."

* * *

"Odile Vane came to me young," the Regent said. "Younger than you. Brilliant, hungry, and marked—oh, yes, marked, the sigil is older than both of us, child, it has passed hand to hand like a candle in a wind for nine hundred years. Each bearer thinks she is the last. Each bearer is wrong."

The pale fire crackled. It made no heat. Lena's palms were sweating anyway.

"She served me," the Regent went on. "As my bound witch. Keeper of my ledgers, walker of my roads, signer of my bargains. Do you know what a bound witch does, Lena Amberdale? No—of course not; Odile taught you nothing true. A bound witch holds the pen. Every soul sold in my court passes through her hand. She writes the price. She witnesses the transfer. She makes it *legal*, and legality, in matters of souls, is the only magic that cannot be broken."

"And she did this," Lena said flatly. "For you."

"For escape," the Regent said. "That was our bargain. Serve me a term of years, and at the end, freedom. Your mistress discovered, as all my bound witches discover, that the term moves. That the ink dries crooked. That freedom, in my court, is a door painted on a wall." It tilted its head. "So she committed treachery. She split her own shadow—no. Not her own."

The pause was surgical.

"She split *yours*. A child's shadow, already loosened by fear, already half-detached by the nights she made you sleep alone in the dark to see if you'd cry. She took the half of you that remembered everything—the bargains, the names, the debt—and she sold it to me as payment for her own contract. And she walked out of my court carrying you, whole-seeming, hollow-hearted, and she hid you in the world above like a coin sewn into a hem."

Lena's hands had closed into fists without her permission. The sigil burned against her fingers.

"You're lying," she said. "Or you're telling it however it serves you. Everyone down here sells the same story twice."

"Ask yourself," the Regent said mildly, "why you have never once been able to remember your mother's face. Ask why you dream of counting. Ask why numbers comfort you more than people." It leaned forward, and the hearth-light steadied, and for just a moment its face was only its own: grey, patient, terribly old. "I am not lying to you, witch. Lying is for creatures who need something. I need nothing. I have everything, including time."

* * *

"So here is my offer," the Regent said, "and I will make it only once, which should tell you how much I value it."

It rose. Up close it was taller than it had seemed, or perhaps height meant nothing to a thing like this; it simply occupied more of the room when it wished to.

"The dragon above dies. You have seen it. Its heart-flame gutters because half its fire was sold to me long ago—I hold that too, along with your shadow, along with ten thousand other small lights. When Vaskarath fails, the wards fail forever, and the world above comes down to meet the world below, and there will be such a reckoning as has not been seen since the first star was lit."

"And you'd rather it didn't," Lena said. "Why? You've got everything."

"I have an *estate*," the Regent said, with the first flicker of something like passion. "Estates require order. Chaos is bad bookkeeping. I would keep the mountain burning, the wards standing, the borders between worlds tidy and enforced. And for that I need a bound witch."

It extended one slate-nailed finger toward her branded palm.

"You, Lena Amberdale. Take back your shadow—whole, restored, every memory returned, fifteen years of theft undone. Take it freely, as a gift, no price. In exchange: the sigil. Surrendered to me, as Odile surrendered hers once upon a time. And you take up the pen. You sit in this chair's shadow and you write the prices and witness the transfers and keep my estate in order, and the dragon lives, and the wards stand, and the stars come home, and everyone you love survives the age that is coming."

The silence after was enormous.

Lena found she was looking at the matching chair across the hearth. At the two seats, facing each other, arranged for conversation. At the low ceiling and the domestic fire and the whole careful architecture of a room designed to feel like a parlor, because that was what a cage looked like when it was built by something patient.

*Odile sat here,* she thought. *In this chair. Across this fire. And she listened to this exact offer, in these exact words, maybe word for word, because a thing like this doesn't improvise—it recycles.*

And Odile had taken it. Had signed. Had served, years, decades, however long, until the term moved and the ink dried crooked and she'd clawed her way out the only way left: by selling a piece of a child.

*That's my inheritance,* Lena thought. *Not the sigil. The chair.*

"You knew she'd send you down eventually," the Regent said, watching her. "Or that you'd come on your own. Either way, you were always walking here. The sigil walks its bearers home. It has done for nine hundred years." It smiled, and the smile was gentle, and that was the worst thing yet. "Say yes, and you can stop running. Isn't that what you have wanted, all these years? A door that locks from the inside? Warmth that doesn't cost you? You could be safe, Lena Amberdale. Safe, and whole, and needed. No more luck-knots sold to strangers who must never remember your face. You would be remembered forever."

God help her, some traitor chamber of Lena's heart turned over at that. *Remembered.* Fifteen years of being nobody, of ducking wardens, of watching customers' eyes slide past her like she was weather. Fifteen years of *never be remembered* carved into her like the brand itself.

And here was a voice offering her permanence, purpose, a seat by a fire, and her own self back into the bargain.

She thought of Dain, bleeding somewhere in the dark with her shadow's light in his arms. She thought of Wren counting vanished constellations with absolute faith. She thought of Sorrel's ruined hands and the dragon breathing smoke like a dying animal, and she thought—clearly, coldly, the way Odile had taught her to think over a ledger—

*What happens to Wren's blood, if I say yes? What happens to the next girl the Court finds valuable? Because someone will hold the pen, and if it isn't me, it'll be someone with fewer reasons to hesitate.*

"If I refuse?" Lena asked.

The Regent settled back into its chair, unhurried, and the hearth dimmed by a degree, and somewhere far above—or below—something began to toll, slow and deep, like a bell counting down.

"Then the offer expires," it said, "and we proceed to collection. Debts do not vanish, witch. They merely change hands." Its grey eyes glittered. "And I should mention—since you will learn it within the hour regardless—that your warden friend has been busy. Theft, in my market. From my shelves. Carrying stolen goods toward the keeper's road even now, wounded, slow, and followed."

Lena was on her feet before she knew she'd moved.

"Ah," said the Pale Regent, folding its hands with satisfaction. "There it is. The answer I was hoping for. Sit down, child—we are not finished. But now, at last, we negotiate honestly."

Behind the Regent, the hearth-fire flared green, and in its light Lena saw, reflected in the glass wall like a scene in a mirror, a small figure limping through the dark tunnels with a caged light cradled against his chest—and behind him, gliding silent as spilled ink, three hollow guards closing fast.
