## 17. The Theft

Dain Corvess had spent eleven years learning how to stand still.

It was the first thing they taught you at the warden academy, before wards, before blade-work, before the border drills that broke boys into men: a warden's greatest weapon was his stillness. Move and you were seen. Breathe wrong and you were heard. Want something too badly and the wanting leaked out of you like heat from a cracked window, and everything with eyes turned toward it.

So he stood still in the shadow of a stall selling bottled griefs, and he watched Lena Amberdale walk toward the Pale Regent's dais, and he did not let himself want anything at all.

The Market of Sold Things spread beneath the mountain like a city built inside a lung. Green glass arched overhead, veined with slow light, and from every arch hung lanterns — but the lanterns were souls. He could see them now, having been down here long enough for his eyes to stop lying to him. Small flames in cages of glass and bone, each one flickering with a color that meant something. Blue for sorrow. Gold for joy. A sickly violet for the ones who had sold themselves willingly, which the Court priced highest, because willingness was rare.

And somewhere in all that hanging light was a grey girl-shape wearing Lena's face.

*Marguerite,* Odile had named her, according to Maren. Fifteen years ago. One soul, split from a living girl, sold to buy passage out of Vellamark. Dain had turned that fact over so many times on the road that its edges had worn smooth. His sister Sera had died on the border stones the night the wards first flickered, and he had spent eleven years telling himself the failure was his — that if he'd been faster, sharper, less drunk on his own certainty, she'd have lived. Then he'd learned the truth: the wards hadn't failed. They'd been *sold out of*, one transaction at a time, by people like Odile Amberdale who treated the fire under the world as currency.

He should hate Lena for what her mistress did. Some nights, walking the ash road with Wren asleep against his back, he tried to. It never took. You couldn't hate someone for surviving a theft committed against them before they could walk.

"Remember," Lena had said, an hour ago, in the alcove behind the stall of bottled griefs. Her palm glowed faintly through her glove, the sigil brightening as the mountain deepened around them. "I talk. The Regent likes to talk — Sorrel said so, the Regent likes to hear itself be clever. While I'm talking, you find her."

"And if I can't find her?"

Lena had looked at him with those tired, level eyes. "Then we've lost, and I serve the Court for a hundred years, and you take Wren home and tell her stories about me." She'd almost smiled. "But you'll find her. You're a warden. Finding things is what you do."

Finding things. Right. What he actually did, these days, was lose them.

* * *

The bazaar had rules. Everything down here had rules; the Undercourt ran on contracts the way the surface ran on sunlight. Dain had spent two days mapping them while Lena's sigil bought them passage — not stealing, not yet, just watching, because a warden planned a breach the way a surgeon planned a cut.

Rule one: nothing in the market moved without being paid for. The hollow guards didn't chase shoplifters; they waited for the debt to register. Which meant the crime wasn't taking the cage. The crime was the moment the ledger noticed the cage was gone.

Rule two: the ledgers were alive. He'd seen them — long scrolls of pale skin stretched across frames of black wood, ink crawling across them like ants, recording every exchange in the bazaar in real time. There was a ledger at every archway, and a master ledger somewhere near the Regent's dais, fat as a fed tick.

Rule three: the hollow guards saw warmth. Not light, not motion — warmth. They drifted through the aisles like drowned men through kelp, faces smooth as eggs, and where a living body radiated heat they turned, slow as weather, and converged.

So Dain had done the hardest training of his life in the last two days: he had learned to be cold.

Warden discipline helped. So did fear. He stood in the lee of the grief-stall and let the mountain's chill sink through his coat, through his skin, into the marrow, and he thought about Sera's funeral — the closed casket, because there hadn't been enough of her left to look at — and the wanting went out of him like a snuffed lamp. Grief made excellent insulation. He'd had eleven years of practice.

A hollow guard drifted past close enough to touch. Its face turned toward him, held, and slid on.

He exhaled through his teeth and moved.

* * *

The cages hung in tiers, and the higher tiers held the older souls. Dain climbed the back of the stall — iron ribs bolted into the glass wall, slick with condensation — and worked his way along a maintenance ledge no wider than his boot, some ancient keeper's walk from the days when this cavern had been something other than a marketplace. Below him, the bazaar murmured. Merchants whose bodies were mostly suggestion haggled with customers whose pockets held years instead of coin. Somewhere a bell rang, and a hundred lantern-souls flickered in answer, like birds startled in a roost.

He found her on the fourth tier.

She was smaller than he expected. That was the thing nobody told you about souls — they weren't grand. They were small, and particular, and heartbreakingly specific. The grey girl-shape pressed her palms against the inside of the glass and looked out with Lena's eyes, except younger, unguarded, the way Lena might have looked at fifteen before the world taught her to level her gaze. When Dain's shadow fell across the cage, the girl-shape looked up.

She didn't speak. Souls down here mostly didn't; the Court sanded the words off them during processing. But she raised one hand and pressed it flat against the glass, and Dain felt the gesture land in his chest like a fist.

*I know you,* the gesture said. Or maybe: *You came.*

"Yeah," he breathed, barely a sound. "Hold on."

The cage was glass and bone, sealed with a wax seal stamped with the Court's mark — a door, closed, over a flame. Warden training said: don't break seals. Breaking seals announced themselves. Every ward in the world, surface or under, registered a broken seal the way a body registered a wound.

But warden training also said: *every lock has a seam.* And this cage had been hung here a long time. The wax had dried and micro-cracked along one edge, and the bone frame had a joint at the base where the maker had been lazy or hurried or both. Dain drew his knife — the plain steel one, not the warded blade, because warded blades sang down here and singing got you killed — and set the edge into the joint, and began, with the patience of eleven years of standing still, to lever.

Below, across the sea of lantern-light, the Regent's voice rose like smoke.

* * *

He couldn't hear the words from the fourth tier. He didn't need to. He knew Lena's voice when she was buying time — he'd heard it in the tollhouse when she'd talked Maren past her grief, and on the road when she'd talked a warden patrol into believing three fugitives were pilgrims. It went low and even and just slightly bored, the voice of a woman with nowhere better to be, and it was the most dangerous sound she made, because it meant she was terrified and refusing to show it.

He hoped to every star that had gone dark that she was using it now.

The joint gave with a click no louder than a knuckle cracking. Dain froze, counted ten heartbeats. Nothing converged. He eased the bone frame apart at the base, enough to slide two fingers in, and found the inner mechanism — a simple latch, absurdly simple, because the Court had never imagined anyone would steal *back*. Theft flowed one direction here. The whole economy depended on it.

The latch opened. The cage swung on its bone hinge, and the grey girl-shape spilled into his hands like cold water, like smoke, like a weight that wasn't a weight at all and yet heavier than anything he'd ever carried.

Her light touched his face. Up close he could see she wasn't quite grey — there was green in her, deep in the flicker, the same green as the sigil on Lena's palm. She looked up at him again, and this time her mouth moved, though no sound came.

*Marguerite,* he mouthed back, because Maren had said names mattered, that a soul with its name held together better than one without. "Marguerite. I've got you."

And that was when the ledger screamed.

* * *

It wasn't a sound, exactly. It was a *change* — the master ledger near the dais flaring white, ink boiling across its pages, and every hollow guard in the bazaar stopping at once, a hundred drowned faces lifting toward the fourth tier like compass needles finding north.

Dain didn't wait to understand. He tucked Marguerite against his chest — she weighed nothing and everything — and dropped from the ledge.

He'd trained for falls like this. Knees soft, roll through the shoulder, come up moving. What he hadn't trained for was landing in the middle of a market where every exit suddenly had a guard drifting toward it, and where the merchants were turning to watch with the mild interest of people who had seen this play before and knew how it ended.

He ran anyway.

The aisle between the grief-stall and the memory-bottlers was narrow and crowded, and Dain went through it the way water goes through cracks — shoulder, elbow, apology, gone. Someone shouted a price at him. Someone else laughed. The Court's subjects, he realized wildly, had never seen anyone run *toward* an exit without paying, and half of them were too astonished to raise the alarm.

Then the first hollow guard rounded the arch ahead of him, and astonishment stopped mattering.

Up close, the guards were worse than they'd seemed from a distance. Their smooth faces weren't empty — they were *erased*, features worn away like letters on an old coin, and where a mouth should have been there was only a shallow dent that opened, as it neared him, into darkness. Cold poured off it. Real cold, the cold of the space between stars, and Marguerite shuddered against his chest and dimmed.

Dain did the arithmetic in half a second. Left: guard. Right: wall of stalls. Behind: more guards, converging, fast now, their drift become a glide become something like hunger.

He went right, through the memory-bottler's stall, smashing through a shelf of bottled afternoons that burst around him in smells — cut grass, bread, rain on hot stone — and out the far side into the service lanes behind the market, where the green glass walls sweated and the keeper's old maintenance stairs spiraled down into dark.

They followed. Of course they followed. The ledger had his scent now, or his heat, or his name — whatever the Court used instead of blood, it had it, and the guards came down the stairs behind him in silence, which was somehow infinitely worse than screaming.

* * *

He lost them in the glass tunnels. Not by speed — he was bleeding from somewhere by then, a gash along his ribs he didn't remember earning, and his breath was coming in rags — but by cold. He found a stretch of tunnel where meltwater ran along the floor from the mountain's frozen veins, and he lay down in it, full length, Marguerite cupped beneath his body like a man sheltering a candle-flame from wind, and let the water pull his heat away until he was nothing but another cold thing in a cold place.

The guards swept past him twice. Once, one of them paused so close he could have reached out and touched its erased face, and he lay in the freezing water looking up at it and thought, with strange calm: *Sera, if there's anything of you left anywhere, now would be the time.*

The guard moved on.

He lay there until his teeth stopped chattering, which took a long time, and then he got up because staying meant dying of cold instead of teeth, and between those two deaths he chose teeth. Marguerite pulsed weakly against his chest. She had wrapped herself around the wound in his side — he could feel it, a pressure like a cool hand — and where she touched him the bleeding slowed.

"Don't," he rasped. "Save it. You'll need it."

Souls couldn't sigh, but she managed something like one, and dimmed obediently, and Dain Corvess — disgraced warden, breaker of his last law, thief — began to climb.

* * *

The way back to the rendezvous took hours, or days; time ran strangely below the mountain, pooled and slow. He navigated by the method Sorrel had taught them: follow the warmth of the stone. The dragon's failing fire still heated the deep rock, faintly, unevenly, like a body cooling in bed, and where the stone was warmest, the living paths ran. Twice he had to hide again. Once he passed a chamber where the walls were lined with empty cages, thousands of them, and he understood with a lurch that the Court was preparing for inventory — preparing for the day the wards stayed fallen and the surface became a market too.

That image put strength in his legs that blood alone couldn't account for.

By the time he reached the alcove behind the great stair where they'd agreed to meet — *if you're not there by the third bell, assume I'm dead,* Lena had said, and he'd said, *same,* and neither of them had laughed — his vision had narrowed to a tunnel of its own, edged in grey. He leaned against the glass and slid down it and sat, because standing had stopped being negotiable.

Marguerite lifted her head from his chest.

She was brighter than she'd been in the cage — feeding on proximity to him, maybe, or on hope, or on the simple fact of movement toward her other half. She looked up the stairwell, where footsteps were echoing down, quick and light, and then she looked at Dain, and she smiled.

It was Lena's smile. Exactly Lena's, the small crooked one she probably thought she kept hidden, and seeing it on a face made of grey light cracked something open in Dain's chest that eleven years of stillness had held shut.

Footsteps. A glove pushed back from a glowing palm. Lena came around the stair's turn at a run, Wren a blur behind her, and stopped dead at the sight of him — sitting in his own cooling blood, soaked to the bone, grinning like an idiot.

"You're late," she said, and her voice broke in the middle of it.

"Had to make a withdrawal." He fumbled the words out through chattering teeth. "The ledger noticed. We need to move — the whole Court will—"

Then the light hit him.

Not Marguerite's light. Something vaster, rising up the stairwell from the depths of the mountain, green-gold and furious, and with it came a sound like a door the size of the sky being thrown open, and a voice that was not a voice saying a word that was not a word:

***Thief.***

Lena's hand found his arm and hauled him upright. "Run first," she said. "Explain later."

Behind them, down in the dark, the Regent's hunt had begun.
