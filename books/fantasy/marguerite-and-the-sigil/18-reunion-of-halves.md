## 18. Reunion of Halves

The shadow-soul weighed nothing and everything.

Dain carried it up from the bazaar cradled against his chest like a wounded bird, blood sheeting down his left arm from the gash the hollow guard had opened in him, and when he set it into my hands I understood at once why he had staggered under it. It was light. Not weight—light. The kind of light that presses on you, that makes your bones feel borrowed.

It was a girl made of grey smoke and guttering silver, no taller than Wren, and she wore my face.

Not my face as it is now—thirty-one years of squinting over charm-work, of flinching from wardens, of learning to be forgettable. She wore my face at sixteen, the night Odile split me in two on the floor of her cottage while I screamed into a rag soaked in willow-bark tincture. She was the part of me that had been cut away so the Court couldn't find her: my boldness, my hunger, my terrible bright wanting. Everything I had spent fifteen years pretending I didn't need.

"Hello," she said, and her voice was mine, younger, unafraid. "You got old."

"I got careful," I said. "There's a difference."

"Not much of one." She drifted closer, and where she passed the air went warm, like standing near an oven door. "You kept the fear. I kept everything else."

Behind us, Vaskarath breathed.

I had thought I knew what a dragon's breathing sounded like from stories—the roar, the thunder of wings. Nobody tells you that a dying dragon breathes like a bellows with a torn seam: long, labored, each exhale a little thinner than the last. The great chamber at the mountain's root was warm only because he was in it, and his warmth was going out like a hearth nobody fed. His scales had gone the grey of old ash. One eye, vast and amber and ancient, watched us without blame, which was somehow worse than blame.

Sorrel knelt by the dragon's jaw, one hand flat against a scale the size of a barn door, murmuring the keeper's cadences I'd heard her use before—low, rhythmic, less prayer than conversation. She didn't look up when Dain slumped against the tunnel wall, pressing his sleeve to his arm. She didn't look up when I held my soul in my two hands and shook.

"Here's the arithmetic," Sorrel said at last, because she was the sort of person who said the arithmetic out loud when everyone else was praying someone else would. "His heart-flame gutters because half of it was sold below, same as your shadow was sold. One flame for another—that's how the Court writes its contracts. Feed the girl-shape to his fire, and the mountain burns again. The wards rebuild. Greyharbor sleeps safe behind walls of dragon-light." She finally turned, and her eyes were red-rimmed and steady. "And you spend the rest of your life half-made. Hollowed in a way no charm can patch. Or—"

"Or I take her back," I said. "Merge. Become whole. And the flame goes out, and the wards stay fallen, and every hollow thing in the world walks through them into every town with a name." My voice didn't crack. I was proud of that, in a distant, useless way. "Those are the choices. That's the whole ledger."

"That's the whole ledger," Sorrel agreed quietly.

The shadow-girl turned her grey face up toward me. "You could just take it," she said. "I wouldn't fight you. I've been waiting fifteen years in a lantern. Do you know what that's like? Being looked at? Being *priced*?" Her smoke-hands curled. "Take me back and burn bright and let the big lizard manage his own death. He's had three thousand years. You've had thirty-one."

"Hey," Dain said from the wall, hoarse. "Don't call him a lizard. He can probably hear you."

"He can definitely hear me," the shadow said, "and I don't care. That's what I'm *for*."

Vaskarath's eye closed, slowly, the lid coming down like a harbor gate. When he spoke, his voice wasn't sound so much as pressure—a vibration in the rock, in my teeth, in the blood behind my eyes.

*"The small one speaks truly. I have lived long, and I am tired, and I did not ask a girl to die for my fire."*

"You didn't ask," I said. "But the Court wrote the contract anyway, and the contract doesn't care who asked."

*"No,"* the dragon said. *"It never does. That is why it must fall."*

I looked down at the girl in my hands. My hands were shaking now; the pride about my voice seemed very far away. Fifteen years I had built a life on the shape of this moment without knowing it. Every rule I'd made—never big magic, never twice for the same customer, never be remembered—was a wall around the hole where she used to be. I had told myself I was prudent. I had been amputated, and I had called the stump wisdom.

If I took her back, I became whole, and the world outside this mountain burned slowly, town by town, starting with the ones too poor to matter. If I gave her up, I saved the world and spent the rest of my life as half a woman, walking around the hole forever, calling the stump wisdom until the day I died believing it.

There was no third line in the ledger. I checked. I checked the way you check a locked door you already know is locked, rattling the handle, hating the handle.

That was when we heard footsteps on the keeper's road.

Small feet. Fast ones.

"Oh no," Dain said. "Oh, absolutely not—"

Wren came around the last bend of the tunnel at a dead sprint, hair full of ash, Sorrel's spare cloak trailing off one shoulder like a captured flag—and behind her, moving with the resigned speed of a woman who had clearly lost an argument somewhere above and was now committed to the consequences, came Sorrel herself, still wearing her keeper's chains.

"I told her to stay with Maren," Sorrel said. "I told her in words. Plain words."

"You said *stay here*," Wren panted, skidding to a stop at the edge of the chamber. "You didn't say *why*. And 'stay here' isn't a reason, it's just a thing people say when they've decided children are furniture." She planted herself in front of me, chest heaving, chin up, and looked at the grey girl in my hands with an expression I had learned to dread, because it was the expression she wore right before she said something true. "That's Marguerite."

The shadow-girl blinked. "What did you call me?"

"Marguerite." Wren said it the way she recited constellations—carefully, like a word that had to be handled gently or it would go out. "Odile named you. Before she sold you. I found it in the ledger, back at the cottage, in the margin, in tiny writing. *For the girl's other half, a name, so the Court can't call her by the first.* Names are doors, Lena. You taught me that. You can't sell a thing with a name unless the buyer knows the name, and Odile gave you a false one to keep." She jabbed a finger at the shadow. "She's not *your soul*. She's Marguerite. She's a person. And you're doing the thing again."

"What thing?" I asked, though I knew.

"The arithmetic thing." Wren's voice cracked, and she pushed through it the way she pushed through everything, headfirst. "One girl or one dragon. Half a woman or a fallen city. You're counting like the Court counts. Like everything has a price and the price always gets paid by whoever's standing closest." Her eyes were wet and furious. "Maren told me a story on the road. About Odile. Did you know Odile tried to do this exact thing? Stand in front of a bad choice and pick which half of herself to lose?"

"She didn't—" I started.

"She did. Maren was there. Odile stood exactly where you're standing, holding exactly this problem, and she picked the clever option, the one that cost her least, and then she spent fifteen years telling herself it was the only ledger there was." Wren stepped closer. "And look how that ended. Look where we're standing. In a hole in the ground, next to a dying dragon, holding a girl in a jar, choosing between two ways to lose."

"Wren," Dain said gently, "there are two options. We've all counted them twice."

"No," Wren said. "You've counted *two*. Counting isn't the same as seeing." She turned and pointed—at me, at the shadow, at the dragon, at Dain bleeding against the wall, at Sorrel, at the dark tunnels beyond, at everything. "Fire," she said. "Everybody keeps saying *fire* like it's a coin. Spend it here, save it there, one purse, one owner. But I remember magic. From before. From when I was little and it was everywhere and nobody owned it." Her voice dropped, went strange and certain, the voice she used for constellations that no longer existed. "My mother used to light our stove with a spell my grandmother taught her, and the spell wasn't *give the fire something to eat*. It was *show the fire how to make more of itself*. A candle lit from a candle isn't smaller. There are just two candles. Fire isn't a purse. Fire is a *door*."

The chamber went very quiet. Even Vaskarath's ragged breathing seemed to lean in.

"That's not how contracts work," Sorrel said slowly. "The Court's whole power is scarcity. One soul, one flame, one debt—"

"Then maybe," Wren said, "the answer isn't playing their game better. Maybe it's breaking the board."

Marguerite drifted up out of my hands. I felt her leave my palms like warmth leaving a window in winter, and she floated between me and Wren, studying the child with my sixteen-year-old face twisted into an expression I recognized from mirrors—hope, held at arm's length, waiting to be disappointed.

"You think I can be shared," Marguerite said. "Split further. Handed out."

"I think you're not a *thing*," Wren said. "I think you're a person, and persons aren't spent, they're *known*. And I think—" she faltered, and for once the certainty wobbled, and she looked at me, really looked, a child asking an adult to tell her she hadn't broken the world by speaking, "—I think if you put a piece of her in the dragon's fire, and a piece in Lena, and a piece in everyone who carried the sigil, then the Court can't hold the debt anymore, because you can't repossess a candle from someone who lit their own."

"That's not—" Sorrel began.

"It might be heresy," Dain said. His voice had gone odd. He was looking at his bandaged arm, at the sigil scar beneath the blood, and I saw something move across his face like weather. "It might be the single largest act of magical heresy in recorded history. Warden law has a whole chapter about it. Chapter nine. *On the Unlawful Multiplication of Flame.* They made it a crime four hundred years ago." He looked up, and he was almost smiling, and he was bleeding, and he said: "They made it a crime because it works."

Nobody moved.

Vaskarath's eye opened again, amber and enormous, and fixed on the small grey girl floating in the dark.

*"Little door-child,"* the dragon rumbled, and the rock shivered with something that was not quite laughter and not quite grief. *"Three thousand years I have burned alone, because that is what dragons are. Singular. Hoarded. A treasure and a weapon and a secret. And in all that time I never once wondered whether the loneliness was the wound, or only its symptom."* His breath came out long and slow, stirring our hair and clothes. *"If the child is wrong, we all die here in the dark, and the Court wins, and the stars stay blind. If she is right—"*

He stopped. The silence stretched.

*"If she is right,"* he said, *"then everything the Court owns is worth nothing, and everything we own is worth everything, and I would very much like, before I end, to be wrong about myself in that particular way."*

Marguerite turned back to me. My own young face, waiting on my answer.

"Lena," she said softly. "Fifteen years ago she cut me out to hide you. Tonight you can cut me open to save everyone. Or—" and here she smiled, and it was my smile, the one I'd forgotten I had, "—or you can listen to the child who names things, and find out what I am when I'm not a price."

Above us, far up through the mountain's glass veins, something began to ring—the green doors of the Undercourt, opening one after another, the Court rising to collect its debts now that the wards no longer bound it. The sound came down through the rock like a countdown.

Wren took my hand. Dain took the other. Sorrel put hers on the dragon's scale. And Marguerite hovered between my palms, brighter than she had been in the bazaar, brighter than a lantern, almost—almost—like a star.

"How," I asked, and my voice finally cracked, and I didn't care, "do we light a candle from a person?"

Wren grinned through her tears, ash-streaked and feral and certain, and said:

"We ask her first."
