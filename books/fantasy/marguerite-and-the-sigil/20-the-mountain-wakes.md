## 20. The Mountain Wakes

Sorrel had spent eleven years learning the exact weight of a dying dragon's breath.

She knew it the way a midwife knows a laboring mother—by rhythm, by temperature, by the small terrible changes that meant *soon* or *not yet*. Vaskarath's breath had been cooling for a decade. It came now in long, shallow pulls through the roots of Mount Sarn, each one fainter than the last, and Sorrel had catalogued them all: the winter the exhale stopped fogging the glass tunnels, the spring the warmth retreated past the third gallery, the night two years ago when she'd sat with her palm flat against his flank and felt, for the first time, nothing at all.

So when the heat came back, she did not believe it.

She was standing at the edge of the great hollow where Vaskarath lay coiled like a mountain of his own—a smaller mountain inside the mountain, scales gone grey as old ash, wings folded tight as a dead man's hands. The girl's shadow burned in the center of him. That was how Sorrel could only think of it: the grey girl-shape they'd carried down from the bazaar, Marguerite, half of Lena Amberdale made of light instead of flesh, sunk into the dragon's chest like a coal sinking into a banked fire.

And around her, spreading outward through the dark in slow rings, embers.

Sorrel counted them because counting was what keepers did when their hands shook. Lena had broken her sigil apart not an hour ago—not given it away, not surrendered it, but *shattered* it, pressed it to pieces and handed those pieces out like bread at a funeral feast. One ember to Dain Corvess, who held it cupped in both palms like water he was afraid of spilling. One to Wren, who had swallowed hers—actually swallowed it, the little fool, and now glowed faintly at the throat like she'd drunk starlight. One to Sorrel herself, sitting warm behind her sternum where her heart used to be the only thing.

One to Vaskarath.

One to three hollow things that had crept down from the tunnels and stood at the edge of the firelight, empty-eyed, waiting to be driven off—and had been offered embers instead. Sorrel had watched Lena do it. Watched her walk up to creatures that would have eaten her at the ford without breaking stride, and hold out her hand, and say, *You were people once. Take this back.*

The hollows glowed now too. Faintly. From the inside. And where the light touched them, they looked—less empty. Not filled. Not yet. But less.

"Keeper." Dain's voice came from behind her, rough with smoke and exhaustion. "You'll want to see this."

"I'm seeing it," Sorrel said. "I don't trust it."

"Trust it anyway."

* * *

The first thing that changed was the scale under her hand.

She'd gone back to Vaskarath because going back to Vaskarath was the only prayer she knew. Eleven years of tending him had worn grooves in her: check the nostrils, clear the ash from the wing-joints, sing the keeping-song even though he hadn't answered it since before the wards fell. She sang it now, voice cracked and stupid with tiredness, one palm flat against the great curve of his ribs—

—and the scale beneath her hand went warm.

Not the false warmth of a fever. Not the residual heat of the shadow burning in his chest. Warmth *rising*, moving, the way warmth moves in a living body, blood-warmth, tide-warmth, the warmth of something deciding to continue.

Sorrel snatched her hand back. Then put it down again, harder, pressing, as if she could hold the heat in place by force.

"Vaskarath," she said. Her voice came out very small in the enormous dark. "Vaskarath, if you can hear me—"

His eye opened.

It had been closed for four years. She had told herself he slept; she had written in the keeper's log, in her own careful hand, *the dragon sleeps*, because the alternative was writing *the dragon is dying* every day until the words wore through the page. Now the eye opened—a slit of gold in the grey cliff of his face, ancient and unfocused and then, slowly, terribly, focusing on her.

He had eyes the color of the wards at full strength. She'd forgotten that. Everyone had forgotten that. The wards had been built from his fire, fed from his heart-flame for six hundred years, and when you looked into a dragon's eye you were looking at the same light that had hung over the border stones and kept the dark things out and the bargains contained, and Sorrel stood there with her hand on his warming hide and wept like a child at a homecoming, because she had genuinely believed she would die in this hole tending a corpse.

"*Little keeper,*" said Vaskarath.

His voice was rust and landslide. He hadn't spoken in nine years. The last thing he'd said to her was *let me go*, and she had refused, and she had never known until this moment whether that refusal had been mercy or cruelty.

"You're awake," she managed.

"*I am more than awake.*" The gold eye rolled, finding the others—the girl Wren glowing at the throat, the warden with his cupped hands, the hollows standing shy at the light's edge, and Lena Amberdale in the center of it all with a scar on her palm where a sigil used to be. "*I am full.* Do you understand what she has done, keeper? Six hundred years I burned alone. One flame, one heart, one point of failure. They fed me like a furnace and called it strength." A sound moved through him that might have been a laugh, if landslides laughed. "*Fire hoarded dies. Fire shared spreads. Where has that child been all my life?*"

"Hiding," Sorrel said. "Same as the rest of us."

* * *

He rose the way weather rises.

There was no dramatic moment, no roar, no shattering of stone—though later, in the songs the children would learn, there would be all of those things. What actually happened was slower and stranger: Vaskarath simply began to move, joint by joint, scale by scale, uncoiling from the shape death had pressed him into, and the mountain adjusted around him with deep groaning sounds like a ship coming about. Ash fell from his wings in sheets. Grey scales flushed color from the inside out—dull copper first, then true red, then a red so deep it shaded toward gold at the edges, the color of the oldest ward-light, the color Sorrel remembered from her childhood before the outlawry, before everything.

Wren shrieked with pure joy and ran directly underneath him, which was the single most dangerous thing anyone had done in the mountain in eleven years, and Vaskarath lowered his head and let the child press her small glowing hands against his nose, and Sorrel decided that some rules had died with the old world and she would not be mourning them.

"He remembers me," Wren said, looking up at Sorrel with wet eyes and a huge grin. "From before. He says he remembers everyone who carried magic before the outlawry. We're all—" she patted his snout, "—we're all in his fire. Like names in a book."

"*Every witch whose charm ever warmed a cold house,*" Vaskarath rumbled. "*Every hedge-healer, every luck-knitter, every child who spoke to rain. Their small magics fed me too, keeper. Did your order know that? All those little fires I gathered and gave back to the wards. You thought the wards drank only from my heart. They drank from everyone. Your masters just preferred one tap to ten thousand.*"

Sorrel thought of the warden-council chambers, the sealed ledgers, the doctrine of the Single Flame. She thought of Dain Corvess, disgraced for asking exactly the wrong question about exactly that. "No," she said. "They didn't know. Or they didn't want to."

"*Then they will learn,*" said the dragon, "*or they will be learned from.*"

And he breathed.

* * *

The fire went *up*.

That was the part no chronicle had prepared her for, because no living soul had seen a dragon breathe purposefully into a mountain's bones. Vaskarath turned his head to the rock itself—to the deep vein of green glass that threaded Mount Sarn like a spine—and exhaled, and the flame did not splash or scatter. It *ran*. It ran up through the glass the way dawn runs up a valley, gallery to gallery, tunnel to tunnel, and Sorrel felt it pass through her own ember like a bell being struck, and heard Wren laughing somewhere above, and heard Dain swear in warden-cant, which meant even he had no protocol for this.

Above them, the mountain opened its throat.

They climbed—raced, really, all of them, dragon-fire lighting the way—to the high ridge where they'd first seen the green doors standing open in the rock, where the Undercourt had been rising like a tide with the wards fallen. The doors were still open. The Court was still coming: pale processions on the lower slopes, heralds with their debt-ledgers, hollow ranks marching in silence, the whole economy of stolen souls mobilized to collect on a world that had lost its wall.

Then Vaskarath's fire reached the surface.

It came up through the ridge-line in a fountain of gold, and it did not burn the grass—it *lit* it, lit the stones, lit the air itself, and the light spread outward across the broken country in a wave, and everywhere it touched, the old boundary stones woke up.

Sorrel had tended dead wards for eleven years. She knew their corpses: the leaning markers, the cold runes, the way birds wouldn't land on them. Now she watched a boundary stone drink fire and stand up straighter. Watched its runes fill with light—but not the old light, not the single great secret burning in one place. This was *woven*. She could see it plainly, the way you can see a fabric's weave when the sun hits it right: thousands of small threads, thousands of small magics, the ember in Dain's palms and the ember in Wren's throat and the embers in three half-remembering hollows and the embers, she realized with a jolt, in every charm-seller and hedge-healer and frightened person within fifty miles who had ever done a small kindness with magic and been punished for it.

The wards rebuilt themselves out of everyone.

"They're not walls anymore," Dain said beside her. His voice was strange—awed and grieving at once, a man watching his life's work be replaced by something better. "A wall breaks at its weakest point. This is—" he searched for the word. "This is a covenant. Break one thread, the rest hold. There's no weakest point. There's no *center* to cut out."

"No heart to sell," Sorrel said quietly.

"No heart to sell," he agreed.

Below, the pale processions stopped.

* * *

Sorrel watched the Undercourt understand what had happened, and it was the most satisfying thing she had ever seen, and she was ashamed of how satisfying it was, and she decided not to be.

The Court ran on debt. That was the whole of it—that was what Odile had understood and exploited, what the Regent had built an eternity upon: every soul in their vaults was a ledger entry, every bargain a chain, power accumulated by holding single things singly. One dragon's heart-flame, held. One girl's shadow, held. One sigil, held. Singular debts, singular collateral, and the entire architecture of the Court resting on the principle that nothing given freely could ever be taken back.

Lena had given everything freely. Had broken the one indivisible thing into ten thousand divisible ones.

Sorrel saw the heralds feel it first—they clutched their ledgers as the books began, visibly, to fail. Ink faded. Names blurred. A debt that reads *one soul, owed to the Court* means nothing when the soul has been shattered into embers and distributed among strangers who owe each other nothing. The Regent's whole treasury was denominated in a currency that had just been abolished.

The green doors began to close.

Not all at once. The Court withdrew the way frost withdraws from a window in a warm room—from the edges inward, reluctantly, leaving damp traces. The hollow ranks turned and filed back down, and here was the thing Sorrel would tell her students someday, the detail that mattered: some of them didn't. Some of the hollowed stood in the new ward-light with their borrowed embers glowing, and looked at the closing doors, and looked at each other, and stayed. On the surface. In the morning. Under stars.

Because the stars—

"The stars," Wren whispered. "Look up. Look *up*."

Sorrel looked up.

The Night Without Stars had lasted nineteen days. She had kept count the way she kept count of everything, marking each blank midnight in the keeper's log, and she had privately believed the stars were gone forever—that whatever the wards had held up, the sky included, had collapsed past mending. Now, above the relit ridge-line, in the band of sky where the wards' woven light met the dark, a star came back.

Just one. A single point of white, tentative as a struck match.

Then another. Then three. Then a scatter of them, east to west, following the wave of the rebuilding wards like sparks following a fuse, and the sky over Vellamark stitched itself back together constellation by constellation while a feral child stood on a mountainside naming every one of them aloud, faster and faster, sobbing and laughing, "*the Harrow, the Lantern, the Sisters, the Old Ox, the—*" until she ran out of names for the ones still returning and simply pointed, mouth open, alight at the throat.

Sorrel sat down on the warm stone. Behind her, Vaskarath settled along the ridge like a living rampart, young again, red-gold in the starlight, breathing slow and deep and *full*. Beside her, Lena Amberdale stared at her own scarred palm as if it were a door she'd finally walked through. Somewhere below, the last green door shut with a sound like a book closing, and the mountain that had been a tomb for eleven years exhaled, and the air that came down the slope smelled—for the first time in Sorrel's adult life—like morning.

"*Keeper,*" Vaskarath said softly, so only she could hear.

She looked up at him.

"*You refused to let me go. It was cruelty, and it was mercy, and I forgive you for both.*" The gold eye blinked, slow, ancient, amused. "*Now. About these new wards of yours. Someone will have to teach the warden-council how a covenant works—and someone will have to explain to me why your order's records list my heart-flame as 'crown property.'*"

Sorrel started laughing and couldn't stop. It hurt. It was wonderful. Below them the world was relighting, and above them the stars were coming home one stubborn point at a time, and far away in Greyharbor, she knew, people were walking out of their houses to look up for the first time in nineteen days.

But the Regent's last ledger had not burned clean. As the light steadied, Sorrel saw it—one line of pale script hanging in the air above the sealed doors, written in ink that refused to fade, addressed in a hand like frost to a single name:

*One debt remains. The witch who broke the sigil owes the Court a name. Come and settle it, Lena Amberdale—or we will come for the one who carries yours.*

Beside her, Lena read it twice. Then she folded her scarred hand closed, and looked at Wren, and at Dain, and at the dragon, and said, "Marguerite's name is mine now. And nobody collects on my family."

Sorrel reached for her staff. "Then we'd better decide," she said, "who's telling the city what happens next."
