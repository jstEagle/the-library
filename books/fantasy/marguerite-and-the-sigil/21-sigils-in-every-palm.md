## 21. Sigils in Every Palm

The first morning of the Greyharbor School of Small Magics, no one came.

Lena stood in the doorway of the old net-mender's shop she'd leased with the last of her charm-money and watched the fog roll off the harbor, and told herself this was fine. This was, in fact, familiar. Fifteen years of selling luck-knots had taught her exactly what an empty doorway felt like.

Wren was not so philosophical. The girl sat on the counter with her heels drumming the wood, a slate across her knees covered in constellation diagrams that no longer matched any sky but were correct anyway, somehow, the way Wren's things always were.

"Nobody's coming," Wren said. "Because they're scared."

"People are always scared. That's not new."

"It's different scared." Wren tapped the slate. "Before, they were scared of *you*. Now they're scared of *themselves*."

Lena looked at her. Six months since the mountain, and the child still did this—said the true thing flatly, like reporting weather, and left everyone else to deal with the wreckage of it.

"You've been talking to Dain," Lena said.

"Dain doesn't talk. Dain stands near things until they confess." Wren hopped down. "I'm going to the harbor. If nobody comes by noon, I'm going to stand on the fish stalls and recite the old names until someone throws a herring at me."

"That's not—"

But the door was already swinging shut behind her, and Lena was alone in a school with no students, which was either a failure or a joke, depending on how much sleep she'd gotten.

She'd gotten very little. That was the thing about building something out in the open: it kept you awake. Hiding had been exhausting too, but it was a tiredness she knew, an old coat that fit. This new life hung strange on her shoulders. Every morning she woke expecting the rules to reassert themselves. *Never big magic. Never twice for the same customer. Never be remembered.* And every morning she got up and broke all three before breakfast, because the school's whole curriculum was being remembered, and its first lesson was that small magic, done openly, done together, was worth more than any secret ever sold.

She turned her palm over. In the grey morning light the scar stood pale against her skin—not the sigil anymore, not the brand Odile had burned into her at thirteen, the mark that had made her invisible to wardens and legible to courts. That was gone, spent, broken into embers on the mountainside and given away. What remained was the shape the breaking had left: a thin line, crossed by three shorter ones, and if you squinted—

A door.

Sorrel had seen it first, the night after everything, when they'd sat around Vaskarath's warming flank eating keeper-bread and drinking something the dragon's keepers called tea only out of politeness. Sorrel had taken Lena's hand without asking, turned it toward the firelight, and gone very still.

"Do you know what you're holding?" the old keeper had said.

"A scar."

"A door." Sorrel traced it with one cracked finger. "The sigil was a lock, girl. Locks are for keeping things in or keeping things out. But a door—" She'd looked up, and her eyes had been wet and fierce. "A door is for walking through. Both directions."

Lena hadn't understood then. She understood it better now, most days. Some days she still woke up reaching for the invisibility like a missing tooth, tongue finding the gap where the secret used to be.

The bell over the shop door rang at half past noon.

Her first student was a fishwife named Berta Kell, sixty if she was a day, hands like knotted rope, who marched in as though entering enemy territory and planted herself in front of Lena's desk.

"I want the fever-charm," Berta said. "My granddaughter burns at night. The physickers bleed her and it does nothing. I'm told you teach it free."

"I teach it free," Lena said. "And I teach you to do it yourself. That's the arrangement. I don't sell charms anymore."

"Then what do you sell?"

"Nothing. That's rather the point."

Berta Kell stared at her the way people stared at things they suspected of being too good to be true, which in Greyharbor was nearly everything. Then she sat down, dragged her rope-knuckled hands onto the desk, and said, "Show me."

Lena showed her. It was a small charm—smaller than anything she'd ever sold, honestly, a breath-warmth and a knot tied in red thread and three words in the old counting-speech that Wren had helped her recover from memory. It would not cure a fever. It would hold a fevering child steady through the night so her body could do the curing itself. Fifteen years ago Lena would have charged two coppers and never asked the buyer's name.

Now she held Berta's hands in hers and said, "Say it back."

And when Berta said it back—wrong, stumbling, but *saying it*, the words passing from one mouth to another like a flame passed from candle to candle—Lena felt the small warm tug in her chest that she'd come to know as the ember answering. Not hers. Never just hers again. But present, the way a hearth is present in a house even when you're in another room.

By evening there were four students. By the end of the week, eleven. Word moved through Greyharbor the way water moves through a hull: quietly, and then all at once, and then everywhere.

* * *

Dain Corvess came to the school on Thursdays, because Thursday was the day he rebuilt the wards, and he liked to complain about it where someone could hear.

He'd changed since the mountain. Not softened, exactly—Dain did not soften, he *settled*, like sediment—but the hunted look had gone out of him, replaced by something more dangerous to his peace: purpose. The Council of Vellamark, in the great institutional panic that followed the relighting of the stars, had needed someone to blame for the wards' collapse, and having failed to hang it on a witch, had settled for promoting the warden who'd survived the border road and knew more about the wards than anyone alive. Dain Corvess, disgraced, was now Dain Corvess, Warden-Commander of the Rebuilt Marches, and he hated every minute of it with the sincerity of a man who had found exactly the right job.

"The eastern boundary stones won't take the weave," he said, dropping onto the bench beside Wren's worktable while Lena pretended to inventory thread. "Third time this month. They keep rejecting the binding."

"Because you're still building walls," Wren said, not looking up from her slate.

"I am building *wards*."

"You're building walls with extra steps." Wren finally looked at him, and Lena hid a smile behind her ledger. The child had appointed herself the school's conscience, its critic, and its resident expert on everything, and neither adult had the heart—or frankly the standing—to argue. "Vaskarath doesn't guard the mountain. The mountain guards itself because everybody carries a piece of the fire. Your stones want a covenant. You keep giving them a garrison."

Dain opened his mouth. Closed it. Looked at Lena.

"She's right," Lena said.

"She's nine."

"She's right, though. Isn't she? You told me yourself the old wards failed because one thing carried them. One dragon. One fire. One point of failure." Lena set down the ledger. "You're rebuilding the same mistake with better masonry. Give the weave to the people. A hundred small keepers along the border, each holding an ember, each tending their stretch of stone. Nothing to steal. Nothing to kill. Nothing to sell."

Dain was quiet for a long moment. Outside, a gull screamed its opinion of the harbor.

"When I was a boy," he said finally, "my father told me the wards were the oldest law in Vellamark. Older than the Council. Older than the crown-law." He rubbed the warden's knot at his wrist—the badge he still wore, though it meant something different now. "I thought that meant the law was strong. I think maybe it meant the law was *brittle*. Strong things bend. Brittle things hold until they don't."

"So change what the law is," Wren said.

"And what would a nine-year-old know about law?"

"Everything," Wren said. "I remember it from before."

And that was the other thing about Wren, the thing the scholars from the capital kept traveling north to ask about and going away disappointed, because the child could not be interviewed, only befriended, and befriending her took months. Wren remembered magic from before the outlawry—not as history, but as habit. She remembered when charms were taught at kitchen tables. When the hedge-healers and the net-menders and the midwives all carried their small magics the way they carried recipes, passed hand to hand, unremarkable, *common*. When magic wasn't a crime or a treasure but a practice, like baking.

That was the world the Undercourt had starved, one stolen soul at a time, hoarding wonder into debt. That was the world the sigil-embers were slowly, stubbornly growing back.

"We'll need teachers," Dain said at last, grudging, which from Dain was surrender. "Border villages. Someone to teach the keepers their stretch of the weave."

"Lena teaches."

"I can't leave the school."

"You just said the school teaches itself now. Eleven students. Berta Kell is already teaching the fever-charm to her whole street." He leaned forward. "Come east with me for a month. See the stones. Help me build the first covenant-station. Wren can mind the shop."

"I can mind the shop," Wren confirmed immediately, in the tone of someone who absolutely should not be allowed to mind the shop and knew it.

Lena looked between them—the disgraced warden and the starless child, her family, assembled out of wreckage—and felt the old fear rise one last time, the fear that had governed fifteen years of her life: *if you go, if you're seen, if you matter, it will all be taken.*

Then she looked at her palm. At the door.

"No," she said, and both of them went still. "Not a month. We'll go together—all three—and we'll come back, because this is home now, and home is the place you return *to*, not the place you flee from. I spent fifteen years learning the difference. I'm not confusing them again."

Dain smiled. It was a rare thing, a Dain smile, weather-rare, and it transformed his face from a closed border into open country.

"Thursday, then," he said. "We'll take the east road."

* * *

They left on a bright cold morning, and the strangest thing, the thing Lena kept turning over like a coin as the miles passed, was that no one hid.

At the tollhouse of Small Mercies, Maren fed them stew and pressed a bundle of herbs on Wren and made Lena promise to write, and when Maren touched Lena's hand in farewell she did it openly, warmly, in front of the tollhouse's other guests, and no one flinched. At the ford where the hollow things had attacked—where Lena had burned her invisibility away in a single act of raw will—the river ran clean, and a young woman was kneeling at the bank teaching two children a water-finding rhyme, an ember-glow faint at her throat. One of the repentant hollows, Sorrel had said; some of them had chosen the shared fire when it was offered, and come back to themselves piece by piece, like frost thawing.

The eastern boundary stones rose against the sky at dusk on the third day, and they were wrong, and Lena saw it at once.

"They're dark," she said. "You said they wouldn't take the weave, but they're not even trying. There's nothing in them at all."

"They're waiting," Dain said. "That's the problem. They're waiting for something I haven't been able to give them."

He set his pack down and unwrapped it, and inside, wrapped in warden's cloth, lay a shard of green glass—a fragment of the Undercourt's doors, cracked loose the night the Court's power collapsed, salvaged from the mountainside. Even now it gave off a faint wrong light, like a lamp burning the wrong fuel.

"I thought we'd bind it into the stone," Dain said. "Use the Court's own substance against it. Turn the enemy's wall into our mortar."

"No," Wren said sharply.

"It's symbolic—"

"It's *wrong*." The child stepped between Dain and the stone, small and immovable, and her voice had that flat certainty that stopped arguments like a hand on a chest. "You don't build a door out of the thing that ate the doors. You build it out of what you want to walk through it *for*."

Silence. Wind off the march-lands. Far to the south, Mount Sarn breathed its gentle smoke, a sleeping animal's sigh.

Lena looked at her palm. At the scar shaped like a door. At the eleven students waiting back in Greyharbor, and Berta Kell's granddaughter sleeping fever-free, and Maren's herbs, and the young woman at the ford, and every small ember scattered through the world like seeds after a fire.

"Wren's right," she said slowly, and the shape of the idea arrived even as she spoke it, the way the best charms always did—not built but *found*, already there, waiting to be noticed. "It shouldn't be my sigil in the stone. It shouldn't be anyone's. It should be *everyone's*. All of them. Every person who carries an ember, every hand that's learned a charm since the mountain—we each give a thread, and the stone holds all of us, and none of us alone."

Dain stared at her. "That's not how ward-work works. Ward-work is—"

"Was," Lena said. "Was one thing. Was dragon-fire and secrecy and a single point of failure, and it fell in one night, and it took the stars with it." She held out her scarred palm toward the boundary stone. "Teach me the weave, Warden. We'll build it new. Together. Right now."

And as her hand neared the stone, the old sigil-shape on her palm caught the last light of the setting sun—and for a heartbeat, just a heartbeat, Lena would have sworn the scar-shaped-like-a-door stood open, and beyond it, faint as dawn, something answered.

Far below the march-lands, deep beneath Mount Sarn, something vast and old and grateful stirred in its sleep—and dreamed, for the first time in an age, of open skies.

But that is another story, and it begins—as all the best ones do—with a knock at a door.
