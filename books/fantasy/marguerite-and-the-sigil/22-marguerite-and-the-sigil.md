## 22. Marguerite and the Sigil

The schoolroom had once been a fish-cannery, and it still smelled faintly of salt and old brine no matter how many times Wren scrubbed the floors or hung the windows open to the harbor wind. She had stopped fighting it years ago. The smell was honest. It said: this building worked for its living before it learned to work for wonder.

Twenty-two students sat on benches salvaged from three different ships and one demolished lighthouse, which meant nobody's bench sat level, which meant everybody rocked a little when they fidgeted, which Wren considered a feature rather than a flaw. Magic should never let you sit still. That was the first thing she taught.

"Again," she said now, pacing the aisle between the benches with her hands clasped behind her back, because teachers were supposed to clasp their hands, and she had practiced it in front of a mirror until it looked natural instead of like she was restraining herself from grabbing something. "From the beginning. And this time, Tam, I want you to actually say the name right. You're butchering her."

Tam was nine and freckled and stubborn as a barnacle. He scowled at his slate, where he'd chalked the lesson's central word in letters that leaned like drunks.

"It's a weird name," he protested. "Marguerite. Nobody's called Marguerite."

"Lots of people are called Marguerite," said Pell from the back row, who was eleven and knew everything. "It means daisy."

"It means *the daisy*," Wren corrected, holding up one finger. "One specific daisy. Which is the whole point of today's lesson, so pay attention, both of you." She turned to face the class, and the room quieted the way it always did when she did this—the small hush of children who could feel that a story was coming, the oldest magic there was, older than charms, older than wards, older than dragons. "Who can tell me what happened to the stars?"

Every hand went up. Of course it did. They'd all been born after the relighting; to them, the stars were furniture, permanent as the moon, as reliable as tide-tables. It was one of Wren's private griefs and private joys that they would never know what it was to look up into a sky wiped clean as a scraped plate.

"Good. Now—who can tell me *why* they went out?"

Fewer hands. Pell's stayed up. Tam's wavered and came down.

"Pell."

"The border wards fell," Pell recited, "because the wards were fed by dragon-fire, and the last dragon was dying under Mount Sarn because its heart-flame was sold to the Undercourt, and—"

"—and *who* sold it?" Wren prompted.

Pell hesitated. This was the part where the recitation always snagged, because the answer wasn't in any of the primers. The primers said *unknown parties*. The primers said *historical actors*. The primers had been written by committee, and committees are terrified of names.

Wren let the silence stretch until it hummed.

"A witch named Odile Vasseur," she said. "My teacher's teacher. The woman who raised my teacher. And she didn't sell the dragon's flame—not exactly. What she sold was smaller and crueler and stranger than that. She sold a shadow. And the shadow had a name, and the name was Marguerite, and that—" she tapped Tam's slate, right on top of his drunken letters, "—is why you're going to learn to spell it properly."

* * *

She told it the way Lena had taught her to tell it, which was the way Sorrel had taught Lena, which was the way stories about painful things should always be told: plainly, without decoration, letting the facts do the bleeding.

"There was a girl," Wren began, settling onto the edge of her desk, legs swinging, because standing still through a whole story was for wardens. "Fifteen years before the stars went out. An apprentice witch, unlicensed, hidden away by her mistress because the law said her kind of magic was a crime. Does anyone remember what the old law was called?"

"The Quiet Edict," said a small girl in the second row named Isbet, who never spoke unless she was certain, and was almost always certain.

"The Quiet Edict. Good. Under the Quiet Edict, a witch couldn't sell so much as a luck-knot without the wardens coming. So Odile hid her apprentice. Hid her so well that she hid her from herself." Wren held up her own left hand, palm out, showing them the scar—a thin pale line across the lifeline, shaped, if you squinted and believed, like a door. Every student in Greyharbor knew to look for it. Half of them bore smaller versions themselves, marks from the ember-gifts that had scattered outward from the mountain that night, passed hand to hand down the years like a cup of water at a fire line. "Odile took a knife of cold starlight and split her apprentice's shadow clean off. Split it, named it, and sold it. Because a girl without a shadow doesn't register on the Court's ledgers. Doesn't show up in their scrying. Can't be found, can't be claimed, can't be *owed*. Do you understand? Odile made her apprentice invisible by making her incomplete."

Tam's hand shot up. "That's horrible."

"Yes," Wren said. "It is. Say more."

"But—it saved her? The apprentice?"

"That's the question, isn't it. That's the question the whole story turns on." Wren slid off the desk and started walking again, because her legs wanted to walk when her mind wanted to work. "The apprentice's name was Lena Amberdale. She ran charm-stalls in smuggler's markets for fifteen years. Luck-knots, fever-charms, small mercies, never big magic, never twice for the same customer, never be remembered. Three rules. She lived by them like a fish lives by water. And she thought she was free." Wren stopped. "She thought she'd escaped. But every step of her running had been paid for in advance, with a piece of her own soul, and the bill came due the night the stars went out."

She gave them the falling of the wards in quick strokes—the midnight dark, the horizon shudder, the panic, the burning of fifteen years of records on a rooftop while wardens swept the streets below. She gave them Dain Corvess, the disgraced warden with his dead sister's matching brand, cornering a fugitive charm-seller in an alley and lowering his hand instead of raising it. She gave them Maren's tollhouse and the truth it held: *a shadow split from a living girl.* She gave them the descent, the green doors, the market where souls hung like lanterns, and the grey flickering girl-shape wearing Lena's own face.

"And here's the part I need you to hold very carefully," Wren said, and her voice changed, and the room changed with it, the way rooms do when a teacher stops performing and starts meaning. "Down in that market, Lena was offered a bargain. Take your shadow back, become whole—and the dragon dies, and the wards stay fallen, and the dark comes in. Or give your shadow to the dragon's flame—stay halved forever, a person with a door-shaped hole in her, so everyone else stays safe. Whole-and-damned, or halved-and-saving. Those were the choices. Those were the *only* choices anyone could see."

Isbet's hand rose, slow, uncertain for the first time all morning. "But there was a third way."

"There was a third way," Wren agreed. "And it didn't come from the witch, or the warden, or the keeper, or the Regent, or the dragon. Where did it come from, class?"

They knew this part. They always knew this part. Twenty-two voices, ragged and gleeful:

"*A child who counted stars!*"

"A child who counted stars," Wren confirmed gravely, "who walked into the Undercourt uninvited, past a hollow guard twice her size, because she was seven years old and nobody had told her yet that there were things she couldn't do." She let them laugh. She waited it out. "Wren—that was me, yes, thank you, sit down, Tam—I stood in that glass hall with the Regent's cold all around us and I said the thing I'd known since before the outlawry, back when magic was still legal and ordinary and *shared*, back when my grandmother sang ward-songs over the soup pot like it was nothing. Fire hoarded dies. Fire shared spreads. One soul fed to one dragon buys one lifetime of borrowed light. But a sigil broken into embers and given away—to the dragon, to the keepers, to the outcasts, even to the hollow things that used to be people and only wanted to be warm again—one sigil given away becomes ten thousand small fires, and no ledger in the Undercourt can hold a debt against ten thousand creditors at once."

She spread her hands.

"So that's what she did. Not a conquest. A distribution. Lena Amberdale walked up to the Pale Regent's throne of stacked contracts and she broke her own sigil like bread and handed it out. And the Regent's power—which was built entirely on singular debts, one soul owed here, one flame owed there—came apart like wet paper. The dragon woke young. The stars relit one by one, north to south, like someone walking down a long dark road lighting lamps. And the wards rebuilt themselves—not as a wall this time, not one great secret guarded by one great sacrifice, but as a covenant. Thousands of small magics, woven together, owned by everyone."

Silence. The good kind. The salt-smell of the old cannery drifted through the open windows along with gull-cries and the creak of the harbor, and Wren thought, as she did at this point in the lesson every year, that if she died tomorrow she could not ask for a better sound than twenty-two children being quietly astonished.

Tam broke it, because Tam always broke it. "So Marguerite was the shadow. But then—did Lena take her back? Did they merge? The primer says—"

"The primer says they became one and lived happily ever after," Wren said dryly. "The primer was written by people who weren't there. Yes, they merged. Yes, Lena got her wholeness back. But Marguerite didn't vanish into her like sugar into tea. Fifteen years in a cage leaves a shape in you. Lena says—she still says—that some mornings she wakes up and feels like two people who've agreed to share a coat. And that's why the name matters. That's why we don't call the shadow *it*, or *the fragment*, or *the lost half*. Odile named her Marguerite, probably as a cruelty or a convenience, a label for merchandise. And Lena kept the name. Kept it as a promise: *you were a person, even when you were property. You get to keep your name.*" Wren looked around the room slowly. "That's the lesson under the lesson, everyone. Whatever the ledgers say you are, whatever anybody paid or sold or traded—you're the one who holds your name. Nobody else."

* * *

The bell rang and the children erupted into the beautiful chaos of dismissal—benches scraping, slates thumping into satchels, Tam already arguing with Pell about whether daisies could be people, Isbet pausing at the door to give Wren one of her rare, precise nods, which from Isbet was worth a parade.

Wren was erasing the board when she heard the tap of a cane on the floorboards, and smiled without turning around.

"You got the tollhouse wrong again," said Lena Amberdale.

Wren turned. Her teacher stood in the doorway leaning on her cane—she'd earned the cane honestly enough in the years since the mountain, though everyone privately suspected she enjoyed the dramatic arrival it enabled—and she was older now, silver threading the dark of her hair, lines around her eyes that hadn't come from fear in a long time. Her left palm, when she lifted it in greeting, showed not a brand but a scar: the door-shape, faded to a whisper, the sigil long since broken and scattered.

"I did not get the tollhouse wrong," Wren said. "Maren's tollhouse. Tollhouse of Small Mercies. It's in my notes."

"It's in your *embroidery*." Lena came in and lowered herself onto the nearest bench, which rocked alarmingly and held. "Maren's place was a waystation, not a sanctuary. And you told it in four sentences when it deserves eleven, because Maren kept the kettle going for three days while we all pretended we weren't grieving, and the kettle matters, Wren. Grief runs on tea. Any fool who tells the story without the kettle is telling it wrong."

"Eleven sentences about a kettle."

"Nine about the kettle, two about the grief. Minimum."

Wren laughed and came to sit beside her, and for a moment they just watched the empty classroom, sun-striped, salt-smelling, scarred benches rocking gently in the draft like boats at anchor.

"You know," Lena said, "when I was her age—when I was *your* age—I thought being known was the most dangerous thing in the world. Never be remembered. That was the rule. I built my whole life inside that rule like a snail builds a shell." She turned her hand over, studying the door-shaped scar. "Now look at me. There's a school with my story in its lessons. There's a holiday. There's a *holiday*, Wren, with bunting. Bunting is the opposite of everything I ever stood for."

"You cried at the festival last year."

"I was moved by the fireworks. That's different."

"They spelled your name in sparks over the harbor."

"*Marguerite's* name," Lena corrected, with the fierce precision she brought to exactly one subject. "Mine got one spark. Maybe two. The rest were hers, and rightly so." She said it lightly, but Wren had been her student for twenty years and knew the undertone: the old guilt, worn smooth now, polished by handling into something closer to gratitude. The dead mistress, the sold shadow, the stolen years—all of it carried, all of it acknowledged, none of it allowed to curdle. That was the third way too, Wren thought. Not just the sigil. The remembering.

"Will you tell it next week?" Wren asked. "The children have questions. Tam wants to know if hollows can be repentant *and* bite."

"Both. Frequently simultaneously." Lena levered herself up with the cane. "Yes, I'll come. But I'm correcting the ford scene. You always make my big magic sound noble. It was terror, Wren. Pure undignified terror with light coming out of it."

"Terror with light coming out of it," Wren said. "That's the best definition of courage I've ever heard. I'm stealing it for the curriculum."

"Steal whatever you like. That's what legacies are for." Lena paused at the door, silhouetted against the bright harbor afternoon, and looked back at the schoolroom—at the mismatched benches, the chalk dust, the slate where Tam's drunken letters still read *Marguerite* in a nine-year-old's careful attempt. Something moved across her face that Wren couldn't name and didn't try to. Some things you witnessed without cataloguing.

"Fifteen years I spent being invisible," Lena said softly. "And now I'm a chapter. Isn't that a strange thing to happen to a person?"

"Not strange," Wren said. "Earned."

Her teacher snorted—but she was smiling as she tapped her way out into the sunlight, and Wren returned to her board, and picked up the eraser, and paused.

At the bottom corner, in chalk, beneath where the day's lesson had been, someone—Tam, almost certainly Tam—had written a single line before fleeing for lunch:

**Marguerite was here.**

Wren looked at it for a long moment. Then she picked up a fresh stick of chalk, and beneath it, in her own neat teacher's hand, she wrote:

**And she is remembered.**

Outside, the gulls wheeled over a harbor full of small boats and small magics, and above it all, unhurried and permanent, the stars' daytime ghosts waited for evening—every one of them accounted for, every one of them home.
