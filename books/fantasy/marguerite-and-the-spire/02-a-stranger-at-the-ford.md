## 2. A Stranger at the Ford

The river in Hollowmere had no name worth keeping. The old maps called it the Sigh, because it moved so slowly through the valley that it seemed less to flow than to breathe, and Felix had lived beside it long enough to know that breathing was all anything did here. The water carried no charge. The stones on its bed held no memory. Even the fish were dull-eyed things that rose to a hook without suspicion, as though wonder itself had been drained from them along with everything else.

Felix counted that dullness a mercy.

Ten years, and they had learned the valley's grammar by heart: how to mend a net so the knots looked like everyone else's knots; how to haggle at the Tuesday market without meeting anyone's eyes longer than courtesy required; how to stand in the rain—real rain, gray and flavorless, none of their mother's wild silver—and feel nothing but wet. That last one had taken years. The first storm they'd stood through after coming down into Hollowmere, they had wept like a child at a funeral, every drop a small cold hand pressing on a door they'd sworn to keep shut.

Now the hands pressed, and the door stayed shut, and Felix mended nets.

It was the third hour past dawn when the stranger came to the ford.

Felix saw him first as a wrongness in the mist—a shape moving against the current's logic, stumbling where the shallows should have been easy, listing toward the far bank like a boat with a sprung hull. The mist off the Sigh lay thick in autumn, wool-white and slow, and the man came apart out of it piece by piece: a boot, a coat the color of wet slate, a face gray as ash with a beard crusted dark along the jaw.

Blood. Old blood, dried and re-wetted, layered like sediment. He had been bleeding a long time.

Felix's hands went still on the net. Every instinct they had honed for a decade said the same thing: *look away*. Be a fisherman. Be a nothing. Let him stagger past, let the valley swallow him the way it swallowed thunder, let someone else find whatever was left of him by the reeds tomorrow.

But the man's eyes found Felix across the water, and there was nothing dull in them. They burned. They were the first things Felix had seen burn in ten years.

"You." The word came out cracked, half-drowned. The man took another step and the river took his legs out from under him, and he went down into the shallows with a sound like a dropped sack of grain.

Felix was moving before they decided to move. That was the thing about the body—it kept its own covenants, older and more stubborn than any made with the self. They waded in, boots filling, the cold biting up through their calves, and hauled the man's head above the water. He weighed almost nothing. Whatever had been eating him had been eating him for days.

"Don't," he rasped, when Felix tried to drag him toward the bank. "Listen. There's no time. They're behind me—they'll ask—you weren't here."

"I wasn't here," Felix agreed automatically, and hated how easily the lie of absence sat in their mouth.

"No." A hand, claw-thin, seized Felix's wrist with a strength that had no business remaining in it. "You *weren't here*. Say you never saw me. Swear it however you swear things. Because if they think you touched me—" His eyes went to Felix's face then, really looked, and something flickered in them. Recognition? No. Something worse. *Understanding.* "Coldharbour," he breathed. "Gods below. It *is* true. You're real."

The world tilted. Ten years of careful, gray, unremarkable life tilted with it, and Felix felt the old vertigo rise—the sense of standing at the edge of a vast height with wind waiting below. "You're delirious," they said. "I'm a netmender. My name is Wren."

"Your name is a bird in a cage," the man said, and laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing that brought up bright red threads into the water, unwinding downstream like ribbon. "And your cage is about to be knocked over. Listen to me. *Listen.*"

He fumbled beneath his coat, fingers clumsy with cold and blood loss, and Felix—hating themselves, hating the curiosity that had always been their ruin—helped him. What came out was wrapped in oilcloth gone soft with wear: a map, folded into a shape too small for its size, and a seal.

Felix knew seals. Everyone in the lowlands knew the Spire's seal, the tower-and-crown stamped in white wax on every writ and warrant, the mark that had ended their family. But this was not that. This was older—heavier metal than wax could hold, a disc of tarnished bronze worked with a spiral that seemed, when Felix's thumb passed over it, to turn very slightly under the touch. It was broken clean in half, the fracture edges bright as if only just sundered.

"The covenant," the man whispered. His grip on Felix's wrist was loosening. "It's failing. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Not weakening. *Failing.* The Spire is falling—not the tower, the *covenant*, the whole—every seal is breaking, one after another, like teeth—" Another coughing fit bent him double. When he surfaced, his burning eyes were dimming, and that frightened Felix more than the blood had. "Your line anchored the first of them. Your mother knew. She tried to tell the order and they—" His mouth twisted. "They wrote her down as a heretic and her children as a cautionary tale. But she left this. She left *everything* in this. Blood remembers what wax forgets."

"My mother is dead," Felix said. Their voice sounded very far away.

"She's dead the way a lamp is dead when the sun comes up," the man said. "Take it. Take it or the wardens take it, and then the lowlands drown, and the valleys after, and there will be no cages left to keep anything in." He pressed the bundle into Felix's hands with the last of his strength, folding Felix's fingers closed over it, and for a moment his face cleared—became young, almost, a courier on a road somewhere with the morning ahead of him. "The Spire is falling," he said again, softer now, almost gentle. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry it had to be you."

Then the mist closed around his eyes the way it closed around everything in Hollowmere, and he sagged against Felix's shoulder, breathing in shallow pulls, alive the way a candle is alive.

Felix knelt in the freezing river holding a dead woman's map and did not know what they were holding. That was the truth of it. They knew only two things with perfect clarity: that the bundle against their chest felt warm as a living thing, and that warmth was the most dangerous sensation they had experienced in ten years.

* * *

They got him to the boathouse—Felix's boathouse, the one room in the valley that was theirs, smelling of pitch and tar and old rope—and laid him on the sail canvas. He drifted in and out. In the gray light through the slats, Felix cleaned the wounds as best they could: three cuts across the ribs and back, deliberate, precise, the work of someone skilled and unhurried. Not robbers, then. Robbers cut where they pleased. These cuts were placed like punctuation. An argument, written in a body.

An interrogation that had traveled a long way and not finished its sentence.

Felix wrapped the map and seal in oilcloth again and, after a long moment of standing in the middle of the small room feeling the walls close in, pushed it deep into the tar barrel beneath the coil of spare net, and ladled cold tar over the top until the bundle was a rumor, a shadow, a thing that had never existed. Their hands shook the entire time. They told themselves it was the cold.

Near midday, hooves on the ford road.

Felix had heard perhaps four riders cross that road in ten years. This was six, at speed, and the sound of them was wrong—not the loose clatter of travelers but the tight rhythm of formation, horses ridden by people who rode as one instrument. Felix stepped out of the boathouse pulling a net after them, letting the work claim their posture, their face, their eyes. *Be a fisherman. Be a nothing.*

They wore gray and white, the wardens. Six of them, cloaks beaded with mist, and the leader was a woman with a face like a closed door and a badge of office that caught what little light the valley allowed: the tower-and-crown, rendered in silver instead of wax. Her horse stopped a precise distance away, near enough to speak, far enough to watch.

"We're looking for a man," she said. No greeting. Wardens didn't greet; greeting implied the other person was a party to something. "Courier's build, gray coat, beard. He'll be wounded. He may have reached the ford by nightfall yesterday or this morning."

"Haven't seen anyone," Felix said. "Mist's been thick. River's high."

The woman's gaze moved over Felix with the unhurried thoroughness of someone counting coins. "Name?"

"Wren. Netmender, up the east bank." The lie came out smooth, and smoothness frightened Felix more than stammering would have. When had they learned to do that? Had it always been in them, coiled and waiting, the way the other thing waited?

"Wren." She said it like she was tasting it for poison. "Alone out here?"

"Valley's full of alone."

Something moved at the corner of Felix's vision—one of the riders dismounting, drifting toward the boathouse with a warden's terrible casualness. Inside lay a man bleeding through sail canvas. Inside lay bootprints in the mud, two sets, one of them made by someone who had waded out into the shallows and back carrying weight.

"Mind if we look?" the rider asked pleasantly.

"Look at what? Nets?"

"At whatever you've got."

Felix heard their own heartbeat, slow and enormous, like the river breathing. And beneath it, fainter than breath, fainter than memory, something else stirred—a pressure against the inside of the sealed door, a taste of ozone at the root of the tongue, the valley's dead air seeming for one impossible instant to *lean* toward them, listening. As if the map in the tar barrel had a pulse. As if it knew its own blood was standing in the mud pretending to be nobody.

Ten years, Felix thought. Ten years of being no one, and it had taken one dying stranger half an hour to make hiding feel like drowning.

"Help yourselves," Felix said, and stepped aside, and began, with steady hands, to mend a net that did not need mending while strangers searched the only home they had left.

The rider came out of the boathouse after a long while, shaking his head. But the woman with the closed-door face did not ride on. She turned her horse in a slow half-circle, surveying the river, the reeds, the mist, and then fixed her eyes on Felix with the patience of someone who had all the time in the world and intended to spend it here.

"Mist's lifting," she said. "We'll wait for it to clear. See what the light shows us."

Out in the shallows, downstream, a thread of red still wound among the reeds, thin and patient, waiting for the sun.
