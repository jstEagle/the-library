## 4. Marguerite

The orders came at dusk, carried by a hawk that should not have been able to fly over Hollowmere.

Marguerite watched it circle the warden's station three times before it dropped, wings folding like a closing book, and landed on the rail of her porch with the tube of oiled leather strapped to its leg. The bird's eyes were wrong—too bright, too knowing, the amber of stormlight in them. A Spire-hawk. She had seen perhaps four in her eleven years of service, and never this far from the tower's shadow.

She took the message with hands that did not shake. Eleven years had taught her that much, at least: how to receive ruin with steady fingers.

*By authority of the High Warden and the Rite of Continuance: the last of the Coldharbour line is confirmed living within the valley bounds. Execute upon confirmation. Bring proof to the boundary stone.*

No signature beyond the seal. No explanation. There never was.

Marguerite stood on her porch a long while after the hawk beat away southward, toward the mountains and the Spire hidden somewhere in their white teeth. The valley lay below her in the blue hour, lamplight kindling window by window in the scattered farms—Hollowmere, the place where magic went to die, where the air itself was thick and silent as held breath. Somewhere down there, a person who had done nothing but be born was going about their evening. Boiling water. Feeding a dog. Unaware.

*Execute upon confirmation.*

She had known, in the way one knows the shape of a stone in one's boot, that this day would come. The Coldharbour name was spoken in the Spire only in whispers, the way one speaks of plague or of gods one has offended. *The storm-callers. The traitors. The ones whose arrogance broke the old world.* Marguerite had been nine years old when she first recited the Litany of Severance, kneeling on cold marble: *We cut them out so the world might heal. We are the knife and the kindness both.*

She had believed it the way children believe—the whole weight of belief, unexamined, load-bearing.

Now she went inside, lit her lamp, and read the orders again by its small yellow light. Then she packed her kit: the short blade for the work, the long blade for the walk, the grey cloak that made wardens into rumor. And beneath these, because she was thorough and had always been thorough, she took the thing no order had ever told her to carry but which had saved her more times than any steel—a slim ledger bound in green leather, her own hand filling its pages for eleven years. Observations. Weather. Anomalies.

The valley's anomalies.

Because here was the thing Marguerite had never written in any report, the thing that sat in her chest like a swallowed coal: Hollowmere was not healing. It was starving.

* * *

She found Felix Coldharbour two days later, and it was almost embarrassingly easy.

Not because they were careless. Because the map found them—or rather, because whatever lived in the map's ink answered something in Felix's blood, and blood calls to blood across any distance, even dead distance, even here. Marguerite had felt it herself, a prickle along her forearms like the moment before lightning, a wrongness in the air that had no business existing inside the dead zone. She had followed that wrongness up the sheep track behind the mill, through gorse and thin rain, to the ruined shepherd's hut where the last storm-caller in the world knelt over a spread of ancient vellum with their own red welling on their fingertip.

They looked up at her and did not run. That surprised her most. They simply looked—tired, cornered, resigned in a way that was worse than fear—and said, "I wondered when you'd come."

"You knew?"

"I've known for ten years." Felix rose slowly, hands visible, empty. Rain dripped through the hut's broken roof and spotted the map, and where it struck the ink, the lines *moved*, crawling like veins under skin. "Every year I thought, this is the year they remember me. Every year I was almost disappointed when they didn't." A ghost of a smile. "Careful what you wish for."

Marguerite drew the short blade. It felt obscene in her hand, small and human against the vast humming thing the map was becoming. "Felix Coldharbour. By authority of the High Warden and the Rite of Continuance—"

"Say it plainly." Their voice didn't waver. "You're here to kill me. Say it plainly, at least."

"I'm here to kill you," she said plainly, and hated how it sounded in the small wet space, how it fell between them like a dropped tool.

"Then do it quickly," Felix said, "or ask your questions first. Wardens always have questions. I could see it in you yesterday, at the ford—you wanted to ask about the courier, about the seal, about why a dying man crawled half the valley to reach *me*. You're curious, Warden. Curiosity is a bad quality in an executioner."

The rain ticked on the roof beams. The map breathed its slow, wrong light between them.

Marguerite lowered the blade an inch. Just an inch. "What is that?"

"It's my mother's handwriting," Felix said, "and it's telling me that everything I was exiled for is a lie. Do you want to know what it says? Because I think—" and here their voice finally cracked, just barely, a hairline fracture in ten years of careful quiet, "—I think you don't know either. I think they never told you what the covenant actually *was*."

"The covenant," Marguerite said, reciting, the words coming up from some deep marble floor of memory, "was the bargain that ended the Storm Wars. The Coldharbours broke it. Their punishment was severance—cut off from power, cast down, contained—so that the realms might know peace."

"That's the litany." Felix's eyes were very dark in the hut's gloom. "Now look at the map. Look at what my mother wrote. *Read it,* Warden. You've earned that much, haven't you? Eleven years watching a valley full of farmers. Read it, and then decide what you are."

Marguerite told herself she stepped forward only to seize the document as evidence.

She read it.

* * *

The map was a covenant-route, drawn in an age when such things were charted like coastlines. Seven seals, seven anchor-points strung from the Spire's crown down through the mortal lands—and beside each, a name. Not places' names. Family names. Bloodlines.

*Veyra. Osselin. Marchetti. Duval. Ashgrave. Corvane.*

And seventh, circled in ink gone brown as old rust: *Coldharbour.*

Six of the seven names were struck through. Struck through in different hands, different centuries, each strike a small brutal act of erasure. Beside the sixth, her mother's writing marched in a tight, urgent script:

*They are killing us one line at a time and calling it justice. The covenant does not bind the storms to us—it binds us to the storms. Each anchor-line feeds the Spire; the Spire holds the sky-veil shut; the veil keeps the deep water out of the lowlands. Cut an anchor and the veil thins. Six lines cut. My daughter will be born under a failing sky. If you find this, understand: we were never the prison. We were the pillars.*

Marguerite read it twice. Three times. The rain kept ticking. Felix watched her with the terrible patience of someone who had already grieved this and was now only waiting for her to arrive.

"This is forgery," Marguerite heard herself say. Her voice sounded far away. "This is—Coldharbour propaganda. Sedition. This is exactly what a traitor would—"

"Flood records," Felix said quietly. "Saltmere. You've served the lowland circuit, haven't you? Tell me the tides have been behaving. Tell me the spring floods have been *ordinary* these last ten years."

Marguerite opened her mouth to say they had been.

They had not been. She knew they had not been. She had stood on the sea-wall at Greyhaven four years ago watching water come up streets that had been dry for two hundred years, and her captain had called it weather, and she had written *weather* in her report because the alternative was a word that did not exist in the Litany. And every year since, higher. Every year, earlier. The ledger in her pack suddenly weighed like a gravestone, all those neat columns of rising numbers she had recorded and never once added up.

"The lines were cut," she said slowly, "and the veil is thinning, and the water is coming in, and the Spire has spent a century hunting the last anchors instead of—" She stopped. Started again. "Why? Why would they *do* that?"

"Because a covenant shared is a covenant that constrains everyone," Felix said. "Seven families feeding the Spire means seven families the Spire must answer to. One Spire holding all the magic means—" They shrugged, a small bitter motion. "You tell me. You wear the grey. What does an order do with a monopoly?"

Marguerite thought of the Spire-hawk's too-bright eyes. Of the High Warden's voice at her induction, silver and certain: *We are the knife and the kindness both.* Of six family names, extinguished, and no one ever asking where the storms went when their anchors died. Of the water climbing the sea-walls year by patient year, patient as anything the Spire had ever taught her.

Her knees felt strange. She realized she had sat down on the shepherd's stool without deciding to.

"I'm supposed to bring proof to the boundary stone," she said. Her hands were flat on her thighs, very still. "Proof of execution."

"And instead you have proof of everything else." Felix crouched, slowly, bringing themselves level with her—not looming, not pleading, just level, eye to eye. Rain ran down their face and they let it. "I'm not asking you to believe me. I'm asking you to believe your own ledger. You've been writing the truth down for eleven years, Warden. All I did was give you the column headings."

The lamp had burned low. Outside, the valley lay dark and silent, and Marguerite understood suddenly that the silence was not peace. It was a held breath. It was a lung waiting to fill. Ten years she had guarded a prison and called it a hospital; ten years she had watched over the last pillar of the sky and sharpened a blade against the day it would be needed.

She took out her orders. The oiled paper, the High Warden's seal, the clean imperative: *Execute upon confirmation.*

Confirmed, then. Everything confirmed. Just not the way the writer intended.

Marguerite held the orders over the lamp flame until they caught, and she did not look away while they burned—she owed the burning that much attention—and when the fire reached her fingers she let it sting before she shook the last scrap to ash on the earthen floor.

Felix stared at the ash. At her. "You don't know me."

"No," Marguerite agreed. She stood, and her legs held, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "But I know the tide-tables. And I know that in a week the hunters will come looking for me too, because I missed my report-window, and they'll assume the worst, and they'll be right." She slung her pack, ledger and blades and all. "So we leave tonight, Coldharbour. Before the hawk comes back. Because if your mother's map is true, there are five other anchor-sites between here and the Spire, and someone needs to see with their own eyes what's become of them."

"And if they're false?"

"Then you can kill me at the boundary stone and bring *that* as proof." She pulled up her hood. "Fair trade."

For the first time, Felix laughed—a short, disbelieving sound, startled out of them like a bird. Then they rolled up the map with hands that trembled only a little, and looked north, toward the valley's edge and everything beyond it.

"We'll need to cross the ford before dawn," they said. "And Warden—when we cross, my magic comes back. All of it. Ten years of it." Their eyes met hers, and Marguerite saw the fear there, and the hunger underneath the fear, twin currents in the same water. "Whatever happens on the other side—don't draw that blade."

Outside, far to the north, thunder muttered. Inside the dead zone, where no thunder had sounded in ten years.

It was getting closer.
