## 5. Out of the Dead Land

### Felix

They left before dawn, when Hollowmere's fog lay thick as wool over the fields and even the crows were still deciding whether to exist.

Felix had packed nothing but what could be abandoned without regret: a knife, a waterskin, bread wrapped in cloth, and the map, which had stopped behaving like paper. It shifted against their ribs inside their coat, warm as a sleeping bird, and every few miles it seemed to breathe. Marguerite walked ahead, warden-grey cloak swapped for a shepherd's brown she'd stolen from a drying line, her sword broken down and strapped beneath it like something shameful.

"You're limping," she said, not turning around.

"I'm walking."

"Your left leg disagrees."

Felix bit down on a retort. Ten years of silence made words rust in their throat; ten years of being no one made conversation feel like standing in an open doorway during a storm. But Marguerite had seen the map open under their blood. She had read their mother's handwriting aloud in a voice gone gentle and strange, and then she had burned her orders in the hearth of Felix's own cottage and said, *I'm coming with you,* as if it were the simplest thing in the world.

Nothing about it was simple. Everything about it was terrifying. And so they walked.

The valley received them the way it always did—indifferently, swallowing the sound of their footsteps, flattening the world into grey grass and grey stone and the river running silver-brown at the bottom of everything. Hollowmere was a place where magic went to die. Felix had told themselves that for ten years like a prayer: *here you are safe, here you are nothing, here the thing inside you sleeps.* They had learned to love the numbness the way a burn victim learns to love cold water.

But now, walking east toward the valley's edge, Felix felt something they had never let themselves feel before.

Anticipation.

It sat in their chest like a second heartbeat, wrong-rhythmed, hungry. The map pulsed warmer with every step. And beneath their skin—faint, distant, like thunder heard through three walls—something stirred that had been still for a decade.

"Don't," Marguerite said quietly.

Felix startled. "Don't what?"

"Whatever you're doing with your face." She glanced back, and her eyes were steady and dark and far too perceptive. "You look like a man walking toward his own execution who's also relieved about it."

"That's absurd."

"Yes," she said. "It is. Keep walking."

* * *

### Marguerite

Marguerite had killed people at this boundary line.

Not many. Three, over eleven years of service—the ones who tried to slip out of Hollowmere in the night, believing the wardens' patrols ended at the fog. They hadn't believed her when she warned them. They hadn't understood that the boundary wasn't a wall but a threshold, and that crossing it awake was different from being born asleep on one side of it.

She watched Felix approach it now, and her hands remembered the weight of a sword hilt, unbidden.

The valley's edge announced itself the way a cliff announces itself to someone walking blindfolded: a change in the air, a wrongness in the light. The fog thinned. The grass grew taller, greener, touched with real color for the first time in miles. Beyond it, the lowlands rolled away gold and green under a sky bruised purple with evening, and somewhere out there the Spire stood like a needle through the world's fabric, leaking its slow poison into the covenant.

Felix stopped three paces from the line. Marguerite saw them plant their feet, saw their shoulders rise and fall once, twice.

"Felix."

"I know." Their voice came out scraped thin. "I remember what it feels like."

"Do you? You were twenty-two." She moved closer, careful, the way you approached a spooked animal—or a lit fuse. "Ten years is a long time for anything to sleep. When it wakes, it won't wake gently."

"I know that too." A ghost of their old dryness surfaced. "You're very comforting, warden."

"Ex-warden." She said it and felt the word land somewhere deep, a door closing on a whole life. "And I'm not trying to comfort you. I'm telling you that whatever happens on the other side of that line, I've seen it happen to others, and none of them described it as painless."

"What did they describe it as?"

Marguerite hesitated. One of the three had wept. One had screamed until her voice tore. The third had simply knelt down in the wet grass and pressed both hands into the earth and said, *oh, there you are,* in a voice full of such terrible tenderness that Marguerite had dreamed about it for months afterward.

"Like drowning," she said finally. "In reverse."

Felix looked at her. Then they laughed—a short, cracked sound with no humor in it—and stepped across the line.

* * *

### Felix

The world came back all at once, and it came back screaming.

It began in their teeth. Then their spine. Then everywhere. Magic didn't return to Felix Coldharbour—it *flooded*, the way water floods a dry channel, tearing at the banks, carrying ten years of silt and debris before it. They went to their knees in the tall grass and the grass *answered*, bending toward them like faces turning, and the sky overhead—which had been merely bruised—went dark in a widening ring, clouds boiling up out of a clear evening as if the horizon itself had been wounded.

*Oh,* Felix thought, from very far away. *Oh, there you are.*

Then the storm found their heartbeat and matched it.

Lightning crawled along the underside of the new clouds in silent veins. The air pressure dropped so fast their ears popped. Every hair on their body lifted. And underneath it all, beneath the terror, ran a current of feeling so vast and so familiar that Felix wanted to weep: this was the texture of their childhood. This was the sound of their mother humming while rain played against the windows of a house that no longer existed. This was home, and home was a monster, and the monster was them.

"Felix!" Marguerite's hands on their shoulders, solid, human, the only fixed point in a tilting world. "Look at me. Breathe. Pull it *in*, don't push it out—"

"I can't—" The words came out doubled, resonant, thunder speaking through their throat. "It's been so long, I don't—I can't hold it—"

"You held it for ten years. Hold it for ten more seconds."

The ground shuddered. Somewhere behind them, back in the dead valley, birds rose in a black sheet. Ahead, the lowlands lay golden and oblivious, farms and roads and a distant village with lamplight beginning in its windows, and above those lights the storm was gathering like a fist.

Felix gathered it instead.

They didn't know how they did it. Later they would think of it as swallowing—a desperate, full-body act of taking the storm back inside, cramming lightning down their own throat, folding wind into the hollow places of their chest where it had lived before. It hurt worse than letting it out. It hurt like pressing a hand against a blade. But the clouds thinned. The pressure eased. The grass released its bend, and the evening returned, shaken but intact, and Felix collapsed forward into Marguerite's arms shaking like a snapped rope.

"There," she said, and her voice wasn't steady either. "There. You did it."

Felix couldn't answer. Their mouth tasted of ozone and copper, and somewhere inside them the swallowed storm coiled and settled, restless as a caged animal, and they understood—with the clarity that only comes after catastrophe—that they would never again be able to pretend it wasn't there.

* * *

### Marguerite

They made camp in a hollow beneath a stand of alders, because Marguerite refused to travel in the dark with Felix barely able to stand, and because she'd heard the horses.

She heard them properly an hour past midnight: hooves on the old covenant road, more than one rider, moving with the disciplined spacing of a patrol rather than the scattered urgency of travelers. Warden hunters. Her former brothers and sisters, come to retrieve what she had failed to destroy.

She woke Felix with a hand over their mouth.

"Riders," she breathed. "Six, maybe seven. Coming up the road. We have maybe four minutes if they stop at the ford, less if they don't."

Felix's eyes went wide and clear in the dark. "The map—"

"Is singing, yes, I can feel it from here, and so can anyone within a mile who knows what to listen for." Which was the problem, and which Marguerite had realized too late: the bloodstained map wasn't just a guide. It was a beacon, and it had been broadcasting since the moment Felix bled on it. "Can you run?"

"Ask me tomorrow."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have."

Marguerite made the decision the way she'd been trained to make decisions: fast, cold, and alone. "Then we don't run. We let them find the camp empty and we take them from the trees. There are six of them and one of me and—"

"And one of me," Felix said quietly. "Whoever I am now."

The riders came exactly as she predicted, torches high, spreading out at the edge of the hollow with the smooth choreography of a hunt. Marguerite counted them from the shadows: seven, as feared, led by a broad figure in half-plate she recognized with a lurch—Ser Aldous Vane, who had taught her the blade, who had once told her that mercy was just murder postponed.

"Coldharbour!" he called into the dark, his voice carrying the easy confidence of a man who has never once been ambushed. "We know you're here. The map called us like a dinner bell. Come out, and perhaps the High Warden will let you die quickly."

Marguerite stepped out of the trees with her stolen sword drawn, and heard Felix do the same behind her, and knew with absolute certainty that seven against two was arithmetic written by corpses.

What happened next, she would spend the rest of her life failing to describe.

Felix raised one hand—not dramatically, not with any gesture she'd been taught to expect from storm-callers—and the sky *opened*. Not above them. Above the riders. A single bolt, white and total, struck the road ten feet in front of Ser Vane's horse and turned the packed earth to glass. The thunder arrived a heartbeat later and knocked two men clean from their saddles. Horses screamed and bolted. Torches died. And in the strobing afterlight Felix stood with their arm still extended, their face lit from below, wearing an expression Marguerite had never seen on them before—

—not triumph. Horror. Pure, naked horror, the look of someone who has opened a door they'd bricked over for ten years and found the room exactly as they left it.

"Go," Felix said, and the word crackled. "*Run.*"

Vane ran. What remained of his patrol ran. And the storm above them curled, and thickened, and did not disperse, and Marguerite turned to see Felix swaying on their feet with lightning crawling up their forearm like a living thing, refusing to be put down.

"Felix," she said carefully. "Let go of it."

"I'm trying."

"Try harder."

"I *am*—" Their voice broke. In the light of their own storm, Felix Coldharbour looked at their hands as though they belonged to a stranger. "Marguerite. It doesn't want to stop. It's been starving for ten years and it doesn't want to ever stop."

Above the alders, the clouds began, slowly and terribly, to spin.
