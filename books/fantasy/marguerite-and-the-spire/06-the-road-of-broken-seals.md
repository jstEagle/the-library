## 6. The Road of Broken Seals

The first waystation stood where the map said it would, though nothing else about it matched the world Felix had known.

It rose from a hillside of gorse and broken slate like the ribcage of some enormous drowned thing—white stone gone grey with rain, arches collapsed inward, moss writing its slow green sentences across every surface. Felix had passed within a mile of this place a hundred times in ten years of foraging Hollowmere's edges, and had never once seen it. The valley's dead magic had hidden it as thoroughly as it had hidden them.

Now, three days beyond the boundary, with storm-sense crackling under their skin like a healing wound, they could not stop seeing.

"There are more," Marguerite said behind them. She had her hood up against the drizzle, hand resting on the pommel of a sword she hadn't drawn since the skirmish at the ford road. "The map shows seven between here and the Spire. This is the closest."

Felix didn't answer. They were looking at the keystone.

Every waystation, according to the blood-script now crawling across the old parchment whenever Felix's fingers touched it, had been built around a seal—a knot of covenant-work buried in the foundation stone, holding one thread of the great binding taut between the storm realm and the mortal one. Seven stations. Seven seals. And each seal, the map insisted, had been anchored by a family. A bloodline sworn to feed the knot a single drop of devotion on each solstice, generation after generation, until the sky forgot how to fall.

The keystone arch above the doorway was carved with a name.

*Vessarin.*

Below it, someone had chiseled a single deep gouge through the letters—the same mark Felix had seen on their mother's map, the mark that meant *extinguished*. Not faded. Not forgotten. Hunted down to the last child and struck from the stone by hands that wanted no one to wonder what had been lost.

"They were shepherds," Felix said quietly. "Before the wardens came. My mother's notes—there was a Vessarin who married into the Ashvale line two hundred years ago. Sheep-farmers. Salt-traders." Their voice caught on something sharp. "People who kept a solstice candle burning and probably never knew why."

Marguerite came to stand beside them. Rain dripped from the ruined arch between them like a slow count.

"The order calls them the Unbound," she said. "The families that broke faith."

"And you believed that."

"I was taught it before I could read." Her jaw tightened. "Yes. I believed it."

Felix stepped through the doorway.

Inside, the waystation opened into a round chamber floored with mosaic—thousands of tiny tiles forming a wheel, or a storm seen from above, spiraling toward a center that had been defaced so thoroughly it was impossible to tell what image had lived there. Rain came through the broken roof in silver threads and pooled in the spiral's grooves, so that the whole floor seemed to move, the storm turning slowly beneath their feet.

At the center, where the seal should have been, there was only a socket. A hollow in the stone shaped like a cupped hand, empty as a pulled tooth.

Felix knelt beside it without deciding to. The moment their palm hovered over the hollow, the sensation struck them—that awful, familiar ache, the feeling of a held breath held too long. But here it wasn't coming from inside them. It was coming from the *absence*, radiating off the empty socket like cold off ice.

Something had been here. Something alive, in whatever way stones can be alive. And it had been torn out.

"This is why the storms are getting worse," Felix heard themselves say. "It's not just the Spire failing. Every one of these—" They looked up at Marguerite, and the words came out colder than they intended. "Your order didn't just kill the families. You killed the seals. You pulled the anchors out of the world and left the rope to fray."

"We didn't know what we were pulling," Marguerite said. Her voice was steady, but her hand had come off her sword and hung open at her side, as if she'd caught herself reaching for comfort. "Felix. I need you to hear all of it before you decide what I am."

"I already decided what your order is."

"Yes. And you're right." She crouched across the empty socket from them, rain running off her hood, and for the first time since the valley she looked tired—not warden-tired, but the bone-deep weariness of someone setting down something heavy after carrying it too far. "So hear the rest anyway. You deserve the whole weight of it."

* * *

They walked while she talked. The map wanted them moving—its blood-script lengthened along the western edge whenever they stopped, as if impatient—and Felix found they could not sit still inside that chamber with its drowning storm-floor. So they took the shepherd's track west through the hills, and Marguerite told it walking, eyes fixed on the path, the way people confess things they cannot bear to say face to face.

"When the Spire was built, nine hundred years ago, there were seven families. The Coldharbours anchored the heart-seal at the summit. The others held the outer knots—the Vessarins, the Ashvales, the Merrowgate line, the Ondrys, the Calloways, the Fenwick-Loes." She recited them like a litany, and perhaps to her they were one; Felix heard the cadence of rote learning, drilled into a child in a cloister classroom. "Each family swore to the covenant. Each was granted protections. Land, standing, exemption from the tithe. In exchange—a drop of blood at the solstice, and a life given over if the covenant ever demanded renewal."

"A life," Felix said flatly.

"One. Once a century, if the Spire required it. That was the doctrine." Marguerite's boots crunched on slate. "And for six hundred years, no renewal was ever called. The families grew comfortable. Powerful. Some of them began asking questions the High Wardens didn't like—whether the tithe was necessary, whether the Spire's authority rested on anything but an old bargain. The Merrowgates published a treatise arguing the covenant could be maintained by collective offering instead of bloodline sacrifice. That was two hundred and eleven years ago."

"What happened to them?"

"Within a decade, the Merrowgate line was dead. Fever, the record says." Her voice did not change, which was itself a kind of scream. "Then the Ondrys. A shipwreck. Then the Calloways, in a fire that took three generations in one night. The Fenwick-Loes simply vanish from the records—no deaths recorded, no lands inherited, as if they'd never existed. By the time I was born, four lines were gone and the order taught that they had *broken faith* and been abandoned by the storm realm for it. Divine judgment. We sang hymns about it."

Felix stopped walking.

The hills around them were green and ordinary, sheep-dotted, drenched in grey light. Somewhere below, a stream argued with its stones. It was unbearable, how ordinary the world remained while Felix stood in the middle of a graveyard nine hundred years wide.

"You hunted them," they said. "Not zealots in the dark. Not rogues. Your order. Systematically. For two centuries. Because they asked whether one life per century was worth the price."

"The ones who asked the question died first," Marguerite said. "The rest were killed for being witnesses." She finally turned to look at Felix, and her eyes were wet and furious and unflinching. "I burned my orders in the valley because I learned the covenant protects both realms. I have spent every mile since telling myself that was the worst of it. It isn't. The worst is that my order has been *finishing the job*. Every extinguished line weakened a seal. Every weakened seal made the Spire's hunger louder. And a hungry Spire justifies anything—more purges, more secrets, more power to the men who hold it. They haven't been guarding the covenant, Felix. They've been starving it on purpose, because a starving covenant needs wardens, and a healthy one doesn't."

The wind moved through the gorse with a sound almost like breathing.

"My mother knew," Felix said. It wasn't a question. The memory surfaced whole: her hands stained with ink, the locked box under the floorboards, the night she'd pressed the family signet into Felix's palm and said *if anyone comes, run to the valley and don't stop running.* "She wasn't hiding me from justice. She was hiding the last anchor from the people dismantling the ship."

"She was hiding the last key," Marguerite said. "There are two seals left, Felix. Yours at the Spire, and one more on this road. The Ashvale station. If the order reaches it before we do—"

"They won't leave it standing."

"No. And they won't leave *you* standing either. Not anymore. Not when killing you is the last step to owning what's left of the covenant entirely."

* * *

They reached the second waystation at dusk.

It was worse than the first. The Vessarin ruin had been abandoned; the Ashvale station had been *executed*. The walls bore scorch-marks in patterns Felix recognized from warden doctrine—cleansing-fire, the sanctioned burn that left no taint. The mosaic floor had been shattered with hammers, tile by patient tile. And in the center, where the seal-socket should have been, someone had set an iron post and mounted upon it a plaque in High Script:

*HERE ENDED THE ASHVALE PERJURY. THE STORM REMEMBERS ITS OWN.*

Felix stood before it a long time, rain gathering on their shoulders, and felt something in their chest go very quiet and very hard.

"They were proud of it," they said.

"They were proud of all of it." Marguerite's voice came from close behind. "That's the part I couldn't—I used to lead novices here. On pilgrimage. I taught children to venerate this post." A breath. "Felix, I'm not asking you to forgive—"

"No," Felix said. "You're not."

They turned. Marguerite stood in the ruined doorway with the last light behind her, and she did not flinch, and she did not reach for her sword, and she did not make it easy by apologizing her way out of the space between them.

"I need you to understand what I'm deciding," Felix said. "Because I've spent ten years believing my family was cursed, and I learned last week we were keepers, and I thought that was the bottom of it. But it isn't, is it? The price of my survival was paid in *them*. In the Vessarins and the Merrowgates and every child who died singing hymns about their own ancestors' shame. Every breath I've taken in Hollowmere was bought with their blood, and I didn't even know enough to mourn them."

"I know."

"Do you?" The storm rose in Felix's voice without permission, static crawling along their arms, the drizzle around them shivering into needles. "Because you still wear the uniform. You still carry the sword. You burned a piece of paper, Marguerite, and you've been walking beside me ever since as if that settles it."

Marguerite unbuckled the sword belt.

She did it slowly, deliberately, holding Felix's eyes the whole time, and laid it in the mud of the threshold with the care owed to something dangerous rather than discarded. Then she straightened, unarmed, in the doorway of a house her order had murdered.

"It doesn't settle it," she said. "Nothing I do settles it. I'm not asking you to trust me. I'm asking you to let me be useful long enough for you to decide."

Felix looked at the sword in the mud. At the iron post with its proud, obscene inscription. At the woman standing unarmed in the rain, waiting to be judged.

Above the hills, far to the north, lightning flickered inside a cloud that had no business carrying it—and under Felix's skin, the storm-sense answered, wild and grieving, like a dog hearing its name called from a burning house.

Two seals left. One of them dying.

"We walk tonight," Felix said. "And Marguerite—you carry nothing I don't watch you carry. Understood?"

"Understood."

They went north together into the dark, and behind them the rain filled the shattered mosaic, tile by tile, rebuilding the storm no one would ever see again.
