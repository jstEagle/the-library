## 7. Storms Over Saltmere

The sea reached for Saltmere before we did.

We crested the last hill on the road from the broken seal at Vell's Crossing, and Marguerite—*I*—stopped walking, because the city below was not a city anymore. It was a half-drowned thing, its lower streets turned to canals, its bell tower rising from gray water like the last tooth in an old jaw. Boats moved where carts should have been. Smoke still rose from chimneys that stood in three feet of brine. And beyond it all, past the harbor bar, the ocean lay flat and gleaming and patient, like a beast that had already won and was only waiting for the city to admit it.

Beside me, Felix went very still.

I had learned to read them by then—the small weather of their face. The way their jaw set when they were afraid. The way they looked at their own hands as if the hands belonged to someone else, someone who had done something unforgivable. I watched them look now, at the drowned streets, at the water lapping the second-story windows of the fishmongers' row, and I saw the shame arrive before any word did.

"This is what happens," Felix said quietly. "When the seals fail. This is what my mother's map was trying to tell me."

"The covenant is collapsing," I said. "The lowlands go first."

"I know." They started down the hill, boots sliding on wet grass. "I've always known. That was the whole point of Hollowmere, wasn't it? Put the storm-child somewhere the storms can't reach, and let everyone else pretend the world is safe." A bitter breath. "It was never safe. It was just quiet where *I* was standing."

I followed. There was nothing else to do. There had been nothing else to do since the night I burned my orders and chose this road, and every mile since had taught me the same lesson over and over: you do not follow Felix Coldharbour because it is wise. You follow because stopping would mean becoming the person you were before you knew the truth, and you cannot go back to being that person. Not ever.

* * *

Saltmere took us in the way drowning cities take in everyone: without questions, without welcome, without room.

The upper terraces were packed with refugees from the lower quarters, families sleeping under awnings, children running messages between neighbors through rain that never quite stopped. The air tasted of salt and rot and woodsmoke. Every third building flew a strip of blue cloth from its door—the old storm-blessing, I realized, the one the wardens' doctrine called superstition. These people had been praying to the covenant their whole lives without knowing its name.

We found lodging in a loft above a chandler's shop, paid for with the last of my warden's stipend, which the chandler accepted with the particular gratitude of a man who no longer trusted coin but hadn't yet stopped taking it. From the loft's single window we could see the harbor, and the harbor was wrong. The tide did not go out. It had not gone out in nine days, the chandler's wife told us, and each day the water climbed another handspan up the seawall, calm as milk, as if the sea were holding its breath.

"It's waiting," she said, wringing out a rag that could not get any wetter. "My grandmother told stories about the Waiting Tide. Said it came once, long ago, before the Spire renewed itself. Said the sea pulls back its anger and holds it, and holds it, and then—" She made a gesture, vague and terrible, opening her hands toward the horizon.

"Then what?" Felix asked.

She looked at them with the flat eyes of someone too tired for hope. "Then everything the water remembers, it gives back."

That night Felix did not sleep. I heard them moving in the dark, pacing the length of the loft, and when I finally lit the lamp I found them standing at the window with their palms pressed flat against the glass, and frost—or something like frost, though the night was warm—blossoming outward from their fingers across the pane.

"Felix."

"I can feel it," they whispered. "The whole coast. It's like—" Their voice cracked. "Hollowmere was silence. Ten years of silence. And now I'm standing in the middle of a scream and I can't stop hearing it."

"Then don't just hear it," I said, before I could think better of it. "You're the last anchor of a dying covenant. Maybe you weren't meant only to carry the cost. Maybe you were meant to hold things together."

They turned and looked at me, and in the lamplight their eyes had that storm-light color, green shot through with gray, and for a moment I thought they would argue. Instead they said, "Every time I use it, Marguerite, I become more visible. And visibility is death. You know that better than anyone—you were sent to be the instrument of it."

"That was before," I said.

"Before what?"

"Before I understood what your invisibility was costing everyone else."

* * *

The sea gave back its memory on the third morning.

There was no warning anyone could heed, because the warning was the silence itself—the gulls going quiet all at once, the harbor water shivering like a struck bell, the fishermen on the seawall turning as one body toward the horizon. I was buying bread. Felix was beside me. And I felt the change in the crowd before I understood it, that animal shift, hundreds of people inhaling at the same time.

Then the wave came.

Not a mountain of water—not the wall of destruction the ballads describe. Something worse. The sea simply *rose*, all along the coast at once, smooth and enormous, and came walking inland like a god taking back borrowed land. It took the harbor district in a minute. It took the fishmongers' row in two. People ran up the terraces in screaming rivers, and the water walked behind them, unhurried, inevitable, filling streets like veins, and I stood frozen in the square with a loaf of bread still in my hand watching ten years of doctrine die in front of me. *The covenant protects both realms.* The High Warden's own words. And here was the proof of the lie by omission: the covenant was failing, and the order whose sacred duty was the balance had known, and had spent its strength hunting a frightened exile instead of mending the sky.

Felix grabbed my arm. "The terrace church," they shouted over the roar. "People are trapped on the roof—the water's cutting them off—"

I saw it. A knot of figures on the slate roof of the drowned chapel, maybe twenty, maybe thirty, hemmed in by a street that had become a strait of churning current. No boats close enough. No ropes long enough. By the time help organized itself, the roof would be gone.

And Felix Coldharbour stepped off the terrace edge into open air.

I will remember that moment until I die. They didn't fly—they fell upward, or the wind caught them, or the boundary between those two things simply stopped mattering. The storm came out of them the way sound comes out of a struck bell. Clouds boiled down out of a morning sky that had been clear; wind screamed down the flooded avenue; and Felix stood on nothing above the chapel roof with their arms spread and their hair whipping and lightning crawling along their sleeves like living embroidery, and they were *magnificent*, and they were *terrified*, and both things were completely true at once.

They pulled the water aside.

Not gently—it was raw, unpracticed, a fist of pressure shoving the flood back from the chapel street, carving a corridor of exposed cobbles through the drown. Lightning struck the current twice, deliberately, and I understood dimly that they were boiling a channel dry, driving the water back with steam and thunder. "MOVE!" their voice boomed with a doubled resonance, storm-voice under human voice, and the people on the roof moved, stumbling and sliding down into the corridor of dry stone, herded by walls of wind toward the high terraces while the sea raged against a barrier it could not name.

It lasted perhaps four minutes. Then Felix dropped—actually dropped, out of the sky, into my arms, soaked and shaking and half-conscious, and the flood surged back into the empty street behind them like a beast reclaiming its den.

By evening, they were a legend.

I heard it in the chandler's loft, from the mouths of strangers: *the Storm-Saint of Saltmere*. A figure out of the sky. A person made of lightning who held back the sea. Someone swore their grandmother had seen the same thing sixty years ago, the last time the Spire was renewed—a Coldharbour, though nobody alive remembered the name. They were calling it an omen. A blessing. The blue cloths multiplied overnight until every door in the upper city wore one.

Felix sat on the loft floor with their back against the wall and listened to the stories climb the stairs, and I watched purpose kindle in their face the way fire kindles in wet wood—slowly, stubbornly, against all expectation.

"They're not afraid of me," Felix said wonderingly. "Ten years I hid because I thought—if they saw what I am, they'd burn me. And they're hanging blessings on their doors."

"They'll hang you on a pyre just as fast if the harvest fails next year," I said, because someone had to say it. "Worship curdles, Felix. I was raised on the doctrine. I know how it turns."

"I know." But they were smiling, faintly, tiredly, looking at their own hands as if the hands might finally belong to them. "But Marguerite—I *held* it. The whole weight of the sea, and I held it. Do you know what that means?" They looked up, and the storm-light in their eyes was steady now. "If I can hold back one wave, I can mend the thing that sent it. The Spire. The covenant. All of it."

"And the price," I said quietly. "The old bargain. One life for a century of order."

The smile faded, but did not die. "One life," they agreed softly. "That's the old bargain. Nobody's written the new one yet."

Below us, in the drowned streets, the sea had begun to withdraw—but slowly, grudgingly, the way a creditor leaves a debtor's door. And far off to the north, past the hills, past the ruined waystations, a light was rising against the clouds that had no business being there: thin, white, vertical, like a pillar of held breath.

The Spire. Visible from fifty miles, for the first time in living memory.

And around its base, tiny with distance, I could see movement. Columns. Torches. The wardens were gathering—and they were marching south, toward Saltmere, toward the Storm-Saint everyone was singing about.

Toward us.
