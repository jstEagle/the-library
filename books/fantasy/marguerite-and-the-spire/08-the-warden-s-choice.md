## 8. The Warden's Choice

### Marguerite

The High Warden's tent smelled of cedar smoke and old parchment, and beneath both—faint as a bruise—the iron tang of the Spire itself. Marguerite had not stood this close to a warden of the inner circle in eleven days. She had almost forgotten how they carried the mountain with them, its coldness lodged behind the eyes.

Vessan Corvale sat behind a field desk too fine for a campaign, his white gloves folded on a map of the lowlands that was already out of date. The ink showed Saltmere whole. Marguerite had walked its drowned streets three nights ago. She said nothing about it.

"Sister Marguerite." He did not look up. "You were reported dead at the ford crossing."

"The report was optimistic."

"And Coldharbour?"

"Alive. Moving north along the old seal-road. Slowly. Their magic is still... unsteady." Each word cost her something she could not name. She kept her face the way the order had trained it: smooth as river stone, giving nothing back.

Corvale looked up then, and smiled, and it was the smile that frightened her more than any threat could have. It was warm. Wardens were never warm unless they wanted something.

"You've done well," he said. "Better than well. Eleven years we believed the last Coldharbour would have to be hunted like a fox, and instead you walk them to us like a lamb that thinks it's found a shepherd." He rose, crossed to her, and pressed something into her gloved hand: a small medallion, silver, stamped with the Spire's seven-pointed star. Full warden-sister rank. The rank she'd been denied for a decade because she asked one question too many at her oath-taking.

"A pardon," he said. "Full restoration. Your name cleared, your question answered, your place restored above even what you lost. All of it, Marguerite, if you deliver Felix Coldharbour to the Spire alive."

"Alive," she repeated.

"The covenant is an old thing and a hungry one. It wants blood freely given at the top of the world—or blood taken there, which amounts to the same in the end." He said it gently, the way a physician describes an amputation. "One life, Sister. One life against a century of calm skies. Against the lowlands not drowning. You've seen Saltmere."

She had seen Saltmere. She had seen a child on a rooftop, and Felix Coldharbour standing hip-deep in black water with lightning wrapped around their arms like a mother gathering up a runaway, pulling the storm down before it could break, spending themselves like a spendthrift heir because strangers were drowning and they could not *not*.

"One life," Corvale said again, softer. "Is their life worth more than ten thousand? You've read the doctrine. You wrote half of it, once, before you let doubt in."

Marguerite looked at the medallion. Silver caught the lamplight and threw it back pale as bone.

"Yes," she heard herself say. "I'll bring them in."

The lie went through her like a blade going through water—no resistance at all, and no way afterward to find the wound.

* * *

### Felix

Felix heard all of it.

They had not meant to eavesdrop; that was the truth they would tell themselves later, turning it over like a stone looking for the soft underside. They had come to the edge of the camp to fill the waterskins, moving quiet as they'd learned to move in Hollowmere, when the wind shifted and carried the High Warden's voice straight into their ears—and after it, clear as a bell rung in an empty chapel, Marguerite's answer.

*I'll bring them in.*

The waterskins slipped from Felix's hands. They caught them against their chest before they hit the ground, heart slamming, magic rising unbidden under their skin the way it always did now when fear came calling—a pressure behind the ribs, a taste of ozone, the ghost of every storm they'd ever swallowed pressing outward like water against a dam.

They crouched in the gorse shadow and made themselves breathe.

*One life against a century.* That was the bargain. That had always been the bargain; the map had told them so in their mother's hand, and they had hoped—foolishly, they saw now—that hope was not evidence. The covenant wanted a Coldharbour on the altar, and here was Marguerite, their Marguerite, agreeing to carry them there.

And yet.

Felix had spent ten years learning to read people the way they'd once read weather: by pressure, by the set of things, by what moved underneath. And what they had heard in Marguerite's voice was not agreement. It was a door closing carefully from the inside. He had heard her lie before—he'd heard her tell the hunter-captain at the broken seal that the trail went cold at the river, heard her voice do exactly this, go smooth and flat as ice over deep water.

But hearing it now, aimed at themselves—

They stayed crouched in the dark long after the tent flap fell closed, long after the camp settled into its watch-rhythms, and the storm inside them beat against its walls and asked, in the oldest language they knew, *what will you do?*

* * *

### Marguerite

She found Felix waiting at their campsite, sitting upright by the dead fire, and knew in the first heartbeat that something had gone wrong. Felix's face was doing what it always did when they were afraid: nothing. Ten years of practice written plain.

"You met with him," Felix said.

"I did."

"And?"

Marguerite sat down across the ashes. Her hands, she noticed distantly, were perfectly steady. She hated them for it.

"He offered me a pardon. Full rank. My name back." She turned the medallion over in her fingers. "In exchange for delivering you to the Spire. Alive."

The silence stretched. Somewhere downslope, a nightjar called its two-note question and got no answer.

"I know," Felix said quietly. "I was at the edge of the camp. I heard you accept."

There it was. Marguerite watched Felix's hands—clenched loose, deliberately loose, the way a person holds still so the shaking won't show—and felt the moment arrive that she had been dreading since the ford, since the map, since the first morning she'd chosen not to put an arrow through the last Coldharbour's throat. Every road she might have walked led here, to this fire, to this sentence she could not take back.

"So," Felix said. "This is where it ends."

"No."

"You said—I heard you say—"

"I know exactly what I said." She leaned forward and held out the medallion, letting the lamplight of the coals find it. "Look at me, Felix. Look at my hands. If I meant to take you, I wouldn't be talking. I've had eleven days and a hundred better chances than this one, and I wasted every single one of them because I couldn't do it. Do you understand what I'm telling you? I am *bad* at betrayal. I burned my orders rather than follow them. Vessan Corvale offered me everything I lost and I took it because taking it gets me inside the Spire with you, past the gates, past the wardens, up the stairs no outsider has climbed in a hundred years."

Felix stared at her. The storm-pressure around them flickered—she felt it, a static lift at the nape of her neck, the air going thin and bright the way it did when Felix's control frayed.

"You should have told me," Felix said at last, and their voice cracked on it, and Marguerite realized with a lurch that the anger in it was not anger at all, or not only. "You should have—you can't make these decisions alone. Not about my life. Not about—" They stopped. Breathed. When they spoke again the words came slower, dragged up from somewhere deep. "Do you know what I thought, crouching in the gorse? I thought: of course. Of course it ends like this. Everyone I've ever trusted has either died or been lying to me, and I'd gotten comfortable, and comfort is just the trap you stop noticing."

"I know."

"You don't get to say 'I know.'"

"No," Marguerite agreed. "But I'll say this anyway, and then you can decide what I am. I lied to Corvale the same way I'd lie to anyone hunting you. If that makes me someone who betrays, then I betray the people who deserve it, and I will keep doing it, and I will not apologize, and I will not ask permission each time, because asking permission would get us both killed." She set the medallion down on the flat stone between them, silver side up, the seven-pointed star catching the ember-light. "That's yours to throw in the fire or keep. Either way, tomorrow I walk into the Spire with a prisoner, and the prisoner walks beside me, and somewhere between the gate and the top of the world, we're going to need a plan better than anything either of us has managed so far."

Felix looked at the medallion for a long time. The wind moved through the gorse. Above the northern horizon, so faint it might have been imagination, the sky pulsed with a sick green shimmer—the Spire's failing light, visible now even from here, the covenant's heart stuttering like a dying lamp.

Then Felix picked up the medallion. Did not throw it in the fire. Closed their fist around it instead, and Marguerite saw their knuckles whiten, saw the tremor finally come and be allowed to come.

"If you're lying," Felix said, "I want you to know that I'll fight. Not to win. I'm not stupid enough to think I'd win. But I won't make it clean for them. Whatever they want at the top of that Spire, they'll have to take it from me fighting."

"I know," Marguerite said. "It's one of the things I—"

She stopped. The sentence she had been about to finish had been waiting eleven days for its moment, and this ash-grey hour beside a dead fire, with betrayal still hanging in the air between them like smoke, was not it. Maybe no moment would be.

"It's late," she said instead. "Sleep. I'll take first watch."

Felix lay down with their back to her, the medallion still clenched in one fist, and for a long time neither of them slept, and neither of them spoke, and the green light on the horizon pulsed and pulsed like something knocking patiently at a door.

Near dawn, Felix's voice came out of the darkness, low and rough:

"Marguerite. When we get to the Spire—whatever the rite demands—it doesn't get to be me. I didn't climb all this way to die politely. Promise me you remember that, even if I forget it myself up there."

"I promise," Marguerite said, and watched the east begin, very slowly, to bleed light.
