## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The rain in Thornvale fell like a rumor — soft, persistent, and impossible to trace to its source.

Wren Winterbourne had learned, over ten years, to love it for exactly that reason. In the lowlands of his childhood, rain had been a thing with teeth. It came when he felt too much, gathered at the edges of his temper like iron filings around a lodestone, and once — only once, he allowed himself the memory on certain grey mornings and no others — it had come down as a wall of water that took the roof off the millhouse and put three feet of river through his mother's kitchen. He had been nine years old. His mother had looked at him across the flooded floor with an expression he had spent a decade trying not to remember, and by winter she had made her arrangements, and by spring he was walking north with a name that wasn't his and a purse that wasn't full and instructions repeated so often they had worn smooth as river stones: *cross the Thornline, boy, and don't look back, and whatever you do, don't call anything.*

So he hadn't called anything. Not once. Not in ten years of ordinary rain.

He rose before dawn on the morning the proclamation came, because he always rose before dawn, because scribes who wanted work from Master Aldous Quill kept honest hours. His room above the chandlery was cold enough to see his breath. He dressed in the dark by touch — wool shirt, wool coat, boots softened at the heels — and went down the stairs past the smell of tallow and last night's candle-smoke into the blue-grey street of Lower Mill Row.

Thornvale was beautiful in the way that forgotten things are beautiful. The valley sat cupped between two ridges of the Thorn Mountains like water in a green hand, terraced with orchards and sheep-pastures, stitched through with streams that ran cold and quick and utterly unremarkable. There was no magic in it. That was the whole point, though almost nobody in the valley knew there was a point at all. They thought their valley was simply blessedly dull — no hauntings, no hedge-witches, no charms against blight ever worth the buying. Wren had heard old Tam Hobb say a hundred times, with the satisfaction of a man describing a sound investment: *Nothing strange ever takes root here.* And Wren would nod along, ink-stained and mild, and feel the truth of it settle over him like a second coat.

Because it was true. Nothing strange took root in Thornvale. Magic died at the border like a cut flower left out of water. He had felt it happen to himself the day he crossed — felt the storm that lived coiled behind his sternum go quiet, then drowsy, then still, until after some months he could no longer be sure whether it slept or whether it had simply, mercifully, drowned. The valley's damp air pressed everything down. Even his dreams were smaller here.

He liked that. He had learned to like that.

* * *

Master Quill's scriptorium occupied the ground floor of a crooked building on Chandler's Lane, and it smelled of oak gall ink, beeswax, and the particular dustiness of vellum that had been waiting a long time to be useful. Wren's bench was by the window, where the light came early. He had copied deeds and wills and inventories of sheep for ten years, and if his hand trembled sometimes on long descenders — a quirk Master Quill attributed to a nervous constitution — well, nervous constitutions were common enough, and nobody in Thornvale looked twice at a scribe whose letters leaned like grass in wind.

"You're late," said Master Quill, who was never awake enough before his third cup of barley tea to mean it.

"The bridge ice," Wren said, hanging his coat. "I walked the long way."

"Mm." Quill squinted at him over his cup. "There's a rider come up from the pass. Crown courier. He'll want fair copies made before noon, and he wants them *good*, which means you, since Fenwick dots his i's like a man stabbing something."

Wren's hands, unwrapping his writing kit, went very still for half a heartbeat. Then they moved again, smooth and ordinary, and he heard himself say, "A crown courier? This far up? What does the crown want with Thornvale?"

"Taxes, likely. Or soldiers wintering. Or some new law about fence heights." Quill waved a dismissive hand. "The crown is a great hungry mouth a long way off. Copy what it says and don't chew on it."

But Wren had spent ten years learning to chew on things quietly, and while he trimmed his pen and ground his ink he found himself listening past the scratch of nibs, past Fenwick's tuneless humming, to the front room where the courier waited — a lean man in travel-grey with mud to the knee and the flat, patient eyes of someone paid to deliver unpleasant things.

The document itself arrived on his bench within the hour: a broadsheet of heavy paper, the royal seal stamped fat and red at its head, and beneath it lines of print that Wren read once at speed and then, with a sensation like stepping off a ledge in a dream, read again very slowly.

*By order of Her Majesty the Queen Regent, and for the protection of all loyal subjects—*

Protection. The word sat wrong already. Protection was what you gave lambs and children and things that couldn't run.

*—it is hereby proclaimed throughout the realm that all persons bearing the hereditary gift commonly called the storm-calling — that is, the power to summon, command, or otherwise compel wind, thunder, lightning, or tempest — shall present themselves to the nearest crown authority for registration, safekeeping, and protection.*

*Any bearer failing to surrender within ninety days of this proclamation shall be deemed an enemy of the peace, subject to seizure, trial, and such penalties as the law provides.*

*Let none shelter or conceal a bearer. Let none withhold knowledge of a bearer. The crown remembers, and the crown will find.*

Wren set down his pen. It was a small motion, deliberate, the kind of motion a man makes when he is afraid that any larger one will shatter something.

Storm-callers. Not gift-bearers generally. Not witches broadly, as the old edicts had mumbled. Storm-callers, named specifically, precisely, the way a hunter names the animal he has tracked for years. Which meant somebody, somewhere behind that red seal, knew there were still storm-callers to find. Knew, or believed, or had been told.

*The crown remembers.* Remembered what? Ten years ago, when he had crossed the Thornline with his mother's purse jingling in his pocket, the hunts had been general, diffuse, aimed at hedge-witches and charm-sellers and the occasional unlucky midwife. Nobody had spoken of storm-callers as a category. Nobody had seemed to know the word.

He copied the proclamation four times, in his best careful hand, for posting at the market cross, the mill, the chapel door, and the toll-house at the valley's mouth. His letters did not lean. His descenders did not tremble. Some deep-buried discipline held him steady, the same discipline that had held him through ten years of not-calling, and he was grateful for it, and he hated it, both at once.

At the toll-house copy, the courier watched him work. Wren felt the gaze like weather coming.

"Fine hand," the courier said.

"Thank you."

"Been in the valley long?"

"Ten years."

"Family?"

"No."

The courier nodded slowly, the way men nod when they are filing something away. "Quiet valley," he said. "Nothing strange ever takes root here, they tell me."

"That's what they tell me," Wren said, and smiled, and the smile cost him more than the copying had.

* * *

That evening he climbed the shepherd's path above the town as far as the old thorn hedge — the ragged, black-limbed line of trees that gave the valley its name and marked, though no map admitted it, the border of the world's forgetting. Beyond the hedge the land rose toward the pass, and the air changed; he could feel it even now, even dampened as he was, a faint pressure against his skin like the memory of a hand. On the far side of that line, his gift would wake. On the near side, it drowned.

He stood at the hedge as the light failed and let himself do the thing he permitted perhaps twice a year: he remembered.

His mother's kitchen, dry and warm, smelling of bread. His father humming off-key. The way storms used to gather around him like moths, drawn to something in his blood, and how his mother had taught him to breathe slow and think of nothing, nothing, nothing, until the clouds lost interest and drifted on. *You're not a weapon, Wren. You're weather. Weather passes.* And then the flood, and the look on her face, and the arrangements, and the road north, and the hedge, and the drowning.

Somewhere beyond those mountains, other people carried the same blood. He had never gone looking. Looking was how people got found. His mother's last letter — burned, years ago, per instruction — had told him only that the family that remained had scattered wide and gone silent, and that silence was the only shield that worked.

And now the crown had learned the word *storm-caller*, and had printed it in fat black letters, and had nailed it to chapel doors in valleys like this one.

Ninety days. He turned the number over. A courier would ride back down through the pass with copies for every town between here and the capital. Reports would go up the chain — *no bearers found in Thornvale, nothing strange takes root there* — unless someone reported otherwise. Unless someone noticed a scribe whose birds went quiet. Unless someone counted the years since the last true storm in the valley and thought it odd that the sky above Thornvale was so very, very well-behaved.

The first drop of rain hit his cheek. Ordinary rain. Valley rain. It fell without teeth, without memory, and he stood in it a long moment, letting it soak his hair, feeling nothing stir in his chest except fear, which was only human, and therefore safe.

He was halfway back down the path when he saw the lights below: lanterns moving up Chandler's Lane toward the scriptorium, more than one, moving with purpose. And with them, cutting through the wet evening air, came a voice he didn't know — a woman's voice, crisp and carrying, asking Master Quill a question he was too far away to hear but whose shape he recognized the way you recognize the shape of a knife before you see the blade:

Someone was asking about maps. Someone was asking about caves. Someone had just arrived in Thornvale, and wanted to know who knew the valley best.
