## 2. A Cartographer's Errand

The road into Thornvale smelled of wet stone and old leaves, and Marguerite Vasseur hated it immediately, the way she hated all places that did not want her.

She had felt it at the border — not a wall, not a ward, nothing so crude as the crown's hunters imagined. Just a softening, like stepping from a bright room into one where the light had been left to go gray. The compass in her saddlebag spun lazily, then settled wrong. The charm sewn into her collar, a hunter's trinket that warmed near any working of magic, lay cold as river stone against her throat.

*Nothing here,* it said. *No one here.*

That was the valley's whole trick, then. Ten years of rumor, three dead informants, and a bounty sealed with the queen's own wax — and the answer was simply that Thornvale swallowed things whole. Magic came in and forgot itself. Names came in and grew quiet. She could see why a man might choose it as a grave.

She rode down through birch and alder, past fences mended with care, past a shrine where someone had left bread for crows that were not there yet. The village proper sat along a slow brown river: two streets, a mill, a chapel with a leaning spire, and a scribe's shop with a sign so faded it read more like an apology than a name. Smoke rose from its chimney in a thin, obedient line.

Marguerite dismounted at the well and let herself be ordinary. She had practiced ordinariness the way other women practiced music — scales every morning until the performance cost nothing. A cartographer's coat, ink-stained at the cuffs. A ledger under her arm. A smile that asked for nothing but directions.

"Vasseur," she told the miller's wife, who had come out wiping flour from her hands. "Commissioned by the Royal Geographic Society. There are caves in the western ridge — limestone, by the rockfall debris downstream — and no one has ever surveyed them properly. I'm to map them before some mining concern does something regrettable."

It was a good lie. It had geology in it, and boredom, and the word *regrettable*, which made people feel clever rather than questioned. The miller's wife nodded along, already fond of her.

"You'll want lodging at the Widow Ansel's," she said. "And if you need documents copied, or letters sent downriver, young Wren keeps the shop by the bridge. Best hand in the valley. Quiet as anything, but his work is beautiful."

*Quiet as anything.* Marguerite smiled and thanked her, and filed the sentence away where she kept everything useful, in the cold drawer at the back of her mind.

* * *

The Widow Ansel's house smelled of lavender and boiled mutton, and Marguerite spent her first evening doing what she always did in a new place: listening.

Thornvale talked easily. That was the trouble with small places — they had no practice at suspicion. Over supper she learned the harvest was late this year; that the miller's son had gone downriver to seek his fortune and written twice; that the chapel bell cracked in a frost nine years ago and no founder would come so far to recast it. She learned that the valley kept no soldiers, paid its taxes in wool and silence, and had not seen a crown official in living memory.

And she learned about Wren Winterbourne — though no one called him that here. He was just *Wren*, the scribe. Come ten years ago, they thought, or eleven. No one remembered exactly. No one remembered much about him at all, which was itself remarkable, because villages remember everything. They remembered which family had owned the scribe's cottage before him. They remembered a flood forty years back. They could not seem to recall whether Wren had arrived in spring or autumn, alone or with company, or what he'd said when he first walked down their road.

He had stepped into the valley's forgetting, Marguerite thought, like a coin dropped into deep water.

"He copies deeds and wills," the widow said, refilling her cup. "And he teaches letters to children on rest days. Gentle with them. Never raises his voice." She paused, with the particular delicacy of gossips everywhere. "Some say he was running from something. But he's paid his tithes and fixed our boundary stones without being asked, so whatever it was, we've decided it can keep."

"And you?" Marguerite asked lightly. "What do you decide?"

"I decide he's lonely." The widow shrugged. "Lonely men make good neighbors."

Later, by candlelight, Marguerite opened her commission and read it again, though she knew it by heart. The seal was the queen's. The hand was her handler's, dry as August:

*Rumor persists of a storm-caller residing within Thornvale. The gift is presumed dormant but not extinguished. Locate. Confirm. Do not engage. Report via the drop at Miller's Bend. You are reminded that the last caller taken alive was worth four hundred crowns to the crown and a heretic's fire to everyone else.*

Four hundred crowns. Enough to buy back her father's maps, seized when the Inquisition burned his library. Enough to start over somewhere the word *cartographer* didn't mean *spy who draws*. She touched the cold charm at her collar and told herself, as she told herself nightly, that a man who hid for ten years had chosen his side by choosing silence.

She almost believed it. That was the trick of the work: you carried your justifications like ballast, and so long as you never set them down, you could walk upright.

* * *

She went to the scribe's shop on the third morning, ostensibly to commission fair copies of her cave surveys.

The bell over the door made a sound like a struck spoon, and Wren looked up from behind a counter stacked with paper, and Marguerite performed her first real assessment of the quarry.

He was unremarkable on purpose, she suspected. Brown hair going gray early at the temples. A face built to be forgotten — pleasant, unlined, arranged around a mildness that sat on him like borrowed clothing. His sleeves were rolled to the elbow and his forearms were stained to the wrist with iron-gall ink, that blue-black bruise-color that never fully washed out of skin. A working scribe's hands. Nothing more.

Except.

Except that the birds outside the window — sparrows quarreling over crumbs on the sill — had gone silent the moment she'd stopped in front of the shop. She had noticed it from across the street: a sudden hole in the morning's noise, a held breath. And now, standing close to him, she noticed it again. Not flight. Silence. As though the birds had agreed among themselves not to draw attention to whatever stood behind that glass.

She had read the hunters' field manuals. She knew the signs they taught: scorched doorframes, melted candle wax, animals that would not drink from a man's well. But the manuals described storm-callers as loud things — thunder given legs, lightning in the blood. They did not describe this: a quietness that gathered around a man like snow around a fencepost.

"You're the cartographer," he said. His voice was low and even, a voice used to reading aloud in empty rooms. "Madame Vasseur. The widow mentioned you."

"Monsieur Wren." She set her ledger on the counter between them, open to a blank page, and let her smile do its small warm work. "I'm told you have the best hand in the valley. I have six weeks of survey notes that look like a spider drowned in my inkwell, and a Society that will pay for beauty."

Something moved behind his eyes — not fear, not yet. Recognition, maybe. The wariness of a man who has learned that strangers are weather: mostly harmless, occasionally ruinous.

"The caves on the west ridge," he said. "They're not much. Shepherds use them in bad weather."

"Shepherds don't draw isobaths." She tapped the ledger. "Paper, please. Good paper — I'll supply my own if yours is pulpy. And I'll need a guide up the ridge eventually. Someone who knows the paths."

"I don't leave the valley," he said.

It came too fast. She watched him hear himself say it, watched the mild face do its work smoothing the words over — *the roads are bad, my work keeps me* — but the first sentence had already landed, bare and true, the way a stone shows through shallow water.

"Neither do most people here, I imagine," she said pleasantly. "That's rather the point of a valley."

He cut her paper. He counted her change. He wrote her a receipt in a hand so fine it looked printed, each letter upright and patient, and while he wrote she studied his fingers and thought: *ten years of ink. Ten years of making yourself useful and small and legible.* A man could bury a great deal under beautiful handwriting. She should know; she buried things under maps.

"Your hands," she said, conversational, tilting her head at them. "You must write all day."

"When there's work."

"Ink gets into the skin, doesn't it. After enough years." She smiled. "My father was an engraver. His hands were black to the elbow by the end. People always guessed his trade wrong, though. They'd say *coal merchant*, or *smith*. Never once guessed right."

Wren set down the pen. For half a heartbeat — no longer, less than the time it takes a candle to gutter — he looked at her with an attention so total it was nearly a physical weight, the way a storm looks at a landscape it hasn't decided upon. Then the mildness returned, seamless, and he slid the receipt across the counter.

"They'll guess what you show them," he said. "Mostly."

"Mostly," she agreed, and laughed, and gathered her ledger, and wished him good morning.

Outside, the sparrows started up again all at once, as if released.

* * *

That evening she climbed the bell tower of the leaning chapel with her survey glass and watched the village settle into dusk. Chimney smoke. Lamps lit one by one. The scribe's shop went dark early, as it did every night, and she marked its position in her mind with the same precision she gave to fortifications.

Below, the valley lay green and harmless, holding its secret the way water holds a stone.

She took out the cold charm and turned it in her fingers. Still nothing. Whatever slept in that quiet shop by the bridge, the valley had wrapped it so thoroughly in forgetting that even a hunter's trinket couldn't find the pulse. Which meant confirmation would have to come the old way: patience, proximity, and a lie maintained daily until it became furniture.

Six weeks, she estimated. Perhaps fewer. She would commission her copies. She would sketch her useless caves. She would laugh at the widow's jokes and teach the miller's daughter to draw coastlines, and every day she would sit a little closer to the truth until it forgot to be afraid of her.

She thought of four hundred crowns. She thought of her father's maps locked in an Inquisitor's cellar, and the letter she would write the day she could ransom them.

She did not think — or rather, she declined to think — about the way he had said *I don't leave the valley*, quick and naked as a flinch. Or how, for that half-heartbeat, his eyes had held the whole gray weight of weather.

*He's hiding something,* she told the dusk, reasonably. *That's the job. That's all the job is.*

Far off on the eastern road, past the border stones where her compass still lied to her, she saw — or thought she saw — riders. Several. Moving fast, kicking up dust even at this distance, coming down toward the valley with the unhurried certainty of men who had never once needed to knock.

Marguerite lowered the glass slowly.

Her commission had said *do not engage*. It had said nothing at all about what to do when the engagement arrived without her — when the crown's answer to her careful question came riding in with steel instead of subtlety, weeks ahead of any report she could send.

She watched the riders descend toward Thornvale, and the cold drawer at the back of her mind opened all on its own, and inside it a single thought sat waiting:

*Whatever comes out of that valley now, it will come out because of me.*
