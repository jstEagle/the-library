## 4. Flight Across the Thornline

Wren had rehearsed this night for ten years without ever letting himself believe it would come.

He moved through Thornvale's sleeping streets with the boy slung across his back — Tam, they'd called him before the hunters' whips took even that from him, though Wren only knew it because he'd whispered it into the boy's ear and felt him stir. The boy was light in the way that fevered things are light, all the weight burned off him, and his blood had soaked through Wren's coat until it pressed cold against his spine like a second skin.

The letter crackled against Wren's chest, folded inside his shirt where it had lived for three days now. *They have found us,* it said, in a hand he almost didn't recognize as his mother's. *Come home, or burn alone.* Addressed to Auren Stormborn. A name no one in Thornvale had spoken in a decade, least of all him.

He passed the scriptorium where he'd copied ledgers and love letters and funeral notices for ten years. He did not look at it. Looking would be a kind of prayer, and prayers were for people who believed they'd come back.

At the valley's western edge, where the orchards gave way to bracken and the hills rose like sleeping animals, the rain began. It always rained here — soft, gray, unremarkable rain, the rain of an ordinary place. Wren had spent ten years telling himself he loved it. Tonight it fell on his face like a hand pressing him down, saying *stay, stay, stay.*

He kept walking.

Behind him, far back but not far enough, a dog barked once and was silenced. Torches bloomed along the river road like a string of orange flowers opening in the dark.

*They've found the boy's trail,* Wren thought, and something in his chest that had been dormant for ten years rolled over in its sleep.

* * *

Marguerite had been following Wren Winterbourne for three days, and she was beginning to hate how good he was at it.

No wasted movement. No lantern. He kept to the high ground where the ferns swallowed footfalls whole, and he varied his pace like a man who'd learned pursuit from someone who'd survived it. She'd mapped half the known world and she could count on one hand the people who moved through wild country like that: soldiers, smugglers, and liars.

*Or exiles,* she thought, crouching at the edge of a stream where his tracks ended in wet stone. *Exiles most of all.*

She should have sent her report to the garrison at Cael Ford by now. Three days ago she'd stood in her rented room with the letter written — *Subject located. Scribe, Thornvale village, ink-stained hands, matches description of Auren Stormborn, age twenty-eight, presumed last survivor of the Vell flight* — and she'd held it over the candle flame and watched the wax curl brown, and she had not lit the corner.

She told herself it was strategy. A bounty paid on confirmation was worth more than a bounty paid on rumor; better to walk him out of the valley herself, deliver him breathing, collect double. She told herself that the way you tell yourself anything at two in the morning, over and over, until the words wear smooth as river stones and stop meaning anything at all.

The truth was simpler and uglier: she liked him. He was dry-witted and careful and he'd sat with her over bad wine and drawn maps in spilled ale on the table just to make her laugh, and when the birds went silent around him he never seemed to notice, which meant either innocence or ten years of very disciplined guilt.

Ahead, torchlight. Not Wren's — too many torches, moving in a spread pattern, the way hunters fan when they've cut sign and mean to drive their quarry toward ground of their own choosing.

Marguerite went still in the bracken and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since Cael Ford. Four riders, maybe five, between her and Wren. If they took him, her commission was fulfilled by other hands and her silence became complicity. If she warned him, she burned her report, her pay, her standing, her name.

If she warned him, she chose.

"Damn you," she murmured — to Wren, to the crown, to herself, she wasn't particular — and turned east, running parallel to the torchline through the wet dark, taking the shortcut only a cartographer would know: the sheep track below the hanging cliff, the ford where the river braided shallow, the rise above the old thorn hedge that marked the valley's border.

Where all magic died. Where he would be safe, if he reached it.

Where she would lose him, if he reached it.

*One thing at a time,* she told herself, and ran.

* * *

Wren heard them before he saw them — the particular wet crunch of hooves on bracken, deliberately loud, men who wanted to be heard. They'd driven him exactly where they wanted: the low meadow beneath the Thornline, the ancient hedge looming black against the stars, its branches dense as woven iron. Beyond it, the pass. Between him and it, four horses and five men, and the boy burning on his back.

"Auren Stormborn." The voice belonged to Captain Vess, the one with the gray beard and the voice like gravel in a sack. He dismounted slowly, savoring it. "Ten years we looked. And here you are, carrying our other prize on your own back. Saves us the trouble."

"He needs a physician," Wren said. His voice came out steadier than he deserved. "He's dying."

"He's evidence," Vess said. "Set him down. Set the letter down too. We know you have it."

Wren set nothing down. He measured the distance to the hedge — forty yards of open meadow, moonlit, hopeless — and felt the old familiar nothing where his gift should be. Ten years of damp valley air had smothered it to embers, and even those embers were behind him now, in the deep valley, while here at the border there was only dead grass and dead sky and five men with iron.

Iron. Of course they carried iron. Someone had done their research.

"You know what I find funny?" Vess strolled closer, boots squelching. "The stories say storm-callers could split the sky. And here's the last one, and he looks like a clerk."

"He is a clerk," said a new voice, from the darkness to the left, and Marguerite walked out of the bracken with her hands visible and her smile easy as Sunday. "Best in the valley. I've hired him myself. Captain — Marguerite Vale, cartographer, commissioned under seal. You'll want to see my papers."

Vess's eyes narrowed. "Vale. The queen's mapmaker."

"The same. I've been tracking him for the crown since spring." She produced a leather folio from her coat, and Wren watched her do it and understood, with a strange floating clarity, that everything had been exactly what it looked like. The wine. The maps in ale. The friendship. All of it commissioned.

Something in him closed like a door.

"There," Marguerite said pleasantly, handing over the folio. "You'll find my full report. Locations, dates, descriptions—"

Vess opened it. Looked down.

Looked down again, because the first page was not a report. It was a map — a beautiful, precise, entirely fictional map of the valley's northern caves, annotated in her neat hand — and while his eyes were on it, her knife came out of her sleeve and went into the throat of the man standing nearest Wren.

It happened fast then, the way terrible things do. The dying hunter dropped. A crossbow came up and Marguerite was already moving, using the falling man as a shield, shouting *run, you fool, RUN* — and Wren ran, the boy jolting on his back, thirty yards, twenty, while behind him steel rang and a horse screamed and Vess roared orders into chaos.

Ten yards. The hedge loomed. Wren hit the branches and they tore at him like living things, thorns raking his arms, his face, snagging the boy's coat — and then he was through, falling, rolling, coming up on his knees in the pass beyond with blood on his hands that wasn't all the boy's.

Marguerite burst through the hedge a moment later, coat shredded, cheek bleeding, alive. Behind her, in the meadow, one hunter lay very still and four others were regrouping around their captain, and none of them were following. Not yet. The Thornline was legend even to them.

"You—" Wren started.

"Don't." She held up a hand, breathing hard. "Whatever you're about to say — don't. I know what my papers said. I know what you saw." Her jaw worked. "I made a choice tonight. Ask me why later, if there is a later. Right now we move, because Vess won't stop, and the next time there won't be a hedge."

They climbed. And as they climbed, Wren felt it.

It began as warmth in his ruined hands, then pressure, then sound — a sound beneath sound, like the hum inside a seashell, like the held breath before lightning. With every step away from Thornvale the valley's long smothering weight lifted off him, layer by layer, decade by decade, and what lay underneath stirred.

His magic woke like a waking animal: stiff, starved, blinking, testing its limbs. He could feel the storm building over the western peaks, forty miles off, feel its charge the way you feel a word on the tip of your tongue. He could feel the iron in the hunters' scabbards behind them, taste the copper of coming rain. The boy's fever flickered against his senses like a guttering candle.

"Wren." Marguerite had stopped, was staring at him. "Your eyes."

"What about them?"

"They're—" She hesitated. "There's light in them. Like the sky before it breaks."

He raised a hand and, without deciding to, without wanting to, felt the wind answer — a small spiral of it, lifting the loose hair from his neck, tugging at the boy's collar, smelling of ozone and far-off rain.

Ten years of nothing. And now this: the storm in his blood, stretching after its long sleep, hungry and enormous and *his.*

"It knows me," he said quietly. It was the truest thing he'd said aloud in a decade.

Marguerite looked at him — at the blood and the thorn-scratches and the light in his eyes — and whatever she saw, she didn't run. She pulled her torn coat straight, nodded once toward the dark pass ahead, and said:

"Then let's hope it remembers whose side it's on. Because I counted six more riders at the ford, and they're between us and the enclave — and I have a feeling, Wren Winterbourne, that you're about to introduce me to exactly what the crown is so afraid of."
