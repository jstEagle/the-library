## 5. The Road of Small Mercies

The boy's name was Tam, and he slept the first two days as though he had been paid to do it.

Marguerite learned this from listening — she was good at listening, better at it than at most honest trades — and from watching Wren Winterbourne move through the small rituals of keeping a wounded child alive. He woke before dawn each morning to change the poultice on Tam's side, where a hunter's blade had opened him like a letter. He boiled water over a fire built low and smokeless, in the manner of someone who had spent ten years practicing invisibility. He fed the boy broth in spoonfuls no larger than a sparrow's sip, and when Tam whimpered in his fever-dreams, Wren murmured to him in a language Marguerite did not know — a language that sounded, absurdly, like weather. Like wind deciding which way to go.

She sat across the fire and told herself she was cataloguing all of it for her report.

*The fugitive exhibits protective behavior toward the courier. The fugitive speaks the old tongue. The fugitive is heading north-northeast, toward the Greymantle passes.*

That was what she told herself. She had been telling herself things for eleven days now, since the moment at the Thornline when her crossbow had swung — one heartbeat — from Wren's chest to the hunter's, and loosed. Since the bolt took Aldric Vane through the throat and everything Marguerite had been for six years went into the mud with him.

*You chose,* said a voice in her head that sounded unpleasantly like her mother. *Now live with the arithmetic.*

* * *

On the third day, Tam woke properly, and the first thing he did was try to run.

He got perhaps twenty feet — a feverish, staggering flight through the bracken, barefoot, arms pumping — before Wren caught him around the middle and went down with him into the wet ferns, holding on, saying nothing, just holding while the boy thrashed and sobbed and finally went still as a caught bird.

"They'll follow me," Tam gasped. "They made me memorize the road. They made me—"

"No one followed us past the river," Wren said. "I'd have felt it."

"Felt it." Tam laughed, high and cracked. "You're mad. You're all mad. You people think you can *feel* storms coming and that's why they burned my village, they said you'd feel it, they said—"

Marguerite watched Wren absorb this the way a stone wall absorbs rain: nothing showing, everything taken in. Then he gathered the boy up like something breakable and carried him back to the fire, and Marguerite looked away, because there are some kinds of tenderness that feel like trespassing.

She busied herself with her maps instead. That was her ritual, her rosary: unroll the oilcloth, check her bearings against the sun, mark their position with a pin of red wax. It was honest work. It was also, she reminded herself, the very instrument of her betrayal — every pin she placed was a breadcrumb for the crown, or would have been, if she ever sent another report.

She had not sent one since the Thornline. There had been a dead-drop at Millbrook; she had walked past it without slowing, feeling the little lead tube in her satchel like a coal she couldn't put down.

"You're doing it again," Wren said.

She looked up. He was watching her over Tam's sleeping head, and his eyes were the grey of sky before rain.

"Doing what?"

"Mapping us." A ghost of a smile. "Every few hours. Like you're afraid we'll wander off the edge of the world if you don't."

"Everything wanders off the edge of the world eventually," Marguerite said. "That's what maps are for. Holding the edges down."

"Hm," said Wren Winterbourne, who was not a fool, whatever else he was. And then, because he was apparently determined to be kind at her: "There's a stream a half-mile east. If you want real water instead of ditchwater. I'll watch him."

* * *

The stream ran cold and quick over brown stone, and Marguerite knelt beside it and let it numb her hands and tried to pray, which was not a thing she had done since she was nine years old, and got nowhere. What came instead was memory, unbidden:

Her father's map room in Corvassa, smelling of linseed and dust. Her father's hands, ink-stained to the second knuckle, teaching her that a coastline was a promise you made to strangers — *here is where the land ends; you may trust this.* Six years old, tracing the Thornvale border with one finger. *No one goes in there, little bird. The valley eats magic and keeps the rest.*

And later — much later — a crown clerk sliding a commission across a table scarred by candle-wax, and the number written on it, a number with enough zeroes to buy her mother's freedom from debtors' prison twice over, and Marguerite hearing herself say *yes* in a voice that belonged to someone hungrier and colder than she had ever meant to become.

She splashed water on her face. When she returned to camp, Wren was cooking something that smelled almost like food, and Tam was sitting up, propped against a log, watching Wren with the wary devotion of a stray that has been fed once.

"There's a story," Wren was saying, "about the night I was born."

"Oh, don't," Marguerite said, settling onto her bedroll. "Let me guess. Lightning struck the birthing house and the midwife's hair stood on end for a week."

Wren glanced at her, and something moved behind his eyes — surprise, maybe, or grief wearing surprise's coat. "Close," he said. "It struck the *oak* outside. Split it clean down the middle. My mother always said the tree died so I wouldn't have to." He turned the spit. "She had jokes like that. Terrible ones. She could make the whole enclave groan."

"The enclave," Tam said. His voice went thin on the word, hungry on it. "You've seen it? Is it true there's nine towers? Nine storms, they say, one for each tower, and the elders can call them down—"

"Tamsin." Wren's voice gentled. "Eat your soup."

But the boy pressed, the way children and spies both press, and so Wren talked — haltingly at first, then with a momentum that seemed to frighten him even as it carried him forward. He talked about a valley beyond the Greymantle passes where storm-callers had hidden for three hundred years. About terraces cut into the mountainside where they grew barley that rippled blue-grey before any weather came. About his mother, Sera, who could read a cloud the way Marguerite read a contour line, and his father, who could not call so much as a breeze but who sang the calling-songs anyway, badly, because someone had to keep them alive. About a sister, Lark, four years old, who had once summoned a thunderclap because her brother wouldn't share honey-bread.

"She got the bread," Wren said. "After that, everyone shared with Lark."

Tam laughed. Marguerite did not laugh, because her throat had closed.

She thought about the file in the crown archives. *Storm-caller insurrection, Year One of Aldous Rex. Sedition suppressed. Records sealed by royal decree.* She had read those words and understood them the way the crown wanted them understood: rebels, punished. A threat, ended. She had never once asked what kind of *sedition* required the deaths of an entire people, or why every record of it was sealed rather than celebrated. Victories get monuments. Only massacres get locks.

"And then?" Tam whispered. "What happened to them? Your family?"

Wren was quiet long enough that the fire popped twice.

"I left," he said finally. "Ten years ago. There were reasons. Good ones, I thought." He looked into the flames. "They're still there, Tam. All of them. That's why we're walking."

*All of them.* Three hundred souls, give or take, tucked into a fold of mountains like a letter in an envelope — and Marguerite knew exactly where the envelope was, because she had drawn the seal herself, months ago, in a report titled *Probable Location of Remnant Population, Greymantle Sector.* Somewhere in the capital, that report sat in a clerk's tray. Somewhere, a colonel had read it and begun counting soldiers.

She hid the knowledge deeper. That was the only phrase she had for it anymore. Each mile, she took the commission — the numbers, the dead-drop, the neat, murderous little report — and buried it further down inside herself, under the memory of Wren carrying Tam out of the ferns, under the sound of a man describing his sister's stolen thunderclap, under the terrible growing weight of being liked by people she was paid to destroy.

* * *

On the sixth night, rain came — real rain, the first since the Thornline — and they sheltered beneath a shelf of rock while it hammered the world silver.

Tam slept. Wren sat at the shelter's mouth, watching the storm with an expression Marguerite could not read, and after a while she found herself sitting beside him, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, which was closer than she had let herself be to anyone in six years.

"It doesn't answer you," she said. "Out here. Does it."

"No." He held out his hand into the rain, palm up, letting it strike. "It's like... shouting into a locked room. I know the words. Nothing comes back." A pause. "Except at the border. At the Thornline, when the hunters had us — I felt it wake up. Like an animal that's been asleep so long it forgot it was dangerous."

"Are you dangerous, Wren Winterbourne?"

He considered the question with more seriousness than she had intended, which was somehow worse.

"My grandmother could drown a harvest with a thought," he said. "My mother could make lightning walk. So yes. Probably. That's rather the point of me." He turned his head, and the rain-light made his face strange and young. "Why did you do it? At the border. Vane had his blade at my throat and you had a clear shot and you—" He stopped. Started again. "You killed a king's man for me. I've been waiting six days for the lie, and it hasn't come, so I'll ask plainly. Why?"

The rain filled the silence, patient as an interrogator.

Marguerite had prepared answers. She was a professional preparer of answers. *Because dead fugitives draw more hunters than live ones. Because I need you to reach the enclave. Because a guide is worth more breathing.* All of them true-adjacent. All of them ready.

What came out instead was: "Because you gave your soup to Tam on the second night and told him you weren't hungry, and I watched you lie about it, and I thought — there isn't anything in the world worth less than a person who'd hand that man to the hunters. And I didn't want to be worth less."

Wren stared at her.

"That's a terrible reason," he said at last.

"I know."

"It doesn't actually explain anything."

"I know that too."

And then he laughed — a short, startled, genuine laugh, the first she'd heard from him — and the sound went through her like a crack through ice, and Marguerite Vale, cartographer, spy, liar, looked at the man she had been hunting and understood with perfect clarity that she had already lost. Not to the crown. Not to the enclave. To a bowl of soup and a bad joke and the way he said *we're walking*, as though she were part of the *we*.

Somewhere far to the north, past the Greymantle passes, three hundred people were sleeping in nine towers, unaware that the woman warming her hands at their kinsman's fire had already sold the road to their door.

* * *

On the ninth morning, the land changed.

It happened between one ridge and the next: the pines thickened, darkened, bent inland as though combed by a permanent wind; the air acquired a taste — ozone and cold stone and something older underneath, something like the smell of a struck bell. Tam stopped dead on the trail and began, silently, to cry.

"We're close," he said. "This is the near-country. The elders ward it. I can feel it in my teeth."

Marguerite felt it too, though she would never have said so: a pressure, a *rightness*, as though the landscape itself were a sentence completing. And she saw Wren stop, and lift his hand, and stare at his own fingers as static crawled visibly along them, blue-white in the shadow of the pines, and heard him whisper — reverent, terrified —

"It remembers me."

Then he straightened, and looked back at her, and his face was the face of a man arriving at a door he had nailed shut from the inside ten years ago.

"Before we go any further," Wren said quietly, "you should tell me what you're really carrying, Marguerite. Because I've watched you hide it for nine days, and we are about to walk into the last home my people have, and I will not bring a knife to their throat."

The wind moved through the pines. Somewhere above, thunder rolled — the first thunder since the Thornline, and it did not sound like weather. It sounded like an answer.

Marguerite opened her mouth, and found that all her prepared answers had finally, mercifully, deserted her.
