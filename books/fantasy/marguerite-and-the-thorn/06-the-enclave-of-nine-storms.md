## 6. The Enclave of Nine Storms

The mountain did not want them to find it.

For three days they had climbed through pine and scree, following a river that shrank to a thread and then to a rumor, and on the fourth morning the boy — Tam, who had not spoken since the hunters' camp — stopped at a wall of granite and pressed his palm flat against it.

"Here," he whispered. It was the first word he'd given anyone in two days, and he spent it like coin from a dying man's purse.

Wren felt it before he understood it: a pressure behind his sternum, the old familiar ache of magic pressing against a door that had been shut for ten years. But this was different from Thornvale's dampening hush. This was not silence. This was *invitation*.

The granite rippled.

Not truly — stone does not ripple — but light moved across its face like wind over water, and where Tam's hand rested, the rock thinned to a grey veil, and beyond the veil Wren saw terraces cut into a hidden valley, nine white towers crowned with lightning-rods of black iron, and gardens where rain fell upward in slow silver threads.

"Nine Storms," Wren said. His voice cracked on the name. He had not said it aloud since he was fourteen years old, since the night his mother pushed him across the Thornline with a forged scribe's letter and told him never to look back. "It's real. I thought—" He had thought, in ten years of copying other people's words for coin, that he might have dreamed it. That grief had built him a palace out of memory and called it home.

Tam looked up at him with eyes too old for his twelve years. "You're the one the letter was for," he said. "They'll want to see you."

Marguerite stood a few paces off, arms crossed against the mountain cold, her map case slung across her back like a weapon she'd forgotten she was carrying. She had been quiet all morning. She had been quiet, Wren realized, since the border, since the moment her knife found a hunter's throat instead of his own — and he still did not know what to make of that silence, only that he had stopped watching his back when she walked behind him, and that this trust frightened him more than the hunters had.

"Is it what you expected?" she asked.

"No," Wren said. "It's better." And then, because lying to her had begun to feel like a habit he could no longer afford: "I don't know what it will be. Ten years is a long time to be dead."

He stepped through the veil.

* * *

The air inside tasted of ozone and rain and something green growing where green should not grow at this altitude. The valley opened around him like a held breath finally released: terraces of barley and storm-lilies, walkways of fitted slate, and everywhere the towers rising, each one humming faintly with a different note, so that walking between them was like walking inside a chord.

People came out to meet them. That was the first thing that undid him — how many there were. He had spent a decade believing himself perhaps the last, one stray ember the crown had failed to stamp out. But here were dozens. Hundreds. An old woman with cloud-white hair whose skin crawled with static when she laughed. A pair of twins no older than six who made the puddles leap. A broad-shouldered man carrying a yoke of water buckets who never touched them; the water carried itself, winding up the path in two obedient ribbons.

Storm-callers. Alive. Singing their small weathers into the world as if no king had ever sworn to burn them out of it.

Wren's knees nearly went. Marguerite's hand caught his elbow — quick, steadying, gone again before he could decide whether to be grateful.

An honor guard met them at the first terrace: four elders in robes the color of thunderheads, their sleeves embroidered with the sigil of the enclave, a circle pierced by nine lines. At their center walked a woman Wren knew would be Elder Serevan even before she spoke, because she had his mother's way of standing, spine straight as a mast, weather gathering behind her eyes.

She stopped three paces away. She looked at him a long time.

"Corvane Winterbourne," she said.

The name hit him like cold water. He had been Wren for ten years. Wren the scribe, Wren who kept his head down, Wren who flinched at thunder because thunder remembered him. Hearing his birth name spoken aloud in his mother's cadence — for Serevan had been her teacher, once — was like hearing a bell rung in a house he'd burned down.

"I go by Wren now," he managed.

"You will go by whatever name keeps you alive," Serevan said. "Though I notice it did not keep the boy alive." Her gaze slid past him to Tam, swaying on his feet, the bandages on his side seeping brown. "And it brought you a stranger wearing a crown's map-case."

Every face on the terrace turned toward Marguerite.

"She saved my life," Wren said quickly. "At the border. The hunters—"

"At the border," Serevan repeated, soft and terrible. "Yes. We know about the border, Corvane. We know about the hunters, and the proclamation, and the queen's little cartographer who has been mapping every valley, every pass, every cave from here to the coast for two years. Did you think the letter came late by accident? Did you think the crown found us by prayer?" She stepped closer, and the air around her crackled hard enough to raise the hair on Wren's arms. "We have watched her reports travel south for a year. We know exactly what she is."

* * *

They took Tam to the healers and took Marguerite to a cell carved into the mountain's root, and they took Wren to the Hall of Records, which was the cruelest choice of the three, though he did not understand why until the doors closed behind him.

The hall was vast and dim, lit by globes of captive lightning that drifted near the ceiling like slow jellyfish. Along its walls stretched shelves of slate tablets, vellum scrolls, brass cylinders — the enclave's memory, older than any kingdom. And in the center of the floor lay an open book the size of a door, its pages thick as hide, its ink the deep blue-black of a sky before hail.

Elder Serevan stood beside it. Two other elders flanked her: a gaunt man named Odran whose gift showed as frost on his beard, and a round, gentle-faced woman called Maren, who would not meet Wren's eyes.

"You asked why the crown hunts us now, after a hundred years of forgetting," Serevan said. "So we will teach you what your mother died keeping, and what we hoped you would never need."

She turned the great book's pages with hands that trembled slightly — whether from age or from what the pages held, Wren could not tell.

"This is the Annals of the Nine Storms. Kept since before the first king. Read it."

Wren read.

The early pages sang. Storm-callers as arbiters of harvest and voyage, taming the squalls off the western sea, calling rain to a kingdom's thirsting fields. There had been hundreds of them then, gathered in nine great houses — hence the name — honored, courted, married into noble blood. A drawing showed storm-callers standing in a ring around the first king at his coronation, their arms raised, weaving a canopy of clear sky above the ceremony. *A gift freely given,* the margin read, *for a king who swore friendship.*

Then Wren turned the page, and the singing stopped.

The handwriting changed. Grew cramped. Grew desperate. Entries no longer dated by season but by day, then by hour. *The king has declared our gifts an abomination.* *House Three burned with all within; the rains we sent to save the crops were named a curse upon them.* *They say we poisoned the old king's deathbed with a storm. They say it in every market square. It is not true. It was fever. It was only fever.*

There was a painting on the next leaf, done in furious strokes: riders in royal colors riding down fleeing figures beneath a black sky, and above them, painted over later in different ink, a single line:

*There was no ancient war. There was only a massacre, and then there was silence, and the silence was called peace.*

Wren's hand shook against the page. "This isn't in any chronicle. I've copied half the archives in the capital's reach. There's nothing—"

"Of course there's nothing." Odran's voice was dry as old frost. "The victors wrote the histories, and the victors needed a war. A war implies two sides. A war can be won honorably. So the massacre became 'the Storming War,' the murdered became 'rebels,' and the nine houses became a legend children are taught to fear. Every record outside these walls was burned or rewritten within a generation. Your scribe's guild holds copies of copies of lies, Corvane. You have spent ten years transcribing the erasure of your own people and calling it honest work."

The words landed harder than any blow the hunters had struck. Wren thought of the ledgers, the deeds, the royal proclamations he had copied in his neat, invisible hand. Thought of the proclamation that had started all of this — *all bearers of the gift must surrender for protection* — and heard, beneath its gentle phrasing, the same sentence written a hundred years ago in this book: *the rains we sent to save the crops were named a curse.*

"They're doing it again," he said quietly.

"They never stopped," Serevan said. "They merely paused, while the last of us hid well enough. Now the young queen wants what her ancestors wanted — a kingdom with no weather but her own — and she has sent her cartographers to draw the map that ends us." She closed the great book with a sound like a tomb sealing. "Which brings us to the stranger in our cells."

"Marguerite saved my life," Wren said. "I've told you. The hunter had his blade at my throat and she killed him. Whatever she was sent to do, she chose—"

"She chose to keep her asset alive," Odran said flatly. "A spy who delivers her prize breathing is worth more than one who delivers him dead. You mistake arithmetic for mercy, boy. We do not."

"You don't know her."

"We know her reports." Maren finally spoke, and her gentle voice was worse than the others'. "We intercepted one, three months past. A survey of the northern passes. Detailed. Professional. With a marginal note in her own hand: *unusual rainfall patterns in high valleys — possible anomaly — recommend further investigation.*" Maren's eyes glistened. "That note, child, is why the army came looking. That note is why Tam's escort died on the Thornline. Four of ours, dead, so that she could refine her maps."

The floor seemed to tilt. Wren thought of Marguerite by the fire on the road, laughing at his terrible stories, teaching Tam to fold paper birds. Thought of her hand catching his elbow on the terrace. Thought of her knife at the border, and the hunter falling, and the look on her face after — not triumph, not duty. Something rawer. He had believed it was remorse.

Hadn't he?

"The council votes at moonrise," Serevan said. "The law of the Nine Storms is old and plain: those who serve the crown's hunt against our people die by it. She will be given the mercy of a swift end, which is more than our dead received." She laid her hand briefly, almost tenderly, on Wren's shoulder — the first kind touch he'd known in this valley, and it chilled him more than the frost-elder ever could. "You may say farewell to her, if you wish. You owe her that much, perhaps. But hear me, Corvane Winterbourne." Her grip tightened. "Do not stand between this enclave and its law. Your mother broke her heart sending you away to keep you safe. Do not repay her by dying for a spy."

* * *

The cell was a slot of shadow in the mountain's root, lit by a single globe of dimmed lightning that buzzed like a trapped fly. Marguerite sat on the stone bench with her back straight and her wrists bound in storm-silk that sparked faintly whenever she moved — silk woven, Wren knew, to drink the strength of any gift she might carry. She carried none. The bindings were theater, and cruelty, and both knew it.

She looked up when he came in. Her face did the thing it always did — assembled itself into composure, wry and cool — but he had watched her for weeks now, and he saw the seam where the composure was stitched on.

"So," she said. "The famous hospitality of the hidden valley."

"They're going to kill you at moonrise."

Something flickered behind her eyes — fear, quickly filed away. "Efficient. I'd have guessed dawn. Executioners love the symbolism of dawn." She studied him. "And you? Are you here to plead their case, or yours?"

"I'm here because I don't know what's true." His voice came out rougher than he intended. "Maren says your maps guided the army. That your notes killed the escort. Is that true?"

Marguerite was silent for a long moment. Outside, far above, the nine towers hummed their chord, and somewhere a child was crying, and the sound of both filtered down through the stone like rain through a roof.

"Yes," she said at last. "Some of it's true. I mapped the passes. I noted the anomalies. I reported what I was paid to report." She lifted her bound hands, the storm-silk sparking. "But if you're asking whether I knew they'd send soldiers to kill children over a marginal note — ask me the question you actually came to ask, Wren. Ask me about the border. Because that's the one that's keeping you awake."

He didn't answer. The globe buzzed. Above them, faintly, the enclave began to toll a bell he had never heard before, low and slow, counting down the hours until moonrise.

"When the time comes," Marguerite said quietly, "will you stand with them? Or will you stand with what you know?"

"That depends," Wren said, "on what you tell me before the bell stops."

Her eyes held his, and for the first time since he'd known her, Marguerite the cartographer — who measured everything, who trusted nothing she hadn't drawn herself — looked afraid of what she was about to give away.
