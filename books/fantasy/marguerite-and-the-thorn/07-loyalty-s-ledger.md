## 7. Loyalty's Ledger

They kept her in a room that had once been a granary, judging by the iron hooks in the ceiling and the sweet ghost of old grain still clinging to the stone. Someone had left a blanket folded on the cot — grey wool, homespun, smelling of woodsmoke and lavender — and Marguerite could not decide whether it was mercy or theater. In her experience the two were often the same thing.

The door had no lock she could see. It didn't need one. The enclave sat in a bowl of mountains where the wind came down off the peaks like something with teeth, and outside her window — a window, at least, they'd given her a window — the air itself hummed with a pressure she felt in her back teeth. Nine storms, they called this place. She had spent three days counting them: nine separate weather systems circling the valley like hounds around a hearth, each one turning on an axis she couldn't see. No army was finding this place by accident. No map in the crown's possession marked so much as a goat track here.

Which meant, she thought, lying on the cot and watching clouds rotate overhead like slow thoughts, that everything now depended on what she did next. And on whether Wren Winterbourne — Wren Aldous, she knew his true name now, learned it from the elders' mouths like a knife being returned to its sheath — would stand beside her or let her fall.

She had betrayed him. She had known that since the border, since the moment she'd put a crossbow bolt through Captain Vess's throat instead of through Wren's heart. The commission was still sewn into the lining of her satchel, the queen's seal pressed into wax the color of dried blood: *Find the storm-caller. Follow him home. Mark the way.* She had done all three. Her reports lay in a dead-drop in a waystation two weeks east, written in her own careful hand, describing a valley she hadn't yet seen and a man she'd described, even then, more tenderly than a spy should describe her mark.

The elders came for her on the fourth day.

* * *

There were seven of them, which struck her as deliberately short of nine, and they arranged themselves in the granary's doorway like a verdict. The eldest was a woman named Serevan, tall and dry as winter grass, with white braids wound through with copper wire that caught the light when she moved her head. Her eyes were the pale grey of sky before hail.

"You've been comfortable," Serevan said.

"I've been patient," Marguerite said. "There's a difference."

"Is there." The elder crossed the room without hurry and stood over the cot. Up close she smelled of ozone and dried herbs, and Marguerite understood suddenly that this woman could kill her with a thought and a gesture, and that everyone in this valley knew it, and that this was simply how order was kept here. "You carry the crown's commission. We took your satchel while you slept. You needn't confess; we already know."

"Then you know I'm worth more to you talking than dead."

Something flickered across Serevan's face — not amusement, exactly, but a recognition of kindred arithmetic. "Speak, then."

Marguerite sat up. Her hands wanted to shake and she forbade them. "You have my maps. You've seen them. Every survey I've made in six years for the crown — every road, every garrison, every waystation between here and the capital. And you have me. I've read the queen's hunt orders. I know the rotation of the witch-hunter companies, their supply lines, their paymasters, the names of the officers who take bribes and the ones who don't." She held the elder's gaze. "Kill me, and you keep your secrets and lose mine. Keep me alive, and you gain the only complete picture of the crown's war machine that exists outside the palace."

"And why," Serevan said softly, "would you give us that? Your loyalty is purchased. We've read the price."

"My loyalty," Marguerite said, "was never theirs. It was rented. There's a difference there too." She heard how it sounded — a spy's sophistry — and pressed on before the elder could name it. "I killed a crown officer to save your storm-caller. Whatever I was when I walked into Thornvale, I'm not that person anymore, and you can verify every word against what I show you. That's the bargain. Not trust. Verification."

Serevan was quiet a long moment. Outside, one of the nine storms turned, and the light in the room went silver and strange.

"The council will consider it," she said at last. "But there is a matter that comes first. A hearing. You'll speak, and others will speak, and then we will decide what you are." She turned toward the door. "Your life may depend on what a boy who isn't a boy anymore chooses to say about you. I suggest you hope he remembers it clearly."

* * *

The hearing was held in the evening, in a hall built of black timber that seemed to drink the lamplight. Marguerite stood on a floor worn smooth by generations of feet, bound at the wrists with cord that hummed faintly — warded, then — and faced not seven elders but the whole gathered enclave, two hundred souls at least, farmers and smiths and children balanced on their parents' hips, all of them wearing the same watchful stillness. Storm-callers. The last of them. She was looking at the survivors of a genocide that officially had never happened, and every face in that hall was asking her the same question: *are you the next knife?*

Wren stood apart, near the elders' bench, dressed in enclave wool instead of his scribe's clothes, and he would not look at her. That frightened her more than the cords did.

Serevan opened the proceedings. "Marguerite Vale, of the capital, cartographer, agent of the crown. The charge is espionage. The evidence is her own hand." A clerk read passages from her commission aloud, and Marguerite listened to her own treachery recited in a flat ceremonial voice, and found it sounded smaller than it had felt. *Find the storm-caller. Follow him home. Mark the way.*

"Before judgment," Serevan said, "the accused may speak."

Marguerite spoke. She laid it out plainly: the commission, the bounty, the years of hunting men like Wren for coin. She did not soften it. Then she told them about the border — Vess's crossbow leveled at Wren's back, the half-second in which she had chosen, the bolt, the blood, the crossing. "I'm not asking you to believe I became good," she finished. "I'm asking you to believe the evidence. I gave up the only future the crown ever promised me. What I know can save you. What I am, you'll have to judge."

Silence. Somewhere in the rafters, wind worried at the shingles.

Then Serevan said: "Wren Aldous. You were raised among us. You will speak of the accused."

And finally he looked at her.

He looked tired, Marguerite thought. Tired in a way that ten years of hiding had never managed, because hiding requires no decisions. He walked forward slowly, and the crowd parted for him the way water parts around a stone, and when he reached the space beside her he stood close enough that she could smell ink and rain on him, the same as always.

"I knew what she was," Wren said, "before she confessed it. In Thornvale I watched her watch me. I saw the patience in her, the hunter's patience, and I told myself I was imagining it because I wanted a friend." A murmur ran through the hall. He lifted his chin. "So understand me: I am not deceived. I am choosing."

"Wren—" Marguerite started.

"Let him finish," Serevan said.

"She saved my life at the cost of hers," Wren said. "Not her comfort. Her *life*. The moment she loosed that bolt, she became a traitor to the crown, and she knew it, and she did it anyway. Everything after — the road, the boy, the miles — she carried our wounded on her own back through the pass when I couldn't. She starved herself feeding Tam. She mapped us a route out of a flood country from memory because she'd rather exhaust herself than ask for help." His voice roughened. "You taught me that a person is what they do when the ledger is closed and no one is keeping score. I've been keeping score. Here it is."

He turned to the elders. "You want truth? Here is all of it. She came to betray me. She stayed to save me. Both things are real, and I will not pretend one away to make the other simpler. If you kill her, you kill the woman who chose us over her queen — and you teach everyone watching that no one who leaves the crown's service can ever come home. Is that the enclave we bled to build?"

The silence afterward had weight, like the pause between lightning and thunder.

Serevan studied Wren for a long time. Then Marguerite. Then the hall.

"The council weighs testimony against evidence," she said at last. "The evidence condemns. The testimony... complicates." She raised a hand before anyone could speak. "She will be held. Not executed. Not freed. Held, under ward, while we consider what use to make of her maps and her knowledge — and of her. This is not pardon. It is postponement. Do not mistake it for trust."

"It's more than I asked for," Marguerite said quietly.

"It's less than you deserve," Serevan replied, "in both directions."

* * *

They untied her wrists and led her back toward the granary through the blue dark, and the nine storms turned overhead, and the whole valley smelled of rain that hadn't fallen yet. At the door, Wren caught her arm.

"You didn't have to do that," she said.

"Yes, I did." He looked older than twenty-eight, older than the valley, older than the wars. "Loyalty used to mean obeying the people who raised me. Tonight it meant telling the truth in front of them. I'm still learning the difference." He hesitated. "Tam's fever broke this morning. He asked for you."

Something in her chest cracked along a seam she hadn't known was there.

"Marguerite." His voice dropped. "My report to the elders — the one I gave when we arrived — I told them where I crossed the border, what roads I used, how I slipped the hunters for ten years. Standard debriefing. But Serevan kept asking about the waystations. The dead-drops. Where reports go, how fast the relays run." He met her eyes, and she saw it land in him at the same moment it landed in her. "They're not deciding whether to trust you. They're calculating how long we have. My letters — the ones I sent over the years, the ones that led the hunters to the boy—"

The wind rose sharply against the walls, one of the nine storms tightening its circle, and far to the east, past the mountains, past the passes she alone had mapped, Marguerite thought of supply lines and relay schedules and a young queen's patience, and understood that postponement was all any of them had ever been granted.

"They're coming," she said. "Aren't they."

Wren didn't answer. Above the hall, the first of the nine storms began, very quietly, to rain.
