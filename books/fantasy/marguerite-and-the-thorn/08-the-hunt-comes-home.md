## 8. The Hunt Comes Home

The birds told us first.

I had forgotten, in the years of quiet ink and quiet rain, that I could hear them — not their songs but their silences, the way a valley holds its breath when something wrong moves through it. On the morning the army came, every bird in the Enclave of Nine Storms went still at once, and the silence fell over the terraces like a shawl drawn up around the shoulders of the world.

I was at the well with Tam when it happened. He was stronger now, three weeks past the border, his wound closed to a pink seam he showed off to anyone who would look. He had taken to calling me *brother* with the careless ease of someone who had decided it was true, and I had stopped correcting him. He was hauling water, arguing cheerfully about whether storm-callers could teach a crow to speak, when the crows themselves rose off the eastern pines in one black sheet and did not come down.

Tam looked at me. I looked at the sky.

"Wren," he said, and his voice had gone small in a way I hated, "what's that sound?"

I heard it then beneath the silence: a low iron hum, distant and rhythmic. Boots. Not a patrol — an army does not walk softly, but from far enough away even an army is only weather.

By noon we could see them from the watchtower: banners crawling up the far ridge like slow red lichen, the sun flashing off helms in scattered, greedy sparks. Three hundred, perhaps more. The elders had sent runners to the passes at dawn; two came back. One did not.

They had found us. Not by accident, not by the old roads that shifted like riverbeds and swallowed strangers whole. They had found us because Marguerite's reports — written months ago, in another life, in her careful cartographer's hand — had charted the passes, timed the patrols, marked the water sources. Her maps had led them here as surely as a compass needle finds north. She stood beside me on the tower then, her face white as vellum, and I watched her understand it too: that her old work had outlived her change of heart, that a thing written can keep killing long after the hand that wrote it has learned better.

"I didn't—" she began.

"I know," I said. And I did know. But knowing is a thin coat against winter.

The elders made their dispositions with the calm of people who had rehearsed this for nine generations. The children into the deep caves. The archives sealed. The wards along the southern approach — old things, woven of stone and song and the standing stones' patient memory — would slow the soldiers but not stop them. The enclave had never been a fortress. It had only ever been a hiding place, and hiding places fail.

"We will not win this," Elder Sef said quietly, standing at my shoulder. "We may survive it. Those are different things. Remember which one you are fighting for."

I remembered. I thought I did.

* * *

They came at dusk, when the light goes long and gold and makes everything it touches look already mourned.

The wards held them for an hour. I felt each one break — not heard, *felt*, a deep subsonic grief in my chest, like ribs cracking one at a time inside my own body. The standing stones sang as they died, a high keening note that rose and rose and cut off, and after each silence the army came on through the smoke of burned ward-stones, and the gap between us and them grew smaller, and smaller.

Marguerite fought at the barricades with a surveyor's hatchet and no fear at all, which frightened me more than anything else I saw that day. The elders called the wind and the fog. Our few trained callers pulled rain out of a clear sky to slick the slopes. It bought minutes. Minutes are not mercy; they are only the shape mercy leaves behind.

And then the line broke at the eastern terrace, and they were among us.

I don't remember deciding. That is the truth I have carried since, and the truth I will carry always: there was no decision. There was a soldier raising a crossbow toward Tam — Tam, who was running water to the wounded, who had argued about talking crows not six hours before — and there was a sound in me older than language, older than the first king, older than the erased war itself.

The sky answered.

It had waited ten years behind the dampening veil of Thornvale, ten years folded small as a seed, and it opened in me the way a flood opens a door. I did not call the storm. I *was* the storm, briefly, wholly — lightning walking down out of a cloudless evening sky in branching white veins, thunder cracking so hard and close that men fell clutching their ears, rain coming sideways in sheets that stung like flung gravel. The air turned to copper and pressure. My hair lifted off my skull. Somewhere very far away I heard myself screaming, or maybe that was the wind; there was no longer any difference.

I threw them back. I broke their formation like a child breaks bread. Lightning took the banner-pole and ran down it and left forty men twitching on the wet grass, alive, dropped like puppets with cut strings. I felt every heartbeat of it. That is the part no one tells you about power: you feel all of it, all at once, every life you touch, a great ringing chord of terror and pain and pulse, and there is no way to be a storm gently.

For one impossible minute, the army retreated. They fell back down the slope in disorder, and our people cheered, and I stood in the center of a circle of scorched grass with the rain streaming off me, shaking, more afraid of myself than I had ever been of the witch-hunters.

Then I saw Tam.

He was twenty feet away, lying on his back in the ruined barley, and he was looking at the sky with an expression of mild surprise, the way he'd looked at everything, as if the world were a map he was still learning to read. There was a bolt in his chest. A crossbow bolt, ordinary and stupid, loosed in the chaos by some soldier whose name I would never know, aimed — I understood it a heartbeat later, the way you understand a blow after it lands — at my back. At me, mid-storm, blind with lightning, deaf with thunder.

Tam had seen it. Tam had run toward it.

I knelt in the mud and the barley and the rain I had made, and I gathered him up, and he was light, he was so light, lighter than the boy who had crossed the Thornline half-dead in my arms, as if he had already begun leaving. His blood ran pink in the rainwater, diluting, going everywhere, going nowhere.

"Brother," he said. Just that. His hand found mine and gripped it once, hard, the way you grip a rope.

Then the grip eased, and the rain kept falling, because I had not learned yet how to make it stop.

* * *

They say the second wave broke against us at moonrise and the third at dawn, and that we held. What I remember is smaller and worse. I remember Marguerite finding me at the eastern wall, hours later, and sitting down beside me in the mud without a word, because there were no words and she knew it, and her shoulder against mine was the only sentence either of us could bear. I remember Elder Sef standing over Tam where they had laid him with the other dead, nineteen of them, nineteen names that would need to be spoken aloud tomorrow, and saying, in a voice scraped hollow: "You saved the enclave today."

And I remember what I said back, because it came out of me flat and cold and clear, like water over stone:

"No. I killed a hundred men and lost a boy, and tomorrow they will come again with fire this time, and the day after with more, and we cannot hold. Hiding failed. Fighting fails. There is one thing left that has never been tried."

Sef looked at me. Around us the enclave smoked and steamed in the gray dawn, the terraces torn, the wards dead, the caves full of frightened children. Nine generations of hiding, ending in ash and mud.

"What thing?" she asked.

"The truth," I said. "All of it. In the capital, before the queen. The massacre. The erased war. Everything the first king buried and every king since has kept buried. I will give them the enclave's location myself — I will hand them the secret I have spent ten years dying slowly to protect — and I will bet them everything that a truth spoken aloud can do what nine generations of silence could not."

"You would gamble every life here on words," Sef said.

"I would gamble them on nothing else," I said. "Words are all that's left."

Behind me, I heard Marguerite stand. When I turned, she had her pack over her shoulder and her hatchet at her belt, and her jaw was set in the particular way it set when she had already finished arguing with herself and won.

"The capital is eleven days' ride," she said. "If we leave within the hour, we'll reach it before the army's report does." She looked east, where the road unspooled toward everything we feared, and then at me, and her voice gentled by a degree I alone would ever have noticed. "Say goodbye properly, Wren. Then come on. We're going to tell the queen exactly what her crown did."

The rain had stopped. The sky over the broken enclave was pale and washed and terribly ordinary, and somewhere beyond the ridge, the birds were beginning, cautiously, to sing again — as if even they understood that the time for silence was over.
