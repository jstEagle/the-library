## 9. The Secret Given Away

### Wren

The throne room of Caer Aldis smelled of beeswax and old stone, and beneath both, faintly, of fear.

Wren felt it the way he had once felt storms — as pressure behind the eyes, a taste like copper on the tongue. Ten years in Thornvale had taught him to read rooms instead of skies, and this room was reading him back. Two hundred courtiers packed along the walls like swallows on a wire. A dozen witch-hunters in their grey lacquered armor, standing too still. And at the far end, beneath a banner so faded its storm-caller sigil had worn to a ghost of itself, sat the young queen.

She was smaller than her coinage. Nineteen, perhaps twenty, with her father's heavy jaw and none of his certainty. Her crown sat on her head the way a bird sits on a branch it does not trust. Wren knew that look. He had worn it for ten years.

"Kneel," said the herald.

Wren knelt. The marble was cold through his trousers. Beside him, Marguerite knelt too, her spine straight as a drawn line, her hands — those ink-stained, steady hands — folded as if she were merely waiting for a table to be cleared. Only Wren could see the pulse jumping at her throat.

Behind them, under guard, the elders of the Enclave of Nine Storms stood in a row, their grey robes travel-worn, their faces carved from old weather. Elder Sable's eyes found Wren's, and in them was neither forgiveness nor blame. Only the question she had asked him on the road: *What are you willing to lose?*

He had not answered then. He would answer now, in front of all of them.

"Rise," said the queen.

Wren rose. His knees ached from three days in a prison wagon. His magic pressed against the inside of his skin like water against a dam — they had bound his wrists with cold-iron cuffs, but iron only muffled; it did not silence. Somewhere above the palace roof, clouds were gathering without permission. He could feel them leaning toward him like dogs hearing their name.

"You are Wren Winterbourne," the queen said. "Also called Corren Ashfall. Also called, by warrant of my father's hand, *the last storm-caller.*"

"I am not the last," Wren said.

The words dropped into the hall like a stone into a well. He heard the ripple go out — the small sounds two hundred people make when they try not to gasp.

The queen leaned forward. "Explain."

"Your Majesty." He kept his voice level. It wanted to shake. Not with fear — with the effort of holding back everything he had carried since the night the half-dead boy pressed a sealed letter into his hands and the past came howling after him across the thornline. "Before I explain anything, I need to ask you to do something no king of your house has done in four hundred years."

"And what is that?"

"Listen to the whole truth before you decide what to do with me."

A murmur. One of the witch-hunters shifted, armor whispering. Marguerite did not look at Wren. They had agreed on that, in the dark of the cell last night, foreheads nearly touching, her voice a thread: *Don't watch me. Watch them. I'll watch you.*

He watched them now.

"The histories say there was an ancient war," Wren said. "A war between the first king, Aldis the Unifier, and the storm-callers who refused to bend the knee. A war fought long ago, by people long dead, over grievances no one remembers. Your schoolbooks teach it in a single chapter. Your priests sing about it once a year."

"Yes," the queen said slowly.

"There was no war."

* * *

### Marguerite

Marguerite had rehearsed this moment a hundred times in her head, and in every version her hands shook. In the hall itself, they did not. She supposed that was what betrayal taught you — or what love did. She was no longer certain which of them had trained her hands.

She stepped forward when Wren nodded, unrolled the first map on the marble floor, and weighted its corners with the smooth river stones she'd carried from Thornvale because she could not bear to leave the valley entirely behind.

"Your Majesty. I am Marguerite Voss, cartographer, formerly in the employ of your father's spymaster. This is my commission seal." She held it up. Let them see it. There was no point hiding anymore; hiding was the disease this whole kingdom was dying of. "Three years ago I was paid to find a man. I found him. What I found along the way is on these maps."

She had redrawn them herself, in the enclave, by candlelight, while elders watched her with murder in their eyes and Wren watched her with something worse — hope. Every mountain range, every road, every mass grave.

"This is the Vale of Serel." Her finger traced the valley where the first battle was said to have been fought. "The histories record nine hundred dead here. Soldiers. Casualties of war." She looked up, met the young queen's eyes, and did not flinch. "They were not soldiers. They were farmers, weavers, children. Storm-callers who had already surrendered. We know because the graves are shallow, and unmarked, and full of people buried with their hands bound. My survey teams mapped eleven such sites across the old kingdoms. Eleven. The 'war' lasted one season. You do not fight a war in one season. You conduct a massacre in one."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses down on the lungs.

Somewhere among the courtiers a woman began quietly to cry.

"The records were burned," Marguerite continued. "The singers were silenced — you can find the arrest warrants in your own archive, sealed with the first king's mark. Even the word *storm-caller* was struck from the census rolls within a generation. Your Majesty, your crown has spent four hundred years pretending nine thousand people never existed. And it has spent the last ten years hunting the descendants of the survivors — the ones who fled north into the mountains and built an enclave out of fog and forgetting — because your father could not tolerate the idea that the lie had living witnesses."

"That is treason," said the Lord Marshal, from the steps below the throne. "Spoken plainly, in the royal presence."

"It is testimony," Marguerite said, "and I will swear it on any altar, under any compulsion, before any truth-reader your priests can produce. Bring one. I am asking for it."

That startled them. She saw it move through the hall like wind through wheat. Everyone expected the spy to hedge. She had hedged for three years. She was done.

* * *

### Wren

The queen raised a hand before the Marshal could speak again. "And the enclave," she said. "You claim it exists. Nine hundred of my father's warrants say otherwise — he believed the bloodline ended at the border of Thornvale."

"Your father believed what he needed to believe," Wren said. "So did I, for ten years. I hid in a valley where magic goes to die, and I told myself I was safe, and I told myself I was the last, and both of those things were lies I needed." He drew a breath. Here it was. The cliff edge. "I know where they are, Your Majesty. Every one of them. Four hundred souls in a valley called the Hollow of Nine Storms, three days north of the Thornline, warded by workings older than your dynasty. I was born there. I left to draw the hunters away from them, and I have been running ever since."

"Wren—" Elder Sable's voice cracked like ice on a pond.

"No." He turned to face her, and his heart broke cleanly, the way a bone breaks — one sharp sound, then numbness. "Forgive me, Elder. But hiding is finished. The hunters found the boy. They found the letter. They will find the valley, whether I speak its name today or tomorrow or never. The only question left is whether they find it as enemies or as guests." He turned back to the queen. "I am giving you the location, Majesty. Freely. On the condition — the *only* condition — that you hear the rest before your soldiers march."

The hall erupted. The Marshal was shouting about strategy, a bishop about heresy, someone about the bounty. Wren stood in the middle of it and felt, strangely, light. Ten years of carrying the secret, and it weighed nothing now that he had set it down. That was the thing no one told you about truth: it did not free you. It just made room for whatever came next.

The queen rose.

She did not raise her voice. She simply stood, and the hall fell silent around her the way water parts around a keel.

"Bring the truth-reader," she said. "Bring the archives. Bring the grave-survey maps. And bring me my father's private correspondence." Her jaw tightened — that heavy inherited jaw, suddenly looking less like her father's and more like her own. "If there are children's bones in the Vale of Serel with their hands bound, then this throne has been sitting on a massacre for four centuries, and I will know it today. Before any soldier moves. Before any pardon is given. Before anyone kneels or hangs."

Her eyes found Wren's. They were grey, storm-grey, and he thought — absurdly, terribly — that she might have been born in the wrong family entirely.

"And if it is true," she said, "what do you want, storm-caller?"

Wren looked at Marguerite. At the elders. At the faded banner with its worn-away sigil, the ghost of a people everyone had agreed to forget.

"Not mercy," he said. "Memory. Remember us honestly. That is all my people have ever asked of any king. It is the only thing we have never been given."

The queen held his gaze a long moment. Then she turned to the Lord Marshal, and her voice, when it came, was quiet enough that the whole hall had to lean in to catch it — and lean in they did, two hundred strong.

"Marshal. Recall the hunt. Every warrant, every rider. Now."

The Marshal went white. "Majesty, the army is already three days—"

"Then they will be three days late," said the queen, "and I will be writing the order myself. Send a falcon tonight." She looked back at Wren, and something passed between them that was not trust, not yet — but perhaps its raw material. "And Master Winterbourne. If your truth is false, no pardon in the world will save you."

"It isn't false," Wren said. "But Majesty — the falcon must fly tonight. Because the commander leading that army answers to your father's spymaster. And he was given orders that no falcon can countermand."

Marguerite went rigid beside him. "Whose orders?"

Wren closed his eyes. He had hoped to keep this piece until morning. But honesty, once begun, was a floodgate.

"The orders were to burn the Hollow of Nine Storms," he said, "and leave no witnesses. And the man carrying them — the man who tortured the boy who brought me the letter — is already camped at the Thornline."

The queen's face went pale as parchment. Outside the high windows, the clouds Wren had been feeling all day finally broke, and rain began to fall on Caer Aldis — ordinary rain, honest rain, drumming on the stones like a thousand small hands demanding to be let in.

"Then we ride at dawn," the queen said.

And in the silence that followed, Marguerite reached over — guards be damned, protocol be damned — and took Wren's cuffed hand in hers.

"Hold on," she whispered. "Both of us. Whatever comes."

Above them, the storm gathered, and waited, and obeyed no one.
