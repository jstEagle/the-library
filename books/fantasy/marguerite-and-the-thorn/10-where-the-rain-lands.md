## 10. Where the Rain Lands

The binding was done in the throne room at dawn, because the queen believed — or her advisors did — that magic surrendered best in the hour when it was weakest.

Wren knelt on cold marble while a circle of nine mages stood around him, their robes the grey of storm-light, their faces carefully blank. He had expected to hate them. He had expected to hate all of it: the ceremony, the silver circlet they set upon his brow, the words in Old Tongue that fell like stones into still water. But what he felt, mostly, was tiredness — a deep, clean tiredness, like the exhaustion after a fever breaks.

*By crown and covenant, by rain and by right,* the eldest mage intoned, *we take back what the sky gave.*

It didn't hurt, exactly. It was more like being unmade gently, thread by thread. He felt the storm that had lived in him since birth — the pressure behind his eyes, the static in his fingertips, the low perpetual hum he had carried so long he'd forgotten it wasn't part of every man's body — and he felt it loosen. Not ripped away. *Loosened*, the way a hand opens after years clenched.

For one terrible moment, the last of it rose up in him and he nearly called it back. Ten years of hiding, two months of blood and road and grief, and now this — the price of peace, written plainly into the treaty the queen's scribes had drafted through three sleepless nights. The enclave would live. Its people would be named citizens, not witches; its location would enter the royal maps under seal, protected rather than hunted. And the last storm-caller born outside its walls would kneel in a marble room and let his gift be folded away like a letter no one would ever read again.

He thought of Tam. The boy who had crossed the Thornline half-dead with a sealed letter clutched in his fist, who had laughed at Wren's terrible cooking on the road, who had died with his arms spread wide against the soldiers' charge, shielding a man he'd known for barely a season. Tam, who had been eleven when the first king's massacre was still called a war in the histories, and fourteen when he learned what it really was.

*Let it mean something,* Wren told the storm inside him. *Let it buy them a hundred years of ordinary mornings.*

And then he let go.

The silence afterward was enormous. Not the dampened hush of Thornvale, which had always felt to him like a hand pressed over his mouth — but a true quiet, the quiet of a house after everyone has gone to bed. The mages lowered their hands. The circlet was lifted from his brow, and where it had rested, the skin was cool.

"Can you feel it?" the eldest mage asked quietly.

Wren flexed his fingers. Outside the high windows, clouds were gathering over the capital — grey, heavy, promising rain. He could see them. He could smell the coming water on the air like any farmer, any fisherman, any ordinary man watching the sky and hoping his roof held.

"No," he said, and found that it was true, and found, to his surprise, that truth sat easily in his mouth. "I can't feel anything."

The mage studied him a long moment. "That is either a mercy or a wound," she said. "Time will tell you which."

* * *

Marguerite was waiting in the palace courtyard, sitting on the edge of the fountain with her boots off and her feet in the water, as though she had grown up doing exactly that in palaces instead of inns and survey camps. Her maps were gone — surrendered, copied, archived under the queen's seal. Her commission was burned. She had watched the paper curl herself, and she had smiled while it happened, that sharp crooked smile Wren had learned to read better than any coastline.

"Well?" she said, looking up. "How does it feel to be an ordinary man?"

"Wet," Wren said, because it had begun to rain.

She laughed — a real laugh, unguarded, the kind he'd only heard from her twice before, both times on the road, both times when she'd forgotten to be anyone but herself. She pulled her feet from the fountain and stood, wringing out her hem without much conviction.

"I spoke with the queen this morning," she said, and her voice changed, became careful in the way he hated. "About Thornvale. About the valley's charter. It's old law, older than the witch-hunts — land granted in perpetuity to those who settle it, answerable to no garrison. I asked whether the pardon extended there." She paused. "It does. You could go home, Wren. A real home. No more hiding, no more false names. Scribes are always wanted in a capital."

"And you?"

"I've been offered a position. Royal Cartographer. First woman to hold it, if you care about such things." She said it lightly, but her eyes were fixed somewhere past his shoulder, on the rain falling silver through the courtyard light. "Every map in the kingdom redrawn under my hand. Every border mine to draw."

Wren looked at her for a long time. He thought about the first weeks on the road, when he had known — had *known* — that she was hunting him, and had walked beside her anyway because loneliness is a hunger and hunger doesn't check its food for poison. He thought about the night at the border, her knife finding a hunter's throat instead of his. He thought about her face when Tam died, and how she had not looked away, and how she had dug the grave herself and refused help, and what that refusal had meant.

"You're going to say yes," he said.

"I'm going to say yes," she agreed. "I'd be a fool not to."

"All right," Wren said. "Then I'll be a fool too."

Marguerite turned sharply. "What?"

"The position. Take it. Draw your borders." He smiled, and it felt strange on his face, this new face that had nothing to hide behind. "But I'm going home to Thornvale. To the real Thornvale, not the hiding place. Someone should be there who remembers why the valley exists. Someone should keep the records straight this time — write down what actually happened, so that in another hundred years nobody gets to call a massacre a war." He shrugged. "I'm a scribe. It's the only thing I've ever been any good at."

"That's not true," Marguerite said. "You're good at cooking badly. You're good at making birds go silent. You were—" She stopped. Her jaw tightened. "You were extraordinary, once."

"I was terrified, once," Wren said. "There's a difference. I spent ten years mistaking one for the other." He reached out and took her ink-stained hand — her hands were as stained as his had ever been, and always would be; some marks don't bind themselves away. "Come with me. Not forever, if you don't want. Come map the valley properly. It's never been done. No one's ever bothered, because no one thought it mattered." His voice caught, just slightly. "I think it matters. I think everything that was forgotten matters, or we end up burying more boys beside roads."

Marguerite looked down at their joined hands. The rain came down harder, drumming on the fountain, running in bright threads down the palace walls. Somewhere beyond the walls, the city was waking — carts and voices and the smell of bread, the whole ordinary machinery of a kingdom that had very nearly murdered a people out of existence and would now, if it kept its word, simply let them live.

"If I come," she said slowly, "it's not exile. I want that understood. Exile implies someone banished me."

"Understood," Wren said gravely. "You are choosing, freely, to move to a damp valley full of caves and bad wine."

"The wine can't be worse than the capital's."

"It's worse."

"Then I'll bring my own." She squeezed his hand once, hard, and let go, and turned to gather her boots, and that was that — no ceremony, no vows, just two people deciding, in a rainy courtyard, where they would stand.

* * *

They left the capital three days later, on foot, because Marguerite insisted that any landscape measured from horseback was a landscape lied about.

The road north took six weeks. They walked through country Wren had crossed in terror a season ago and found it transformed by ordinariness — wheat fields, way-stations, children running alongside them shouting questions about the storm-caller of the songs, because already the balladeers had gotten hold of the story and were mangling it beautifully. In one village an old woman pressed honey cakes into their hands and said, "My grandmother talked about the burning years. Nobody believed her. Thank you for making them believe her."

Wren carried the cakes a long way before he could eat them.

They passed the place where Tam was buried. They had raised a cairn there in autumn, on the shoulder of a hill facing east toward the enclave, and in the winter rains it had settled and softened, moss already claiming the stones. Marguerite added a flat rock she'd carried from the capital, absurdly, in the bottom of her pack. Wren said the names of Tam's family, which Tam had given him once, shyly, on the road — names Wren had promised to remember even if no one else did. Then they walked on.

The Thornline announced itself the way it always had: a change in the air, a thinning, a sense of stepping through a curtain. Wren braced himself out of habit — ten years of crossing that border had taught his body to expect the muffling, the closing of doors.

Nothing happened.

Of course nothing happened. There was nothing left in him to close doors against. He stood on the line itself, one foot in each world, and laughed until Marguerite looked at him with genuine alarm.

"It's just weather," he managed. "On both sides. It's just — weather."

* * *

Thornvale in spring was green almost past bearing. The valley floor had flooded in the thaw and the scribe's cottage where Wren had lived his small gray life stood ankle-deep in memory — they pumped it out, swept it, opened every window, and Marguerite pronounced it "structurally sound and aesthetically criminal," which was, he had to admit, accurate.

She redrew the valley through April and May. He watched her work sometimes, in the evenings, her maps pinned across every wall, her tongue between her teeth, and he wrote beside her — the true history, the one that began not with the ancient war but with the first king's fear, and the fires, and the nine families who fled into the mountains and built the Enclave of Nine Storms and taught their children to hide. He wrote Tam's name in it. He wrote the hunters' names too, because a record that only remembers its own dead is not a record; it is a grudge. He tried to write it the way rain falls — on everyone, without preference, without mercy, without cruelty either.

The elders of the enclave sent word in summer: the first traders had come down from the mountains openly, under the queen's seal, selling storm-glass and mountain wool in the market towns. Children were being taught the old songs again, the ones about calling thunder, though no child born now would ever call anything. The gift was dying out anyway, the elder's letter said — perhaps it always had been, perhaps the binding was only hastening what time would have done gently. Perhaps that was all right. Perhaps a people is not its magic. Perhaps a people is its memory, and its bread, and its songs, and who it chooses to sit beside when the rain comes.

On the first evening of autumn, Wren and Marguerite sat on the cottage step with bad local wine — she had been right, it was worse than the capital's — and watched the rain come up the valley.

It fell the way it had always fallen here: soft, persistent, gray-gold in the last light, beading on the thorn hedges that gave the valley its name. Ordinary rain. It had been ordinary all along, he understood now. The valley hadn't stolen anything from him. It had only ever asked him a question, every day for ten years: *who are you, when you are no one?*

He knew the answer now. He was a scribe. He was a keeper of records. He was the man Marguerite had chosen, and who had chosen her, and who had buried a boy whose name he would speak aloud every year on the anniversary, so that forgetting would have to fight him for it.

"Look at that," Marguerite said, nodding at the rain. "Completely unremarkable."

"Yes," Wren said, and lifted his cup to the sky, to the valley, to the boy on the hill, to the whole unremarkable blessed world. "Isn't it wonderful."

The rain came down on Thornvale, and it landed where it landed, and it needed to be nothing else — and neither, Wren thought, feeling the cool drops reach his face, neither did he.
