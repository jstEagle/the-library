## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The candle on Jude's desk had burned down to a stub the color of old teeth, and the ledger was still not balanced.

Jude Pemberton had been balancing accounts since the age of nine, first for a merchant house in Vell, then for a magistrate who liked his numbers cooked, and finally — after the exile, after the storm, after everything — for the Guild of Impossible Things. In all those years they had never once closed a book that refused to close. Tonight the book was refusing.

"Shadow, one, traded to Mrs. Halloway of Candle Row," Jude read aloud, quill hovering. "Received in exchange: three jars of pickled walnuts and a promise. Shadow returned to Mrs. Halloway upon fulfillment of promise. Promise unfulfilled. Shadow... where is the shadow?"

"Behind you," said Pip.

Jude did not flinch. This took discipline. Pip had a gift for arriving without arriving — a small, solemn child of perhaps ten years who moved through rooms the way fog moved through a keyhole. They were standing at Jude's shoulder now, barefoot, holding a half-eaten apple, watching the ledger with the grave attention of a much older soul wearing a much younger face.

"That's not funny, Pip."

"It wasn't a joke. There's a shadow behind you. It's been there since supper. It belongs to Mrs. Halloway, I think. It's waiting for its paperwork."

Jude turned. The corner behind the filing cabinet did look a little darker than corners had any right to look. With a sigh, they flipped back four pages, found the marginal note in their own hand — *Halloway shadow, temporary custody, see promise, see walnuts* — and made the correction. The corner brightened by a degree. Somewhere across the city, presumably, Mrs. Halloway felt a little more substantial and didn't know why.

"That," said a voice from the doorway, "is why the Guild pays you the big money."

The Guild paid Jude eleven crowns a month and unlimited access to the tea that Sable smuggled in from a country that did not exist. Sable herself leaned against the doorframe with her arms crossed, silver-haired, sharp-eyed, wearing the particular smirk of a forger admiring someone else's honest work. She was, by her own cheerful admission, the finest counterfeit document artist in the nine provinces, and possibly in several provinces that had been invented specifically to give her competition.

"The Guild pays me nothing," Jude said. "The Guild owes me four months' back wages, which I have recorded in triplicate."

"And I've offered to forge a receipt showing you've been paid," Sable said. "Every night you refuse. Every night it wounds me."

"Because then the ledger would say I was paid, and the ledger would be lying, and if the ledger starts lying, we're all lost."

Sable pressed a hand to her heart. "The integrity. It's unbearable. Come upstairs when you're done. Oren's making the stew that tastes like victory."

"Oren's stew tastes like boots."

"Victory *boots*."

She vanished down the hall, and Jude bent back over the ledger, and for a while there was only the scratch of the quill and the tick of the candle and the small, domestic music of a place where impossible things were kept in good order.

The Guild of Impossible Things occupied a narrow building on a street that appeared on no map, wedged between a chandler's shop and a house that was, on Tuesdays, slightly larger on the inside. The guild's charter was older than anyone could prove and stranger than anyone could explain. Its business was the custody of things the world had misplaced: traded shadows, bottled lullabies, the last words of people who died before saying them, a door that opened onto a summer that never happened. Every item had an entry. Every entry had a debt attached — who owed what, to whom, and by when. The guild did not collect debts so much as *remember* them, and in a city that forgot as quickly as this one did, remembering was the rarest service of all.

Jude was three lines from the bottom of the page when the boots arrived.

Oren Vail had been a knight of the realm for twenty-two years, which he described as "a long apprenticeship in being lied to." He was a wall of a man gone soft at the edges, gray-bearded, with a bad knee that predicted rain and a habit of standing in doorways as if guarding them out of pure reflex. He filled the study's entrance now, wooden spoon in hand, apron dusted with flour.

"Supper," he said.

"Two lines."

"You said that an hour ago."

"Then I lied, and now the ledger's corrupted, and it's your fault." Jude signed the line, blew on the ink, and closed the book with the small satisfied thump of a day properly accounted for. "There. Balanced. Shadows reconciled, lullabies counted, the door on Tuesday noted and released. Forty-one years of guild accounts, and not one entry missing."

Oren grunted — his highest register of praise — and led the way upstairs.

* * *

The kitchen of the Guild of Impossible Things was the warmest room in any world, including the summer that never happened. A fire cracked in the grate. The table was scarred and huge and always had one more chair than there were people, because the guild had learned long ago to leave room. Sable was already there, feet on a second chair, dealing a hand of cards against herself and cheating at it. Pip sat cross-legged on the windowsill, apple core balanced on their knee, watching the sky.

"Stars are bright tonight," Pip said.

"Good omen?" Oren asked, ladling stew.

"Dunno. They're just bright. Brighter than usual. Like they're trying to say something before somebody makes them stop."

Sable looked up from her cards. "Cheerful as ever, Pip."

"I'm a medium. Cheer isn't in the job description."

Jude took their bowl, took their chair, and let the warmth soak in. This was the hour they loved best, though they'd have denied it under oath. The ledger closed upstairs. The family around the table. Oren's stew — which really did taste like boots, but boots simmered in forty years of stubborn affection. Outside, the city murmured, and the guild held its impossible inventory, and everything, for one more night, was where the books said it should be.

"Tell the story," Pip said suddenly. "The storm story."

"Pip." Oren's voice went gentle and iron at the same time.

"I like it. It's got weather in it."

"I don't tell the storm story," Jude said, in the tone that ended conversations, and had, for three years, ended every conversation that approached it.

Sable, who feared nothing on this earth except blank paper and sincerity, opened her mouth — and stopped. Her eyes had gone past Jude's shoulder, to the window, and her face had changed.

"Jude," she said. "What time is it?"

Jude glanced at the mantel clock. "Midnight. Why?"

"Look at the sky."

They all looked. Pip had gone very still on the windowsill, apple core forgotten.

The stars were not going out. Jude would remember that distinction later, would turn it over a hundred times in the months to come. A star going out is an ending, a candle snuffed, a light that stops. This was different. The stars were being *unwritten* — one constellation at a time, the way ink fades from old paper, the way a name disappears from a record no one has checked in fifty years. The Hunter went first, his belt dissolving like sugar in water. Then the Ship. Then the Sisters, then the Wolf, then the great slow Wheel. No clouds. No smoke. The sky stayed black and clean, and the stars simply stopped having ever been there.

"Nobody move," Oren said quietly, and his hand had gone to his hip where a sword hadn't hung in six years.

"It's not the sky," Pip whispered. Their voice had the flat, distant quality it took on when they were hearing something on the other side of the world. "It's the *remembering*. Something's eating the remembering."

And then, from upstairs, came a sound no one in the guild had ever heard before: the sound of the ledger opening itself.

Jude took the stairs three at a time. The study door stood open. The ledger lay on the desk, closed when they had left it, now spread wide under the guttering candle, its pages turning one by one in a wind that did not exist.

Jude stood in the doorway and watched forty-one years of accounts erase themselves.

It started at the back — the oldest entries, the ones in faded iron-gall ink, the debts recorded by clerks three generations dead. The words didn't smudge or burn. They *paled*, like breath fading from glass. A trade of shadows from the reign of the old queen: gone. A bottled lullaby, deposited by a mother who never came back for it: gone. A promise kept in a jar, a door's Tuesday lease, a grief held in trust for a family that had forgotten it existed — page after page after page, the ink lifting off the paper as if it had only ever been resting there, as if the paper had finally exhaled.

"No," Jude said. "No, no — *no*—"

They lunged for the desk, slammed both hands flat on the open page, as if they could hold the words down by weight alone. Under their palms the ink continued to fade, patient and total, and the paper beneath it was not going blank the way paper goes blank. It was going blank the way paper goes blank *when it was always blank*, when nothing had ever been written on it at all. The past wasn't being erased. It was being *unclaimed*.

Behind them, the others crowded into the doorway. Sable's face was bloodless. Oren had gone very still, the stillness of a man watching a battle he already knew the ending of. Pip stood between them, small and straight, listening to something no one else could hear.

"Jude," Pip said. "The ledger's not the only thing."

Jude looked up. "What?"

"The whole city's books. The registries. The parish rolls. The tax records. Everything old." Pip's eyes were wide and dark. "Everything older than fifty years. It's all going blank at once. And there's a sound, Jude. There's a sound like — like glass. Far away. Like something very big just broke, and everything it was holding up is starting to fall."

Jude looked down at their hands, flat on fifty years of nothing, and felt the first cold edge of a thought they would carry for thirteen turns of the moon:

Someone had just defaulted on the oldest debt in the kingdom.

And the ledger knew it.
