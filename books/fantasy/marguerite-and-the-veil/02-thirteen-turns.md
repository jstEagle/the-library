## 2. Thirteen Turns

Sable heard the wards break before anyone else did.

Not because her ears were better — though they were; a forger's ears have to catch the difference between a real seal and a good one, and that difference is always small — but because she was awake. She was always awake at this hour, hunched over her workbench with a loupe in one eye and a half-finished decree of passage under her hand, copying the Lord Marshal's cramped signature for the eleventh time until it stopped being forgery and started being truth.

The sound came through the walls like a bell struck underwater. A high, thin ringing that didn't fade so much as *shatter*, spreading outward into a thousand smaller sounds, each one the death of something.

She set down her pen very carefully, the way you do when your hands want to shake and you refuse to let them.

"Well," she said to the empty room. "That's not good."

* * *

By the time she reached the guildhall's common room, everyone was already there, which told Sable everything about how bad it was. The Guild of Impossible Things kept strange hours — Oren slept like a soldier because soldiers learn to sleep when sleep is offered, and Pip slept like a cat because children do — but nobody was sleeping now. Jude stood at the long table with the great ledger open before them, both hands flat on the page as if holding down a lid. Oren stood behind them with his arms crossed, wearing the particular stillness he wore when old instincts were shouting. And Pip sat on the table itself, legs swinging, staring out the window with an expression no child should have to wear.

"Tell me," Sable said.

"The border wards are gone." Jude didn't look up. "All of them. The whole ring, north to south, every stone. They didn't fail. They were *released*."

"Released by whom?"

"That's the question, isn't it."

Oren spoke without turning from the window. "I felt it in my teeth. I haven't felt wardwork since the war. It's like hearing a building fall — you know the shape of it by the sound." His voice was flat, which for Oren meant loud. "Someone let go of the biggest working on the continent."

"And the stars," Pip said quietly.

Everyone looked at the window. Sable looked too, and felt something cold walk down her spine and put its feet up.

The sky over the city had always been crowded. The kingdom sat under more constellations than any map could hold — the Lantern-Bearer, the Nine Sisters, the Old Ox, the Weeping King — stars so thick and reliable that navigators hadn't needed charts in three hundred years. You could find your way home blind drunk in a snowstorm, because the sky remembered where everything was.

The Weeping King was gone.

Not clouded. Not dimmed. Gone, the way a word is gone when you look away from a page and back again. In its place: clean black nothing, edged on either side by stars that suddenly looked nervous.

As they watched, one of the Nine Sisters went out. Then her sister beside her. Then the third.

"It's eating them," Pip said. "From the top down."

"Stars don't get eaten," Oren said. "Stars don't—"

"These did," said a voice from the doorway.

* * *

Mistress Corvane was ninety years old if she was a day, and she moved like a woman who had decided decades ago that hurrying was for people with less authority. Behind her came Master Ilsette, the guild's archivist, carrying a box.

Not just any box. Sable knew boxes the way priests knew scripture, and this one made her mouth go dry. Black oak. Iron corners worked with the guild's oldest sigil — not the current mark, the *old* one, the impossible thing with seven sides. Sealed with wax that wasn't wax. She'd tried to forge that seal once, as an exercise, twenty years ago. She'd gotten within a hair of perfect and then dreamed about it for a month, waking up screaming, and never touched it again.

Corvane set the box on the table, directly on top of the ledger's blank pages.

"Fifty years," she said. "Everything older than fifty years is going blank. Every record in this city, every registry, every roll. And the wards are down, and the stars are going out constellation by constellation, starting from the oldest." She looked around the room, and her eyes — pale, sharp, unhurried — rested last on Sable. "You're all clever people. Someone say the obvious thing."

"Something is erasing history," Jude said. "In order. Oldest first."

"History isn't being erased," Ilsette said. Her hands were trembling on the box. "Debts are being collected."

Silence. Pip stopped swinging her legs.

"The guild has existed for six hundred years," Ilsette went on, "and in six hundred years we have made exactly one rule above all others: *everything owed must be written down.* Not because we're sentimental. Because memory fails, and paper doesn't. Every debt this guild has ever held or been held by is in these books." She tapped the ledger under the box. "And tonight, everything older than fifty years went blank. Which means someone has called in — or defaulted on — debts older than fifty years. All of them. At once."

"How many are there?" Oren asked.

Ilsette's jaw tightened. "We don't know. That's rather the point of the blanks."

"But you know one," Sable said.

Every head turned to her. She shrugged, keeping her voice light the way she kept all dangerous things light. "You brought the box, mistress. Nobody brings a box to deliver information they don't have. That seal is older than the guild's current charter. Whatever's inside, you've been sitting on it a long time, and tonight is the night you decided to stop."

Corvane studied her for a long moment. "You waste yourself on forgery, girl."

"I flatter myself it's the opposite."

The old woman broke the seal.

It came apart without resistance — which frightened Sable more than any struggle would have. A seal like that should have fought. Instead it opened like a door that had only ever been waiting, and inside lay a single page, folded in thirds, the paper brown and soft as old skin.

Ilsette unfolded it with both hands and read it aloud. Her voice shook on the first line and steadied after, the way voices do when what they're reading is worse than the shaking.

"*Let it be entered in the book of the Guild of Impossible Things, this third day of the Harvest Moon, in the year of the Long Frost: that Aldric, son of Maren, heir to a dying crown, owes to this guild a debt unnamed and unpriced, incurred in desperation and sealed in blood, to be paid when called upon, in whatever form the guild shall name. Witnessed by the well. Witnessed by the dark. Witnessed by the thing that remembers so he does not have to.*"

Nobody breathed.

"Aldric," Oren said finally. "Aldric *Vane*. The king."

"There was no other," Corvane said.

"The date," Jude said. Their voice had gone very quiet, the way it did when they were doing arithmetic they hated. "The year of the Long Frost. That's—" They looked up. "That's four years before his coronation. He was a boy."

"He was nineteen," Oren said. "I was in the palace guard by then. I remember the winter. The wards were failing even then — the old king was dying and the border stones were flickering like bad lamps. There were nights we could see the far mountains through the wards, plain as day, where nothing should have been visible at all." He paused. "Then spring came, and the wards were stronger than they'd ever been, and nobody asked why. Kings fixed things. That was the story."

"And the story was bought," Sable said slowly, "with a debt. To us. To this guild." She laughed, short and humorless. "We've been holding the mortgage on the kingdom's magic for fifty years and nobody thought to mention it?"

"We didn't know," Ilsette said. "The page was sealed by the masters of that era, with instructions it be opened only when the ledger demanded it. Tonight the ledger demanded it."

"Why?" Pip asked. "Why would the ledger demand it?"

Jude answered, and their voice was terrible in its calm. "Because the debt is being paid right now. Or collected. Look at the pattern. Records older than fifty years — blank. Wards older than the records — released. Stars older than everything—" They gestured at the window, where the Nine Sisters were now five sisters, then four. "It's unwinding. From the bottom of the books upward. Something took the king's payment and it's coming due, and everything the debt touched is being pulled back into the dark to settle accounts."

"So we collect," Oren said. "We go to the king and we present the page. Debt-collecting is the oldest trade there is. Even kings answer a lawful claim."

"Do they?" Sable said. "When the claim is *this*? When answering means admitting what he sold?" She shook her head. "Men don't pay debts that shame them, Oren. They burn the ledger. They burn the ledger-keepers."

"Then we'd best be quick," said Pip.

They all looked at her. She was still watching the sky, chin propped on her fists, and outside the window the last of the Nine Sisters went dark, and the constellation of the Old Ox began, star by star, to blink out like candles at the end of a feast.

"Thirteen turns," Pip said. "That's how long until the moon goes dark. I don't know how I know. I just do. But when the moon goes dark, whatever's collecting is going to finish, and there won't be anything left to save. Not the stars. Not the records." She turned and looked at them, small and grave. "Not him."

Corvane closed the box. "Then hear me. This guild does not chase kings. This guild does not make war on crowns. And this guild especially does not gamble its members against a power old enough to eat constellations." She looked around the room, daring objection. "Which is why I am ordering none of you to do anything at all."

She pushed the box across the table toward Jude.

"I am simply noting that the debt is ours to collect, that the collection window is thirteen turns of the moon, and that the guild's first law says everything owed must be written down." Her mouth twitched, the ghost of a smile. "Everything owed. Everything *remembered*. The rest, as ever, is your own affair."

Outside, the Old Ox lost its horns.

Sable looked at her family — Jude with their hands on the dead ledger, Oren with his soldier's stillness cracking into something older and angrier, Pip with her terrible knowing eyes — and felt the familiar click of a decision being forged, smooth and irreversible, in some back room of herself.

"Well," she said. "I'll need new papers. Good ones. If we're going to walk into the capital and dun the king of the realm, we can't exactly sign our names."

Jude almost smiled. Almost.

"Pack light," they said. "We leave before moonrise."

Above the rooftops, the last star of the Old Ox went out, and the sky began, patiently, on the Weeping King's neighbors.
