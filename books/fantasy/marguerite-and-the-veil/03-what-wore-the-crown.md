## 3. What Wore the Crown

The cart smelled of pickled herring and old lies, which was fitting, because we were both.

"Debt-collectors," Sable said, adjusting the collar of her merchant's coat. "You realize I've forged us an entire company. Ledgers of record. A seal. Three generations of reputable, boring creditors who have never once been chased out of a city."

"We have been chased out of four cities," Oren said from the far end of the cart bed, where he sat with his arms crossed like a man attending his own funeral.

"That's why the forgery is so good. It accounts for the chasing."

I lay back against a crate of actual herring and watched the capital grow on the horizon — first as a smudge, then as a suggestion, then as a fact. Veymar had been described to me a hundred times: the white walls, the seven towers of the palace rising like fingers pressed against the sky, the river bending around it all like something trying to get away. The descriptions were accurate and useless. What no one mentioned was the silence. A city that size should hum. This one held its breath.

Above it, where stars should have been, there was nothing. Not cloud-cover. Nothing. The sky over Veymar had been scrubbed clean, and the absence had a shape if you looked too long.

"Don't look at it," Pip said quietly, beside me. They were small enough to fit in the gap between crates, and they'd been sitting very still for an hour, which Pip never did unless something was wrong. "The empty part. If you look at it too long it looks back at your ledger."

"My ledger's blank anyway," I said. "Half of it."

"I know." Pip didn't say anything else. They didn't have to. We'd all spent two weeks not saying anything else about the pages that had gone white at midnight, the fifty years of guild history erased like chalk off a slate. We'd stopped saying it because saying it made it more true, and we needed it to be less true for a while longer.

The cart rolled through the river gate without stopping. Sable's papers were perfect; the guard barely glanced up from his dice. That was the thing about Veymar nobody told you either — the city wasn't afraid of smugglers. It was afraid of something much larger, and it had no attention left over for the rest of us.

We unloaded at a chandler's warehouse that owed our fictional company money, argued loudly and professionally about interest rates in front of three witnesses, and by dusk we were simply four debt-collectors lodging above a tavern called the Drowned Bell, with legitimate business at the palace come morning.

That night I couldn't sleep. I went up to the roof with the warding glass wrapped in sacking under my arm, and I told myself I was being careful, doing reconnaissance, balancing risk against reward the way I balanced every account. All true. But underneath the bookkeeping there was a simpler reason: I wanted to see what was eating my guild's oldest page.

* * *

The palace was visible from the roof, seven towers black against the starless quarter of the sky. Through the warding glass — a lens of smoked crystal older than any of us, older than the guild, possibly older than the kingdom — the towers resolved into detail, and the detail was wrong.

Wards are supposed to look like weaving. Threads of intent, knotted and layered, the way Oren described the border wards he'd once stood watch beneath. What I saw over the palace was not weaving. It was digestion. Slow concentric rings, turning inward, each one faintly thinner than the last, everything drawn toward the central tower the way water finds a drain. And at the center —

At the center was a window, lit gold, and behind the glass a man.

Even magnified, even warded, even from half a mile away, I knew him. King Aldric the Third, the Restorer, the Warden-King whose face was stamped on every coin that had ever passed through my hands. He sat at a table with a meal untouched before him, and he was very still, and the stillness was the worst part. People fidget. People breathe unevenly, scratch, shift their weight. This man sat the way furniture sits.

Then he moved, and I understood that I had been wrong to call him a man at all.

His hand rose — smoothly, too smoothly, joints gliding like oiled pulleys — and turned a page of a book he wasn't reading. His head tilted at an angle no neck should find comfortable. And when the light of the lamp crossed his face, I saw that his eyes weren't looking at anything in the room. They were looking *through* it, through the wall, through the city, through me, at some vast interior distance. There was something behind those eyes. Something patient. Something that had been wearing this body for thirty years the way I wore my coat, and had long since stopped noticing the fit.

A hollow man. A glove, and something enormous inside it, flexing its fingers one at a time.

I lowered the glass. My hands were shaking, which annoyed me. Fear is an expense, and I don't carry unbudgeted expenses.

"He knows we're here."

Pip's voice came from the roof door. They climbed out and sat beside me without asking, pulling their knees up, staring at the palace with their naked eyes — which for Pip is worse than any glass, because Pip doesn't need lenses to hear things.

"You felt that?" I said.

"I've felt it since the gate." Pip hugged their shins. "It's like standing next to a big bell that someone struck a long time ago and it never finished ringing. Only it's not ringing at us. It's ringing at him. From the inside."

"Pip—"

"I know. Don't touch things. You always say don't touch things." They were already reaching toward the roof edge, toward the distant gleam of the palace's outer gates, iron and gold, a mile away and impossibly loud to whatever sense lived in that child's palms. "But Jude. Somebody in there is crying."

"The king?"

"No." Pip's face did something complicated. Pip is nine going on ninety, and I have seen them hear murders and tax frauds and one memorable ghost horse without flinching, but their face now was the face of a kid. "No. That's the thing. It's not the king crying. It's — under him? Inside him? Like a room inside a room. There's a boy in there, Jude, and he's locked in, and he's been crying so long he doesn't make sound anymore, he just — shakes. And the big thing wears him like a coat and it doesn't even know he's there. Or it knows and doesn't care. I can't tell which is worse."

I looked through the glass again. The figure at the table had gone still once more. Somewhere behind those patient eyes, a boy was shaking in a locked room, and had been for thirty years, and the whole kingdom called it peace.

"It took his memories," I said. "That's the debt. That's what the ledger says he owes. His own past, sold or stolen, piece by piece, to keep the wards standing. And now the wards are falling anyway, and the stars are going out, and the thing wearing him is getting hungry."

"Do we still collect?" Pip asked. It was a real question. Pip asks real questions the way other children ask for sweets — constantly, and at the worst possible moments. "If he's a victim. Do we still collect a debt from somebody who got robbed?"

I thought about the blank pages. Fifty years of the guild's memory, gone white. I thought about the stars going out constellation by constellation, and Oren standing watch at night with his jaw set like a man guarding a grave. I thought about the voice in the well we hadn't found yet, and the boy who had promised it *anything*, and how many kingdoms are built on exactly that kind of promise.

"A debt's a debt," I said, and heard how hollow it sounded, and hated it. "But maybe we're not collecting from him. Maybe we're collecting *for* him. There's a difference. It's a thin difference, but ledgers are made of thin lines."

Pip leaned against my arm. Small, warm, solid. "The boy in there," they said. "When I listened closer — just for a second, I know, I know, don't touch — he wasn't crying about being locked in. He was crying because he couldn't remember his mother's face. He keeps almost having it and then it goes. Over and over. Forever."

Below us, the Drowned Bell rang midnight. Above us, the empty quarter of sky pressed down like a thumb.

And then Pip went rigid against my side.

"What," I said. "What is it."

"The bell," Pip whispered. "Jude, the bell rang twelve times and the palace heard it. The big thing heard it. It turned its head." Their hand found mine and gripped hard enough to hurt. "It's looking at our window. It knows a ledger came to town. It says—" Pip swallowed. "It says we're late. It says the debt's been called, and it's been waiting thirty years for someone to come collect, and if we want the boy, we'd better hurry, because it's starting to eat the rest of him at the next new moon."

Far away, in the lit golden window, the king's head turned — slowly, smoothly, terribly — until those empty eyes faced directly across the sleeping city, at the tavern called the Drowned Bell, at the roof, at us.

Then the lamp went out, and the window went dark, and somewhere in the ashes of a monastery a week's ride east, I would have sworn a well began, very softly, to laugh.
