## 4. A Debt in Ash

Oren had burned a monastery once, thirty years ago. He'd been given the order by a captain who'd been given it by a lord who'd been given it by the crown, and he had done it because that was what soldiers did: they carried fire down the chain of command until it landed somewhere and caught.

He had never asked whose monastery. He had never gone back to look at what was left.

Now he was standing in exactly that — not the same one, he told himself, though the ash didn't care about distinctions — and the wind was moving through the ribs of it like breath through a dead man's chest.

"The trail stops here," Sable said behind him. She had her hood up against the gray drizzle, her ledger-satchel strapped tight, and she was looking at the ruin with the flat professional expression she wore when she was cataloguing something expensive that had been destroyed. "The debt's thread runs straight into this ground and frays. Whatever Aldric gave away, he gave it here."

"Or it was taken from him here," Pip said. The child stood at the edge of the courtyard, barefoot as always despite Jude's protests, toes curled into cold mud. Their eyes were half-closed, the way they got when they were listening to something the rest of them couldn't hear. "There's an old voice under the floor. It's still talking."

"To whom?" Jude asked.

Pip opened their eyes. "To whoever's left."

* * *

They made camp in what had been the refectory, because it was the only roofed thing remaining, and even its roof was more suggestion than structure. Oren built the fire with hands that remembered how to do it in the dark, in the rain, in a hurry, and sat back on his heels and looked at the walls.

Char marks climbed them like ivy. Underneath the char, faintly, you could still see where frescoes had been: saints, probably, or kings, or whatever this order had painted instead. The faces were all burned away. Just bodies and robes and raised hands, gesturing forever at nothing.

He knew that gesture. He'd seen it on his own men, the night the palace guard was dissolved. The king's new council had called it a restructuring; Oren had called it being handed his severance and shown the gate after twenty-two years of service, his oath annulled, his name struck from the rolls so cleanly that his own mother's pension had stopped coming, because officially he had never existed to be anyone's son.

"You're staring," Sable said, without looking up from her work. She was copying the faded debt-thread onto fresh paper, tracing it with a stylus dipped in silver ink, trying to keep it from fraying further. "Staring at ruins is a luxury for people with clean consciences."

"I have a clean conscience."

"You have a clean record. Different animal." She glanced up. "You served them."

"I served the crown."

"The crown is them."

Oren fed a stick to the fire and said nothing, which was answer enough, and Sable, mercifully, let it be enough.

Jude came back from the perimeter shaking rain off their coat. They'd taken first watch without discussion — they always did, since the confession, since the night around the guild fire when Oren had learned what his storm-calling friend had done to earn their exile, and had absorbed it the way the family absorbed everything: completely, without flinching, and then never once held it over them. Oren envied that grace. He hadn't earned his own yet.

"Anything?" Jude asked.

"Pip says there's a voice under the floor."

"There's always a voice under the floor," Sable muttered. "In my experience the voices under floors are either rats or debts."

"In this case," Pip said, appearing between them with the uncanny silence children have, "it's both."

* * *

The well was in the center of the cloister, or had been. Now it was a ring of scorched stone with a darkness in the middle that the rain refused to fall into. Oren noticed that first — the drops veering, almost politely, around the opening, as if the well were holding its breath.

Pip knelt at the rim and pressed both palms flat to the stone.

"It's not a ghost," they said. "Ghosts are finished. This is a recording. Something happened here that the world hasn't finished playing." The child's face went strange and old. "A bargain. A boy stood right here and bargained."

"Aldric," Jude said quietly.

"Aldric." Pip looked up. "It wants to show us. But it wants something in exchange. That's the rat part."

"What does it want?"

Pip hesitated, and Oren saw the child weigh whether to lie, and decide against it, because this family didn't lie to each other about costs. Not anymore. Not after the exile story.

"A memory," Pip said. "From whoever opens it. One memory, freely given, into the well. It says it's hungry and it's been waiting fifty years."

"No," Oren said immediately.

"Yes," Sable said at the same moment, and the two of them looked at each other.

"It eats memories," Oren said. "That's what the Veil does. That's what we're fighting. We're not feeding it."

"This isn't the Veil," Pip said. "This is older. This might be where the Veil learned the trick." The child's voice dropped. "And it's not asking for much. It says it will take the memory closest to shame."

Everyone was quiet. Rain ticked on stone. The fire cracked in the refectory behind them.

"The closest to shame," Oren repeated.

"You don't have to," Jude said. "We find another way. We always—"

"I'll do it."

Sable turned fully around. "Oren."

"It should be me." He heard his own voice come out steady, parade-ground steady, the voice he'd used to order the torches lit. "I've been circling this place since we arrived like it owes me something. Maybe it does. Maybe I owe it. I burned monasteries for that crown. Plural. I never once went back to look at the ashes, and now the ashes are looking back." He rolled his shoulders, the old injury in the left one singing. "If the price of the king's memory is one of mine, that's arithmetic I can live with. Better than most of my arithmetic."

"Freely given," Pip warned. "It has to mean it. If you give it grudging, it won't take, and it gets... annoyed."

"And if it takes too much?"

"It can't," Pip said. "That's the rule. One memory, freely offered, honestly named. Anything else would break the bargain, and bargains are the only law down there."

Oren nodded slowly. Then he walked out into the cloister, alone, and knelt at the well.

* * *

Up close, the dark inside it had texture. Not emptiness — patience. Fifty years of it, folded down into a hole in the ground, waiting for someone to come and owe it something.

"I know what you are," Oren said. His breath fogged. "Or I know what your grandchildren became. You take memories. You hold them. You call it keeping."

The dark did not answer, but it listened. He could feel it listening, the way you feel a hand on the back of your neck.

"I served King Aldric's father, and then I served Aldric, may he rest wherever he actually is, because the man on the throne now is wearing him like a glove. Twenty-two years. I carried the crown's fire to places like this one." He gestured at the ruin without looking at it. "I told myself orders were orders. I told myself the chain of command was a kind of innocence. It isn't. Every link knows what it's passing down."

He swallowed. Here it was. The memory closest to shame, and it wasn't even the burning — it was smaller than that, meaner than that, the way real shame always is.

"There was a monk here," he said. "Not this monastery. Another one, the winter before it closed. An old man who brought the garrison soup in the snow because he thought soldiers were just boys far from home. And when the order came, I let him walk back down the mountain ahead of the flames because I told myself the fire would follow the road and he'd turn off at the shrine." A breath. "He didn't turn off. I know he didn't turn off, because I watched the smoke change color that evening, and I did the arithmetic every soldier does, and then I wrote it up as a successful action."

His voice didn't shake. He'd trained it not to, and now he hated it for obeying.

"That's the memory. Freely given. Honestly named. Take it."

He leaned over the dark and let it go — not pushed, not thrown, just opened his hand around it the way you open a fist around something you've been gripping so long you forgot it was there. The well drank. There was no light, no sound, only a sensation like a page turning somewhere very deep.

Then the dark spoke, and it sounded like a boy.

* * *

What rose out of the well was not a ghost and not a vision but a memory played in the air above the stones, thin as smoke and twice as fragile:

A boy of maybe twelve, kneeling where Oren knelt, in a kingdom visibly dying — Oren could see it in the memory's edges, the way the fields lay gray, the way the sky itself seemed malnourished. The wards were failing even then. The boy's hands were bleeding from where he'd clawed at the well's rim.

"Please," the boy-Aldric said. "My father's dead, the wards are dying, the border will fall before spring. Everyone says there's nothing left to bargain with. But I'm the king now. I have to have something."

The dark in the well stirred, pleased, patient.

"You have yourself," it said. "Kings always do. Give me your yesterdays — all of them, one by one, across thirteen turns of the moon — and I will hold your borders up with them. Memory makes fine mortar. No one will ever know. Not even you."

"And if I run out of yesterdays?"

"Then you will be a very effective king," the voice said, warm as banked coals, "and a very empty man. Do we have terms?"

The boy wiped his nose on his sleeve, the way children do when they are being braver than their bodies want. He looked, for one terrible second, like every young soldier Oren had ever led into a mistake made by older men.

"Anything," the boy said. "I promise anything. Just save them. Save all of them."

The memory shivered, thinned, and sank back into the well like a coin into black water.

Silence. Rain. Behind Oren, the family stood in a row, and nobody spoke, because there was nothing to say that the ash hadn't already heard.

But in Oren's open hand, where the well had returned his change — bargains always return change, Pip had said, it's how you know they're honest — lay a splinter of light no bigger than a fingernail. Warm. Humming faintly, like a struck bell that wouldn't finish ringing.

The first shard. The boy's promise. The root of the whole rotten, holy, monstrous debt.

Sable reached him first and took his arm, steadying him, saying nothing, which was her way of saying everything. Jude crouched to look at the shard without touching it. Pip simply leaned against Oren's side, small and solid, and stayed there.

"Thirteen turns," Jude said finally. "One down. Twelve to go, and the moon doesn't wait for grief."

Oren closed his fist around the light. It pulsed against his palm like a second heartbeat — a boy's heartbeat, fifty years dead and still going.

"Then we don't wait for it either," he said, and found, to his surprise, that the words had weight in them, the good kind, the kind you could build with. "Next turn. Next shard. Let's go collect a king."

Behind them, unnoticed by any of them, the well's dark had begun — very softly, very patiently — to laugh.
