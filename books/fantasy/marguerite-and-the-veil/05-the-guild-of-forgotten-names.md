## 5. The Guild of Forgotten Names

Pip had a rule about doors that weren't there: knock anyway.

The others thought it was a joke. It wasn't. Doors that weren't there were the only kind that ever opened for Pip without being asked twice, because a door that wasn't there had already decided to trust you. You just had to be polite about it.

So when the alley behind the tannery dead-ended in a wall of wet brick — no arch, no seam, no lantern, nothing but the smell of hides and the sound of the city holding its breath — Pip walked up and knocked on the air three times, soft as asking.

The wall knocked back.

"See?" Pip said over their shoulder. "Told you."

Behind them, Oren shifted his weight the way he did before fights, armor-less and hating it. Sable stood with her arms crossed, wearing the face of a fishmonger's widow she'd invented two days ago and had grown oddly fond of. And Jude — Jude was very still, the way they got when a ledger balanced too neatly, like the world was about to hand them a bill.

"You're sure this is it?" Sable asked. "Because I've been thrown out of nicer alleys."

"The crying told me where to go," Pip said simply. "It's been telling me since the palace gates. Not the king's crying. A different one. Lots of them, all tangled up, like a choir that forgot its own song." Pip pressed their palm flat against the brick. "They're behind here. The ones whose names went missing."

Jude exhaled slowly. "People erased by the Veil."

"People *unwritten*," Pip corrected. "There's a difference. Erased means something took you away. Unwritten means nobody bothered to write you down in the first place anymore. The Veil doesn't just eat kings' memories. It eats bookkeeping. Little bits. A tax record here. A marriage line there. A name off a gravestone. Nobody notices because the ledger of the whole kingdom just... closes around the hole, smooth as water." Pip looked up at them all. "Except us. We're bookkeepers."

That was why they'd come. That was why Jude had spent four nights cross-referencing census pages against parish rolls against guild rosters, hunting for the gaps — the places where ink should have been and wasn't. Thirteen hundred people, give or take, who had simply stopped appearing in anything. Not dead. Dead left marks. Just *absent*, the way a tooth is absent.

The wall opened.

Not dramatically. The bricks simply agreed, one after another, to have always been a doorway, and then they were looking into a courtyard strung with lanterns made from jam jars, where maybe forty people sat at long tables eating stew out of mismatched bowls. Every one of them turned to look. And every one of them, Pip realized with a lurch, was *hard to look at* — not invisible, not blurred, just slippery, the way a word is slippery when it's on the tip of your tongue. Pip's eyes kept sliding off a baker's forearms, an old woman's laugh lines, a boy's crooked smile, finding nothing to hold onto.

A woman came forward through the crowd, and Pip understood immediately why she led them.

She had no face.

Not scarred, not masked. Where her features should have been there was a smoothness like the inside of a pearl, catching the lantern light and giving none of it back. And yet Pip could feel her attention the way you feel sun through a window — warm, total, and impossible to look away from.

"You knocked," she said. Her voice was lovely and slightly wrong, like a bell rung underwater. "Nobody knocks anymore. They pound, or they beg, or they don't come at all."

"We're bookkeepers," Pip said. "We knock."

Something moved across the pearl of her face — not a smile, but the place where a smile would go if faces worked that way. "I know what you are. The Guild of Impossible Things. You collected my sister's shadow once, forty years ago, when a moneylender bought it to frighten her debts away. She never forgot you." She tilted her head. "I'm called Marrow. It isn't my name. My name was the first thing the Veil took, so we don't waste names here. We lend them. We trade them. We keep them safe the way you keep everything else."

Oren stepped forward, and even slippery-eyed, everyone saw a soldier. "You know why we've come."

"To unbind the king." Marrow's voice didn't change, but the courtyard did — the clink of spoons stopped, the jam-jar lanterns seemed to burn lower. "Word travels among the unwritten faster than among the written. We've felt every shard you've pulled free. Like hearing a stranger hum a song you'd forgotten you knew." She raised one handless-seeming gesture toward the tables. "You want allies. Here are forty people who owe the Veil nothing because the Veil has already taken everything. Ask."

Sable, practical as a knife: "What do you need?"

"Passages," Marrow said. "You forge documents. Forged papers are still papers. A name on a page is a hook the world can hold onto. Give our people names again — borrowed ones, new ones, any ones — and they can walk out of these alleys and be seen. Be *counted*." Her pearl face turned toward Jude. "And in exchange, we'll give you what you actually came for. The well. The monastery well where Aldric made his bargain. Our people remember things the records don't, because the Veil never bothered to erase what it didn't think mattered. One of ours was a novitiate there. He remembers the night of the bargain better than the king does."

Pip felt the family go quiet in that particular way they did — the way of people who had learned that gifts from strangers always had teeth.

"And the price?" Jude asked. Their voice was flat and careful, a merchant's voice, a debtor's voice.

Marrow regarded them for a long moment. Lantern light slid off her face and found nowhere to land.

"You carry a story," she said. "The story of your exile. Why you were cast out. What you did. It's the last true proof of who you are — the one document no guild has, no crown holds, no forgery can duplicate. It lives in you the way a name used to live in our people." She spread her hands. "Give it to us. Let us keep it. Not destroy it — we don't destroy. Keep it, the way we keep everything the world has dropped. And in return, the unwritten rise, and the well gives up its secret."

* * *

They talked it through in the corner of the courtyard, low, while stew went cold in front of them and forty slippery-eyed people pretended not to listen and failed completely.

"It's a trap," Sable said flatly. "Or if it's not a trap, it's worse. It's fair. Fair things cost the most."

"They're not asking for blood or betrayal," Oren rumbled. "They're asking for a story. Stories keep. I've carried mine fifty years; it hasn't rotted yet."

"That's not the point." Sable leaned in. "The point is what Jude *is*. You cut a person's exile out of them, what's left? I've forged ten thousand identities. Do you know how many of them held? The ones built on something real. Take away the real thing and you've got paper over a hole. I would know. I *am* paper over a hole."

"I'm not a hole," Jude said quietly.

"No," Sable said. "But you're afraid you might be one. That's why they're asking. That's why it costs."

Pip said nothing for a while. Pip was listening — not to the family, but to the courtyard, to the choir of tangled crying they'd followed here. Up close it resolved into something else. Not grief, exactly. *Patience.* Forty people who had learned to exist without being recorded, who had built lanterns out of jam jars and a society out of being forgotten, and who wanted, more than revenge or restoration, simply to be *written down somewhere*.

"They're not taking it to hurt you," Pip said finally. All three of them looked over. "I hear them. Underneath everything. They want to hold it because nobody's ever trusted them to hold anything. That's the whole price. It's not the story they need. It's being the kind of people a story gets entrusted to." Pip shrugged. "Which doesn't make it cheaper. Just makes it honest."

Jude stared into the cold stew for a long time.

"I called the storm," they said at last, so quietly only the table heard. "You all know that much. But the story — the whole of it — is the only thing I own outright. Everything else is guild property, guild debt, guild risk. That story is mine." They looked up. "If I give it away, what am I?"

"Ours," said Oren, without a heartbeat's hesitation.

"Mine," said Sable. "Chosen, not owed. There's a difference, and it's better."

Pip reached across and put one small hand on Jude's wrist. "You'll still be the one who balances the books. Nobody can lend that away."

Jude laughed — a short, cracked sound, half grief. "When did you all get wise?"

"About the same time you got stubborn," Sable said.

* * *

Marrow received the story at midnight, in the center of the courtyard, under the jam-jar lanterns, with all forty of the unwritten standing witness.

She didn't take it with magic. There was no cutting, no pulling, no cold wind of extraction. She simply held out both hands and said, "Tell it," and Jude told it — the whole of it, from the corrupt magistrate to the drowning court to the innocents swept away alongside the guilty, every detail spoken aloud for the first time in eleven years. Pip watched the words leave Jude like threads leaving a loom, and watched them settle into Marrow's cupped hands as something faintly luminous, a knot of light the size of a sparrow's heart.

"There," Marrow said softly, closing her fingers over it. "Kept. Not owned. If ever you need it back, you have only to ask — though I warn you, what's kept changes in the keeping. Things held by many hands grow warmer."

Around the courtyard, the unwritten began to murmur — and as they murmured, they *sharpened*. Pip's eyes, which had slid off them all evening, started to catch: the baker's flour-white forearms, the old woman's laugh lines, the boy's crooked grin. Not because they'd been given names yet. Because someone had finally trusted them with something heavy, and weight is the beginning of being real.

"One of ours will take you to the well at first light," Marrow said. "A man called Brother Fen. He was there, the night the boy king knelt at the lip and promised anything." Her pearl face inclined toward Jude, and for a moment — just a moment — something flickered across it, almost features, almost a warning.

"But go quickly, bookkeepers. Because the Veil felt that story leave you the moment you spoke it aloud. And it knows now what you're trading in." The lanterns guttered, all together, though there was no wind. "It knows what memories freely given can do. And it has decided, I think, to stop losing."

Pip felt the crying-choir in the walls go suddenly, utterly silent — and in the silence, far off toward the palace, heard something vast begin to move.
