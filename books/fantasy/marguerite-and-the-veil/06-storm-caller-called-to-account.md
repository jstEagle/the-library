## 6. Storm-Caller, Called to Account

The woman with no face kept her word.

She came to the guildhouse at dusk on the third day after the payment, moving through the door without opening it, and she set something down on the long table where we did our accounting: a small clay jar, sealed with wax the color of a bruise, and inside it — I could feel it before she even finished setting it down — my exile story, whole and humming, like a wasp in a bottle.

"Your proof of who you were," she said. Her voice had no mouth to shape it, but it arrived anyway, soft as dust settling. "As agreed. The Forgotten Names will stand with you at the thirteenth turn."

Then she was gone, and the jar sat there on the table between a stack of bottled lullabies and Oren's whetstone, and nobody moved for a long time.

It was Sable who finally spoke. "That's it? That's what you gave them?"

"That's what they asked for," I said.

"I know what they *asked* for." She turned the jar over in her hands, frowning at the wax seal. "I want to know what it is. You told us you were exiled. Everyone's exiled from somewhere. Pip ran away from a house that smelled of onions. Oren got pensioned off with a medal and no pension. But this—" she held it up to the lantern light, and the thing inside shifted, pressing against the clay like something alive, "—this has weight. This has *weather* in it."

Pip was standing very still by the window. Pip, who heard things the rest of us couldn't, who had pressed their small hand to the palace gates and come back gray-faced with the sound of a child crying inside a king's skull. Pip looked at me the way you look at a door you've suddenly realized has been locked your whole life.

"You never told us why," Pip said quietly. "You told us the *what*. Storm-caller. Exiled. Never again. You never told us the why."

Oren said nothing. He'd stopped sharpening his knife. That was how I knew he already suspected. Old soldiers can smell a battlefield in a story the way horses smell rain.

I looked at the jar. My whole history, sealed up and traded away so that people whose names had been eaten could have names again. It had seemed like a fair price at the time. It still seemed like a fair price. That wasn't the problem.

The problem was that I'd spent eleven years hoping nobody would ever ask me to open it.

"It's not going to bite," I said, and reached for the wax.

* * *

Here is what the ledger of my life says, or would say, if anyone still kept such a book:

My name was not always Jude Pemberton. For the first twenty-two years of it, I was Jules Vane, of the Marches, apprenticed to a weather-witch named Mother Casp who taught me that storms are not weapons, they are *arguments* — the sky arguing with the sea, the heat arguing with the cold — and that a storm-caller's job is not to command the argument but to give it somewhere honest to go.

I was good at it. That's the part people don't understand about disasters. They're never done by bad craftsmen. They're done by good ones, on the worst day of their lives.

The magistrate of Harrow Fen was called Doss. He ruled the river towns with a tax ledger in one hand and a gallows-warrant in the other, and when the flood year came and the granaries went to rot, he solved the problem by declaring the poor of three villages guilty of hoarding, and scheduling their trials all on one morning, in one courtroom, because hanging them in batches was efficient and efficiency, he liked to say, was justice wearing its work clothes.

I know now that there were a hundred ways to stop him that didn't involve the sky. Petition the crown. Bribe the guards. Burn the courthouse at night, empty. I know this because I have had eleven years to learn it, and learning it is the punishment.

But I was twenty-two, and Mother Casp had died that spring, and the sky over Harrow Fen had been building an argument for nine days, hot against wet, pressure against pressure, and every hour Doss's court sat in session another name went onto his list, and I stood on the levee road with my hands shaking and thought: *the storm wants to land anyway. Better here than on the fishing fleet. Better here than on the harvest.*

Better, I thought, aimed at the guilty.

Storms are arguments. And arguments do not care whom they convince.

I called it down on the courthouse at midmorning, while the trials were in session, while the gallery was full — full of gawkers, yes, full of Doss's men, but also full of wives waiting to hear whether their husbands would hang, full of children brought along because there was no one to leave them with, full of a clerk named Anselm Petch who owed me two coppers and always paid his debts, full of forty-one people who had done nothing worse than be in a room when the sky decided to take sides.

Doss drowned at his own bench. So did Anselm Petch. So did a girl of six whose name I made myself learn afterward, all forty-one names, and I wrote them in the back of my first ledger in ink I mixed with river water, and I have carried that page through every year since like a stone carried in the mouth.

They took my name for it. Not hanged me — the Marches needed storm-callers too badly, and mercy is cheap when it's also useful. They unmade Jules Vane instead. Struck the records. Gave the storm a new owner, a dead one, and sent me out into the world as nobody, with a warning carved into the law like a scar: *Jude Pemberton may not call rain, wind, thunder, or lightning within any border of any crown, upon pain of death.*

And I kept the promise. Eleven years. Through droughts I could have broken. Through fires I could have smothered. Through the wards falling and the stars going out and the Veil tightening its grip on a hollow king — I kept my hands in my pockets and my eyes on the ledgers, because a debt unpaid by me does not excuse a debt unpaid by anyone else.

That is the story in the jar. That is what I traded to the faceless woman so that the forgotten could be remembered.

And now everyone at the table knew it, because telling it aloud was the only way left to tell it, and the silence afterward had a texture like wet wool.

* * *

Sable broke it first, because Sable always breaks silence first; she cannot abide a pause she didn't forge herself.

"Forty-one names," she said. "You learned all of them."

"All of them."

"And you keep the page."

"Ink mixed with river water." I turned my cup on the table. "I'm not asking for absolution. I'm telling you so you know what you're traveling with. Because here's the thing that's been sitting in my chest since the monastery, since the well, since Pip heard that child crying inside Aldric's head—"

I stopped. Oren waited. He was good at waiting; it had been his profession.

"The Veil bargained with a terrified boy," I said. "A boy who said *anything*, who meant it, who couldn't have known what anything cost. And everything since — the stolen memories, the starless sky, the wards drinking a man dry — all of it grew out of that one desperate bargain, made by someone who thought he was saving people." My voice cracked on the last word, which was fitting, because it was a lie both times I'd used it. "I killed forty-one innocents to punish one guilty man, and I did it wide awake, at twenty-two, with every reason to know better. Aldric was a child. So tell me — *tell me* — why the kingdom's memory should belong to the guild instead of the crown. Why I get to decide the Veil is the monster. What right does a person like me have to judge anybody?"

Nobody answered right away. Pip climbed onto the bench beside me, which Pip only did when someone needed sitting-with rather than talking-at.

Then Oren set his knife down flat, blade toward himself, the way knights do when they're surrendering something.

"I served the crown for thirty years," he said. "I burned a village in the Vellish war because my commander told me it was a supply depot, and I believed him, because believing was easier than asking. There were people in it, Jude. I found out after. I got a commendation." His voice was gravel and old smoke. "So when you ask what right you have — I'll tell you what right I have. None. None at all. And I'm going to walk into that throne room with you anyway, because the question isn't whether we're clean enough to judge. We're not. Nobody is. The question is whether the erasing stops with us or doesn't."

"You drowned a courtroom," Sable said, matter-of-fact, counting on her fingers. "I've forged death warrants. Real ones, early days, before I grew a conscience — men hanged on my handwriting. Pip talks to dead people without asking permission. We are not a tribunal of saints, Jude. We're a *guild*. Of impossible things. You think impossible means pretty?" She pushed the empty jar aside and laid her hand flat on the big ledger, the one with fifty years of blank pages. "Impossible means doing the thing nobody clean would dare. Somebody has to hold the kingdom's memory. Better us than a parasite. Better us than nothing."

"But the storm," I said. "When it comes to it — and it will come to it, I can feel it coming like weather — I swore never again. And if I break that oath, I don't get to be sorry afterward. Sorry doesn't drain a courtroom."

"No," Pip said softly. They were looking at me with those eyes that saw the inside of things. "But you're not twenty-two anymore. And you wouldn't be calling it angry. You'd be calling it the way Mother Casp taught you — giving the argument somewhere honest to go." They touched my sleeve. "The Veil takes memories. You *give* them back. That's the difference. That's the whole difference, maybe. It holds everything hostage, and you — you read debts aloud and let people go free. Even the king. Even the worst parts."

Oren nodded slowly. "Grace isn't being innocent," he said. "It's being forgiven and getting up anyway."

I looked around the table — the forger, the knight, the medium, the blank ledger, the empty jar that had held my whole self until this morning — and I understood that they had absorbed the worst of me the way sand absorbs rain: completely, without ceremony, and leaving the ground firmer than before.

"Thirteen turns," I said. "One moon left."

As if the word had been a summons, the lantern guttered — though there was no draft — and outside the window, far off past the dead stars, the horizon flickered once, twice, like something vast turning over in its sleep.

And on the guildhouse doorstep, silent as delivered mail, lay a single black feather and a card bearing the royal seal, with four words written across it in ink that gleamed like oil:

*We know what you are.*
