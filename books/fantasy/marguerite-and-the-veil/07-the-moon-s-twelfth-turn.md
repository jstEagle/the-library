## 7. The Moon's Twelfth Turn

Sable had forged death warrants, papal bulls, a marriage license for two people who turned out to be the same person, and once — her masterpiece, though she'd never been paid for it — a royal pardon backdated forty years so a hanged man could be legally resurrected. But she had never forged the hand of the Veil itself.

She was doing it now, by lantern-light, in the cellar of a tannery three streets from the palace, and her hands were steadier than her heart.

"Again," Jude said, pacing. They'd been pacing for an hour. The floorboards knew their footfalls by name.

"It's right."

"It's wrong. Look at the wax."

Sable looked at the wax. The seal was a perfect impression of the king's privy signet, pressed into blood-red wax gone slightly amber at the edges to suggest age. Around it she'd written the command in the high ceremonial hand that only three living scribes could produce, and she'd killed two of them in the forgery by making the third one's flourishes slightly wrong. The words read: *By the Crown's own will, the Veil is commanded to yield up what it holds, that the debt of the realm be paid in full.*

"It's right," she said again, and this time she believed it, because she had done the thing she always did when a forgery mattered: she had stopped copying and started *being*. For six hours she had been a dying scribe named Corvel Haste, who believed in the Crown, who feared the Veil, whose wrist ached in exactly the way that made his terminal letters droop. She was still half him. Her wrist ached.

Jude picked up the decree and read it for the eleventh time. "It orders the Veil to give us the shards."

"It orders the Veil to *obey the Crown*. That's the trick. The Veil wears the king, so it has to take orders from the king's seal. It's never had to before — nobody's ever been stupid enough to command it." She smiled the smile she used when selling a bad coin to a good man. "Nobody's ever been stupid enough."

"And if it's smarter than stupid?"

"Then we're all hanged, and I'll have forged the warrant for that too, probably, out of habit."

Pip laughed from the corner, where they sat cross-legged with Oren's enormous hand resting on their head like a paperweight made of gentleness. The laugh was a little too bright. Sable noted it and filed it where she filed all the things she couldn't fix: under *later*.

Twelve turns of the moon. Two shards recovered from the ashes of the monastery and the well beneath it — no, one from the well, one from the ash, and the third from a beggar woman's locket, traded fair. Five still scattered. And the vault beneath the palace held two of them, according to the faceless woman's map, drawn in ink that only showed itself to people whose names had been eaten. Sable had looked at the map and seen blank vellum. Pip had looked at it and seen the way.

"The moon rises in four hours," Oren said. His voice did what it always did, which was make a fact sound like a promise kept. "We should eat."

They ate. That was the thing about the end of the world, Sable had learned: it kept regular meal times.

* * *

The vault of Aldric's palace was not beneath the palace. It was beneath the *hill* the palace stood on, and it was reached by a stair that the faceless woman's map showed threading through a wine cellar, a collapsed chapel, and a stretch of tunnel where the bricks had been laid by prisoners and each one bore a thumbprint pressed into the clay before firing. Sable counted the thumbprints as they passed. Four hundred and six. She counted because counting kept her from thinking, and she was not thinking about the decree in the oilcloth case against her chest, and she was definitely not thinking about the fact that forgeries worked best when the forger believed in them, and she had, for six hours, believed in the Crown's mercy.

The vault door was iron, older than the palace, older than the dynasty, and it had no lock.

It had a *question*. Letters moved across its face like ants, rearranging, and Oren — who had stood guard at this door for six years in another life, before the Crown discarded him the way it discarded everything it used — read them aloud in a voice gone flat as slate.

"It asks who commands."

"Then it's lucky we brought an answer," Sable said, and broke the wax.

The sound the decree made when it was read was not a sound. It was a *subtraction*. The air in the tunnel went thin and tasteless, and the lantern flames stood up straight and pale, and every thumbprint on every brick seemed to press inward, as if the wall were flinching. The letters on the door stilled. Then, one by one, they arranged themselves into a single line:

*THE CROWN COMMANDS. THE CROWN OBEYS.*

The door opened on darkness that smelled of cold stone and old ink.

"Nobody," Sable breathed, "has ever been stupid enough."

"Save the gloating," Jude said. "Gloat on the way out."

* * *

The vault was a long room of shelves, and the shelves held the kingdom's spare parts. Crowns of defunct provinces. A sword that had ended a war by being refused. Jars of sealed borders. And on a shelf at the far end, in a niche lit by a lamp that burned without oil, two objects that pulled at the eye the way a missing tooth pulls at the tongue: a sliver of glass that remembered a boy's birthday, and a coin that remembered his mother's voice saying his name.

Pip went to them the way water goes downhill. The child's hand hovered over the sliver and Pip's face went through three expressions Sable had no names for, ending in something old.

"This one's the birthday," Pip whispered. "He had a dog. The dog's name was— " Pip stopped, pressed their lips together, and shook their head. "It's not mine to say. Not yet. That's the rule, right? We give them back, we don't spend them."

"That's the rule," Jude said softly.

Sable took the coin. It was warm. Coins were never warm. She wrapped both shards in wool, in oilcloth, in the false bottom of her satchel, and her hands were doing the work automatically while the rest of her stood very still inside the forgery she had made, because the room had gone quiet in the wrong way.

Quiet like a held breath.

Quiet like a forger's client reading the bill.

"Jude," she said. "We're noticed."

"How can you—"

"Because it's too *easy*. Doors that ask questions should ask *better* ones." She was already moving, already repacking the case, her mind running the forgery backward through its own logic like a pick through a lock. "The Veil wears the king. The king's seal commands it. But a seal is only ink and wax, and the Veil has had fifty years to learn the difference between ink and *will*. I gave it an order it couldn't refuse, and orders you can't refuse get *audited*—"

The lamp without oil went out.

In the dark, something breathed. Not lungs. Pages. The sound of a vast ledger turning its own leaves, somewhere above them, beneath them, inside the hill itself.

Then the letters on the vault door — they could see them now, glowing faintly through the dark, because the dark had a texture and the letters were cut into it — rearranged one final time:

*FOUND.*

* * *

They ran.

The tunnels that had taken an hour to walk took a lifetime to run through. Behind them the vault door stood open, and it did not slam, because the Veil did not need to slam doors; it simply made every door in the world into its door. Sable felt it in the bricks under her palms as she steadied herself around corners — the thumbprints of the dead prisoners pressing up like questions. Above, faint through forty feet of hill, the palace bells began to ring, and they rang wrong, arrhythmic, a pattern with no pattern, and Oren said, in the voice of a man reading his own sentence: "That's the alarm for a guest of the Crown. Not an intruder. A *guest*."

"Meaning what?" Jude shouted.

"Meaning it's not hunting all of us. It's *inviting* one of us."

They came up through the wine cellar into the tannery yard, into the ordinary night, and the ordinary night had a moonrise coming, the twelfth, the penultimate, and the sky in the east had gone the color of a bruise deciding. Sable counted heads out of habit. Jude. Pip. Herself.

Three.

"Where's Oren."

Pip was standing very still in the middle of the yard, head tilted, listening to something with the ears the child used for listening to the insides of things. When Pip spoke, their voice was careful and small.

"He's walking. Toward the palace. He's not — he's not being dragged. It's not like that. It's like when you're asleep and you know you're dreaming but you walk anyway because the dream asked *nicely*." Pip's chin trembled and held. "It's using his name. The one it ate. It's calling him by the name the Crown gave him before it took it back, and he can't not answer. Nobody can not answer their own name."

Sable had forged a decree commanding the Veil itself, and it had worked, and it had worked *too well*, because now the Veil had read her handwriting, and had answered it the way any bureaucrat answers an order it cannot refuse: by complying, and by sending a bill.

The bells rang their patternless pattern. The bruise-colored sky climbed. And in Sable's satchel, wrapped in wool, the two stolen shards had begun — very faintly, very patiently — to glow, like coals deciding whether to become a fire.

Pip looked up at her, and the child's eyes were wet and furious and twelve years old and older than the hill.

"It left a message," Pip said. "For you. It knows your work. It said—" Pip swallowed. "It said: *one shard for one knight, moonrise tomorrow, bring the forger's hand or he forgets himself by the thirteenth turn.*"

Jude turned slowly, and Sable saw in their face the thing she'd been dreading since the well: not fear for themselves. Arithmetic. The cold, guild-ledger arithmetic of what a person is worth against what a kingdom is.

"Don't," Sable said. "Whatever you're tallying in there, Jude Pemberton, don't."

The moon broke the horizon, one turn from the last, and Jude said, "We have until moonrise tomorrow," in a voice that had already begun to forge a plan — and Sable, who could fake any hand in the kingdom, could not read this one at all.
