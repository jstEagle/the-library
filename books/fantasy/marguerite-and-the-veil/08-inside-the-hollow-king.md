## 8. Inside the Hollow King

Pip had been dead once, briefly, and it turned out dying was a lot like falling asleep in a strange house. You knew the rooms were wrong. You went into them anyway.

That was the trick of walking into a king's dreams. You didn't knock. You just stopped insisting on being awake.

Sable had drawn the door on the floor of the safehouse attic — a chalk circle, a keyhole, the guild's sigil rendered in flour-thick lines — and Oren's stolen knight's oath, folded eight times, sat in the middle of it like bait. Jude held Pip's hands. Sable held the lantern with the shuttered flame that didn't flicker.

"Rules," Jude said, for the third time.

"Don't eat anything. Don't promise anything. Don't tell it my real name."

"And?"

Pip grinned at them, gap-toothed and nine years old forever, or however long they'd been nine. "And come home before supper."

Jude's grip tightened. They were bad at goodbyes, Jude was. They treated every parting like an entry in a ledger that might not balance. Pip squeezed back and let go, and lay down inside the chalk, and closed their eyes, and fell sideways out of the world.

* * *

The king's dream began with a birthday.

It was a small one — a boy turning seven, maybe eight, in a kitchen rather than a hall, because this was before, when Aldric hadn't been anyone yet. There was a cake with too much honey. There was a woman laughing, flour on her knuckles, and Pip understood without being told: *mother*. The dream loved her so much it had kept her warm all these years, like a coal banked under ash.

Then the ash moved.

It came through the kitchen wall the way damp comes through plaster — not fast, not dramatic, just inevitable. The honey darkened. The woman's laugh stretched thin as wire. And a voice spoke from somewhere below the floor, below the foundations, below whatever a dream stands on:

*He is mine now. He gave himself. You are only what he forgot.*

Pip did what Pip always did with monsters. They waved.

"Hello," Pip said politely. "I'm looking for a knight."

The kitchen shuddered. The birthday boy looked up from his cake, and his eyes were the king's eyes, ancient and hollowed, and for one terrible moment he saw Pip — truly saw, the way only the dead and the dreaming can see a medium — and whispered, "Is someone there? Is someone *there?*"

"I'm here," Pip said. "Hold on. We're coming to get you back."

The kitchen tore away like wet paper.

* * *

After that the dream became a country of shredded things.

Pip walked through fields of half-remembered wheat where every stalk bore a name instead of grain. They crossed a bridge made of a single repeated betrayal — a friend's face, over and over, each plank a different year the friend had lied. They passed a corridor of doors, and behind every door a version of Aldric lived a life he'd been permitted to keep: Aldric the soldier, Aldric the son, Aldric the king on the one day a year the Veil let him feel his own hands.

And everywhere, threaded through it all, the well-voice. Not loud. Patient. The voice of something that had never once needed to hurry, because everything forgotten belonged to it by default.

*You smell of the ledger,* it said, finding Pip on the bridge of betrayals. *Little clerk of impossible things. Do you know what we keep here?*

"Stolen goods," Pip said.

*We keep what was given.*

"It wasn't given. It was bargained. A boy in a hole promised 'anything' because he was scared and his kingdom was dying. That's not giving. That's a child signing a contract he couldn't read."

The bridge creaked. Somewhere far off, the stars that weren't stars — the dream's poor copies, guttering since the wards fell — dimmed a little more.

*And you,* said the voice, silk over stone. *You who speak for the dead. How many of your ghosts gave themselves freely?*

Pip thought about that honestly, because Pip always did. "Some," they admitted. "Some didn't. That's why I carry them instead of eating them. There's a difference between remembering someone and consuming them. You've confused the two."

For the first time, the voice hesitated.

In that hesitation, Pip heard it — faint, rhythmic, human. Snoring.

* * *

They found Oren asleep in a memory of a siege.

Not fighting it. Caged in it. The old knight stood behind bars made of his own worst day — the gate he'd failed to hold, the order he'd obeyed, the faces — replaying in a loop around him like weather. Every time the memory reached its ending, it began again, and Oren, even sleeping, even dreaming, flinched.

Pip put their fingers through the bars and touched his wrist.

Oren woke like a landslide. "Pip." His voice was gravel and disbelief. "Child, you cannot be here. If it takes you—"

"It won't." Pip looked at the cage, really looked, and saw what the Veil had missed, because the Veil had never been a person and didn't understand people. The cage wasn't made of the memory. It was made of Oren's *refusal* to remember it — his shame, clutched tight, turned to iron.

"You have to give it up," Pip said. "Not to me. To him. To Aldric. It's his dream — everything freely given back belongs to the dreamer, not the parasite. That's the crack in it. That's why it keeps everything locked. Because a gift would kill it."

Oren stared at them. Around them the siege-loop ground to a halt, and the well-voice rose like floodwater.

*No,* it said, and for the first time it was not patient. *No. Nothing returns. Nothing returns.*

"Everything returns," Pip said, "if you open your hand."

Oren closed his eyes. And Pip watched a man decide, in the space of a heartbeat, to stop being ashamed of the worst thing he'd ever done — watched him take the memory of the failed gate and hold it out, not to Pip, but to the dreaming boy-shape standing lost among the shredded wheat beyond the bars.

"For you," Oren said. "All of it. Freely. So you know you weren't the only one who failed."

The bars didn't break. They simply stopped being necessary.

* * *

They ran then, because the Veil screamed — a soundless scream that bent the whole dream-country sideways, wheat-fields combusting into black snow, corridors of doors slamming open one after another like a row of teeth. Pip grabbed Oren's hand and pulled him toward the chalk-door feeling, the way home, the shuttered lantern burning somewhere above everything.

Behind them, the voice followed, no longer silk, no longer patient:

*Thirteen turns, little medium. One remains. Come to the throne room. Bring your ledger. Bring your storm-caller. I will show you what memory costs.*

And beneath the threat, almost drowned, almost tender, they heard the other voice — the boy's, the king's, seven years old forever in a kitchen full of honey:

*"Is someone there?"*

"Yes," Pip said aloud, waking, gasping, gripping Jude's hand hard enough to bruise. "Yes, we're coming."

The attic was dark. Sable lowered the lantern. Jude's face was pale as ledger paper.

"Pip," they said. "Your nose is bleeding. What did you find?"

Pip wiped their face, grinned the gap-toothed grin, and delivered it like a debt collected:

"The way to starve it. Everything it holds, if it's given back freely, stops being its. Oren's already free — look at him. And the Veil knows we know. It's invited us to the throne room tomorrow night. Last moon. It thinks it's a trap."

Oren, awake and hollow-eyed and lighter than Pip had ever seen him, said quietly: "Isn't it?"

"Sure," Pip said. "But it made one mistake. It showed me the boy. And the boy wants us to come."

Outside, the thirteenth moonrise began — thin, red, and already failing — and somewhere across the city, in a palace full of stolen years, something vast and patient started counting down.
