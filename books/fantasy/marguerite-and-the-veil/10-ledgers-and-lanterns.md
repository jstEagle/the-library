## 10. Ledgers and Lanterns

The stars came back on a Tuesday.

I know because Pip was keeping count of the days on the tavern wall in chalk, one small mark for each morning since the Veil shattered, and when the first constellation relit — the Weaver, her thread of seven stars spilling across the northern sky like something spilled and then lovingly mopped up — Pip ran through the guildhall shouting that it was Tuesday, that Tuesdays were lucky, that they had always known this and no one had ever believed them.

We all went outside. All of us, the whole impossible family, standing in the frozen street in our nightclothes with our breath steaming, watching the sky stitch itself back together constellation by constellation. The Hunter. The Drowned Girl. The Thirteen Sisters, who had been the last to go out and were somehow the first to return, as if they'd only been waiting for permission.

Sable cried. I'd never seen Sable cry before, and neither, judging by their expression, had Sable. They kept wiping at their face like it was a forgery someone else had made and they were checking the seams.

Oren put his hand on my shoulder. He didn't say anything. Oren had stopped saying much of anything since the throne room, since the storm, since we carried Aldric's body — living, breathing, but light as an empty coat — down from the dais and laid him on the guild's own cot because there was nowhere else in the kingdom that wasn't built on what had been done to him.

Pip stood in the middle of the street with their arms out and their face turned up, catching starlight like rain.

I thought about ledgers. I couldn't help it. That's the shape my mind makes: every wonder becomes an entry, every miracle a line item. *One sky, restored. Paid in full.*

* * *

The rebuilding took longer than the unmaking, which is the way of most things.

The kingdom had kept its records in two places: the crown archives, which the Veil had curated for sixty years the way a moth curates a wardrobe, and the memories of people, which the Veil had eaten. What was left was a nation of citizens who remembered their own names — mostly — but not always their neighbors', not always their debts or their deeds or the boundaries of their own fields. Deeds burned in one village and stood valid in the next. A widow in Marrowford discovered she had legally never existed, and therefore could not inherit her own house.

So they came to us.

They came to the Guild of Impossible Things with their half-memories and their grievances and their desperate, hopeful faces, and they asked if it was true, what the criers were saying — that the guild's books held everything, that the old ledger with its faded pages had filled back in overnight, every debt older than fifty years restored in ink so fresh it smudged.

It was true. We still don't entirely know why. Master Quillon theorized that the ledger had been drinking from the same well as the Veil all along — that the guild's oldest debt and the crown's oldest theft had been two ends of one transaction, and when the parasite died, everything it had swallowed drained back into the nearest open vessel. Which happened to be a book on my desk.

Sable theorized that the ledger simply got tired of being blank. Knowing Sable, I believe them.

The first weeks were chaos. Oren took over the door, deciding who saw the masters and who waited, and he was very good at it, because Oren has a face that says *I have been a knight and I am not impressed by your title.* Sable sat in the back room verifying documents against the ledger, hunting the forgeries — and there were forgeries within a fortnight, people trying to write themselves into history, invent ancestors, erase debts. It offended Sable professionally. "Amateurs," they muttered, stamping REJECTED across a claim of noble birth so audacious it was almost admirable. "No provenance work at all. No aging technique. If you're going to lie to a kingdom, lie *well*."

And me? I sat at the big table with the ledger open and heard cases.

That's what they became, though no one planned it. Cases. A miller and a lord who both claimed the same water rights, each holding papers the other said were false. Two brothers who couldn't remember which of them had promised to care for their mother, and whether the promise survived the forgetting. The woman from Marrowford, whose name — Bess — we found on page four hundred and twelve, in an entry from fifty-three years ago: *one name, traded for safe passage through the border fog, value incalculable, repayment deferred.*

"Deferred until now," I told her, and wrote her name back into the world while she held her daughter's hand and shook.

Here is what I learned, sitting in that chair: justice administered by consent looks slower than justice administered by crown. There's no decree, no guard at the door, no weight of the throne behind the ruling. There's just a table, a book, and people who have to look at each other while they talk. It takes days sometimes. It takes patience I did not know I had.

But when the miller and the lord left, they left having agreed — grudgingly, with a handshake that would have embarrassed a wet fish, but agreed — and neither of them needed to be compelled. That was the difference. Nobody had to be *compelled*. After sixty years of a kingdom held together by one man's erasure, I think we were all starving for the ungainly, stubborn, voluntary truth of people choosing.

* * *

Aldric abdicated in the spring.

He came to the guildhall himself, without escort, wearing plain wool instead of the crown — the crown was in a locked box under the floor of the vault, and three separate councils had voted on what to do with it and failed to agree, which felt right. Some things should be hard to decide.

He looked smaller than the hollow king had. Of course he did; the hollow king had been mostly Veil, a vast patient thing wearing a man like a glove, and what remained was just Aldric: sixty-odd years old with the eyes of someone who had lived every one of those years twice, once as himself and once as a stranger reading about himself in a book.

He stood in our doorway, and Oren looked at him for a long moment, and then stepped aside. Whatever passed between them — the discarded knight and the king who had discarded him — passed silently, and I suspect it will take them both some years to finish saying it.

"I've read the ledger," Aldric said, when we were seated around the big table with tea going cold in front of us. "All of it. My whole life, itemized." He smiled, and it was a real smile, tired and crooked. "You gave me back my mother's lullaby. Do you know I'd forgotten her voice entirely? And there it was, page nine hundred and six. *One lullaby, sung by firelight, traded for a kingdom's breath.*" His hands were steady. They hadn't been steady in the early weeks; he used to wake screaming with his hands clawing at his own head, trying to hold in memories that kept trying to leave, as if sixty years of habit outlasted the thing that taught them. "I owe this guild more than any ledger can hold."

"No," I said. "You really don't. That's rather the point."

He laughed — startled, rusty, like a door opening in a house long shut. Then he grew serious, and pushed a document across the table. His abdication, already signed. Beside it, a second paper: a proposal for a council, drawn by lot from every shire, with the guild's books as the kingdom's record and the guild's table as the court of last appeal.

"I can't fix what was done with my crown," he said. "But I can stop being the reason anyone kneels."

Pip, who had been uncharacteristically quiet, tugged his sleeve. "Are you sad?" Pip asked, with the directness of children and mediums.

Aldric considered it honestly, which most adults don't. "Some," he said. "Sixty years is a long time to be a king, even if I wasn't really there for it. But mostly—" he looked around the guildhall, at the mismatched furniture and the chalk marks on the wall counting the days and the smell of Sable's ink and Oren's pipe, "—mostly I think I'm going to find out who I am when nobody needs me to be anything. I haven't gotten to do that since I was eleven years old and a voice in a well asked me what I'd give."

"What will you give?" Pip asked.

"Nothing," Aldric said. "Not anymore. That's the whole lesson, isn't it? Everything remembered, nothing owed."

He left the papers on our table and walked home through streets where people recognized him and didn't bow, just nodded, the way you nod at a neighbor. I watched him go from the window and felt something in my chest unclench that I hadn't known was clenched.

* * *

On the first night of the new council — the lot-drawn, argumentative, gloriously ordinary council of farmers and fishwives and one extremely smug retired forger who swore up and down they'd entered the lottery fairly — I opened a fresh ledger.

The old one stays on the shelf behind glass now, pilgrims come to see it, which Sable finds hilarious and Oren finds tiresome and Pip finds useful, because pilgrims tip. Its pages are full again, every faded line restored, every small debt accounted. But a ledger is a living thing, and living things need room to grow.

The family gathered around the desk. That's what we are — I've stopped flinching from the word. Family. Not the one that exiled me for the storm I called on the magistrate's court, the one that weighed my grief against their law and found me wanting. This one. The forger who cries at stars. The knight who guards a door that leads to a table instead of a throne. The child who hears crying inside kings and walks into dreams to answer it. The family that heard my confession — *I killed innocents alongside the guilty, I drowned a courtroom, I am not clean* — and absorbed it without flinching, and said, in essence: *yes, and you're ours anyway.*

Oren poured four cups. Sable sharpened a pen that did not need sharpening, because Sable rituals are sacred. Pip climbed onto the chair beside mine and watched the blank first page with enormous solemnity.

"You have to get it right," Pip said. "First entries matter. Everyone knows that."

"Everyone knows that," I agreed.

I dipped the pen. Outside, the Thirteen Sisters burned bright and complete in the window, all thirteen, no gaps. Somewhere across the city, lanterns were being lit for the council's first session — hundreds of them, one per household, consent made visible, a kingdom agreeing to remember itself.

I wrote:

*Everything remembered, nothing owed.*

Then, beneath it, because a ledger should begin as it means to continue, I added the day's date, the names of the four witnesses, and one final line — an entry I suspect will keep this guild busy for the rest of our improbable lives:

*Received into care: one kingdom, slightly damaged, contents valuable. Terms: ongoing. Custodians: the undersigned, and whoever else proves impossible enough to join us.*

There was a knock at the door before the ink was dry.

Oren answered it, and I heard a voice I didn't know — young, frightened, urgent — say words that made the whole family go still around the table:

"Please — they said you collect impossible things. Something is writing on the inside of my skin, and it isn't my handwriting, and it started the night the stars came back."

I looked down at the fresh ledger.

The ink on my last line was moving.
