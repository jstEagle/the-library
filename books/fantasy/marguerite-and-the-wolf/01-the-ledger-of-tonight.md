## 1. The Ledger of Tonight

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its accounts in ink that never dried.

Wren Winterbourne had learned this on his first night as ledger-keeper, seven years ago, when he'd blotted a column of entries and found the ink bleeding *upward*, out of the page, pooling against his palm like something trying to climb back into the world. Master Corvane had laughed at him — a dry sound, like pages turning in an empty room — and told him that impossible trades could not be settled with ordinary arithmetic. A man who sold his shadow for a second chance at love did not pay in coin. A woman who traded her name for safe passage across the sea did not receive change. The guild's ledgers recorded what was given and what was taken, and the difference between them was called *debt*, and debt was the only currency the guild truly dealt in.

Wren balanced those debts every night, alone, in the counting room at the top of the guildhouse, where the candles burned without flickering because even fire was afraid to move here.

He was good at the work. Better than good. This was because of the other thing about Wren Winterbourne, the thing the guild had hired him for and never spoken aloud: he heard the dead.

Not ghosts. Nothing so theatrical. He heard them the way other men heard rain against glass — a constant, murmuring presence at the edge of things, voices pressed thin between the world and whatever came after. They whispered in the walls of old houses. They muttered in the grain of coffin-lids. And they clung, above all, to objects: to rings and keys and letters, to anything that had been loved or hated long enough to remember it. The dead were terrible gossips. They remembered everything and forgave nothing, and Wren had spent thirty years learning to let their voices wash past him like water, catching only what the ledgers required.

*Entry the four hundredth,* he wrote tonight, dipping his pen. *One memory of a summer orchard, sold by a farmer's widow to buy her son a death that would not hurt. Paid in full.*

The candle flames leaned toward him, listening. They always did that when he wrote. He'd stopped finding it strange around year three.

That was when the whisper came.

It slid up through the floorboards first — the dead often traveled through wood, through the long memory of trees — and then it was simply beside his ear, close as breath, though the counting room held no one but himself and the patient, watching candles.

*"Tonight,"* said a voice he knew.

He knew all the guild's dead by now. This one was Aldous Fenwick, a clerk who had kept these same ledgers ninety years ago and died at this very desk with a pen still behind his ear. Aldous had been whispering fragments at Wren for months — never whole sentences, never anything useful, just the restless muttering of a man who had died mid-calculation and could not stop.

But tonight Aldous said, clear as a struck bell:

*"Tonight. Check the date, boy. Tonight is written."*

Wren set down his pen. "Written where?"

The dead rarely answered direct questions. But Aldous had been a clerk, and clerks respected procedure. *"The Prophecy of Thirteen Moons,"* he breathed. *"Shelf nine. The black binding. Look up tonight's date, Wren Winterbourne, and you will understand why I never finished my last entry."*

The counting room went very quiet. Even the dead elsewhere in the walls seemed to lean closer.

Wren should not have gone. He knew that even as he rose from the desk, even as his chair scraped back across stone with a sound too loud for the room. The guild had exactly one rule for its employees, delivered by Master Corvane with the flat finality of a nail driven into a lid: *read nothing on shelf nine.* Not because the books were dangerous, Corvane had said. Because they were *accurate*, and accuracy was worse.

Shelf nine stood in the archive below, behind a door that required no key because the door itself did not wish to be opened by anyone who hesitated. Wren did not hesitate. That frightened him more than anything else — how easily his feet found the stairs, how naturally his hand fit the iron ring, as if some appointment had been made for him long ago and he was only now arriving, late and expected.

The archive smelled of dust and cold iron and, beneath both, faintly, of moonlight. Moonlight has a smell; most people never notice it, but Wren lived among the senses of the dead, and the dead noticed everything.

The black binding sat exactly where Aldous had said, spine uncracked, title stamped in silver that had not tarnished in a century: *THE PROPHECY OF THIRTEEN MOONS.*

He carried it upstairs. He told himself he would only check the index. He told himself a great many sensible things, and every one of them dissolved the moment he laid the book on his desk beside the open ledger and saw — before he'd touched a single page — that the ledger's ink had begun to move.

Not bleed. *Move.* The column of debts crawled like ants disturbed under a stone, rearranging themselves, and Wren watched with the particular horror of a man whose life's work is being rewritten in front of him as the numbers gathered into words, the words into lines, the lines into a single sentence that stood out from the page in relief, raised like a scar:

**The thirteenth moon turns tonight. The bone-carver hears. Let him be entered.**

"Entered," Wren said aloud. His voice sounded very small. "Entered *where?*"

Aldous answered from everywhere and nowhere, and for the first time in ninety years the dead clerk sounded afraid. *"In the book, boy. In the book. We are all in the book. Why do you think we never leave?"*

And Wren understood — in the way one understands falling, all at once and far too late — that the whispers had never been random. Every fragment Aldous had ever muttered, every half-sentence from the walls, every dead voice that had ever brushed past him in the dark: they had not been gossiping. They had been *reminding*. Keeping appointments. Calling him, patiently, decade after decade, toward a date written before his grandfather was born.

The dead had been waiting for him all along.

The prophecy book fell open on its own.

Moonlight poured through the counting-room window though the sky outside held no moon — poured *from the page*, silver and cold and rising like water over the desk, over his hands, over the ledger where his own neat handwriting was dissolving into blankness. The last thing Wren heard clearly was Aldous Fenwick, sighing with something like pity:

*"Mind the wolf. She bites, but she means well."*

Then the page took him.

* * *

He landed badly, which was generous of the ground to allow at all.

Moss broke his fall — moss deep and springing and wet with dew, smelling of loam and pine and that unmistakable cold-silver scent of moonlight, except here the moonlight was real, enormous, pouring down through a canopy of black pines in shafts so solid they looked carved. Somewhere water moved over stones. Somewhere else, farther off, something howled, long and low, and the forest went silent around the sound as if holding its breath.

Wren lay still and took inventory, as he did after any impossible trade crossed his desk. Fingers: present, ten. Bones: intact, as far as he could tell. Hearing: unfortunately, fully functional. Because the dead were here too. Of course they were. They murmured in the roots beneath him, in the bark of the pines, in the moss itself — a chorus of small voices, centuries deep, and every single one of them, when he strained to listen, was saying the same thing:

*She comes. She comes. Mind the wolf.*

He sat up.

She was already there.

At the edge of the nearest shaft of moonlight stood a wolf the size of a horse, gray as winter ash, her coat stirring in a wind he could not feel. Her head was lowered. Her eyes — and this was the detail that stopped Wren's heart and held it, suspended, between beats — her eyes were not a wolf's eyes. They were amber and ancient and unbearably human, and they were fixed on him with an expression he recognized from the ledgers, from a thousand entries of grief and grievance and unpaid debt.

Recognition. And beneath it, raw as an open wound: *hope.*

"You're late," the wolf said.

Her voice was a woman's voice, roughened by disuse, by years — by decades — of saying nothing to no one. She stepped fully into the moonlight, and Wren saw that around her neck, half-swallowed by fur, hung the broken chain of a crown.

"I've been waiting a hundred years," Queen Marguerite said, "for someone who could hear me. Tell me, bone-carver — can you also *write*?"
