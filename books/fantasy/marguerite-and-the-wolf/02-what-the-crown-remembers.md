## 2. What the Crown Remembers

The wolf did not move.

That was the first mercy of the forest — that it let him stand. Wren Winterbourne stood ankle-deep in moss the color of drowned coins, ledger still clutched to his chest like a shield, and the wolf watched him with eyes that were not a wolf's eyes at all. They were grey as slate before rain. They were patient in the way of people who had waited a very long time for an appointment.

"You're late," the wolf said.

Her voice came out of her throat wrong — too human, too careful, as if speech were a language she'd had to relearn from the outside. Wren's knees nearly folded. Not from fear. From recognition. He knew that voice. He'd heard it a hundred times in the margins of the guild's ledgers, in the whisper between entries, in the sound a page makes when it remembers being a tree.

"You're dead," he said.

"I was *unmade*," said the wolf. "There is a difference, and your guild profited by it."

* * *

Wren

She led him through the forest without touching the ground. That was how he understood what she was — not a wolf wearing a woman, but a woman worn down to wolf, the way a coin wears to a blank disc after a century of hands. Where she stepped, the moss remembered being palace carpet. Where she paused, the air remembered trumpets. The dead clung to her like burrs, and for once Wren did not flinch from their murmuring. For once, they were all listening to the same thing.

"Marguerite," he said, testing it. "Queen Marguerite. You're in the Prophecy of Thirteen Moons."

"I'm in the *ledger*," she said. "The prophecy is just the ledger's shadow."

They came to a clearing where a stone basin held water so black it reflected no moon. She stopped at its edge and looked at herself — at the wolf looking back — and something in her posture broke and reassembled, quick as a snapped bone set crooked.

"A hundred years ago," she said, "I had a sister who loved riddles and a kingdom that loved me badly. And I had a crown that was never mine by right of conquest but was mine by right of keeping — my grandmother's, and hers before. The Guild of Impossible Things came on a winter night with a contract written in ink that smelled of my own blood. They offered my sister a trade." Her muzzle lifted slightly. "Do you know what they traded?"

"No."

"Me. All of me. Every memory, every name, every grief and coronation and stolen apple from the orchard at Vell. They took the queen out of Marguerite and left the shape, and into the shape they poured a king. A hollow king, crowned with my crown, fed on my remembering. And the land—" here her voice did something terrible, went flat and young, "—the land has been starving ever since. A kingdom cannot live on borrowed memory. It digests nothing. It only grows hungry."

Wren's hands were shaking. Not from cold. From the sound beneath her words — the dead in her bones, thousands of them, her own selves, whispering in a language only he could hear. Fragments. A name half-erased. A lullaby missing its middle. A face that surfaced and sank like something drowning.

"I can hear them," he said quietly. "Your memories. They're not gone. They're *buried*."

The wolf turned her head. Those slate eyes fixed on him with an intensity that made the forest hold its breath.

"Say that again."

"They're in your bones. Bones keep what flesh forgets. I'm a bone-carver — it's my trade, and my curse, that I hear what the dead remember. And you, Marguerite—" he swallowed, "—you are the most crowded grave I have ever stood beside."

* * *

Marguerite

He was a thin, ink-stained man with sawdust in his hair and terror worn smooth into something almost like courage, and Marguerite wanted to eat him.

Not truly. But the wanting was there, old and animal, because he had said the one word no one had said to her in a hundred years: *Marguerite*. Her name, spoken aloud, landed in her chest like a key turning in a lock that rust had sealed. She felt doors open inside her that she had not known were doors.

"Carve them back," she said. "If you can hear them, carve them back."

"It isn't that simple." He crouched at the basin's edge, opened his ledger, and began — absurdly, horribly — to do sums. "Memory is a debt, and debts have interest. Every year you've been wolf, the fragments have sunk deeper. If I carve wrong, I carve false. If I carve true—" he looked up, and his eyes were kind, which was worse than cruelty, "—you will remember everything. Including the night it happened. Including her."

"Her."

"Your sister. The one who brokered the trade."

The forest went very quiet. Even the dead in her bones seemed to lean closer, waiting.

Marguerite thought of Isolde — of course she still had the name; names were the last things to go, the marrow of a person — Isolde, who had laughed with her mouth full, who had once sworn on their grandmother's grave that she would rather be unmade than see Marguerite harmed. Isolde, who had been promised, the guild's clerks would surely have written, *a life, a title, a place at the hollow king's side*.

"She sold me," Marguerite said, and heard her own voice come out steady, which meant somewhere inside her the grief had already finished its work and hardened into something she could carry. "My own sister signed the paper."

"She was twenty-two," Wren said softly. "The dead remember that too. They remember she wept while the ink dried. It doesn't excuse it. But the dead are honest in both directions — they keep the crime *and* the tears."

"Do not," Marguerite said, low, "make me forgive her tonight."

"I wouldn't dare. I'm only the clerk." He turned the ledger toward her, and by moonlight she saw his handwriting — small, exact, humane. "But if we're going to do this, we do it honestly. All of it. Grief first. Throne later, maybe never."

Something in her chest — some wolf-thing that had ruled her for a century — wanted to bare its teeth at *maybe never*. Something older, something queen-shaped, recognized the terms of a fair contract when it heard one.

"Begin," she said.

* * *

Wren

He worked by moonlight with a carving knife meant for whalebone and saints' relics, and what he carved was not flesh. He laid his palm against the great curved bone of her foreleg and *listened*, and what he heard he traced — not cutting, but calling, each stroke of the blade a question the bone answered.

A fragment rose: snow on a balcony, and a boy selling roasted chestnuts who bowed to her because he liked her, not because he feared her. She shuddered under his hand.

Another: her grandmother's voice, teaching her that a crown is a burden you agree to carry in public so others can lay theirs down in private. Marguerite made a sound like tearing cloth.

Another — and this one he almost didn't carve, because it was Isolde at nineteen, dancing badly at midwinter, laughing so hard she snorted wine, and Marguerite under his knife began to weep the way wolves weep, silently, hugely, her whole body a struck bell.

"I forgot she was funny," Marguerite whispered. "A hundred years, and I kept the betrayal and lost the laughter. That's the true theft, carver. Not the throne. They let me keep my hate because hate is cheap. It was the *love* they sold."

Wren carved on until his fingers bled and the moon leaned westward, and fragment by fragment the wolf in front of him grew heavier with humanity, her spine straightening, her eyes going from slate to storm-grey to something with lashes, though the shape held. She was becoming a queen the way ice becomes water — same substance, different law.

Near dawn, the last fragment of the night surfaced, and it was not a memory at all. It was a message, pressed into her hip-bone by whatever clerk had handled her sale a century ago — a clerk's note, hidden in the deepest layer, the way clerks hide things:

*Sale irregular. Buyer unknown. The crown chose its own wearer. Warn the thirteenth moon.*

Wren read it three times. His mouth went dry.

"Marguerite," he said. "The guild didn't make your king. The *crown* did. Your sister wasn't trading with the guild." He looked up at her, at the woman-wolf-shape trembling on the edge of restoration, at the pale line of dawn breaking through the trees. "She was trading with *it*."

Far off, beyond the forest, a bell began to toll — twelve strokes, slow as a funeral, and then, impossibly, a thirteenth, thin and clear and wrong, like a note played on a bone.

The twelfth turning had begun.
