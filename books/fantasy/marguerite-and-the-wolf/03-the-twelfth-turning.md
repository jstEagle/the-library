## 3. The Twelfth Turning

The twelfth moon rose the color of a wound, and I felt it in my bones before I saw it — all of them, even the ones that were not mine.

Wren said the palace would be quiet at this hour. He was wrong. Palaces are never quiet; they only pretend, the way liars do, holding their breath while they decide what to take from you next. We crouched in the hedgerow below the east wall and listened to it breathe: the slow suck of the thing on the throne drawing memory up through the stones like water through roots. A hundred years of borrowed lives, pooled beneath our feet. I could feel mine down there too — a cold thread of me, woven into the palace's foundations, holding up its walls.

"They built their kingdom on what they stole from you," Wren murmured. In the moonlight his face was all angles and shadow, a face made for carving. "No wonder it won't let you back in."

"Then we go in through the door it forgot." I touched the wolf's tooth at my throat — the one piece of myself the guild had never catalogued, because grief was not yet a tradeable commodity when I lost him. "The servants' stair. My sister had it bricked over after the coronation. Bricks remember being bricks before they remember being walls."

He looked at me then with that expression I was learning to dread — the one that meant he could hear something dead whispering nearby, and the something was saying my name.

* * *

The guild found us at midnight, as guilds do.

Not with soldiers. With a table. It simply stood in the middle of the gallery we were crossing, candlelit, set for two, and behind it sat an old woman whose face I knew from a hundred portraits in my sister's receiving rooms: the Guildmother herself, hands folded, patient as arithmetic.

"Wren Winterbourne," she said warmly. "Bone-carver, ledger-keeper, hearer of the unquiet. We have an offer."

"Don't," I said.

"She should hear it too." The Guildmother turned her eyes on me, and they were kind, which is the worst thing a creditor's eyes can be. "Absolution, carver. Every entry against your name, struck through. The debts of your apprenticeship, your father's debts, the little matter of the prophecy you've been reading out of turn. All of it forgiven. You need only do nothing. Walk away tonight. Let the thirteenth moon rise over a settled account."

"And her?" Wren asked quietly.

"The queen is not an account, child. She is a loss. And losses must be written off."

I watched him across that terrible little table. I watched him look at the pen they'd laid out for him, silver-nibbed, waiting. I thought: he has spent his whole life hearing the dead beg, and here is someone offering to make the begging stop forever. No one refuses that. No one sane refuses that.

Wren picked up the pen.

And reached past the Guildmother's contract to the ledger lying open beneath it — the original, the first one, bound in grey calfskin, the one I had smelled on the wind since the forest: ink and tallow and my own name. He tore the page free with the pen still in his fist, snapping the nib, and the candlelight went strange, and the table was just a table, and the Guildmother's kind face was a mask hanging on nothing.

"You can't write off a queen," he said. "She was never yours to lend."

Then we ran, and the palace woke around us like a body remembering pain.

* * *

Our guide betrayed us at the third turning of the stair, and I should have known. His name was Pell, and he had died forty years ago, and Wren had heard him asking, ever so politely, to be useful. The dead want to help; that is how they get used. Pell led us true through the servants' warrens, past the memory-vaults where the crown kept its interest payments — shelves of small glass hearts, each one beating faintly with somebody's childhood — and then, at the door to the throne room, he stepped aside and let the guards through, because the crown had promised him rest, and the dead will trade anything for rest.

They took me quietly, professionally, the way you take a debt. Chains of ledger-paper, each link inscribed with a clause. They did not gag me. The crown wanted to hear me scream in my own voice; it had been borrowing everyone else's for a century.

They put me before the throne, and I looked at the king.

He was worse than a monster. He was almost a man — a hollowed shape wearing a young man's posture, eyes like windows with the shutters painted on. The crown sat on his head the way a leech sits, fat and glistening, and I could see my memories moving inside it. My mother's lullaby. The smell of the orchard in autumn. My sister's laugh, before my sister learned to sell things. All of it circling in that golden ring, keeping the thing alive, keeping the king upright, keeping the lie breathing.

"Marguerite," the crown said, with my stolen voice, sweetly. "You came home."

* * *

**WREN**

They gave him a choice, because institutions always do. It makes the cruelty easier to file.

The Guildmother's voice came from everywhere now, from the walls, from the paper chains, from the ledger open on the throne-room floor: *Trade the gift, carver. Your hearing for her freedom. One impossible exchange, fairly entered. Sign, and walk out deaf to the dead, and we call it even.*

And Marguerite looked at him from her knees, and did not say don't, and that was worse than if she had.

Wren knelt beside her instead of the contract. He took out his knives — the good ones, whalebone-handled, the ones he'd used on saints' relics and sinners' knuckles alike — and he took her hand, and he said, "This will hurt more than the trade. I'm sorry."

"What are you doing?"

"What the guild never understood." He set the blade's edge against the fine bones of her wrist, where the smallest memories live. "They think memory is written in ink. It isn't. It's written in bone. It always was. I'm not trading anything. I'm giving it back."

He began to carve.

And every stroke of the knife cost him a voice. The first cut returned her mother's lullaby, and he lost the sound of rain on his workshop roof. The second gave her the orchard, and took away his father's last words. He carved the taste of honey-bread into the bone of her thumb and went deaf to the clerk who had whispered the first warning. He worked down her arms, her shoulders, the ridge of her collarbone, faster now, the crown shrieking above them, the guild's clauses unraveling off her wrists like burnt thread — and with every memory restored, another of the dead fell silent in his ears, until the world was very large and very quiet and there was only the knife, and her bones filling up with herself, and one voice left, the nearest, the dearest, saying *thank you* in a language older than words.

Marguerite stood up out of her chains a queen entire.

The last thing Wren heard — the very last, before the silence closed over him like deep water — was her heartbeat, steady as a drumbeat, saying what her mouth had not yet found the way to say.

Then the thirteenth moon broke through the throne-room window, and the crown turned toward her, hungry, and everything that happened next happened without him hearing any of it at all.
