## 4. A Hand Unbound

The thirteenth moon rose the color of a wound gone clean — white at its heart, red only at the rim, as if it had bled once and decided against it again.

Marguerite stood in the ruined courtyard of her own palace, and her bones sang.

Not with pain. With *herself*. Every memory Wren had carved back into her — the taste of honeyed pears stolen from the kitchen, the weight of her sister's head on her shoulder during the long winter of the plague, the first time she'd worn the crown and found it heavier than grief — all of it hummed through her like a struck bell that would not finish ringing. She was whole. She was *named*. And the thing on the throne knew it.

It had stopped pretending to be a king hours ago. The borrowed face had sloughed away like wet paper, and what sat beneath the crown now was a shape of hunger wearing the outline of majesty — tall, jointed wrong, its skin the gray of pages left too long in rain. The crown itself burned cold above it, a ring of stolen light.

"You are ledgered," it said, and its voice was every clerk who had ever written a price beside a name. "You were sold fairly. Signed and witnessed. The guild holds your receipt."

"I hold my bones," Marguerite said.

Wren stood at her shoulder, and she could feel him trembling — not from fear, she thought, but from the effort of *not listening*. For one hundred days he had heard the dead whisper through her, reading her memories out of marrow like a man deciphering letters written in a language only grief spoke fluently. He had carved them home with a bone-knife and paid for each stroke with pieces of his own gift, and now the dead were quiet in him, quieter than they had ever been since he was a boy, and he did not yet know if the silence was freedom or amputation.

The crowned thing rose from the throne. The courtyard stones cracked beneath it in a pattern almost like writing.

"The prophecy demands a ruler," it said. "Thirteen moons. Restore the queen or feed the land to me. Those are the terms. I do not make the terms. I only *collect*."

"Then you have read the terms badly," Marguerite said, and walked forward.

* * *

Wren watched her go and understood, in the way he understood things now — plainly, without the dead translating — that this was the part no ledger had predicted.

She crossed the courtyard with the moonlight running down her like water off fur, and the wolf was still in her walk, the long easy lope of something that had spent a century being hunted and had learned to move like weather instead of prey. She stopped three paces from the thing wearing her crown.

"I know what you are," she said.

It flinched. Actually flinched, the way a debtor flinches at a knock.

"You are not a king," Marguerite said. "You are not even a thief. A thief takes and keeps. You take and *starve*, because nothing you swallow stays down. You are hunger wearing an office. You are the shape the guild made when they needed somewhere to put everything they cut out of me — my memory, my claim, my name — because such things cannot simply vanish. They must go *somewhere*. So they poured me into a vessel and called it majesty." Her voice did not rise. It grew quieter, which was worse. "And my sister signed the bill of sale."

The thing's many joints shuddered. "Name yourself queen," it hissed. "Take the throne. The prophecy will bind us together forever — ruler and remnant, crown and hunger. That is the law."

"Yes," Marguerite said. "That is the law. And I have been governed by other people's laws for a hundred years, and I find I am finished with them."

She reached up — slowly, so slowly Wren forgot to breathe — and lifted the crown from the thing's head.

It came away easily. Of course it did. It had never belonged to the creature at all; it had been waiting, all this time, for the hand it was made for.

The thing collapsed inward like a fire losing its draft. What remained was small. A figure the size of a child, gray and translucent, clutching at itself — and inside it, faintly, a man's outline. Hollow-cheeked. Weeping without eyes. The king who had been hollowed, who had been a vessel too, who perhaps had never wanted any of it.

Marguerite looked at him for a long moment. Then she crouched, the way one crouches to a frightened animal, and said, in the voice Wren had heard her use on wolves and frightened clerks alike:

"You were fed on me. You did not choose to eat. I name you what you are: a man named Aldric Vane, third son of a minor house, who wanted so badly to matter that he let them empty him to fill the space. I name you, and naming is not law. Naming is just telling the truth where the truth can hear it."

The little gray king looked up at her. His mouth moved. No sound came out — but Wren, deaf to the dead and hearing everything else, saw the shape the mouth made, and it was *thank you*, or possibly *forgive me*, or possibly both, which in the end were often the same word.

Then the crown in Marguerite's hands went dark, and the thing that had worn it came apart into ash that smelled, briefly and impossibly, of honeyed pears.

* * *

The guild arrived at dawn, as guilds do — too late to stop the deed, early enough to file the paperwork.

They came through the courtyard gates in their gray coats with their gray faces, led by the Master of Accounts, whose smile Wren had trusted for eleven years. Behind them, porters carried the ledgers. All of them. Every book of impossible trades ever balanced, stacked in a tower that should not have fit through the gate and did anyway.

"Winterbourne," the Master said warmly. "You've caused a terrible mess. But the guild is merciful. Return the queen's ledger, sign a confession of theft, and we'll call it an accounting error. You'll keep your position. Your gift, even — what's left of it."

Wren looked at Marguerite. Marguerite looked at Wren.

"He can't hear the dead anymore," she said. "He spent it. On me. On *us*."

"Then he has nothing left to trade," the Master said, "and nothing we want. How sad." He turned to go.

"No," Wren said.

His voice came out steady. He was surprised by it. He had spent his whole life speaking in whispers, half-drowned under the dead's constant murmuring, apologizing for taking up air. Now there was room in his skull for his own voice, and it filled the space like a bell fills a tower.

"No," he said again. "You don't get to call it an error. Errors are accidents. This was arithmetic. You sold a queen and bought a kingdom and balanced the books, and the books balanced *because she paid*. So here is the last entry, and I'm making it myself."

He pulled the original ledger of her sale from inside his coat — the one he had stolen from the guild vault while Marguerite was being dragged away in chains, the one he had carried against his heart through every hour since — and held it up.

Then he set it on fire.

The flame caught strangely, hungrily, blue at the edges, and the Master of Accounts made a sound like tearing silk. Because the ledgers were not paper, not truly; they were bindings, and when one binding burned, all bindings felt it. Down the line of porter-carried books, covers began to smoke. Spines curled. A hundred years of impossible trades — the memories sold, the names unmade, the debts collected from people who had never agreed to owe — caught light one after another, like lanterns being lit in reverse.

The guild screamed in unison, which was the first honest sound Wren had ever heard them make.

And then there was ash, and dawn, and quiet.

* * *

They walked out of the forest at midmorning, side by side, and the trees parted for them the way trees part for no one.

Behind them, the palace stood empty and would stay empty; Marguerite had refused the throne with the same steadiness she had refused vengeance. "Let the land choose its next keeper honestly," she'd said. "Or let it learn to keep itself. Either way, not me. I've been an instrument long enough. I'd like to try being a person."

Ahead of them, the world. Ordinary, unledgered, unwritten.

Wren carried the Prophecy of Thirteen Moons under his arm — the old book that had swallowed him, now thin and light as a pamphlet. At some point in the burning night its final page had changed. Where the prophecy had once demanded a restored ruler or a devoured land, there was now only blankness, and in the blankness, in ink that had not been there before, in handwriting he recognized as his own:

*Here ends the account. Nothing further is owed.*

He touched the words once, to be sure they were real. They were. The first true sentence he had ever written, and no ghost had dictated it.

"You're crying," Marguerite observed.

"It's the smoke," Wren said.

"Mm," she said, in the tone of someone filing a lie under *charming* rather than *false*.

They came to the edge of the forest, where the trees gave way to a road, and the road gave way to a country that had never heard of either of them. Somewhere beyond the hills, a village was waking up. There would be bread, and work, and evenings, and no appointments kept by the dead.

Wren realized, with a wonder that felt almost like fear, that he could not remember the last time he had been listened to. Really listened to — not consulted as an oracle, not used as an instrument, not endured. But Marguerite had been hanging on his every word for a hundred days, and she was walking beside him now, matching his stride, and when he glanced at her she was already looking at him as though he were the remarkable one.

"So," she said. "Bone-carver. What does a man do, after he's finished a miracle?"

Wren considered. The morning was bright. The book under his arm weighed almost nothing.

"I thought I might learn to carve things that aren't prophecies," he said. "Birds, maybe. Small animals. Things that stay carved."

Marguerite smiled — the first smile he had seen on her that cost her nothing — and took his arm as if she had been doing it for years.

"Well," she said. "I hear the north roads are beautiful this time of year. And neither of us hears anything else."

They walked out of the page together, and the story, having no more use for them, let them go — which is, in the end, the only happy ending any ledger never wrote.
