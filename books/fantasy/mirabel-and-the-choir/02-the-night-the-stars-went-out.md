## 2. The Night the Stars Went Out

The wards failed at dusk, and Coral Ishikawa knew it before anyone told him, because his inkwell froze.

Not chilled — *froze*, solid as river ice, though the archive room held a comfortable fire and the evening was merely autumn-crisp. He sat with his pen hovering over an entry he'd been composing for twenty minutes — *one bottled thunderstorm, medium-violent, acquired from the widow Hask of Lower Fenwick in trade for three jars of preserved plums and an apology* — and watched the ink go stiff and dull in its well like something dying.

He touched the glass. Cold poured off it.

"Ah," Coral said, to no one, because there was never anyone. That was rather the point of him.

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its archive beneath a chandler's shop in the town of Marrowgate, three streets from the border, and Coral had spent eleven years cataloguing its holdings in rooms no one visited. The guildmasters came down on quarter-days to sign what needed signing. The rest of the time the ledgers were his: their order, their silence, their terrible completeness. He had recorded the bottled storm. He had recorded the debt of one forgotten name owed by a man who no longer existed anywhere but in Coral's careful hand. He had recorded things that would have gotten other men hanged, which was why the guild paid him in coin that never passed through visible hands and why, when people asked the chandler what was below his shop, the chandler said *cellars* and left it at that.

Coral liked his life. This was not a thing he admitted often, even to himself, but it was true. There was pride in keeping an orderly record of a disorderly world. Somewhere out there magic went feral and bargains soured and impossible things accumulated like driftwood against a seawall, and here, below the chandler's shop, every piece of it had a number, a date, and a shelf. The world could be survived if it could be indexed. Coral had built his whole self on that belief, quietly, the way you build a house over a fault line without telling the house.

Now his ink was frozen and the fault line was moving.

He climbed the stairs two at a time and pushed open the trapdoor into the chandlery, where old Bremmer stood in the doorway staring east with a candle guttering in his fist.

"You feel that?" Bremmer said.

"I felt my ink freeze."

"That's not weather." Bremmer's voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a man handling his own fear so it wouldn't spill. "Border wards just dropped. All of them. I can feel the quiet from here."

Coral stepped out into the street. Dusk was coming down violet and ordinary over Marrowgate's rooftops, and underneath the ordinariness was a sensation he had no better word for than *absence* — the way a tooth leaves absence behind, the tongue returning again and again to the gap. The border wards had run along the eastern hills for six hundred years. Every child learned the shape of them before they learned letters: a hum in the teeth, a faint pressure behind the eyes when you walked too near the boundary stones. Coral had grown up inside that hum. He had never once heard silence from that direction.

He heard it now. It sounded like held breath.

By full dark, the first reports came in from the watchfires, and they made no sense together, which was how Coral knew they were true. The ward-stones hadn't cracked; they'd gone dark, all of them, in sequence — not randomly, but in a pattern, west to east, like lamps being snuffed by someone walking a corridor. The pattern took four hours. Then the reports stopped, because the watchers on the far stones stopped sending them, and nobody wanted to write down why.

Coral went back below and did not sleep.

* * *

At midnight the sky went out.

He was at his desk when it happened, and later he would be unable to say whether he saw it or felt it first — whether the stars vanished or the world simply flinched. But he looked up through the archive's high slit window, the one built to let in exactly one blade of night sky, and the stars were gone. Not clouded. Not veiled. *Gone*, all of them, in the same instant, as if the sky were a page and someone had scraped the vellum clean.

The dark that followed wasn't ordinary dark. Ordinary dark still has depth to it, distance, the suggestion of things waiting to be seen. This dark was flat. It pressed against the window like water against glass. For one long moment Coral had the distinct, sickening impression that the sky was much closer than it should be, and empty, and aware of him.

Then his candle caught up with events and steadied, and the world resumed pretending to be the world.

In the streets above, dogs began to howl, and then, more terribly, stopped all at once. A bell somewhere rang twice and thought better of it. Coral sat very still in his chair with his hands flat on the desk, feeling the way a man feels standing in a house when the foundations shift — not fallen yet, not falling yet, just *moved*, everything half an inch from where it belonged.

He should have gone up. He should have gone to the guildmasters with his report, sealed the lower doors, done the orderly thing. Instead he found himself opening the drawer where he kept the copies he was not supposed to have made.

Every archivist makes them. Coral had never met the rule that forbade copying forbidden pages, but he understood it lived somewhere in the guild's unwritten law, right beside *record, don't read* and *keep, don't ask*. Eleven years ago, a junior then, cataloguing a crate of seized materials from a burned house in the capital, he had come across a single loose folio in a hand far older than any living guildsman's — a fragment of something called the Emberhold Ledger — and he had copied it before his conscience finished forming the objection. He'd told himself it was professional thoroughness. He'd known it was hunger. Record-keepers are not supposed to be hungry, and so he had folded the copy small and hidden it the way you hide anything you love and shouldn't.

He unfolded it now by candlelight and read it for perhaps the hundredth time, and for the first time it answered him.

The fragment was mostly inventory — the old ledger had been an inventory too, that was what had snagged him, a kindred soul across three centuries — but between the entries ran a passage in a tighter, more urgent script:

*...the binding holds while the fire holds. The fire is fed on light given freely: hearthlight, starlight, the small bright loyalties of a kingdom. Take away the feeding and the flame gutters. When the flame gutters, look first to the sky, for the crown will spend the stars before it spends the realm...*

*When the flame gutters, look first to the sky.*

Coral set the page down. His hands were steady, which surprised him. Inside, something was curdling — dread going over, like milk left out, into something harder and more useful.

Because the outage pattern matched. He had spent the evening telling himself the ward-failure sequence meant nothing, coincidence, noise. But he had copied that fragment eleven years ago, and in eleven years of idle hours he had, without quite admitting it, memorized it — and the fragment included a marginal diagram, a little chain of marks showing the order in which the old ledger claimed the outer lights had failed during some previous calamity, west to east, stone by stone, *like lamps snuffed by someone walking a corridor*.

It matched. Stone for stone. Four hours, west to east, ending at the sea cliffs.

Which meant this had happened before. Which meant it was happening again. Which meant the sky had not gone dark by accident; it had been *spent*.

And the guildmasters — Vess, Ondrel, the others — would know. They had to know. The Emberhold Ledger wasn't some stray folio that fell out of history; it was the guild's own founding record, the oldest book in the archive, and Coral had only ever been allowed to catalogue its crate, never its contents. They kept it locked. They kept it locked the way you keep a thing locked when the thing is a confession.

Above him, faintly, came the sound of boots — many boots, moving with purpose, in a building that had housed one nervous chandler for thirty years.

* * *

They arrived within the hour: five guildmasters, travel-stained, having ridden hard from wherever the quarter-day convocation had stranded them, and they sealed the archive with a speed that told Coral everything before anyone said a word.

Ondrel himself chalked the seal-marks on the trapdoor. Master Vess stood in the archive room among the shelves, and her eyes found Coral immediately — found him, weighed him, and moved on with a deliberateness that was worse than suspicion.

"The archive is closed," Ondrel announced, to the room, to the shelves, to Coral as an afterthought. "No entries. No retrievals. No inquiry into current events. The guild's position is that the phenomenon is under assessment."

"The sky," Coral said. "The sky is under assessment."

"The sky," Ondrel said, "is beyond the guild's remit." Which was such a magnificent lie that Coral nearly admired it. The guild's remit was everything impossible, and the sky had just become the most impossible thing in it.

Vess crossed to him while the others argued about seals and messengers. She was seventy, sharp as a paper edge, and she had taught Coral his craft — the numbering, the dating, the discipline of writing down terrible things in a level hand. She lowered her voice so only he could hear.

"You've been down here all night?"

"All night."

"And?" Her eyes flicked, once, to the drawer where he kept nothing, officially. "Anything... irregular?"

Coral had rehearsed lies his whole life. He was the guild's invisible man; misdirection was his native language. And he heard himself say, smoothly, "Routine. A bottled storm, a plum debt. Nothing irregular."

Vess studied him for a long moment. Whatever she saw, she filed it — he could see her file it, the small nod, the internal shelving — and she said, "Good. Keep it that way. Questions are more dangerous than answers, Coral. Answers you can lock in a box. Questions get out."

She turned to go. Then paused, and added, without looking back: "The stars will come back or they won't. Either way, the ledgers stay closed. That's not cruelty. It's how we've survived six hundred years of exactly this."

*Exactly this.* The words went through him like a splinter finding its depth. She hadn't said *things like this*. She had said *exactly this*. She knew the pattern. They all knew the pattern. The wards had failed in a sequence written down three centuries ago, and the people whose entire function was to remember had decided, collectively, to forget on purpose.

They worked through the small hours — chalk marks, wax seals, chains on the inner doors — and Coral assisted, because assisting was what he was for, and because invisible men survive by being useful. He handed Ondrel the sealing wax. He held the lantern. He wrote, when instructed, a single line in the day-log: *Archive sealed by order of the convocation. No further entries.*

His pen hovered over the line, and he thought of his frozen inkwell, and the flat black pressing against the window, and a kingdom's worth of small bright loyalties spent like coins from a dead man's purse.

He wrote the line. His hand did not shake. He was almost proud of that, and the pride disgusted him.

* * *

They left before dawn — all but Vess, who remained to oversee the seal, and who finally climbed the stairs to the chandlery as the sky began, impossibly, to lighten. The stars stayed absent, but the sun rose anyway, wan and gray, like a lamp burning low on its last oil. Coral stood at the trapdoor watching her go, and Vess stopped on the third step and spoke without turning around.

"You'll want to look. I can see it in you. I put it there myself, teaching you to care about what's written." She gripped the rail. "So hear me, boy. The last keeper who looked — his name isn't in the rolls anymore. Not struck out. *Never written.* We unmade him so thoroughly that I'm the only one alive who remembers he existed, and some mornings I doubt even that. That's what inquiry costs here. Not punishment. Erasure."

"Yes, master," Coral said.

"And Coral—" Now she turned, and her face in the gray light was old and tired and, he thought, afraid. "If you do look anyway. If you're fool enough, or brave enough, or hungry enough. Don't tell me what you find. Let me go on not knowing. It's the only mercy either of us will get."

She climbed out into the gray morning and was gone.

Coral stood alone in the sealed archive, in a building full of every impossible thing the realm had ever tried to contain, behind chalk marks and wax and chain, holding a line in the day-log that said *no further entries*.

The forbidden copy sat in his drawer, warm as a coal.

He thought about the nameless keeper, unwritten out of existence. He thought about Vess's fear, which was the fear of a woman who had already decided, decades ago, that survival was worth this. He thought about the sky, flat and close and spent, and about a sentence in a dead hand: *look first to the sky, for the crown will spend the stars before it spends the realm.*

The crown. Not the wards, not the weather, not the gods. The crown.

Coral took out a fresh sheet, dipped his pen — the ink had thawed sometime in the night, which he chose not to think about — and wrote, in his smallest, tightest, most deniable hand, an entry for a ledger that did not exist:

*Night of the blank sky. Wards failed west to east, four hours, matching Emberhold fragment, marginal diagram, exact. Guild response: seal and silence. Conclusion unavoidable: this has happened before, and they let it. The fire is guttering. Something is spending us.*

He signed it with no name at all — a cartographer's mark, a tiny compass rose, the signature of a man who mapped things he could not claim.

Then he unlocked the drawer, put the Emberhold fragment beside it, and began, methodically, in defiance of chalk and wax and chain and six hundred years of the guild's first law, to plan how a man with no official existence might get into the capital — and what he would need to take with him when he went.

Above, through the slit window, the blade of sky stayed black and starless, and waited.
