## 3. A Girl Who Sings to Locked Doors

The doors of the Cathedral of Saint Vellum had no locks.

Mirabel had counted them her first week — eleven doors between the orphan dormitory and the choir loft, and not one of them a lock, because the cathedral was old in the way that made people proud, and pride did not permit bolts. *A house of God keeps its doors open*, the choirmaster said every autumn to the new intake of children, most of whom had been left on its steps precisely because their parents wanted them kept somewhere. The doors stayed open. The wind came through. In winter the novices stuffed rags along the sills and pretended not to notice that the great west door, tall as three men, stood ajar all night like something breathing with its mouth open.

So it was strange, the morning Mirabel turned fourteen, to come down the north stair and find the refectory door shut.

Not locked — there was nothing to lock. Just shut, and holding, when she put her hand against it and pushed. The oak didn't groan. It didn't give at all. It held the way a held breath holds.

Behind her, the other orphans filed past toward breakfast, shouldering the door open without thought, and it swung for them easy as anything. Mirabel stood there with her palm flat on the wood, feeling the faintest tremor under the grain, like a plucked string still ringing somewhere below hearing.

She'd been humming. She realized that only afterward — the warm-up scale Choirmaster Doss drilled into them before dawn, do up to so and back down, the one she sang half-asleep while her hands were still tying her belt. She'd been humming it at the door, and the door had decided, politely, to stay where it was.

"You'll be late," said Perrin, tugging her sleeve. He was nine and missing two teeth and believed Mirabel knew things, which was the closest thing to a religion he had.

"The door's stuck," she said.

"It's not stuck for me."

She let it go. She ate her porridge. But all through matins she watched the doors — the sacristy door, the cloister gate, the little door into the bell stair — and noticed what she had spent fourteen years not noticing: that they moved differently around her. That when she passed the cloister gate singing under her breath, it drifted closed behind her like a cat following at heel. That the drafty corridor outside the choir loft, which howled all winter, went quiet when the choir sang and only quiet, never silent, as if the walls were listening through the stone.

She told herself it was coincidence. She was good at telling herself things; orphans get early practice.

* * *

Choirmaster Doss found her at the end of the week, in the loft, alone with the evening light coming through the rose window in slabs of red and gold.

He was not a large man. He was made entirely of edges — sharp elbows, sharp collarbones, a face like a folded letter — and his hands were the hands of someone who had conducted for forty years and considered the human voice a raw material, like ore. The other children straightened when he entered a room the way grass straightens after wind. Mirabel had learned, over nine years in the choir, to read him the way sailors read weather: by the set of his mouth, the angle of his chin.

Tonight the weather said *interested*.

"Sing the Vellum Sequence," he said. "From the top. Alone."

It wasn't punishment. That was the first odd thing. Punishment-solos were sung facing the wall, so the offender could contemplate their errors. This was sung facing him, and he pulled the high stool from the organ bench and sat, and folded his hands, and waited.

The Vellum Sequence was the oldest thing in the choir's book — older than the cathedral, the choirmaster claimed, older than the kingdom. Seven phrases, no words anyone could translate, a melody that climbed in places where melodies weren't supposed to go. The choir sang it once a year, on the Feast of the Founding, and every year Doss corrected the same six children in the same six places, and every year Mirabel sang it and felt, for about four seconds in the fifth phrase, like the inside of her chest had been opened and aired out.

She sang it. Alone, in the emptying loft, her voice going up into the red light and hanging there.

And the doors began to close.

She heard them — she couldn't not hear them, now that she knew what to listen for. The sacristy door below, swinging home. The cloister gate, latchless, settling into its frame with a soft wooden click. The great west door itself, three hundred years ajar, drawing shut by degrees, the hinges making a sound like a long slow sigh. By the time she reached the fifth phrase the whole cathedral had gone still around her, draftless, breathless, every opening sealed, and the note she was holding seemed to be holding them — the way a finger holds a spinning coin flat against a table.

She stopped. The silence rang.

Doss hadn't moved. His eyes were bright and fixed, and for one unguarded moment his face was not a folded letter but an open one, and what was written there made Mirabel take a step back.

Hunger. There was no better word. She'd seen hunger on the faces of the starving who came to the kitchen door in bad winters. She had just never seen it aimed at a sound.

"Do that again," he said quietly.

"The fifth phrase?"

"All of it." He rose, and his shadow went long across the floorboards. "Again. And this time, when you reach the fifth phrase, don't stop. Hold it until I tell you."

"How long—"

"*Until I tell you.*"

She sang it again. She held the fifth phrase, and the cathedral held it with her, and somewhere in the middle of it she understood that the doors weren't stuck or blessed or broken. They were *obeying*. Something in the old melody was a command, and something in her throat could speak it, and the building — wardless, lockless, proud old Saint Vellum with its open doors — was doing exactly as it was told.

Her chest hurt. Not like strain. Like a hook, set gently behind the breastbone, with line running off it somewhere she couldn't see.

"Enough," said Doss.

The doors sighed open. The evening wind came back in. Mirabel stood in the rose-light shaking slightly, and Doss looked at her for a long moment with that open-hungry face, and then folded himself away again, all edges, all business.

"Feast of the Founding is in ten days," he said. "You'll have the solo line. Be in the loft an hour before dawn each day. Don't sing the Sequence outside this room." A pause, thin as a razor. "Don't sing it to anyone."

"What is it?" Mirabel asked. "What does it *do*?"

Doss gathered his music case. At the door — the door that had shut for her and would not, she noticed, shut for him — he paused.

"It opens," he said. "And it closes. Everything depends on which end of the song you're standing at." And he was gone, footsteps precise on the stair, leaving Mirabel alone in a cathedral full of slowly swinging doors and a hook behind her sternum that had not, she was almost sure, been there that morning.

* * *

She practiced. Of course she practiced. Nine years in the choir teaches you that gifts are debts and debts are paid in rehearsal hours, and if Doss had finally chosen her for the solo line then some part of her — the part that had spent childhood watching prettier, louder girls get chosen — was singing with joy before her mind caught up and asked what, exactly, she'd been chosen *for*.

The dawn hours in the loft were cold and gold and hers. She learned the fifth phrase the way you learn a wild animal: slowly, carefully, one hand extended. She learned she could hold a door shut from thirty feet. She learned distance mattered less than attention — that if she *thought* at a door while singing, really thought, it heard her. She learned that the effect faded when she stopped, everything drifting open again, the cathedral exhaling.

And she learned, on the fourth morning, that she was being watched.

Not by Doss — Doss watched openly, hungrily, taking notes in a small black book he thought she couldn't see. This was different. On the gallery opposite the loft, in the shadow under the organ pipes, a shape that resolved, when she stopped singing and stared, into an old woman in chorister's grey — one of the retired voices, the ones who lived in the alms rooms and came to services like furniture, present and unremarked. Sister Orennet. She had a voice like a cracked bell and hands knotted with age, and she was looking at Mirabel with an expression that was not hungry at all.

It was afraid.

"Straight-backed girl," Orennet said, before Mirabel could decide whether to pretend she hadn't seen her. Her voice was a dry rustle. "Who taught you to breathe like that?"

"No one. Everyone. Doss—"

"Doss teaches you to spend." The old woman came forward into the light, moving like something careful of its own joints. "I'm asking who taught you to *keep*."

"I don't know what you mean."

"No." Orennet looked out over the nave, at the doors standing open in the morning air, and her fear made sense suddenly, the way a map makes sense once you find the compass rose. "No, you wouldn't. They never tell you what you are until they need you to be it." She turned back, and gripped Mirabel's wrist with fingers like dry roots, and her whisper was urgent and low. "Listen to me, girl, because I'll only say this once and then I'll be too much of a coward to say it again. When they take you to sing for the crown — and they will, it's why Doss has been weighing voices these three years like a fishmonger — when you stand in that hall and feel the pull, you remember that your voice is yours. Whatever they tell you the song is for. Whatever it feels like it's doing. The note comes out of *you*. You can bend it. Do you understand me? You can always bend it."

"Bend it how? Sister, what happens at the crown? What—"

Footsteps on the stair. Orennet released her wrist and stepped back into the organ-shadow, folding herself into furniture again, and by the time Doss appeared she was gazing out the window with the mild vacancy of the very old.

"Mirabel." Doss's voice had a new texture this morning, smooth and ceremonial, like silk over wire. "Wash and dress in the good cassock. Today you sing for the palace."

* * *

The summons had come at midnight, Mirabel learned — a palace rider at the cathedral's west door, which was, for the first time in living memory, shut. The rider had hammered and shouted until the porters unbarred it, and had delivered a card heavy with gilt edging: *His Majesty's pleasure requires the presence of the Cathedral Choir's principal treble, at the hour of Terce.*

Principal treble. Her name, spelled correctly, on palace card stock. Doss carried the card like a relic and looked at Mirabel, all the way across the refectory, with the expression of a man watching an investment ripen.

The palace was twenty minutes' walk through streets still hung with the panic of the dark nights — lanterns burning at noon, chalk wards scrawled over thresholds by frightened householders, a knot of people outside the astronomer's tower arguing about stars. Mirabel saw none of it clearly. She walked in a bubble of her own pulse, rehearsing scales in her head, touching the place behind her breastbone where the hook lived.

The throne room was smaller than the cathedral's nave and colder than either. Banners hung motionless — no windows open here, no drafts, the air thick and used. Courtiers lined the walls in colors Mirabel had only ever seen on altar cloths. And at the far end, on a throne that was more iron than gold, sat King Aldous.

Except.

Mirabel had seen the king twice before, from a distance, at processions — a broad man, silvering, with a laugh people described as famous. The man on the throne had the right face and the right breadth, but he sat wrong, the way a coat sits wrong on a different-shaped man. And when he turned his head to look at the entering choir, the movement had a lag to it, a half-beat delay, as though the head were remembering how necks worked. His eyes found Mirabel among the rows of children and stayed on her with an attention that had no warmth in it at all. Recognition, maybe. Or inventory.

"Your Majesty," Doss announced, bowing to a depth Mirabel had never seen him achieve, "the choir of Saint Vellum presents its principal treble."

"Let her sing," said the king.

The word *her* arrived a half-second after his mouth finished moving. Mirabel felt it like a stone dropped in her stomach. No one else seemed to notice. The courtiers breathed; the banners hung; the world proceeded, agreeing not to see.

They gave her the Vellum Sequence, of course. Doss had known — had probably arranged it, had perhaps been arranging it for years, weighing voices like a fishmonger. The court hushed. The cold room listened. And Mirabel, fourteen years old, in a borrowed good cassock, opened her mouth in the house of the thing wearing her king, and began to climb the old melody into whatever was waiting at the top of it.

The hook behind her breastbone pulled taut.

Somewhere far above the ceiling, far above the dead blank sky, she felt — distinctly, undeniably — something answer.
