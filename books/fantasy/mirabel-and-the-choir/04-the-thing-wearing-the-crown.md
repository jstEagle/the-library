## 4. The Thing Wearing the Crown

The tribute went into three crates and a lie.

Two crates held what the capital expected: preserved impossibilities, catalogued and harmless. A feather that remembered being a wing. A compass that pointed at whatever you'd lost most recently — the guild had priced it high and shipped it grudgingly, because the court's collectors loved a toy more than a truth. The third crate held the real tribute, the one no receipt would ever mention: a single ledger bound in grey calfskin, its pages recording thirty years of small payments the crown had made to keep certain wards fed, written in Coral's own hand, in ink that could not be forged or erased.

Coral had packed it himself, wrapped it in oilcloth, and sat on the crate lid while the carter nailed it shut, because he did not trust anyone else to understand what they were carrying.

"You look like a man escorting his mother to her execution," Vess said, watching from the archive door.

"I'm escorting a ledger."

"To the king."

"To a warehouse," Coral said. "The king will never see it. Some under-clerk will sign for it, some steward will shelve it, and in forty years someone will burn it to heat a room." He said it lightly, because that was the ritual, because that was how the guild survived: everything important passed through the world disguised as nothing.

Vess didn't smile. She was old the way driftwood is old, weathered down to the hard parts, and she looked at him a long moment before she spoke. "You've been asking questions."

"I've been doing my job."

"Your job is to write things down." She came closer, and her voice dropped to the register she used for the oldest rules. "Not to read them back. Not to wonder. Write, seal, forget. That's the trade we make, Coral. We keep the realm's secrets safe by never once looking at them straight."

He thought of the entry he didn't remember writing. *One crown, kept alive. One fire, spent keeping it.* He thought of the blank sky where the stars should have been, eleven nights now, and the way the guildmasters had sealed the star-charts and called it a printing error.

"Yes, Master Vess," he said.

She held his gaze a moment longer, then pressed something into his hand — a travel chit, stamped with the guild's seal. "Deliver the tribute. Sign nothing you haven't read twice. And Coral—" she paused at the door, half-turned away, "—in the capital, keep your eyes on your feet. Court is a place where looking up gets people noticed, and noticed is worse than dead."

* * *

The road to the capital took nine days, and Coral spent them mapping.

It was habit more than duty. The Guild of Impossible Things paid him to record, but no one had ever forbidden him from drawing, and so he drew: the way the wards along the King's Road flickered at dusk like lamps running out of oil; the way birds flew in straight lines now instead of following the old songlines, as if the invisible architecture of the world had been taken down for repairs; the way travelers no longer swore by the stars, because there were no stars to swear by, only a smooth black ceiling that gave back nothing.

In the town of Harrow's Ford, an innkeeper told him the priests said the darkness was penance. In the same town, a child told him the darkness was a lid, and something underneath was holding it closed from below. Coral wrote both explanations in his field book, side by side, and marked neither true nor false. That was the guild way. But his hand hesitated over the child's version longer than it should have.

On the ninth day the capital rose out of the winter fog, white walls and slate roofs, the cathedral spire stitched through with scaffolding where masons worked to replace stones that had cracked all in one night, eleven nights ago, when the wards failed. Above the palace, where the royal banners hung, the air had a faint shimmer, like heat off a summer road, though it was deep winter. Coral had seen that shimmer before, in ledgers. It was the signature of a warding paid for with something other than stone and spellwork.

The guild's tributary office stood in the shadow of the cathedral, a narrow building squeezed between a chandler and a moneylender, and Coral delivered his crates to a clerk with ink-stained fingers and eyes that never quite focused. The clerk signed for the two display crates without reading the manifest. When Coral mentioned the third crate — the grey calfskin one — the clerk's eyes focused all at once, sharp as needles.

"That goes to the undercroft," the clerk said. "Separate receipt. You'll wait."

Coral waited four hours in a room with no windows and a clock that ticked unevenly, as if it too were lagging behind something. When the under-steward finally came, he was a soft-voiced man who counted the oilcloth folds twice and made Coral sign a receipt written in cipher. Then, unexpectedly:

"You'll attend court tonight," the under-steward said. "Tribute-bearers are presented at the winter revels. It's tradition."

"It's not in the manifest."

"It's in the tradition." The man smiled without warmth. "The crown likes to see who serves it. All of it. Even the parts that think they're unseen." He said it pleasantly, the way you'd mention weather, and Coral felt the words settle somewhere beneath his ribs like a swallowed stone.

* * *

They dressed him in guild grey and put a paper rosette in his hat, and then a footman with a face like a closed door walked him through corridors that got warmer and quieter the deeper they went, until sound itself seemed to be paid some kind of toll. The winter court gathered in the Long Gallery, a hall built to make everyone in it feel briefly magnificent and permanently small. Chandeliers burned with a light that was too steady — flame that didn't flicker, which Coral knew from the ledgers meant flame that wasn't entirely fire.

He kept his eyes on his feet, as instructed. He catalogued: marble, gilt, two hundred pairs of shoes, the smell of wax and roasted chestnuts and, underneath it, faintly, something like a snuffed candle — that same burnt-out smell he'd caught in the archive the night the stars went dark.

Then a herald's voice rang out, naming the next arrival, and the whole room turned like a tide going out, and Coral — against instruction, against every rule Vess had ever taught him — looked up.

King Aldous entered the Long Gallery.

He was not what Coral expected. The ledgers described a king in the language of debts and bindings, all weight and dread, and Coral had imagined something heavy, something wrong-shaped. But Aldous was handsome in the way a coin is handsome: precise, minted, identical on both sides. Tall, silver at the temples, dressed in winter blue with the crown sitting on his head like it had grown there. He moved through the crowd the way a keel moves through water, parting it without effort, smiling exactly enough at exactly the right faces.

And his reflection lagged.

Coral saw it in the long mirror that lined the gallery's eastern wall — saw it because the mirror was positioned so that the king crossed its view at an angle, and for three full seconds Coral had a clean sightline of both the man and his image.

The man raised his hand to greet the Duchess of Vale. The reflection raised its hand a half-second later.

The man turned his head toward the musicians. The reflection followed, late, catching up with a small smooth rush, like water finding its level.

Nobody else seemed to notice. Two hundred courtiers, trained from birth to watch the king, and not one of them glanced at the mirror — or if they did, their eyes slid off it the way eyes slide off a ledger page you've been ordered not to read. Coral understood, with a cold settling certainty, that this was not a flaw in the courtiers. It was a feature of the king. Whatever moved inside that shape was still learning it, still rehearsing it, thirty years into the performance and still half a beat behind.

*One crown, kept alive.*

The oldest debt in the oldest ledger. Coral had copied that page as an apprentice, forbidden to understand it, and he had copied the payment schedule too: *one voice per generation, drawn from the singing orders; one fire, sustained; one shape, maintained.* He'd thought *shape* meant the crown itself. Standing in the Long Gallery with his heart going slow and enormous in his chest, he understood it meant the man wearing it.

Aldous stopped ten feet from Coral. It was part of the ceremony — the king paused before each tribute-bearer, received a bow, offered a word. The footman murmured Coral's name and guild, and Aldous turned those minted features upon him, and smiled.

"Grey and quill," the king said. His voice was warm, resonant, pitched to carry exactly as far as it needed to and no farther. "The record-keepers. You men see everything, don't you?"

"Only what's written down, Majesty," Coral heard himself say.

"And do you write it all down?" The king's eyes were brown and ordinary and utterly without anything behind them, like windows of a house where the lights are lit in rooms no one lives in. "Every little thing? Even the things people would rather you forgot?"

"Especially those, Majesty."

Something flickered across the king's face — not anger, not amusement. Recognition, maybe. The look of a thing meeting another thing that knows what it is. For a moment the warmth in the voice thinned, and what was underneath was neither warm nor cold but *patient*, the patience of a debt collector who has never once been in a hurry.

"Then write this down," Aldous said softly, and leaned close, and for a moment his reflection in the gallery mirror caught up perfectly, as if the performance required precision only here, only now. "Tell your masters the account is current. Tell them the payments continue. And tell them—" the pause stretched, delicate as a wire, "—the crown remembers what it is owed."

He straightened. The smile returned, minted and flawless. He moved on to the next tribute-bearer, and the room breathed again, and Coral stood very still with his hands folded, feeling the message sink into him like ink into cloth.

Behind the king, in the eastern mirror, the reflection was still bowed.

It straightened a full second after the man did.

* * *

They let the tribute-bearers go at midnight. Coral walked back through the fog-thick streets with the guild's paper rosette still in his hat and his mind moving faster than his feet, laying the night out like a map and marking the places where it didn't fit.

The lagging reflection. The flame that wouldn't flicker. The shimmer above the palace that matched the ward-signatures in thirty years of tribute ledgers. *One voice per generation, drawn from the singing orders.* Eleven nights ago the border wards had collapsed and the stars had gone out, and the guildmasters had sealed the charts and called it a printing error, and tonight the crown had told him, in front of two hundred witnesses who heard nothing, that the account was current and the payments would continue.

Payments. Voices. Singing orders.

There was a choir in the capital. Every capital had one — the orphan choristers of the cathedral, famous for voices that could hold a note steady as a plumb line. Coral had filed their existence years ago, in a ledger of cultural assets, between a bridge that hummed in high wind and a well that answered questions. He'd never thought about them since. Now the entry surfaced whole, and beneath it, in his own handwriting, a marginal note he had no memory of making:

*Ask the choir what they remember.*

His blood went cold, and then hot. Someone — himself, some version of himself, some night he couldn't recall — had already started down this road. Had already been warned, maybe. Had already been edited.

Vess's voice in his memory: *record-keepers who ask questions become records.*

Coral stopped at the corner where the tributary office's lamp glowed dull through the fog, and he took out his field book, and by the light of that lamp he wrote, in plain unsealed ink, the first honest sentence he had ever committed outside the guild's binding script:

*The king's reflection is late. The debt has a face. I intend to find out whose.*

He looked at the words a long time. There was no taking them back; even unsealed ink changed a man. Then he closed the book, and turned his collar up against the cold, and began — quietly, methodically, the way he did everything — to plan how a man nobody had ever noticed might get close enough to a cathedral choir to ask a girl with a steady voice what she remembered.

Above the palace, the shimmer held. Beneath the black ceiling where the stars should have been, something patient waited on its payments.

And somewhere in the capital, in an orphan loft above the nave, a choirgirl was asleep — the last note of an old song, not yet knowing she'd been written into the score.
