## 5. What the Choir Remembers

The winter court smelled of tallow and orange peel and something underneath both — something old, like a cellar that had been shut too long. Mirabel stood in the antechamber of the Hall of Audiences with forty other choristers, all of them dressed in the same dove-grey wool, all of them breathing the same careful breaths, and she tried to remember that she had wanted this.

She had wanted this. She had told herself so every night for a week, lying in the narrow dormitory bed with Sera snoring two cots over: *you wanted this, you asked to be noticed, well, now you've been noticed.* The choirmaster had come to her personally. Not a note passed through an older girl. Not a summons chalked on the practice-room board. Master Corvane himself, standing in the doorway of the refectory with his long hands folded, saying her name like it was something he'd been keeping in his pocket.

"Mirabel. You'll sing for the crown at midwinter court."

She had said yes. Of course she had said yes. Girls in the orphan choir did not say no to the crown; girls in the orphan choir mostly did not get asked anything at all.

Now she stood in the antechamber while a chamberlain with a voice like dry paper explained the protocol — eyes down when the king entered, no singing until signaled, and above all, *do not look directly at His Majesty's face for longer than courtesy requires.* Mirabel thought that was an odd instruction. Then she thought about what the other choristers whispered, and decided it was probably just court manners. Courts were full of odd manners. That was practically their whole trade.

The doors opened, and they processed in.

The Hall of Audiences was built to make a person feel small, and it worked. Pillars of grey granite went up and up into shadow; banners hung from the clerestory like the skins of enormous animals; and at the far end, on a dais under a canopy of cloth-of-gold gone dull with age, sat the throne, and on the throne sat the king.

King Aldous. Thirty years on the throne. Mirabel had seen his face on the coin in her palm every day of her life, the strong jaw, the level brow, the crown rendered in such small detail it was only a jagged suggestion of metal. The real thing was bigger than the coin suggested. It sat low on his head, close-fitting, dark — not gold, she realized as they drew near enough to see. Not gold at all. A grey metal with a faint oily shine, like iron that had been wept on.

They arranged themselves in the crescent the chamberlain had drilled into them, and Mirabel found herself in the second row, which meant she could see past the shoulders of the taller girls, which meant she could see the king.

He was looking at them already. At her, she thought, then corrected herself — he was looking at the choir, obviously, that was what kings did when choirs sang for them. He had a pleasant face. That was the first thing. A kind, tired, ordinary face, the face of a man who might run a counting house or judge a pie contest. And he was smiling, and the smile reached his eyes, and everything about him was exactly right.

Everything about him was exactly right, and that was the wrong thing about him.

Mirabel couldn't have said why she felt that. It was a feeling like the one she got when a note in someone else's voice sat a hair off true — not wrong enough to name, wrong enough to itch. She watched him fold his hands. She watched him incline his head toward the choirmaster. And she saw, very clearly, in the polished black floor beneath the dais, his reflection.

His reflection moved a half-second after he did.

She blinked. The floor was black marble, ancient, scuffed by thirty years of courtiers' shoes; light came from a hundred candles and none of them steady. Reflections lied in rooms like this. Everyone knew that.

But she kept watching, because watching things was what she did instead of talking, and the next time the king lifted his hand to his chin, the reflection lifted its hand a heartbeat late, smooth as syrup, and caught up only when the movement finished. Like a man walking slightly behind his own footsteps. Like sound arriving after lightning.

*Don't look at His Majesty's face for longer than courtesy requires.*

Maybe the chamberlain hadn't been talking about manners.

Master Corvane raised his hands. The choir breathed in, forty pairs of lungs in perfect unison because he had beaten unison into them for years, and the opening note of the Advent anthem rose into the vaulted dark.

Mirabel sang. She always sang; her body knew how even when her mind wandered. The anthem was an old one, older than the choir's newest members, older maybe than the cathedral itself — the harmonies had that worn-smooth quality of music nobody remembered composing. She let her voice settle into the alto line, tucked under Sera's soprano, and for eight bars everything was ordinary. Candles, cold stone, the ache in her feet from standing.

Then the ninth bar came, and the crown looked at her.

There was no better way to put it. She felt it happen — a shift in the air of the hall, a pressure change like the moment before thunder, and then a sensation against her sternum that she could only describe as a hook. Not pain, exactly. *Tension.* As if somewhere inside her chest a fisherman's line had gone taut, and at the far end of the line, patient and immense, something had begun to reel.

Her voice kept coming out. That was the horrible part. Her mouth opened, her breath flowed, the notes emerged clean and true — truer than true, richer than she had ever managed in practice — but they weren't entirely hers anymore. It was like being played. Like discovering she was an instrument that someone else had picked up, and the someone else was very skilled and very hungry.

The anthem climbed toward its high phrase, the one where the altos held a suspended note while the sopranos resolved above them, and the pull strengthened. Mirabel could feel her own voice being drawn out of her in a long silver thread, feel the hall drinking it. She looked desperately sideways. Sera was swaying slightly, her eyes glazed, her face flushed with pleasure — whatever the hook was, it felt good, or felt like nothing at all, to everyone else. Forty children singing their hearts out and only one of them feeling the line go taut.

Only one of them who mattered, maybe.

*Let go,* she told herself. *It's just singing. Singing is safe. Singing is the one thing—*

But singing had never felt like this before. Singing had always been hers, the one possession no orphanage could inventory. And now something on the dais was holding the other end of it.

In the ninth bar of the second verse — she would remember this later, the precision of it, how her terror kept perfect count — she did the only rebellious thing her training allowed. She bent the note. Just a hair. A quarter-tone flat, the smallest sin a chorister can commit, the kind of error Master Corvane punished with extra hours of scales.

The hook slackened.

Not released — slackened, the way a fishing line goes loose when the fish stops fighting, and she felt the crown's attention slide off her like water off oilcloth and settle instead on Sera, on the row behind, on the whole sweet unsuspecting body of the choir. Mirabel sang flat for the rest of the anthem. Every note, deliberately, secretly, a hair below true, and if Master Corvane noticed he was too deep in the performance to show it, and the hall heard only a choir singing beautifully, and the crown drank and drank and left her alone.

When the last chord faded, the king applauded.

His hands came together softly, twice, three times, and the sound was correct and warm and generous, and Mirabel watched his reflection applaud a half-second later and felt the floor tilt under her certainty. Because here was the thing she couldn't stop noticing, the thing that made her stomach drop through the marble: the reflection wasn't smiling.

The king smiled. The reflection did not. It just watched, with the king's borrowed face gone blank and attentive, the way a mask watches when the wearer sets it down.

Then she blinked, and the reflection was smiling again, and the chamberlain was dismissing them, and forty choristers bowed and processed out of the Hall of Audiences into the colder, kinder dark of the corridor, and Mirabel's chest still ached where the line had been.

* * *

"You're white as whey," Sera said, catching her arm in the cloister afterward. "Was it the king? Everyone says seeing him up close is overwhelming."

"It was overwhelming," Mirabel agreed, because it was easier than the truth.

They were back at the cathedral by late afternoon, the choir dismissed to supper, and Mirabel slipped away from the refectory line with bread hidden in her sleeve — an old habit, hoarding food, one the matrons had never trained out of her — and went looking for Brother Anselm.

Anselm was not technically a chorister anymore. He had been, sixty years ago, before his voice broke and never quite reassembled; now he was the one who swept the choir loft, tuned the little portative organ nobody used, and sat in the corner of the rehearsal room during evening practice with his hands folded, listening. He was the oldest person Mirabel had ever spoken to voluntarily. He had a way of looking at you as though you were a page in a book he'd read long ago and half forgotten, and was pleased to be reminded of.

She found him in the loft, sweeping candle wax off the boards in slow strokes.

"Brother Anselm. Can I ask you something strange?"

He didn't stop sweeping. "I'd be disappointed if you didn't."

"When the choir sings for the crown." She kept her voice low. The loft was empty but sound carried strangely in this building; she'd learned that young. "Does it ever feel — to anyone — like something is *taking?*"

The broom stopped.

Anselm leaned on it and looked at her for a long time, and she watched the half-forgetting fall away from his face, watch him arrive somewhere decades distant and stand there squinting.

"Sit down, child," he said.

She sat on the organ bench. He lowered himself into his corner chair with the care of a man handling his own bones, and folded his hands, and was quiet so long she thought he wouldn't answer at all.

"I sang for King Aldous's coronation," he said finally. "Thirty years ago. I was fourteen. My voice hadn't broken yet — I was the treble they saved for the high phrases, same as your friend Sera is now." His eyes had gone somewhere over her shoulder, at the wall, or through it. "You know what a coronation is. Oath, anointing, crown. What they don't tell you is that some coronations are also bindings. Some crowns are not put on. They are *tied on.* With song."

"The binding," Mirabel said. The word came out of her before she understood it, and Anselm's head turned sharply.

"What did you say?"

"I don't know. I've never heard that word before today. It just—" She pressed her hand flat against her sternum, against the ghost of the hook. "Something happened in that hall. Something pulled at my voice. Like a line going tight. And when I sang a hair flat, it let me go and took the others instead."

Anselm had gone very still. Stillness, Mirabel thought, was a language old people spoke fluently.

"A hair flat," he repeated softly. "And it released you." He laughed once, without any humor in it at all. "Thirty years I've wondered if anyone would ever work that out. We tried it, you know. In the early days. After we understood what we'd done. The whole choir, bending every note, starving it — and it grew angry, child. It took the strongest voice in the room entire. Old Master Ellery, our choirmaster then. They said his heart gave out at rehearsal. It did not give out. It was *taken,* as payment, as a lesson." He rubbed his face with a hand like driftwood. "So we stopped fighting it. We taught ourselves to sing beautifully and mean nothing. You can do that, if you practice. Empty the heart out of the music and keep the shell. The crown drinks the shell and gets no meal. Most of us learned. The ones who couldn't—" He shrugged, a small terrible shrug. "The ones who couldn't are why the choir has always taken in orphans. No one misses an orphan's heart."

The candle in the loft guttered. Mirabel became aware that she was gripping the edge of the organ bench hard enough to hurt her fingers.

"What binding?" she said. "What did the choir do?"

"It sang the king onto the throne," Anselm said. "That's what I'm telling you. The choir has always been more than music, girl — why do you think the crown funds us, houses us, feeds us, in a kingdom that lets its soldiers beg? We are a tool that has outlived the memory of its use. Thirty-one years ago there was a crisis — you'll find no record of it, records burn conveniently in this country — a war of succession, or worse, something that wore succession like a coat. And there was a ritual, an old one, from before the wards, from when the borders were sung rather than walled. A binding requires three things. A vessel. A fire. And a name, freely given." He held up three crooked fingers, one at a time. "The vessel was the crown itself — don't ask me where it came from, that page of the story was never ours. The fire was the last dragon, poor creature, though I didn't learn that till years later. And the name—"

He stopped. His hand closed slowly around the third finger, folding it down, hiding it, an unconscious gesture, a man trying to take back a word that had already left his mouth.

"The name was given freely," he said. "That was the horror of it. Nobody stole anything. Somebody *gave.* Gave a name to the binding, freely, and the binding needed a bloodline to hold onto across the years, and so it reached down the family of the one who gave. Generation to generation. A voice in every era, born into the choir or brought to it, each one a note in a very long song." His eyes came back from wherever they'd been and fixed on her, and they were wet, and furious, and afraid. "I've watched four of them come up through this choir, Mirabel. Four children with that particular weight in their voices. I taught two of them myself. I sang at their funerals — or what we called funerals; there was never anything to bury. And when I saw the choirmaster write *your* name on the midwinter list, in his own hand, when I heard you sing the Advent psalm and heard what sits inside your voice like a coal inside ash—"

He didn't finish. He didn't need to.

Mirabel sat on the organ bench in the cold loft of the only home she had ever known, and felt the shape of her whole life rearrange itself around a single sentence. Not *you have a gift.* Not *you are special.* But: *you are the next note. You were always going to be the next note. The song was written before you were born, and your grandmother's mother's mother wrote it, and everyone who was ever kind to you was tuning an instrument.*

"The choirmaster," she said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "He chose me. Personally. He said my name like he'd been keeping it."

"He keeps a score," Anselm said quietly. "An old one. Older than him. He inherited it, the way I inherited this broom. There's a page in it, child, a page he'd burn the cathedral down before he showed you — the original page, the one with the giving in it. If you ever want to know whose name holds your life together, that's where it's written."

"Where?"

"In the score," Anselm said. "In the binding-song itself. Where else would you keep a name?" He rose, slowly, joints cracking like green wood, and took up his broom again, and resumed his sweeping, because in this cathedral the oldest survival skill was looking busy. "And now you should go to supper, and sing beautifully tomorrow, and mean nothing, and stay flat whenever you safely can. And Mirabel—"

She paused at the loft stair.

"Don't ask him to see the score," he said. "Curiosity is how they catch the clever ones. If you go looking, go looking like a thief. Quietly, and at night, and prepared to run."

She went down the stairs into the lamplit nave, where the evening service was assembling, where the choir was gathering in its dove-grey rows, where Master Corvane stood by the lectern turning pages in a great leather-bound score with hands that were gentle and patient and, she understood now, had always been the hands of a jailer feeding a pen.

Above the altar, in the high window, the evening sky was going dark.

And there were no stars in it at all.
