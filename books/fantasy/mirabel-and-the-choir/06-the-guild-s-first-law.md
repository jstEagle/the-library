## 6. The Guild's First Law

The Guild of Impossible Things had three laws, and Coral could recite them in his sleep, because he had copied them onto the flyleaf of every ledger he'd ever opened, four hundred and eleven of them by now.

*First: keep the record. Second: keep it closed. Third: if you must speak of what is written, speak only to those who already know.*

He'd never thought much about the order of them until this week. Keep the record first. Keep it closed second. And silence — silence came third, which meant it was a consequence, not a virtue. Silence was what happened when you did your job properly. It wasn't the job itself.

Or so he had believed for thirty-one years.

The morning after the stars went out, he climbed the guildhouse stairs with his boots still wet from the flooded lower ward and his hands shaking in a way he told himself was cold. The masters convened at dawn on days like this — days when something impossible stopped being catalogued and started being *real*. He had attended perhaps six such convocations in his life. He had always sat in the corner with the minutes-book and written down everything, and no one had ever once looked at him.

He took his corner now. The seven masters arranged themselves along the crescent table: Master Quillon with his silver-rimmed spectacles, Master Ondrey who smelled perpetually of lamp oil, Mistress Havel who never finished a sentence she could finish with a look. And at the center, Master Corvane, First Keeper, whose voice was so soft that everyone in the room had to lean toward him, which Coral suspected was the entire point of speaking softly for forty years.

"The wards fell at dusk," Corvane said. "All of them. Every border, every threshold-ward, every grave-light. By midnight the sky was bare."

"An attack?" asked Ondrey.

"No army crossed," said Corvane. "No enemy claimed it. Which leaves us with the possibility I would prefer not to name."

Coral's pen stopped.

Because he knew. He had known since two o'clock that morning, sitting in the archive with a forbidden page spread under a green lamp, cross-referencing an outage pattern against entries older than the guildhouse itself. The pattern matched. Of course it matched. The last time the sky had gone dark all at once, no army had crossed then either, and the entry from that year — Year 312, Ledger of the Long Quiet — read, in a hand very like his own:

*The binding holds. The fire feeds it. The crown wears what the fire keeps alive.*

Dragon-fire. He had spent the night telling himself there were other explanations. Wards failed; wards were mortal things; even impossible things aged. But the pattern didn't fail the way old things failed. Old things failed piecemeal, ward by ward, a border here, a grave-light there, over years. This had been a single exhalation. One breath out, across the whole realm at once.

That wasn't decay. That was a lung.

"Master Corvane." He heard his own voice before he had decided to use it, thin and strange in the hush. "A question for the record."

Every head turned. That alone should have warned him. In six convocations, no one had ever turned to look at him. The corner was supposed to be furniture.

"The outage pattern," Coral said. "It matches the entry of Year 312. The Long Quiet. The binding sustained by dragon-fire." He kept his voice level, clerk-flat, as though he were reading back minutes rather than asking the question that had been eating him since midnight. "Is the binding failing?"

The silence afterward had texture. Coral had spent his life among silences — the silence of sealed ledgers, the silence of masters deciding what not to write — but this one was new. This one was the silence of men looking at each other over a body.

"Strike that from the minutes," said Corvane.

"I haven't written it yet," Coral said.

"Then don't."

Mistress Havel leaned forward. She had kind eyes, or eyes that had learned kindness as a tool, which amounted to the same thing in practice. "Keeper Ishikawa. How do you come to know the contents of the Year 312 ledger?"

And there it was. The trap laid flat and open, almost courteous, the way the best traps were. Because there was only one honest answer, and the honest answer was *I copied it*, and copying forbidden pages was itself against the law — the second one, the one about keeping things closed — and so either he lied to seven masters in a sealed room, or he confessed to a smaller crime in order to pursue a larger truth.

"I archived it," Coral said. "Twenty years ago. Under instruction from my predecessor. Some pages are marked *copy but do not index*. I copied them. I did not index them."

"You memorized them," said Ondrey.

"I remember everything I copy," Coral said. It was not a boast. It was a symptom. It was the reason they'd hired him at fourteen, a half-hidden boy with ink-stained fingers and nowhere else to go: because a keeper who forgot was worse than useless. A keeper who forgot wrote the same secret twice, and a secret written twice was a secret told.

Corvane removed his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, slowly, giving the room time to arrange its face. When he spoke again, his voice was softer than ever, which meant it was more serious than ever.

"The Year 312 entry is sealed under the third law. You know that."

"It's sealed under the second law," Coral said. "Closed. Not destroyed. There's a difference, or the guild wouldn't keep it at all."

"The difference," Corvane said, "is that a closed thing can be opened by the guild, and a spoken thing cannot be unspoken by anyone. You have opened nothing today, Keeper Ishikawa. I advise you to keep it that way."

"With respect, Master — the stars are out. All of them. Whatever the entry describes, it is happening *now*, above our own roof, and the record exists to—"

"To what?" Corvane asked, and there was something almost gentle in it, which frightened Coral more than anger would have. "Finish the sentence, Keeper. The record exists to *what*?"

Coral didn't answer. He understood, in the way one understands a thing a half-second after it matters, that there was no good ending to that sentence. *To preserve the truth* — and the guild did not deal in truths, it dealt in records, and a record was not the same animal at all. *To serve the realm* — and the realm had a king, and the king had a crown, and the guild had survived three hundred years by knowing exactly where the crown ended and the record began.

"The convocation is concluded," Corvane said. "Keeper Ishikawa will remain."

* * *

He remained. The others filed out — Havel last, her glance at him brief and unreadable, though later he would decide it had been pity — and then Coral stood alone in the crescent room with the First Keeper, and the morning light through the high windows lay across the table like something spilled.

"Do you know why the guild has three laws?" Corvane asked.

"Order of operations, Master."

"No." Corvane came around the table, and up close he was smaller than he seemed from the corner, a narrow man with a scholar's stoop and hands stained the same brown as Coral's own. "The laws are in that order because the first law protects the world, the second protects the guild, and the third protects *you*. We keep the record because a world without memory devours itself. We keep it closed because a memory loose in the world becomes a weapon in someone's hands. And we keep silent because—" he paused, choosing, "—because the people who carry secrets are the first ones other people come looking for. Silence isn't piety, Ishikawa. It's armor."

"And if the armor hides a wound?"

Corvane smiled faintly. "Then the wound is recorded. In a closed ledger. Where wounds belong."

"The binding is failing." Coral heard his voice crack on *failing*, and hated it. "Whatever sleeps behind the second law, it's waking up, and you want me to write *no anomalies noted* in the minutes of the day the sky went black."

"I want you," Corvane said, "to write exactly what I tell you to write. That is the whole of your office. It has been the whole of your office since the guild was founded, and it is the reason the guild has outlived every kingdom that tried to own it. Kings fall, Ishikawa. Ledgers endure. Do you understand what I am offering you? Continuity. The long view. The only chair in this realm that no crown can reach."

"The crown reached the sky," Coral said quietly.

For a moment — just a moment — something moved behind Corvane's eyes that was not authority. Something tired. Something that looked, absurdly, like a man standing on a shore watching a ship he'd helped launch sail past the edge of any map he owned.

"Go home, Keeper," Corvane said. "Sleep. Copy nothing. Index nothing. Tomorrow the minutes will say the wards suffered a temporal failure, cause under investigation, and in ten years no one will remember there was another answer. That is not cowardice. That is how the world survives its own history."

Coral gathered his minutes-book. At the door he stopped, because there was one more question, and he was a man who wrote questions down for a living, and some of them refused to stay filed.

"Master Corvane. The Emberhold. Does it still stand?"

This time the silence lasted long enough that Coral counted his own heartbeats in it. Four. Five. Six.

"That name," Corvane said at last, "does not appear in any indexed ledger. Which means, by the guild's own rules, it does not exist. And yet you know it." He put his spectacles back on, and the tired thing behind his eyes went out like a covered lamp. "Go home, Ishikawa. And take the long stairs. The archive stair is closed for maintenance."

The archive stair was never closed. In thirty-one years it had never once been closed.

* * *

He took the long stairs, and then, because he was who he was, he doubled back through the scriptorium gallery and came down into the archive from the map-vault side, the route known to exactly two living people, the other being Vess, who had shown it to him when he was nineteen with the words *someday you'll need to get into your own archive without being seen doing it, and on that day you'll thank me.*

The lamps in the archive were lit. That was the first wrong thing. The archive lamps were lit at dawn by the junior keepers and doused at dusk; it was mid-morning, and every green glass lamp on every iron shelf was burning, which meant someone wanted the archive to look occupied while being empty.

The second wrong thing was the smell. Fresh-cut vellum shavings and a ghost of lamp oil — someone had been working here recently, and recently meant within hours.

Coral walked the aisles with his heart going hard and quiet, past the Ledger of Bottled Weather, past the Debts of Name and Shadow, past the Long Quiet shelf, until he reached his own row. His ledgers. Four hundred and eleven volumes in his hand, spanning twenty years, the complete interior weather of the impossible world.

Ledger 388 was open on the reading desk.

He had not left it open. He never left anything open; closing the book was the last motion of every working day, a small ritual, spine cracked once, covers pressed flat. Someone had opened it. Someone had opened it to the entries of the winter court, the tribute deliveries, the pages where — he flipped forward, fast, his fingers suddenly clumsy —

Gone.

Pages 114 through 119. Razored out. Not torn — razored, cut close to the spine with a blade sharp enough to leave the remaining stubs smooth as river stones, and Coral knew that cut, had taught himself that cut at fifteen from Vess's own demonstration, *a proper excision leaves no rag to catch the eye.* Six pages. His own handwriting, gone. The winter court. The king's reflection lagging half a heartbeat behind his smile. The entry he had written at three in the morning because he couldn't not write it, the one that began *the oldest debt in the archive has a face, and the face is on the coin.*

Someone had read his ledger. Someone had found the same thread he'd found. And instead of sealing it, indexing it, arguing it before a convocation — they had cut it out.

He checked the next volume. Pages missing there too — the Year 312 cross-reference, his marginal note comparing the outage patterns. And the next. Volume by volume down the row, a surgeon's progress through his twenty years, taking only the pages that touched the crown, the binding, the fire. Eleven volumes touched. Forty-some pages gone. His life's work returned to him full of holes, like a net that had begun to refuse its own catch.

He stood in the green lamplight holding Ledger 388 and felt something break in him that he suspected would not be recorded anywhere, because the person who would have recorded it was holding the pen, and the pen was shaking.

"Coral."

Vess stood at the end of the aisle. She was seventy if she was a day, small and dry as a pressed flower, with white hair pinned in the same severe coil she'd worn since before he was born, and she was carrying a lamp of her own, unlit.

"How did you—" he began.

"The long stairs were closed," she said. "So you came the other way. You always come the other way. Sit down, boy, before you fall down."

He sat. The reading chair received him like it knew him, which it did; he'd worn his shape into it over twenty years.

"Who did this?" His voice came out sanded flat. "Which master?"

"Does it matter?"

"It matters *enormously*—"

"It matters which master gave the order," Vess said, "and I will tell you that, because you'll work it out by sundown anyway and I'd rather you worked less. Corvane ordered it. Ondrey held the lamp. Havel refused to watch." She set her unlit lamp on the desk beside his ruined ledger, a small deliberate gesture, like placing a stone on a grave. "Now ask me the question that actually matters."

Coral looked at the razored stubs. At forty pages of his own mind, excised. At the woman who had taught him the excision cut in the first place.

"Why does the guild protect the crown?" he asked. "That's the real question, isn't it. Not *what* is wearing it. Why does the guild — why do *you* — keep its secrets? Three hundred years. What does the guild get?"

Vess lowered herself into the opposite chair with the care of a woman managing old joints and older decisions. For a while she said nothing, and Coral let her, because he had learned patience in this room the way other men learned prayer.

"When you were fourteen," she said finally, "and I brought you in off the step of the guildhouse, do you remember what I told you the guild was?"

"A library for things that shouldn't exist."

"Mm. And what did you think that made us?"

He thought about lying to her and discarded it in the same instant; lying to Vess had never once worked, and she had a razor too.

"Custodians," he said. "Keepers."

"Yes. That's what I told you, and it's what I believed, and it is what I have spent fifty years discovering is only half true." She folded her hands. "The other half, Coral, is this: the guild does not only record impossibilities. The guild *administers* them. Every bottled storm, every debt of name, every sealed page — there is a counterparty. There is always a counterparty. Someone sold that storm. Someone owed that name. And the guild holds their paper." Her eyes, pale and clear and terribly old, held his. "Do you understand? We are not the memory of the impossible world. We are its *bank*. And a bank survives by ensuring that its largest accounts remain solvent."

"The crown," Coral said. His mouth had gone dry. "The crown has an account."

"The crown has the largest account in the archive." Vess's voice never rose; it grew quieter and quieter, the way dangerous water grows deep. "Older than the guildhouse. Older than the rolls. The binding, the fire, the thing that wears Aldous's shape — the guild doesn't guard that secret out of loyalty, boy. The guild guards it because if the secret dies, the guild dies with it. We are not the custodians of that vault. We are *collateral*."

"So Corvane knows. They all know. And they'll let the stars stay dark, let the wards rot, let whatever it is feed, because the alternative is—"

"Is the guild admitting, in front of the whole realm, that it has spent three hundred years keeping the kingdom's leash and calling it a library." Vess nodded slowly. "Yes. That is the arithmetic. Corvane is not evil. Corvane is *solvent*. There is a difference, and the difference has killed better people than either of you."

Coral looked down at Ledger 388, at the smooth-cut stubs where his own testimony used to live.

"You razored pages yourself," he said. "You taught me the cut."

"I did. Hundreds of times. And every single time, I told myself it was the second law." She unfolded her hands, and he saw that they trembled, just slightly, just enough. "Fifty years, Coral. And then last night the sky went black, and I stood at my window and I thought: the record exists to *what*? And I discovered I no longer knew how to finish the sentence either."

Something loosened in his chest and hurt going through, like a splinter leaving.

"Vess. The Emberhold. Is it real?"

She was quiet for a long moment. Outside, somewhere far above, the guildhouse bell rang the hour, muffled by stone, counting a time that no longer matched the sky.

"It stands," she said. "East of the Ashfall Road, past where the indexed maps stop. And Coral — listen to me now, because I will only say this part once." She leaned forward, and her voice dropped to the register she used for things that must never be written. "Record-keepers who ask questions become records. I have watched it happen. I have *made* it happen. Corvane will strike your name from the rolls within the month — sooner, if you give him cause. When that day comes, everything you are will be a closed entry, and closed entries can be handled by anyone with a key."

"Then I'll need to stop being only a record," Coral said.

Vess studied him for a long time. Whatever she saw — the boy from the guildhouse steps, the man with the shaking pen, the keeper with forty holes where his courage used to be filed — it made her nod, once, slow.

"Then tonight," she said, "before they seal your desk, go to the Long Quiet shelf and pull the Year 312 ledger. Look at the binding entry. Read the marginalia in the left margin — the hand that isn't ours, the one that predates the guild." She rose, joints cracking, and took up her unlit lamp. "There's a name in that margin, boy. A name freely given. And it belongs to someone who is still alive, still singing, and does not know what her grandmother paid."

She paused at the end of the aisle, a small dry figure in the green light, and for a moment she looked less like his mentor than like a woman standing at the edge of her own final entry.

"And Coral," she added, without turning around. "When you find her — and you will find her, because you're the only keeper in this building who remembers everything — do not write her down. Some names are only safe while they're unwritten."

Then she was gone, and Coral sat alone in the archive with his mutilated ledger and the bell tolling overhead, and after a while he got up, and walked to the Long Quiet shelf, and pulled the Year 312 volume down into his arms like something rescued from a fire.
