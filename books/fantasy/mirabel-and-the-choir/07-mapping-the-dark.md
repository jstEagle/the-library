## 7. Mapping the Dark

Coral had never liked the under-cathedral.

The Guild kept its tribute-vaults there — three floors of shelved impossibilities sunk into the cathedral's foundations, below the reliquaries, below the crypts, below even the old bone-rooms where the first archivists were interred standing up because they'd asked to be buried with their ledgers. The Guild's code said the vaults were the safest place in the realm: warded, sealed, dark as a closed eye.

That was before the wards failed.

Now the under-cathedral was simply dark, and Coral went down into it anyway, on the fourth night after the stars went out, carrying a lantern he was not supposed to need and a satchel of maps he was not supposed to have made.

He told himself it was inventory. The Year 312 volume had a gap where the razored page had been, and razored pages left traces — ghost-impressions on the leaves behind them, pressure-marks in the binding glue, sometimes a sliver of ink caught in the gutter of the next sheet. Reading those traces required instruments. The instruments were stored in Vault Nine, sub-level three, and so Coral filled out a requisition slip in his own small invisible hand, and forged Vess's countersignature beside it, and felt the guild's first law settle over his shoulders like a coat cut for someone else.

*Record-keepers who ask questions become records.* He could hear her saying it. He went down anyway.

The stairs were lit at intervals by ward-lamps that had burned without tending for two hundred years. All but one of them were dead. The last lamp guttered at the second landing and gave out while Coral stood beneath it, and the dark came down on him like water closing over his head.

He did not panic. Panic was for people who hadn't spent thirty years learning to move through archives by touch. He stood still, counted his breaths, and let his other sense open — the cartographer's sense, the one the Guild pretended didn't exist because a keeper who *mapped* things might someday map the Guild itself.

Every archive has a shape. This one he knew the way you know your own hand: nine vaults fanned around a central stair, corridors running north-south, load-bearing walls marked in the original plans with mason's crosses. He could walk it blind. He had walked it blind once before, during the flood of 309, when the lamps drowned and the bottled rivers threatened to drown with them.

But tonight the dark was wrong.

It wasn't empty dark. It was *occupied* dark — thick, textured, pressing against his skin like wet wool. The unbound magic that had been leaking into the world since the wards fell had pooled down here, in the deepest room of the deepest building, and it moved. He could feel it moving, slow currents of it, eddying around the shelving the way water eddies around pilings. His internal map dissolved. A map is a record of what stays put, and nothing down here was staying put.

Coral got out his lantern, struck the flint twice, and got nothing. The flame wouldn't take. Of course it wouldn't. Flame needed air with an opinion about itself, and the air down here had forgotten what it was for.

He stood in the black and thought about going back up.

Then, from somewhere deep in the vaults — deeper than Vault Nine, deeper than the bone-rooms — he heard humming.

Not singing. Humming. A single line of melody, unhurried, looping back on itself, the kind of tune a person hums when they're frightened and refusing to admit it. And the strangest thing happened as he listened: the dark around him *organized*. Not everywhere — just along the path of the sound, a corridor of calmer air, as if the melody were drawing a line through the chaos and the chaos was too surprised to resist.

Coral knew exactly one person in the capital whose voice did things to magic.

He followed the humming.

* * *

Mirabel had been coming down to the under-cathedral for six nights now, and every night she told herself it was the last.

The choirmaster thought she was sleeping. The other choristers thought she was praying. Both assumptions were useful, and both were lies. What she was doing was hiding — not from people but from the pull, the hook-feeling behind her sternum that had started the night she sang for the crown and hadn't stopped since. Upstairs, in the choir loft, in the dormitory, in the refectory, she could feel it all the time now: a faint constant traction, like a fisherman testing a line. Down here, under three floors of stone and dead wards, the line went slack.

She should have found that comforting. Instead it terrified her, because it meant the thing wearing the crown could reach her anywhere in the city except directly beneath it, and she couldn't decide whether that was mercy or design.

So she came down, and she sat in the dark among the Guild's shelved impossibilities, and she hummed.

The humming wasn't a plan. It was instinct. On the first night she'd discovered that the dark down here behaved strangely — that it seemed to lean toward her, press against her, get into her mouth when she breathed — and that when she hummed the leaning stopped. The melody she used was the oldest one in the choir's book, the plainsong they sang at the winter investiture, the one the old chorister Tobin called *the floor of the house*. She didn't know why it worked. She only knew that when she hummed it, the dark kept a polite distance, and she could think.

Tonight she had gone deeper than usual. Past Vault Five, past the corridor of bottled weather, down the narrow stair to the lowest gallery, where the shelves held things the labels wouldn't name. Her candle — real wax, hoarded from the sacristy — had burned to a stub and gone out an hour ago, and she'd stayed anyway, humming in the absolute black, because going back up meant the hook pulling tight again.

That was when she heard footsteps.

They came down the long gallery toward her, careful, counting their way — someone navigating by memory, touching the shelf-ends as he passed. She stopped humming. The dark surged immediately, a cold weight settling against her shoulders like hands, and she bit down on a yelp and forced the melody out again, louder, and felt it hold.

The footsteps stopped.

"Hello," said a voice. A man's voice, young-ish, dry, controlled — the voice of someone who had practiced sounding calm in the dark. "I don't know if you can hear me. I'm going to assume you can, because you've been humming for six nights and the acoustics down here are excellent."

Mirabel's heart slammed. "Who are you?"

"Coral Ishikawa. Guild of Impossible Things." A pause. "Which I suppose means nothing to you."

"It means the seals on the tribute crates," she said. "I've been reading them. There's nothing else to read."

Another pause, and then — she would remember this afterward — a small huff of laughter, startled out of him. "You've been sitting in our vaults reading crate-seals."

"Somebody has to. Your wards are dead. Anyone could walk in here."

"I know," he said. "I'm anyone. I walked in here."

She shifted on her crate, angling her voice toward him. He sounded closer now, maybe ten paces, somewhere in the aisle across from her. "Why are you in the dark? You have lanterns. I've seen the guild men use them."

"The lantern won't light. Nothing burns down here anymore." A rustle — a satchel being set down, perhaps. "The air's full of loose magic since the wards fell. It doesn't leave room for fire."

"And your humming cure?" she said. "Does it work for you?"

"It's not a cure. It's—" He hesitated, and she heard him choosing words with the care of a man handling something fragile. "It's a survey line. When you hum, the magic stops moving. It holds still long enough to be measured. Do you know what you're doing?"

"No."

"Neither do I," he said. "But I know what it looks like. It looks like a keel. Like you're laying a keel through the dark, and everything else is arranging itself along it." Another pause. "What's the song?"

"Investiture plainsong. The oldest one we have."

Something changed in his breathing. She couldn't have said what — a catch, a lengthening — but it was there, and then gone, smoothed away.

"That makes sense," he said, in a voice that suggested it made terrible sense. "May I come closer? I'll keep talking so you know where I am."

"You could just hum," she said. "Then I'd know where *you* are."

"I don't hum."

"Everyone hums."

"I assure you I don't. It's a documented deficiency." But his voice was still dry, still level, and she found — unexpectedly, absurdly — that she wasn't afraid of him. Six nights alone in the dark, and the first person to find her was a guild clerk who admitted to being unable to hum. She decided this was either very good luck or very bad, and that she'd find out which.

His footsteps came on, measured, each one announced: "Shelf-end. Corner post. Step down — there's a step here, mind it." And then he rounded the end of her aisle and stopped, and even though she couldn't see him, she felt him seeing her: a girl of seventeen on a packing crate in a dead cathedral cellar, choir robe still on because she'd come straight from evensong, hands folded like a good student.

"Oh," he said quietly. "You're the chorister. From the court. From the—"

"Winter ceremony. Yes."

"The king's choir."

"The king's choir," she agreed, and heard how flat her own voice went, and saw — or sensed — him register it.

* * *

They negotiated, in the way of two cautious animals, over the next hour.

Coral explained, in his precise ledger-clerk's cadence, what he was doing down here: recovering traces of a destroyed record. He did not say which record. Mirabel noticed the omission and filed it away; she was a chorister, and choristers learn to hear what isn't sung.

Mirabel explained, in turn, why she came down: to be somewhere the crown couldn't pull at her. She did not say what the pulling felt like. Coral noticed the omission and filed it away; he was a record-keeper, and record-keepers learn to read what isn't written.

But the dark made liars expensive. Every evasion cost breath, and breath was the only currency down here, and gradually — without either of them deciding to — they began spending it honestly.

"There," Coral said, an hour in. He had settled onto a crate six feet from hers, close enough to talk low, far enough to be proper. "Do you feel that?"

"Feel what?"

"The current. It's turned. Since you started humming again it's been circling us — do you notice how the pressure comes from the east side now, steady, instead of all directions?" He was rummaging in his satchel as he spoke; she heard paper, and charcoal, and the particular scratch of a man drawing in the dark. "I've been trying to chart this place since the wards fell and it's impossible. The magic moves faster than I can fix points. But you—" The scratching paused. "You make it hold still."

"I make it *polite*," Mirabel said. "There's a difference."

"Is there?"

"Yes. Polite things can change their minds."

He laughed again, that same startled huff, and she realized she was pleased at having caused it, and decided not to examine that.

"What are you drawing?" she asked.

"A map. Obviously."

"You can't see anything."

"I don't need to see. I've got the guild's original plans memorized — footprints, wall thicknesses, shelf runs. I'm laying them over what I can feel of the currents. If your song holds the pattern stable for another quarter hour, I'll have the first accurate chart of the under-cathedral's magic since it was built." The charcoal resumed. "Hold the note you're on. That one. The long one."

Mirabel held it. She was, she reflected, holding a note in total darkness for a stranger who drew maps by memory, in a vault full of bottled thunderstorms, while the stars outside stayed gone — and it was the most *seen* she had felt since the night she sang for the crown. Because the crown looked at her like a meal. The choirmaster looked at her like a tool. This man looked at her — she could feel the attention of him across six feet of black — like a *colleague*. Like the other half of a method.

"Tell me what you feel," he said. "Precisely. I want to annotate."

"The pressure's lighter on my left. Heavier behind me. When I change notes, there's a lag — like the dark takes a moment to agree." She frowned in the black. "And underneath it there's a rhythm. Very slow. I thought it was my own pulse at first, but it's not. It's slower. Much slower."

The charcoal stopped.

"How much slower?"

"Like — one beat for every hundred of mine. Maybe more."

Silence. Then Coral said, very carefully, "Describe the beat."

"Heavy. Deep. Like a drum wrapped in wool. And it stutters a little, near the top of it. Like a breath catching."

In the dark, she heard him set down the charcoal with exaggerated gentleness, the way you set down something you might otherwise drop.

"I've read about that," he said. "In a book I probably shouldn't have read. Under the Emberhold, the records say, the dragon-fire doesn't burn evenly. It gutters. Keepers used to time their inspections to the stutter." His voice had gone quiet, stripped of its dryness. "Mirabel. We're sitting inside the range of the king's binding. The whole city sits inside it. But down *here*, with your song holding everything still — we can hear its heartbeat."

Mirabel's hands had gone cold in her lap. "Is it dying?"

"Everything's dying. The question is what dies first." She heard him take a breath, and let it out, and make some internal decision — she was getting good at hearing those. "I'm going to tell you something I haven't told anyone. Not the guild. Not my mentor. You, because you're a stranger in the dark who makes the impossible hold still, and because if I'm wrong about you, no one will ever believe your testimony anyway."

"That's flattering."

"It's true. Listen." And he told her — quickly, low, in the clipped cadence of a man reciting from memory because the memory was safer spoken fast — about the ledger entry he didn't remember writing. About the forbidden page he'd copied years ago, the outage pattern, the dragon-fire, the binding. About the razored pages. About the king whose reflection lagged half a second behind his face.

When he finished, the dark pressed close, and the slow wool-wrapped heartbeat pulsed beneath them, and Mirabel found that her humming had continued without her deciding it — the plainsong, the floor of the house, steady as a floor should be.

"My turn," she said.

She told him about the doors that held shut when she sang. About the choirmaster's hungry watching. About the hook behind her sternum, the pull she felt everywhere except down here. About the old chorister Tobin, and the thing he'd almost said before the choirmaster's shadow crossed the cloister, and the word he'd mouthed that she hadn't understood until tonight:

*Binding.*

Coral was silent for a long moment.

"They're feeding the choir to it," he said finally. "Aren't they. Voice by voice. That's what the crown does with choristers."

"I don't know."

"You know. You feel the hook." No cruelty in it — just precision, the precision of a man who dealt in facts and thought facts were a kindness. "Mirabel. The guild erased pages to hide this. The choirmaster is feeding girls to it. The king is dead and wearing himself as a mask. And you and I are sitting in a hole under the cathedral, and I have a partial map, and you have a song, and between us we have approximately nothing."

"Not nothing," Mirabel said.

She reached out in the dark and touched the edge of his satchel, and then, finding his sleeve, the paper laid over his knee — the map-in-progress, all those careful charcoal lines she couldn't see. She traced one line with her fingertip, feeling the groove of it.

"This is a map of the dark," she said. "No one's ever had one of those. And I'm the reason it exists. That's not nothing. That's a beginning. Maps start somewhere."

Coral didn't answer right away. When he did, his voice had changed — quieter, and underneath the dryness, something that sounded almost like relief, the relief of a man setting down a weight he'd carried alone so long he'd forgotten it was heavy.

"When I was a boy," he said, "I copied maps for the guild. Borders, coastlines, trade roads. And I always left myself out. You can't draw yourself onto a map — there's no symbol for 'the person holding the pen.' So I got in the habit of not existing. Thirty years of not existing." A breath. "Tonight, for the first time, I mapped something with someone. And it's the first map I've ever been *on*."

"Where are you on it?" Mirabel asked. "Show me."

"Six feet from you. On a crate. Annotated: *source of stability, sings, cannot be charted without*."

She smiled in the black, where no one could see it, which meant it was the most honest smile she'd given in weeks.

"Alliance, then," she said.

"Alliance," Coral agreed. Then, because he was who he was: "With terms. We meet here, every third night. You sing, I chart. We share everything we find — everything, no omissions, I'll know if you omit. And neither of us tells the guild or the choir until we know enough to survive telling."

"And when we know enough?"

The slow heartbeat pulsed beneath them, stuttering at the top of its rise, like a breath catching. Somewhere above them the cathedral bells tolled midnight into a sky with no stars in it.

"Then," Coral said, "we write it down where no one can razor it out. And we make them read it."

Mirabel took up the plainsong again, the floor of the house, and laid it down fresh through the dark, and felt the magic settle along it, polite, measurable, *held*. Above her the hook waited. Below her the binding breathed. Between the two, on a packing crate in the deepest room of the capital, a choirgirl and a cartographer bent together over a map that existed nowhere but in charcoal and memory —

— and three aisles away, unheard by either of them, something in the dark shifted its weight, and began, very softly, to listen.
