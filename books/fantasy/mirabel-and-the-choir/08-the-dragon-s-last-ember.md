## 8. The Dragon's Last Ember

The razored page had left a ghost of itself behind.

Coral discovered it the way he discovered most things: by accident, at three in the morning, holding a ledger up to a candle at the wrong angle. Where the blade had cut away the page about the binding, the pressure of the pen had gone through to the sheet beneath. Ink ghosts. Half-words pressed into paper that had never been meant to carry them.

*...emberhold... north fork, where the river forgets its name... she burns low...*

*She.*

He sat in his cell of an office in the guildhouse and read the ghost eleven times. Then he packed.

Not much to pack. A cartographer travels light: ink, pens, three blank ledgers, a roll of good vellum, the compass Vess had given him when he was nine years old and still allowed to have a mentor who looked him in the eye. He took the master seal off its hook and put it back twice before leaving it. He wasn't guild business anymore, whatever the rolls said. The rolls said nothing about him now. That was the point of erasure — you became your own permission slip.

He left before dawn, through the coal door, past the sleeping porter whose snores he had catalogued years ago under *Ambient Hazards, Subsection: Human*. The city didn't look up as he went. Cities never did. That had always been the comfort of it, and lately it felt more like a diagnosis.

* * *

North meant three days on roads that were forgetting what they were for.

He saw it happen outside the village of Harrow's Bend, where the highway simply stopped believing in itself halfway down a hill. The ruts continued for twenty paces and then frayed into meadow-grass, and the grass grew *through* the idea of the road, and beyond that there was just country, trackless and sullen, as if the land had been asked a question and decided not to answer. A carter stood at the edge with his mule, staring.

"Third one this month," the carter said, without being asked. "Roads go stupid. You want the north fork?"

"I do."

"Then follow the river. Rivers remember longer than roads." He spat. "Most things don't."

Coral walked the river for two days. He mapped as he went because mapping was how he prayed — every bend noted, every ford measured, every ruined ward-stone sketched with the date of its failure estimated from the moss. It steadied him. It also terrified him, because a map is a promise that the world will hold still long enough to be recorded, and the world was breaking that promise everywhere he looked.

On the second evening he found a bridge that existed only when you weren't looking directly at it. He crossed it by walking backward, which felt ridiculous until it worked, and after that he stopped being surprised by anything.

The river forgot its name on the third day.

He knew because the water changed. Not its course — its *sound*. A river with a name makes a particular noise, a running commentary, gossip between banks. This one had gone quiet in the middle of its own noise, like a man mid-sentence struck dumb, and the water slid past gray and apologetic. Coral stood on the bank and wrote in his field ledger: *Unnamed tributary (formerly ?). Water clear. Fish present but listless.* Then, because no one would ever read it and so he could afford honesty: *I am afraid.*

The north fork climbed into hills that the maps called the Teeth, though no map had ever bothered to draw them well. Old country. Warded country, once — he passed the stumps of boundary stones with their sigils burned out, each one gutted from the inside, the way a lamp is gutted when something drinks the oil.

And always, growing as he climbed, a warmth in the air. Wrong warmth. Summer warmth in late autumn, coming from nowhere, smelling faintly of hot iron and incense and something older underneath, something like the inside of a struck bell.

* * *

He smelled the Emberhold before he saw it: sulfur and myrrh, a church smell welded to a forge smell, and beneath both the dry scent of a fire running out of wood.

It sat in a caldera like a crown sits on a head — too large for what held it, obviously important, visibly wrong. Black stone, no windows, no door that he could find on the first circuit or the second. Heat shimmered off its walls in slow waves, and the shimmer had a rhythm to it. In and out. Long pause. In and out.

Breathing.

Coral stood at the caldera's rim with his notebook open and his pen dry in his hand, and understood, in the way you understand a cliff edge — all at once, from the stomach — that he was not looking at a building.

He was looking at a wick.

"There's no door," he said aloud, because saying things aloud kept the fear at the size of words. "There's never a door. Doors are for things that want visitors."

"Or things," said a voice like a landslide learning gentleness, "that have given up expecting them."

The wall in front of him opened. Not swung — *unfurled*, black stone unfolding like a hand unclenching after a very long time, and the heat rolled out over him, and Coral Ishikawa, erased record-keeper of the Guild of Impossible Things, stepped through the only door in the world that had been waiting for him specifically.

* * *

Inside was a cathedral built around a fireplace, or a fireplace grown into a cathedral — the architecture made no commitments. And in the center, coiled through the dark like a river of embers trying to remember being a river, lay the dragon.

She was dying the way a city dies: not all at once, in districts. Whole lengths of her had gone gray and cold, scale fading to ash-color, and those parts did not move. But along her spine the old fire still ran, guttering gold, flaring and failing, flaring and failing, and each flare lit the vaulted dark overhead and showed him carvings — wards, thousands of them, cut into the stone, all of them drinking from her. She was not lying in the Emberhold. She was *plugged into* it.

"Serevaine," Coral said. Her name was on the razored fragment's margin, in his own handwriting, from a copying he did not remember doing. Saying it felt like stepping onto the bridge while looking at it.

"You're the little ink-man." Her eye found him — one eye, vast, amber, the pupil a vertical slit gone soft at the edges like a candle flame about to lie down. "The guild sends its tribute of silence every year. You send nothing. I like you already."

"The guild doesn't know I'm here."

"No. They'd have sent someone duller." The great head shifted; scales ground against stone with a sound like a ship changing its mind. "You have questions written all over you. Ask. I haven't the strength for the long version of anything."

Coral opened his ledger. His hands were steady, which surprised him. "The binding. The king's crown. What is it, exactly?"

"A leash," Serevaine said, "worn as a crown. Or a crown worn as a leash. The distinction stopped mattering around the year your grandfather was born." The flame in her throat pulsed; the wards overhead drank; somewhere far south, Coral understood, stars stayed lit because of this pulse, this exact pulse, keeping time. "Your dead king — the one in the storybooks, Aldous the Last — he didn't die. That was the bargain. Something came wearing the shape of a succession crisis, and the price of the realm continuing was a king who continued with it. Forever. Fed. Kept."

"And the fire—"

"Is the other half of the contract. Every binding needs fuel. They used me." A sound came out of her that might have been a laugh, if landslides laughed; it moved loose ash across the floor. "I agreed, little ink-man. That's the part your guild razored out of its own memory. There was no conquest. There was a *signing*. I put my fire under their contract because the alternative was watching everything end, and dragons are sentimental about endings. We prefer them postponed."

Coral wrote: *Fuel: voluntary.* His pen hesitated. "And King Aldous? The real one? Did he sign too?"

For the first time the vast eye focused on him with something sharper than exhaustion.

"He signed a coronation oath," Serevaine said. "That's all anyone told him it was. The thing that wears his face now — it didn't take the throne. It took the *oath*, and the oath took him, and the binding has been wearing him ever since like a coat it grew into. He has been awake inside it the whole time. Thirty years. Watching his hands sign things." The flame guttered low, very low, and the dark leaned in, and the wards overhead creaked like a ship in weather. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? The crown was never his choice. Nothing since the oath has been anyone's choice. That is what a binding *is*. It eats choosing."

The words went into Coral and found the thing he'd been carrying since the archive, since the starless sky, since Vess said *record-keepers who ask questions become records*. He thought of his own name struck from the rolls. He thought of Mirabel — the choirgirl from the starless archive, humming the dark navigable — and the hungry attention of the choirmaster, and wondered what choices she was still permitted.

"When you go out," he said quietly. "What happens?"

"Now? Everything." No drama in it. Inventory. "The binding fails. The thing in the crown loses its fuel and starts spending the kingdom instead — light, names, roads, children's reflections, whatever's nearest. Your border wards are already down because I've been rationing myself. I have perhaps a season. Less, if it gets greedy." The eye closed, opened. "Unless the contract is broken properly. Freely given can be freely returned — that's the old law, the deep law, the one even bindings must obey. But no one living knows the terms anymore. I knew them once. I've forgotten most of what I was."

Coral looked down at his ledger, at the ghost-words, at thirty years of entries in a hand no one was ever meant to see, and heard himself say:

"I keep records. Tell me everything you remember. All of it. Even the parts that don't make sense yet."

The dragon regarded him for a long moment, and the fire along her spine flared — brighter than it had in years, she seemed surprised by it herself — and somewhere far to the south, Coral would learn later, three stars flickered back into the blank sky for the space of a heartbeat, and a choirmaster in the capital looked up from his scores and frowned.

"Little ink-man," Serevaine said, "you should know that the last person who wrote down my terms was the one who razored them out."

"Then I'll write them somewhere razors can't reach."

"Where?"

"In people," Coral said. "I've been invisible my whole life. I know exactly how many places there are to hide a truth." 

The dragon laughed again, and this time it was unmistakably a laugh, and the whole Emberhold warmed by a degree.

So he sat down on the warm stone beside a dying creature the size of a cathedral, opened a fresh ledger, and began to write as she spoke — the oath, the terms, the name freely given, the deep law, the loophole, the cost. And near dawn, when her voice had worn down to embers, she said the thing that sent him out of the caldera at a dead run:

"One more term, ink-man, and mark it well. The binding renews at the winter court. When the crown next feeds—" the flame sank, and the dark rushed close, and her voice came from very far away, "—it will look for a new anchor. Young. Unbound. With a voice that holds doors shut."

Mirabel.

Coral was already moving.
