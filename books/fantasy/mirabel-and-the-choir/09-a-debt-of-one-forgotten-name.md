## 9. A Debt of One Forgotten Name

The choirmaster's score was kept in a locked case in his study, and Mirabel had been told since her first week in the orphan choir that the case was not to be touched. Not by choristers. Not by anyone. The score was the choir's inheritance, Master Ondrel said — the original binding-song, written down three generations ago, and it was to be treated with the reverence one gave a relic.

Mirabel had never wanted to touch it before. She had sung from copies like everyone else, thin paper transcriptions that left out whole passages, marked *omitted for brevity* in Ondrel's cramped hand. She had assumed the omissions were liturgical. Old songs were long. Nobody needed all of them.

But that was before she had stood in the great hall and felt something hook into her chest through her own voice. Before she had learned that the choir once sang a king onto a throne. Before the old chorister Sef, who was dying by inches in the infirmary wing, had gripped her wrist with fingers like dry twigs and said: *Ask him why the copies are short.*

So on the evening after the third rehearsal for the restoration ceremony — the ceremony the crown had announced with banners and bells, *the night we bring back the stars* — Mirabel waited until Ondrel went down to vespers, and she picked the lock of his study with a hairpin.

She had expected to feel guilty. What she felt instead was calm, cold, and very awake, the way she'd felt the first time her voice made a door hold shut. Some part of her had been waiting her whole life for permission to do exactly this.

* * *

The study smelled of ink and candle tallow and, underneath, something faintly sweet that she couldn't name until she saw the dish of candied violets on the desk — the kind Ondrel handed out to choristers who sang especially well at feeding rehearsals. Feeding rehearsals. She made herself use the word now, even alone, even in her own head. Sef had taught it to her. *He calls them strengthening sessions,* Sef had said. *We called them feedings, back when there were still enough of us old ones to say so out loud.*

The locked case sat on a stand beneath the window, walnut and brass, its lock older and kinder than the study door's. It opened to the same hairpin.

Inside, the score lay on a bed of blue cloth. It was larger than she'd imagined — vellum pages bound in boards covered in scarred leather, the kind of book that got carried in processions. The first page bore the choir's crest: a flame inside a circle of notes. Beneath it, in faded iron-gall ink:

*For the keeping of the crown, and the keeping of the quiet.*

She turned pages. The notation was archaic but readable; she'd been trained on worse. The binding-song unspooled across the vellum in long braided lines — seven voices interwoven, then nine, then twelve, a structure so intricate it made her fingers itch. This was what they'd been singing fragments of for years without knowing it. This was the whole animal, and the copies were its shed skin.

And in the margins, in at least four different hands spanning decades, someone had been annotating. Not theology. Arithmetic.

*Third voice holds 40 breaths before strain.*
*The new girl (Ilsette) thins after the high passage — note for rotation.*
*Rotation necessary. Voices wear. Ask the steward for two more from the foundling intake, spring if possible.*

Foundling intake. Spring if possible. As if ordering flour.

Mirabel sat down on the study floor because her legs decided the matter without consulting her. She kept reading. That was the terrible thing about her, she thought distantly — she would always keep reading. Even now, kneeling on a dead man's carpet among his candied violets, she turned the page.

* * *

It was near the back that she found the razored edge.

A stub of vellum where a page should be, cut close to the spine with a blade — no, not razored. She leaned close. The cut was clean but the remaining stub was rough along its top margin, as though something had been pasted over it and later torn away. And when she tilted the page toward the candle, she saw why.

There was writing underneath. Pressed hard enough to emboss the page beneath it, the way a scribe's pen leaves ghosts when the ink is scraped but the memory isn't. She'd done it herself a hundred times, drafting letters on soft paper. You could read a ghost if you raked a candle low across it and let the shadows pool in the grooves.

She turned the page sideways. Then upside down. Then she held the flame nearly flat against the vellum, close enough that the heat stung her knuckles, and the ghost-writing rose out of the parchment like something surfacing through dark water.

It was not music. It was a record. Ledger-hand, the neat commercial script she'd seen once on a guild courier's manifest — and beside it, in a different, older hand, a passage copied out with care:

*Let it be entered that the binding requires a name freely given, and that a name given under compulsion rots the work within a season. Let it be entered further that the crown cannot take; it can only receive. Therefore the choir must give, and give gladly, or the whole craft fails.*

Below that, the ledger-hand again:

*Received, this 14th of Frostmonth, the free gift of one (1) name, given willingly by the bearer, in exchange for the binding's maintenance and the realm's light. Name: VESPERA ILLIONE SANDOVAL. Relation to choir: first voice. Condition of gift: joyful.*

Mirabel read the name four times.

Her grandmother's name. Her grandmother — Vespera, whom everyone called Vespa, who had died when Mirabel was six, who had taught her to pitch-match against the wind chimes in the courtyard, who had held her face in both hands and said, *You have my voice, little bird, and one day you'll know what that costs.* Everyone had assumed it was an old woman's poetry. Grandmothers said things like that.

*Condition of gift: joyful.*

Her grandmother had given her name *gladly*. To keep the binding alive. To keep the stars lit. And the binding had taken it — and with the name, Mirabel understood with a lurch that had nothing to do with her stomach, everything else. The way Vespa had stopped singing in her last years except alone, in the courtyard, small songs with no power in them. The way she'd sometimes looked at Mirabel mid-phrase with an expression Mirabel had never had words for and now did: *inventory*. A woman checking whether the thing she'd lost had grown back in the next generation.

*You have my voice, little bird, and one day you'll know what that costs.*

The candle guttered. Mirabel steadied it with a hand that did not shake, which surprised her.

Because here was the arithmetic, and she was good at arithmetic, Sef had made sure of it: a name freely given sustained the binding. Names wore out — the annotations proved that, the whole marginalia was a shepherd's log of a flock being grazed. So the choir gave another. And another. Ilsette, who'd "gone home to family" when Mirabel was nine, except Ilsette had no family, that was why she was here. Brother Anselm, who'd "taken a silent order." Tavia, whose bed they'd told the younger girls had been reassigned.

Three generations of choristers, each one handing over a name — freely, gladly, because Master Ondrel knew how to make giving feel like an honor. Because he chose girls with strong gifts and no families and he wrapped the taking in ceremony and candlelight and candied violets, and the choir sang, and the crown received, and the stars stayed up, and nobody ever wrote down where the names went.

Except someone had. Someone had kept a ledger — guild-hand, guild-script — and someone had hidden a copy of the crucial page inside the score itself, pasted flat and later torn away, leaving only the ghost. Which meant whoever hid it had wanted it found eventually, by someone patient enough to hold fire to the truth.

Sef. It had to be Sef. He'd been first voice before Vespa. He'd have known the page existed. He'd spent forty years in this building watching girls get rotated out, and he'd put the evidence where only a chorister with a reason to open the forbidden case would ever look.

*Ask him why the copies are short.*

Mirabel closed the score. Sat in the dark with her hand flat on the leather cover, feeling her own pulse come up through it.

Her sanctuary was a feeding pen. The courtyard where she'd learned harmony was a paddock. The vespers, the violet candies, Ondrel's warm hand on your shoulder after a good rehearsal — *well sung, little bird* — all of it husbandry. And the worst part, the part that made her press the heel of her hand against her mouth, was that it had worked. The stars had stayed lit. Children in the capital had grown up under a full sky because girls like her had been cropped like wheat and never told.

She was not going to cry. She noted this about herself with mild interest, the way you note weather. There was a version of her that cried, and she could feel that version standing just behind her shoulder, but the version holding the candle was newer and harder and had been assembled over the last month out of pieces: a hook in the chest, a lagging reflection, a dying man's grip on her wrist.

She took the score's final leaf — blank vellum, good stock — and a pencil from Ondrel's desk, and she copied the ghost-page entire: the requirement, the name, the condition, the date. She pressed hard. If they burned her copy, she would write it again. If they burned that, she would sing it. A name freely given can also be freely sung back — Sef had started telling her that last week, in the infirmary, between coughs, and she hadn't understood yet what he was arming her for.

She understood now.

She was the last note of an old song. And the song was not finished. It was waiting for its final phrase, and the final phrase was hers to give or withhold — freely, gladly, on her own terms, which was the one thing Ondrel's machinery had never accounted for. His whole craft ran on gladness, and gladness could not be farmed forever. Gladness could learn.

She slid the score back into its case, locked it, locked the study, and was halfway down the stairs before she heard the front door below and froze on the landing, hairpin still in her sleeve.

Voices. Two of them. One was Ondrel's, smooth and unhurried.

The other was low and dry and wrong in a way she felt in her teeth before her mind caught up — a voice that arrived a half-second after the mouth moved. She'd heard it once before, across a winter court, and she had hoped never to hear it again.

The thing wearing the crown had come to the choir school. At night. Unannounced.

Through the banister rails, Mirabel looked down into the entry hall and saw the candlelight bend around a tall figure in plain traveling clothes, no circlet, no guard — and saw Ondrel bowing, actually bowing, to a man he should never have met outside of ceremony.

"—the girl is nearly ready," Ondrel was saying. "Another rotation and she'll hold the lead part without strain."

"Strain is acceptable," the king said, in that voice that lagged its own mouth like a bad reflection. "Loss is not. The ember is very low, choirmaster. Whatever you must spend, spend it. I want the ceremony moved forward."

"Forward? Your Majesty, the preparations—"

"Moved *forward*." The word came out flat and absolute, and the candles in the entry hall all leaned toward the king at once, every flame in the room tilting like grass under wind, and Mirabel's skin went cold because she recognized that too — that was hunger, that was the binding pulling at the nearest fuel, and the nearest fuel was a building full of girls.

"As you command," Ondrel murmured.

"And choirmaster." The king paused at the door, half-turned. In the dark beyond the threshold, where his face wasn't quite catching the light, Mirabel saw the reflection in the entry-hall glass arrive late — saw it still finishing the turn after the body had gone still. "If anyone has been asking questions about the old gifts — the names — you will tell me. Immediately. There is a record-keeper loose in the city who has forgotten how to be invisible."

The door closed. The candles straightened slowly, like soldiers relaxing after an inspection.

Mirabel stood on the landing with her heart slamming and her copy of the ghost-page pressed against her ribs, and she understood two things with perfect clarity.

The ceremony would come soon — sooner than any of them had planned for.

And somewhere out in the starless city, a cartographer with ink-stained fingers was already looking for her.
