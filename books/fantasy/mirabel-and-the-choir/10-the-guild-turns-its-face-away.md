## 10. The Guild Turns Its Face Away

They took my ink first.

I want to be precise about that, because precision is the only prayer I have left. It wasn't the ledgers they came for at dawn — not yet. It was the ink. Master ink, the guild's own formula, the stuff that binds a record to what it records so that no hand can alter it after the fact. Three clerks in grey robes stood in my cell in the archive's undercroft while the eldest of them uncapped my pot with a little silver spoon and poured it out into a lead flask, and none of them would look at me while they did it.

"You're not accused of anything," the eldest said, which was the guild's way of saying *you are already condemned; we simply haven't finished the paperwork.*

I had come back from the Emberhold with frost in my boots and a dead man's name in my mouth and a fragment of razored page pressed flat between two sheets of waxed paper, and I had slept four hours, and then I had walked up the seven hundred steps to the Chapter Hall to give them everything. That was the plan. That was always going to be the plan, even knowing — especially knowing — what Vess had told me on the stairs a week before: *record-keepers who ask questions become records.* I had thought about it every step of the way down from the mountains. I had thought about it while Serevaine's ember-light still stained the inside of my eyelids, that vast guttering gold, her voice like a landslide remembering it used to be a river. *The crown was never Aldous's choice,* she'd said, and then she'd shown me the shape of the binding in her own failing fire, thirty years deep, fed on names.

I thought maybe the truth would be too big for them to sit on. I thought the guild had been built for exactly this — impossible things, catalogued, contained, understood.

I had forgotten the first law. Or rather, I had known it all along and told myself it meant something gentler than it does.

*The guild keeps silence. The guild keeps its face turned away. What is recorded is not what is known.*

* * *

The Chapter Hall smelled of tallow and old vellum, as it always did, and the nine masters sat in their crescent of high chairs with the morning light behind them so that their faces were all shadow and their seals were all visible. I had seen this arrangement a thousand times from the clerk's bench below, taking minutes. It is a very different room when you stand where the impossible things stand.

Master Quillon spoke first, because Quillon always speaks first; he believes silence reads as weakness.

"Cartographer Ishikawa. You requested formal audience."

"I did." My voice held. I'd rehearsed it against the undercroft wall, which sounds mad but works. "I request it be entered into the record that I requested it."

A flicker among the masters. Good. They understood what I was doing. Everything said in this room would go into a ledger, and everything in a ledger could someday be read, and I intended there to be a ledger.

"Entered," said Quillon, in the tone of a man humoring a child. "State your business."

So I stated it. I laid the waxed papers on the reading table and unwrapped them one by one, and I will give the masters this: they listened. All nine of them, motionless, while I told them about the pattern in the star-outage — the same spiral decay I'd copied years ago off a forbidden page describing the collapse of the northern wards, three centuries back. While I told them about King Aldous and his reflection that lagged half a heartbeat behind his smile. While I told them about the Emberhold, and Serevaine, and the binding, and the name freely given that anchored it, and the choir that had been feeding choristers to the crown for generations because a freely given name can be renewed but never revoked by force — only sung back, only unmade by the same door it entered through.

While I told them that when the last dragon went out, everything went with her. The wards. The light. The stars, which were never stars at all but the far end of the binding, burning in a sky that was itself a kind of ledger.

When I finished, the hall was so quiet I could hear the tallow spit in its sconces.

It was Vess who moved first. She didn't speak. She just looked at me from the third chair, and her face did something I had never seen it do in twenty-two years of apprenticeship and service: it apologized. Before anything had been decided. Before anyone had said a word. She looked at me the way you look at someone at a funeral who doesn't yet know it's theirs.

"Master Ishikawa," said Master Corvel, oldest of the nine, his voice like a drawer closing, "where did you obtain these materials?"

"The Emberhold. The dragon herself."

"That site is sealed under statute eleven."

"The site is dying," I said. "The realm is dying. Statute eleven can file a complaint."

Someone below the masters' crescent — a clerk, someone I'd shared a desk with for a decade — made a small sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. I didn't look. Looking would have gotten him erased too.

Corvel folded his hands. "The Guild of Impossible Things exists to keep such knowledge from doing harm. Harm is not averted by disclosure. Harm is averted by containment. This has been the guild's purpose since before the crown, since before the kingdom, since before any institution now standing. You are proposing that we break thirty years of maintained silence over a matter the guild itself helped to bind."

The floor tilted slightly under me. I kept my feet. "Helped to—"

"You know we did," Corvel said, not unkindly, which was worse. "You copied the page yourself, years ago. Why do you think it was forbidden? Not because the knowledge was false, Cartographer. Because it was true, and true things in the wrong hands burn like everything else." He leaned forward into the light, and I saw that his eyes were tired in a way I recognized — the tiredness of a man who has carried a secret so long it has grown into his spine. "The binding holds. It has held for thirty years. It is ugly. Most bindings are. But it holds, and the alternative to an ugly thing that holds is a beautiful thing that ends, and this guild was not built to choose beauty."

"The binding doesn't hold," I said. "That's the entire point. The wards failed. The stars went out. Serevaine is guttering like a candle in a doorway, and when she goes, the crown goes hungry, and a hungry crown feeds on whatever is nearest. It has already eaten a generation of orphan girls. It is scheduled to eat a ceremony full of them within the month. Containment isn't holding, Master Corvel. Containment is the meal."

Silence again. And in the silence I watched each of the nine masters arrive, one by one, at the same place: not the truth, but the cost of the truth. I watched them do the arithmetic I had done on the mountain and refuse the sum.

It was Quillon who said it, because Quillon always says the ugly part aloud so the others don't have to.

"If the crown falls, the succession fails. If the succession fails, the border lords move. If the border lords move, the wars come back — the real ones, the ones from before the wards, the ones your maps show as blank spaces because we agreed as a guild never to chart them again." He spread his hands. "You are asking us to trade a managed horror for an unmanaged one. We decline."

"It's not managed," I said. "It's starving. Those aren't the same."

"No," Corvel said softly. "But they rhyme, and we have learned to live with rhymes."

* * *

They did it properly. That's the thing I keep circling and can't put down: they did it *properly*. No rage, no drama, no guards dragging me out by the collar. Erasure at the Guild of Impossible Things is a liturgy, and I sat through all of it because running would have meant admitting it had power over me, and because some stubborn clerk-shaped piece of me wanted the minutes taken accurately.

They struck my name from the rolls with a blunt silver stylus, the way you strike a debtor's name, the way you strike a dead man's. They read the declaration: that Cartographer Third Class Coral Ishikawa had violated the first law, that his access was revoked, that his works were seized for review, that his name was removed from all indices, cross-references, and marginalia, and that no member of the guild would henceforth acknowledge his existence, upon penalty of sharing it.

*Upon penalty of sharing it.* I wrote that phrase in my head, in master ink, in the only ledger they couldn't seize.

Then they took the books. Fourteen years of my hand. The bottled thunderstorm catalogue. The debt of one forgotten name. The migration patterns of things that don't officially exist. The map of the archive itself, drawn from memory, corrected nightly, more accurate than the building deserved. Clerks carried them out in armloads, and I watched my life leave the room in other people's hands, and the strange thing — the thing I'll tell Mirabel someday, if there is a someday — is how light I felt. Not hollow. Light. As if the books had been ballast and I had mistaken the sinking for standing still.

Vess did not come to say goodbye. Of course she didn't. Acknowledgment was the crime. She walked past me in the corridor outside the Chapter Hall with her eyes fixed on the middle distance like a good guildswoman, and if her sleeve happened to brush mine as she passed, well. Accidents happen in corridors.

Something slid into my coat pocket, weightless, practiced, in the space between one step and the next.

She was gone before I could do anything so stupid as turn around.

* * *

I didn't open it until I was three streets from the guildhall, in the doorway of a chandler's shop that smelled of tallow and rain, with my collar up and my name already beginning to feel like a rumor. Old habits. Twenty-two years of keeping secrets teaches you where to stand when you read them.

It was a book. Small, fat, bound in grey leather gone soft at the corners — my own first-year field manual, the one every cartographer gets, the one I'd assumed was in the seizure pile. Inside the cover, in Vess's cramped hand, a single line:

*They kept the vault pages. Ask yourself why the rest were enough to condemn you but not enough to burn.*

And tucked against the flyleaf, folded twice, a map.

My own work — she'd pulled it from my seized ledgers, or had it copied; either way it was my linework, my hatching, my obsessive little annotations. But annotated further, in her hand, in ordinary mortal ink that any magistrate could read: the undercroft beneath the cathedral, the ceremonial hall above it, and beneath *that*, a space that appeared on no official plan. A vault. The crown's true vault, where the original ledger of the binding slept beside whatever remained of the man it was woven around. She'd marked the blind spots — the places the wards' sightlines crossed and cancelled, gaps I would have found myself eventually but not eventually enough. And at the bottom corner, no signature, no seal, just four words in handwriting that shook only slightly:

*The guild was wrong.*

I stood in the chandler's doorway with the rain coming down and the city around me going about its dimmed, wardless, candle-lit business, and I understood two things at once, the way you sometimes understand things all at once, whole, like a chord.

The first was that I had lost everything. Name, rolls, books, belonging. By nightfall I would be a rumor with wet boots. The guild's erasure was thorough; somewhere a clerk was already scraping my initials off a shelf label.

The second was that I had never been freer. Every door the guild had closed for me, the erasure had opened. I could walk into the cathedral. I could talk to a choirgirl whose voice held doors shut. I could carry the truth into the one place it had to go, and no rule of mine could stop me, because I no longer had any rules. They hadn't destroyed my life. They'd released it from evidence.

Exile, it turns out, is just authorship with the safety off.

I folded the map along Vess's creases and put it inside my shirt, against my chest, where the important documents go. Then I did the last thing a guildsman would do and the first thing a free man must: I bought a cheap notebook and a pot of ordinary ink from the chandler, ordinary black ink that any hand could alter, and on the first page I wrote, in my own name, which was now mine alone to spend:

*Coral Ishikawa. Day one. The king is in the vault. The girl is in the choir. There is a ceremony at month's end, and it is a feeding.*

*I am going to need a better map.*

Somewhere across the city, under a cathedral roof, a rehearsal was starting. I could almost hear it — or maybe that was wishful thinking, or maybe it was the wards themselves, thin as eggshell now, humming their last sustained note. Either way, I knew where the sound lived, and I knew the girl who made it, and I knew that by the time the crown called its great ceremony to restore the stars, I intended to be standing in the dark beneath it with a map of every place it couldn't see.

I buttoned my coat, turned my collar to the rain, and walked toward the sound.
