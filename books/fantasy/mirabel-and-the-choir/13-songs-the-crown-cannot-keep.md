## 13. Songs the Crown Cannot Keep

The old chorister's name was Brother Anselm, and he had been dying for as long as Mirabel had known him.

He sat in the same corner of the cloister garden every day, wrapped in a blanket the color of old snow, feeding crumbs to pigeons that no longer came. The winter had thinned him to a suggestion of a man — wrists like kindling, breath that whistled faintly on every inhale, as if his lungs were playing a broken flute. The choirmaster had stopped assigning him to services months ago. *Let him rest,* he'd said, in the tone he used for things he wanted forgotten.

Mirabel brought him tea anyway. She had been bringing him tea since she was nine years old and still small enough to hide in the organ loft, and Anselm was the only one who never told her to come down.

Today she came with more than tea. She came with the question that had been sitting in her chest since the night at court, since the hook behind her sternum, since the old woman's whisper about the binding: *the choir sang him onto the throne.*

She set the cup in his hands and waited while he drank, because Anselm did nothing quickly anymore, and because she had learned that patience was its own kind of key.

"Brother Anselm," she said. "When the choir sings, what is it we're actually doing?"

He looked at her over the rim of the cup. His eyes were milky at the edges but sharp at the center, the way old glass is sharp. "You know what you're doing," he said. "You've always known. You just haven't let yourself say it."

"The doors hold when I sing."

"The doors hold." He nodded slowly. "And the windows don't rattle. And the candles burn straighter. And the sick children in the infirmary sleep without dreaming of drowning." He set the cup down with a click. "A voice like yours isn't music, girl. It's mortar. It's the thing you pack between stones so the wall stands."

Mirabel knelt in the frost-wet grass beside him, not caring about her choir robes. "Then tell me what the choir built."

Anselm was quiet for a long time. A crow crossed the blank sky overhead — the sky that had been blank for weeks now, black from horizon to horizon, no stars, no moon, nothing but a darkness so complete it felt less like night than like a lid. She watched him watch it.

"You felt it at court," he said finally. "The crown pulling at you."

"Like a fishhook."

"Worse. Like a debt collector." He coughed, wet and long, and pressed a handkerchief to his lips. When it came away there was rust on it. He folded it small and hid it in his sleeve, an old man's habit of tidiness even now. "I was in the choir thirty-one years ago, girl. I stood in the second row, alto line, when they crowned Aldous the First of that name. Everyone says it was a coronation. It wasn't. It was a *binding*. We didn't sing a king into his crown. We sang a crown onto a king, and the song had teeth."

Mirabel's hands went cold in her lap. "Teeth."

"A binding needs three things." He held up fingers that trembled. "Fire to burn it into the world — that was the dragon, though none of us knew it then; we thought the light in the east was just the dawn coming early. Ink to write it down — and there were men in grey robes with ledgers, standing at the edges of the cathedral, writing everything, and I have wondered my whole life what became of their books. And a name." He lowered the hand. "A name freely given. That's the keystone. You can bind anything with fire and ink, but it won't *hold* unless someone gives it a name out of their own free will, and means it, and doesn't take it back."

"Whose name?"

Anselm looked at her for a long moment. Something moved behind his eyes — pity, or fear, or both wearing each other's clothes.

"I don't know," he said. "That was kept from us. Even the choirmaster then — old Father Bertrand — only knew his part. But I'll tell you what I do know, and you'll want to sit back on your heels for it, because it's the only reason I've stayed alive this long, rotting in this garden instead of singing in that hall." He leaned toward her, and his voice dropped to the register he'd used to teach her breath control a lifetime ago: low, steady, intimate. "A name freely given can be freely sung back. That's the flaw in every binding ever made. Fire burns one way. Ink dries one way. But a voice — a voice goes both directions. What was given forward can be carried backward. Sung home. Unmade."

Mirabel's heart was beating somewhere up near her throat. "Sung back by whom?"

"By the one who gave it, if they live. Or by their blood, if they don't. Or—" he tapped her sternum, once, gently, right where the hook had caught her— "by whoever carries the note. The name lives in the song, child. It has lived in our repertoire for thirty years, hidden inside a hymn everyone thinks is about harvest. Every chorister who ever learned the Descant of the Turning Year has been carrying a piece of a prison key in their throat and calling it music."

*The Descant of the Turning Year.* Mirabel knew it the way she knew her own heartbeat. She'd sung it at every festival since she was six. The rising middle phrase that made her chest ache in a way no other music did. She had thought it was beauty. It was architecture. It was the seam.

"But it can't be one voice," Anselm said. "You could stand on the roof and sing until your lungs bled and you wouldn't crack it alone. The name was given by many voices braided together — the whole choir, in unison, that day. So it must be ungiven the same way. The full choir, singing the descant backward, all of them meaning it, all of them free." He smiled, and it was a terrible smile, thin and bitter. "Do you see the joke of it? The crown needs us to keep singing. It feeds through the song. And the only weapon against it is also the song. It's been sitting in the cage with the bird all along."

* * *

She thought about it for two days before she understood the shape of the trap.

The choir rehearsed every morning in the great hall beneath the cathedral — the starless archive, some of the cartographers called it, though the choristers just called it *below*. It was a beautiful room, vaulted and cold, with acoustics so perfect that a whispered note came back to you dressed in harmony. Mirabel had loved it her whole life. It was the place she went when the orphanage felt too big and the city too loud.

Now she noticed what she had spent her life not noticing.

The choirmaster stood at the door during rehearsals. Not conducting — he conducted from the front, of course — but positioned at the entrance, keys on his belt, watching who came in and who went out. He had dismissed the lay brothers who used to carry water to the singers. He had moved the rehearsal schedule twice in a week, without explanation, so that no one outside the choir could predict when the room would be full. Last Tuesday he had sent away a visiting deacon with a smile that didn't reach anywhere near his eyes.

And he had changed the repertoire.

The Descant of the Turning Year was gone from the folders. In its place was new music — thick, ornate, exhausting compositions that Mirabel had never seen before, pages copied in the choirmaster's own cramped hand. Music that pulled. She felt it every time they sang it: that hook behind her sternum, setting itself, reeling in a little line at a time. The other choristers came out of rehearsals pale and quiet, rubbing their chests, complaining of dreams they couldn't remember.

He was keeping them fed to it slowly. A spoonful of voice at a time. And he had locked the one song that could end it behind his own body and his own keys.

Mirabel stood in the loft after evening service, watching him below, extinguishing candles one by one. He worked methodically, left to right, and the dark followed him across the nave like a tide. When the last candle died he stood a moment in the absolute black — and she would have sworn, would have testified before any judge, that he stood there *comfortably*. The way other men stood in sunlight.

Then his head turned, slowly, and found her in the loft, though no light remained to find her by.

"Mirabel," he called up, pleasantly. "Stay a moment after tomorrow's rehearsal. I have a new solo I'd like you to look at."

Her blood went to ice water. *A solo.* Alone with him. Alone with the man who fed girls to crowns.

"Yes, Master," she called down, and heard her own voice come out steady, and marveled at it.

* * *

She didn't sleep that night. She sat by the dormitory window with the black sky pressing against the glass, and she made herself think like a keeper of something, because that was what she was now — Anselm had said so, in his garden, with his rust-spotted handkerchief hidden in his sleeve. *You're the last note of an old song, girl. Notes don't get to choose whether they're sung. But they choose how.*

The plan assembled itself the way a chord assembles: one voice at a time.

First: the choir. Thirty-two voices, and she needed all of them, willing and free. Not coerced — Anselm had been explicit. *Freely given, freely taken back. If even one of them sings under orders, the song won't hold.* Which meant she couldn't force them, couldn't trick them, couldn't even lead them too hard. She had to make them *want* their own freedom. She began small, at supper, with questions disguised as complaints: *Doesn't the new music feel strange to you? Doesn't your chest hurt after? Don't you dream of falling?* And one by one, the answers came back frightened and true, and one by one, choristers started seeking her out in corners, lowering their voices, asking what she knew.

Second: the song. The descant was struck from the folders, but it wasn't struck from memory. Mirabel had sung it since she was six. They all had. She began rehearsing it silently, mouthing the notes during the choirmaster's interminable new compositions, teaching it with her eyes across the rows — a raised eyebrow on the third phrase, a finger tapping the rhythm against her thigh. Sotto voce rebellion. The choirmaster watched her constantly now. Let him watch. Watching wasn't hearing.

Third: the moment. Because the choirmaster guarded the rehearsals like a jailer, and the ceremony — whatever the crown was planning, the heralds had been crying it through the streets for days, *a great restoration, a ceremony of light* — would be guarded like a fortress. She needed a door he wasn't standing in.

It was the fourth thing, the thing she hadn't planned, that arrived on the third morning.

She came down early to the starless archive, earlier than anyone, hoping to stand in the empty room and feel the descant move through it once, alone, just to know the room remembered. And there was already someone there.

A young man stood in the middle of the floor holding a lantern turned low, and he was sketching — actually sketching, fast and precise, in a battered notebook — the vaulting, the pillars, the sightlines. He looked up when her foot scraped the stone, and she saw a stranger's face go through surprise, alarm, and recognition in the space of a breath.

"You're the choirgirl," he said. "From the court. The one whose voice held the doors."

"And you're the one who draws maps of places that don't exist," she said, because the guild's seal was half-visible on his satchel, and because the whole capital had heard rumors of erased record-keepers by now.

"Coral Ishikawa," he said. "Formerly. Currently nobody, which turns out to be useful." He closed the notebook partway, but not all the way — she noticed that, noted the instinct to keep his work visible, and liked him a little for it. "I need to tell you something, and I only have maybe ten minutes before the jailer arrives."

"How do you know about—"

"Everyone knows about the choirmaster's new habits. Nobody knows why. I do." He stepped closer, and the low lantern threw his shadow huge against the pillar behind him. "The crown is going to feed at the ceremony. Hundreds of voices, drawn dry, poured into the binding to keep it alive another thirty years. And I've found the vault beneath this cathedral where the real king is tied to it — and I know how the binding comes apart. From below. With ink." He paused. "But ink alone won't be enough. The binding holds from above too. It holds in the *song*."

Mirabel felt the plan and the map click together like two halves of a lock.

"There's a hymn," she said slowly. "Struck from our folders. Hidden inside the harvest music for thirty years. A name was woven into it — a name freely given — and what's freely given can be sung back. But it takes the full choir, all of them willing, and the choirmaster keeps us like—"

"Like a larder," Coral said grimly. "I know. I've mapped his blind spots. There are three ways into this room he doesn't watch, and one moment in the ceremony when every eye will be on the crown and no eye will be on the choir." He opened his notebook fully now, turning it toward her, and she saw the cathedral rendered in his quick sure lines — and threaded through the pillars, in a different ink, a melody. Her melody. The descant, written out note by note, annotated in a cartographer's careful hand: *third phrase rises here — this is the seam.*

"You wrote it down," she breathed. "You found it."

"I found a razored page in a guild ledger with fragments of a coronation anthem. Your humming filled in the rest." He almost smiled. "That's the thing about maps and songs, Mirabel. They're the same craft. Both of them are how you find your way through the dark."

Above them, far off, a door boomed open. Footsteps in the nave — unhurried, familiar, methodical. The choirmaster, coming down to count his flock.

Coral snapped the notebook shut. "Ten minutes was optimistic. Can you learn three entrances by Sunday?"

Mirabel was already moving toward the choristers' stair, her mind laying the descant over the cathedral's architecture, phrase by phrase, pillar by pillar, the song becoming a map becoming a plan.

"I grew up in this building," she said. "I *am* the map."

At the top of the stair she paused, one hand on the cold rail, and looked back down at him — the erased man with his forbidden notebook, standing in the dark room where she'd learned to sing, holding between them the only two halves of the only key.

"Sunday," she said. "Tell your ink to be ready."

And she climbed toward the lightless morning, toward rehearsal, toward the jailer's counting eyes — carrying the descant behind her teeth like a lit coal, singing it silently, *freely*, with every step.
