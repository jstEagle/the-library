## 14. Vess's Bargain

The summons came the way guild summons always came: folded into something ordinary. This time it was tucked inside a baker's receipt Coral had bought that morning — two coppers for a loaf, and beneath the price, in Vess's cramped hand, *the counting room, dusk. come alone. burn this.*

He burned it. Then he went.

The counting room sat at the heart of the guildhall like a heart indeed, though no one had called it that in a century. It was where the guild weighed what things cost — not coin, mostly, but other currencies. A year of someone's memory. The color blue, briefly misplaced from the world. The last honest word spoken by a dying man. Coral had catalogued three thousand such transactions and never once been allowed to watch one happen.

Tonight the room was empty except for Vess.

She stood at the long counting table with her sleeves rolled, her gray-streaked hair pinned up with whatever had been nearest — tonight, a stylus, which meant she'd dressed in a hurry or hadn't slept. Two lamps burned on the table between them, and between the lamps lay a single ledger.

Coral knew it at a distance of ten feet. He'd know it blindfolded by the weight of its silence. His own book — the master copy, the one seized from his desk when they struck his name from the rolls. Someone had re-bound it. Someone had been inside it.

"You've read it," he said.

"Every page." Vess didn't look up. "Twice. Once as a record-keeper, once as—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening. "Once as me."

"And?"

"And it's the finest piece of work the guild has produced in two hundred years." Now she looked up, and her eyes were red-rimmed and steady. "Which is exactly the problem."

* * *

She poured tea without asking. That was Vess — she'd been pouring him tea since he was nine years old and faking his way through his first inventory, and she had never once asked whether he wanted it, because she already knew. He took the cup anyway. His hands needed something to hold that wasn't her throat or his own hair.

"The masters convened last night," she said. "Seven hours behind sealed doors. I was permitted to attend as recorder, which means I was permitted to write down what they decided, not what they said." She slid a folded paper across the table. "This is what they decided."

Coral unfolded it. Guild script, formal, bloodless:

*Item the first: the record-keeper formerly designated Coral Ishikawa remains stricken from the rolls. His restoration is not under consideration.*

*Item the second: the evidence described herein is classified under First Law. Its destruction is authorized.*

*Item the third: the Emberhold does not exist.*

He read the third line four times. "They're going to erase Serevaine."

"They're going to erase everything," Vess said. "The dragon. The binding. Aldous. Your maps. The girl." Her voice didn't waver, but her hands, wrapped around her own cup, had gone white at the knuckles. "There's a protocol for it. There's always a protocol. We've done it before — you've seen the gaps in the older ledgers, the decades that don't add up. You thought those were sloppiness."

"I thought they were sloppiness," Coral said quietly. "I built my whole life on thinking they were sloppiness."

"Well. They weren't. They were us."

* * *

He set the tea down because his hands had started to shake, and he would not shake in front of her, not tonight.

"Say the rest," he said. "You didn't summon me at dusk to read me an execution order. Say the part you actually came to say."

Vess exhaled. For a moment she looked every one of her sixty-odd years, and then some — looked like a woman who had spent forty years keeping other people's secrets and had just discovered the weight of her own.

"There's a way back," she said. "For you. The masters offered it before I did — I'm their messenger, that's all, so don't mistake this for mercy. Here is the bargain." She counted it off on her fingers, the way she'd taught him to count off inventory items when he was small. "One: your name returns to the rolls. Clean entry, backdated, as if the erasure never happened. Two: the ledgers stay closed. All of them. The binding, the dragon, the crown — sealed under First Law for the duration of the current reign and beyond. Three: the evidence is destroyed. Tonight, by your hand, in front of witnesses. Four—"

"Four," Coral said.

"Four." She met his eyes. "You abandon the king. Whatever's in that vault stays in that vault. Whatever's wearing him walks free. And the girl goes home to her choir and never learns why her voice matters, and in twenty years she teaches it to some other child, and the wheel turns, and the kingdom keeps its light, and nobody burns."

The lamps hissed. Somewhere above them, the guildhall settled against the starless night with a sound like an old man shifting in bed.

"That's the bargain," Coral said.

"That's the bargain."

"And if I refuse?"

Vess's face did something terrible and small. "Then the erasure becomes thorough. Not just the rolls, Coral. The records. The memories. There are guild members whose entire craft is forgetting, and they are very good, and they will find you, and one morning you'll wake up in some border town knowing how to hold a pen and nothing else about yourself. I've seen it done. I signed the orders once, thirty years ago, and I have regretted it every day since, which is why I'm telling you this now instead of letting them do it to you in your sleep."

* * *

Coral walked to the window. Below, the capital lay dark in the way it had lain dark for weeks now — not the friendly dark of night but the flat, sourceless black of a sky with nothing behind it. People had stopped looking up. That was the cruelest part, he thought. Not the missing stars themselves but how quickly everyone agreed to stop missing them.

"You know what I keep coming back to?" he said. "Chapter nine of my own ledger. The debt of one forgotten name. I copied that page when I was nineteen. I didn't understand it. A name freely given — I thought it was poetry. Some old ritual language that meant nothing anymore." He turned around. "It wasn't poetry. It was Mirabel's grandmother. A real woman who walked into that choir and gave away the only thing she had, and we wrote it down, and then we razored it out, and then we forgot her twice."

"Coral—"

"The guild's first law is silence," he said. "I've lived by it my whole life. I believed in it. Do you know why? Because I thought silence was protection. I thought we were the vault door standing between dangerous truths and people who'd be hurt by them." He picked up the ledger — his ledger — and held it out over the table, over the lamps. "But we're not the vault door. We're the thing in the vault. We've been feeding the crown for thirty years and calling it stewardship."

"If you destroy that book, the binding can never be undone. The words, the terms, the original name — it's all in there. You'd be burning the only key."

"No," Coral said. "You'd be burning the only key. I'm not holding it." He set the ledger back down, gently, the way you set down something living. "I'm not destroying anything, Vess. And I'm not coming back."

* * *

Something in her shoulders let go — not defeat, he realized. Relief. Terrible, guilty relief, the kind a person feels when a long dread finally resolves into a shape they can fight.

"Good," she said, and her voice cracked on it. "Gods forgive me, good. I sat here for six hours hoping you'd say yes and praying you'd say no, and I couldn't tell you which prayer was which."

"Then why deliver the offer at all?"

"Because if I didn't, they'd have sent Master Corvel, and Corvel doesn't argue. He just starts the forgetting." She was already moving, pulling open drawers, her hands quick and certain. "And because I needed you to hear the terms out loud so you'd understand what happens next. They vote at dawn. Full conclave. And Coral — they're not going to debate it. The crown has been the guild's largest client for three hundred years. Every master in that room owes their position to the arrangement. They will choose the institution. They always choose the institution."

"Not all of them."

Vess paused. In the lamplight her face was unreadable, and then, deliberately, she made it readable — showed him the thing underneath, the thing she'd carried alone for thirty years.

"No," she said. "Not all of them."

She pressed something into his hand: a thin folio, unmarked, warm from hiding against her body. Inside were pages — copies, in her own hand, of everything the conclave intended to erase. The binding's terms. Serevaine's location. The vault's approach. And on the last page, a single line in fresh ink:

*The guild will survive this decision. Whether it deserves to is another matter. Go. Don't look back. Don't write to me. And Coral — when you fix this, fix it loudly. I am so tired of quiet.*

* * *

He left through the archive stair, the way he'd come a thousand times, past shelves of impossible things sleeping in their bottles and boxes and bound volumes. At the bottom of the stair he stopped, once, and put his palm flat against the wall — the plaster he knew better than his own skin, the building that had raised him, the only home he'd ever had outside of Vess's kitchen.

Then he walked out into the starless dark, toward the cathedral district, toward Mirabel, toward the vault and the king and the fire running low.

Behind him, in the counting room, the guild lit its lamps and began, page by page, to forget.

By dawn, the rolls would be clean. By noon, the ledgers would be ash. And somewhere in the guildhall's deepest shelf, a new entry was being written in the master ink — the ink that made things true — recording that the Guild of Impossible Things had never kept a king, had never fed a flame, had never known a girl who sang to locked doors.

The guild chose the institution.

Coral chose the truth.

And far to the east, in a hollow mountain, the last dragon's ember sank another fraction lower, and the night grew one hour darker, and the clock that no one could see ticked toward the ceremony where hundreds of voices would rise together — and be taken.
