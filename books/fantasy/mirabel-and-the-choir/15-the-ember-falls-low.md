## 15. The Ember Falls Low

The light went out of the world the way a candle goes out when someone cups their hand around it — not all at once, but in stages, each one smaller than the last.

Mirabel noticed it first in the choir loft. The morning rehearsal had always needed no lamps; the great rose window over the cathedral's west transept poured enough gold across the floor to warm the boards even in winter. But that morning the window gave only a pale suggestion of itself, like a window remembered rather than seen. The choirmaster called for candles before Terce, and the choristers exchanged glances over their scores, because candles at morning rehearsal had never once been called for in living memory.

By noon, the bakeries were lighting ovens by tallow-light. By evening, the whole capital was a city of small flames — ten thousand windows lit against a dusk that came too early and stayed too long.

"It's her," Mirabel said quietly, to no one, to everyone. "She's going out."

Old Saret, the chorister with the ruined voice who still sat in the back row because he refused to stop singing, heard her anyway. He always did. He leaned close, his breath smelling of licorice root, and said nothing at all, which from Saret was a kind of agreement.

The realm's remaining light dimmed to candle-strength. That was not a figure of speech. Mirabel walked home through streets where shopkeepers had set out lanterns at midday and found them barely brighter than the paper screens they burned behind. Shadows had stopped behaving. They pooled in doorways and clung to corners and sometimes, when Mirabel looked too long, they seemed to lean toward her, hungry for the little warmth she carried. Everyone carried less warmth now. She could feel it in the crowd — a city of people unconsciously huddling toward one another, strangers pressing shoulder to shoulder at crossings as if the dark between them were something that could be caught.

Panic spread the way frost spreads across glass: slowly, then all at once.

First there were the prayers. Then the processions. Then the looting of the chandler's shops on Copper Lane, and the fistfights outside the granaries, and the rumor — repeated so often it acquired the weight of scripture — that the stars had not gone out at all but had *fallen*, and were lying in the fields beyond the walls, and whoever gathered them up would rule whatever was left of the sky.

On the fourth day of the dimming, a woman drowned herself in the Fountain of Saint Vellum because she believed she had already died and this was the afterlife. On the fifth day, the bells rang wrong — three times at dawn instead of twice — and half the city took it for an omen and the other half took it for a mistake, and both halves were frightened.

And on the sixth day, the crown made its announcement.

* * *

They posted it on the cathedral doors themselves, which told Mirabel everything before she read a word. Royal proclamations went to the town criers and the broadsheet presses. Only things meant to be sacred went on the cathedral doors, nailed up beside the notices about feast days and funerals.

**BY ORDER OF HIS MAJESTY THE KING:**

**THE STARS SHALL BE RESTORED.**

**ON THE FEAST OF THE LONG NIGHT, ALL VOICES OF THE REALM ARE SUMMONED TO THE GREAT CATHEDRAL TO SING THE OLD HYMN OF LIGHTING. LET EVERY SOUL BRING ITS VOICE, THAT THE DARKNESS MAY BE DRIVEN BACK AND THE SKY MADE WHOLE AGAIN.**

**ATTENDANCE IS NOT COMPELLED. ATTENDANCE IS HONOURED.**

Mirabel read it three times. Around her, the crowd read it too, and she watched hope move through them like a tide — shoulders lifting, breath catching, an old man crossing himself with trembling fingers and whispering *thank the crown, thank the crown.*

She felt sick.

Because she knew what the Old Hymn of Lighting was. Every chorister knew it; it was in the beginner's folio, the first real piece they learned, four parts and a descant that climbed like a bird released. It was beautiful. It was also, she now understood, a feeding song. It was built to gather voices — hundreds of them, thousands — and pour them into a single channel. She had felt the shape of that channel once already, singing for the court, the hook in her chest, the pull like a hand closing around something soft and essential inside her.

One voice, the crown could sip from. A choir of thousands, offered freely, honoured, celebrated—

It would be a banquet.

"They'll think they're being saved," Saret said beside her. His voice was very quiet. "That's the cruelty of it. It will feel like salvation right up until it isn't."

"The choirmaster knows." Mirabel's hands had gone cold inside her gloves. "He wrote the arrangement. I've seen his score — the one he keeps locked. There are markings in it, instructions, lines that aren't music at all."

"Then you know what you have to do between now and the Feast."

"I don't." Her voice cracked and she hated it. "Saret, I don't know anything. I know a name has to be sung back — my grandmother's name, freely given, freely returned — but the binding needs the full choir to carry it, and every rehearsal between now and the Feast is going to be guarded like a prison. He watches me. He counts us in and out. Yesterday he moved my place in the formation so I'm standing directly beneath him, in the pulpit's shadow, where he can put his hand on my shoulder if he needs to."

"What does the hand mean?"

"I don't know," Mirabel whispered. "But Coral is somewhere under our feet right now, in the dark under the cathedral, and I can't reach him, and the ember is dying, and in nine days the entire city is going to walk into that building singing with everything it has."

She looked up at the proclamation again, at the words **THE STARS SHALL BE RESTORED**, and understood that they were true. That was the worst part. The stars *would* be restored — briefly, guttering, fed on the brightest part of every soul in the square. And then the flame would fail again, faster, because a fire fed on stolen light burns hotter and dies sooner, and next time there would be fewer voices left to feed it.

A trap wearing the face of a rescue. Hope and hunger in the same announcement, and no way to tell the crowd one from the other.

* * *

Rehearsal that evening was the strangest Mirabel had ever endured.

The choirmaster — Master Ellery-Vane, though no one had ever dared shorten it — stood before them glowing like a man whose ship had come in. He had new robes. He had new arrangements, copied fair and bound in ribbon. He had, most tellingly, a *joy* about him that Mirabel had never seen in eleven years of his instruction, and it terrified her more than any anger ever had.

"Children," he said, spreading his arms in the candlelight, "in nine days, you will be part of the greatest act of faith this kingdom has ever witnessed. The eyes of the realm will be on this choir. Sing as you have never sung."

He began to teach them the new setting of the Hymn of Lighting, and Mirabel felt the trap close around her note by note.

He had rearranged it. Of course he had. The old version let each voice keep its own line, four streams braided together. The new version wove them tighter and tighter as it climbed, pulling every part toward the descant — *her* line, she realized with a lurch, the descant sat above her range exactly, the highest thread, the one the whole song would hang from. In the old hymn the descant was ornament. In this one it was a spine. Everything else would lean on it. Everything else would pour into it.

He had written her a harness out of harmony.

"And the descant," Master Ellery-Vane said, turning to her with that terrible joy, "belongs to Mirabel. Naturally."

"Naturally," Mirabel said.

Her voice came out steady. Some part of her — the part that had learned to sing locked doors shut, the part that had spent years making her gift small enough to hide — stepped forward and did the work while the rest of her screamed. She sang the descant through once, alone, and heard how beautifully it fit, and understood that he had designed it to fit, the way a lock is designed for one key.

When rehearsal ended, he dismissed the others and kept her behind, and for one long moment they simply stood in the empty loft among the guttering candles.

"You feel it, don't you," he said softly. "In the music. What it wants."

Mirabel said nothing.

"It doesn't want to hurt anyone, child. It wants to *hold*. All that light, all those voices, scattered and wasted in the dark — and you could gather them. You could be the vessel. Generations of choristers before you were only ever spoons, dipping. You'd be the cup." He touched her chin, tilting her face up, almost tender. "Your grandmother would have been proud."

The name landed like a stone dropped down a well. Mirabel kept her face still, kept her breathing even, kept every secret she had behind her teeth — *you fed her to it, you fed all of them, I found the page, I know what you are* — and said only:

"Yes, Master."

He smiled and released her, and as she walked down the loft stairs into the dark, she counted the days again. Nine days. Nine rehearsals. Nine chances for him to notice her bending notes flat, or reaching Coral below, or doing anything at all except the exact thing he had written for her.

Behind her, in the loft, she heard him begin to hum the descant to himself. Practicing her part. Learning it by heart.

As if he intended to keep it.
