## 16. Infiltrating the Ceremony

The cathedral had been mapped a hundred times before Coral ever mapped it, but never the way he mapped it now.

Every cartographer in the Guild of Impossible Things knew the Cathedral of the Choir as a fixed point — the one structure in the capital that appeared on every chart, in every ledger, unchanging, unquestioned. It was the guild's habit to trust what everyone could see. That was precisely why Coral trusted nothing about it.

He spent four nights walking it. Not the nave, not the choir loft, not any of the places the ceremony's stewards were busy draping with white linen and hanging lanterns that burned with a light too steady to be fire. He walked the edges. The service corridors behind the reliquary. The stair that went down from the bell tower and stopped at a wall that was newer than the stones around it. The crypt door that the groundskeeper unlocked with a key he kept on a cord around his neck and never spoke of.

And everywhere he walked, he drew.

His erased status, which had felt like dying ten days ago, turned out to be a kind of invisibility no guild seal could purchase. Guards looked through him. Stewards stepped around him without seeing him. A man with no name, no livery, no ledger-line anywhere in the kingdom's records was a man the world had agreed not to notice — and the world kept its agreements even when it didn't know it was making them.

*Blind spot,* he wrote, in the margin of his working map, at the junction where the pilgrims' corridor met the undercroft stair. *Two sentries, both facing outward. They watch for people coming up. No one watches for people coming down.*

*Blind spot,* again, by the linen stores: *Stewards rotate at the third bell. Forty seconds, door unwatched.*

*Blind spot,* at the great west doors: *Four hundred choristers will enter in procession. After the two-hundredth, the counters stop counting. They count heads because the crown told them heads matter. They have not yet learned why.*

That last line he wrote and then sat looking at for a long time, because he knew exactly why. The crown wanted voices. Hundreds of them. And when Serevaine's flame guttered low enough — and it was guttering; you could see it now in the sky itself, a brownish film over the stars' empty seats, a dusk that never lifted — the crown would draw on all of them at once, and it would not be gentle about it.

Mirabel had explained it to him in the safehouse above the chandler's shop, her hands moving the way they did when she talked about music, shaping something invisible in the air. "A binding takes a name," she'd said. "But a feeding takes a choir. Grandmother's name built the cage. Every ceremony since has been the crown — eating."

"Then we don't stop the ceremony," Coral had said. "We get inside it."

Now, on the fourth night, with his map of blind spots nearly complete, he stood in the shadow of the bell tower and watched the last lanterns go out, and let himself feel, for one breath, how enormous the thing was that they were about to do. Then he folded the map into his coat and went back to the chandler's, because fear was a luxury for after.

* * *

The two choristers Mirabel had chosen were called Tam and Odile, and Coral approved of them within a minute of meeting them, for the simple reason that they were frightened and did not pretend otherwise.

Tam was sixteen, a boy with a cracked voice and a stubborn jaw, who had been in the orphan choir since he was six and had noticed — the way children notice, sideways and without words — that choristers who sang well at ceremonies sometimes didn't come back to rehearsals afterward. "They say they were promoted," Tam said. "Promoted where? Nobody sings there. I've heard the rooms upstairs. Nobody sings there."

Odile was older, nineteen, and had been Mirabel's deskmate since childhood, and had seen the razored page. She had read it twice. She hadn't slept properly since. "My grandmother sang in this choir," was all she said, when Mirabel asked if she was sure. "I want to know what happened to her voice."

Coral laid his map across the chandler's worktable, weighting the corners with candle stubs, and walked them through it the way he'd once walked apprentices through archive routes: briskly, precisely, leaving no room for the imagination to wander into panic.

"You enter with the procession. West doors, fourth rank, behind the tall ones — tall choristers block the counters' sightlines, and the counters stop counting after two hundred anyway. You do not sing. You mouth the words. Mirabel will tell you why that matters." He glanced at her; she nodded. "At the third bell of the ceremony, when the stewards rotate, you leave your rank one at a time, not together, and you take the pilgrims' corridor down to the undercroft. There is a door at the bottom of the undercroft stair that is newer than its wall. Behind it is the way down to the vault."

"And you'll be below already," Mirabel said. Not a question. She'd made him promise.

"I'll be below already," Coral confirmed. "If anything goes wrong above — if the singing changes, if you feel the pull start—" he looked at each of them in turn, "—you run. Not toward me. Out. Whatever happens below, the realm needs the choir more than it needs one cartographer."

"That's not true," Tam said.

"It's mathematically defensible," Coral said.

"It's still not true," Tam said, with the flat certainty of the young, and Odile almost smiled, and Mirabel reached over and squeezed Coral's wrist once, briefly, which settled the argument better than any words would have.

They rehearsed the route until midnight. They rehearsed it again at dawn. By the time the bells began ringing for the ceremony itself — a peal so long and so loud it seemed less like celebration than like the city being shouted awake — Coral had already gone down.

* * *

The undercroft smelled of cold stone and old incense, and beneath that, faintly, of something scorched. Coral moved through it with his map held open against the wall, checking angles he'd memorized, and found the blind spots exactly where he'd drawn them. Two sentries at the stairhead, faces turned outward toward the cathedral's noise. The linen-store passage empty during the rotation. The new door in the old wall, locked.

He had expected the lock. He had not expected to pick it in under a minute, or to feel, as the tumblers gave, a shudder run through the stone — deep, rhythmic, like a heartbeat in the building's bones. The ceremony had begun. Above him, four hundred voices were finding their first note.

He went through the door and closed it softly behind him, and started down.

* * *

Vess found him on the second landing.

Coral heard the footsteps and pressed himself into an alcove, hand going to the razor he'd taken to carrying — and then stopped, because he knew the tread. Slow, uneven on the left side, the walk of a woman who had spent forty years descending archive stairs with her head bent over books.

"Your left foot drags on the turns," Vess said to the darkness. "It always did. You'd have known that if you'd ever listened to yourself instead of only listening to doors."

Coral stepped out of the alcove. His mentor stood three steps below him, holding a shuttered lamp, and she looked — older. Ten days older, somehow, though it had been months. Her face had the set of someone who had been making decisions in the dark and did not enjoy a single one of them.

"You shouldn't be here," Coral said. "If the guild—"

"The guild knows I'm here. The guild sent me." She said it flatly, and watched him absorb it, and watched the hurt arrive. "Yes. They worked out where you'd enter. I told them I'd intercept you. Erase you quietly, before you could reach the vault. It's what the first law requires."

Coral's hand tightened on the razor. Below them, muffled by stone, the choir swelled into its second hymn, and the heartbeat-shudder in the walls quickened to match it.

"So erase me," he said.

Vess looked at him for a long moment. Then she did something Coral had never seen her do in fifteen years: she smiled, small and tired and entirely without armor.

"Do you know," she said, "in thirty years of keeping records, I have erased four hundred and eleven names. I could recite every one. I used to tell myself that remembering them was a kind of penance. It isn't, Coral. It's a kind of hoarding. You keep the names in a drawer instead of giving them back." She shifted the lamp to her other hand. "I am done hoarding."

She reached into her coat and brought out a key.

It was not large. Iron, old, worn smooth at the bow where a hundred hands had gripped it, and it caught the lamplight with a dull gleam that Coral recognized instantly, because he had catalogued it once, years ago, in a ledger entry he'd assumed was ceremonial: *One key, master, opens what the guild agrees should stay shut.* The Master Key. The single object that made the Guild of Impossible Things possible — the thing that opened every sealed archive, every warded vault, every door the guild had ever declared forbidden.

"They'll know you took it," Coral said.

"They'll know I gave it away. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole point." Vess held it out. "You were right, Coral. About all of it. The dragon-fire, the binding, the crown. I checked your claims against records even you were never allowed to see, and every line of yours was true, and the guild's response was to choose the lock over the truth. Because the lock is what we are. Take away the secrets and there's no guild left — just a room full of cowards with good handwriting." Her voice didn't rise. It didn't need to. "I've decided I'd rather be a coward with honest handwriting. But you — you were never a coward. You were just invisible. There's a difference there too."

Coral took the key. It was heavier than it looked, the way true things usually are.

"Why?" he said. It came out rougher than he intended. "Why risk everything? You spent your whole life inside those walls."

"Because I read your ledgers before they seized them." Vess's eyes glinted in the lamplight. "All of them. Even the one with the entry you don't remember writing. Do you want to know what it said?"

The hairline crack from all those months ago — the entry in his own neat hand that he had no memory of making. Coral nodded once.

"It said: *The record-keeper will be given the key when he stops asking permission.*" She shrugged. "Guild prophecy is mostly nonsense. But someone, a long time ago, wrote your name into our oldest book, and wrote that entry beside it, and I have been waiting my entire career to find out who you were going to become." She stepped back, down the stairs, away from him. "The vault door below is warded with guild-work. Your picks won't touch it. That key will. Go free your king, cartographer. Map something worth signing."

"Vess." He said it before he could stop himself. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me." She raised the lamp in something between a salute and a farewell. "Write me down somewhere. That's thanks enough. One name, freely remembered."

Then she turned and climbed back up into the dark, dragging her left foot slightly on the turns, and Coral stood alone on the landing with the master key burning cold in his palm and the choir thundering overhead.

* * *

The last door took the key without complaint.

The wards that should have flayed him unraveled at the iron's touch like thread off a spool, and the vault opened, and the heat rolled out to meet him — dry, vast, ancient, the heat of a forge that had been banked for thirty years and was now nearly out. Far below, in the dark, something that had once been a king lifted its head.

And far above, four hundred voices rose together, and among them — bending every note a hair flat, buying seconds, fighting — Coral could almost hear Mirabel.

He put the key around his neck, took out his ledger, and started down.
