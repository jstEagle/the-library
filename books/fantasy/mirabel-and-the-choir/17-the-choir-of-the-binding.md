## 17. The Choir of the Binding

The cathedral held four hundred voices, and every one of them was afraid.

Mirabel felt it before the first note — fear moving through the ranks like a draft under a door. The choristers stood in their crescents below the dais, white robes, candle-strength light guttering along the walls because the realm's remaining glow had been funneled here, into this room, for this hour. Above the altar, on a cushion of black velvet, sat the crown.

It did not look like much. That was the horror of it. A circlet of dull gold, older than any coin, resting where a relic rests. But Mirabel had sung to locked doors and felt them hold, and she knew what it was to touch magic with her voice, and what she felt now was the crown *listening*. The whole room tilted toward it, the way flowers tilt toward sun.

*Sing*, it seemed to say. *You came here to sing.*

The choirmaster raised his hands. He had dressed for the occasion in vestments she'd never seen, deep blue shot with silver thread, and his face wore an expression she had trusted for nine years and could no longer read at all. Hunger, maybe. Or grief. She had learned in the last month that those two faces were often the same face.

"Children," he said, and his voice carried the way it always did, warm as bread, "the stars went out because we stopped singing. Tonight we begin again."

Four hundred throats opened. Mirabel opened hers with them.

The first chord hit her like cold water.

She had expected the crown to pull. The old chorister, Brother Anselm, dying in his narrow bed behind the choir dormitory, had told her what to expect: *it will drink you, girl, the way a drain drinks water, and you'll feel it as pleasure right up until there isn't enough of you left to feel anything.* So she had braced herself for a hook, a hand, a weight.

What she got was a current. The moment the harmony assembled itself beneath the vaulted dark — four hundred voices braided into one long rope of sound — the crown took hold of the rope and began to *haul*. She felt it in her sternum, in her teeth, in the soft places behind her eyes. It wasn't painful. That was the trap. It felt like being filled. Like the one perfect note she'd been chasing all her life finally arriving, except it kept arriving, and arriving, and something in her chest was being paid out to buy it.

*Steady*, she told herself. *Flat. Every note, a hair flat.*

That was the plan. Her plan, built from Anselm's confession and three sleepless nights of testing against locked doors: a binding is a song, and a song is made of notes agreeing with each other. If the choir agreed perfectly, the rope would be seamless, and the crown would drink down its length without spilling. But if one voice bent — just a hair, just enough to make the harmony grind — the rope frayed. And a fraying rope takes longer to swallow.

So Mirabel sang the ceremony's great hymn, *O Light Remembered*, and bent every note flat by the smallest interval a trained ear could catch. Not enough to be heard as wrong. Enough to be felt as friction.

Around her, two other voices did the same. Tam and Wren, the choristers Coral had smuggled in through the blind spots he'd mapped — the disused sacristy stair, the rood-loft access nobody had used since the fire of her grandmother's time. They'd rehearsed this in whispers for two days: *bend low, hold low, never let your note settle.* Tam sang with his eyes shut and his jaw tight. Wren, small and fierce, looked like she was chewing the flatness out of every syllable.

And the crown noticed.

Mirabel felt the attention swing toward her section like a searchlight. The pulling intensified — a sudden deep drag, as if the thing above the altar had leaned closer — and the hymn's second verse began to come apart at the edges. A soprano near the front wavered. An alto lost her line entirely and clung to the nearest voice. The choirmaster's hands cut sharper shapes in the air, correcting, gathering, and the choir reassembled itself because that was what choirs did, they followed the hands—

—and the seamlessness returned, and the drinking sped up, and Mirabel understood with a lurch that the crown didn't care about perfection. Perfection was just efficiency. What it cared about was *flow*.

Fine. Then she would not give it flow.

She changed tactics mid-phrase. Instead of bending flat, she began to breathe wrong — taking air a half-beat early, so her entries landed slightly ahead of the beat, then slightly behind, a tiny stutter woven through her line. To anyone listening from the nave it would sound like nerves. To the crown it must have felt like grit in the gears, because the dragging sensation stuttered too, caught, resumed, caught again.

Beside her, Wren caught on and copied her. Then Tam. Three voices out of four hundred, out of rhythm by a hair, out of tune by a hair, and the great hymn limped forward like a cart with a cracked wheel.

The choirmaster turned his head.

Just slightly. Just enough. His eyes found Mirabel across the crescent of sopranos, and for one suspended moment the music seemed to happen somewhere else, in some other room, while the two of them regarded each other across nine years of bread-warm trust and everything she'd learned since. His mouth did not change shape. His hands did not stop conducting. But his eyes said, with terrible clarity: *I know what you're doing, and I know why, and if you stop now I might forgive you.*

She sang flatter.

His eyes closed for half a second — an old man's brief prayer or an executioner's sigh — and then he turned back to the altar and gave the crown exactly what it wanted, pouring his own considerable gift into the rope of sound, and Mirabel realized with a fresh wave of cold that he wasn't guarding the rehearsals anymore. He was feeding. He had been feeding all along, willingly, and whatever was left of him in there had decided to go down conducting.

*Hold on, Coral*, she thought, and put her shoulder against the note.

* * *

Below the cathedral, in the dark under the crypt, Coral moved through the vault's throat with a stolen key and a map drawn from memory.

He had spent eleven years mapping spaces no one was supposed to enter, and he had never once been grateful for the skill until tonight. The passages beneath the cathedral predated it — older stone, older angles, corridors that forgot to be straight. Without light they were unnavigable; without his maps they were unnavigable anyway. He counted steps, checked chalk marks, and thought about Mirabel's voice the way a man thinks about a rope holding him over a drop.

Because he could hear it. Faintly, through forty feet of ancient masonry, the hymn came down to him warped and vast, like weather. And threaded through it, when he stopped and pressed his palm flat to the wall, he could feel the difference her flatness made — a tremor in the stone, a hesitation in the hum of the binding that filled these depths like water fills a cistern.

*She's still fighting.* Good. Keep fighting. Buy me ten more minutes.

The master key Vess had pressed into his hand an hour ago fit locks that hadn't been opened in thirty years. Door after door yielded — storerooms full of tribute ledgers, a chamber of sealed instruments whose purposes he chose not to guess, and then a stair going down, down, into stone so old it had stopped pretending to be architecture and become geology.

At the bottom was the vault door.

It was not locked. That frightened him more than any lock would have. The door stood ajar on hinges of black iron, and beyond it the air glowed faintly, ember-colored, pulsing in slow rhythm — and the rhythm matched the hymn overhead, contraction and release, contraction and release, the whole chamber breathing with the song.

Coral stepped inside.

The binding filled the room like a web fills a doorway — visible only where the ember-light caught it, threads of gold running from floor to ceiling, converging, all of them, on a single point at the center: a chair. On the chair sat a man.

King Aldous. The real one. Diminished to a whisper of himself, hands folded in his lap, eyes open and fixed on nothing, gold threads running into his wrists, his throat, his temples. Thirty years of anchoring. Thirty years of being the knot the crown tied itself around.

Coral had the ledger under his arm — the original, Vess's parting gift, the book with the razored page restored in his own careful hand. He had planned the next step a hundred times. Read the binding aloud. Offer his name in guild ink. Unmake it from below while Mirabel unmade it from above.

But the king's eyes moved. Found him. Focused.

"Don't," Aldous said. His voice was rust and paper. "Whatever you've come to do — don't."

Above him, faint through the stone, the hymn swelled toward its climax, and the golden threads brightened, drank, tightened — and Mirabel's flat notes ground against the machine of the ceremony like sand in a bearing, buying seconds, buying seconds.

Coral opened the ledger.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I've read every page of what this cost. I'm doing it anyway."

The king's ruined face did something that was almost a smile. "They always say that," he whispered, as the threads began to turn toward Coral like serpents scenting warmth, "right before the crown learns their names."

The ember-light flared. The first gold thread whipped free of the chair and struck toward him, humming a single hungry note —

— and overhead, Mirabel's voice bent flat, and the whole chamber shuddered, and Coral began to read.
