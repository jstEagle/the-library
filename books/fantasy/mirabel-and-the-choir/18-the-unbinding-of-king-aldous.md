## 18. The Unbinding of King Aldous

The vault door had no handle.

Of course it didn't. Coral had spent three days mapping the cathedral's blind spots, and every one of them had led him here, to a wall of grey stone beneath the choir loft, where the mortar was older than the cathedral and the stone was older than the mortar. The door was there — he could feel it the way a cartographer feels a coastline he's never walked, some certainty in the fingers before the eyes confirm it — but there was no handle, no keyhole, no seam.

There was only the guild's master key, warm in his pocket where Vess had pressed it into his hand, and a single line of instruction she had scratched into the wax paper wrapping it, in the guild's oldest shorthand, the one they used for debts that could not be spoken aloud:

*The door opens to what it is owed.*

Coral knelt. His knees found the cold stone and his breath fogged in air that had not been disturbed in thirty years. He took out the key and held it flat on his palm, and the key did something keys should not do: it leaned. It tilted toward the wall like a compass needle finding north, like iron finding a lodestone, like a name finding its owner.

He pressed it to the stone.

The wall drank the light. Not dimmed it — drank it, the way the sky had drunk the stars, and for one vertiginous moment Coral was back in that first terrible night, standing on the guildhall roof with his ledgers clutched to his chest, watching the dark eat the constellations one by one while the guildmasters sealed the windows and told each other it was weather. It had never been weather. It had been hunger. Thirty years of hunger, patient as interest accruing, and it had all been flowing through this wall.

The stone unfolded. Not swung — unfolded, the way a page unfolds, and Coral thought: *of course, of course, everything in this kingdom is a book, and we have all been living inside someone else's margins.*

He went down.

The stair was narrow and spiraled the wrong way, widdershins, against the turn of the world, and with each step the air grew warmer and stranger. Coral counted the steps out of habit — two hundred and six — and marked the turns in his head, because a man who had spent his life mapping other people's secrets did not stop mapping just because he was descending into the dark to unmake a king. If he died down here, someone should at least be able to find his body. That was the whole of the guild's creed, really. *Someone should be able to find you.* They had just forgotten, somewhere in the centuries, that the finding should be allowed.

At the bottom, the stair opened into a room that was not a room.

It was a binding diagram the size of a chapel, rendered not in chalk or ink but in absence — lines of nothing cut into the floor, into the walls, into the air itself, and along those lines of nothing moved a light that made Coral's teeth ache. It was the color of the dragon-fire he had seen once, as a child, from a hilltop too far away to matter: gold shot through with a green like the first leaf of spring, the color of a world that still believed in itself.

And in the center of the diagram, woven into it like a fly in amber, hung a man.

He was standing — or something in the shape of standing — suspended a hand's width above the floor, and the lines of the binding ran through him, into him, out of him, threading his wrists and his throat and the crown of his head. He was thinner than the king Coral had glimpsed at the winter court. His hair was grey where the king's had been brown. His face was the same face and not the same face, the way a map is the same country and not the same country — accurate in every line, and missing everything that mattered.

His eyes were open. They had been open for a long time.

"You're not the choirmaster," the man said. His voice was rust and dust and something underneath the rust that was, impossibly, gentle. "The choirmaster comes on the solstices. To count me. Like a merchant counting stock."

"I'm not the choirmaster," Coral agreed. His own voice sounded small and enormous in the diagrammed dark. "I'm the record-keeper."

The man's eyes moved — slowly, with the effort of a man lifting weights — and found the ledger under Coral's arm. The original. The first ledger, the one Vess had stolen back for him, the one with the razored pages and the surviving fragment and the entry in ink that predated every other ink in the guild's archive: *One crown, one name, one flame. The name freely given. The flame freely spent. The debt runs while the fire burns.*

"You're the record-keeper," the man said again, and something moved through his ruined face that might have been laughter, if laughter could be worn down to a thread. "Thirty years I have waited for the record-keeper. Do you know what I asked the choirmaster, the first solstice? I asked him to bring me the ledger. To let me see the terms of my own captivity. He told me the guild's first law." The thread of laughter frayed. "Silence."

"The guild's first law," Coral said, "is the reason the stars went out."

"I know." The man closed his eyes. Opened them. "I know everything that happens above me. I feel it the way a coin feels the pocket. The wards failing. The dark. The girl." Something in his voice caught on *the girl*, and Coral felt the catch like a hook of his own. "She sings, and the binding loosens. Did you know that? For a few measures, I can almost remember my own name."

"Mirabel," Coral said.

"Mirabel," the man repeated, tasting it. "No. That's her chosen name. Her grandmother's name was—" He stopped. The lines of the binding flared, and he made a sound like a man swallowing broken glass. "It takes it back. Whatever I try to give away, it takes back. That is the whole of the arrangement. That has always been the whole of the arrangement."

Coral set the ledger down on the floor, on the one clean circle of stone at the diagram's edge, and knelt beside it, and opened it to the page he had read a hundred times and never once read aloud. The binding's text. The original terms, written in the guild's master ink — the ink that did not fade, did not smudge, did not forgive. He had spent his whole life keeping other people's secrets in books no one was meant to read. This was the secret under all the secrets. The realm's peace, purchased with a stolen man. The borders, warded with a caged flame. The stars, lit by a name that had never been freely given at all.

Because that was the flaw. He had found it in the razored fragment and confirmed it in Serevaine's guttering ember: a binding of this kind required consent. A name, freely given. And the name on this page — the name woven through every line of the diagram, the name the crown had been wearing like a coat for thirty years — had been given freely, yes. But not by the man hanging in the middle of it.

It had been given by the crown. For him. A forgery of consent, thirty years old, and every forgery has a flaw, and every flaw has a cost, and the cost had been coming due for a long time.

"King Aldous," Coral said.

"Don't," the man said. "Don't call me that. He's wearing it. If you call me that, you're calling him."

"Then tell me what to call you."

The man was quiet for a long time. Above them, faint as a memory of a memory, Coral could hear it beginning — the ceremony, the choir, hundreds of voices gathering in the cathedral overhead like water gathering behind a dam. The crown was drawing. He could feel it through the floor, a slow pull at the base of his skull, at the back of his teeth. It had felt like that his whole life, he realized. Every headache, every sleepless night, every nameless dread. They had all been living downstream of this.

"Al," the man said at last. "My mother called me Al. No one has called me that in thirty years."

"Al." Coral took out the guild's master ink. It was a small black bottle, unremarkable, the kind that sat in every guild scribe's kit, except that this one was older than the guild and heavier than its size, and when he unstopped it the smell was of lightning and cold iron and the moment before a storm breaks. "Then listen to me, Al. I'm going to read the terms of your binding aloud. That's the first step. The binding was made in words, and words can be unmade in words — but they have to be the right words, in the right order, witnessed, and written."

"Written where?"

Coral looked at the blank final page of the ledger. Then he looked at his own hand. Steady. He was surprised by how steady it was. He had imagined this moment on the road, in the feral countryside, in every sleepless camp — imagined it with dread, with terror, with the cold sweat of a man walking toward his own execution. He had not imagined it with this strange, settling calm, like a ledger finally balancing.

"Written here," Coral said. "In the amendment column. Every binding has one. The guild taught me that before they taught me to keep silent. A contract is a living thing. It can be amended — but an amendment must be paid for in the same currency as the original. The original was a name, freely given." He met Al's eyes. "The amendment will be too."

The silence that followed was very long, and very deep, and somewhere in the middle of it Coral understood that Al had already understood, because Al had been a king, and kings learn to read the shape of a sacrifice the way sailors read weather.

"No," Al said.

"It's not a request."

"I know it's not a request. That's what makes it unforgivable." The binding lines flared again, and this time Al fought them — Coral saw it, the whole ruined architecture of a man straining against the thing that held him, and gaining perhaps a finger's width of movement, thirty years of captivity buying thirty years of interest and one finger's width of defiance. "Listen to me, record-keeper. I have had thirty years to think about what that crown does with what it takes. It doesn't spend it. It *wears* it. If you give it your name, it will wear you. It will walk around in your shape and sign your hand and smile at the girl with your mouth, and you will be where I am, and there will be no one left to come down the stairs, because you are the one who comes down the stairs."

"I know."

"You don't—"

"I *know*," Coral said, and his voice cracked the dark like a struck bell. "I have spent my whole life being no one. Invisible. A half-hidden man writing half-hidden books. Do you know what the guild erased me with? A single line through my name. One line, Al. Thirty seconds of work, and I was never born. And do you know what I discovered, walking the road of unmade wards with a choirgirl who sings to locked doors?" He laughed, and the laugh surprised him — it came out steady too. "I discovered that being no one is a kind of freedom. Everyone else spent their lives holding on to their names — defending them, polishing them, dying for them. I have nothing to defend. Which means I have everything to give. A name is only worth what it costs to lose it. Mine costs nothing." He smiled, and it was not a bitter smile, or if it was, it had gone past bitter into something cleaner. "Which means it's worth everything."

"That's not how worth works," Al whispered.

"It's exactly how worth works. It's the first law of ledgers. Value is set at the moment of exchange." Coral dipped the pen. The master ink came up the nib in a thread of dark light. "The crown took a name that was never truly given. A forged consent. The binding has been running on a flaw for thirty years, and the flaw is why the fire gutters, why the wards fail, why the stars went out. A freely given name, in the master ink, witnessed by the original ledger — that closes the flaw. That replaces the anchor. The binding doesn't die. It transfers."

"And you transfer with it."

"And I transfer with it," Coral agreed. "For as long as it takes. Maybe forever. I've read the terms, Al. I know what I'm signing. I've spent my whole life reading terms on behalf of other people. Let me read one on my own behalf, for once, and let it be the truest thing I've ever written."

Above them, the choir was rising. He could feel it now not just as pressure but as music, bleeding down through thirty feet of stone — hundreds of voices, bent a hair flat, fighting the crown note by note, buying him seconds. Mirabel. She was up there, holding back the hunger with her throat, with her grandmother's name held behind her teeth like a lit coal, and every second she bought him was a second she was paying for in the only coin she had.

He did not have time for Al's forgiveness. He had time only for Al's witness.

"I need you to witness this," Coral said. "That's all I need from you. The binding requires a witness who is party to the original contract. That's you. You don't have to consent. You don't have to forgive me. You just have to see it, and say that you saw it. Can you do that, Al? Can you be my witness?"

The man in the binding looked at him for a long, long moment. And Coral watched something move through that ruined face — not gratitude, not yet, maybe not ever, but recognition. One keeper of a hidden life recognizing another.

"You are either the bravest man I have ever met," Al said, "or the best accountant."

"Both," Coral said. "The guild trained me."

And he began to read.

He read the original terms aloud, every word, in the guild's oldest cadence, the rhythm that made words into walls and walls into words. *One crown, one name, one flame. The name freely given. The flame freely spent. The debt runs while the fire burns.* The diagram drank the sound. The lines of nothing began to glow, and the light ran up through the binding and into the man at its heart, and Al cried out — not in pain, or not only in pain, but in the way a man cries out when a splint that has held a broken bone for thirty years is finally, finally removed.

Coral turned to the amendment column. The blank page. The last page he would ever write in.

He wrote his name.

*Coral Ishikawa. Cartographer, second class. Record-keeper of the Guild of Impossible Things. This name I give freely, of my own will, to stand in the place of a stolen king, until the debt is discharged or the fire goes out.*

The ink went into the page and the page went into the world. Coral felt it leave him — not pain, not exactly, more like a door opening in a house he had thought was fully furnished, a cold clean draft blowing through rooms he hadn't known were there. His name. His mother's voice saying it, the guildmaster's pen striking it out, Mirabel saying it on the road in the dark, *Coral, look, the river's flowing the wrong way* — all of it, gathering, lifting, pouring down into the diagram like water finding its level.

The binding took him.

The lines of nothing moved. They came off Al like snakes uncoiling, like bandages unwinding, like thirty years peeling away in a single breath, and they turned — all of them, the whole vast architecture of the crown's great hunger — and found him.

Coral had braced for pain. What came was worse and stranger: recognition. The binding knew him now. It had his name, and a binding with a name is a thing with a grip, and it closed around him the way winter closes around a house — thoroughly, impersonally, completely. He felt himself being woven. Wrists. Throat. The crown of his head. He was lifted a hand's width off the floor, and the diagram's light ran through him, and somewhere very far away he heard Al fall — actually fall, a real body hitting real stone, a man landing in his own weight for the first time in thirty years.

"Record-keeper," Al gasped. "Your name. What was your—"

"Gone," Coral said. He could feel it going, the way you feel a word on the tip of your tongue going. "Doesn't matter. Listen to me. The crown is starving. The girl is singing. When the binding breaks from above, you run. You don't look back, you don't help me, you run east and you don't stop until you see the sun. Do you understand? That's the last order I'll ever—"

The crown turned on him.

He felt it before he understood it — a presence in the binding, vast and furious and *awake*, the thing that had been wearing a king for thirty years suddenly finding its anchor replaced, its meal interrupted, its name-plate pried off its door. It came down the lines of the diagram like fire down a fuse, and it was starving, and it had just been handed a name freely given, and it intended to collect.

The light in the room went the color of the starless sky.

"Coral!" Al was on his feet, stumbling, a man learning to walk again, reaching for him. "Coral, what's your name, tell me your name—"

"I told you," Coral said, and the binding tightened, and the dark came in like a tide, and he thought, with the last corner of his mind that was still his own: *Mirabel. Now. Whatever you're going to do — now.*

Above him, through thirty feet of stone, the choir changed key.

* * *

The dark had his name. It was saying it back to him.

That was the thing no one warned you about, Coral thought, in the small white room of himself that was still left. It doesn't take your name like a thief. It *uses* it. It said *Coral Ishikawa* the way the guildmaster had said it, the way his mother had said it, the way Mirabel had said it across a campfire on the road of unmade wards — and every time it said it, it wore a little more of him, and he felt his shape being tried on like a coat, and it fit, God help him, it fit, because he had spent his whole life making himself easy to wear.

*Invisible man,* the crown said, in a voice like the space between stars. *You gave me exactly what I needed.*

"No," Coral said. He was on his knees in the diagram, or the diagram was on its knees in him; the distinction had stopped mattering. "I gave you what you *deserve*. There's a difference. It's in the terms."

*The terms,* the crown said, and laughed, and the laugh was thirty years of winter court, thirty years of a reflection that lagged half a second behind its face. *The terms are whatever I say they are. I have worn a kingdom. Do you think I cannot wear a clerk?*

"I think," Coral said, and here he did the only thing left, the thing the guild had trained into him so deep it survived the loss of his own name: he kept records. Out loud. Into the dark. A record-keeper to the end. "I think you are a forgery. I think you have been running on a flaw for thirty years. I think the fire is guttering and the girl is singing and every second you spend wearing me is a second you are not spending stopping her. And I think—" the white room of himself was very small now, candle-small, ember-small, but it was *his*, "—I think she's better at this than you are."

The crown screamed.

And through the scream, faint, impossible, rising up through the floor like water rising through a well — a name. Not his. An older name, a grandmother's name, given freely, sung fully, carried by hundreds of voices that had caught it and refused to let it go, and Coral felt the binding shudder around him like a rope coming taut from both ends at once.

The ink on the last page began to burn.

*Hold on,* Coral told himself, in the white room, in the candle-dark, with his name in a monster's mouth and a girl's song in the stones. *Hold on. It's almost balanced.*

The ledger burned, and the crown screamed, and the stars — far above, beyond the stone, beyond the dark — began, one by one, to flicker back.
