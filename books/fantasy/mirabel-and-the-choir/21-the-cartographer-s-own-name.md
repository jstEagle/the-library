## 21. The Cartographer's Own Name

The ink was the last thing to change.

Coral had expected the maps to be hard — three months of surveying after a lifetime of drawing from other people's reports — but the work itself came easily, almost greedily, as though the realm had been waiting to be measured honestly. What resisted him was the signature line.

Every map he had ever made ended the same way: a small stamped seal in the lower corner, the interlocked rings of the Guild of Impossible Things, and beneath it no name at all. *Recorded by the Office of Records.* That was how it had always been. That was the whole architecture of his life — accurate, essential, anonymous. He had drawn the coastline of Vell Harbor eleven times over thirty years and not one fisherman who sailed it knew his hand had made the chart that kept them off the Teeth.

Now the seal was ash in every sense. The guild's rolls had been opened to the light in the weeks after the ceremony, and what the light showed had dissolved the institution faster than any decree could have. Masters had scattered. Ledgers were being copied into public archives by clerks who wept as they worked, reading for the first time the bargains their own grandfathers had struck. The building on Candlewick Lane stood empty except for Vess, who had taken a room on the ground floor and hung a sign that said simply: *Records. Freely given, freely read.*

And so there was no seal to stamp. There was only the blank space where a name might go.

Coral sat in the loft of the cathedral — Mirabel's cathedral now, though she'd laugh at anyone who called it hers — with the great master map of the realm spread across four trestle tables pushed together. It was nine feet of fresh vellum, and he had been working on it since the first snow. The wards ran across it in silver thread now instead of the old guild cipher: a continuous lattice from the western fens to the eastern passes, each node marked where a choir held a note, each thread humming faintly under his fingertips when he traced it, the way a plucked string remembers.

He dipped his pen. He held it over the corner.

His hand did not shake. He noticed that with some surprise. Thirty years of invisible work, and when the moment finally came to be visible, his hand was steady as a ruled line.

*What are you afraid of?* he asked himself. Not mockery — he'd had plenty of time to make peace with the possibility of mockery, and found he didn't much care. Not responsibility — he had unbound a king; a signature was nothing next to that. It took him another week of finishing coastlines before he understood what it actually was.

It was permanence. A guild seal could be struck through, razored out, denied. A name stayed. A name said: *I was here, I saw this, I will answer for it.* Every entry he had ever hidden behind the office's anonymity had been, in its way, a refusal to answer. He had told himself it was humility. Sitting there with the pen full and the vellum white at the corner, he admitted it had also been cowardice, dressed carefully in guild grey.

He wrote:

*Surveyed and drawn by Coral Ishikawa, cartographer.*

Then, because it felt dishonest to stop there, smaller beneath it:

*Who keeps what he records, and records what he keeps.*

Vess would hate the second line. He wrote it anyway.

* * *

Aldous came to see the map on a grey morning in late winter, alone, which was still strange enough that Coral had to keep reminding himself it was real.

The man who arrived at the cathedral loft wore a plain wool coat and carried his own satchel, and the guards — the two he'd insisted on keeping, mostly so people would stop trying to give him back a throne — waited at the bottom of the tower stair out of the wind. His reflection moved with him exactly, without lag, without hesitation. Coral still checked sometimes. He suspected he always would. But the reflection was only ever a reflection now, dim and honest and half a second late for the ordinary reason: light takes time.

"You've done the eastern passes," Aldous said, standing over the vellum with his hands clasped behind his back like a man in a museum afraid to touch anything.

"Provisionally. The high road through the Serpent's Collar is still guesswork above the treeline. Nobody's surveyed it since the wards fell, and the roads up there—" he tapped a stretch of dotted line, "—were forgetting their destinations for a while. They're better now. The choirs re-taught them."

"The choirs." Aldous smiled. It was a private smile, the kind he seemed to have rediscovered along with everything else — meals eaten slowly, doors left open, opinions offered and then defended like they mattered. "Mirabel tells me the pass roads hum if you sing at them."

"They do. She thinks it's charming. I think it's going to make my job considerably harder, because a road that changes pitch when you're sad is not a road, it's an instrument, and instruments need tuning." He said it dryly, but the truth underneath it was warm and he knew Aldous could hear it: the world had come back strange and alive, and Coral had never loved his work more.

They stood together in silence for a while. Snow ticked against the loft windows. Below, faintly, the morning rehearsal was running — forty voices working through the warding-song, rising and settling like breath. The lattice on the map caught the sound somehow; Coral had stopped questioning that months ago. The silver threads brightened a shade, all of them at once, like a hearth when someone adds wood.

"I wanted to ask you something," Aldous said. "And I want you to refuse me if the answer is no. I've had enough of people not telling me things."

"That makes two of us."

"Yes. Well." He turned from the map. In the flat winter light, without a crown and without the thing that had worn him, his face was just a face — lined, tired, sixty years deep in a life that had been stolen from him for thirty of those years and returned to him folded and creased. "You know what I am now. Not a king. The council rules, badly, and I advise them, worse, and mostly I garden. But there is one thing I know that no one else alive knows, because I was there when it happened, before the binding, before everything."

"Serevaine," Coral said.

"Serevaine." Saying the name aloud still changed the air in a room. "When she leaves — and she will leave, dragons are not made for our weather or our griefs — she'll take the old dragon-road east. Over the Collar, past the edge of every map ever drawn, into whatever is east of east. And I promised her, thirty years ago, in the last conversation we ever had as free creatures, that if she ever flew home, someone would chart the way so that others could follow. So her road wouldn't be lost the way mine was." He looked down at his own hands, as if checking they were still his. "I can't walk it. I'm too old, and too known, and honestly too frightened — I spent thirty years in a vault; my legs are cowards now, whatever my heart says. But you."

"Me," Coral said.

"You spent your whole life making maps of places you'd never seen, from reports you couldn't verify, and they were *true*, Coral. Every one. I've checked, these last months — I had nothing but time and a grudge against my own history. Your work held. Now the wards are lit, the roads remember themselves, and there is a road east that has never once been mapped by anyone honest." Aldous met his eyes. "Chart it. Not for the council. Not for a guild. For the record. For whoever comes after us, so they know the way to find her again, if she ever wants finding."

Coral looked at the map. Nine feet of realm, every inch of it signed now, every ward-node bright. East of the Collar the vellum went blank — not empty, he corrected himself, *unwritten* — fading at the edge into the pale raw hide where nothing had ever been recorded at all.

He thought about the first ledger entry he'd ever made that he hadn't understood: a hairline crack in an orderly life. He thought about razored pages, and a girl singing locked doors open, and an ember in a lantern that Mirabel carried everywhere like a sleeping bird. He thought about how, for thirty years, he had believed invisibility was safety, and how it had taken the whole sky going dark to teach him that a record no one can see protects no one.

"When does the pass clear?" he asked.

Aldous's smile this time was not private at all. "By thaw. The choir says the Collar sings best in spring."

* * *

He told Mirabel that evening, in the loft, while she coaxed the ember through its nightly feeding — a low sustained note, barely more than breath, that the little flame leaned into like a cat.

"You're leaving," she said. Not a question. She didn't stop singing; the ember flared gently, and her voice around it sounded amused, which should not have been possible and was.

"For the season. Maybe longer. The Collar in thaw, then east until the maps run out." He was packing as he talked, because if he sat down to say it he'd say it badly. Ink, pens, the good dividers, the waterproof roll for the vellum. "Aldous is coming as far as the foothills. After that it's me and a mule and a lot of country nobody's measured."

"And the choir?"

"Yours. Obviously yours. It was always going to be yours; I just drew the lines around it." He closed the satchel. "I'll want copies of the warding-song. All the variations, every node's pitch, the works. If I'm mapping a road that hums, I need to know the music."

"You'll get it." She let the note fade. The ember settled to a steady gold, and in its light her face was younger and older than her years at once, the way everyone's had become since the stars came back — people carrying the memory of the dark politely, like a coat they'd agreed to keep wearing indoors. "You know what the funny thing is? When I met you in the starless archive, I thought you were the most closed person I'd ever seen. You handed me a map of the dark and wouldn't tell me your own name."

"It was against regulations."

"I know." She laughed. "That's what I mean. Look at you now. Signed work. Public records. You're practically scandalous."

"I contain multitudes," Coral said, "and I've catalogued every one."

She sobered then, and held out her hands, palms up, the way she did when she meant something. The ember light lay in them. "Come back before winter. The choir's learning a new verse — Serevaine's verse, the one the old chorister remembered wrong all those years. We got it right in the end. It needs a low part. Your kind of low. The kind that holds."

"I don't sing."

"Everyone sings. Some people just haven't been written down yet." She shrugged, guileless, devastating. "Consider it a survey. Field notes on your own voice. I'll expect a full report."

He laughed — actually laughed, out loud, in a cathedral loft, which would have been unthinkable a year ago — and picked up his satchel, and at the door he stopped, because there was one more thing, and he had sworn off leaving things unsaid.

"Mirabel. The map in the guildhall. The big one, the new master copy. Did you see what's in the corner?"

She shook her head.

"Go look tomorrow. Bring the choir." He adjusted the satchel strap on his shoulder, suddenly unable to look directly at her. "I put something there besides my name."

"What something?"

"You'll see." He stepped out onto the stair, into the cold blue evening, where the first star of dusk was already lit and holding — steady, catalogued, *recorded by Coral Ishikawa, cartographer, who keeps what he records* — and below him the city glowed with a thousand small flames, every one of them accounted for, every one of them free.

Behind him, faint through the stone, the choir began the evening verse, and somewhere far to the east, past the Collar, past the edge of the oldest map in the world, something vast and winged lifted its head toward the sound — and, he would swear forever after, answered it.
