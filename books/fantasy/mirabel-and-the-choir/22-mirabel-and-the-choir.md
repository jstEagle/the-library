## 22. Mirabel and the Choir

The ember lived in a lantern of blown glass, and the lantern lived on the choir loft's east rail, where the morning light could find it.

Mirabel had learned that much in the first week: fire kept by song wanted to be seen. Serevaine's last spark — no bigger than a thumbnail, warm as a sleeping bird against her palm when she carried it — dimmed if she left it shut away in a drawer or a strongbox. It brightened when voices gathered near it. It leaned toward singing the way a plant leaned toward a window.

So they had built it a place of honor instead of a place of safety, which was the opposite of everything the old choirmaster had ever taught her, and Mirabel found she liked the lesson better this way.

"Again from the top," she said, and forty-two faces turned toward her in the loft's amber light.

Forty-two. She still counted them every rehearsal, the way you count coins you're afraid of losing. Before the unbinding there had been sixty choristers in the cathedral choir; the crown's long feeding had thinned them year by year, one voice at a time, each departure explained as an apprenticeship elsewhere, a marriage, a fever. The ones who remained had spent the winter learning what had really happened to the missing, and some of them had wept until they couldn't sing at all, and some had packed their bags, and Mirabel had let them go with her blessing because a choir built on compulsion was just a smaller version of the thing they'd burned down.

But others had come. Word traveled strangely now that the wards were sung instead of sealed — word came on foot, mostly, and by river barge, and once by a man who claimed he'd followed the sound across three counties because his dead mother used to hum the tune. Farmers' daughters with voices like unhinged doors. A stonemason who sang bass so low the floorboards answered him. Two of Vess's former guild clerks, who confessed on their first day that they couldn't carry a tune in a bucket but could read any notation ever invented, and were hired on the spot as the choir's first librarians.

And Tam and Wren, of course. Her two loyal choristers from the ceremony night, who had stood beside her while the crown tried to drink the world. They sat in the front row now, where she could see them, an arrangement she pretended was musical rather than personal.

"From the top," she said again, and raised her hands.

The warding-song began, as it always began, with the low voices: the mason and the clerks and half the men, holding a drone like bedrock under everything. Then the tenors laid the road of it over the top, the long ascending line that mapped the border in sound — north to the Fangs, west along the Ashwater, south to the sea-cliffs where the wards had failed first and failed worst. And then, when the shape of the realm was standing in the air, the altos and Mirabel herself set the light into it, note by note, star by star, the way Coral had once taught her that a map wasn't the territory but a promise made to it.

Above them, through the loft's open arch, the sky did what it always did now when the song took hold: it flickered.

Not all at once. That was the miracle nobody in the capital had quite stopped marveling at, eight months on. The stars came back the night the binding broke — Coral had told her later that he'd watched the first one return from the vault steps, a single point of light over the Emberhold road, and had sat down in the ash and laughed until he cried — but they hadn't come back all together. They returned the way a bruise heals, in patches, in answer. A village sang the warding-song and its sky thickened with stars within the week. A valley that refused, that clung to the old guild-sealed wards out of habit or fear, stayed dark a little longer, until someone's grandmother organized the children into rounds and the darkness gave up grudgingly, star by stubborn star.

The realm was learning that light was not a tax collected at the top. Light was a thing you made, together, or didn't.

Mirabel lifted the final phrase — *keep, keep, be kept* — and held it, and felt the forty-one other voices hold it with her, and felt beneath all of them the small fierce warmth of the ember flaring in its glass lantern like a heart agreeing.

She cut them off cleanly. Silence rushed in, good silence, earned silence.

"The north line's still muddy," said Tam, who had appointed himself the choir's chief critic and tolerated no sentimentality, least of all hers. "Bars thirty through thirty-four. The tenors are rushing."

"We are not rushing," said the tallest tenor, a ferryman named Osk with forearms like hawsers, "we are *eager*."

"You're eager. Eager is rushing with excuses." Tam turned to Mirabel. "Also the new girl, Pella — she's got the star-count right but she's watching your hands instead of the line. If you get sick, the whole eastern reach goes dark."

"I'm not planning to get sick."

"Nobody plans it. That's why they call it sickness." He said it without heat. This was the other thing the choir had learned: the old way had put everything in one throat, one chosen girl fed to the crown a generation at a time, and look how that ended. The new way was redundancy. Every section had two singers who could lead it. Mirabel was teaching herself, note by note, to become unnecessary — which was, she was discovering, the strangest and sweetest form of being loved.

"Pella," she called down to the front row, where a wiry girl of perhaps thirteen sat with her music clutched like a shield. "Eyes up here only for the first beat, then you're on your own. The song knows where it's going. You just have to not grab its arm."

Pella flushed and nodded and wrote something ferocious in her margin.

Mirabel was about to run bars thirty through thirty-four — twice, for Osk's sins — when she saw him.

He came up the loft stair the way he came everywhere, quietly, sideways, as though apologizing in advance for occupying space. Eight months of knowing Coral Ishikawa had not cured him of entering rooms like a rumor. But he'd stopped wearing the gray hood, and he'd started meeting people's eyes, and today he was carrying a roll of paper under one arm with the particular care he reserved for things that mattered, and when he reached the rail he didn't hover at the edge of the light the way he used to. He stepped straight into it.

"Don't stop on my account," he said. "I'm working."

"You're always working," Wren said, grinning. "Master Cartographer."

"Not master anything," Coral said, but he didn't argue the way he would have a year ago, when the words *his work* and *his name* in the same sentence would have sent him into a spiral of deflection that could last an afternoon. He unrolled the paper on the rail beside the lantern, weighted the corners with two tuning forks and a heel of bread he'd clearly stolen from breakfast, and Mirabel came to look over his shoulder because she always did, because watching Coral draw was like watching someone think in lines.

It was the eastern reach. She knew it instantly — the coastline she'd sung into being every morning for half a year, rendered now in his fine, certain hand: the cliffs, the drowned forest off Grayhaven Point, the little scatter of islands that appeared on no older map because the old maps had been drawn from guild records and the guild had never bothered charting water too shallow for tribute barges.

"You've signed it," she said.

There, in the lower corner, in ink so dark it looked wet: *Coral Ishikawa.* No guild seal above it. No cipher, no registry mark, none of the anonymous conventions that had hidden him inside his own life's work for twenty years. Just his name, sitting on his map like a lighthouse on its rock.

"It's the ninth one," he said. His ears had gone faintly pink, which they still did. "Vess has commissioned eleven. The archive rebuild wants a full set for the reading room — maps people can *use*, she says, with the wards marked in plain language instead of ledger-cipher. I told her I'd need to walk the coast myself for the eastern sheets. Charting from secondhand records is how the guild ended up blind in the first place."

"When do you leave?"

"Tomorrow's tide." He smoothed a wrinkle in the paper, not looking at her. "Two months, maybe three. Grayhaven's far, and the drowned forest doesn't map itself."

Mirabel looked at the map, and at the lantern with its patient flame, and at her choir below, running the muddy bars without being asked, Tam glaring the tenors back onto the beat. Something in her chest did a complicated maneuver that she chose not to examine in front of forty-two witnesses.

"Well," she said. "You'll want to hear the eastern reach sung properly before you chart it. The song's changed since spring — we found the old coast-road verse in the chorister's score, the one the crown suppressed, and it turns out the ward holds truer if the third phrase resolves high instead of low. You can't hear that difference on paper."

"No," Coral agreed solemnly. "I would need to attend several rehearsals. Possibly all of them. For accuracy."

"For accuracy," Mirabel agreed, and they smiled at each other over the map like two conspirators whose conspiracy was simply Tuesday.

* * *

They sang the evening service as the light went gold and then blue, and afterward Mirabel climbed to the loft alone, as she did most nights, to bank the ember.

Banking was a misnomer, really. There was nothing to feed it, nothing to trim. The keeping was all attention: she opened the lantern's little door, cupped her hands around the flame without touching it, and sang to it — not the great warding-song, but the small private verse she'd written for it in the first weeks, when it had guttered terribly one night after a messenger brought news of trouble on the southern border and Mirabel had been terrified, genuinely terrified, that the last dragon-fire in the world would go out in a glass jar because she'd been careless about locking the cathedral doors.

The verse had no words anyone else used. It was mostly her grandmother's name, sung slow, the way you'd say it over a grave and a cradle at once. *Sera. Sera-venne. Come home, come home, be warm.*

Her grandmother's name, freely given seventy years ago to bind a king, freely sung back last spring to unmake the binding — and now given a third time, in the smallest coin, nightly, to keep a fire company. Mirabel had thought at first that spending the name again would feel like loss. It didn't. Names, the old chorister Maren had told her on her deathbed, aren't purses you empty. They're wells. The drawing is the filling.

The ember pulsed against her palms, steady, drowsy, content. Sometimes — and she had told exactly one person this, and that person had immediately sketched it — she could feel something vast moving behind the flame, far away, eastward, alive. Serevaine had risen from the Emberhold the morning after the unbinding, shaking off thirty years of being a wick like a dog shedding rain, and had flown east without a backward glance, a streak of gold against the returning stars, and the whole capital had stood in the streets and watched until she was gone. But the ember remembered her. Or perhaps the ember *was* her, a little, the way a letter is its writer. Either way, when Mirabel sang the small verse, she sometimes felt the enormous slow attention of a dragon turning one ear, somewhere past the edge of the map, toward a girl in a loft, and finding her acceptable.

"Goodnight," Mirabel whispered, and closed the lantern door, and the flame settled to its patient glow.

Below her, the cathedral was emptying. She heard the mason's laugh, Osk's terrible whistling, the librarians arguing about shelf order. Through the open arch, the sky was doing its nightly work: stars coming out in their patched, healed, human-tended constellations. Over the eastern hills, where the wards ran newest and proudest, the sky was thick with them, a spill of silver that no one had seen in thirty years and that children now took for granted, which Mirabel considered the single greatest achievement of her entire life — that a seven-year-old in Grayhaven could look up and be bored by miracles.

Footsteps on the stair. She didn't turn; she knew the weight of them, careful and sideways even on stairs.

"You missed the muddy bars," she said. "Tam fixed them. Osk has been forgiven, provisionally."

"I heard from the gallery." Coral came to stand beside her at the rail, his sketchbook already open, and Mirabel saw tonight's page: the loft drawn in his quick sure strokes, the lantern, the arch, and — he'd done this before, and she'd never told him she'd noticed — a small figure at the rail with her hands cupped around a light. "I've been thinking about the eastern sheets," he said. "About what to put in the margins."

"Margins?"

"The old guild maps never had any. Margins were where error lived, so we left them blank." He turned the page back, studying his own drawing. "But a blank margin is just a lie with better posture. So the new ones have notes. Where the wards sing true, and where they need work. Which villages have strong singers and which need teaching. Where the roads forget themselves in fog." He paused. "Where the cartographer slept badly, and why."

Mirabel laughed. "Vess approved marginal confessions?"

"Vess *requested* them. She says a map that admits what it doesn't know is the only kind worth trusting." He closed the sketchbook. For a moment he was quiet, looking out at the stars, and Mirabel had known him long enough now to wait — Coral's silences weren't emptinesses; they were ledgers being balanced.

"When I was invisible," he said finally, "I thought that was safety. Erase yourself enough and nothing can be taken from you." He shook his head slowly. "Turns out it was just loss, prepaid. In installments."

"And now?"

"Now—" He looked at her, and the lamplight and the ember-light met in his face, and he didn't look away, which was still new, which might always be new, which she hoped never stopped being new. "Now I sign my work. Now I sleep badly in interesting places and write down why. Now I have—" he gestured, slightly helplessly, at the loft, the lantern, the choir below filtering out into the starlit square, "—all this. A fire that knows me. A song that lets me sit in on it. A—"

"A cartographer," Mirabel supplied.

"A cartographer," Coral agreed gravely, "who will require lodging in Grayhaven, and possibly a guide who knows the coast-road verse, for accuracy."

"For accuracy," Mirabel said, and this time neither of them bothered pretending otherwise.

Out over the eastern hills, a star brightened — just one, briefly, as though something vast and far away had turned an ear toward them and found them acceptable. The ember glowed in its glass. Below, the last choristers crossed the square, their shadows long and their voices carrying scraps of the warding-song into the ordinary blessed dark, which was no longer dark at all, only night, only the honest turning of the world, lit from below by everything they'd made together.

Mirabel took Coral's hand, and opened the lantern door, and sang her grandmother's name into the flame — come home, come home, be warm — and began, at last, to map the rest of her life.
