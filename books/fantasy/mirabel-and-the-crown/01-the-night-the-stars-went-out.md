## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The stars went out all at once, like a hall of candles snuffed by a single passing breath, and Odile Fairweather was the only one in the valley who saw it happen.

She would remember that later—the *only one*—and it would sit strangely in her chest, somewhere between pride and grief. But at the time, standing on the frost-stiff grass behind the guildhouse with a census ledger under her arm and ink still wet on her thumb, she felt nothing so grand as either. She felt, mostly, cold.

It was the eve of the annual census. Tomorrow morning, all nine members of the Ward-Keepers' Guild of Fairweather Vale would assemble in the long room and be counted, named, and recorded, as they had been every autumn for as long as anyone could remember—though Odile had begun to suspect, in her quiet and unquiet way, that "as long as anyone could remember" was doing a great deal of heavy lifting in that sentence.

Tonight, though, there was only the counting of marker stones, which fell to her because it always fell to her. Nine keepers, ninety-nine stones, and the outermost ring always went to Odile, because the outermost ring was farthest from the fire, and Odile was the one nobody thought to argue for.

She didn't mind. That was what she told people, anyway, when they bothered to ask, which was rarely. *I don't mind.* It was almost true. There was a particular peace to walking the ward-line alone at dusk, touching each stone with two fingers, feeling the faint warm pulse beneath the lichen like a sleeping heartbeat. The stones knew her. They hummed a little brighter when she passed, though she'd never mentioned this to anyone, because mentioning it would mean someone might come watch, and then it might stop being true.

So she was at stone seventy-one, the one shaped like a hunched old man, when the sky emptied.

There was no warning. No flicker, no dimming, no polite gradual fade such as clouds or fog provide. One moment the valley's bowl of night was crowded with stars—thick as spilled salt, wheeling slow and certain—and the next moment there was nothing above her but black. Not cloud-black. Clouds have texture, weight, a grey suggestion of something behind them. This was a black with edges. A black that had been *applied*, like paint over a window.

Odile stood very still.

Her first thought, absurdly, was that she had gone blind in the space between one breath and the next, and her second thought was that this was unlikely, because she could still see the marker stone clearly, and her own hands, and the pale ribbon of the ward-light running from stone to stone along the valley's rim like thread through beads.

Then the wards woke up.

All of them at once. Ninety-nine stones began to hum—not the sleepy contented hum she knew, the one like a cat purring in its sleep, but a high thin keening that she felt in her back teeth. The ribbon of light between the stones brightened until it hurt to look at, guttered, steadied, guttered again. And underneath the humming, if you pressed your palm flat against the stone—which Odile did, because her hands did things before her mind finished deliberating—you could feel something else. A tremor. A shudder running around the whole ring of the valley, like a dog shaking off rain.

Or like a chain testing its links.

"Hallo," Odile said to the stone, because talking to stones was another thing she did without deliberating. "Hallo, what's wrong, then?"

The stone, naturally, said nothing. But the hum changed pitch when she spoke, dipping toward something almost like recognition, and Odile had spent enough years among wards to know that wards did not *recognize*. Wards responded to bloodlines and sigils and the ritual words recited at each quarter-turn of the year. They did not lean toward a person like a plant toward light.

She took her hand off the stone and looked up at the empty sky for a long time.

Then she did what she always did with anything too large to hold: she put it down carefully, picked up her ledger, and started walking toward the guildhouse, because tomorrow was the census, and whatever had happened to the stars, somebody official ought to be told, and she supposed she was as much somebody as anyone.

* * *

The guildhouse sat in the crook of the valley where the river bent, a low stone building with a turf roof and windows that glowed amber against the dark. From the outside it looked exactly like what it was: a place where nine aging people kept small duties going with small magic. Inside, it smelled of beeswax, dried yarrow, and the particular staleness of rooms where arguments have been having the same argument for forty years.

Odile came in through the kitchen door, as she always did, and found the kitchen full.

That was odd in itself. On census eve the others usually scattered early—to bed, to prayer, to Elder Maren's elderberry wine, which was technically medicinal and technically terrible. But tonight all eight of them were gathered around the long table, and none of them were talking.

Eight faces turned toward her as she entered. Eight expressions performed, more or less successfully, the look of people who are not alarmed.

"You're late," said Elder Bram.

"I'm not," said Odile. "The count took longer. The stones were—" She stopped. Everyone was looking at her with a strange intensity, and she realized with a small cold drop in her stomach that they already knew. Whatever had happened out on the line, they had known before she'd reached stone seventy-one. Possibly before she'd left the house. "The stones were singing," she finished. "And the stars are gone."

Nobody moved.

"Gone," repeated Elder Maren, in the tone of a woman confirming a rumor she finds distasteful.

"All of them. At once. Like snuffing a candle." Odile set her ledger on the table, next to the bread. "I came to report it."

"Yes," said Bram. "Thank you, Odile. You may go to bed."

And there it was—the sentence she had heard ten thousand variations of across twenty-six years of life. *You may go.* *That will be all, Odile.* *Run along, child.* She had been the youngest member of the guild since she was four years old, when she'd been brought through the gates during some emergency nobody would describe to her, and in twenty-two years she had somehow never managed to become not-the-youngest, not-the-peripheral, not-the-one-nobody-thought-to-argue-for.

Ordinarily she would have gone. Ordinarily, obedience was easier than the friction of insisting, and Odile had built her whole quiet life out of easy things.

But the sky was empty, and the wards were keening loud enough now that even indoors she could hear them, a sound like wet glass rubbed by a thumb, and Elder Bram's face in the lamplight had a quality she had never seen on it before. Bram was the guild's fixed point, its north star, a man whose eyebrows alone could end an argument. His face was always composed. Tonight it was composed the way a bandage is composed—something underneath, held down.

"Elder," Odile said, "with respect. The stars didn't fall. Falling takes time. I watched. They went out *together*, all of them, like—" she groped for it— "like a lamp being shuttered. From inside."

Silence. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked; the house settling, or listening.

Elder Maren exchanged a glance with Elder Sorrel. It was a fast glance, a flicker, but Odile had spent a lifetime learning to read the small languages of overlooked people, and she read it perfectly: *not here, not now, not in front of her.*

"You've had a long walk," said Maren gently. "Cold air plays tricks. In the morning, after the census, we'll walk the line together and see about your singing stones."

"The wards are failing," Odile said. "Not playing tricks. Failing. I've tended those stones since I was seven. I know their voices better than I know yours."

Someone at the far end of the table—Cousin Pell, who was technically family and functionally furniture—made a small shocked noise at her tone. Odile ignored him. She was looking at Bram, and Bram was looking at the tabletop, and his hand lay flat beside the bread loaf with the stillness of a man holding himself down by his own fingers.

"The census is tomorrow," Bram said at last, without raising his eyes. "Ninety-nine stones will be counted. Nine keepers will be recorded. The rites will be observed. That is all any of us need concern ourselves with tonight."

"And the sky?"

"The sky," said Bram, "is above our station."

It was such an absurd sentence that Odile nearly laughed. The sky, above the station of the ward-keepers, whose entire purpose—whose entire *name*—was the keeping of the boundary between the valley and everything beyond it, including, presumably, the part of everything that lived overhead. She opened her mouth to say so.

"Don't," said Bram quietly.

It wasn't anger. That was the worst part. It was tiredness, deep and worn smooth, like a stair worn hollow by centuries of feet. He raised his eyes to hers at last, and for one unguarded moment Odile saw what was held down under the composure, and it frightened her far more than the empty sky had.

Bram wasn't surprised. Bram wasn't even afraid, exactly. Bram was *grieved*—grieved in the specific way of a man watching an appointment arrive that he had long ago made his peace with, the way you make peace with a debt you know is coming due.

He had known. The thought arrived in Odile's mind with the softness of a snowflake and the weight of an avalanche. *He has known this was coming. Perhaps for years. Perhaps for longer than I've been alive.*

"Goodnight, Odile," said Bram.

This time she went. There was nothing to be gained by staying; she understood, with the instinct of the overlooked, that the real conversation had already happened before she walked in, and that her presence in it was incidental. She took her candle from the kitchen shelf and climbed the narrow stairs to her room under the eaves.

* * *

Her room was small and sloped-ceilinged and hers, the one territory in the world that was unquestionably so. She lit no lamp. Through the little window, the valley spread out below: the dark shapes of rooftops, the silver thread of the river, the rim of hills—and above it all, the hole where the sky used to be.

She sat on her bed with her knees drawn up and let herself feel it properly at last, the thing she had been carrying level-headed through the kitchen and steady-handed along the ward-line. Fear. Not the sharp kind, the jump-at-noises kind, but a low wide dread, like water rising slowly in a cellar. The stars had been there her whole life. Her mother—whoever her mother had been; that door in her memory was smooth and blank, polished by years of not being opened—her mother must have seen these same stars. Generations of Fairweathers had counted these same ninety-nine stones under this same spilled-salt sky, and tonight it had been switched off, casually, completely, like a lamp in a room nobody was using anymore.

And the wards knew. That was the thing she kept circling back to, the splinter in her mind. The wards weren't failing the way a wall fails, dumbly, under force. They were failing the way a promise fails—reluctantly, with trembling, with a sound like grief. As if they had been holding something for a very long time, and whatever they had been holding had finally stopped being held *with* them.

Odile got up, dug under her bed, and pulled out the tin box where she kept the things that were hers alone: a chipped blue bead, three interesting rocks, a feather from a bird she couldn't identify, and a journal. The journal was her most secret possession, because in it she wrote down the questions she wasn't allowed to ask.

She turned to a fresh page by window-light that wasn't starlight anymore, and wrote:

*Nine keepers. Ninety-nine stones. Zero stars.*

*The elders were not surprised.*

*Bram looked like a man whose bill had come due.*

She paused, pen hovering. Then, smaller:

*I asked what happened to the sky and he said it was above our station. Nobody says that unless they already know what's up there and wish it weren't.*

Below that, after a while, she added:

*Maren glanced at Sorrel. Not-in-front-of-her. Which means there is a front-of-her and a behind-her, and I have apparently always lived behind myself.*

She blew on the ink, closed the book, and sat for a while in the dark, listening to the wards keen around the valley rim like a hundred distant violins tuning for a concert that would never begin.

Sleep, when it finally came, was shallow and full of a dream she wouldn't remember: a garden under a black sun, and a girl's voice calling a name that wasn't quite hers, and the smell of ash.

* * *

She woke to shouting.

For one merciful second she didn't know why, and the second was the last peaceful one she would have for a long time. Then the sound resolved itself—voices in the yard below, many voices, and under them that keening from the stones, louder now, no longer distant, as though the whole ring of the valley had drawn in close to listen.

Odile was at the window before she was fully awake.

The yard below was full of lanterns. Villagers—dozens of them, wrapped in blankets over their nightclothes, faces tilted upward toward the starless sky like a field of pale flowers. Old Tam the miller was shouting something about the river running backward. Someone was weeping. Someone was saying, over and over, in a flat stunned voice, *"the lights, the lights, where are the lights,"* and Odile's chest tightened, because of course—the valley folk had their own names for the stars, little household gods of them, the Shepherd, the Ladle, the Weeping Sisters, and every soul down there had grown up under those lights the way fish grow up in water, and the water had been taken away in the night.

Down in the yard, the guild elders had come out onto the steps. She saw Bram raise his hands for quiet, saw the crowd still itself, saw him begin to speak—words she couldn't catch, measured, calming, the voice of a man who had rehearsed something.

And she saw what the crowd, in its fear, did not see.

She saw Elder Maren, standing just behind Bram, turn her head and look—not at the crowd, not at the sky, but out along the eastern ward-line, toward the ash-fields beyond the last marker stones, with an expression Odile had no name for yet. An expression of someone checking whether a thing had arrived on schedule.

*Expected,* Odile thought. The word settled into her with a chill that had nothing to do with the frost on the window. *They expected this. Maybe not tonight, maybe not this year—but they knew it was coming, they knew what it meant, and they let us all go to sleep under a shuttered sky without one word of warning.*

The census was at dawn. In a few hours, nine keepers would stand in the long room and be counted, and recite the rites, and pretend that the world was the shape it had always been.

Odile dressed quickly, in the dark, and took up her ledger, and her tin box, and her questions.

Whatever the elders knew, they had decided the valley didn't need to. Fine. She had spent her whole life being the person nobody told things to. Nobody had ever noticed that this made her very, very good at finding them out.
