## 2. A Guild of Small Duties

The morning after the stars went out, the Fairweather guild did what the Fairweather guild always did: it had breakfast.

Odile stood in the doorway of the common hall with her boots half-laced and watched it happen. Elder Bram at the head of the table, buttering his bread as though the sky were still whole. Old Sennet arguing with the kettle. Maren and Tilda splitting a honeycomb down the middle with the solemn precision of surgeons dividing a patient. Nine of them altogether, nine aging ward-keepers in nine mismatched robes, eating porridge under a ceiling that no longer matched anything outside.

Nobody spoke about the stars.

That was the thing Odile noticed—she noticed things; it was her one talent and nobody had ever asked for it—and once she'd noticed it, she couldn't stop noticing it. Not one of them looked up. Not one of them said *did you see*, or *what do you suppose*, or even the guild's traditional response to any crisis larger than a broken fence post, which was *we'll take it up at census*. The census was tomorrow. The elders knew something. She could feel it the way you feel weather coming through a healed break in your ankle: not information, exactly, but pressure.

"Odile," Bram said without looking up from his bread, "you're on the outer line today."

"The outer line," she repeated.

"You volunteered."

"I did not volunteer."

"You volunteered last spring," said Sennet, who kept the duty roster the way misers keep coins, "and volunteering is forever. That's how volunteering works."

"It is not how volunteering works."

"It's how it works here." He tapped the roster with one yellowed fingernail. "Marker stones eleven through fourteen, then the ash-field boundary, then home before dark. Take your chalk. Take your lunch. Don't talk to strangers."

"There are never any strangers."

"That's why we don't want you talking to them."

There was a small round of laughter at this—the same small round of laughter that greeted every joke Sennet made, because the guild had long ago run out of new jokes and had decided, collectively, to be loyal to the old ones—and Odile took her chalk and her lunch and went out into the colorless morning to do the smallest job in a guild of small duties.

* * *

The outermost marker stone was called Fourteen, because the guild had numbered its markers in the order they were built rather than the order anyone would find convenient, and Fourteen stood an hour's walk past the orchards where the valley began to forget it was a valley. The grass grew taller there. The hedges went feral. And the stone itself rose out of a tussock like a bad tooth: gray, squat, carved over with spirals so worn that only the deepest grooves still held their shape.

Her mother had taught her the tending rite when Odile was seven years old.

*Chalk in the left hand, love, because the wards read the heart first and the heart sits slightly left. Three circles, sunwise. Then press your palm flat and say the words, and don't worry if you flub them, the stone has heard worse.*

She could still hear her mother's voice saying it, warm and slightly breathless, because her mother had been a woman who treated everything—even ward-magic, even the covenant that held the sky together—as though it were a recipe she'd gotten from a friend. *Don't worry if you flub them.* Her mother had died eleven years ago, and the guild had held a quiet funeral, and then the guild had gone on being exactly the guild it had always been, which was to say: smaller every year, older every year, and performing its rituals with the particular devotion of people who no longer remembered why.

Odile knelt in the wet grass and drew her three circles, sunwise, left-handed.

The chalk went down gray and came up *grayer*. That was the trouble. When she was a child the lines had glowed faintly blue for a heartbeat after each stroke—a little breath of light, a little acknowledgment, the stone saying *yes, I hear you*. Now the lines just sat there, dull as pencil marks. She pressed her palm to the spiral anyway and recited the words.

*By root and by rim, by the turning of the ring, hold fast, hold kind, hold the line between—*

Nothing. No hum. No warmth rising through the stone into her hand. For as long as she could remember there had been *something*, the way there is something in a house even when it's empty—not presence exactly, but the memory of presence. Today the stone felt like what it was: a rock.

She sat back on her heels and looked at it for a long time.

"Well," she told it, because she had inherited her mother's habit of talking to things that couldn't answer, "you're not going to tell me either, are you."

The stone, being a stone, declined to comment.

* * *

Here is what nobody outside the valley knew, and what almost nobody inside it remembered: the Fairweather guild had once been great.

Not merely respected—*great*. There had been ninety members once, then sixty, then thirty, and the histories in the archive (the ones Odile was allowed to shelve but not, technically, to read) spoke of ward-keepers walking the border in pairs, singing the renewal rites in harmony, their marker stones blazing like a crown of lamps around the whole valley floor. Travelers had come from beyond the mountains just to stand at the ward-line and feel it thrum. Children had been sent here as apprentices from villages whose names Odile had never heard, because those villages lay outside, and outside was a word the guild used the way other families used words like *debt* or *shame*: rarely, quietly, and with a downward glance.

Now there were nine. Now the apprentices had stopped coming forty years ago, and nobody could quite say why, in the way that nobody can quite say why a well goes dry. Now the rituals were maintained by rote, and rote is a strange servant: it keeps everything exactly, and understands nothing.

Odile had joined at six years old, handed over by a father she barely recalled, and she had spent thirty-one years learning every small duty the guild contained. She could mend a ward-line's chalkwork better than anyone, including Bram, who pretended otherwise. She could recite all four hundred and twelve verses of the Ring-Song, including the boring ones. She knew which elder liked their tea scalded and which liked it sad and lukewarm, and she brought both kinds without being asked, because being useful was the closest thing she had to being seen.

And she was not seen. That was the honest arithmetic of her life. Maren was the guild's beauty. Tilda was its wit. Sennet was its memory, Bram its authority, and Odile was—she had heard Bram describe her once, to a visiting factor, in the mild apologetic tone men use for weather—"our steady one."

*Steady.* As if she were a table.

She used to lie awake some nights inventing dramatic departures. She would walk out past Fourteen and keep walking, and the guild would discover, in her absence, that steadiness was load-bearing. But morning always came, and with it the roster, and with it the small duties, and she stayed, telling herself it was duty. Duty was a respectable word. It fit nicely over the loneliness, the way a lid fits over a pot, and if you didn't look too closely you could almost pretend the pot wasn't still hot underneath.

* * *

At noon she ate her lunch against the sunny side of Marker Twelve and counted the guild's dwindling magic the way you count teeth in a comb.

It wasn't that the magic was gone. It was that it had gone *thin*, like soup stretched over too many bowls. Sennet could still coax a wisp-light out of the air, but it burned greenish and guttered if he sneezed. Maren's mending-charm worked on socks and failed on sweaters, for reasons that had occasioned a three-day argument. And the renewal rites—the great seasonal singings that should have poured strength back into the ward-line—had been shortened twice in Odile's lifetime, "for practicality," until what remained was less a rite than a summary of a rite, the way a traveler's tale of a feast is less nourishing than the feast.

"We do what we can," Bram always said, when anyone raised it. "We do what we can."

But lately—and Odile dated this precisely, to the night of the autumn equinox, when the ward-line had flickered like a candle in a draft and every keeper in the hall had felt it in their teeth—lately *what we can* seemed to be shrinking faster than the guild was willing to admit.

A shadow fell across her bread.

"Talking to the stones again?" Tilda dropped down beside her, sixty years old and built like a sparrow, and stole the crust Odile had been saving for last, which was also tradition.

"Somebody has to keep them company."

"They're rocks, Odile."

"They're *listening* rocks. There's a difference. A rock that's part of a covenant holding the sky on is at least entitled to conversation."

Tilda chewed the stolen crust and squinted down the line of markers, ten gray teeth receding into the haze. For a moment her face did something un-Tilda-like: it went careful.

"You felt it too, then," she said quietly. "Last night. The hum stopping."

Odile's heart knocked once, hard. "You felt it?"

"Everyone felt it. Nobody's saying it." Tilda brushed crumbs from her robe with great deliberation. "Bram was awake before the bell. I saw his lamp. And Sennet went out to the archive at midnight—I heard him rattling around in there like a moth in a jar. Elders don't go to the archive at midnight unless they're looking for something they hoped they'd never need."

"What would they need?"

"If I knew that, girl, I'd be an elder." Tilda stood, joints crackling like green firewood, and looked down at her with an expression Odile couldn't file anywhere. Pity, maybe. Or apology. "Do your rounds. Come home before dark. And Odile—"

"Yes?"

"Don't let them make you think you're not paying attention. You're the only one of us who still does."

Then she was gone down the hill, a sparrow-shaped silhouette against a sky with no stars waiting behind its daylight, and Odile sat alone with her stolen crust and the strangest feeling: that she had just been given a blessing and a warning in the same breath, and wasn't sure which was which.

* * *

She finished the line by late afternoon. Eleven, twelve, thirteen—each stone duller than the last, each rite met with the same silence, until by Fourteen she had stopped pretending the chalk might glow and simply did the work, sunwise, left-handed, palm flat, the words spoken clearly into a wind that carried them nowhere.

That was when she saw the ash-field.

It lay beyond Fourteen, a broad gray sweep where nothing grew—old ground, old burning, ground the guild's maps marked with a single word: *outside*. The ward-line ended at its edge, or had once. Now the final stretch of chalk lay broken across the tussocks like a snapped thread, and beyond it the ash stretched away under the starless noon-dark toward hills she had never seen and had been taught, gently and thoroughly, never to wonder about.

Guild law was simple about the ash-field: no member crossed it, no stranger crossing it was welcomed, and any disturbance along the boundary was reported to the elders *before* it was investigated. Report first. Always report first. She could hear Sennet's voice reciting it, could hear her own voice agreeing with it, thirty-one years of small dutiful agreement.

But the wind was carrying something across the ash. Faintly. Unmistakably.

It smelled of burnt feathers and cold iron, and beneath that—impossibly, absurdly, at the edge of the dead ground where nothing living should be—something sweet. Like night-blooming flowers. Like a garden that had no business existing in a place where nothing grew.

Odile stood at the broken end of the ward-line with her chalk in her left hand and her heart going like a kettle, and she thought about reporting first. She thought about it very seriously, the way you think about a door you have already opened.

Then she stepped past the last marker stone, out onto the ash, and went to see what had fallen from the dark.
