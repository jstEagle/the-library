## 3. What Fell from the Dark

The guild law was written on the inside of the meeting-house door in letters so old they had gone brown as tea: *No stranger crosses the ward-line. No stranger is fed, sheltered, or named.* Elder Bram liked to say the law had kept the valley safe for nine hundred years, which Odile had always privately thought was like saying a locked door had kept a room tidy.

She thought of that law now, standing at the edge of the ash-field with her boots sinking into grey powder and the morning sky above her the color of unwashed wool. The stars were still gone. Everyone in the valley was pretending otherwise—milk was still being churned, arguments still being had over fence posts—but there was a new quality to the pretending, a hollowness, like a house where someone has died and everyone keeps speaking a little too loudly.

The elders had called an emergency census for tomorrow. Emergency censuses were, by definition, not supposed to be possible; the whole point of a census was that it happened whether or not anything was wrong. Odile had been sent out to walk the outermost ward-line and "assess," which in practice meant she would be blamed for whatever she found.

So she found it.

* * *

The ward-line ran along the northern ridge where the valley's hills rose up like cupped hands. For nine hundred years the marker stones had hummed—a sound you felt more than heard, a bee-in-the-bones vibration that every Fairweather learned before they learned their letters. This morning the stones were silent. Worse than silent: when Odile laid her palm against the third marker, the stone was cold in a way stone should not be, cold the way the space between stars must be cold, and beneath her hand she felt nothing at all. Not emptiness. *Absence.* As though something had reached into the world and scooped the magic out like the flesh of a fruit.

"Right," she said aloud, because talking to herself was one of the few habits the elders hadn't managed to scold out of her. "You're either broken or you're dead, and either way I'm going to catch it for it."

She followed the line north. The ash began a quarter mile on—not ordinary ash, not hearth-ash or woodsmoke, but a fine pale grey fall that lay over the bracken like snow that had given up halfway. It had come down in the night. She could tell because the spiderwebs held it, each strand furred white, dozens of little nets strung between the gorse all catching the same grey fall. It got into her hair. It tasted faintly of iron and, strangely, of lilacs.

And then, in the middle of the ash-field, there was a woman.

Odile stopped so abruptly that a plover behind her had to swerve. The woman lay on her back in the grey fall with her arms flung wide, as though she'd fallen from a very great height and had decided, upon reflection, to be graceful about it. Her hair was black shot through with silver, spread out around her head in a corona, dusted with ash until she looked half-made of the sky's leavings. Her dress had once been magnificent—you could see the bones of magnificence in it, embroidery gone ragged, hem burned away to fringe—and now it was magnificent the way a shipwreck is still a ship.

There was no shadow under her.

It was a bright grey morning, the kind of light that gives everything at least a ghost of a shadow, and the gorse threw shadows and the marker stones threw shadows and Odile's own feet made two small dark smudges in the ash. But beneath the woman there was only ash, undisturbed, as if the light itself had agreed not to mention her.

Odile stood at a careful distance and conducted the internal argument of her life.

*Guild law,* said the sensible voice, which sounded disconcertingly like Elder Bram. *Stranger beyond the ward-line. You are to turn away, return, and report.*

*Report what?* asked another voice, hers. *That the wards are dead, the stars are gone, and there's a woman in the ash-field with no shadow? Bram will ask me three questions and I'll have no answers, and then he'll do that thing where he looks through me like I'm a window.*

*That's not your problem.*

*Everything is my problem. That's what being overlooked means. Nothing gets noticed except by whoever notices it, and apparently that's me.*

The sensible voice gave up, defeated less by compassion than by curiosity, which in Odile's experience was the stronger force anyway.

She knelt beside the woman. Up close the stranger was worse and better—worse because her lips were cracked and her collarbones stood out like the frame of a boat under canvas, better because she was breathing, slow and even, the breath of someone sleeping rather than dying. Ash had settled in the hollows of her eyes. Her hands were elegant and filthy, and on the first finger of her right hand was a ring: a band of dull metal set with a stone that was not any stone Odile knew, dark but full of moving light, like looking into a well at night and seeing stars that weren't there anymore.

Because they weren't anywhere anymore.

"Oh," Odile said softly. "Oh, you're one of theirs, aren't you. One of the star-folk."

The woman did not wake. Odile hovered a hand over her forehead—guild protocol for checking fever, the one piece of healing lore every member knew—and felt heat radiating off her skin like a banked oven. Burning up. Half-starved, by the look of her, and burning, and lying in a field of ash that had fallen from a sky with no stars in it.

"You're going to die here," Odile told her conversationally. "If I leave you. That's what happens to people in fields. I want you to know I'm aware of that, and I'm going to do it anyway, probably."

The woman's brow creased. Whether at the words or the fever, Odile couldn't say.

Here was the trouble, and Odile turned it over as she shrugged off her own coat—the good coat, the one with the mended elbows that she'd spent three evenings re-mending. The trouble was that guild law about strangers wasn't superstition. She'd always assumed it was, the way you assume the scary stories are just stories, but last night the stars had gone out and this morning the wards were dead, and the woman had arrived *with* the dying, the way smoke arrives with fire. Turning her away might genuinely be the safe thing. Feeding her, sheltering her—that might be how the valley lost whatever it had left.

But the other trouble, the deeper one, was this: Odile had spent twenty-six years being the person nobody turned toward. The youngest of nine guild members, the one whose name got left off the duty rosters because everyone assumed someone else had written it, the one who ate last and spoke least and was remembered chiefly as "the Fairweather girl, the small one." And she knew—with a knowledge that lived in her body rather than her mind, the way you know the shape of your own hands—exactly what it would mean to lie in a field while the whole world stepped carefully around you.

Nobody deserved that. Not even a stranger. Perhaps especially not a stranger.

"There," she said, hauling one of the woman's arms across her shoulders. The woman weighed almost nothing, which was its own kind of horrifying. "Up you get. Don't wake up yet, please, I haven't worked out what I'll say to you. I've had twenty-six years' practice talking to people who don't answer, so honestly you're ideal company."

The woman's head lolled against Odile's shoulder. Her hair smelled of lilac and burnt sugar, and underneath that, faintly, of cold.

* * *

Getting her back to the valley took most of the day, because Odile could not carry her and could not leave her and so devised the compromise of half-dragging, half-carrying her from hedge to hedge, resting at each one and delivering a running commentary to keep her own courage up.

"I'm taking you to the tannery cottage," she explained, somewhere past the second mile. "It's empty since Master Corwin died, and nobody goes there because they think it's unlucky, which is unfair to Corwin, who was only unlucky in the ordinary ways. There's a stove. There's a well. There's a great deal of tanner's salt, which we will not use on you, that was a joke, I hope you're not conscious enough to have taken offense."

At the fourth rest stop, in the lee of a drystone wall, the woman's eyes opened.

They were grey eyes, or silver, or both—the color of the ash, the color of the starless sky, and for a moment Odile had the vertiginous feeling of being looked at by the weather. The woman's cracked lips moved.

"...name," she whispered.

"Odile," said Odile, startled into honesty. "Odile Fairweather. Ward-keeper's guild, junior-most, indefinitely. What's yours?"

The woman's face did something terrible then. It wasn't pain exactly, though pain was in it—it was the look of someone reaching for a thing on a shelf that has never been there, patting the empty wood, patting again. Her mouth shaped a word. Nothing came. She tried again, and what came out was a thin thread of sound, a syllable that broke in the middle like ice underfoot, and then her eyes filled with a horror so complete and so quiet that Odile felt it in her own chest like a swallowed stone.

"I don't—" the woman rasped. "I can't—"

"It's alright," Odile said quickly, absurdly, the way you soothe anyone mid-crisis regardless of whether things are in fact alright. "Names go missing. Everything's gone missing lately. The stars went out last night—all of them, at once, like snuffed candles. Did you see? Were you—were you up there?"

The woman stared at her. And then, slowly, she lifted her right hand—the one with the dark ring—and held it up between them, turning it, watching the trapped light move in the stone. When she spoke again her voice was barely there, sanded down to a whisper.

"The court is dark," she said. "I ran. I ran and I fell and I fell and I—" Her breath hitched. "What fell with me?"

"Ash," Odile said. "A whole field of it. It tastes of lilac."

Something moved through the woman's face—recognition, or grief wearing recognition's coat. Her eyes closed. "Then it followed me," she whispered. "Or I led it. I don't remember which." And she was unconscious again, sliding down against the wall, her weight going boneless, the ring's dark stone pulsing once, twice, like a heart deciding whether to bother.

Odile sat in the ash with her back against the drystone wall and let herself, for one full minute, be afraid. It seemed only fair. She'd been brave for miles; a minute of fear was owed.

Then she got her shoulder under the woman again and stood up.

* * *

The tannery cottage smelled of old salt and forty years of pipe smoke, and it was, Odile thought as she got a fire going in the little stove, the safest place in the valley precisely because no one would ever look for anyone here. She built the fire high, boiled water, coaxed barley broth into the woman a spoonful at a time when she surfaced enough to swallow. The woman drank like someone who had forgotten drinking was allowed and kept expecting it to be taken away.

Toward dusk, Odile sat back on her heels and took proper stock of her situation, which was as follows: she was harboring a stranger in violation of the oldest law of her guild. The stranger was fae—or star-born, or whatever precise word the guild used and never explained to her. The stranger had no name, no shadow, and possibly no memory. And Odile had maybe a day, perhaps two, before the emergency census concluded and someone noticed that the junior-most member had not reported back from the ward-line.

Elder Bram would ask his three questions. She would have no answers. He would look through her like a window.

Except—he wouldn't, actually, she realized, sitting very still. Because Bram wouldn't need to ask questions. Bram would take one look at the woman's face and *know* her. Odile had seen it this morning, hadn't she? The way the elders had gone quiet when the stars failed—not surprised. *Prepared.* The way Bram had said "begin the census" in a voice like a man closing shutters before a storm he'd long been promised. The elders expected this. Whatever this was—whatever had fallen out of the dark and into her arms—they had been waiting for it, and they had not told her, and they had not told anyone.

On the cot, the woman stirred. Her eyes opened partway, found Odile, and fixed on her with an effort that seemed enormous.

"You stayed," she whispered.

"For now," Odile said. "I'm still deciding whether that was wise."

The corner of the woman's mouth moved—not quite a smile, but the ground where a smile might someday be planted. "Wise," she murmured, "is a very small country. I was born in a large one." Her eyes drifted closed again. Just before sleep took her, her hand crept across the blanket and caught the edge of Odile's sleeve, holding it the way a drowning person holds driftwood—without pride, without plan, simply holding on.

Odile sat in the firelight and did not pull her sleeve free.

Outside, the sun went down into a sky with no stars waiting behind it, and somewhere north, along a dead ward-line, the ash was still falling.
