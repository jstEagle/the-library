## 4. The Elders' Silence

### Odile

The guild convened at dawn, which was itself an admission of guilt. The Fairweather guild held its meetings at dusk, always had—after the day's rounds were walked, after the tea had gone cold in its pot, when everyone was too tired to argue properly. Dawn meetings were for emergencies. Dawn meetings meant someone had decided, overnight, that the rest of them needed managing.

Odile knew because she was the one who lit the lamps.

She was always the one who lit the lamps. Nine members, and somehow the task fell to her every single time, as though the lamps would simply refuse to burn for anyone else. She suspected Elder Wren had told the others this once, years ago, and it had calcified into fact the way things did in the valley: Odile Fairweather lights the lamps, Odile checks the outermost marker, Odile is the one who notices things, which is precisely why no one asks her what she's noticed.

She lit the last lamp in the meeting hall and then did not leave.

This was technically against protocol. Junior members delivered their reports and withdrew; she'd sat through a hundred such meetings, waiting in the antechamber with her hands folded, listening to the muffled murmur of voices deciding things about her life without her. But today the antechamber door had swollen with damp and wouldn't shut all the way, leaving a finger-width gap, and Odile told herself she was only staying to fix it later. She arranged herself on the bench beside the gap because the bench was there. Because her feet hurt. Because the hall smelled of beeswax and old paper and something else now—something cold and mineral, like the air off a glacier, and she wanted to know where it came from.

It came from the woman they'd carried in from the ash-field.

They'd laid her on the long table where the census rolls were usually spread, and Odile could see her through the gap: still unconscious, still wrong in a way Odile couldn't name. Her hair was silver-white but her face was young. Her skin had the faint luminosity of something that had once glowed brighter and been dimmed. And when the morning light through the windows touched her, it didn't seem to land—it bent, slightly, the way water bends around a stone.

No shadow. Odile kept coming back to that. Everyone else in the room cast one in the lamplight, long and ordinary. The woman cast nothing at all.

"Seal the doors," said Elder Bram.

His voice did something to the room. Bram was the eldest of them, past eighty, with a beard like frost on iron and hands that shook unless he was working magic, at which point they went perfectly, unnervingly still. He was also, Odile had long suspected, the only elder who actually remembered why any of their rituals existed rather than simply performing them. When he spoke, even Wren stopped fussing with her shawl.

"The doors are sealed," said Elder Cass, from her post by the lintel. "Wards drawn. If she wakes—"

"If she wakes," Bram said, "she will be very polite about it, and you will all feel like fools, and then she will ask us questions we cannot answer."

A silence. Odile leaned closer to the gap, breath held.

"You know her," said Elder Wren. It wasn't quite a question. Wren was the sharpest-eyed of the elders despite—or because of—the cloudiness of her gaze, and she was looking at Bram now with an expression Odile had never seen her wear. Fear, maybe. Or the memory of fear, worn smooth by decades of disuse.

"I knew someone," Bram said carefully, "who looked like that."

"Someone," repeated Elder Fen, the youngest of the elders, forty if he was a day, with the permanent air of a man doing sums he didn't like. "Or *her*. There's a difference, Bram, and I think you know it."

"There are nine of us left," Bram said. "Nine, out of a guild that once numbered three hundred. You want to spend our remaining strength arguing taxonomy?"

"I want to know," Fen said, "why the wards failed on the same night the stars went out, and why you looked at that sky like a man reading a letter he'd been dreading for thirty years."

Through the gap, Odile saw Bram's hands close slowly around the back of a chair. They went still.

*He expected it,* she thought, and the thought landed in her stomach like a swallowed stone. *Last night, when the stars went out—he wasn't surprised. He was* ***waiting.***

"The covenant—" Wren began.

"*Don't.*" It came out of Bram like a whipcrack, and then, horribly, his voice gentled into something almost pleading. "Don't say that word in here. Not with her lying ten feet away. Names have weight, Wren. You know that better than anyone—you've forgotten enough of them."

Odile's mouth went dry. *You've forgotten enough of them.* What kind of thing was that to say? What kind of thing was that for Wren to simply *accept*, nodding slowly, as though Bram had reminded her of a chore?

"She should be confined," said Cass. "The lower cellar. Warded."

"She's half-dead."

"She's *something*, and none of us knows what, and until we do, she stays where she can't do harm." Cass folded her arms. "Bram. You of all people should understand precaution."

Something passed across Bram's face then—guilt, Odile thought, or its fossil record—and when he spoke again his voice had gone flat and official, the voice he used for census day.

"Very well. The stranger goes to the lower cellar. Restrained, warded, fed." He paused. "And no one speaks to her. No one tells her where she is, or what happened to the sky, or anything of guild business. Is that understood? If she asks questions, you bring the questions to me. Not answers. Questions."

"And if she asks who we are?" Fen asked.

"Tell her nothing."

Odile slid off the bench, silent in her soft-soled ward-keeper's boots, and was halfway down the corridor before her heart stopped hammering. Behind her, faintly, she heard Fen's voice rise:

"—thirteenth turn, Bram. If it's really the thirteenth turn, confinement isn't going to save us—"

Then the sound of a door closing, firm and final, like a lid.

### Bram

Bram had buried four elders, two wives, and one terrible secret, and he had found that the order of difficulty ran roughly thus: wives first, elders second, and the secret a distant third, because the secret never stayed buried. It climbed out of the ground every thirteen years and stood at the foot of his bed wearing the faces of everyone he'd ever lied to.

Last night it had worn the whole sky.

He stood at the window of his study above the meeting hall, watching the valley wake under a starless dark that daylight couldn't touch. That was the worst part—not the night, but the day. The sun rose, the mist lifted, the chickens carried on being idiots in the yard, and yet overhead, where stars ought to have been waiting patiently behind the blue, there was only a flat black absence, like a page torn out of a book. The villagers below would notice soon. Some already had; he could see old Marta Tull standing in her garden staring up, her washing forgotten in the basket.

By evening they'd come up the hill asking questions. By tomorrow the questions would have teeth.

Bram opened the drawer of his desk and took out the ledger.

It was a plain thing, bound in grey leather gone soft as cloth, and it had sat in this drawer for longer than most of the guild had been alive. He hadn't opened it in eleven years. His hands, steady enough for magic, shook now as he turned to the page marked with the seal of the covenant—a circle of eight points, seven of them inked over, one still open.

Seven signatories dead. One fled. And the renewal due at the thirteenth turn, which was now, which was *this moon*, which he had known was coming the way you know a storm is coming—in your knees, in your sleep, in the way the birds went quiet.

*We had time,* he told himself, for perhaps the ten-thousandth time. *There were others. Someone else would act.*

That was the lie the last signatory had died believing, and Bram had inherited it like a debt, and he had done exactly what generations before him had done with the debt: he'd passed it down, smaller and quieter each time, hidden inside the rituals, tucked behind the forgetting.

Because that was the other thing in the drawer, under the ledger. A small stoppered vial of grey glass, empty these many years, that had once held the draught of unremembering. He set it on the desk and looked at it and felt the full weight of what he'd done—not cruelly, never cruelly, that was the thing he clung to at three in the morning. Gently. Kindly, even. Children slept, and woke lighter, and never asked why the guild of three hundred had become a guild of thirty, then fifteen, then nine. Never asked why the marker stones needed tending if nothing beyond them had stirred in living memory. Never asked what the covenant was, because the word itself had been taken from them, softly, spoonful by spoonful, mixed into the ceremony wine.

*Better a small peace than a large despair,* his own teacher had murmured, pressing the vial into Bram's young hands. *Let them keep the rituals. Let them keep the valley. Take only the question.*

And Bram had taken the questions, year after year, generation after generation, and now the bill had come due and the only person alive who might help pay it was lying senseless on a table downstairs with no shadow and no name.

*She signed it too,* he thought. *Mirabel of the Autumn Court, signatory of the fae, and she ran. Eleven years ago, when the first stars guttered, she ran, and I let her go, because a fugitive signatory was more use to me alive and gone than dead and questioned.*

He had told himself that too. He had told himself a great many things.

A knock at the door. "Elder Bram?" Odile's voice, muffled. "The kettle's on, and Elder Wren's asking whether the census is postponed or—"

"Postponed," he called back, too quickly, and heard the pause on the other side of the door. A small pause. An Odile-shaped pause, the kind she made when she was filing something away in that quiet head of hers. He'd have to remember she noticed things. He'd have to remember that of all the guild, the ones he'd dosed lightest were the ones who noticed most, because the draught took memory and left curiosity untouched, and some minds grew thorns around every hole punched in them.

"Thank you, child," he said, gentler. "Go get some rest. It's been a strange night."

"Yes, Elder," said the door, and her footsteps moved away—but slower than they needed to, he thought. Lingering.

Bram looked down at the ledger, at the eighth point of the circle, unfilled. At the empty vial beside it. At the window, and the torn black page of the sky.

Thirteen turns. The words circled him like crows.

*She signed it too,* he thought again, and this time the thought had teeth, because if Mirabel had run rather than renew it—if a creature centuries old, with magic in every step, had looked at the price of renewal and *fled*—

Then what hope was there for nine old ward-keepers and a valley full of sleeping children?

He put the ledger away. He did not put away the vial. Some habits, he reflected grimly, were the last memories a man kept.

Downstairs, faintly, he heard the cellar door open and close, and someone's footsteps descending toward the stranger.

Someone was disobeying him already.

He found, to his considerable surprise, that he could not bring himself to mind.
