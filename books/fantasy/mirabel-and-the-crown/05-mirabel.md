## 5. Mirabel

The first thing Mirabel learned about her cage was that it had been built by someone who understood cages very well and hated them.

She knew this because of the hinges. They had been oiled—recently, by the look of them—and the latch was set at a height convenient for a standing person to open without stooping. Whoever had made this cage had expected to open it often. Expected, perhaps, to regret opening it. There was a small shelf inside, and on the shelf a clay cup, and the cup had been glazed in a cheerful green that no one glazes a thing with unless they mean for its user to feel less like a prisoner and more like a guest who has overstayed.

Mirabel sat on the cot with her back against the wall and took inventory of herself, which was a shorter exercise than it should have been.

Her hands: present, thin, the nails broken from crawling through ash. Her hair: silver-white, matted, full of grit. Her shadow: absent. She checked twice, tilting her hand against the grey light that came through the barred window, and both times the light fell clean to the floor beside her, unbroken, as though she were not there at all. That was wrong. She was almost certain that was wrong. She had a strong feeling—a feeling like an old bruise pressed—that she had once had a shadow worth having, long and dramatic, the kind that swept ahead of you into rooms.

And her name. She reached for it the way you reach for a word on the tip of your tongue, and found the same shapeless hole. Not gone, exactly. *Sealed.* As if someone had wrapped it in cloth and put it away in a high cupboard, and she could see the outline of the parcel but not open it.

What she had instead were fragments, and they came to her uninvited, the way weather comes:

A court beneath a sky with no stars in it, lit instead by ten thousand hanging lanterns of pale glass, so that everyone below walked in a false and beautiful dusk and pretended not to notice what was missing overhead.

A throne room where the floor was polished riverstone and she had once, she was nearly sure, run down its length barefoot and been scolded for it by someone whose face she could not hold.

A sound—*the sound*—of stars going out. Not all at once. One by one, like windows being shuttered in a house at the edge of town, and everyone in the court pausing mid-sentence each time, counting, and then resuming their conversations a little too loudly.

Someone's hand in hers. Small. A child's hand. That fragment hurt in a way the others did not, a deep specific ache, and every time it surfaced she pushed it back down, because she did not have room for it yet.

*Later,* she told herself, as she had told herself for however many days she had been walking before she collapsed in a field of ash. *Survive first. Remember later.*

It was a good arrangement, right up until the girl arrived with the supper tray.

* * *

The girl was not what Mirabel expected of a jailer.

Jailers, in her experience—and her experience was now a strange, moth-eaten thing, but she trusted this piece of it—came in two varieties: the cruel, who enjoyed the work, and the dutiful, who had stopped seeing prisoners as people. This girl was neither. She was perhaps nineteen, brown-haired, plain in the way of someone who has decided not to be looked at, and she carried the tray with the careful resentment of a person doing a chore she had argued against and lost.

She set the tray on the shelf through the feeding slot without a word. Bread, a bowl of something stew-like, the green cup filled with water.

"Thank you," Mirabel said.

The girl flinched. Actually flinched, as though gratitude were a thrown stone.

"You're not supposed to talk to me," Mirabel guessed.

"I'm not supposed to do a lot of things." The girl said it flatly, to the tray, and then—this was the interesting part—she didn't leave. She stood there with her fingers hooked on the slot's edge, looking at Mirabel with an expression Mirabel recognized from courts and councils and every gathering of powerful people she had ever attended: the look of someone who has noticed the emperor is unclothed and is deciding whether anyone else has noticed too.

"What's your name?" Mirabel asked.

"Odile." Then, quickly, as if the word had escaped and needed catching: "Fairweather. Odile Fairweather."

"And mine?"

Odile's jaw tightened. "They said you don't have one."

"They said," Mirabel repeated pleasantly. "Did they say why?"

"No." A pause. "They said a lot of things wouldn't be explained to me. That's most of what they say."

Mirabel bit into the bread. It was dense and slightly stale and the best thing she had eaten since—well. Since she could remember, which was a smaller span of time than she liked. She chewed thoughtfully and watched Odile watch her eat, and began, quietly, to conduct an experiment.

Because here was what Mirabel knew about herself, even nameless: she had spent a very long life in a court full of people who recited rules at each other. Rules were the court's native language. *One does not run in the gallery. One does not ask the Queen her age. One does not mention the sky.* And she had learned, over centuries, to sort people instantly into those who spoke rules and those who spoke questions. The rule-speakers were safe and useless. The question-askers were dangerous and occasionally magnificent.

So she tested.

"The bread's good," she said. "Is it yours?"

"We take turns baking. Today was mine, so if it's bad, that's my fault."

"It's bad," Mirabel said gravely. "It's the worst bread I've ever eaten. I'd like another loaf."

Odile blinked. And then—the corner of her mouth moved. Not quite a smile. The *memory* of a smile, kept somewhere safe, brought out briefly into the light. "Elder Hesta says humor is a sign of a weak mind."

"Elder Hesta sounds delightful."

"She's ninety-one and can lift a ward-stone with her mind, so yes, we're all very fond of her." Odile said it dryly, and then seemed startled by her own dryness, the way a person is startled to find water coming from a tap they thought was sealed.

Question-asker, Mirabel noted. Disguised as a rule-follower. The best kind.

* * *

The tests continued over the following days, because Mirabel had nothing else to do and a lifetime of practice at reading people, and because—she admitted this only to herself, in the dark, in the honest hour—because she was lonely, and loneliness makes even interrogation feel like company.

She learned the shape of the guild through Odile's answers, or rather through Odile's careful non-answers, which were more informative. Nine members left. Rituals performed "as they've always been done." Wards maintained "at the markers." When Mirabel asked *why* the wards existed—not how, not where, but *why*, the question under all questions—Odile went still in a way that told Mirabel everything.

"I don't know," Odile said finally. "That's the true answer. We keep them because keeping them is the duty. Nobody asks why anymore. I asked when I was eight and Elder Bram told me the asking was the danger." Her hands twisted together on the far side of the bars. "I stopped asking for eleven years."

"And now?"

"Now the stars went out and the wards failed and a woman fell out of the dark with no shadow," Odile said, "so I've decided the asking was maybe never the danger."

Mirabel laughed—an actual laugh, rusty, surprising them both. It echoed strangely off the stone, because there was no shadow to soften it, and Odile stared at her as if the laugh itself were evidence of something.

"You really don't remember anything," Odile said. It wasn't a question, but Mirabel answered it anyway.

"I remember a court. Lanterns instead of stars. A floor I ran down barefoot. A child's hand in mine." She flexed her fingers, remembering the pressure of that small grip. "I remember that I signed something. I don't know what. My hands remember ink." She held them up, palms out, as if the proof might be visible. "Whatever I was, I was important enough to sign things and foolish enough to run afterward."

"From what?"

"That," said Mirabel, "is the question, isn't it? And I suspect we're both going to hate the answer."

* * *

On the fourth day, Odile brought the supper tray and, along with it, a folded scrap of paper, which she passed through the slot with the furtive speed of someone smuggling contraband, which she was.

Mirabel unfolded it. On it, in a careful, cramped hand, was a list. *Thirteenth moon. Ledger. Adjustments. She signed it too.*

She read it three times. The last line snagged her like a fishhook. *She signed it too.* Overheard, clearly, from Elder Bram's mouth, and written down by a girl who was beginning to understand that her elders' silence had walls behind it.

"Where did you hear this?" Mirabel asked.

"Through the archive door. Bram doesn't know I listen." Odile's chin lifted slightly. "He thinks I'm obedient. Everyone thinks I'm obedient. Being overlooked is a skill. I've had a lot of practice."

There it was again—the flash beneath the plainness. Mirabel had known queens who would have traded half their kingdoms for that particular talent, and here it sat in a ward-keeper's daughter like an unlit lamp.

"*She signed it too,*" Mirabel read aloud. "Odile. What do you think that means?"

"I think it means you signed whatever the covenant is. The thing the wards are for." Odile's voice dropped. "I think Bram knows you. I saw his face when they carried you in. He looked—" she searched for it— "*guilty*. Elders aren't supposed to look guilty. They're supposed to look like they know what they're doing."

"And instead?"

"Instead they look like men standing in a house where the floors have started to hum."

Mirabel folded the paper and held it against her chest. "You shouldn't have brought me this. If they find out—"

"If they find out, I'll say I dropped it." Odile shrugged. "Nobody punishes me for anything. That's the one advantage of being nobody." A pause, and then, quieter, with something raw underneath: "It's also the whole of it. There's nothing else."

And there—there was the moment. Mirabel had conducted her little tests for four days, sorting and probing, waiting for the rule-recitation, the flinch toward duty, the comfortable recitation of *we keep the wards because we keep the wards.* Instead she had gotten questions. Stolen papers. Dry jokes aimed at elders. A girl who had been erased so thoroughly she mistook her own invisibility for a personality.

Two outsiders, Mirabel thought, recognizing each other across a set of bars. One who had lost her name to the dark, and one who had never been permitted one bright enough to lose.

"Odile Fairweather," she said formally, "you are the worst jailer I have ever had, and I include in that count a fae knight who once imprisoned me for a century and forgot to feed me for the first decade."

"A century?" Odile's eyes widened. "How old *are* you?"

"Rude question. Unanswerable. Possibly unanswerable forever." Mirabel smiled, and it felt strange on her face, like wearing a garment from a previous life—which, she supposed, it was. "Ask me something better."

Odile thought. She was visibly, endearingly bad at thinking of questions quickly, as though all her questions had been stored so long they'd grown tangled. Then: "The child. Whose hand do you remember?"

Mirabel went quiet.

The fragment rose again, that small warm grip, and this time she let it stay. "I don't know," she said honestly. "But I think—I think I'm going to find out. And I think it might be the reason I'm still alive, when everything else I knew is dead or dark."

Outside the little barred window, the sky was doing what it always did now: nothing. No stars. No moon yet either—the thirteenth moon was still weeks off, Bram's stolen notes said, and every day it did not rise was a day borrowed.

Odile gathered the empty tray. At the door she paused, hand on the latch, and did not turn around when she spoke.

"There's a marker stone past the ash-field. The outermost one. I tend it alone—it's always been my assignment, nobody wants the walk." Her voice was carefully casual, the way voices go when they are carrying something fragile. "Tomorrow I'll be out there a while. If a caged woman were the sort to escape, that would be the day the door was oiled and the guard was elsewhere. Hypothetically."

"Hypothetically," Mirabel agreed solemnly.

The latch clicked. Footsteps faded down the corridor. And Mirabel sat alone in her well-oiled, thoughtfully-shelved, gently-glazed cage, holding a stolen scrap of paper, and allowed herself—for the first time since the stars had died—to hope.

Because the covenant needed signatories. The note said so, between the lines: *thirteenth moon, ledger, adjustments, she signed it too.* And a signatory who had fled was not the same as a signatory who had given up. She had run from the court because running had seemed like survival. But survival alone was just a longer way of dying, and she had walked far enough through the starless country to know it.

If the girl opened that door—if the girl came *with* her—

Mirabel lay back on the cot and addressed the ceiling, which was the closest thing she had to a confidant.

"Well," she told it. "I suppose tomorrow we find out whether I can pick a lock, or whether I have to wait for a nobody to become a somebody."

The ceiling, in the manner of ceilings, said nothing. But somewhere above it, beyond the roof, beyond the valley walls, the empty sky waited too—and it was not patient, and it was not empty by accident, and somewhere in its vast dark, things that ate names were already turning toward the smell of a fugitive noblewoman hiding in a guild of forgetting.

Three days, Mirabel judged. Perhaps four.

Then the thirteenth moon would begin to rise, and every debt in two realms would come due at once.
