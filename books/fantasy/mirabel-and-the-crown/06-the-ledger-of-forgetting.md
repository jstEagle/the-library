## 6. The Ledger of Forgetting

The guild archive smelled the way all old places smell when no one loves them enough: dust and tallow and the faint sweet rot of paper that had been meaning to be read for forty years.

Odile let herself in before dawn, which was not difficult, because the door didn't lock. Nothing in the Fairweather guild locked, really. The wards were supposed to make locks unnecessary, and now the wards were dead as winter flies, and still nobody had thought to buy a bolt. It was, she reflected, a very guild-like oversight. We protected everything so long that we forgot how to protect anything at all.

She brought a candle, a stub of bread she wasn't hungry for, and Mirabel's parting words from the night before, delivered through the bars of the storeroom window with the particular dry patience of someone who has been imprisoned by amateurs:

"You want answers? Guilds like yours always write things down. They have to. Memory fails; ink doesn't. Look for the boring books, Odile Fairweather. Nobody hides secrets in exciting ones."

*Look for the boring books.* Odile looked around at the archive, which consisted of one long room, three shelves of ledgers, a cabinet of census rolls, and a stuffed owl that had been losing its eyes since before she was born. Boring books were rather the entire collection.

"Right," she told the owl. "You keep watch."

The owl, in the manner of owls, said nothing. She was beginning to notice a pattern with her confidants.

* * *

She started with the census rolls because they were nearest, and because there was a comfort in them—the annual counting of the guild, nine names in nine careful hands going back two hundred years, the same nine names repeating and thinning like a braid worn down to threads. Her own name appeared at the bottom of every recent page, written small. *Odile Fairweather, ward-tender, outer markers.* Even in ink she was an afterthought.

But it was the third shelf that stopped her.

The ledgers there were bound in grey—not black, not brown, but a grey so deliberate it could only have been chosen, the color of weather, of fog, of things seen unclearly. Their spines bore no titles. Only numbers. And the earliest number was older than any census roll in the room.

Odile pulled the first one down and felt the weight of it settle into her hands like something alive pretending to sleep.

The opening page was written in a hand she recognized, because she'd spent her childhood copying it: Elder Bram's, though younger—sharper, less tired, the handwriting of a man who still believed his pen was doing good work.

*Ledger of Adjustments,* it began. *Being a true record of memories removed from members of the Fairweather Guild, per the Rite of Quiet Keeping, for the preservation of the covenant and the peace of the valley.*

Odile sat down on the floor without deciding to. Her legs simply ended their arrangement with the rest of her.

*Adjustments.*

She knew the rite. Of course she knew the rite—or she knew its shell, the way you know a song whose words have been washed out of it. Every child raised in the guild underwent the Quiet Keeping at seven years old. It was described as a blessing: a gentle ritual, candles and warm milk and Elder Bram's low voice, after which you slept deeply and woke feeling *lighter*, everyone said so, lighter and calmer and ready to begin your duties. Odile remembered the morning after hers. She remembered feeling smooth inside, like a stone from the riverbed. She remembered thinking that this was what belonging felt like.

She turned the page.

The entries were meticulous. Bram had been many things, but he had never been sloppy.

*Member: Hesta Fairweather. Age 7. Adjustment: memory of mother's death (witnessed). Reason: grief interferes with ward-sensitivity training. Retained fragments: none.*

*Member: Colm Fairweather. Age 7. Adjustment: memory of attempt to leave the valley via north pass. Reason: departure ideation. Retained fragments: general unease at high altitudes (useful).*

*Member: Wren Fairweather. Age 7. Adjustment: memory of conversation overheard between signatories regarding the Thirteenth Turn. Reason: [entry torn out].*

Odile read the names twice, three times. Hesta, who baked bread and hummed off-key and had once—hadn't she?—had once done something, been somewhere, lost something. Colm, who complained of headaches whenever he climbed the ridge road and had never wondered why. Wren, who was the quietest of them all, quiet as a held breath, and who flinched, Odile now realized with a cold sliding feeling, whenever anyone mentioned the moon.

Every name had a page. Every page had entries. Some members had one adjustment. Some had four, five, six, spread across decades like a slow leak.

And then she found her own.

*Member: Odile Fairweather.*

Her page was thick.

Not thick with years—she was younger than most of them—but thick in the way a book is thick when pages have been *added*. Loose leaves interleaved among the originals, updates appended across time, Bram's hand growing older down the margins until another hand took over, and another. She counted. Eleven entries. Eleven adjustments, more than anyone else in the guild, more than double poor flinching Wren.

Her candle guttered. She steadied it with a hand that did not entirely feel like hers.

*Entry 1. Age 7. Standard Quiet Keeping performed. Adjustment: memory of the night of the Falling Star (age 4), including presence of visitor at the western marker. Reason: subject spoke of the event repeatedly and asked questions regarding the ward-line. Retained fragments: fondness for star imagery (harmless), unexplained comfort in the company of strangers (monitor).*

Odile's mouth went dry. A visitor. At the western marker. When she was four.

*Entry 3. Age 9. Adjustment: memory of discovery of loose stone behind the archive cabinet. Reason: subject located Ledger of Adjustments and read several pages. Note: this is the second such occurrence. Recommend relocation of ledger; recommend also consideration of whether subject's retention is anomalous. Ordinary adjustments hold. Hers do not. She finds her way back to the edges of things.*

There was a longer note beneath, in Bram's oldest, most tired hand, pressed hard enough into the paper to tear it slightly, as if the pen had been gripped by someone arguing with himself:

*I begin to suspect we are not adjusting this child. I begin to suspect we are damming her. And I have seen what happens downstream of dams.*

*Recommendation withheld. Request guidance from remaining signatories. Request denied. There are no remaining signatories. There is only me, and I am so tired, and the thirteenth turn is coming, and perhaps—perhaps if she remembers enough, when the time comes—*

The entry stopped there. Unfinished. The next line was in a different hand, brisker, colder:

*Entry 4. Age 11. Adjustment: memory of friendship formed at western marker with unknown child (see Entry 1). Reason: recurring dreams causing emotional instability. Subject described the friend as "the girl with no shadow." Retained fragments: habit of leaving offerings of bread at the markers (repurposed as duty).*

The girl with no shadow.

The bread. All those years Odile had left bread at the outermost stones, and everyone had praised her diligence, her devotion to small duties, and it had never once occurred to her to ask why she'd started. Because she hadn't started. Someone had taken the reason away and left the habit standing, the way you might strip a house down to its frame and call the skeleton architecture.

She was crying before she noticed, which annoyed her. She wiped her face on her sleeve with the brisk efficiency of someone tending a chore and kept reading, because Mirabel was right about her, had been right from the first hour: Odile Fairweather did not stop at the edge of things. She leaned over them.

*Entry 7. Age 14. Adjustment: memory of overhearing council discussion of covenant lapse. Reason: standard. Note: subject again retained fragments. Subject is asking why the stars matter. No other member asks why. I am beginning to think the erasures made us blind and made her the only one who can see, and I do not know whether that is my failure or my hope.*

*Entry 11. Age 17. Final note. I have stopped adjusting her. Let the record show it. Not mercy—cowardice, or wisdom, or both. If the covenant falls, we will need someone who remembers how to be curious. We will need someone the forgetting didn't take root in. Gods forgive me, I may have been keeping her sharp on purpose. Or she simply cannot be dulled. Either way: if anyone reads this, find Odile Fairweather. She notices what the rest of us were made unable to.*

The candle had burned down to a coin of wax. Outside, the sky was going grey at the edges, the colorless, starless grey that had replaced dawn, and somewhere below her feet Mirabel was waking in her cage, and somewhere across the hall nine elders—eight elders, Wren had taken to her bed—were preparing for a day of rituals maintained by rote, each of them carrying holes in their hearts shaped exactly like the missing pieces of themselves, and calling the holes peace.

Odile closed the ledger gently, the way you close a coffin.

* * *

She sat for a long while afterward with her back against the shelf, the grey-bound book in her lap, watching dust drift through the candle smoke.

Here was the thing she kept circling, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth: she had spent her whole life believing herself overlooked. The last name on the census. The ward-tender of the outer markers, given the farthest walks and the smallest thanks. And she had built a whole quiet philosophy around it—that some people were simply background, that her lot was to be the one who noticed while others were noticed—and it had hurt, but it had at least been *natural*, a fact of the world like weather.

Now the ledger lay open in her mind, and the philosophy lay in pieces.

Because what if it hadn't been natural? What if her invisibility had been *installed*? Eleven adjustments. Fragments deliberately retained—"unexplained comfort in the company of strangers," "fondness for star imagery"—like a gardener pruning a tree into a shape. Had the guild made her small? Had they pruned and pruned until what remained was a person suited only for margins?

And yet.

And yet Bram's own hand admitted it: *she cannot be dulled.* Eleven times they had cut her, and eleven times she had grown back toward the light, asking why, finding the loose stone, reading the forbidden pages, walking out alone to the markers where the interesting things happened. You could dam a river, but the river kept a direction.

Maybe that was the horror. Not that they had tried to erase her. That they had failed, and she would never know how much of what remained was truly hers, and how much was what they'd *chosen* to leave—fragments curated like a museum exhibit. Fondness for stars: harmless, retain. Comfort with strangers: monitor. Which parts of Odile were Odile, and which parts were Bram's annotations?

A floorboard creaked in the corridor.

Odile went very still, ledger hugged to her chest like a stolen child. The creak came again—slow, heavy, familiar. She knew that tread. Everyone in the guild knew it. Elder Bram walked the halls before dawn every morning, checking the dead wards out of habit, a man inspecting a fence around a field where nothing grew anymore.

His shadow passed under the archive door. Paused.

For one absurd moment Odile considered hiding. Then she considered that she was twenty-two years old, sitting on the floor of a room she'd cleaned since childhood, holding proof that the man on the other side of the door had cut pieces out of her mind eleven times, and that hiding was precisely what she'd been engineered to do.

She stood up. She put the ledger back on the shelf—no. She didn't. She tucked it under her arm instead, and when the door opened and Bram filled the frame, candle in hand, his face already arranging itself into the mild apology he wore for her the way he wore it for everyone—

"Elder," she said, before he could speak. "I know about the Quiet Keeping. I know what you took from me. I've counted."

Bram went white in the candlelight. Not the flush of guilt caught—something worse. The look of a man who has been waiting twenty years for this knock on the door and hoped, every single morning, it would never come.

"So," he said quietly. His eyes went to the ledger under her arm, and then to her face, and something in them broke and reformed, broke and reformed, like ice on the river in thaw. "Eleven?"

"Eleven."

He set his candle down on the cabinet with a hand that trembled, and lowered himself onto the stool by the door as if the weight of the question had finally, after all these years, been permitted to land.

"Then sit down, Odile," he said, "because there were things even the ledger doesn't say. Things I only ever told the signatories—and the last of them died believing someone else would tell you." He rubbed his face with both hands. "Thirteen turns, child. Do you know what comes at the thirteenth turn?"

Above them, beyond the roof, beyond the valley walls, the empty sky pressed down like a held breath.

"No," Odile said. "But I'm going to."
