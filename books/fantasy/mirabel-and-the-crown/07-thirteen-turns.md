## 7. Thirteen Turns

Elder Bram had not slept properly in forty years, and he had come to regard this as a professional qualification.

A man who slept well, he reasoned, was a man who believed things were fine. A man who lay awake listening to the wards hum their thin, tired note through the floorboards of the guildhouse—a man like that understood his work. And Bram's work, whatever the census ledgers said, had never been keeping the borders. His work was remembering what everyone else had been made to forget.

He sat in the archive room at the bottom of the guildhouse stairs, where the candlelight went amber and the air smelled of dust and old tallow and, faintly, of burnt feathers—the smell of memory-magic, which clung to a room the way guilt clings to a coat. Before him lay the ledger. Not the census ledger, not the tithe ledger, but the third one, the one kept behind a false panel that Odile Fairweather dusted every week without ever once, in eleven years of dusting, asking why the panel didn't open.

That was the trouble with the erasure-work done well. You stopped noticing the shape of what was missing.

Bram opened the ledger to the page he had read so many times the ink had begun to look nervous.

*Adjustments, Fairweather line.* His own hand, younger and steadier, recording each removal in the tidy script of a man who still believed he was being merciful.

*Odile Fairweather, age four. Adjustment: first sight of the marker-line crossing. Reason: child asked why the stones wept.*

*Odile Fairweather, age six. Adjustment: the visitor. Reason: see attached.*

There was no attachment. There never was. He had learned early that if you wrote *see attached* on enough pages, you could go years without looking at what you'd attached them to.

He rubbed his eyes. Somewhere above him, the guildhouse creaked through its nightly litany—Hesper locking the herb cupboard twice because once no longer felt finished, old Wenna shuffling to the window to count stars that weren't there anymore, and finding, as she did every night now, her counting hands going slack in her lap.

Nine of them left. Once there had been sixty-three.

Once there had been a covenant.

---

The covenant, Bram thought, was rather like the guild's roof: nobody thought about it until it leaked, and by then the argument was about buckets instead of carpentry.

It had been signed before his grandmother's grandmother drew breath—an accord between the human valley and the star-born court beyond the markers, renewed every thirteen turns of the moon by living signatories from each realm. The fae gave the sky its stars; the humans gave the court its anchor in the world; the ward-keepers held the seam between. Thirteen moons, thirteen turns, renewal upon renewal, an unbroken chain of signatures stretching back further than any human record.

And chains, Bram had learned, do not break all at once. They break link by link, quietly, while everyone admires how well the chain is holding.

He had been thirty when Elder Sorrel died—the last signatory, the last person alive whose name meant anything to the covenant's old magic. Bram remembered the funeral clearly, which was itself an irony he appreciated only later, given his profession. He remembered Sorrel on her deathbed gripping his wrist with a strength that embarrassed them both.

"You'll renew it," she'd said. "You'll find the new signatories. It must be signed by the willing, Bram. That's the whole of the law. Willing hands, both realms."

"Of course," he'd said. "Of course, Elder."

And she had died believing him, with an expression of such perfect peace that Bram had carried it ever after like a stone in his boot. She had died *comfortable*. She had died certain that the guild she'd served for ninety years would do the one thing it existed to do.

She had died believing someone else would act.

Bram had spent the following year trying. He had gone down into the archive, into the deepest shelf, and pulled out the Rite of Renewal, and read it, and read it again, and discovered the flaw in his inheritance: the rite required signatories who *knew*. Who knew what the covenant was, what it cost, what the stars actually were—not lights, but promises, each one a vow the fae court had hung in the sky where both realms could watch it kept. The rite required people who understood why the valley's children were taught to bow to the dark.

And the guild's children knew nothing. Knew less than nothing. Knew a set of rituals by rote and a list of small duties and a pleasant fog of "this is simply how things are done."

Because Bram's own elders had erased them. Because their elders had erased *them*. The forgetting went back so far that no one alive had chosen it; they had merely inherited it, the way you inherit a leaky roof, and patched it each generation with fresh adjustments rather than fix the carpentry underneath.

He could have taught them. That was the road not taken, and he walked past it every day of his life. He could have stood in the common room and said: *Listen. The stars are vows. The wards are a handshake older than kingdoms. We have let the handshake go limp for want of anyone willing to grip it.* He could have named a signatory, trained them, told them the truth entire, and let a willing soul step forward.

Instead, forty years ago, a younger Bram had looked at the faces of his guild—kind, dutiful, incurious Hesper; steady Wenna; little Tam with his head full of songs—and thought: *they would be frightened. And I would be the one who frightened them.*

So he had adjusted them instead. Small trims, mostly. A memory here of a question asked too sharply. A night there when someone saw something move along the ward-line that had no business moving. He told himself each cut was kindness. He told himself the covenant could wait; thirteen moons was a long time; there would be a better season for truth.

Thirteen moons became twenty-six. Twenty-six became fifty-two. The chain rusted link by link, and Bram sat in the archive room with his ledger and his candle and his *see attached*, telling himself the same lie with increasingly poor conviction: *next turn. Next turn I will tell them everything.*

He was very good at waiting. It was, he had come to understand, the coward's version of patience.

---

* * *

On the ninth day after the stars went out, Bram climbed the stairs to the common room to preside over the morning meal, and found the guild arguing about porridge.

This was not unusual. What was unusual was the subject beneath the subject.

"—all I'm saying," Hesper was declaring, spoon aloft, "is that salt belongs in it. My mother put salt in porridge and her porridge was famous."

"Your mother also claimed she'd seen a marsh-wight," said Tam, "and the porridge was famously *gray*."

"It was *rustic*."

"It was punishment with lumps."

Bram stood in the doorway and let the warmth of it wash over him, and hated himself a little for how much he loved these people, and how thoroughly he had lied to every one of them. Nine aging ward-keepers bickering over breakfast in a house whose purpose had just failed for the first time in living memory. The stars were gone. The wards were down. And the great crisis of the age had been met, so far, with a dispute about seasoning.

He almost smiled. Then he caught Odile's eye across the room.

Odile was not arguing. Odile was watching him with that particular stillness she had—the stillness of someone standing very quietly at the edge of a conversation, waiting to be noticed, and knowing she wouldn't be. Eleven years of dusting the false panel. Eleven years of tending the outermost stone alone. Eleven years of being the one who noticed things, and being thanked for it in the way one thanks furniture.

*She asked me last week,* Bram remembered, with a lurch, *why the panel doesn't open.*

And he had answered, "Because it's just paneling, dear," and she had said, "Oh," in a voice that had not believed him even slightly, and he had gone to bed and lain awake until dawn, which for him was standard, but for once not comforting.

Something was coming loose. He could feel it the way he felt weather in his knee: the girl was starting to pull threads.

"Salt," he said firmly, "in moderation. Both positions honored. Now—business."

The room settled. Nine faces turned to him, and Bram felt the full weight of the speech he had rehearsed for nine nights and abandoned nine times.

"The stars," he began, "are—"

Gone. Dead. Eaten. *Vows, and the vows have been called due.* Every true sentence queued up behind his teeth and jammed there, forty years of cowardice forming a clot.

"—are a matter requiring study," he finished.

Silence. Wenna's spoon stopped halfway to her mouth. Even Hesper lowered the salt.

"Study," repeated Tam.

"Careful, methodical study," said Bram, with the confidence of a man walking backward off a cliff. "The guild has protocols for celestial irregularities. In the meantime, daily duties continue. Marker rounds, ward-tending, census preparation. All as normal."

"As normal," Odile said quietly, "with no stars."

"As normal," Bram agreed, and heard, in his own voice, the exact tone Sorrel must have heard from him forty years ago—*of course, Elder*—the tone of a man promising something he had already decided not to do.

Wenna frowned at him, slow as a rising tide. "Bram. When my husband died, I remember—" She stopped. Her brow furrowed. Her hand drifted to her temple, the way it always did when she reached for something and found the shelf empty. "I remember... I remember that I remember something about this. About the dark. Something someone told me once, and then..."

"And then?" Odile asked softly.

"And then it's like reaching into a pocket and finding the hole." Wenna looked around the table, suddenly uncertain, suddenly old. "Does anyone else feel that? Like there was something we all used to know?"

Nobody answered. But everybody shifted. Nine chairs creaked in the same key.

Bram's stomach turned to lead. This was the thing he had never planned for—that erasure might not hold forever, that the removed memories might leave *edges*, and that edges, prodded often enough, might start to itch. The starless sky was prodding. Every night, nine people counted nothing where light should be, and the nothing was rubbing against the holes he'd cut in them.

"I'll look into the archives," he said. "For protocols."

He fled to the archive. It was, he reflected on the stairs, becoming less a refuge than a habit with a door.

---

* * *

In the archive, he did not open the ledger. He opened the Rite of Renewal instead, and laid out the documents of succession, and faced, at last, the arithmetic he had avoided for four decades.

The covenant needed two willing signatories—one of each realm—to renew it before the thirteenth moon turned. The thirteenth moon was now fewer than three turns away. He had no human signatory, because he had spent forty years manufacturing people incapable of consenting to anything. And he had no fae signatory at all, because the fae court—

The fae court, the records said, had fallen silent two generations ago. No envoy at the turning festivals. No answer to the ritual letters, which the guild had continued sending into the dark with decreasing sincerity, like notes posted to a friend who'd moved without leaving an address.

*They think we abandoned it,* Bram realized, with a coldness that started in his spine and spread outward. *Or worse—they think we never intended to renew at all. Forty years of silence from our side. From their side, silence too. Two peoples each waiting for the other to knock.*

And in the meantime, something else had knocked. Something that ate stars.

He pressed his palms flat against the Rite's pages. *Willing hands, both realms.* Where in all the broken world was he to find a fae noble willing to sign, and a human soul who even understood what they'd be signing?

The candle guttered. And Bram, staring at the *see attached* beside Odile's name, age six, felt the oldest memory of all rise up unbidden—the one adjustment he had never recorded in the ledger because it had never been an adjustment. It was simply his, kept secret even from himself in the way that the worst secrets are.

Age six. Odile Fairweather, small and solemn and forever underfoot, standing at the outermost marker stone, talking to someone on the other side of the ward-line. A child of the other realm, pale-haired, laughing at something Odile had said. Two girls passing things through a gap in the world: a ribbon for a river-stone, a joke for a joke.

Bram had found them there at dusk. And he had done his duty. He had taken the visitor's memory from Odile, and reported the breach, and filed the incident, and told himself—as he would tell himself for forty years—that the fae child had been sent home safely to her court.

But the report had come back, years later, in a box of the court's returned correspondence: the child had never arrived home. She had vanished somewhere between the ward-line and the stars, and her name had been struck from the court's rolls, and the court had sent one final letter, addressed to the guild, that the guild's then-elders had never answered.

Bram had answered it. Alone. Late at night, thirty years ago, with hands that shook. *We do not know where she is,* he had written. *If she lives, she does not remember us, and we do not remember her. Forgive us. We are a guild of small duties, and this duty was larger than we were.*

No reply had ever come.

Until, nine days ago, something had fallen out of the broken dark into the ash-field by the outermost stone. A woman with no shadow. Half-starved. Pale-haired.

Bram had recognized her face the moment they carried her in. He had ordered her confined not out of caution—or not only—but because his knees had nearly given way beneath the weight of recognition, and a man cannot confess while falling.

*She came back,* he thought, and the thought cracked something open in his chest that had been sealed since Sorrel's death. *After everything. After the court fell silent and the stars went out, she crawled back across the dark to the exact stone where she'd lost her name. And the only person in either realm who ever knew her—the only person whose erased memory holds her face—is Odile Fairweather.*

Willing hands, both realms. One of them possibly asleep in the confinement cell below, if she still had a name to sign with. The other currently dusting a false panel and pulling threads and looking at Bram with eyes that missed nothing, because he had trimmed everything else from her and left the noticing.

He could tell her. Tonight. He could climb the stairs, find Odile, and say: *Sit down. There is a covenant, and a court, and a girl you loved before you could write your own name, and I took it all from you, and I am sorry, and I need your help.*

His hand was actually on the ledger, actually lifting it, when he heard the footsteps overhead—quick, light, deliberate, heading not toward the common room but toward the confinement stair. Toward the cell. Carrying, if his ears were right, a covered bowl and a bundle that clinked faintly, the way a bundle clinks when it contains, among other things, a spare key.

Bram stood alone in the amber dark with forty years of cowardice in one hand and the truth in the other, and realized, with something between horror and a terrible relief, that he had waited exactly long enough for the choice to be taken out of his hands.

Above him, soft as snowfall, the guildhouse door opened and closed.

Odile was leaving. And she was not leaving alone.
