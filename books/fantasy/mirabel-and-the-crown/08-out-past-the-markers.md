## 8. Out Past the Markers

The guild hall slept the way old things sleep: loudly.

Odile had counted on that. Elder Bram snored in a rhythm she could set a water-clock by—three long breaths, a wheeze like a kettle changing its mind, three more. Sister Henn's door leaked candlelight until well past midnight, but it always went dark before the third bell, because Sister Henn believed discipline was something you could schedule. And the youngest of them, Tam, who was forty-one and still called the youngest, talked in his sleep about turnips.

So when Odile slipped down the corridor with her boots in her hand and her heart hammering somewhere up around her ears, she moved through a house of predictable noises, and none of them broke.

She had rehearsed this. That was the humiliating part. She'd rehearsed it for six nights, lying awake under the eaves, composing arguments she would never use, escape routes she would never need, speeches to Bram that began *Elder, I must protest* and ended, in every version, with her being patted on the head and sent back to tend marker stones. She was Odile Fairweather, after all. The one they forgot to summon. The one whose name trailed off at the end of roll calls like a sentence no one bothered to finish.

Nobody overlooked themselves out of a locked cellar.

But nobody had ever actually locked the cellar, either. That was the thing she'd discovered on the fourth night, standing in front of Mirabel's door with a lamp and no plan: there was no lock. There was a chair wedged under the handle from the outside, and a warding chalk-circle someone had drawn with a shaking hand, and a sign that said DO NOT ENTER in Bram's careful script—and nothing else. No iron. No binding. Nothing a woman with a broom and a grievance couldn't undo.

They hadn't even thought she needed containing properly. Or they hadn't thought *Odile* needed containing properly. Either way, the insult and the opportunity were the same object, and Odile had stood there holding both until her arms got tired.

Now the chair was back against the wall where she'd found it, the chalk was smeared with a wet thumb, and the door swung open onto darkness and the smell of cold ash.

"About time," said the darkness.

Mirabel sat against the far wall with her knees drawn up, and even starved and shadowless and half-feral, she managed to make it look like a throne. Her eyes caught what little light there was and gave none of it back.

"You could have knocked," Odile whispered. "I brought food."

"I could smell the bread through the floor. Do you know what that does to a person? Three days of bread-smell through floorboards." Mirabel rose in one fluid motion, took the bundle, and then paused, her fingers resting on the cloth. She looked at Odile properly, which few people ever did, and it was like being caught in weather. "You're doing this. You're actually doing this."

"I'm—" Odile's voice did something embarrassing. She cleared her throat. "I'm returning you to wherever you came from. It's guild law that strangers be turned away. I'm simply... turning you away very thoroughly."

"Very thoroughly," Mirabel repeated. Something flickered across her face—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile might live if it had survived hard winters. "And yourself?"

"What about myself?"

"Are you turning yourself away too?"

Odile had an answer prepared for this. She'd prepared it on night five, between composing her speech to Bram and imagining Tam's turnip monologue. It was a good answer, sensible, about duty and reconnaissance and how someone ought to see the ward-line from the outside to assess the damage.

What came out instead was: "Nobody will notice I've gone until the census. Maybe not even then."

It hung there in the dark, too honest, and Mirabel looked at her for a long moment without saying anything at all, which was somehow worse and better than sympathy.

"Well," Mirabel said finally, shouldering past her into the corridor. "Then we'll give them something to notice. Which way is out?"

* * *

The valley at night, without stars, was a held breath.

Odile had lived her whole life under those wards, and she had never realized how much light they made—the faint silver shimmer along the border-stones, the way the air itself seemed to remember the sky even when clouds covered it. Now the shimmer was gone. The markers stood dark as teeth pulled from a jaw. When she looked up, the space where stars should have been wasn't black exactly; it was *absent*, a nothing shaped like everything, and looking at it too long made her eyes water and her thoughts slide sideways.

"Don't," Mirabel said quietly, catching her elbow. "Looking up here is a habit. Out there, it's a wound."

"That's comforting."

"It's not meant to be. It's meant to keep you from walking into a ditch." A pause. "Although we may walk into several ditches regardless. I don't know this country."

"You don't know any country. You told me—you can't remember—"

"I know that." Sharper than intended. Mirabel exhaled. "I know that. But I remember *how* to travel, the way you remember how to swim. Roads want to be followed. That much survives." She glanced back at the guild hall, small and lit and utterly unaware, one window glowing where Sister Henn had apparently broken her own schedule. "Your elders knew the stars were dying. Didn't they."

It wasn't a question, so Odile didn't answer it. She just walked.

The outermost marker stone was hers—had been hers for eleven years, since she was fourteen and the assignment was given to her precisely because no one else wanted the walk. She'd resented it once. Then she'd stopped resenting it, and then she'd stopped noticing she'd stopped, and only now, standing before it for possibly the last time, did she understand that the stone had been her one friend that never forgot her name. Because it didn't have ears. Details.

She pressed her palm flat against it. Cold. Colder than stone should be in late summer.

"The ward-line runs through here," she said. "Or ran. Feel—it's like touching a dead hearth. You can tell a fire was meant to be here."

Mirabel crouched beside the stone and laid two fingers on its surface. For a moment her whole body went rigid, and Odile saw—for the first time, clearly, unmistakably—that the woman was afraid. Not of the stone. Of what the stone told her.

"Yes," Mirabel breathed. "This was ours. This was *mine*, once, I think—I helped raise this line, or my people did, before..." Her hand curled into a fist. "There was a covenant. Two names on it. One from each realm. And the renewal—" She stopped herself, jaw tight. "Fragments. That's all I have. Fragments and bread-smell."

"Then we'll find the rest out there."

"Out there," Mirabel echoed, and rose, and stepped over the ward-line the way you'd step over a sleeping dog you hoped wouldn't wake.

Nothing happened. No alarm, no flash, no thunder of elder fury. The wards were dead; crossing them was like stepping over a threshold in a burned house. Somehow that was worse than any alarm would have been.

Odile stood on the inside. One step away.

Here was the strange arithmetic of her life: eleven years of tending this stone, and she had never once crossed it. Ward-keepers kept. That was the whole of the oath, the whole of the guild, the whole of her—keepers kept, and stayed, and were small, and were safe. Beyond the line was the world her grandmother had described only in warnings: colorless skies, roads that lied, fae and worse, the slow erosion of everything a person was.

Her grandmother had also once hummed a song while kneading bread, a song in no language Odile knew, and smiled at nothing, and Odile had asked her about it and been told *never mind, little wren*. There were a great many never-minds stitched through Odile's childhood, now that she thought about it. She was thinking about it rather a lot lately.

"Odile." Mirabel's voice, from the other side. Patient. Not urging. Just present, the way the stone had been present.

Odile stepped over the line.

* * *

The world outside was wrong in ways she hadn't been warned about, because the warnings had all been about monsters.

The sky was the first thing. Not dark—dark she'd expected—but *drained*, a gray like wet ash stretched from horizon to horizon, giving off a light that came from nowhere and cast no shadows. Without shadows, everything looked flat, painted, a mural of a world rather than a world. Odile kept reaching out to touch things to confirm they had depth. A fencepost. A gate. Mirabel's sleeve, once, before she caught herself.

"Sorry," she muttered.

"For what?"

"Nothing. Habit. You're very—present. It's disorienting."

Mirabel made a sound that was almost a laugh. "You have no idea how long it's been since anyone accused me of that."

The road was the second wrongness. It was a good road, well-made, running straight toward hills that might have been pretty under a real sky. But every mile or so it did something Odile had no word for: it *hesitated*. They'd crest a rise and the road ahead would simply... not be sure of itself, the ruts fading, the edges blurring, a stretch of thirty paces that seemed to be trying to remember whether it had ever gone anywhere. Then it would firm up again, embarrassed, as if nothing had happened.

"It forgets itself," Odile said, the third time it happened.

"Everything out here forgets itself." Mirabel walked with her hood up and her chin down, watching the ground. "That's what the dark does first. Not the grand horrors your guild warns children about. It starts small. A road forgets where it goes. A village forgets yesterday. A woman forgets her own name." Her voice stayed level, but her hand drifted to her collarbone, to the hollow of her throat, where a name would sit if she'd had one. "It eats memory the way fire eats a house—from the softest things inward."

"And us? Will we—"

"We'll be careful. We'll hold on to each other's names. Say mine."

"You don't have one."

"Say the one you gave me, then. The one you call me by."

"Mira," Odile said. "Mira."

"Again."

"Mira. Mira." It felt absurd, chanting it on an empty road under a dead sky, and also it felt like the most important work she'd done in eleven years of important-feeling, actually-empty work. "Does it help? Saying it?"

Mirabel was quiet for a few steps. "I don't know. But when you say it, something in me sits up straighter. Like a dog hearing its name in another room." She glanced sideways. "Yours is Odile. In case you were wondering if anyone was keeping track."

"Someone has to," Odile said lightly, and hated how true it was, and loved, unexpectedly, that Mirabel had said it at all.

* * *

They walked through what had been morning into what should have been noon, and noon never quite arrived. The gray light neither brightened nor dimmed. Odile's stomach informed her it was lunchtime anyway, and they sat on a milestone to eat the rest of the bread, and this was where the exhilaration ambushed her.

She'd expected terror. Terror she had, in plenty—a low constant hum of it, like a hive behind her ribs. But underneath the terror, ridiculous and unstoppable, was *joy*. She was outside. She was beyond the markers. Every rule she'd ever obeyed was a day's walk behind her and shrinking. She was sitting on a milestone in a broken world eating stolen bread with a fugitive fae noblewoman, and no one had summoned her, assigned her, or forgotten to invite her, because there was no one left to do any of those things. The freedom of having nothing to lose, it turned out, felt almost exactly like flying.

"What are you smiling at?" Mirabel asked suspiciously.

"Nothing. Everything. This is terrible and I'm having a wonderful time."

"You're mad."

"Probably! Probably I'm mad. Eleven years of talking to a rock, what did everyone expect." She tore the last of the bread in half and handed the bigger piece over without thinking about it, and watched Mirabel notice that she'd done it, and noticed Mirabel deciding not to comment, and felt something settle warmly in her chest that she decided not to comment on either.

They ate. The road forgot itself again, righted, forgot.

It was Mirabel who saw the smoke first—or not smoke, exactly. A smudge on the gray horizon, like a thumbprint. A village, or what had been one. As they drew closer, Odile saw chimneys, rooftops, a well, fields half-tended—everything structurally intact and everything subtly abandoned, the way a house looks when the people are home but asleep for a hundred years. Except the people weren't asleep. Figures moved in the lanes. Slowly. Aimlessly. A woman stood at her own garden gate opening and closing it, opening and closing it, her face smooth as the drained sky.

A man sat on a stool by the well, and as they passed he looked up at them with sudden desperate focus.

"Do you know," he said, "what happened yesterday?"

Odile opened her mouth.

"No," Mirabel said softly, taking her arm. "Neither do we. Neither does anyone. That's the whole trouble."

The man's face fell, and he turned back to the well, and Odile let herself be led past, her heart going like a drum. Behind them the woman at the gate said, to no one, "Do you know what happened yesterday?"

"They don't remember yesterday," Odile whispered. "Any of them. How far does it go? What happens when it reaches—"

"Further back. Day by day. Unless something stops it." Mirabel's grip on her arm tightened. "Which is why we're going to stop it. The keystones. Whatever's left of the covenant. We find the pieces, we put the sky back together, and this woman gets to close her gate for the last time and go inside."

"How do you know about the keystones? You said you only have fragments."

Another silence, longer this time. When Mirabel answered, she was looking straight ahead.

"Because one of the fragments is a map. And it's getting clearer the further we walk—which frightens me more than the knights do, though you don't know about the knights yet either." She stopped at the edge of the village, where the road bent westward toward hills and a river glinting dully in the shadowless light, and pointed with her chin. "West. There's a river-gate shrine, if the fragment's honest. First keystone. And Odile—"

"Yes?"

"Whatever we meet on that road: whatever offers you comfort, or belonging, or an easy answer—remember this village. Remember the gate." Mirabel's eyes were grave. "Out here, the kindest voice is usually the trap."

They turned west together, two small figures on a road that couldn't remember where it was going, and behind them the woman at the garden gate asked the empty air, once more, gently, hopefully:

"Do you know what happened yesterday?"
