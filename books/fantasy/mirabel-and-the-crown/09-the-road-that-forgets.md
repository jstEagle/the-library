## 9. The Road That Forgets

### Odile

The road out of the valley did not believe in itself.

That was the only way Odile could describe it, walking its length on the second morning beyond the fallen ward-line. The road forgot where it was going. They would crest a hill and find it simply stopped, a ribbon of packed earth ending in grass, as though the ground had lost interest halfway through the thought. Then, fifty paces on, it would begin again, sheepish, as if nothing had happened.

"It's the dark," Mirabel said, when Odile pointed this out for the third time. "The starless dark doesn't eat things all at once. It nibbles. It starts with the small things—roads, rivers, the names of hills—and works up to the large ones."

"Like us?"

"Like anyone." Mirabel walked with her hood up, which Odile had learned was not modesty but camouflage. Without her shadow, Mirabel drew the eye in ways that made villagers nervous even before she spoke. "A road is only a habit the earth has learned. Take away the stars and the earth forgets its habits."

Odile turned that over. In the guild, they had taught that the wards held back *danger*—wild weather, wild beasts, wilder folk. Nobody had ever said the wards held back *forgetting*. She wondered how many other things the guild's lessons had quietly declined to mention. She was beginning to suspect the number was large.

They crested a hill just past midday and saw, at last, proof that other people existed: a village huddled in a river bend, thatch and timber and a curl of honest chimney smoke. Beside the river, a flat-bottomed ferry waited at a rickety dock, and beside the ferry waited a man so old he seemed less built than accumulated, like a cairn that had been adding stones for ninety years.

"Villages mean questions," Odile said. "Questions mean answers. Answers mean—" She stopped, because she had been about to say *trouble*, and she realized she couldn't remember what she'd been afraid of when she left. Not exactly. She remembered the shape of the fear, the way you remember the shape of a coat you've outgrown.

Mirabel glanced at her. "Answers mean the ferryman," she said, and started downhill.

The village had the particular silence of a place where everyone had forgotten what they were about to say. A woman stood in a garden holding a watering can over flowers that were already drowning. A boy chased a dog in a circle, and every time he caught up to it he seemed surprised to see it, laughed, and started again. On a fence post, someone had nailed dozens of small wooden tags, each scratched with a single word: *Tuesday. The brown cow. Mother's face. The song about the miller.* Tokens, Odile realized. Little anchors against the tide.

"Good afternoon," Odile tried, to the woman with the watering can.

The woman looked up with the pleasant blankness of someone waking. "Is it?" she said, genuinely pleased. "Afternoon. I'd wondered."

Odile's chest tightened. She had spent her whole life in a valley that remembered everything—the same nine faces, the same rituals, the same stories told until they wore grooves. She had thought memory was a burden. She had not known it was a floor.

The ferryman watched them come with eyes the color of river water over stones—gray, and moving, and older than they looked. He was mending a net that did not need mending, which Odile suspected was the oldest profession in the world.

"You'll be wanting the crossing," he said. His voice was gravel and patience. "Everyone wants the crossing. Nobody remembers why."

"We're going to the River Gate," Mirabel said. "The drowned shrine, up the fork where the water runs green."

The ferryman's net went still. "Now that," he said, "is a thing I haven't heard in a long while. And I forget most things, so that's saying something." He looked them over, the hooded woman and the girl with the guild-calluses on her hands. "I don't take coin. Coin's just a promise somebody else made. I take stories. One true story each, told to me and meant for me, and I'll carry you to the fork. Lie to me, though—" he smiled, and it was a kind smile, which made it worse, "—and the river will know. The river remembers what I forget. It's a spiteful arrangement, but it keeps me honest."

Odile opened her mouth to offer the story of the night the stars went out, and closed it again. That wasn't hers. That belonged to all of them, the whole guild, and she'd learned enough about ownership lately to be careful with it.

"I'll go first," Mirabel said.

She sat on the dock's edge, and Odile sat beside her, and the ferryman leaned on his oar.

"I had a name," Mirabel said. "I can't tell you what it was, because I don't have it anymore. But before I lost it, I had a garden on a balcony that overlooked a court where the ceiling was the sky, and in that garden I grew a flower that only bloomed at night, in starlight, because I was sentimental and my mother said so." Her voice was even, but Odile saw her hands. "The night the first stars went out, I went to my garden and the flower had already closed. It knew before I did. And I stood there in the dark and thought: *I should do something.* And then I thought: *someone else will.*" She looked at the water. "That's my true story. It's not a good one. But it's true, and I'm still ashamed of it, which is how I know."

The ferryman was quiet a moment. "The river likes shame," he said. "It's honest, at least." He turned to Odile. "And you, keeper's girl."

Odile's mind went, helpfully, blank.

She had stories. She had twenty-two years of them—or she was told she did. But when she reached for one, true and hers and worth the telling, her hands came back empty. There was the time she'd gotten lost in the ash-field as a child—but the edges of that memory felt sanded, smooth, wrong. There was the census, the rituals, Elder Bram's cough. There was tending the outermost marker stone alone, which was less a story than a weather report.

"I'm thinking," she said.

"Take your time," the ferryman said. "The river's patient. It's one of its few virtues."

She thought about the marker stone. She thought about how, every month, she'd walked out to it alone, and how the loneliness had felt like a duty, and the duty had felt like a purpose, and how she had built a whole self out of that circular reasoning. She thought about the ledger she'd found, the page with her name, thick with entries in Bram's cramped hand. *Adjustments.* How many of her stories had been taken before she ever got to keep them?

"I used to tend a stone," she said slowly. "Out past the last house, past the last field. Nobody else wanted the job because it was far and cold and nothing ever happened. I took it when I was twelve and I kept it because—" she frowned, "—I told myself it was because someone had to. But that's not the true story. The true story is that it was the only place in the valley where nobody looked *through* me. Everyone else saw a Fairweather, one of nine, interchangeable. But the stone didn't know that. To the stone I was just the person who came. And I used to talk to it. Tell it things. Silly things. What the frost looked like. What I was afraid of." She laughed, surprised by the ache in it. "I was the most important person in the world to a rock, and that was enough. That was the best I had. And I think—I think someone decided that was a *problem*, that I was too content being small, and they took it away from me. Not the stone. The *liking* of it. I still went, every month, like a clock. I just couldn't remember why it mattered."

The dock was very quiet. The boy and the dog had stopped circling and were watching from the fence.

The ferryman wiped his eye with the back of a rope-scarred hand. "Well," he said gruffly. "That'll do. That'll do entirely. Get in the boat before I start weeping and sink us all."

They boarded. The ferryman poled them out into the current with an ease that made his age seem like a costume he wore to avoid being asked for help. The river ran green-glass calm, and Odile watched the village shrink behind them—the woman still watering her drowned flowers, the boy waving, then stopping, then frowning at his own hand as if unsure what it was for.

"Keeper's girl," the ferryman said, conversationally, as the banks narrowed. "You said someone took your liking of things. You know that for certain, do you?"

"I found a ledger," Odile said. "My page is thick."

"Mm." He poled. "Then here's a free story, since you've paid so well. In this country, memory's the only coin there's ever been. The dark doesn't want your gold. It wants your yesterdays. And folk like you—" he glanced back at her, "—folk who've already been robbed, you come to a river like this and you find out you're carrying less than you thought. It's a hard lesson. The river teaches it gently, if you let it. The dark teaches it the other way."

Ahead, where the water forked, something glinted. Not sunlight—there was no sun worth the name. It was a green shimmer, deep and slow, like the river was thinking about something.

"And that," the ferryman said, and for the first time his voice lost its ease, "is where I leave you. The fork runs to the drowned shrine, and the shrine has a keeper, and the keeper doesn't take stories." He brought the ferry against the near bank and held it steady with the oar. "It takes payment of a more personal kind. Whatever you're carrying that's worth the most to you—that's what it'll ask for. It always knows. Don't bother lying to it. The river remembers what I forget, and it forgets nothing else."

Odile stepped onto the bank. Her legs felt strange, the way they had when she'd first crossed the ward-line—as if the ground were a language she was only beginning to learn.

"Thank you," she said. "For the crossing."

"Thank the stories," the ferryman said. He was already poling away, and then he paused, and looked at her with those gray moving eyes, and said something that followed her up the bank like a stray dog: "Keeper's girl. When it asks you to pay—and it will—don't pay the first thing it asks for. It starts with what you'd miss least. Make it work for the rest."

### Mirabel

Mirabel had forgotten many things, but she remembered how to be afraid, and the fork of the river frightened her.

Not the water itself. Water was honest; it went where it must and said so. What frightened her was the green shimmer, and what lived beneath it, and the fact that she had stood on this bank before—she was almost sure she had, in another life, under another sky—and she could not remember whether she had been welcome.

They walked the near bank toward the shrine, and the green light grew, and Odile chattered, which Mirabel had learned was how Odile managed fear. She catalogued things aloud. *The reeds grow in spirals here, did you notice. The water sounds like it's saying something. Do you think ferryman was a hundred, or a hundred and ten.* Mirabel answered what she could and let the rest wash over her, and found, to her private astonishment, that she did not mind. In her court, talk had been currency and maneuver. Odile's talk was just a person keeping the dark at arm's length with her voice. Mirabel had begun, without noticing, to do the same.

The drowned shrine appeared where the green ran deepest: a ring of standing stones, half-sunk, their tops breaking the water like the backs of sleeping animals. Between them, the river pooled and turned, slow as a held breath. And on the largest stone sat the keeper.

It had a shape, roughly, and the shape was roughly a woman, and the rest was river. Water moved through her the way thought moves through a mind, and where her eyes should have been there were two smooth stones worn round, and when she turned her head to look at them, every ripple in the pool turned with her.

"Two travelers," the keeper said. Her voice came from under the water and from the air at once. "One with no shadow. One with a hole where her childhood should be." The stone eyes rested on Odile. "You want what sank with my shrine. The keystone. It fell into my keeping when the sky went out, and I have kept it well. I keep everything well. That is my whole nature, and it has made me very lonely."

"We'd like to trade for it," Mirabel said.

"Everything is a trade." The keeper's attention slid back to Odile like a current. "I know what I want from each of you. From the shadowless one, a memory of her court—those are rich, those are rare, those are dying out like the stars that lit them."

"No," Mirabel said, before she had decided to say it. Her hand had gone to her chest, to the place where her name used to live. "Ask me something else."

The keeper tilted her head, and the pool tilted with it, water lapping higher up the stones. "Interesting," she said. "You'd rather I take something you have two of than the one thing you have left. Very well. From you, then: a memory of the flower on the balcony. The night-blooming one. I have heard it in your voice since you sat on that dock, and I have wanted it ever since."

Mirabel went still. It was the story she'd told the ferryman—the one true thing she had left whole. She looked at Odile, and Odile looked back, and something passed between them that had no words yet but was clearly being drafted.

"Take it," Mirabel said. "But I tell it to you first. All of it. If you're going to have it, you'll have it properly."

The keeper's stone eyes crinkled, which should not have been possible. "You bargain like a courtier and pay like a farmer's wife. Sit, then. Tell."

Mirabel sat on a half-drowned stone and told the garden entire—the balcony, the star-flower her mother had teased her about, the night it closed early and she had stood in the dark thinking *someone else will*. She told it slowly, and when she finished, the keeper reached out one water-made hand and drew the memory out of her like a thread from a sleeve, and Mirabel felt it go, and felt the shape of the hole it left, and found—to her surprise—that the hole was bearable. The shame stayed. The shame, apparently, was not for sale.

"And you," the keeper said, and now the whole pool went still, every ripple standing at attention. "Keeper's girl. I want a memory of water."

Odile's face did something complicated. "Of water," she repeated.

"You have one. It glows in you like a lamp. Learning-water. A mother in it. I want that one."

"No," Odile said—fast, flat, and then she stopped, because Mirabel could see her doing the arithmetic. *What else do I have?* The ledger with her name on it, thick as a psalter. Twenty-two years with pages missing. The keeper had said the memory glowed, and Mirabel could see it too, now that she looked—could see it the way you see a lit window in a dark house. One window. In a house that should have had dozens.

"Odile," Mirabel said quietly. "How many memories of your mother do you have?"

Odile didn't answer. Her jaw worked. "I have—" She stopped. Started again. "I have this one. I have this one, and I have the *fact* of her, the way you have the fact of a door you've never opened. I have her name. I think I have her name." Her voice went thin. "I assumed there were more. I assumed everyone's mothers were just... background. But you talk about your mother like she's a *person*, and I—"

"Odile." Mirabel took her hand. It was cold. "Look at me. The ferryman said don't pay the first thing it asks for. This is the first thing it's asking for. So we don't pay it. We make it work."

The keeper watched them with the patience of water, which is the patience of everything that will outlast you. "I will have my payment," she said, not unkindly. "But I am not a thief. Choose, keeper's girl. The lamp, or something else. But know this: I chose the lamp because it is the brightest thing you carry. Whatever else you offer will cost you *more* of it. That is how payment works when the purse is light."

The green light pulsed. Somewhere beneath the pool, in the drowned dark of the shrine, the keystone waited—a shard of the old covenant, a piece of the sky that had fallen and been kept well.

Odile stood at the water's edge with one memory of her mother held in her two hands like a lamp, and Mirabel watched her weigh it, and watched her understand, at last and fully, exactly how much had been stolen from her—and how little she had left to lose.

"Ask me again," Odile said to the keeper, and her voice did not shake, which Mirabel would think about for a long time afterward. "Ask me what else you'll take. And I'll tell you what I've decided you can have."

The keeper smiled with her river-face, and the pool began, very slowly, to drain.
