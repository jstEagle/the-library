## 10. Keystone: The River Gate

The river did not appear on any map Odile had ever seen, which was the first strange thing about it. The second was that it appeared at all.

They had been walking for three days through a country that kept forgetting itself—roads that unraveled into meadow halfway to the horizon, signposts whose letters slid off their boards like rain down a window—and then, without warning or announcement, there was water. A broad silver ribbon of it, cutting across the land with the confidence of something that had never once doubted its own existence.

"It's *loud*," Mirabel said, and there was wonder in her voice, which Odile found odd until she remembered: a court beneath an extinguished sky. Perhaps fae courts were quiet places. Perhaps this woman had not heard running water in longer than Odile had been alive.

"It's a river," Odile said. "Rivers are loud."

Mirabel gave her a look—the particular look she reserved for moments when Odile stated the obvious, which was often, and which Mirabel claimed to find charming rather than tiresome, though Odile had her doubts. "In the Hollow Court," Mirabel said, "water moved when we asked it to. It was very polite. This—" she gestured at the churning brown-green expanse, "—this is doing whatever it likes."

"That's what makes it a river."

* * *

Bram's messenger-wisp had found them two nights ago—a trembling ball of guild-light carrying his instructions in his cracked old voice: *The River Gate lies where the water forgets to flow downhill. The keystone sleeps in the drowned shrine beneath. Take care, girl. The river-spirits bargain hard, and they can smell a lie the way you smell bread.*

Odile had read the message three times before the wisp guttered out, and each time the same phrase snagged in her chest like a fishhook: *where the water forgets to flow downhill.* Rivers forgot things now. Everything forgot things now. The dark ate memory first—that was what the ferryman had taught them, trading passage for one true story apiece—and apparently rivers were no exception.

They followed the bank downstream for most of the morning, Odile's boots squelching in mud that seemed genuinely surprised to be stepped on, until the river widened and slowed and began, unmistakably, to move in the wrong direction. Water climbed. It pooled upward over smooth stones and hung there, quivering, like a held breath.

"There," Mirabel said quietly.

Beneath the impossible pool, half-buried in silt, stood a shrine. Odile could see its outline through the water: pale stone pillars, a domed roof furred with weed, and at its heart, on an altar of white rock, something that caught the light and threw it back in pieces. Even drowned, even dimmed, it glowed. Her heart began to hammer against her ribs.

The keystone. Their first one. Three days of colorless sky and roads that forgot themselves and villages full of people who could not remember yesterday—all of it pointed here, to a circle of light under a river that flowed uphill.

"Well," Odile said, and her voice came out smaller than she intended. "I suppose we're going swimming."

* * *

The problem, of course, was that Odile Fairweather could swim perfectly well and had absolutely no idea how she'd learned.

She discovered this standing at the water's edge, testing the cold with one foot, when Mirabel asked the reasonable question: "Can you actually swim?"

"Of course," Odile said automatically. And then stopped. Because she could feel the knowledge sitting in her body—her arms remembered the pull of water, her lungs remembered the rhythm of breath—but when she reached for the *memory* of it, the way you reach for a name or a face, her hand closed on nothing. There was a shape where the memory should have been. An absence with edges.

"I learned somewhere," she said slowly. "I must have. I just don't..." She trailed off. She had grown used to these small holes in herself since reading the ledger of forgetting—the gaps where whole seasons of her childhood should have been, smoothed over so neatly she'd never noticed them until she went looking. But this one hurt differently. This one had water in it.

"The ledger," Mirabel said gently. "Your page was thick."

"My page was thick." Odile stared at the water. Somewhere in the guild archive, in Bram's careful cramped handwriting, there was a line item that explained why she couldn't remember learning the most basic skill of her own body. *Adjustment: seasonal. Routine.* As if taking a child's memories were the same as pruning a hedge.

"We don't have to go in today," Mirabel offered. "We could camp. Rest. I could teach you to swim properly, if you like—fae children learn in still pools, someone holds your hands—"

"I'm not a child, and I'm not afraid of the water." Odile heard how sharp her voice came out and winced. "Sorry. I'm not—it's not the swimming I mind. It's that something was taken from me and I didn't even know enough to miss it."

Mirabel was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "I know exactly what that's like. My name was taken, and I only know it's gone because everyone else has one." She smiled, small and crooked. "At least yours came with a body that remembers. That's more than I got."

It was such a Mirabel thing to say—grief worn lightly, like a coat carried over one arm—and it loosened something in Odile's chest. She sat down on the bank and pulled off her boots.

"Fine," she said. "But if I drown, you're explaining it to the river-spirit yourself, and I promise you she bargains harder than I do."

* * *

The water was colder than it had any right to be, given the season, and it pushed back against her like a living thing deciding whether to permit her entry. But her body knew what to do. The moment she committed—arms sweeping, legs kicking, face turned up toward a sky that offered no sun to steer by—she moved through the current as though she'd been born in it. Whatever had been cut from her mind, her muscles had kept the truth.

Mirabel swam beside her, and Odile tried not to stare, but it was difficult. The woman moved like the water had been waiting for her personally. No splashing, no effort—she simply *arrived* at places, the way light arrives through a window.

"You're showing off," Odile called.

"I am demonstrating cultural heritage," Mirabel replied with tremendous dignity, and then immediately capsized, which served her right.

The shrine's entrance was an arched doorway in the shrine's side, half-collapsed, its lintel carved with figures Odile couldn't quite make out through the murk—people, maybe, kneeling, arms raised toward something above them. Stars, perhaps. Whoever built this place had been praying to the same sky that had gone dark.

She surfaced inside the dome, in a pocket of air trapped beneath the ancient ceiling, and hauled herself onto the altar's edge, dripping and gasping. The keystone sat three feet away.

Up close it was beautiful in a way that made her throat ache. A shard of crystal the size of a loaf of bread, shot through with veins of light that pulsed slow and steady, like breathing. Like a heartbeat. When Odile leaned close, she felt it hum against her sternum—the same note the valley wards had sung, back before they failed. Back before everything failed.

"Don't touch it yet," Mirabel said from the doorway, treading water. Her voice had changed. Gone careful. "Look around first. Shrines like this are never empty."

"She's right," said a voice from everywhere and nowhere. "I never am."

* * *

The river-spirit rose out of the flooded floor of the shrine the way fog rises off a pond at dawn—slowly, and all at once, and with a great deal of personal presence. She was made of water in the way that Mirabel was made of starlight: not literally, but essentially, the way a story is made of words. Her hair was current. Her eyes were deep pools with light moving far down in them. When she spoke, droplets fell from her lips and each one landed with the sound of a struck bell.

"A ward-keeper's daughter," the spirit said, looking at Odile, and Odile's stomach dropped, because she had told no one that. Had she? Could the spirit see it somehow, written on her the way the ledger was written in ink? "Come to take what my river guards. Come to take the Gate's key."

"We've come to restore it," Odile said. "To reforge the covenant. The stars went out. We're trying to bring them back."

"Mm." The spirit drifted closer, and the water in the shrine stirred with her, eddying around Odile's ankles where she sat. "Everyone who comes to me says some version of that. *Restore. Renew. Return.* Words are cheap. The river does not accept payment in words." She circled once, unhurried, and Odile noticed that wherever the spirit passed, the water forgot which way it had been flowing. "The ferryman told you, I think. Memory is currency. Here, below, it is the *only* currency. You wish to carry my keystone out of my keeping? Pay me a memory of water."

"A memory of water," Odile repeated.

"One that is truly yours. Not borrowed, not witnessed secondhand, not invented. The river knows the difference. It always knows." The spirit's bell-voice softened. "And it must matter to you. A memory of water that means nothing is counterfeit coin. I will taste it, weigh it, and if it rings false, you will leave here with nothing but wet clothes and my considerable displeasure."

Odile looked at Mirabel. Mirabel looked back, and her expression was sympathetic and grim in equal measure, the expression of someone who had already paid prices Odile couldn't imagine and knew there was no way around this one.

"What happens to the memory?" Odile asked. "If I pay it. Do I get it back someday? When the covenant's restored?"

The spirit was silent long enough that a drop of water fell somewhere in the dark and rang like a funeral bell. "No," she said simply. "Paid memories become part of the river. They are not destroyed—they are *kept*. I will hold yours as long as the river runs. But it will no longer be yours. You will stand on the bank someday and watch the water and feel nothing in particular." She tilted her head, currents shifting in her hair. "That is the price. That is always the price. Nothing worth guarding is guarded for free, little keeper. Ask your elders. Oh—" a ripple that might have been a smile, "—they cannot answer. Can they."

* * *

Odile sat on the altar for a long time after that, turning the offer over, examining it from every side the way she'd once examined the ledger entries—looking for the trick, the loophole, the hidden clause.

There wasn't one. There never was. That was the whole cruelty of it.

A memory of water. She closed her eyes and searched, and the search itself was instructive, because it showed her exactly how much had been taken. There was the lake behind the guildhall—she remembered its existence, its gray-green surface, the way ice formed at the edges in winter—but the remembering was flat, like describing a painting of a thing rather than the thing. There was rain on the marker stones during her solitary rounds, and that memory lived, warm and real, but it was recent, and it was hers alone, and it felt too small. Too thin a coin for a keystone.

And then, underneath all of it, there was the absence again. The shaped hole. The place where learning-to-swim should have been.

Her body remembered the water. Which meant her body had been *in* water, young, learning. And there was only one person who would have taught her. Guild children learned everything from guild members; the Fairweather guild had nine members and had always had roughly nine members, aging together like trees in a copse. Someone had held her hands in the lake behind the guildhall. Someone had let go, gradually, and stayed close, and shouted encouragement, and been proud.

Her mother. It would have been her mother. Odile had almost no memories of her mother left at all—the ledger had seen to that, page after thick page—and the few that remained were fragments: a laugh heard through a wall. The smell of soap and lamp oil. Hands.

Hands holding hers in cold green water. *Kick, little fish. I've got you. Kick.*

She hadn't known she had even that much. She hadn't known until this moment, this shrine, this price—and now that she knew, she understood the trap perfectly. The spirit wanted a memory that mattered. The only memory of water that mattered was one she'd discovered thirty seconds ago, one she'd never gotten to keep long enough to grow used to. Grief, arriving precisely on schedule, precisely where it could be spent.

"They designed it this way," she said aloud. Her voice sounded strange in the dome, flattened by water and stone. "Bram and the others. They took everything that mattered, and now the world charges me in memories, and the only ones I have left to pay with are the ones I'm finding in the gaps. It's like being robbed and then taxed on what's left."

"Yes," the spirit said, without sympathy and without apology. "And?"

Odile opened her eyes. "And I'll pay it."

"Odile—" Mirabel started.

"No. Listen." She turned to face the fae woman in the doorway, water lapping at her shoulders. "Every day out here, the dark eats a little more of everyone we meet. That village that couldn't remember yesterday. The city ahead, if the wisp's map is true, where people wear tokens because they've lost their names. If we fail—if the thirteenth moon turns and the covenant stays broken—there won't be anyone left to remember anything, including my mother. At least this way, she becomes part of the river. Rivers run forever. That's more than the dark leaves you." She swallowed. "And besides. She taught me to swim so I wouldn't drown. I think she'd rather the lesson got used."

Mirabel looked at her for a long moment. Something moved behind her eyes—recognition, maybe, or grief recognizing grief. "You sound like a signatory," she said finally. "Someone willing to pay. That's rarer than you'd think. In my court, everyone wanted the covenant's protection and no one wanted its price. That's why it died. Not from enemies. From *arithmetic*."

* * *

The paying was simple, which made it worse.

The spirit drifted close and extended one hand of living water, palm up. "Think of it," she said. "All of it. Hold nothing back—a partial payment bounces, like a bad coin. Think of the water. The cold. The hands. Give it to me."

So Odile thought of it. Deliberately, fully, for the first time since discovering it existed: the lake behind the guildhall, gray-green under a summer sky that still had stars waiting in it somewhere. The shock of cold water closing over her shoulders. Her mother's hands under hers, steady, certain, letting go a little more each day. *Kick, little fish. I've got you.* The moment she'd swum her first length alone and turned around and her mother was standing waist-deep, laughing, so proud it seemed to fill the whole lake. The way the water had felt alive that day—not frightening, not indifferent, but *glad*, as if it had been waiting for her too.

She held it all, every detail, and then she let it go, the way you release a bird you've been keeping, and the spirit closed her hand around it, and Odile felt it leave.

It didn't hurt. That was the terrible part. It felt like exhaling. Like setting down something heavy she'd forgotten she was carrying. For one clear instant she was aware of the whole shape of the memory—its weight, its warmth, its edges—and then the awareness drained away downstream, and where it had been there was a smooth, quiet nothing, and she could not remember what she had just lost, only that she had just lost something, and that her eyes were wet, and that the water around her tasted of salt, which rivers should not taste of.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, that's—"

"Gone," the spirit said, not unkindly. "And kept. I will hold her hands in mine as long as the river runs, little keeper. Every current in me will remember your mother teaching you to kick." The bell-voice gentled further. "That is not nothing. Paid memories are not dead things here. They are *weather*. Yours will be a warm one."

Then she stepped aside—or dissolved aside, or became aside—and the keystone lay bare on the altar between them, pulsing its slow heartbeat of light.

"Take it," the spirit said. "And take a warning with it, free of charge, because I liked your mother's hands, whoever she was. The next gate is harder than mine. The Mountain Tooth does not want coins. It wants burdens. Go prepared to carry what isn't yours." Her pools-of-light eyes flicked to Mirabel, lingering, and something passed across her face like a shadow of a fish. "Both of you. Especially you, star-born. The mountain remembers what you ran from."

Mirabel went very still in the water.

Odile lifted the keystone. It was lighter than it looked, and warm, and when her fingers closed around it the hum against her sternum resolved into something almost like words—almost like a name being spoken very far away. The shrine's veins of light brightened. Above them, through the flooded entrance, the uphill-flowing river shivered, and for one moment—one only—it flowed the right way, downhill, toward the sea, like a river remembering its manners.

Then it settled back into strangeness, and the spirit began to sink, folding downward into the flooded floor like a curtain drawn shut.

"Wait," Odile called. "What was she like? My—I paid a memory of someone. What was she—"

But the shrine was only water and stone and weed-furred pillars, and the bell-voice, fading, answered from everywhere:

"Warm. She was warm. Now go, before the dark notices what you're carrying. It has been watching the gates, little keeper. It watches them all."

* * *

They swam out into the gray afternoon light and stood dripping on the bank, the keystone wrapped in Odile's spare cloak and slung across her back, humming faintly against her spine like a second heartbeat.

Mirabel wrung out her hair and studied Odile sideways, the way she did when she was deciding whether to say a thing. "You're crying," she observed.

"I know."

"Do you know why?"

Odile considered the question honestly. There was a hollow feeling under her ribs, a grief-shaped room with the furniture removed. She knew something had been in it. She could see the pale rectangles on the walls where pictures had hung. "Something about my mother," she said. "Water. I think she taught me to swim." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand and managed something that was mostly a smile. "Which explains why I float like a cork and have no idea why."

Mirabel laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her—and then sobered, and did something she had never done before. She reached over and took Odile's hand. Her fingers were cool and sure, and they held on.

"When this is over," Mirabel said, "when the stars are back and the covenant holds—we're going to find a lake somewhere, you and I. And I'm going to teach you to swim again. Properly. With someone holding your hands." Her jaw set, stubborn as bedrock. "Not everything taken has to stay taken. Some things can be given twice."

Odile couldn't speak for a moment. She nodded instead, and squeezed the cool fingers, and they stood together on the bank of a river that flowed uphill, under a sky with no stars, holding hands like two people who had decided, against all available evidence, that the future was a place worth walking to.

Then Mirabel stiffened. Her head turned sharply east, toward the moorland road they'd come by, and all the warmth went out of her face at once.

"Odile," she said quietly. "Get behind me. *Now.*"

Out on the gray horizon, at the exact spot where the road disappeared into moor, something gleamed. Armor. Moving fast. And riding at its head, a banner Odile had never seen but Mirabel clearly had—a banner that made the fugitive fae noblewoman go pale as bone.

"Hollow Court knights," Mirabel breathed. "They've found us."
