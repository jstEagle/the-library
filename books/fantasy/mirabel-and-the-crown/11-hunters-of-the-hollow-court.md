## 11. Hunters of the Hollow Court

I had forgotten, in my long years of forgetting, what it felt like to be hunted.

No—that was a lie, and I knew it even as I thought it. Forgetting was not the same as losing. The body keeps its own ledger. My body remembered being hunted the way it remembered hunger: with a cold bloom in the belly and every hair on my arms standing to attention like soldiers who had never been told the war ended.

"They've found us," I said, and heard how thin my voice came out.

Odile did not get behind me. Of course she didn't. In the two weeks since I had woken half-dead in her ash-field, I had learned perhaps three things about Odile Fairweather with any certainty, and the first was that when someone said *get behind me*, she would instead step *beside* you, squinting at the danger as though it were an interesting cloud formation she intended to sketch later.

"That's armor," she said now, shielding her eyes with one hand. "Real armor. Not like the guild's ceremonial stuff. It moves."

"It moves because they are riding toward us at a gallop."

"Yes, that would do it." She paused. "How many?"

I counted without wanting to. Six riders, and the banner-bearer behind them, and something else—something that walked rather than rode, at the rear, wrapped in gray. My stomach dropped through the ferryman's boat, through the river, through the drowned shrine below it, and kept going.

"Seven," I said. "And a tethered one."

"A what?"

"You'll see." I caught her elbow. "Odile. Listen to me. Whatever happens—if they take me, if they—" I made myself say it. "If they take me, you run back across the moor with the keystone and you don't look back. Swear it."

"I don't make oaths I intend to break," she said, which was, I was learning, her way of refusing things. "Also, you're gripping my arm hard enough to leave marks, so perhaps we could discuss this while moving?"

We ran.

The village of yesterday-less people parted around us without seeing us; that was the mercy of a place where no one could hold a memory longer than a night—we were already forgotten before we reached the well. Behind us, hoofbeats struck the packed earth of the road, and each strike rang strangely, too clean, too bright, like bells struck by someone who hated music.

I knew that sound. I had grown up beneath it. The knights of the Hollow Court shod their horses in silver for exactly this reason: so that everyone would hear them coming and have time to be afraid. My mother had called it *cruel courtesy*. The Court called it *honor*.

The Court called many cruel things honor.

* * *

We made the treeline at the edge of the orchard as the first rider drew level with the well. Through the branches I watched him rein in—a tall figure in gray-lacquered plate, visor down, the hollow sigil of the Court stamped on his breast: a circle with its center eaten away. He sat motionless a moment, head tilted, listening the way the fae listen—not with ears but with whatever it is that hears names.

Then he spoke, and my heart stopped, restarted badly, and stopped again.

"The trail is fresh," he said. His voice was low and even and terribly familiar. "She passed through here. The villagers will have seen her."

A second rider laughed softly. "Seen her, maybe. Remembered her? Unlikely. This place is already mostly dark. Ask them anything and they'll give you yesterday's answer."

"Ask anyway. The Lady wants certainty."

The Lady. I pressed my back against an apple tree and breathed through my mouth so the sound of my breathing wouldn't carry. *The Lady.* So she had a name among them now. When I fled the court, the thing in the sky had been only the Devourer, the unmaking, the reason the stars went out one by one like candles pinched by a careful hand. Apparently in my absence it had acquired manners.

Odile leaned close, her lips nearly touching my ear. "You know that one," she whispered. "The first rider. Don't tell me you don't—I've watched you lie for two weeks and your face does a thing."

"My face does not do a *thing*."

"Your face is doing it right now."

I closed my eyes. "His name is Serel," I said. "Serel of the Ninth Lance. He was—" What was he? What word survived the dark? "He was my friend," I said finally, because *more* was a door I couldn't open in an orchard with death trotting past.

"Your friend is hunting you."

"Yes."

"And he doesn't remember you."

"No."

Odile was quiet a moment. Then, in the gentle, maddening tone she used for stating the obvious to elders: "That seems like the worst thing I've ever heard."

"It is at least in the top five."

* * *

They fanned out through the village, and I learned what a tethered one was, and I wished I hadn't.

They brought it to the well square—the gray-wrapped thing from the rear—and unwound its bindings, and underneath there was no monster, which was somehow worse. There was a person. An old woman, fae by the ears, human by the stoop, her eyes white as boiled milk. A *hollowed* one—one the Devourer had emptied and the Court had leashed, because an empty mind makes a fine instrument. It remembers nothing, so it cannot be lied to. It feels nothing, so it cannot be bribed. It simply *finds*, the way water finds the sea.

It turned its blind face slowly, reading the residue of memory the way I might read tracks in snow. I watched it pause over the well. Over the ferryman's landing. Over the spot where Odile and I had stood not an hour past, trading our true stories for passage.

Then it turned, unhurried, toward the orchard.

"Move," I breathed.

We moved. We crashed downslope through bramble and old leaf-rot, Odile swearing in the inventive, apologetic manner of someone raised around nine disapproving elders, and behind us the silver-shod hooves began to ring.

"They're coming!" she shouted.

"I *noticed!*"

"There's a stream—I saw it from the ridge—it cuts through a gully—"

"Water breaks a tracker's line. Good. Odile, that's genuinely good thinking—"

"Don't patronize me while we're fleeing for our lives, it's very confusing!"

We hit the gully at a slide, went into the cold water hip-deep, and scrambled up the far bank into a stand of birches just as the first knight reached the gully's rim above us. For one suspended moment I looked up through the white trunks and saw him silhouetted against the colorless sky, visor down, utterly still.

Then Serel's voice came down through the trees, calm as ever.

"She went downstream. Split. Two of you follow the water. The rest, with me—we'll cut the valley off at the ford."

He knew. Even without remembering me, some part of him knew how I'd move. That was the cruelest arithmetic of it: the dark had taken his memories of me, but not his knowledge of me. Ten years of loving someone leaves grooves deeper than the self. He was tracking me with the shape of his own erased devotion and never once wondering why his chest ached when he said my direction aloud.

We ran until the light failed, which in a starless world comes early and without apology.

* * *

We made camp in a hollow beneath a fallen oak, no fire, sharing a blanket because the nights outside the wards bit differently than the nights within—sharper, hungrier, as if the dark itself were checking pockets. Odile divided the last of the ferryman's bread with scrupulous fairness, giving herself the smaller half and pretending not to notice me noticing.

"So," she said, after a while. "Serel."

"What about him."

"When you said *friend*, you did the face again."

"I don't have a face that does things."

"You absolutely do. It's a very expressive face for someone who claims to be a fugitive." She chewed thoughtfully. "My grandmother always said the guild records everything whether or not anyone writes it down. Faces. Hands. She said hands remember what heads are made to forget."

I looked at my own hands then. Long-fingered, court-pale still, though two weeks of road dirt had begun to argue with them. They were trembling slightly. I put them under the blanket where Odile couldn't log the evidence.

"He loved me," I said. The words came out flat, surprising me with their steadiness. "Or I think he did. Neither of us ever said it in words—the Court doesn't approve of words for soft things—but it was understood. Understood things are supposed to be safer than spoken ones. Nothing to erase, see? Nothing to hold against you." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "The dark took the understanding anyway. Took all of it. And now he rides for the thing that unmade him, wearing its sigil on his chest, and he doesn't even know what he lost. He can't mourn it. You can't mourn something you don't remember existing."

Odile was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had gone careful, the way it did when she was handling something breakable—usually me.

"That happened to me too," she said. "Not exactly. But the ledger—the guild's ledger—there were pages about me. Things taken out. I don't know what. I can't miss what I don't remember either." She pulled her knees up. "Maybe that's why I understand. Why I believed you about the covenant when the elders wouldn't. We're both walking around with holes shaped like people."

"Holes shaped like people," I repeated. "Yes. That's precisely it." I stared into the black where the fire wasn't. "Do you know what I'm afraid of, Odile? Not the knights. Not the Lady. I'm afraid that when this ends—*if* it ends—I'll stand in front of Serel with the stars burning overhead and his mind restored, and he'll look at me, and there will be nothing there. Because love isn't stored whole. It's stored in ten thousand small moments, and most of those are gone forever. I can give him back his *memory* of me. I can't give him back the ten years."

The silence stretched. Somewhere far off, a nightbird sang three notes and forgot the fourth.

"Then you make new ones," Odile said simply. "That's what my journal is for. You can't recover what's gone, but—" she shrugged, awkward, earnest, infuriatingly hopeful— "apparently you can witness new things into being real. The ferryman said that. A true story freely given. Maybe love works the same way. Maybe it can be *made* again, not just found."

"You have an absurd amount of faith in stories."

"I'm training to be a ward-keeper. Stories are basically wards made of words." She lay back against the oak root. "Sleep, Mirabel. I'll take first watch."

"You don't know how to keep watch."

"How hard can it be? Look at things. Notice them. It's the family business."

Despite everything—despite the silver hoofbeats still ringing somewhere in my skull—I smiled, and slept.

* * *

I woke to Odile's hand clamped over my mouth.

Her eyes were enormous in the dark. She pointed.

At the lip of our hollow, silhouetted against the faint gray of pre-dawn, stood a knight in hollow-sigiled plate. Visor down. Sword drawn but lowered. Perfectly, terribly still.

I had time for exactly one coherent thought—*they waited for us to sleep, of course they waited, the Court never fights fair, the Court calls that honor too*—and then the knight reached up and lifted his visor.

Serel's face.

Older than I remembered. Thinner. With a pallor I recognized now, having crossed the starless lands myself—the pallor of someone being slowly eaten from the inside. But the same face. The same grey eyes that had once, across a crowded court, found mine first in every room.

He looked at me, and I watched him look, and there was nothing in his gaze. No recognition. No warmth. No hatred, even—hatred would have been something. He regarded me the way one regards a locked door: an obstacle with an interesting mechanism.

"Lady Mirabel of the Autumn Line," he said. "Surrender the keystones and come willingly to the Court, and your companion will not be harmed."

*Lady Mirabel of the Autumn Line.* My title, recited perfectly. My name, spoken like an inventory entry. Somewhere inside me something cracked along an old fault line.

"Do you know who I am?" I asked him. My voice shook. I let it. Let him hear it. Some animal part of me hoped the sound itself might reach whatever grooves the dark hadn't quite filled.

"You are the fugitive signatory," Serel said. "You broke faith with the Court. You fled as the sky failed. These facts are known to me."

"That's *what* I am. Do you know *who*?"

A pause. The smallest pause. And in it I saw—or dreamed I saw, and I have wondered since which it was—something flicker behind his eyes. A bird startled in an empty hall.

"...I know my orders," he said at last, and his voice had changed by a hair's breadth, tightened by a hair's breadth, and if I had loved him less I would never have noticed. "Come willingly, lady. Or we take you. The tethered one has your scent now. There is nowhere left between here and the mountain that we cannot follow."

Behind me, Odile shifted her weight, slow and deliberate, and I felt rather than saw her hand creep toward the keystone bag. Toward the river-gate stone that hummed faintly against her hip like a sleeping heart.

And Serel's gaze flicked—to her, to the bag, back to me—and he frowned, a small crease between his brows, the frown of a man trying to recall a word that sits on the tip of the tongue.

"Why," he said slowly, "does the human smell of *river*?"

Because, my love. Because she paid a shrine a memory of water to save my life. Because she chose me before she knew what I was. Because—

"Mirabel," Odile whispered, barely a breath. "On three. The gully. *Run.*"

"One," she said.

Serel's sword came up.

"Two."

Somewhere beyond the ridge, six more sets of silver-shod hooves began to ring.

"Three."
