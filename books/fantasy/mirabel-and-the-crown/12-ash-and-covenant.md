## 12. Ash and Covenant

The moor smelled of rain that had given up halfway down and simply hung in the air instead, a fine grey mist that beaded on Odile's eyelashes and made the whole world feel like it was being viewed through a breath-fogged window. She had never seen a moor before three days ago. She was beginning to suspect she would dream about it for the rest of her life, whatever length that turned out to be.

"Your boots are wrong," Mirabel said, not looking back.

"My boots are *fine*."

"They squelch in a rhythm. Left, left-right, left. Anything hunting us by sound will know exactly where you are and also that you have no sense of occasion."

Odile looked down at her boots—good guild-issue leather, oiled every season per the maintenance schedule—and felt a flash of entirely unjustified loyalty to them. "The maintenance manual says—"

"The maintenance manual," Mirabel said, "was written by people who thought wards were a chore list." She said it without venom now, which Odile had learned was new. In the first days after the escape, every mention of the guild had come out of her like a splinter. Now it came out like weather: present, unavoidable, occasionally damp.

They crested a rise of blackish heather, and Odile stopped.

Below them the moor opened into a shallow bowl, and in the bowl's center stood a waystone—a leaning finger of granite older than anything in the valley, crusted with lichen and, faintly, with light. Not starlight; there was no such thing anymore. It was its own small stubborn glow, the kind of light that seemed embarrassed to be noticed.

"That's one of the old covenant markers," Mirabel said quietly. "I haven't seen one standing since—" She didn't finish. She'd developed a habit of not finishing sentences about the past, the way other people developed tics.

"It's beautiful," Odile said, and meant it, and then heard what Mirabel heard: the squelch behind them. Not hers.

Left, left-right, left. Then again, from the right flank. And again, from somewhere ahead, which should have been impossible, unless—

"Ambush," Mirabel said, with the flat calm of someone reading a grocery list. "*Down.*"

Odile went down into the heather as the world tore open.

* * *

There were six of them, maybe seven—the mist made counting a courtesy rather than a fact. They came out of the grey like figures drawn in charcoal, tall and armored in plates the color of drowned things, and each of them carried not a sword but a ring of small iron fetters linked on a chain, and the fetters *sang*. That was the worst part. They sang a thin, sweet note that slid into the ear and started unlatching things—Odile felt it tug at the memory of her mother's kitchen, at the smell of bread, at the sound of Elder Wren laughing at her own terrible jokes—and she clapped her hands over her ears and knew it wouldn't help because the song wasn't coming through her ears.

"Hollow Court knights," Mirabel breathed beside her. "Memory-takers. They don't kill you, Odile. They empty you and leave you walking." Her voice did something Odile had never heard in it before. It shook. "I hoped they wouldn't come this far west."

"You *hoped*?"

"I'm allowed one hope per week. I spent Tuesday's on dry socks."

A knight broke from the mist ten feet away, and Odile saw its face—or the place where a face should have been. There was a helm, and inside the helm there was a smooth dark nothing, like a pond at night, and reflected in that nothing she saw herself, small and muddy and utterly outmatched.

The fetters swung toward her.

And Mirabel stood up.

Later, Odile would try to describe it and fail, and try again, and fail better. What she saw first was Mirabel's shadow—which she didn't have, hadn't had since the ash-field, a fact Odile had filed away as one more grief among many—except that now, for one moment, she *did* have one, and it was enormous. It poured out across the moor like spilled ink, covering the heather, climbing the waystone, swallowing the knights' own shadows whole. What she saw second was fire—but not fire the way fires worked. This flame burned *upward through things*, from the inside out, silver-white and utterly silent, and where it touched the iron fetters they glowed and cracked and the songs cut off mid-note like throats closed.

The nearest knight dropped its chain as if it had been holding a snake. The memory it had been carrying—the stolen sweetness of someone's childhood, still humming inside the iron—came loose into the air and scattered like pollen.

"No," said the knight. Its voice was a hollow in the shape of a voice. "No—you were *gone*—"

"I was," Mirabel agreed. She was swaying now, the great shadow flickering like a lamp low on oil. "Everyone keeps assuming gone means finished. It's a very common mistake. I've made it myself."

She raised one hand, and the silver fire leapt—chain to chain, knight to knight, six small suns blooming in the mist—and every fetter burst, and every stolen memory went home or went free, Odile couldn't tell which, and the knights staggered back clutching their helms as though something inside them had been struck. One of them fell to its knees in the heather. Another simply turned and walked into the mist, fast, the way you walk away from a door you've decided never to open again.

Then it was over, and the mist was just mist, and Mirabel folded up like a chair.

Odile caught her before the ground could. She was lighter than she should have been—all of her was, as if she'd been slowly giving pieces of herself away for years—and she was cold, cold as the waystone looked, and her eyes when they opened were unfocused and frightened.

"Odile." A pause. "That is your name. Odile Fairweather. Say it back so I know I still keep things."

"Odile Fairweather," Odile said, and her voice cracked in the middle of *Fairweather*, which was embarrassing, and she pressed on anyway. "You're burning yourself up. Whatever that was—it costs too much. Don't ever do it again."

"Noted," Mirabel murmured. "Filed under requests I intend to ignore."

* * *

Odile built a fire under the lee of the waystone, because the waystone's glow kept the mist at a polite distance and because her hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking. She made tea from the last of the dried bramble-leaf, which tasted like punishment, and wrapped Mirabel's hands around the cup and held them there until they stopped trembling.

For a long time neither of them spoke. The moor ticked and settled around them. Somewhere far off, something that might have been a nightbird gave a call that no nightbird had any business making, and then thought better of it.

"You almost died," Odile said finally. "To save me. Or to save—" she gestured vaguely at the waystone, the mission, the whole doomed errand of two keystones still to find and thirteen moons bleeding away. "Which?"

Mirabel looked into her cup. Steam curled up past her face, and for once she let a silence sit without decorating it.

"When I fled my court," she said, "the stars were already going out. Not all at once—that would have been honest. One constellation at a time. A slow theft, dressed up as astronomy. My court debated it for a hundred years. Committees, Odile. We formed committees about the death of the sky." A ghost of a smile. "I was the signatory. Of the covenant your guild guarded. The fae hand on the old pact—I signed it when I was young and the ink was still wet on the world, and when the stars began dying I told myself someone else would renew it. Someone braver. Someone whose job it was." She turned the cup. "Then I ran. And the running took my name, because names are the first thing the dark eats, and I let it take mine as penance. Which was cowardly even as penance goes."

Odile sat with that. The fire popped. The waystone glowed its modest, embarrassed glow.

"Elder Bram said 'she signed it too,'" Odile said. "In the archive corridor. I heard him. He knew who you were the moment he saw you, and he locked you in a cage instead of telling us the sky was dying on his watch."

"Yes."

"He let nine people dust marker stones for thirty years thinking it was *chores*."

"Yes," Mirabel said again, softer. "And I let an entire court believe the problem was being handled. We are both very good at letting people believe things, you and I. It's practically our shared heraldry."

"Don't," Odile said. "Don't put us in the same sentence like that. You ran from a dying heaven. Bram lied to children. Those aren't—"

"And you," Mirabel said, gently, terribly, "opened the ledger, read your own page, and stayed another full day before you came for me. Everyone fails at the speed of their fear, Odile. Yours was just faster than most."

Odile wanted to argue. She opened her mouth, found nothing in it but the true shape of that day—the ledger's thick page under her fingers, her own name written down and crossed out and written down again like a sum that wouldn't balance—and closed it again.

The fire burned down a little. She fed it a twist of heather.

"Why did you burn the fetters?" she asked at last. "You said it costs you. You've been rationing yourself like the last loaf in a famine since I met you. Why spend it on six knights who'd have emptied me and walked on?"

"Because of what they carry." Mirabel set down the cup. "Memory-fetters don't hold memories like jars. They hold them like *hostages*. Every chain those knights swing has someone's childhood screaming inside it. When I break one, the memory goes free—back to its owner if the owner lives, out into the world if they don't. Either way, it stops being a weapon." She looked at her own hands, the firelight moving over them. "I spent a hundred years watching my court hoard beauty while the sky went out. I am not going to spend whatever I have left watching stolen things be used as chains. Even if the spending kills me. *Especially* then. It's the only currency I have that the dark can't counterfeit."

"That's a terrible plan," Odile said. "That's the worst plan I've ever heard, and I grew up with Elder Petch, who once tried to stop a flood by yelling at it."

"Yelling?" Mirabel's mouth curved despite itself. "At water?"

"He was very firm."

For a moment they were both quiet, and the quiet was different than it had been—an hour ago it had been two travelers sharing a fire out of necessity, and now it was something with weight and warmth, something that had been forged in the last hour the way iron is forged, badly and hotly and permanently.

"Thank you," Odile said. "For the fire. For telling me the truth. For—for letting me see it cost you. Everyone in the guild pretended the rituals cost nothing. Pretended everything cost nothing. It's the loneliest kind of lie."

Mirabel reached over and, with great ceremony, tucked a sprig of heather behind Odile's ear, where it immediately began shedding tiny purple petals down her collar.

"There," she said. "Now you look like a hedge witch who lost a fight with a shrub. Freely given. First entry in what I hope will be a long account between us."

"I look ridiculous."

"You look like someone worth following into a hollowed-out fae court," Mirabel said, "which is the highest compliment available in my current circumstances."

Odile laughed—actually laughed, out here on the end of the world—and the laugh surprised her so much she nearly choked on bramble-leaf tea.

It was Mirabel who went still first. Her head tilted, the way a deer's does, and Odile followed her gaze to the waystone.

The stone's modest glow had changed. It pulsed now—slow, deliberate, like a heartbeat remembering itself—and as they watched, lines woke along its surface, old carved script filling with pale light, and the light gathered at the stone's tip and pointed.

Not upward. East. Toward the mountains, where according to Bram's half-legible notes the second keystone slept in a place called the Mountain Tooth.

But that wasn't what made Odile's stomach drop.

What made her stomach drop was the mist beyond the stone, where the light fell—and the shapes standing in it. Three of them. Tall. Armored in the color of drowned things. Not attacking. Waiting.

And the foremost of them lifted its helm—not off, never off, but *up*, a few inches, a courtesy—and from beneath it came a voice Odile recognized, because she had heard it shouting orders through the ambush, and it was rougher now, and slower, and it said:

"*Mirabel.*"

A pause, long enough for the fire to pop twice.

"You don't remember me," the voice said. "But I remember everything. Come and see what they made me forget."
