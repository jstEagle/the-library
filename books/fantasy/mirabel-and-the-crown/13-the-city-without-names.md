## 13. The City Without Names

The city announced itself first by smell—woodsmoke and wet stone and something faintly sweet, like bread left too long near honey—and then by the lights. Not stars. There were no stars anywhere in the sky, not here, not anywhere they had walked since the valley's wards fell. But lanterns, hundreds of them, strung between crooked rooftops like a constellation that had gotten tired of the heavens and moved indoors.

Odile stopped at the crest of the hill and stared.

"It's *big*," she said.

Mirabel, standing beside her with her hood up and her shadowlessness hidden beneath a borrowed cloak, made a sound that might have been agreement or might have been indigestion. They had been walking for six days since the moor, since Mirabel burned through the knights' memory-fetters and collapsed into Odile's arms smelling of ozone and burnt sugar. Six days of colorless sky and roads that forgot themselves mid-stride. A city was a miracle.

"It's also full of people," Mirabel said, "and people are complicated."

"You're people."

"I'm fae. We're people the way a storm is weather." She tugged her hood lower. "Come on. If we stand on this hill much longer we'll look like exactly what we are: strangers deciding whether to be trouble."

---

The gate had no guards. It had a woman with a ledger and a bowl of small clay tokens, and she pressed one into Odile's palm before Odile could say a word.

"Wear it," the woman said. Her voice was pleasant and flat as a well-read book. "Round your neck. Don't lose it."

The token was gray clay, unglazed, stamped with a spiral. On its back, someone had scratched a single word: *Tallow*.

"I'm not—" Odile began. "My name is Odile. Odile Fairweather."

The woman looked up from her ledger. For a moment there was nothing in her face at all—a door closed against winter—and then something flickered behind it, brief as a moth. Pity, maybe. Or memory of pity.

"Names go first," she said gently. "It's how it starts. The dark eats names before it eats anything else. A name is just a story you tell about yourself, and stories are the easiest meal there is." She tapped the token. "So we wear these instead. Clay doesn't taste like anything. The dark gets bored."

"And Tallow? Is that—" Odile looked down at the token. "Is that my name now?"

"It's your *token*. Names are for people who still have them." The woman smiled, and it didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "Welcome to Correnvale, Tallow. Mind the third bridge; it lies about where it goes."

---

Mirabel found her an hour later in the market square, sitting on a fountain's edge, turning the clay token over and over.

"Yours says *Brick*," Odile said.

"I know. I find it insulting. I was a noblewoman of the Starlit Court. I have been called 'Radiance' and 'Daughter-of-the-Second-Dawn' and once, memorably, 'the Scourge of the Amber Hall.'" Mirabel sat down beside her. "And now I am Brick."

"You could refuse to wear it."

"I could also walk into the sea wearing lead boots. Both would be quicker ways to die." Mirabel plucked the token from around Odile's neck and examined it. "Tallow. Hmm. Greasy, useful, burns well. Better than mine, honestly."

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny." But Mirabel's humor had a hairline crack in it tonight; Odile had learned to see those. She handed the token back and said, more quietly: "There were people in that square, Odile. Hundreds. And every one of them flinched when I asked directions. Not because I'm fae—they couldn't tell, I'm warded—but because I used the word *name*. One man dropped his entire basket of eels."

"What did you ask him?"

"How to find lodging. He said, 'Ask for what you need, not who you are,' and then he cried a little and gave me an eel."

Odile looked down at the token in her palm. "Do you think it works? The clay?"

"I think," Mirabel said slowly, "that when the dark came for my court, it took the names first, and everything else followed like water finding a crack. So yes. I think clay might genuinely be the most brilliant thing humans have ever invented." She paused. "Which is a low bar, given what else you've invented. Have you seen your own farming tools?"

"Gentle humor, Mirabel. You promised."

"I am being extremely gentle. For me."

---

They lodged at an inn called the Second Bell, because the innkeeper could no longer remember why it was called that but remembered that it had been called something, and *Second Bell* felt close enough. The rooms were clean. The stew was good. Nobody asked their names, which was the whole point, and nobody asked where they'd come from, which was a mercy, because Odile wasn't sure she could explain the ash-field and the fallen ward-line and the guild of nine aging ward-keepers who had known the end was coming and told no one.

She lay awake that night listening to the city breathe, and she thought about tokens.

*Tallow*, scratched in clay. A self you could put on and take off. A belonging that cost nothing.

She fell asleep holding it, and if she dreamed, she didn't remember—which, lately, felt less like sleep and more like practice.

* * *

The token-seller's stall appeared on their fourth morning in Correnvale, in the crooked lane behind the Third Bridge (which did, in fact, lie about where it went), and Odile noticed it the way you notice a song you half-remember: with a lurch, before you know why.

The stall itself was unremarkable—weathered wood, a striped awning, shelves of small drawers like an apothecary's. What drew the eye was the merchandise. Tokens, hundreds of them, but finer than the gate-woman's rough clay. These were carved of bone and riverstone and pale blue glass, each polished smooth, each hung on a cord of braided thread. Each bore a different mark: a key, a moth, a crescent moon, a running fox.

A man sat behind the stall, old and neat, with white hair combed like frost on a window and hands folded as though he had all the time the world had forgotten to take from him.

"Looking or browsing?" he asked pleasantly, as Odile slowed.

"Browsing," said Odile, who was looking.

"A wise distinction." He gestured at the shelves. "Identity tokens. Finer work than the municipal clay, if you'll forgive the boast. The clay keeps the dark out. Mine do something rather more interesting." He lifted a glass token to the light; inside it, faintly, something swirled like milk in tea. "These don't just replace a name. They *give* you one. A history. A place. Wear the Fox, and you'll remember a childhood in the eastern hills, a mother who sang off-key, a favorite meal. All of it true-feeling. All of it yours."

Odile's mouth went dry. "That's not real."

"No," he agreed cheerfully. "Neither is a name, if you follow the philosophers far enough. A name is a story. I simply write better ones than the dark does." His eyes crinkled. "And unlike the dark, I charge fairly."

Beside her, Mirabel had gone very still—the particular stillness of a cat watching a bird that has made a tactical error. "Where do you get them?" Mirabel asked. "The histories."

"Ah." The seller spread his hands. "Collected. Traded. Some donated willingly by folk who had more past than they wanted. Grief is heavy, you know. Some people pay *me*."

Something cold walked down Odile's spine. She thought of a ledger in a locked archive, pages listing adjustments, her own page thick as a hymnal. She thought of Elder Bram's tired voice: *We took the questions out of them so they wouldn't hurt themselves asking.*

"Have you ever sold one," she heard herself say, "to someone who'd lost their real memories? Someone who might want them back someday?"

The seller studied her for a long moment. Then he reached beneath the counter and set out a single token, apart from the others.

It was silver. Small. Stamped with a star.

"This one's been waiting a long time," he said. "For the right wearer. It holds a complete identity—birth, family, trade, the lot. Coherent. Warm. Belonging without cost, which as any honest merchant will tell you, is the rarest commodity in creation." He slid it across the counter toward her. "Try it. Just hold it. No obligation."

Odile should have stepped back. She knew that the way you know a stove is hot. Instead she picked it up.

Warmth bloomed up her arm like sunrise. And behind her eyes, soft as a tide coming in, arrived—

*a kitchen with copper pots, a mother humming, a sister laughing, a door always open, a town that knew her, a chair at a table with her name carved into it, a life, a whole life, seamless and golden and hers—*

"—odile?" Mirabel's voice, distant. "*Odile.*"

She surfaced gasping. The silver token lay in her palm, and her cheeks were wet, and she could not have said whether the tears belonged to the false life or the true one.

"It fits you," the seller said softly. "I thought it might. That's the tragedy of my trade, dear—it fits everyone."

"How much?" Odile whispered.

"*Nothing,*" said Mirabel, and her hand closed over Odile's wrist like a shackle, and her voice had an edge Odile had only heard once before, on the moor, right before the knights' fetters burned. "She's not buying. We're leaving."

"The lady speaks for you, then?" the seller asked Odile, mild as morning.

And that was the question, wasn't it. Odile stood in the crooked lane with a golden life pooled warm in her hand, and she thought: *no one in the valley ever chose me. Nine ward-keepers and I was the one sent to tend the outermost stone alone. My own elders edited me like a bad draft. But this—this token doesn't know me and already it wants me. It fits.*

Belonging without cost.

Except.

Except she thought of the ferryman on the forgetting river, and the price of passage: *one true story each.* She thought of Mirabel carrying her ledger-grief up the mountain tooth, weeping for memories that weren't even hers to mourn. She thought of the covenant, and what it demanded, and what she had already paid, and the shape of the truth forming in her chest:

*Everything worth having costs. Anything free is somebody else's story wearing your face.*

She set the silver token on the counter. Carefully. Like setting down something sleeping.

"No," she said. Her voice shook and she let it. "No. I've had my identity stolen by professionals. At least they had the decency to feel guilty about it."

The seller's smile flickered—just at the corners, just for a heartbeat—and underneath it, for one instant, Odile saw something ancient and hungry and terribly sad.

"Pity," he said.

"Yes," said Mirabel, drawing Odile away by the elbow. "It really is."

They walked fast, and neither spoke until the crooked lane had spat them out into the market square, among the eel-baskets and the lanterns and the hundred clay-necked citizens of Correnvale.

"You felt it," Mirabel said at last. Not a question.

"I felt a whole life." Odile wrapped her arms around herself. "It was *nice*, Mirabel. That's the horrible part. It was warm and easy and it fit, and some traitor part of me wanted to live there forever."

Mirabel was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "When the dark took my court, it didn't break down the gates. It knocked, and offered everyone a version of themselves they liked better. Most of them opened the door." She touched her own clay token—*Brick*—where it hung against her throat. "Ugly little lump of dirt. But it's *mine*-shaped. Do you understand? It asks nothing and gives nothing. It's just a rock that says *you're allowed to exist here.*"

"That's the saddest sentence I've ever heard."

"It's the truest one I know." Mirabel's mouth quirked. "Besides, if anyone gets to rename you, it should be me. I have excellent taste. I was going to suggest 'Wren.' Small, overlooked, surprisingly difficult to kill."

Despite everything, Odile laughed—a wet, cracked sound, but a laugh. "Better than Tallow."

"Infinitely better than Tallow."

They turned toward the Second Bell, toward stew and sleep and the road tomorrow, and Odile was almost—almost—steady again.

Then she saw the child.

Small, maybe seven years old, standing at the mouth of the crooked lane they'd just left. Clay token around her neck. Watching them with an expression no seven-year-old should own: the flat, patient look of someone waiting for an appointment.

As Odile met her eyes, the child raised one small hand—not waving. Holding something up.

Silver. Stamped with a star.

And when she spoke, her voice was wrong for her body—older, layered, like several people speaking in careful unison:

"She held it. She chose. As required." The child tilted her head. "But the covenant isn't finished with her yet, Wren. And neither am I. Ask her—ask her what she traded at the river gate, and what she got back in change. Ask her what the ferryman slipped into her pocket while she was crying."

Odile's hand flew to her cloak pocket—the one she hadn't checked since the river, the one that had seemed heavier these past days in a way she'd blamed on tiredness.

Her fingers closed around something small and smooth and cold.

Not clay. Not silver.

Bone.
