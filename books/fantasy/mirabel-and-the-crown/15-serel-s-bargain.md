## 15. Serel's Bargain

The girl was easier to catch than she should have been.

Serel had tracked her for two days through the forgetting country—through fields where the grass grew in gray and the roads doubled back on themselves like old men retracing their steps—and the whole time the girl had made no effort at all to hide. She built fires. She sang under her breath, badly. She left footprints in ash that a deaf hound could have followed.

It was almost insulting.

Almost.

But Serel had learned, over the long service of the Hollow Court, that insult was often just another word for grief wearing armor. The girl walked loudly because she had never had to walk quietly. She sang because no one had ever told her not to. She moved through the starless world like someone who still believed it would be kind to her.

*She doesn't know what we are,* Serel thought, watching from the ridge as Odile Fairweather crouched by a stream, filling her waterskin with the exaggerated care of someone who had read about water but rarely trusted it. *She doesn't know what I am.*

He almost envied her.

The order had been simple: take the ward-keeper alive. Do not harm the fae woman—the Lady of the Dusk Court, the signatory who fled—until the Hollow One could speak with her personally. As for the human girl, well. The Hollow One had smiled when he said it, which in Serel's experience meant the harm was of a subtler kind.

*Bring me the keeper,* he had said, *and I will give her back everything the guild took.*

Serel had not asked what that meant. Asking questions in the Hollow Court was how you ended up standing watch at the edge of the devoured sky, counting stars that were no longer there, wondering why your chest ached every time you passed the western road.

He descended the ridge now, quiet as falling snow, and put his hand over the girl's mouth before she could finish her first syllable of surprise.

"I'm sorry," he said, and meant it, which was more than most knights of the Hollow Court could say. "This will be easier if you don't fight."

She fought anyway. Of course she fought. She fought with the graceless, furious competence of someone who had spent her whole life being overlooked and had recently discovered that being overlooked was an excellent tactical advantage. Her elbow found his ribs. Her heel found his instep. She got one hand free and did something to his wrist that a guild ward-keeper absolutely should not have known how to do.

"Stop," he said, tightening his grip. "Please. I don't want to bind you. Binding hurts."

"Then let go!"

"If I let go, you'll run, and then I'll have to bind you, and then it will hurt, and then we'll both be cross about it." He paused. "I'm offering you tea instead. Metaphorically."

That stopped her. He felt it in the sudden stillness of her shoulders—the particular stillness of a person recalculating.

"...Tea," she said, muffled against his gauntlet.

"There's a hollow under the yew tree fifty paces east. Dry floor. A fire that doesn't smoke. And a kettle." He hesitated. "I stole the kettle from a village where no one remembered owning it. I've decided that makes it morally neutral."

"You're very strange for a knight," said Odile Fairweather.

"Yes," said Serel. "I know."

* * *

The hollow under the yew tree was exactly as advertised: dry, dim, smelling of old leaves and older stone. Serel built the fire with the small economy of long habit while the girl sat across from him, wrists unbound, watching him with the expression of someone cataloguing exits.

There were none. They both knew it. The firelight reached exactly as far as the walls of the hollow and no farther; beyond it, the gray dark pressed like a held breath.

"You're one of them," she said at last. "The ones hunting Mirabel."

"I am one of the ones hunting *you*, actually. Mirabel is a separate errand." He set the kettle on the coals. "You're the easy part."

"Flattering."

"It wasn't meant to be. It was meant to be honest, which is rarer." He turned the kettle handle toward her, an old courtesy. "Ask your questions, keeper. You're going to regardless. Everyone does."

She studied him for a long moment. Firelight caught the gray of her eyes—the same gray as the sky outside, he noticed, the same gray as everything the dark had touched. But there was something underneath it. Something the dark hadn't gotten to yet.

"What happened to your memory?" she asked.

And there it was. Not *why are you hunting us*. Not *what do you want*. The ward-keeper looked at a stranger in black lacquered armor and asked after his soul.

"The same thing that happened to yours," Serel said quietly. "Only mine was taken by choice. Yours was taken while you slept."

Her face went very carefully blank. He recognized the technique—it was what soldiers did before a wound finished arriving.

"My memories weren't taken," she said. "I just... don't have many. Guild children are taught to live in the present. It's a discipline."

"It's a ledger," said Serel. "I've seen it. We all saw it, in the years before the sky went out, when the Hollow Court still sent envoys to the valley and the valley still pretended to be merely a valley. Nine names. Pages and pages of 'adjustments.' Your page was the thickest."

The kettle began to sing. Neither of them moved to pour it.

"You're lying," she said. "To make me compliant. That's what Bram would—" She stopped herself, but too late, and Serel watched the word land somewhere inside her and stay there.

"Bram," he repeated softly. "Elder Bram. Third chair of the Fairweather guild. Keeper of the western archive. The man who signed off on forty-one memory adjustments over thirty years and called each one 'maintenance.'" He poured the tea at last, two clay cups, steam rising between them like a small ghost. "Yes. Him."

Odile took the cup because her hands needed something to do. He understood that too.

"Why are you telling me this?" she asked. "You captured me. You're supposed to be delivering me to whatever ate the stars. Why give me worse news first?"

"Because the offer only works if you understand what you'd be getting back." Serel wrapped both hands around his own cup and did not drink. "And because I wanted one person in this whole gray world to hear the truth from someone who isn't selling anything."

Except, said a dry voice in the back of his skull, you are. You're selling it right now. You've been selling it since the ridge.

He ignored the voice. He was good at ignoring voices. It was practically a court skill.

"The Hollow One knows what the guild did," he said. "Knows it better than the guild ever did. Every adjustment leaves a residue—a shape in the mind where the memory used to sit, like a footprint in snow. Most minds heal over the print eventually. Grow smooth. Forget even the forgetting." He looked at her. "Yours hasn't. Yours is full of footprints, keeper. Hundreds of them. All pointing somewhere."

"Where?"

"That's the offer." He set down his cup. "Come with me willingly. Give me Mirabel—her location, her habits, the keystone she carries, whatever you know of her plans. In exchange, the Hollow One will restore every stolen memory. Whole. Unbroken. Your childhood entire. Everything the guild cut out of you, returned in a single breath."

Silence. The fire popped. Somewhere above them, beyond the yew roots, the colorless wind moved through grass that couldn't remember being green.

"And if I refuse?" Odile asked.

"Then I bind you and deliver you anyway, and the Hollow One takes what he wants from your mind the slow way, and you get nothing back but scars." He said it plainly, because she deserved plainness. "I would prefer the tea version."

She laughed. Actually laughed—a short, startled sound, quickly strangled, as if laughter were another thing the guild had trained out of her and it had escaped anyway.

"The tea version," she repeated. "You're offering to sell my own life back to me, and you're worried about hospitality."

"I find," Serel said, "that cruelty goes down easier with a cup in your hand."

* * *

Here is what Serel did not tell her: the restoration was real. That was the terrible part. The Hollow One did not deal in lies—he dealt in truths arranged like knives.

Serel had seen it done once, to a deserter they had caught three winters ago. The man had sold his brother's hiding place for the return of his mother's face. For one heartbeat he had stood in the center of the camp with his eyes wide and wet and *full*, seeing her, remembering her, laughing or sobbing or both—

And then the Hollow One had closed his hand, and the man had knelt, and served, and never spoken of his mother again, because the Hollow One kept what he gave. Restoration, in the Hollow Court, was a loan. Interest accrued daily.

But the heartbeat itself. The heartbeat was honest.

"Before you answer," Serel said, "there is something you should see. Not the whole thing. Just—" He searched for the words. "A taste. So you know I'm not bluffing. The Hollow One permits it. He says it sweetens the bargain."

Odile's hands had gone white around her cup. "How can you show me a memory I don't have?"

"Because it isn't gone, keeper. It's only folded away. And I hold the key." He rose, crossed the small space between them, and knelt so that his eyes were level with hers—an old courtesy, knight to civilian, captor to captive, and beneath both of those, one tired soldier to one frightened girl. "This will feel like standing in a doorway. One heartbeat. Then it closes. Do you understand?"

"No," she said honestly.

"Good. Honest people survive longer." He raised his hand. "Breathe."

He touched two fingers to her brow, and spoke the unlocking word—the one that tasted like snowmelt and sounded like a door in an empty house—and the fold opened.

For one heartbeat, Odile Fairweather saw her childhood whole.

Serel saw it reflected in her face, the way you see lightning in a window without hearing the thunder. He saw her eyes go wide. He saw her lips part around a name—*Mira*—a child's name, a nickname, half-laughed. He saw her hands come up as if to catch something falling. He saw her look at *him*—at the knight holding the key—with an expression that cracked straight down the middle of everything she believed about herself, and in that crack he glimpsed:

A summer evening. The shadow of the old ward-line thrown long across the grass. Two girls sitting on opposite sides of a border neither of them owned—one in a guild smock with ash on her knees, one in a dress the color of dusk, passing a stolen apple back and forth through a gap in the wards because the gap was exactly apple-sized and neither of them had thought to question why such a gap existed.

Laughter. An oath sworn in the solemn grammar of nine-year-olds. *Best friends forever, even if they tell us not to be.* A pinky hooked through the ward-gap like a promise made flesh.

And then—elders' robes. Raised voices. A door closing. A candle burning down. A page in a ledger growing thick, thick, thick—

The heartbeat closed.

Odile made a sound that Serel would remember for the rest of his long, gray service. It was not a scream. It was smaller than a scream and infinitely worse—the sound of a person finding a room in their own house they had never known existed, and hearing, faintly, from inside it, someone calling their name.

"Mira," she whispered. "Her name was—she called me—I *knew* her. Before. I knew her *before*." Her eyes snapped up to his, blazing, wet, ferocious. "They didn't erase a stranger. They erased my *friend*. My best friend. They sat me down at some table and they took her out of me like a splinter and they let me grow up around the hole she left and I *felt* it, didn't I? All those years. I felt the hole and I thought it was just me. I thought I was just—"

"Small," Serel said quietly. "Overlooked. Invisible."

"They *made* me invisible." Her voice shook. "Why? Why would they—if we were friends, if she was fae and I was—why would that matter enough to cut out?"

"Because a friendship across the ward-line is a covenant in miniature," Serel said. "Two realms, bound by willing hearts. The guild spent three hundred years making sure no one in the valley ever loved the other side enough to miss it. Because love asks questions. And questions renew things. And renewed things can't be quietly allowed to die." He paused. "Your elders didn't steal your happiness, keeper. They stole your *witness*. There's a difference, and it's crueller."

Odile sat very still. The tea went cold between them. Outside the hollow, the gray dark leaned closer, patient as debt.

"So," she said at last, and her voice was terrifyingly level. "Let me make sure I understand the shape of this. If I betray her—if I hand over the woman who has been the only person in twenty years to look at me and see someone worth looking at—you'll give me back the memory of why she matters. You'll let me feel, for one heartbeat, exactly what I destroyed to get it."

Serel opened his mouth to give the official answer, the scripted answer, the answer the Hollow One had written for him in his smooth voice like oil on water.

What came out instead was the truth.

"Yes," he said. "That is precisely the shape of it. It is the cruelest offer I have ever been ordered to make, and I once watched a man trade his daughter's name for a night of warmth." He bowed his head. "I am sorry, Odile Fairweather. I have been sorry since the ridge. Sorry is worthless, I know. But I wanted you to hear it from the one part of me the Court hasn't collected."

She looked at him for a long time. And then—this was the thing Serel would turn over in his mind for nights afterward, the thing that would not lie flat—she smiled. Small and crooked and entirely unbroken.

"You keep saying 'the one part of me,'" she said. "Which means there's a part of you they *haven't* collected. Something they missed." She tilted her head. "Something that aches when you pass the western road. Something that stirs when you say her name, even though you don't remember why."

The fire popped. Serel did not move. Inside his breastplate, behind the gray lacquer of his obedience, something turned over in its sleep—and for one impossible instant he smelled woodsmoke and rain on a moor, and heard a voice saying *Serel, you great solemn idiot, sit down before you fall down*—

Gone.

"I don't know what you mean," he said.

"Of course you don't," said Odile gently. "That's rather the point."

And that was when the darkness at the mouth of the hollow shifted, and unfolded, and stood up.

Mirabel stepped into the firelight with ash in her hair and fury in her eyes and the third keystone glowing at her throat like a swallowed star, and she looked from the knight to the ward-keeper and said, in a voice that made the yew roots tremble:

"*Serel.*"

And Serel—who had not heard his name spoken in kindness since before the sky went out—forgot, entirely, whose side he was on.
