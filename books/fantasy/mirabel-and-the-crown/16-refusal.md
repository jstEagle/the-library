## 16. Refusal

The cell they put me in was, I had to admit, nicer than my room at the guild.

That was the insulting part. Serel's people had given me a chamber in the hollow tower with a real bed, a basin of water that steamed, and a window—barred, but a window all the same—looking out over a moor the color of old bruises. They wanted me comfortable. Comfortable prisoners say yes.

I sat on the bed with my back against the cold stone and turned the ledger-page fragment over in my hands for the hundredth time. I'd sewn it into my sleeve before Mirabel and I ever left the valley, the way you carry a splinter you can't dig out. *Fairweather, Odile. Adjustments: seven.* Seven removals. And somewhere beneath all of them, a girl standing at the ward-line with her hand pressed flat against air that shimmered, talking to someone on the other side.

Someone who laughed like water going over stones.

"Stop it," I told myself out loud. "You're being bait. Act like bait."

The trouble with acting like bait is that nobody teaches you how. Do you sulk? Do you seem agreeable? I decided to split the difference: I would be polite, unhelpful, and quietly furious, which was more or less my natural state after twenty-odd years in the Fairweather guild.

The door opened without a knock. Serel filled it the way winter fills a doorway.

He hadn't changed since the ambush on the moor, except that now he carried no fetters and made no move to draw the long pale blade at his hip. His armor was the gray of things that have forgotten what color they were. His face was handsome the way an empty house is handsome—all the structure of beauty with nobody home behind the windows.

Except. Except that twice on the moor, when Mirabel had burned through his knights' memory-chains, I had seen something flicker in those windows. A candle lit and snuffed. Lit and snuffed.

"You did not sleep," he said. His voice was low and even, a voice for reciting oaths.

"I slept fine," I lied. "Your bed is lumpy."

"It is filled with goose down." He considered this. "Perhaps you are unused to comfort."

"Perhaps I'm unused to prisons with good linens." I hugged my knees. "What do you want, Serel?"

He crossed the room—not close, never close—and stood by the window with his hands clasped behind him, a man inspecting a horizon he did not own. When he spoke again, there was something almost gentle in it, and that frightened me more than the sword had.

"The Hollow Court has kept a record of you," he said. "Since the night the stars went out. The dark that eats memory does not eat everything at once. It eats from the edges inward. Names first. Then faces. Then the reasons we loved the faces. You are one of only two living creatures on this side of the ward-line whose memories it cannot fully digest."

"Mirabel," I said.

"Mirabel," he agreed, and said the name carefully, the way you set down something breakable you don't remember owning. "And you. Which makes you valuable. Which makes you *fixable*."

I didn't like the word fixable. Fixable implied broken, and broken implied someone else got to hold the glue.

"Here is my offer," Serel said, turning to face me at last. "The dark took your guild's thefts and made them its own—it wears your erased memories like stolen coats. But coats can be returned. I have stood in the presence of the thing below the court. I have heard it turn the pages of what it has eaten. It will trade. Every adjustment. All seven. Your mother's voice. Your childhood whole. The girl at the ward-line and whoever she was laughing with. Everything the ledger took and everything the dark has chewed since. Restored. Whole. *Yours* again."

My heart was doing something embarrassing in my chest.

"And the price?" I asked, because there is always a price; I had learned that from a river-spirit and a mountain and a ferryman who charged one true story per crossing.

Serel's empty windows flickered. Lit. Snuffed.

"Mirabel," he said. "Lead her to the Court Beneath. Walk her through the gate yourself. Tell her it is sanctuary. She trusts you—I have watched her trust you, and I do not understand it, but I have watched it. She goes to the throne. The covenant dies unfinished. And you walk away with every piece of yourself."

* * *

Here is what I want it on record that I did not do: I did not cry.

I wanted to. My eyes prickled and my throat went tight as a drawstring bag, because he had said *your mother's voice*, and I could not remember my mother's voice, not the sound of it, only that she had existed, only that learning to swim beside her had cost me that memory at the river gate and I had paid it gladly and regretted it every night since, quietly, the way you regret a tooth.

But I did not cry, because crying would have told him the offer was working, and the offer *was* working, and that was exactly why I couldn't let it show.

Instead I asked questions. It's what I do. It's apparently what I've always done—the one thing no ritual ever managed to scrape out of me.

"How do I know you'll keep the bargain?" I said. "How do I know the dark won't just take the memories back once Mirabel's caught? How do I know you're not lying right now?"

Something moved behind Serel's face. Not a candle this time—a draft through a shut house, stirring dust.

"I do not know if you are lying," he said slowly, as though the thought were arriving in him from a great distance. "I know that I was sent to bring her in. I know that I have brought in eleven others, and each time I forgot one more reason it mattered. I know that—" He stopped. His hand rose halfway to his breastplate, where a knight of the Hollow Court wore his memory-fetters, and found nothing there, because Mirabel had burned them off him on the moor, and he had not yet worked out what that meant.

"You feel it too," I said softly. "Don't you. The hole where she used to be."

"Do not," he said, and his voice cracked like river ice, "presume to tell me what I feel."

"There's a shape in you shaped exactly like her," I said. "I've seen it. On the moor, when she burned the fetters—you looked at her like a man looking at a door in a wall he'd lived in all his life and never noticed. You can't remember her. But you *miss* her. Missing doesn't need the memory. That's the whole point of missing."

He stared at me. For three full heartbeats the empty house had someone moving around inside it, and then the shutters came down and he was winter again.

"You have until moonrise," he said. "Give your answer to the guard. If it is yes, you will be escorted to the Court Beneath. If it is no—" He paused at the door. "If it is no, then I am sorry. The Court does not leave loose threads."

The door closed. I sat very still until his footsteps faded, and then I let myself shake, just a little, just once, and then I stopped, because I had work to do.

* * *

The trick, when it came to me, came sideways, the way all the best things in my life arrived—uninvited, unexplained, and smelling faintly of the guild archive.

I was pacing, because pacing helps, and my feet took me through a sequence without asking my permission: two steps, pivot on the left heel, two steps, pivot on the right, weight low, hands loose. A pattern. A *drill*. My body knew it the way my body knew how to swim, except I had never been taught to fight in my life. The Fairweather guild taught census-taking, marker-tending, and the proper way to apologize to an elder.

But some elder, some version of me, some *erased* version of me, had learned this drill so thoroughly that deletion hadn't taken. The guild could cut the story out of my head. They could not cut it out of my feet.

Which meant, I reasoned, watching the guard through the barred window as he made his slow circuit of the tower, that other things might survive too. Things done ten thousand times until they lived in the hands instead of the mind.

Like lockwork.

I knelt by the door. There was a keyhole, because the Hollow Court, for all its terrors, ran on locks the same as any granary. And my fingers, hovering near it, began to *want* things. The pick was a sliver of the bed-frame I'd loosened over two days of pretending to nap. My hands took it and moved with a confidence I did not possess—three small turns, a counter-pressure here, a listening pause there—and I realized I wasn't thinking the movements at all. I was remembering them. With my fingers. Somewhere in the thick blacked-out pages of my ledger, a younger Odile had picked locks, many locks, probably guild locks, probably *for reasons the elders had found alarming*, and they had burned that out of my mind and left it, absurdly, in my thumbs.

The lock clicked open like a small polite cough.

"Oh," I whispered, grinning like an idiot in the dark. "Oh, you *beautiful*, terrible, memory-stealing old frauds. You couldn't delete what mattered."

I slipped into the corridor.

* * *

Escaping a hollow tower staffed by memory-eaten knights is mostly a matter of walking like you belong. This, at least, the guild had taught me openly: walk like you belong, and the world will bill you later. I kept my chin level, my pace unhurried, and my ledger-page fragment hidden in my sleeve, and I passed two knights who looked straight through me, because a girl in a plain gray dress carrying herself like a minor official is invisible everywhere in creation. Some inheritances are useful.

The trouble started at the stair, where Serel was waiting.

Of course he was. Loose threads get gathered. He stood at the landing with the pale blade drawn—not pointed at me, just drawn, the way you light a lamp before a difficult conversation—and behind the emptiness of his face the candle was flickering hard now, lit, snuffed, lit.

"You picked the lock," he said.

"Your bed-frame is poorly seasoned oak," I said. "Also, I'd like it noted that I'm choosing to refuse. Formally. In case anyone's keeping minutes."

"Odile." My name in his mouth sounded wrong, borrowed. "The offer stands until moonrise. After that, the Court takes what it wants. From both of you. It does not need your consent. Consent is only required for the *gentle* version."

"Then here's my answer," I said, and I stepped backward onto the narrow window ledge, three stories above the bruise-colored moor, wind pulling at my hair. "No. To all of it. To the trade, to the throne, to the gentle version and the other kind. You want to know why I won't give her up? I'll tell you. It's not because I remember loving her. I don't. Someone stole that from me, maybe twice. It's because out of everyone in two worlds—the guild that raised me, the elders who lied to me, the dark that's eating everything—she is the only person who ever *chose* me without a ledger entry, without an adjustment, without wanting anything back. You don't get to price that. Nobody gets to price that. Not even me."

Serel took one step forward. "You will fall."

"Probably!" I agreed cheerfully, heart hammering. "But here's the thing about falling. It's a drill too."

I let myself drop off the ledge.

Two steps, pivot on the left heel, weight low, hands loose—my body folded into a roll I had no conscious knowledge of, shoulder, hip, shoulder, up and running across the wet heather while the impact rang up through my bones and something in my knees filed a formal complaint. Behind me I heard Serel shout, and boots on stairs, and horns—horns, oh no, horns were bad—but ahead, down the slope, at the treeline where the moor gave way to black pines, a light was coming. Not torchlight. Starlight, or the memory of starlight, silver and furious and familiar.

She broke from the trees at a dead run, hair streaming, eyes wide with a terror she wasn't bothering to hide, and I ran harder, and we collided somewhere in the middle of the heather hard enough to knock us both sprawling.

"You *idiot*," Mirabel gasped, gripping my arms, checking me over with frantic hands. "You absolute—I felt it, I felt you decide something, I felt it like a bell ringing, and I've been running for an hour—are you hurt? Did they touch you? Odile, are you—"

"I jumped out of a window," I said, breathless, absurdly proud.

"You *what*?"

"Rolled, actually. It turns out I know how to roll. Also pick locks. Also, apparently, infuriate fae knights, though that one might be native talent." I grabbed her hand and hauled us both upright. "Run now, explain later?"

We ran.

Behind us the horn-calls tangled and multiplied, and then, cutting through them, a single voice—Serel's voice, bellowing an order I couldn't make out, and the pursuit behind us faltered, stuttered, slowed. I risked one glance back.

Serel stood alone on the slope between us and his knights, sword still drawn, and he wasn't following. He was holding the line. *His own line.* His knights shouted at him—*the prisoners, my lord, the prisoners flee*—and he stood there with the candle behind his eyes burning steady now instead of flickering, and he said, in a voice that carried across the moor like a crack in ice:

"They went north. Toward the fens. We will search south."

It was a lie. An enormous, treasonous, magnificent lie, told by a man who could not remember why he was telling it, and I saw the exact moment he realized he'd done it—the confusion breaking over his face like a wave—as Mirabel pulled me into the trees and the dark swallowed us both.

* * *

We walked most of the night, north and east, until the horns faded and the pines gave way to a valley floor soft with moss, and only then did Mirabel stop, plant her hands on her hips, and look at me the way Elder Bram used to look at ledgers that wouldn't balance.

"A window," she said.

"Three stories."

"You can't fight. You can't ride. You cried for an hour after the mountain trial because your hands were blistered."

"That was a private moment between me and my hands."

"And yet you jumped out of a tower, rolled like a soldier, and escaped a fortress of the Hollow Court." She crouched, picked up a stick, and drew in the moss: a circle, a slash, a spiral. "Show me the roll."

"I'm not doing the roll."

"Odile."

"Fine." I did the roll. It was, I'll admit, beautiful. Moss is forgiving. I came up grinning despite myself, and she was staring at me with an expression I'd begun to collect lately—wonder layered over grief, like frost on a window you can still see through.

"They didn't take it out of you," she said quietly. "Whatever they cut away—the guild, the dark, all of it—the true things grew back. Like roots under a flagstone. You keep finding your way *around* the erasure. Do you understand what that means?"

"That I'm stubborn?"

"It means," she said, taking my hands, and hers were warm and shaking slightly, "that what matters in a person isn't stored where thieves can reach it. It means the covenant chose better than any of us knew when it bound itself to *you*. It means—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started over, smaller. "It means when you gave your memories away at the river and the mountain, you gave from the topsoil. The deep roots are still yours. They were always going to be yours."

I looked at our hands together and thought about a girl at a ward-line, pressing her palm against the shimmer, laughing with someone on the other side whose laugh sounded like water over stones.

"Serel let us go," I said. "Did you see? He lied to his own knights. He doesn't remember you, Mirabel, but he's *choosing* you anyway, blind, the same way I—" My voice did something inconvenient. I cleared my throat. "There must be something left in him too. Something the fetters never reached."

"Yes," Mirabel said softly. "And the Court Beneath will have noticed. When a hound turns on the hunt, the master looks for the wound." Her face had gone grim, and older, and I remembered suddenly that she had been a noblewoman of a court older than my guild, older than my valley, and that the fear in her was not for herself. "They'll move the last keystone now. Tonight, if they can. Whatever window we had—"

Above us, through a tear in the clouds, a single point of light appeared where no star should be. Small. White. Falling.

Not falling, I realized, as it brightened and began to descend toward us in slow, deliberate spirals. *Delivered.*

A messenger-wisp, guild-made, guild-sealed, glowing the particular gold of Elder Bram's workshop—and trailing behind it, like a ribbon, a thread of memory meant for me alone.

Mirabel and I looked at each other.

"Bram," we said, at the same moment.
