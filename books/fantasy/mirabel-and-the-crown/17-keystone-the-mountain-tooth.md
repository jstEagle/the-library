## 17. Keystone: The Mountain Tooth

The mountain had been visible for two days before they reached it, which Mirabel said was rude of it.

"Mountains should announce themselves gradually," she explained, squinting up at the peak that speared the colorless sky like a needle someone had abandoned mid-seam. "A foothill. A polite ridge. Something. This one just *looms*."

"It's called the Mountain Tooth," Odile said.

"I can see why. It's showing off." Mirabel adjusted the strap of her pack and glared at the summit as though it might apologize. It did not. "There's a shrine at the top. There's always a shrine at the top. Fae never put anything important anywhere you could reach it by cart."

Odile checked her satchel for the fourth time that morning: the river keystone wrapped in wool, the ledger fragment, three apples going soft, and the wisp-lantern Elder Bram had sent them, still glowing faintly with his last message. She had read it eleven times. *The third stone sleeps where the court drowned. Forgive an old man his cowardice.* She hadn't forgiven him yet. She was saving it, the way she saved the last apple—something to look forward to.

"Your hands are shaking," Mirabel observed.

"They're not."

"You're holding the lantern like it's a bird that might escape."

Odile looked down. Her hands were, in fact, shaking. "It's cold," she said, which was true, and also a lie, because what she actually felt was the memory she'd been carrying since the ferryman's boat: *one true story each*, he'd asked, and she'd given him the story of learning to swim, and then discovered halfway through telling it that she no longer owned it. The river-spirit had taken it. The story came out smooth and hollow, like a stone worn round by water that had never touched her.

She had less inside herself than she'd thought. That knowledge sat in her chest now, permanent as a pebble in a boot.

Mirabel stopped walking. "Odile."

"Yes?"

"The trial at the Tooth. I know what it is. I've been trying to decide whether to tell you, and I've decided that lying to you by omission makes me feel like Bram, and I would rather be flayed alive."

"That's very dramatic."

"I'm fae. Drama is load-bearing." Mirabel took a breath—the first Odile had ever seen her take unnecessarily—and said, "The Mountain Tooth doesn't test strength. Anyone can climb. It tests weight. Each climber must carry something up the mountain that isn't theirs. And at the Tooth itself, the mountain chooses what that something is."

"Carries what?"

"A memory," Mirabel said. "Someone else's worst one."

The wind came off the moor behind them and pushed at their backs, impatient, like the world wanted them to hurry toward the thing that would hurt them.

"And if we refuse?" Odile asked.

"Then we go home, and the thirteenth moon rises over a valley with no stars, no wards, and no guild, and everyone we've ever—" Mirabel stopped. Recalculated. "Everyone *you've* ever loved forgets how to love anything. Memory goes first. You've seen the villages."

Odile had. She thought about the ferryman who traded stories because his own had leaked away; the woman in the last village who kept asking Odile her name every few minutes, delighted each time, as if meeting her anew. There was a version of that that wasn't horrifying. There was a version of that that was almost sweet. That was the trap of the dark—it made forgetting feel like mercy right up until there was nothing left to be merciful about.

"All right," Odile said. "Whose worst memory do I carry?"

Mirabel looked at her for a long moment. Then she smiled, and it was the wrong smile, the one she wore like armor, bright and brittle.

"Mine," she said. "And I'll carry yours. That's how the trial works. Exchange. The mountain doesn't want your grief, Odile. It wants you to hold mine while I hold yours, and see if either of you survives the view from the top."

* * *

They made camp at the mountain's foot, in the lee of a boulder shaped like a sleeping animal. Odile built the fire because Mirabel claimed fire-building was "a peasant art" and then watched with unconcealed admiration while Odile arranged the kindling.

"You could just say thank you," Odile said.

"I could say *that was competent*."

"You could say thank you."

"I could say *thank you, that was competent*." Mirabel accepted the tea Odile handed her and cradled it in both hands. For a while neither spoke. The fire cracked. Somewhere above them, wind moved through stone with a sound almost like voices.

"Tell me about yours," Odile said finally. "The guilt. If I'm going to carry it, I'd rather hear it from you than from a mountain."

Mirabel stared into the flames. When she spoke, her voice had none of its usual ornament. It was plain as unbleached cloth.

"My court was dying slowly—not all at once, the way yours went. Star by star. And I was the signatory. I held the fae half of the covenant in my keeping, the way your guild held the human half. Renewal was my duty. Every thirteen turns, I was meant to stand at the ward-line and speak my name into the stone." Her thumb traced the rim of her cup. "I told myself the third turn didn't matter. The stars were only dimming. Then the fifth turn. Then the ninth. And each time, leaving got harder, because my court had begun to whisper that the covenant was a leash, that the humans were beneath us, that renewal was submission. And I—I liked being admired more than I liked being dutiful. So I stayed. I sang at the beautiful parties and let the keystones crack."

"What happened on the thirteenth turn?"

"I ran." The words came out flat. "When the stars began to go out entirely, when the Hollow Court rose and started devouring light instead of merely hoarding it, I fled across the ward-line to warn the humans. That part was brave, if you like. But I fled *before* the signing. I left my court without a signatory and arrived at your valley after yours were already dead. I was too late in both directions, Odile. I am the reason both halves failed." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious about it. "Serel followed me. He believed in me enough to follow me into exile, and I couldn't even tell him why we were running, because telling him meant admitting what I'd done. And then the dark took his memories, and now he serves it, and somewhere in that hollow armor is a man who loved a coward."

Odile set down her own cup. She didn't say *it wasn't your fault*, because it partly was, and Mirabel would have heard the lie. She didn't say *you're making it right now*, because that was obvious and cheap.

What she said was: "You could have run farther. You ran *toward* the problem. Cowards don't do that."

Mirabel laughed—a small, startled sound, like a bird landing. "That's terrible logic."

"I'm a guild clerk. Logic is load-bearing for me."

* * *

The climb began at dawn, and the mountain began taking things almost immediately.

It happened at the first switchback. One moment Odile was hauling herself over a ledge of gray rock; the next, the ledge was warm under her palms and smelled of woodsmoke and old paper, and she was nine years old again, standing in the guild archive while Elder Wren opened the ledger of adjustments and read aloud the list of everything taken from her. Not remembered—*carried*. That was the difference, and the difference was everything. This was not her memory returning. It was Mirabel's grief wearing Odile's past like a coat, and Odile felt it from the outside: the horror of a stranger reading her own theft aloud, the vertigo of discovering her invisibility had been engineered, page after thick page of erasure in a careful clerk's hand.

"Oh," Odile whispered, clinging to the rock. "Oh, this is what it felt like."

She had lived that moment once, before the river took other pieces of her. Now she experienced it secondhand, fresh, and understood for the first time why Mirabel sometimes went quiet in the middle of sentences, staring at nothing, hands pressed flat against her own ribs as if holding something in.

Above her on the trail, Mirabel made a sound. Odile looked up and saw her stop dead on the path, one hand against the mountainside, and knew the exchange had completed. Mirabel was carrying the ledger-grief now. Odile carried the abandonment.

They climbed.

The mountain was cruel in a patient way. It did not throw storms or shake loose stones. It simply made the weight real. By the third hour, Odile's shoulders ached as though she'd been carrying Mirabel herself, and worse than the ache was the intimacy of it—knowing another person's worst hour from the inside, feeling the shape of her shame, the exact texture of the moment she chose admiration over duty. There was nowhere to put it down. It settled against her spine and breathed.

At noon they rested in a hollow below the treeline, such as it was—the trees here were gray and leafless, holding their branches like raised arms. Mirabel sat with her head between her knees.

"Yours is heavy," she said, muffled.

"I know. I'm sorry."

"Don't apologize. That's the point of the thing." She lifted her head. Her face was blotchy and her hair had escaped its braid entirely, and Odile thought she had never seen anyone so unguardedly miserable and so unbearably dignified at the same time. "How do humans bear it? Carrying your own worst moments around all the time? We fae can set memories down. Store them. Trade them away. You just—*hold* everything?"

"We share it," Odile said. "That's what talking is. You hand someone a piece and they hold it a while, and it weighs less after."

Mirabel considered this. "That's why you make me tell you things."

"That's why I make you tell me things."

"You're a very bossy clerk."

"I'm a very bossy *ward-keeper*," Odile said, "and we're losing daylight."

* * *

The Tooth itself was a spire of black stone rising from the summit plateau, and the shrine at its base was nothing like the drowned shrine at the River Gate. No water, no moss, no gentle decay. Just a circle of standing stones around a pedestal of bare rock, and on the pedestal, a keystone the color of a winter dawn—pale gold shot through with veins of silver, humming faintly, like a struck bell that refused to finish ringing.

Between them and the pedestal stood the mountain.

It had no face. It didn't need one. The air simply thickened, and a presence gathered the way weather gathers, and a voice that was less a voice than a pressure against the mind spoke:

*TWO CLIMBERS. TWO BURDENS. SET THEM DOWN HERE, AND THE STONE IS YOURS.*

"How?" Odile asked. "Set them down, how?"

*GIVE THEM BACK TO THEIR OWNERS. FREELY. THAT IS THE TRIAL: NOT THAT YOU CARRY, BUT THAT YOU RETURN WHAT WAS NEVER YOURS TO KEEP.*

Odile looked at Mirabel. Mirabel looked at Odile. And Odile saw the problem at exactly the same moment Mirabel did, because they had spent ten days learning to think in tandem, and it was becoming frankly inconvenient.

"Giving them back means keeping them," Mirabel said quietly. "If I return your ledger-grief, you keep it. All of it. Permanently. The mountain won't carry it for you anymore."

"And if I return yours, you keep the guilt. Forever. No setting it down, no trading it away, no fae tricks." Odile's throat was tight. "It wants us to choose each other's pain over relief."

"It wants us to choose *each other*," Mirabel corrected softly. "Over relief."

The pressure of the mountain waited. It was very good at waiting. Mountains generally were.

Odile thought about the ledger. About the thick pages in her own name, the engineered invisibility, the childhood friendships scraped out like mistakes from a rough draft. She had spent weeks wanting that grief gone. On the ferry she'd envied the villagers their forgetting. In the city of tokens she'd nearly bought a false self, light as a coat you could take off.

But the grief was hers. It was the truest thing she owned. And it had led her here—to this mountain, to this woman, to the edge of a covenant that might save everything. Erased people couldn't have done any of this. Grieved people could.

"Give it back," she said. "All of it. I'll carry it myself."

Mirabel's composure cracked clean down the middle. "Odile—"

"No. My turn to be bossy." Odile stepped closer. "You gave me your story on the ferry and it weighed less after. That's the whole trick, Mirabel. That's what humans do. We don't set things down. We hand them to someone who'll hold them a while, and then we take them back lighter. So give it back. And I'll hold it whenever you need. That's not the mountain's trial. That's mine."

For a heartbeat Mirabel looked young—older than her centuries, younger than her sorrows. Then she nodded, once, and reached out, and pressed her palm flat against Odile's sternum.

The ledger-grief came home.

It landed like a stone dropped into deep water—all the horror of the archive, the stolen years, the thick pages, the mother whose face Odile suddenly realized she could no longer fully picture, and underneath it all, steady as bedrock, the knowledge that she had walked out past the markers anyway. That grief had not stopped her. Nothing had stopped her. She gasped, and staggered, and Mirabel caught her arm, and Odile stood in the full weight of her own history and found her legs held.

"Now you," she said.

Mirabel closed her eyes. "I abandoned them. Say it back to me."

"You abandoned them," Odile said. "And then you crossed the dark alone to warn strangers. Both things are true. Carry both."

The guilt returned to Mirabel like a tide, and Odile watched her take it—watched her knees bend, watched her breath go ragged, watched her stay standing anyway, tears cutting tracks through the dust on her face, mouth curved in something that was not quite a smile but was its close cousin.

"Both things are true," Mirabel repeated. "Oh. Oh, that's—yes. Fine. Yes." She wiped her face with her sleeve, thoroughly undignified, entirely herself. "The mountain can witness that I hate this trial."

*THE STONE IS CLAIMED,* said the mountain, and there was—if Odile wasn't imagining it, and she didn't think she was—something almost like approval in the vast slow voice. *FEW RETURN THEIR BURDENS WILLINGLY. FEWER STILL RETURN THEM KINDLY.*

The keystone lifted from the pedestal of its own accord and drifted to Odile's hands, light as a moth, warm as bread. When her fingers closed around it, the silver veins flared, and for one impossible instant the colorless sky above the summit flickered—a single point of light, high and hard and far, like a star remembering itself.

Then it was gone. But Odile had seen it. They both had.

"Two stones," Mirabel whispered.

"One left." Odile wrapped the keystone in wool beside its brother and felt the two hum together, harmonizing, like voices finding a chord. "The Court Beneath. Where the devoured stars are."

Mirabel was quiet for a moment. When she spoke, her voice had gone carefully neutral in a way Odile had learned to distrust.

"There's something I haven't told you about the Court Beneath."

"Mirabel."

"There's a throne there. And the thing sitting on it wears the faces of everyone it has eaten." She met Odile's eyes. "Including, I suspect, mine."

Far below them, at the mountain's foot, a line of gray figures had appeared on the road they'd traveled—knights in hollow armor, moving without haste, without fatigue, without mercy. At their head rode a tall shape whose helm was turned upward, toward the summit, toward the two small figures silhouetted against the fading flicker of a remembered star.

Serel had found the trail. And this time, he wasn't following Mirabel.

He was following Odile.
