## 18. The Thirteenth Moon Rises

Bram had always believed he would die in winter, because everything in the valley died in winter eventually, and he had long ago made his peace with being an ordinary thing. He had not expected to die with the thirteenth moon swelling fat and wrong above the eastern ridge, a moon the color of old milk, casting light that warmed nothing and forgave nothing.

He sat in the guild hall's great chair—the chair that creaked when anyone else sat in it, though it never creaked for him, which he had privately considered one of the small indignities of leadership—and watched his guild come apart.

It was, he reflected, happening rather more politely than he'd feared.

"The roads past the markers are gone," said Hesta, who kept the seed-ledgers and who had not raised her voice in forty years and did not intend to start now. "Wren walked to the second bend on Tuesday and came back having forgotten Tuesday. We should go while we still remember where we're going."

"Go *where*?" demanded Corvin, who was ninety-one and had been demanding things since roughly the age of four. "There is no *where*. The stars are out. The wards are down. The world outside has been eating itself for twelve turns of that—" he jabbed a finger at the window, at the pale swollen thing in the sky— "and you want to walk into its mouth with a knapsack?"

"Better its mouth than sitting here waiting to be swallowed."

"Speak for yourself. I intend to be indigestible."

A few of the younger members laughed—Tamsin, Pell, little Odile's cousin Rook, who had her same habit of laughing quietly at the back as if apologizing for it. Bram let the laughter run its course, because laughter was a kind of mortar and the hall needed all the mortar it could get.

Then Elder Sable stood, and the laughter died the way frost dies at dawn.

Sable was the youngest of the elders and the most frightened, and she wore her fear the way some people wore fine cloth: openly, so everyone could see how much she had paid for it. "Or," she said, "we open the doors. All of them. Every ward-stone, every marker, every seal the Fairweathers have kept for nine hundred years. The dark took the stars because we stopped feeding the covenant. Perhaps if we stop resisting it—if we *invite* it—it will take less."

The silence that followed had weight. Bram felt it settle on his chest like a stone he'd been carrying for decades and had only just admitted was there.

"You want to surrender," Corvin said flatly.

"I want to stop pretending we are protectors," Sable said. "We are nine old people and two children reciting words we don't understand over stones that have gone cold. I want us to stop pretending before the pretense kills someone."

And there it was. The truth, finally spoken aloud in the hall where truth had been ritually removed from the agenda three generations ago. Bram closed his eyes.

*She signed it too,* he thought, the way he had thought it every night for weeks. *She signed it too, and I put her in a cage, and then she was gone.*

Odile had taken Mirabel out past the markers eleven days ago. Eleven days. No word. No wisp. The girl had freed a stranger the elders had ordered confined, crossed a ward-line that had stood since before the guild had a name, and vanished into a colorless world—and Bram, who should have sent hunters, who should have sounded the bell, had instead sat in this chair night after night and felt something he could only describe as *hope*, and hated himself for it, because hope in a man like him was just cowardice wearing a borrowed coat.

"Enough," he said, and his voice cracked in the middle of the word like an old branch, which was fitting. "Both of you are right. That's the trouble. Hesta's right that we can't stay. Sable's right that we've been liars." He pushed himself up from the uncreaking chair, and his knees announced their opinions loudly enough that Tamsin moved to help him and he waved her off, because a man had to keep some pride even at the end. "So here is what we will do, and here is why, and I am going to tell you the whole of it, because I have spent my life handing out forgetting like bread, and I am done."

They stared at him. Nine faces—or eight; Wren had wandered off somewhere around midday and might currently be anywhere between the well and yesterday—and Bram drew the breath that hurt most, the one he'd been saving.

"In the archive," he said, "there is a ledger. You'll find your names in it. You'll find lists of things taken from you: your grandmother's funeral, your first love, the year of the flood, the reason you flinch at bells. I did that. Me, and the elders before me, going back to Aldous Fairweather himself. Not because we were cruel. Because we were *afraid*. The covenant between our valley and the star-born court required renewal every thirteen moons—a signing, a sacrifice, a living hand from each realm. And each time the turn came, people asked questions. Why must it be one of ours? What happens to the ones who sign? And questions, in a covenant built on willing sacrifice, are poison. A sacrifice that hesitates fails. So we cut the questions out. We cut out the grief and the doubt and the memory of every signing, and we raised children who tended stones by rote and never once asked why the rote mattered."

Hesta's hands had gone white on her ledger. Rook had stopped breathing entirely.

"The last renewal was due thirteen years ago," Bram went on, quieter now. "The fae signatory never came. She was a noblewoman of the court—Mirabel, though that name was already half-eaten when she reached us. She had signed the covenant once before, long ago, and she came to sign again, and I—" His voice did the branch-thing again. "I looked at her, and I saw the thirteenth moon coming, and I saw what signing had cost her the first time, and I was a coward. I told her the turn had been miscounted. I gave her tea with forget-moss in it and put her across the ward-line and told myself the next generation would handle it. And the signatory on our side—old Marta, who remembered everything because I could never bring myself to touch her memories—waited, and waited, and died last spring believing someone else would act."

He looked around the hall at the faces of people whose lives he had edited without their permission.

"There is no elegant ending to this confession," he said. "I stole from you. All of you. I stole the worst parts of your lives because I couldn't bear to watch you carry them, and because carrying them would have made you dangerous, and dangerous people ask questions, and questions end covenants. And now the covenant has ended anyway, because of me, and the stars are dead because of me, and the only two people doing anything about it are a girl I made invisible on purpose and a woman I turned away at the door."

Nobody spoke. Outside, the thirteenth moon climbed another finger's width above the ridge, patient as debt.

It was Corvin, of all people, who broke the silence. The old man levered himself up, crossed the hall with his cane clicking like a slow clock, and stood in front of Bram.

"You miserable old thief," Corvin said.

"Yes."

"You took my wedding day. I found the gap in my own life and named it grief without knowing what for."

"Yes."

Corvin hit him. It was not a hard blow—an old man's blow, more indignation than force—but it caught Bram across the jaw and sat him back down in the uncreaking chair, and it was, Bram thought dimly, the most honest transaction the guild hall had hosted in thirty years.

"Now that's settled," Corvin said, rubbing his knuckles. "What do we do?"

* * *

What they did, in the days that followed, was the work of taking apart a lie carefully enough that the pieces might still be useful.

Hesta opened the ledger and read every name aloud, including her own, and wept only twice, which those who knew her agreed was a record. Sable organized the evacuation—not a flight, she insisted, but a *walk*, orderly and remembered, with journals so that no one would lose the road to forgetting. Tamsin and Rook catalogued the ward-stones, marking which were merely sleeping and which had gone dark forever.

And Bram began to die.

He had known for some time—he'd been coughing blood into a handkerchief since midsummer and hiding it with the same practiced ease with which he'd hidden everything else—but knowing a thing and admitting it were different skills, and he had only ever been good at the first. Now, with the moon rising and his guild walking itself toward whatever came next, he let the second skill catch up to him. He took to his bed in the little room behind the archive, the one that smelled of dust and lamp oil and ninety years of other people's secrets.

On the fourth night, the messenger-wisp came.

It arrived through the wall like a moth made of moonlight, no bigger than his palm, pulsing gently—the old guild craft, the carrying-light, the spell every Fairweather child learned before they learned letters. Wisps carried memories. That was their whole purpose and their whole danger: a wisp could hold a moment of a life, whole and warm, and deliver it into another mind like a coal passed hand to hand.

Bram had sent hundreds of wisps in his time. Removal wisps, mostly. Little grey moths carrying stolen moments away to the vaults.

This one would be different.

He worked through the night, because the craft required the memory to be given freely or it spoiled, and Bram intended—for once, for the first time—to give freely. He laid his hands over the wisp's soft light and fed it, deliberately, piece by piece.

He gave it the third keystone. That was the practical part, the part Odile needed: *the Court Beneath, beneath the hollow hill past the drowned shrine and the mountain tooth, down the stair that only appears when both keystones are set in a living hand. The keystone sits enthroned where the court's heart used to beat. It cannot be taken. It can only be returned—carried out by someone the court remembers loving. Remember that, girl. Remembering is the whole of it.*

He gave it the warnings. *Serel will be there. Serel of the Hollow Court, who loved Mirabel before the dark ate his remembering. Do not kill him if you can avoid it. He is not your enemy. He is what my cowardice looks like when it wears armor.*

And then, because a wisp given freely holds everything the giver holds, whether he means it to or not, it took the rest.

It took the image of Odile at six years old, standing at the outermost marker in the rain, holding her little copper rod perfectly straight because she believed—she *believed*, with her whole small stubborn heart—that the valley's safety depended on her posture. Bram had watched her from the archive window and thought, with a tenderness that had frightened him: *this one asks questions. This one will break everything open.* And so he had begun, gently, patiently, the work of making her invisible. Not because she was worthless. Because she was *dangerous*, and he had confused the two for so long they had worn each other smooth.

It took the memory of Marta on her deathbed, gripping his wrist: *"Someone else will act, Bram. Someone always does. That's the covenant's cruelest trick—it waits until the cowards are sure they've gotten away with it."*

It took the taste of the forget-moss tea he'd served Mirabel, sweet and vile, and the look on her face as she drank it—grateful, *grateful*, because she had wanted so badly to stop hurting.

And it took, last of all, the apology itself, which was not eloquent. Bram had written eleven drafts and burned ten, and what remained was this, plain as a fence post:

*Odile Fairweather. I do not ask forgiveness, because forgiveness is a door and I have locked too many doors in my life to presume one opened for me. But hear this: you were never overlooked. You were hidden, and hiding is a thing done to precious things. Everything I took from you, I took because you shone, and I could not bear the light, and I mistook my blindness for your smallness. Go to the Court Beneath. Set the keystones. Reforge what I broke. And when you stand before the covenant and it asks for your sacrifice—know that a foolish old man, too late and by half, believed you equal to it.*

The wisp swelled with all of it, glowing gold-white now instead of grey, heavy as a full purse. It circled the room once, twice, brushing against his cheek like a question.

"Go to her," Bram whispered. "Past the markers. Follow the broken ward-line south. She'll leave a trail—you'll see. The girl never could do anything halfway."

The wisp hovered a moment longer, and Bram could have sworn it hesitated, the way living things hesitate at thresholds. Then it slipped through the wall and was gone, a spark dwindling over the valley, over the cold marker stones, out past the ward-line into the colorless dark—carrying, at last, a truth instead of a theft.

Bram lay back. The cough came, and this time he didn't bother with the handkerchief. Through the window, the thirteenth moon hung directly overhead now, swollen and pale, and as he watched, its edge seemed to *sharpen*, like a blade being turned.

"Well," he said to the empty room, to the ledger, to the ghosts of every elder who had ever sat in the uncreaking chair. "Too late and by half. But not by more than half."

He closed his eyes. Somewhere far to the south, he liked to believe, a wisp was finding a girl who asked questions, and a fugitive with no shadow, and pressing a dying man's memory into their hands like a coal passed hand to hand—

—and somewhere closer, much closer, down in the valley where the wards had failed, the first of the dark's servants stepped over the outermost marker stone, and the thirteenth moon began, very slowly, to fall.
