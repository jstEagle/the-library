## 19. Keystone: The Court Beneath

The stair down into the hollow court had been built for processions.

I knew that the way I knew my own hands—by their absence. There should have been banners, I thought, watching Odile's boots find each worn step ahead of me. There should have been lanterns lit with starfire, and singers in the galleries, and a hundred voices crying welcome as we descended. Instead there was only the dark, patient as a held breath, and the sound of two small figures walking into the belly of everything I had abandoned.

"You're doing the thing," Odile said, without turning around.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you stare at the back of my head like it owes you an apology."

"It might," I said. "I'm still deciding."

She snorted—a human sound, inelegant and warm—and kept climbing down. That was the thing about Odile Fairweather that no one had warned me about: she made the dark feel like a room you were sharing rather than a mouth you were entering. Nine days on the road that forgets itself, and she still stopped to name flowers that had gone gray with grief. She still argued with the ferryman's ghost about the fairness of his prices. She still, gods help me, hummed.

"Tell me again what we're walking into," she said now, because she always asked twice. Once for the facts, once for the fear. I had learned to honor both.

"A throne room," I said. "Or what's left of one. The third keystone sits where my mother's crown used to rest—on the seat itself, if the old law holds. The devoured stars are kept there too. All of them. Every light the dark has swallowed since the covenant began to fail."

"And Serel?"

I was quiet a moment too long, and she heard it. She always heard it.

"Serel will be there," I said. "He is the last knight the Hollow Court has left, which means he is the last knight who remembers how to kneel. Whatever serves the dark wears him like armor now. It will not let him step aside simply because we ask nicely."

"Have we ever asked anything nicely?"

"Once," I said. "In a village where nobody could remember yesterday. You asked the ferryman nicely for passage, and he took one of your true stories and gave us the boat."

"He took my story about the marker stone," she said, softer. "The one where I was eight and thought the wards were singing to me." Her hand went briefly to her coat pocket, where she kept the ledger-page fragment she'd stolen from her own guild's archive—the page that said her life had been edited, trimmed, folded down into something compliant. "It felt strange, giving it away. Like paying with a finger."

"That is what memory is," I said. "A finger of the soul. Most people never notice until someone takes one."

* * *

The stair ended at a door the size of a cathedral's heart.

It had been silver once—I remembered that much, remembered doors that sang when they opened, tuned to the footsteps of those who belonged. Now the metal was black, and when Odile pressed her palm against it, it did not sing. It exhaled. A long, low note, like something waking from a sleep it had not agreed to.

"It knows you," Odile whispered.

"It knew me," I corrected. "There's a difference. The door remembers Mirabel of the Starlit Court. I'm not entirely sure she survived the trip."

"Then we'll introduce ourselves," Odile said briskly, and pushed.

I have lived a long time by fae reckoning, long enough to watch mountains decide to be valleys, and I will tell you plainly: nothing in all those years undid me like watching a human woman with ash on her sleeves shove open the door of my dead homeland as though it were a tavern door and she were late for supper.

Beyond it lay the court.

Oh, it was beautiful still. That was the cruelest part. Beauty outlives its purpose the way a ring outlives the hand that wore it. The pillars rose up into darkness that should have held a ceiling and held only sky instead—sky without stars, sky like a lid screwed down over a grave. The floor was obsidian, polished by ten thousand years of dancing feet, and it reflected us back at ourselves: two small figures, one gray-skinned and hollow-eyed, one human and stubbornly, defiantly warm-colored, like a coal refusing to go out.

And there, upon the dais at the far end, sat the keystone.

It was smaller than I had expected. Smaller than the river gate's drowned shard, smaller than the mountain tooth we had bled and wept to carry down from the trial-cliffs. A simple wedge of pale stone, no bigger than a loaf of bread, resting on the throne where crowns are set. Around it, suspended in the air like fruit on an invisible tree, hung the devoured stars. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Each one dim, each one wrapped in gauze of shadow, but each one—when I looked closely, when I let myself look—still faintly glowing. Still burning, somewhere underneath. Still *there*.

"They're alive," Odile breathed. She had gone very still, the way she did when the world handed her something too large to hold all at once. "Mirabel. They're not dead. They're *kept*."

"Kept," I said, and the word tasted like iron. "Yes. That's what the dark does. It doesn't destroy. Destruction would be mercy. It keeps things. It keeps them the way my guild—" I caught myself. "The way *your* guild kept memories. In ledgers. In jars. Labeled and shelved and owned."

Odile looked at me then, and I saw her do the arithmetic she was always doing: the guild that raised her, the dark that ate the sky, and somewhere between them, the same appetite. The hunger to hold what should be free.

"So we take them back," she said.

"So we take them back," I agreed. "And then we run very fast, because—"

"I know," she sighed. "Because nothing this easy is ever actually easy."

From the shadows between the pillars, Serel stepped out.

* * *

He had been beautiful once. I say that not as vanity or nostalgia but as evidence: the Hollow Court bred its knights for grace the way orchards breed trees for fruit, and Serel had been the finest of the harvest. Tall even for our kind, with hair like poured moonlight and eyes the color of deep water over deep stone.

The dark had kept the shape and taken everything else. He moved now with a puppet's economy, no wasted motion, no grace at all—grace requires choice, and choice had been eaten. His armor was black glass. His sword was drawn. And his eyes, when they found me across that vast dark floor, held nothing whatsoever.

Nothing, and then—one flicker. One heartbeat of something that might have been confusion, or pain, or the ghost of a man trying to remember why the sight of me made his chest hurt.

"Mirabel," he said. My name came out of him flat as a coin dropped on stone. "The covenant-breaker. The fled signatory. You are commanded to stop."

"Commanded by whom?" I asked. I kept my voice level. Beside me, Odile had gone taut as a bowstring; I could feel her gathering herself, ready to do something reckless and human and probably involving that iron poker she'd carried since the moor. "Who gives the command, Serel? Say its name."

"There is no name," he said. "There is only the keeping."

"There is always a name," I said. "That is the first law of both our realms. Nothing exists unnamed. Whatever holds your leash, it named itself something, once, before it learned to be ashamed of it."

His sword arm trembled. Just slightly. Just enough.

"Stand aside," Odile said. She stepped forward—not in front of me, but beside me, shoulder to shoulder, which was somehow more frightening to witness than any charge. "We don't want to fight you. We want the keystone and the stars. Let us pass, and maybe we can even give you yours back."

"Give me—" His brow furrowed, a crack in the glass. "Give me what?"

"Yourself," Odile said simply. "You've been robbed, Serel. Same as me. Same as every kid my guild ever raised on rote and forgetting. Someone reached into you and took out the parts that made you *you*, and left the obedience behind like a shell." She was walking forward as she talked, slow, palms visible, the way you approach a spooked animal. I wanted to scream at her to stop. I did not stop her. I had learned, these nine days, that Odile Fairweather walked toward the things that frightened her, and that this was not bravery exactly—it was more fundamental than bravery. It was simply how she was built. "And here's the thing I learned about stolen things," she went on. "They can be returned."

"You carry nothing," Serel said. His voice was fraying at the edges. "You are human. You have no magic worth the name."

"No," Odile agreed. "But I'm carrying hers." She glanced back at me, and her eyes were wet and furious and absolutely certain. "Aren't I?"

And I understood.

Gods forgive me, I understood, and I almost refused—because what she was asking was not strategy. It was not mercy as tactic. It was mercy as *weapon*, the sharpest thing either realm had ever forged, and it would cost her.

"Are you certain?" I asked her quietly. In our tongue, in the old tongue, so that Serel could not follow. "What you're holding. What I gave you on the mountain. If you spend it here—"

"Then it goes where it's needed," she said. "That's the whole point of carrying things, isn't it? Otherwise you're just hoarding." She smiled at me, wobbly and bright. "Go on. Tell him. Tell him everything."

So I did.

I stepped past her, out onto the obsidian floor, alone before the last knight of my broken court, and I spoke his memory aloud.

I told him of the night we met, three hundred years ago, at the festival of first frost—how he had stepped on my hem and apologized so many times I finally laughed just to make him stop. I told him how we had watched the comet of the Red Year from the western tower, shoulder to shoulder under a sky so full of stars it seemed the dark didn't stand a chance. I told him how he had asked me, shyly, whether I thought stars ever got lonely, and how I had answered that they couldn't possibly, because look at them—all those lights, all together, holding the whole sky up between them.

I told him that he had loved me. Not possessively, not desperately—gently, the way you love a season. And that I had known, and that I had left anyway, when the stars began to die, because I thought running was the same as fighting, and I had been wrong, and I had carried that guilt up a mountain and handed it to a human girl to hold for me because I could not bear the weight myself.

"And she carried it," I told Serel, and my voice broke, and I let it. "She carried mine so that I could walk. And now she is giving it back to you—not the guilt. The *memory*. The true one. The one they scooped out of you and replaced with obedience. Take it back, Serel. It was always yours."

Odile opened her hand.

The memory rose from her palm like a spark rising from a banked fire—a small, warm light, gold-edged, humming with the festival of first frost and the comet of the Red Year and a young knight asking if stars get lonely. It drifted across the space between them, and Serel dropped his sword—it rang against the obsidian like a bell tolling—and caught it in both hands the way you catch something living.

The change was not dramatic. That is the truth I will swear to, whatever histories get written. There was no blast, no shattering, no cry to the heavens. He simply stood there, holding a little light, and his face—his frozen, glassy, puppet face—*thawed*. Slowly, from the eyes outward, like ice on a spring pond. And when he finally looked up at me, his eyes were deep water over deep stone again, and they were full of three hundred years.

"Mirabel," he said. But it was a different word this time. It was heavy with festivals and towers and comets, and underneath it, grief, and underneath the grief, something that had waited so long to be felt that it came out as barely a whisper:

"You came back."

"I came back," I said.

"Why?" And then, before I could answer, his gaze went past me—to Odile, standing small and resolute on the threshold of my ruined home, having just given away the most precious thing she carried. "And who is *she*?"

"She's the reason," I said. "Both reasons. Come and meet her properly. You're going to like her. Everyone does. It's insufferable."

* * *

Serel wept, briefly and privately, facing a pillar, the way soldiers do. Then he turned around and was a knight again—but a different kind of knight, one who had chosen his kneeling, and the difference showed in how he stood.

"The keystone," he said. "Take it. Quickly. The dark will feel his servant break—it felt it already, I think. The moment your friend gave the memory back, something below us... shifted." He paused. "There is something else you must know. The stars are not merely stored here. They are being *used*. Fed upon, slowly, one dream at a time. That is how the dark sustains itself. If you take the keystone and free them all at once—"

"It'll be hungry," Odile said grimly. "And it'll know exactly who to blame."

"It will know exactly where to look," Serel corrected. "Which is worse."

"Fantastic," Odile muttered. "I miss wards. Wards were boring. Boring was underrated."

I crossed the floor to the dais. Up close, the keystone was warm—that surprised me, though perhaps it shouldn't have. Everything true is warm, in my experience. It sat on the throne like a bird that had landed there by accident and wasn't sure it was allowed to stay. When I lifted it, the devoured stars around me stirred in their shadow-gauze, hundreds of tiny lights flickering like a field of fireflies waking, and a sound went through the great hall that was not a sound so much as a *leaning*—the whole ruined court, the pillars, the dark sky, the buried hope of two realms, all leaning toward Odile Fairweather and the wedge of stone in my hands.

Two keystones we had. Here was the third. And beneath my feet, far below the foundations of my dead and lovely home, something enormous turned over in its sleep and began, slowly, to wake.

"Mirabel," Serel said quietly. "It's coming."

Above us, through the ceilingless dark, the thirteenth moon began to rise.
