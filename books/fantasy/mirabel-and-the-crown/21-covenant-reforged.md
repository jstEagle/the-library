## 21. Covenant Reforged

**Odile**

The Court Beneath had no ceiling, and that was the first mercy of it. After the long descent—down stairs of fused glass, through halls where devoured stars hung like dead lanterns in nets of black silk—they came at last to the signing floor, and above it there was only open dark, vast and waiting, as if the sky itself had been holding its place for them.

The final keystone sat enthroned on a dais of ash-grey stone, humming faintly, like a kettle someone had forgotten to take off the fire. It was smaller than Odile had imagined. All three of them were—the river gate's drowned silver, the mountain tooth's jagged granite, and this one, which looked almost ordinary, a wedge of pale quartz shot through with veins of light that pulsed when she breathed.

*When I breathe,* she thought. *It keeps time with me.*

She supposed that was what a covenant was, in the end. Something that kept time with you whether you noticed or not.

Mirabel stood beside her at the dais's edge, dressed in what remained of her court regalia—a gown of moth-wing grey, singed at the hem from the moor, a circlet she had found in the ruins and put on with hands that shook. Serel knelt ten paces behind them, his broken fetters scattered around him like shed skins, weeping without any apparent awareness that he was doing so. He had been weeping since Odile pressed his true memory back into his hands. She suspected he would weep for some time yet. Some things, once returned, could not be set down again.

"It wants us both," Mirabel said. Her voice was steady. Her hands were not. "A living signatory from each realm. Bound by willing sacrifice."

"I remember," Odile said. "You offer your immortality."

"And the covenant asks more of you." Mirabel turned to face her fully, and in the light of the keystone's veins her eyes were the exact grey-green of the valley after rain. "Every remaining memory of home. The guildhall. The marker stones. Elder Bram's voice. Your mother's—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Everything, Odile. Not the erased things. Those are already gone. The things you *have*. The ones you chose to keep."

The words went into Odile like cold water into a boot—slowly, then all at once.

She had known, of course. Since the mountain, since Bram's dying wisp had reached them on the road with its apology folded inside instructions, she had known what the price would be. Knowing a thing and hearing it spoken aloud by the person who would remain to witness your not-knowing were two very different weathers.

"Will it hurt?" she asked, because it seemed important to ask practical questions while she still had a self to ask them with.

"Yes," Mirabel said honestly. "And then it won't. That's the cruel part. You won't remember enough to grieve properly. You'll just feel—" she gestured vaguely at her own chest, "—this. A shape where something used to be."

"A shape," Odile repeated. Like the hollow where a tooth had been. Like the silence where a star had been. Everything in this whole wretched year came back to shapes left behind.

She thought about the ledger. *The Ledger of Forgetting*, with its neat columns and its thick page bearing her name. She had spent months furious about entries made without her consent—memories lifted out of her childhood like splinters, leaving her invisible even to herself. And now here she stood, about to do the same thing to herself, except this time—

Except this time she was choosing it. That was the entire difference. That was, apparently, everything.

"The guild taught us that duty was doing what you were told," she said slowly. "I think I've learned it's actually this. Doing what must be done, with your eyes open, because no one else will." She looked up at the open dark above them. "The stars didn't go out because they wanted to. They went out because everyone who should have tended them decided someone else would. I'm done being someone else."

Mirabel made a small sound—not quite a laugh, not quite a sob. "You're certain?"

"No," Odile said. "But certainty is for people with better information. I'm *willing*. I'm told that counts."

"It counts," Mirabel whispered. "In the old tongue of the covenant, willingness is the only coin accepted."

They stepped onto the dais together.

The keystone rose to meet them—or perhaps they sank toward it; the geometry of the place had stopped making polite sense some hours ago. Its veins of light flared, and Odile felt the other two keystones answer from the satchel at her hip, three notes sounding at once, a chord she felt in her teeth and her sternum and somewhere behind her navel.

"Say your part," she told Mirabel. "I'll say mine. And then—" she managed something that was mostly a smile, "—then you'd better remember enough for both of us."

"Oh, I intend to," Mirabel said fiercely. "I intend to be insufferable about it. Every detail. I'll tell you how you took your tea until you want to drown me in the river gate."

"That's the spirit."

Mirabel lifted her hands. Light gathered along her fingers—not the borrowed glow of the keystone but something older, silvery, hers, the first magic Odile had seen her wield without cost or collapse. Whatever an immortal gives up, apparently, it buys back everything else.

"I am Mirabel of the Star-Born Court," she said, and her voice filled the hollowed hall the way water fills a cup, "last signatory of the old covenant, and I renounce my deathlessness. Let my years be counted. Let my endings come. In exchange: let the sky be lit, let the border hold, let the two realms keep faith as long as either has someone willing to stand at the line and *remember why*."

The chord deepened. Odile felt it pull at her now, gently, like a tide taking hold of an ankle.

She closed her eyes. And because she was Odile Fairweather, who had spent her whole overlooked life noticing what others missed, she did not reach for the grand memories first. She reached for the small ones, deliberately, one by one, holding each up to the light like a woman inventorying a house before handing over the keys.

The smell of the guildhall's morning porridge, slightly burnt, always slightly burnt. The way Elder Wren hummed off-key during censuses. The exact weight of the outermost marker stone under her palms when she tended it alone. Her mother's laugh, low and surprised, the day she finally stopped swallowing water and swam.

*Goodbye,* she thought to each of them. *Thank you. I'm sorry I have to spend you.*

Then she spoke her part.

"I am Odile Fairweather of the Valley Guild," she said, "and I give what I have kept. Let the sky be lit. Let the border hold. Let the two realms keep faith—and let me be the one who stands at the line and remembers why, even if I must learn the why anew every morning, even if I never learn it whole."

"Witnessed," said Mirabel, tears streaming freely down her face now, unashamed. "Witnessed and kept."

The covenant took her.

There was no other word for it. The light rose up through the dais, through her boots, her spine, her skull, warm and total, and Odile felt herself being *read*—every memory lifted, weighed, thanked, and spent, poured out like coins into a bottomless bright purse. She tried to hold on. She held on to the last one as long as she could, the small one, the burnt porridge, because it seemed right to end on something humble—

And then it was gone, and she was standing on a dais in a strange beautiful hall, and the sky above her was filling with stars.

* * *

**Mirabel**

Later—much later, though how much later Mirabel could not have said, for time in a reforging court moves like honey off a spoon—she would remember the sound.

Not the thunder of the stars reigniting, though there was thunder; the whole sky cracked alight at once, star after star after star, billions of small fires catching their breath in sequence, and the devoured ones torn free from the dark's nets and hung back in their places, blinking, astonished, alive. Not the roar of the court around them shaking off its ash, walls remembering their colors, floors remembering their polish. Not even Serel's cry, which was half grief and half joy and entirely human-adjacent, whatever he was now that he was neither bound nor boundless.

No. What Mirabel remembered was the silence of the girl beside her.

Odile stood amid all that returning light with her hands loose at her sides and her face utterly blank, and she was crying—great silent tears, running down and off her chin, dripping onto the keystone at her feet—and she clearly, terribly, had no idea why.

"Odile?" Mirabel caught her arm. "Odile, look at me. It's done. We did it. The sky—"

Odile looked at her. And there was nothing in her face. No recognition, no wariness, no warmth, none of the dry skeptical humor that had carried them across a forgetting country. She looked at Mirabel the way one looks at weather. At furniture. At a stranger.

"Do I know you?" she asked politely.

The words went through Mirabel like a spear through wet paper.

*I knew this would happen,* she told herself. *I agreed to this. She agreed to this. This is the bargain, this is the price, this is—*

But knowing a thing and seeing it were two very different weathers. She had warned Odile of exactly that, hours or years ago, on a moor, over a fire. She hadn't understood it until now.

"You knew me," Mirabel said, and her voice cracked in the middle like a dropped plate. "We crossed the whole world together. You pulled me out of an ash-field. You gave away your swimming, your mother, your—"

"I don't—" Odile frowned, gentle and baffled, wiping at her cheeks with the heel of her hand. "Why am I crying? There's nothing sad. Is something sad? The stars are lovely."

"They are," Mirabel managed. "They're very lovely."

Above them the last of the devoured stars settled into place, and the great hall filled with a light like early morning, and Odile Fairweather stood in the middle of it, empty-handed and weeping at nothing, and Mirabel understood with terrible clarity that this was the moment the whole journey had been building toward. Not the signing. Not the sacrifice. This. The aftermath. The part no covenant ever wrote terms for.

The guild had solved this problem before, she realized. Badly. They had solved it with ledgers and erasure and children raised on holes. But there was another way. There had always been another way, if anyone had been brave enough to try it.

Memory could be taken. Memory could also be *given*.

"Odile," she said. "What's your name?"

Odile blinked. "I—" A pause. A genuine, frightened pause. "I don't know. I know that I had one. I can feel the shape of it. It's—" her hand drifted to her chest, to the hollow, "—it's gone too, isn't it? I paid it. I paid everything."

*Oh, you foolish brave girl.* Mirabel took both her hands. They were cold. They had always been cold; some things survived even covenants.

"Then I'll tell you a secret," Mirabel said. "Names aren't found. They're given. That's the oldest law in either of our realms, older than the covenant, older than the stars. My court forgot it. Your guild perverted it. But the law itself is simple: a name spoken in love, witnessed and kept, is more binding than any birthright."

"I don't understand."

"You don't need to. You only need to listen." Mirabel drew a breath, and reached—not for her own memories, which were hers to keep, but for something new. Something that had not existed until this moment and belonged to no ledger anywhere. "I, Mirabel of the Star-Born Court, signatory of the renewed covenant, do hereby speak a naming. Witness me, sky. Witness me, stones. Witness me, Serel, stop sniveling and pay attention."

From behind them, wetly: "I'm witnessing."

"This woman," Mirabel said, and her voice steadied as she went, gathering strength from the words themselves, "crossed a forgetting country to mend a sky she had never seen. She paid her whole past for a future she will not remember earning. She asked practical questions at the end of the world. She was kind to strangers when the law said turn them away, and she was kind to *me*, and I was the worst kind of stranger there is. I cannot give her back what she gave. But I can give her this."

She leaned close, forehead nearly touching Odile's, and spoke the name.

Not Fairweather. That name belonged to the valley, to the guild, to the ledger with its thick pages—it had been an inheritance, and inheritances were exactly what had failed everyone. This name would be a promise instead. It had no history. It had only a future. It meant, in the old tongue of the star-born, roughly: *the one who stays.*

"Oriel," Mirabel said. "Your name is Oriel. It's yours. Freely given, freely kept. And every morning you forget why you trust me, I will tell you again, and every evening you wonder why you stayed, I will show you the sky, and eventually—" her voice broke and she let it, "—eventually the telling will become its own kind of memory. A chosen one. The first entry in a book no one else gets to write in."

Odile—Oriel—stood very still.

The tears had stopped. She was looking at Mirabel with an expression that was not recognition, not yet, but was its first cousin: the look of a person hearing a song they've never heard and somehow knowing the next note.

"Oriel," she repeated, testing it. Then, with the ghost of something dry surfacing through the blankness, something that made Mirabel's heart lurch with hope: "Is there a surname? Or do I get to earn that?"

Mirabel laughed—wet, undignified, wonderful. "Earn it. Yes. Exactly that. You'll earn it."

"Good." Oriel looked up at the blazing sky, at the hall remembering its colors around them, at the kneeling knight and the humming keystones, and her brow furrowed slightly. "One question. Practical, I'm told I ask those."

"Anything."

"Why does my chest hurt? There's a shape in it. Like a tooth socket." She touched her sternum. "Something lived there. Something good, I think. I can tell it was good, because missing it hurts in a *grateful* way. Does that make sense?"

Mirabel's throat closed. She mastered it.

"It makes perfect sense," she said. "And I'll tell you what lived there. Slowly. Over tea. Over years. And someday, if you want, we'll write it all down in a journal—your journal, your memories, chosen this time, every one consented to—and the first entry will already be written."

"By whom?"

"By me. In my handwriting. It says: *Today I met a girl in an ash-field who broke the rules to feed me soup.*"

Oriel considered this. The stars wheeled overhead, brand-new and ancient. Somewhere far away, a valley slept beneath a lit sky, its wards humming softly, keeping time.

"That sounds," Oriel said at last, "like the beginning of a very good story."

"It is," Mirabel said. "I've read ahead. Trust me."

And offering her arm—an ambassador's gesture, a friend's gesture, a gesture whose meaning Oriel could not yet recall but received anyway, like sunlight through a window whose glass she couldn't remember installing—she led the last signatory of the covenant out of the Court Beneath, up toward the morning, and home.
