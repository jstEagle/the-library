## 1. The Valley That Forgot

The snow came down the way it always did in Hollowmere: without ceremony, without mercy, and without a single flicker of magic to soften it.

Vesper Thackeray had been walking in it since dawn, and by midmorning her boots had soaked through to the second layer of wool, her breath had frozen twice on the scarf across her mouth, and her mood had settled into the particular gray quiet she wore like armor whenever the company crossed back into the valley. Not that anyone would have called it a mood. Doss said she had two faces—the one for contracts and the one for everything else—and both of them looked, from the outside, exactly the same.

"Pass's clear," called Tam Wren from up ahead, his voice flattened by the falling white. He stood at the crest of the old cart road where it bent around the shoulder of Mount Sorrel, one hand shading eyes already red-rimmed with cold. "No drifts worth cutting. No wolves either."

"Wolves are smart," Vesper said, coming up beside him. "They know better than to work this road in winter. It's only fools and freight that come through now."

"And us," Tam said, grinning through the ice in his beard.

"Us," she agreed, and let him have the joke, because that was how the company worked—small kindnesses passed hand to hand like flint and steel, struck often enough that nobody thought about what they were for until the night they were needed.

Below them, Hollowmere opened up like a cupped palm.

The valley ran eight miles end to end, cradled between the Sorrel range and the lower, darker teeth of the Grayfell hills, and in winter it filled with cloud the way a bowl fills with milk. From the pass you could see all of it at once: the frozen ribbon of the Mere itself, black under its lid of ice; the fields lying fallow and white; the smoke of a hundred hearths rising straight up in the windless cold; and at the valley's heart, the town of Hollowmere Proper, huddled around its market square and its records-house and its shrine—which was not a shrine anymore, just a roofed stone archway where people used to leave offerings and now mostly left nothing at all.

Twenty years ago, Vael's voice had gone out of the world here. Not just here—everywhere, if you believed the scholars, though the scholars had never had to live inside the silence the way Hollowmere had. The god of hearths and held oaths, the god whose name was carved over every door lintel in the valley, the god to whom Vesper had sworn herself at fourteen with a wax candle and a bleeding thumb—he had simply stopped answering. No thunder. No sign. One morning the prayers went up and nothing came back down, and the world outside had shrugged and moved on to newer gods, and Hollowmere had stayed, holding its dead god's name in its mouth like a stone.

Magic didn't work here. That was the other thing, the thing the pilgrims would learn soon enough if the rumors proved true. Hedge-witches' charms curdled. Blessed relics went dull as river stones. A healer who'd come through six years back had tried a mending-salve on Tam's split knuckles and watched it foam and die like soap in grease. Whatever current ran through the rest of the world, it did not run through Hollowmere. The valley was a hole in the weave, a dead tooth in the living jaw of creation, and the people who lived there had made their peace with it the way you make peace with bad soil or a lame leg: you stopped talking about it, and you got on.

Vesper touched the medallion at her throat without meaning to. Blank iron, worn smooth, no sigil stamped on it—because when Vael fell silent, his mark had faded off every consecrated piece of metal in the valley, and hers had gone blank along with them. She'd kept it anyway. Everyone in the company knew better than to comment. Mostly.

"You're doing the thing," Tam said.

"What thing."

"The medallion thing. Rubbing it like a lucky coin." He started down the switchback, boots crunching. "You know what Doss says. Dead gods don't pay out."

"Doss also says he can drink Brannic under the table, and we've all seen how that ends."

She followed him down into the cloud, and the valley closed over them like water.

* * *

The contract was simple, which was why they'd taken it. Escort a wool merchant named Aldous Fenn and his two wagons from the pass down to Hollowmere Proper, then stand over him while he sold his stock at the winter market, then see him back up to the high road three days hence. Forty crowns, half up front, paid in coin that hadn't been clipped. In a hard season, it was honest bread.

Fenn himself rode the lead wagon with the nervous energy of a man who talked when he should have been silent, which was most merchants, which was why the company charged extra for their company.

"No trouble on the road?" he asked as they came alongside. "I heard there was trouble on the road."

"There's always trouble on the road," Vesper said. "That's why you hired us."

"I heard there was—" He lowered his voice, glancing at the wagons behind him, at his own driver, as if the snow might be listening. "I heard there was a man at the Salt Latch, up past the pass, saying prayers were being answered again. Out east. In Corvale, maybe. Whole villages, he said. Candles lighting themselves. Sick children waking whole."

The words dropped into the cold air and hung there.

Tam stopped chewing the strip of dried apple in his cheek. Behind them, Brannic—big, slow, gentle as a plow horse until he wasn't—shifted in his saddle and looked at Vesper without looking at her, the way soldiers do.

"That so," Vesper said.

"That's what he said." Fenn's laugh came out thin. "Drunk, probably. Or one of those new-preacher types, stirring up custom before spring. You can't believe half of—"

"No," Vesper said. "You can't."

But she'd felt it. Not belief—something older and less comfortable than belief. A prickle at the base of her skull, the feeling of being watched from a room you thought was empty. Because if prayers were being answered again out east, in Corvale, in the living world beyond the pass, then the silence that had defined her entire adult life was not a law of nature after all. It was a local condition. And conditions could change.

She didn't say any of this. She rode, and the wagons creaked, and the snow kept falling with the patience of something that had all the time in the world.

* * *

Hollowmere Proper smelled the way it always smelled in deep winter: woodsmoke and wet wool and the mineral breath of the frozen mere, and under it all, faintly, the tallow-and-onion smell of the Gilded Ewe, the tavern where the company always lodged because the beds were clean, the ale was cheap, and the landlady, Marta Quill, asked no questions that couldn't be answered with coin.

They delivered Fenn to the Woolwrights' Rest, took their half payment, saw the wagons stabled, and by late afternoon the four of them—Vesper, Doss, Tam, and Brannic—were installed at their usual corner table in the Ewe's common room, close to the fire and closer to the door, which was the only seat in any room Vesper ever truly trusted.

Doss sat with his back to the wall, as always. He was fifty-one and looked older, gray going to white at the temples, a face like a map of somewhere unpleasant. Twenty-two years leading the company, and he still checked the window latches before bed. He was drinking small beer and pretending it was large, which was his way of staying sharp.

"Three days," he said. "Fenn sells, Fenn loads, Fenn leaves. Easy wages."

"Easy wages," Tam agreed, and put his feet up on the hearthstone until Marta threw a rag at him.

The common room filled as the light died. Farmers in from the outlying steadings, come to trade before the deep snow sealed the side roads. A pair of charcoal-burners with forearms black to the elbow. The miller's sons, loud and flushed. Ordinary people carrying ordinary winter, and Vesper watched them with the particular attention she gave all rooms—not threat assessment, exactly, but something adjacent to it. Old habit. Older than the oath, almost.

It was nearly full dark when the stranger spoke.

He sat alone near the far wall, a drover by his coat, road-worn, with the hollowed look of a man who'd traveled farther than he'd meant to. He wasn't drunk. That was the first strange thing. The second strange thing was that he wasn't talking to anyone. He was staring into his cup and speaking low, the way men speak to themselves, except that what he was saying had a shape to it, a cadence, and Vesper realized with a slow cold turn of her stomach that he was praying.

"—and I asked, and it answered," he was murmuring. "By the Ember's grace, I asked, and it answered me. The fever broke. My daughter's fever broke, and the candle—it lit itself, I swear it on my life, the wick was black and cold and it lit itself—"

The room did not go quiet. That was the third strange thing, and the worst one. In any other tavern in the realm, those words would have turned every head. Here, in Hollowmere, in the valley that had mourned its god for twenty years, the drover's whisper simply... dissolved. Conversations continued. Dice kept rattling. Marta wiped the same spot on the bar twice. The miller's sons laughed at something that wasn't funny.

Everyone had heard. Everyone was pretending they hadn't.

Vesper looked at Doss. Doss was looking at his beer. Tam had gone very still, the way he did before a fight, except there was no fight coming. Brannic's big hands were flat on the table, motionless.

Only the drover seemed to know he'd said anything at all, and even he trailed off after a moment, as if hearing the silence press back against him, and buried his face in his cup, and did not speak again.

Vesper's chest hurt.

Not metaphorically—that was the thing she would remember later, turning it over in the dark of her room above the tavern, unable to sleep, listening to the wind worry the shutters. Her chest had actually hurt, a single deep ache behind the sternum, brief as a swallowed ember going down, there and gone before she could name it. She'd pressed her fist to her breastbone right there at the table, and Doss had glanced over, and she'd said "heartburn," and he'd believed her, or pretended to.

Now, alone, she took out the blank medallion and held it in her palm. Iron, smooth, featureless. For twenty years it had been exactly as heavy as iron should be, no more, no less.

She could have sworn it was warm.

"No," she said aloud, to the empty room, to the wind, to whatever might be listening. Her voice sounded very small. "You're dead. I watched you die. I grieved you. I built a whole life on top of your grave, and I am not digging it up for a drunk drover and a warm coin."

The medallion cooled under her fingers, or she imagined it did. Outside, the snow went on falling over Hollowmere, over the shrine with its empty archway, over the records-house where the ledgers slept, over the whole forgotten valley, patient as a held breath.

Downstairs, faintly, she heard the drover settle his bill and ask Marta, one last time, in a voice too low to carry:

"You're sure there's been nothing? Nothing strange at all?"

And Marta Quill, good soul, kind soul, lied to him gently and said no, nothing, nothing strange at all.

Vesper lay back and stared at the ceiling beams and told herself the ache in her chest was the cold, or the ale, or twenty years of habit wearing thin.

She almost managed to believe it.

Almost.
