## 2. Salt and Silence

The medallion hung against her sternum like a coin of dead water.

Vesper Thackeray had worn it for twenty years—long enough that the leather cord had been replaced four times and the silver had gone dark as a drowned man's lip. It was a plain thing, unadorned: a disc stamped with the sigil of Vael, god of hearths and thresholds, though the stamping had worn shallow now, so that in certain light the emblem looked less like a flame cupped in two hands and more like a wound that had learned to close.

She touched it the way other people touched lucky charms. Not for luck. For ballast.

"Thackeray." Doss's voice came down the line of the column, gravel over gravel. "You're doing the thing."

"I'm not doing a thing."

"You're rubbing your necklace like you're trying to start a fire off it. You do it when we take the salt road. Every time."

She dropped her hand and made herself look at the road instead. The salt road ran along the eastern rim of Hollowmere, white-crusted and glittering under a sky the color of old pewter, and the valley below it lay buried to its eaves in snow. Chimney smoke rose from the village of Cinderlow in thin gray threads, straight up, because there was no wind—there was never any wind in Hollowmere, or if there was, it moved like something apologetic, something that didn't want to be noticed. The whole valley had the stillness of a held breath. Twenty years of held breath.

"It's not a necklace," she said. "It's a medallion."

"Ah." Doss nodded with tremendous gravity. "A dead-god medallion. My mistake entirely."

Behind him, Ferren snorted into his scarf. Brakka said nothing, because Brakka never said anything before noon, but she adjusted the straps of her shield in a way that Vesper had learned, over six years, to read as laughter.

"You're all very funny," Vesper said. "Truly. When the work dries up I'm sure the traveling carnival will take you."

"We're funny because it's how we love you," Ferren said. He was the youngest of them, twenty-two and freckled even in winter, and he had the particular cruelty of the young, which is to say he meant every joke as an act of devotion. "Besides, everyone in this company has their grief. Yours just happens to be wearable."

Vesper let that one go unanswered, because it was too close to true, and because the column had reached the switchback where the road narrowed and the drop on the left went down three hundred feet of ice-glazed scree. Work first. Grief kept. That was the order of things, and she had built her whole life on the order of things.

They were escorting a salt merchant's caravan down into Cinderlow—six wagons, twelve mules, one nervous factor named Pell who kept looking at the sky as though it owed him money. Routine contract. The kind of work Hollowmere offered, because Hollowmere offered nothing else: roads watched, goods guarded, disputes settled at knifepoint or by negotiation, whichever came cheaper. No monsters. No sorcery. No miracles.

That was the bargain the valley had made, whether anyone had signed it or not. Magic did not follow here. Prayers went up from Hollowmere like sparks from a wet fire and came down again as nothing. The gods—the great wheeling names that the rest of the world swore by, Cindral of the Choir, Hravn of the Long Winter, the hundred lesser thrones—did not reach into this valley. And Vael, her Vael, hearth-lord, threshold-keeper, the god whose voice she had heard once, clearly, the way you hear your own name called across a field—

His voice had stopped. Mid-prayer. Mid-word.

And the world had gone on, and Vesper Thackeray had gone on, and the medallion had stayed around her neck like a question she refused to stop asking.

* * *

She had been fourteen, and it had been the night of the Kindling, the last night of autumn, when every household in Cinderlow set a candle in its window for Vael and left the door unlatched so the god could come in if he pleased.

Her mother had been dead three years by then; her father had followed the following spring, coughing his lungs out into a basin while the priest-archivist Maren Ostwick sat with them through the long nights, reading aloud from the Ledger of Hearths because Vael's scripture said no one should die without a story in their ears. Afterward Maren had taken her in—not adopted her, exactly, nothing so formal, but given her the loft above the records-house and a job dusting shelves and a place at his table, and that had been enough. That had been everything.

On the night of the Kindling, she had knelt at her window with her candle lit and her hands folded, and she had prayed the way only fourteen-year-olds pray: completely, shamelessly, holding nothing back.

*I have no one,* she had told him. *Maren is kind but he is old and I am afraid of the day the records-house is quiet and I am alone in it. I don't want much. I want to matter to something. I want to be chosen for something, even something small. Even just to be seen.*

And the candle flame had leaned toward her.

Not flickered—leaned, deliberately, the way a dog turns its head—and warmth had poured off it like breath, and a voice had filled the small room, a voice like a fire settling in a grate, low and crackling and impossibly gentle:

*Little wick. I see you. I have always—*

And then nothing.

Not silence the way a room is silent. Silence the way a snapped rope is silent. The flame had stood straight again, ordinary, indifferent, and the cold had come back into the room like water filling a footprint, and Vesper had knelt there until the candle burned down to a puddle and her knees went numb, saying his name into the dark. *Vael. Vael. Vael.*

Nothing. Nothing. Nothing.

In the morning she had asked Maren, carefully, casually, the way you ask about weather, whether the god had ever gone quiet before. The old archivist had looked at her for a long moment with an expression she couldn't read—she would spend years trying to read it, and fail—and had said, "Prayers are strange things, child. Sometimes they're answered in ways we don't recognize."

It wasn't an answer. She'd known even then it wasn't an answer. But she was fourteen, and grieving, and the alternative to accepting it was to accept that she had been abandoned mid-sentence by the only voice that had ever told her she was seen, and fourteen-year-olds cannot survive that. So she had accepted it. She had folded it up small and put it away, and she had taken the medallion from the shrine box—her father's, his father's before him, the hearth-mark stamped in silver—and she had put it on, and she had decided, with the terrible clarity of the young, that she would build a life that needed no choosing.

A life of contracts and wages. A life where worth was measured in completed jobs and kept oaths between people, which were at least oaths people could actually keep. A life where if she was going to be unseen, she would at least be useful, and if she couldn't be loved by heaven, she could be relied upon by mortals, which was colder but it held.

She joined a mercenary company at seventeen. She got good. She got very good. And somewhere in the years of it—somewhere between the border skirmishes and the caravan escorts and the winter she'd spent guarding a granary from starving men and hadn't slept properly since—Hollowmere's silence had soaked into her like salt into wood, until she and the valley were the same thing: soundless, preserved, waiting for a rot that never came.

* * *

"Earth to Thackeray." Ferren fell into step beside her as the column started down into the valley proper, boots crunching on rime. "You went somewhere."

"Checking the treeline."

"The treeline is a mile away and full of nothing."

"Then I checked it thoroughly."

He grinned. Snow had gotten into the creases of his face and made him look younger, somehow, which shouldn't have been possible. "Doss says we winter in Cinderlow. Says the factor's already talking about a spring run to Marrowford, and Marrowford pays triple what the valley does."

"Marrowford's outside the pass," Vesper said.

"So?"

"So nothing. It's fine." She said it too evenly, and Ferren noticed—he always noticed, that was his curse—but mercifully he let it lie, because the road had steepened and the mules were complaining and there was work to do.

But the truth of it sat in her chest all the way down the switchbacks, cold and specific: she did not leave the valley. In eleven years of soldiery she had turned down every contract that took her past the snowline, and she had constructed reasons for each refusal—rates, timing, the state of the roads—and every reason had been true the way a wall is true. Solid. Load-bearing. Built precisely to keep something out.

Because outside the valley, magic worked. Everyone knew it. Outside the pass, hedge-priests threw real charms and relics actually warmed and prayers were answered often enough that no one thought twice about it. And Vesper had never tested herself against that. Had never wanted to know what it felt like to stand in a place where the divine was a living current in the air and feel—what? Nothing? Or worse than nothing?

Or worse than worse: to feel *seen*, after twenty years of practicing not needing it.

No. Better the silence. Better the salt road and the dead medallion and the crew's easy mockery, which was a kind of love she could hold without burning her hands. She had made her peace. She had built her house on the silence and furnished it well.

That was what she told herself, anyway, right up until the evening the world cracked open.

* * *

They made Cinderlow by dusk. The village came up out of the whiteness gradually—first the smoke, then the roofs, then the amber windows, then the smell of woodsmoke and boiling salt-pork and the particular mineral tang of the evaporation sheds. The inn was the Hearthstone, three stories of black timber with a sign depicting a kettle that had never once in living memory boiled, and they took the common room by storm the way companies do: boots, noise, dice, the factor installed by the fire with a brandy he didn't touch.

Vesper sat with her back to the wall, as always, and ate, and let the noise wash over her. This was the other half of the life she'd built: this exact hour, the hour after arrival, when the work was done and the pay was promised and the company became a single loud animal around the tables. Doss was arguing economics with the innkeeper. Ferren was losing dice to Brakka with the serene confidence of a man who had never once won. Someone had started a song. The fire in the great hearth cracked and settled, and Vessel watched it the way she always watched fires—with a fondness she had never examined and did not intend to.

It was near midnight when the farmer came in.

She remembered later that she'd noticed him first as weather—a change in the pressure of the room. He was old, bent under wet snow, and he came through the door without knocking, which no one in Cinderlow did, and he stood there dripping with an expression Vesper had seen on faces in burning houses and drowning boats: the expression of a man who has run out of everywhere else to go.

"Tobbin," the innkeeper said. "God's sake, man, it's midnight—"

"My daughter." His voice came out shredded. "The fever. Three days. The physicker's done everything, there's nothing—" He stopped. Swallowed. And then, in front of forty strangers, in a valley where prayer had been a closed door for twenty years, Tobbin the farmer got down on his knees on the filthy floorboards of the Hearthstone inn and prayed.

"*Vael.*" The name cracked out of him like a bone breaking. "Hearth-lord. Threshold-keeper. I know—I know you don't answer here, I know it's been years, I know we're forgotten, but she's nine, she's *nine*, and I am asking—" His voice failed and rebuilt itself. "I am asking you to look at her. Just look at her. That's all. Look at my girl and tell me that's not worth one miracle."

Silence. The company had gone still; even the dice had stopped. Vesper found she was standing—she didn't remember standing—and her hand was pressed flat against the medallion, hard enough to hurt, and the fire in the great hearth had gone strangely, terribly quiet, the flames standing up straight and tall and attentive, like soldiers called to attention—

The candle on Tobbin's nearest table relit itself.

It had been snuffed hours ago, a guttered stub in a dish of tallow. Flame bloomed up out of the cold wick, small and steady and gold, and the light of it reached across the room and lay across the kneeling farmer's shoulders like a hand.

Tobbin made a sound that Vesper would hear for the rest of her life. He scrambled up, weeping, babbling thanks, and ran back out into the snow toward his daughter's house, and behind him the room erupted—chairs scraping, voices colliding, someone laughing, someone swearing, Ferren saying "*Did you see—*" and Doss saying "Sit *down*, all of you"—and through all of it Vesper stood frozen with her fist around a dead god's medallion while something inside her chest

stirred.

One heartbeat. That was all. One heartbeat in which the silence she had lived inside for twenty years turned over in its sleep, and a warmth spread outward from beneath her sternum like ink dropped in water, and a voice that was not a voice, that was older than voice, that was the memory of a voice, brushed the inside of her ribs and said—or almost said, or began to say, or *remembered saying*—

*Little wick.*

Then it was gone. The hearth-fire resumed its ordinary crackling. The candle on the table burned on, mundane, explicable, a trick, a fluke, a draft through a door someone had left open. Around her the company argued itself hoarse about what they'd seen, and the innkeeper brought out whiskey nobody had paid for, and by degrees the night reassembled itself into something like normalcy.

Vesper sat back down. She ate nothing. She drank nothing. Under the table, hidden from every eye, she held the medallion in both hands, and it was warm.

Not body-warm. Not pocket-warm. Warm the way a stone is warm that has lain all afternoon in sun, warm from somewhere else, warm as if something on the far side of it were breathing.

*Twenty years,* she thought. *Twenty years of silence, and the week I stop pretending I don't care, you choose to knock.*

She should have felt joy. Some part of her, some buried fourteen-year-old part, was screaming with it. But what she felt, mostly, sitting in the noise of her own company with her thumb moving slowly over the worn hearth-mark, was the feeling of ground shifting under a house she had spent two decades building.

Something was awake in the valley.

And whatever it was—it knew her name.
