## 3. A Prayer Answered

The candle had been dead for three days when the farmer knelt beside it.

Vesper noticed him because he was doing it wrong. Everyone in Hollowmere knew how to kneel to a silent god—low, quick, and without expectation, the way you'd nod to a grave. But this man, Halvard Brann by name, a barley farmer from the eastern terraces whose land had gone to rot in the wet autumn, knelt like a boy at his mother's knee. He knelt as if someone might answer.

She was standing at the taproom's edge with a mug of small beer she wasn't drinking, watching the snow come down past the windows of the Drowned Lamb in long, slow curtains. The tavern smelled of tallow smoke and wet wool and the particular sourness of beer kept too near the fire. Behind her, Doss was arguing with Tam about dice, and Brenna was asleep sitting up, which she could do anywhere, which Vesper had always privately envied.

Halvard Brann was dying. Everyone knew it. The wasting sickness had come through the eastern terraces that autumn, taking first the livestock and then the old men, and it had settled into Brann the way winter settles into a house—slowly, thoroughly, room by room. His skin had gone the color of the tallow candles. His hands shook so badly he'd had to set his mug on the floor between sips.

And now he was kneeling before a candle stub that had guttered out three days ago, in a valley where no prayer had been answered in twenty years, and he was praying aloud.

That was the wrong part. That was what made Vesper's hand tighten around her mug until her knuckles went white. You didn't pray *aloud* in Hollowmere. Not anymore. Not since the Silence. Praying aloud was like crying at a funeral where nobody else grieved—it marked you, and worse, it marked everyone who heard you, forced them to remember what they'd agreed to forget.

*Vael of the Hearth-Flame, keeper of the low fire, hear me—*

Brann's voice cracked on the second word. He started over. The tavern went quiet around him in that peculiar way taverns go quiet—not all at once but in rings, conversation dying nearest the kneeling man first and spreading outward like frost across a windowpane. Even Doss stopped arguing. Even Brenna opened one eye.

*—hear me, though I have not been faithful. Hear me, though the fire is cold. My wife is buried under the east rowan and my daughter is in Corren's Ford and I am alone in a house full of dark, and I cannot—I cannot do another winter alone, Vael, I cannot—*

His voice broke entirely. And Vesper felt something she hadn't felt in twenty years.

Not in her ears. Not in the air. In her chest—a heat behind her sternum, sudden and searing, like swallowing a coal whole. It flared once, bright and total, and in that single heartbeat of burning she understood three things with absolute clarity, the way you understand things in dreams or fevers:

Something was awake.

It had been asleep inside her—or hidden, or caged, she couldn't have said which—and it had heard Brann's prayer before Brann finished speaking it.

And it knew her name.

Not *Vesper*. Not the name Maren Ostwick had written in some ledger, the name the crew used, the name scrawled on escort contracts. The other name. The one underneath. It moved through her like a hand moving through water, formless and knowing, and it did not speak—there were no words—but she felt herself *recognized*, felt herself turned over and examined like a stone lifted from a riverbed after twenty years of current, and the recognition was so intimate and so vast that terror and longing hit her simultaneously and she could not tell them apart.

Then the candle lit.

No match. No taper carried from the hearth. The wick, blackened and bent, simply took flame—softly, without ceremony, the way a candle lights when a careful hand brings fire to it. A small yellow bloom in the dimness of the Drowned Lamb, throwing Brann's shadow huge against the far wall.

The silence afterward was total. Total in a way Vesper had never heard a room be silent—not held breath, not waiting, but something closer to the silence of animals before an earthquake. Every person in the Drowned Lamb had seen it. Every person had also, Veper realized with a strange distant clarity, already begun the process of deciding they hadn't.

Because that was the covenant of Hollowmere. Unwritten, unspoken, maintained for twenty years with the discipline of any faith: *the gods are silent, magic does not follow, we are a plain people in a plain valley.* The covenant had been their shelter. Break it, and what came in behind the broken wall would be everything they'd spent two decades refusing to name.

A woman near the door laughed—too loud, too sudden—and said something about a draft, about how the wind off the pass found every crack, and heads nodded, grateful, and the noise of the tavern resumed like water closing over a dropped stone. Conversation rebuilt itself brick by brick. Someone ordered more beer. Tam resumed cheating Doss at dice. The world performed its own continuity.

But Halvard Brann stayed kneeling. And Vesper stayed standing, both hands around her mug now, because if she let go of it she was afraid her hands would shake visibly, and the coal-heat behind her sternum was fading but not gone—it sank downward as it faded, settling somewhere deep beneath her heart like an ember raked into ash to hold overnight, banked, patient, *waiting*.

Brann rose at last, slowly, one hand braced on the bench. He looked at the candle for a long moment. Then he looked around the tavern—at all those deliberately turned faces—and Vesper saw something move across his ruined face that she recognized with terrible precision, because she'd worn the same expression for twenty years looking at her blank medallion: hope, immediately followed by the fear of having hoped.

He picked up the candle with both trembling hands, cupping the flame like a bird, and shuffled out into the snow.

Nobody followed him with their eyes except Vesper.

* * *

She didn't sleep that night. She told herself it was the beer, then the weather, then the coming contract—they were escorting a wool merchant's wagons down to Corren's Ford in two days' time, and the passes were filling with early snow. She lay in her bunk above the stable with her boots on, staring at the rafters, and the lie wore thin fast, because none of those things explained why her chest still felt warm.

Warm. Twenty years of cold and one heartbeat of fire, and now this residual warmth, faint as sun through south-facing glass, that she kept pressing her palm against as if she could confirm it or dismiss it. She couldn't do either. It was simply there, the way a bruise is there, announcing itself only when touched.

She thought about the day the voice died.

She'd been fourteen. She remembered it the way you remember wounds—with unbearable specificity and no useful perspective. Late autumn, same as now. She'd been at the shrine above the millpond, the little stone alcove where half of Hollowmere had sworn their oaths, and she'd been mid-prayer—the oath-prayer, the binding words every child of twelve summers spoke, though she'd been late to hers, Maren's fault, Maren's gentle endless patience—and the words had been *I give you my low fire, Vael, and ask your warmth in return,* and between one syllable and the next the god had simply

stopped.

Not faded. Stopped. Like a door closing. She'd stood in the alcove with the last word hanging unfinished in the cold air and waited, and waited, and the pond had frozen a little further out while she waited, and nothing had come back, ever, and she'd walked down to the records-house where Maren sat copying inventories and said, "He's gone," and Maren had put down his pen and looked at her for a long time with an expression she'd spent twenty years misreading as sympathy.

Grief, she'd thought. Maren grieving with her.

Now, lying in the dark above the stable, she turned that memory over and found its edges wrong. Because Maren hadn't been surprised. She'd known that even then, in the animal way children know things, and she'd buried the knowledge because the alternative was unbearable. But tonight, with a banked coal warming her sternum and a dead candle burning in her memory, the knowledge surfaced and would not go back down.

Maren Ostwick had not been surprised that the god went silent.

Maren Ostwick had looked at a fourteen-year-old girl standing in his doorway with a broken oath in her mouth, and his face had done something complicated and quick and *guilty*, and then he'd said, "Come in, child. Have some tea."

*Tea.* As if a god's death were an occasion for tea.

Vesper rolled onto her side. Below her, in the stable, the horses shifted and sighed. Snow ticked against the shutters. Somewhere out in the village a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

And the warmth in her chest pulsed.

Once. Gently. Like something turning over in its sleep.

She sat up so fast she cracked her head on a rafter, and sat there in the dark with her scalp singing, one hand pressed flat against her sternum, listening—to the stable, to the snow, to her own blood—and feeling utterly certain that whatever had answered Halvard Brann's prayer through her, whatever had turned her over like a river-stone and *known* her, was still in there. Still listening. Still waiting for her to be alone with it.

"Go back to sleep," she whispered, to it, to herself, to the rafters.

The warmth pulsed again, softer, almost apologetic.

She did not sleep.

* * *

In the morning the snow had stopped and the light came down off the mountains hard and white, and Hollowmere looked exactly as it always looked, which Vesper understood now was a performance, had always been a performance, a valley-wide pantomime of ordinariness maintained the way you maintain a dam—constantly, and against pressure.

She went down to the common room and the company was already at breakfast, and for one merciful moment everything was normal: Doss complaining about the wool merchant's rates, Tam stealing bread off Brenna's plate, Brenna letting him because she was planning something. Ryn was sharpening knives that didn't need sharpening, which is what Ryn did instead of talking.

"You look terrible," Doss said, by way of good morning.

"Didn't sleep."

"Heard about the candle," Tam said, mouth full, and the table went carefully casual, which is to say everyone became very interested in their food. "Whole village's talking about it. Draft, most likely. Wind off the pass finds every crack."

"That's what I heard," Vesper said.

"Draft," Brenna agreed solemnly, and stole the bread back.

They let it lie there, the word *draft*, like a coin on a table that everyone could see and nobody would pick up. Vesper loved them for it and hated the necessity of it, and ate her porridge, and felt the coal shift behind her sternum when she swallowed, and wondered—not for the first time, but for the first time with real fear—what would happen the next time someone in this valley prayed aloud.

She got her answer sooner than she wanted.

They were loading the merchant's wagons at midmorning, hauling bolts of wool and crates of salt pork up into the beds, breath steaming, boots squeaking on packed snow, when the screaming started from the lane behind the chandlery.

Not pain-screaming. Vesper knew pain-screaming; she'd catalogued every kind across fifteen years of contracts. This was the other kind, the rarer kind—the sound a person makes when reality comes apart in front of them. She rounded the wagon with her hand already on her knife and found a knot of villagers gathered at the mouth of the alley, and pushed through them, and stopped.

Halvard Brann lay in the snow at the end of the alley.

He'd collapsed there, apparently—on his way home from the Drowned Lamb, presumably, the candle still cupped in his two hands. He was dying now in earnest, the wasting sickness finishing in minutes what it had planned for weeks, and the villagers stood in a ring around him the way people stand around anything they can't touch, and his daughter wasn't there, and his wife was under the rowan tree, and he was alone in a house full of dark after all.

Except for the candle. Which was still burning. Had burned all night, somehow, in the open snow, in the wind, without going out.

And Brann, gray-faced, snow melting on his lips, was praying again. Aloud. Loudly enough now that there was no pretending, no draft, no wind off the pass. Loudly enough that the whole ring of villagers heard every word.

*Vael, I know you're there. I know what I saw. Don't take me in the dark—don't let the dark be the last thing—*

And Vesper's chest caught fire.

Not the banked coal this time. Not a pulse, not a warmth. Fire—searing, rising, filling her ribs like a lungful of flame, and this time it wasn't content to stay inside. Heat rolled off her skin. The snow around her boots began, very quietly, to melt—not everywhere, not dramatically, just in a neat circle perhaps two feet across, steam curling up off the wet cobbles, and nobody was looking at her, everyone was looking at Brann, and she stepped backward out of the circle before anyone could turn—

—and the candle flame *stood up*.

Straightened, lengthened, burned white-hot for one impossible instant, tall as a finger, bright as a forge-light, and every person in that alley saw it, and Brann saw it, and Brann smiled.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, there you are." And he closed his eyes, and died with the smile still on his face, in the light of a candle that would not go out.

The flame shrank back to ordinary size. Kept burning. Would not go out.

And inside Vesper's chest, the fire sank slowly, reluctantly, back down to its banked coals—and left behind, in the cooling, one clear and unmistakable impression, wordless as before but shaped, this time, like speech. Shaped like a name being spoken very softly, very close, by something that had been waiting twenty years for the chance.

Her name. The true one. The one under *Vesper*.

The one she'd never told anyone, because she'd never known it herself.

The crowd around the dead farmer began to murmur, and the murmur had a different texture than last night's pretense—this was awe curdling toward fear, fingers pointing not at the candle but at each other, at the sky, at the shrine above the millpond. Old Mrs. Corbet fell to her knees right there in the slush. Somebody was running for the priestless chapel. Somebody else was running, Vesper noted with cold professional clarity, for the records-house.

For Maren.

She backed away down the alley, out of the ring, unnoticed, her heart slamming, the melted circle of cobblestones steaming faintly behind her like a signature. Above the rooftops the mountains stood white and indifferent, and beyond them, beyond the pass, beyond the edges of everything she knew, the world was waking up—and something inside her had woken first, and it knew her name, and it was calling her by it.

She had to find Maren. She had to know what he'd done, what he'd hidden, what he'd been keeping from her since before she could remember.

The coal behind her sternum pulsed—patient, warm, and terribly awake—as she walked, fast, toward the records-house, and the snow began to fall again around her, melting, she noticed, a full arm's length before it touched her shoulders.
