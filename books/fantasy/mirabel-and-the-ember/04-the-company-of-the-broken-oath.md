## 4. The Company of the Broken Oath

Sergeant Aldous Doss had counted his men's fears the way other officers counted arrows, and by the third night after the candle in the Gilded Hearth relit itself, he was running low on reassurance.

He sat at the long table in the back room of the Wayhouse of the Nine Bells, a room the company had rented for the winter with coin earned escorting wool carts and one nervous tax collector through the pass. The fire burned low. Outside, snow came down in that patient, endless way Hollowmere snow did, as if the sky had all the time in the world and no intention of spending it anywhere else. Inside, eleven mercenaries argued about whether to abandon the only valley half of them had ever called home.

"—I'm not saying we run," said Brannic, who was absolutely saying they should run. He stood with his back to the fire, arms crossed over a chest like a beer barrel, and his voice carried the particular wounded dignity of a man who had been laughed at and intended to be right about it anyway. "I'm saying we take contracts elsewhere. There's work down in Corvale. Caravan guard, wall duty, honest violence."

"Honest violence," repeated Sefa, from her corner, where she was cleaning a crossbow she did not need to clean. "You've never done an honest thing in your life, Brannic."

"I've never done an *answered prayer* either, and I'd like to keep it that way."

That got a laugh out of some of them—the thin, brittle kind of laugh that Doss recognized for what it was, which was fear wearing a jester's coat. He let it run its course before he spoke.

"Walk me through it," he said. "The candle. You were there, Brannic. Tell it again."

Brannic shifted. He didn't want to tell it again. He wanted to tell it again very badly. These two hungers fought openly on his face, and wanting won, the way it always did.

"Old Tam Wicker," he said. "Farmer up past the mill. His boy took a fever—lung fever, the kind that takes them young. Tam's wife lit a candle at the shrine of Vael, you know, the little one by the crossroads, the one nobody's tended since—" he waved a hand, since forever, since the silence, since the world stopped listening—"and she prayed. Out loud. Begging, more like. And the candle went out in the draft, and she prayed harder, and then—"

"Then it lit itself," Sefa said quietly. "From nothing. No match, no spark. We all saw it."

"We all saw it," Brannic agreed, and his voice dropped, and that was worse than the shouting. "And the fever broke that same night. Boy's up and eating porridge this morning like nothing happened."

Silence settled over the room like another fall of snow. Doss looked around the table at his people. Twenty-two years he'd led the Broken Oath—that was what they called themselves, with the gallows humor of soldiers whose founding captain had sworn a vow to a god and then watched the god go deaf mid-battle; the name was a joke and a scar both—and in twenty-two years he had learned that companies didn't break on battlefields. They broke in back rooms, in winter, around fires, in the space between one man's fear and the next man's agreement.

"Corvale's four days' march," he said. "Through the pass. And what do we find there, do you think? Quiet?"

"Quieter than here," Brannic said.

"You've heard the same rumors I have." Doss kept his voice level, unhurried, the voice he used for talking men off ledges. "Prayers answered in Marrowdale. A statue in Corvale itself that wept real tears, or so three separate merchants swear. The Choir's preachers crawling out of whatever holes they crawled into when the gods went quiet. It's not just Hollowmere. The whole realm is waking up. Running doesn't get you away from it. It just gets you away from home."

"Home," muttered Osk, the youngest of them, barely shaving regularly. "Is it still home? Half the village is pilgrims now. You can't buy a bed. Old Mother Cass is charging two bits a night to sleep in her barn, and she used to give us stew for free."

"She's old and poor and the times are strange," Doss said. "Let her charge."

"And the other thing." Osk hesitated, glanced around, lowered his voice as if the walls might be listening, which, given recent events, no longer felt like superstition. "The other thing people are saying. About the foundling."

Doss felt it before he understood it—a small cold drop behind his breastbone, like the first leak in a roof before the storm.

"What about the foundling?" he asked, too evenly.

"Just talk. Tavern talk. That the miracles started here, in Hollowmere, first of anywhere. That the valley's magic-dead, always has been, and maybe that's not emptiness. Maybe it's a *keeping*. Like something's been stored here. Hidden here." Osk shrugged, uncomfortable. "And that the ones who hid it would've needed help. Someone to carry it in. Someone to raise it."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs, a floorboard creaked under the weight of someone pacing, and Doss knew without looking that it was Vesper, because it was always Vesper when the nights got long, walking her rounds of a house that wasn't a fort and needed none.

"That's tavern talk," Doss said. "Tavern talk is like arrow-fire. Most of it misses everything."

"And the rest of it?" Sefa asked. She set down the crossbow and looked at him with those flat grey eyes that missed nothing, that had been missing nothing for fifteen years. "Where is Vesper tonight, Sergeant? Where's she been every night since the candle? Walking. Talking to herself. Or talking to that medallion of hers, if Fen's telling it true."

Fen, at the far end of the table, had the grace to look ashamed. "I saw her holding it," he mumbled. "In the stable yard. Just—holding it. Staring at it. And Doss, I swear on my mother, it was warm. I saw her breathe on it like she was warming her hands, but her hands weren't cold. It was the medallion that was warm."

"It's brass," said Brannic. "Brass holds heat."

"Not through a Hollowmere night it doesn't."

* * *

They sent him up eventually, or rather they stopped pretending they weren't waiting for him to go, and Doss climbed the stairs of the wayhouse with a candle of his own—one that required lighting, thank the absent gods—and found Vesper Thackeray exactly where he knew she'd be: at the narrow window at the end of the hall, looking out at the snow, turning something over and over in her fingers.

She didn't startle. She never startled. In twenty years he could count on one hand the times he'd seen her genuinely surprised, and most of those involved pie.

"You're supposed to be sleeping," she said. "We've got the miller's grain run tomorrow. Two carts, the pass road, wolves if the weather turns."

"You're supposed to be sleeping too, and here you are, guarding a window that guards nothing."

"Somebody has to."

He leaned against the wall beside her, close enough to share warmth, far enough to leave her the illusion of solitude. Below, the village lay blue-white under the moon, smoke rising straight from a hundred chimneys, and beyond it the dark shoulder of the hills where the shrine of Vael stood with its newly famous candle. Pilgrims had already begun to gather there, even at this hour—he could see their little fires strung along the road like beads, hear the faint carry of a hymn, thin and wavering, sung by voices that hadn't sung anything in twenty years.

"They're arguing downstairs," Vesper said.

"I know. I was there."

"About leaving."

"Some of them. Brannic mostly. The rest are waiting to see which way the wind blows."

"And which way does it blow, Sergeant?" She finally turned her head and looked at him, and in the candlelight her eyes were the color of wet slate, and tired in a way that sleep wouldn't touch. "Downstairs. Which way do we blow?"

Doss had faced down reavers on the ice coast, held a bridge against forty bandits with six men, once talked a mob out of burning a tax office with nothing but a barrel of ale and a lie about reinforcements. None of it had prepared him for the plainness of the question, or for how much it cost her to ask it.

"The company goes where the company's needed," he said. "And right now, everything that's coming is coming here."

"You believe that."

"I believe the pilgrims believe it. They're not walking four days through snow to Corvale. They're walking here. To the valley that forgot the gods." He paused. "Or thought it did."

Vesper looked back out the window. Her thumb moved over the medallion in her hand—an old habit, he'd seen it ten thousand times, the blank disc of tarnished silver she'd worn since she was a girl, the god-mark of Vael worn smooth and featureless, as if the god himself had been rubbed off it. Every soldier in the company had teased her about it at one time or another. *Dead god's penny*, Brannic called it. *Her lucky nothing.* She'd taken it all with the flat half-smile she wore like armor, and Doss had privately wondered, over the years, whether the teasing hurt her or whether it was the only tenderness anyone offered, mockery being a kind of attention, and attention being the thing she seemed to want least and stand nearest to.

"Your medallion," he said.

Her thumb stopped moving.

"Fen says it's warm."

"Fen should mind his own watch."

"He says it was warm enough to steam in the cold." Doss turned to face her fully. "Ves. Look at me."

She did, and what he saw in her face frightened him more than any answered candle ever could—not guilt, exactly, and not fear, though both were there, but *recognition*. The look of a woman who had been waiting for a knock at the door and had finally heard it, and was trying to decide whether to answer or climb out the back.

"It's been warm for three days," she said quietly. "Since the candle. Off and on. Like—like a coal under ash. Sometimes I think I'm imagining it. Sometimes I hold it and it's so hot I have to switch hands." She gave a short laugh with no humor in it whatsoever. "Twenty years it's been cold as river stone. Dead as the god it stands for. And now—"

"Now?"

"Now sometimes I put my hand over my heart and I can't tell which one's beating."

The words dropped between them and neither moved to pick them up. Down below, the hymn on the road swelled and faded, swelled and faded, like breathing. Snow ticked softly against the window glass.

"Doss," she said, and her voice changed, dropped its armor, became for one unguarded moment the voice of the fourteen-year-old girl he'd first met on the training yard, all elbows and fury and grief, "if something's happening—if it's happening *because of me*—"

"Then we handle it," he said. "Same as everything. Same as the bridge, same as the ice coast. One problem at a time, one blade each, nobody left behind. That's the oath."

"It's a broken oath. It's in the name."

"Aye, well." He almost smiled. "We broke it together. Seemed fair to keep it broken together."

She looked at him a long moment, and something in her shoulders came down half an inch—not peace, nothing so generous as peace, but the temporary truce of a woman deciding to carry the fear a while longer instead of drowning in it.

"Get some sleep, Sergeant," she said. "Grain run at dawn."

"Yes, Captain."

"I'm not the captain."

"No," Doss agreed, heading for the stairs. "But you're the reason we'll follow, and that's worth more."

* * *

He did not sleep. He sat in the back room instead, alone with the banked fire, doing the arithmetic sergeants do in the dark: provisions, coin, blades, loyalty, and the exchange rates between them.

The company could leave. That was the truth of it. Pack up, take the pass south before the deep snows closed it, and let Hollowmere keep its miracles and its madness. Brannic would lead the argument. Osk would follow, and maybe two others. Sefa would stay—Sefa would stay if Vesper walked into the sea, which was its own quiet problem, though not tonight's. Fen would stay out of stubborn devotion. The rest would split along lines no one could yet see, and a company that splits in winter, in a valley filling with strangers who believe the divine has come home to roost—

A company that splits dies. Not all at once. But it dies.

And against that ledger, one line item that refused to balance: the medallion. Warm. Three days warm. And Vesper, who lied beautifully and often, who could look a customs officer in the eye and swear a cargo of smuggled steel was farm tools, who had once maintained for a full week that she had *not* lost the company's pay chest despite everyone having watched her lose it—Vesper had told him the truth tonight, plainly, without being forced, because she was more frightened than she was proud.

That had never happened before. That was the part that scared him.

The fire settled. Upstairs, the pacing had stopped; either she slept or she'd gone still at the window again, watching the road. Doss fed a stick to the coals and watched it catch, and thought about the word Osk had brought up from the taverns like a burr stuck in his boot.

*A keeping.*

Hollowmere was dead to magic. Everyone knew it, the way everyone knows a house is haunted—by story, not experience. Spells fizzed at the snowline. Hedge-witches got headaches. The gods, when they'd still spoken anywhere, had never spoken here. Twenty years of silence, and the silence had been so complete, so *settled*, that folk had stopped calling it silence and started calling it peace.

But a dead valley and a hidden valley look the same from inside.

And if something had been kept here—kept safe, kept secret, kept *contained*—then whoever had done the keeping had needed a place where nothing divine could reach it. And they'd needed hands. Ordinary, mortal, magic-dead hands. Hands that belonged to no god and no guild and no order, hands that could be paid.

Hands like theirs.

Doss stared into the fire and felt the shape of the thought settle over him, cold and enormous, like the snow on the roof. Maren Ostwick, the archivist-priest who'd raised the foundling girl. The foundling girl who grew into the finest sword in the company, who wore a dead god's medallion that was no longer dead, who had appeared in this valley from nowhere twenty-odd years ago in the keeping of a priest with too many keys.

*Tavern talk,* he told himself. *Arrow-fire. Mostly misses.*

But sergeants survived by believing the arrow that hadn't landed yet.

Above him, faint through the floorboards, he heard Vesper's door open, her boots on the stairs, her step in the hall—unhurried, controlled, the walk of a woman going to check on something. Then the front door of the wayhouse, opening onto the cold.

Doss rose and followed as far as the window, and looked out.

Vesper stood in the yard with her cloak over her nightclothes, bare-handed in the falling snow, and she had the medallion lifted in her palm, held out from her body like a lantern. Even from above, even through the dark and the drift, Doss could see it: a faint glow between her fingers, ember-colored, pulsing slow as a sleeping heart. Around her feet, in a circle perhaps three paces wide, the snow was melting. Not drifting aside. Melting, steaming gently, grass showing green and impossible in the dead of winter, and Vesper standing at the center of it with her face turned up to the sky, lips moving around words Doss couldn't hear—

—or words that weren't for him.

He backed away from the window, heart hammering, and made himself a soldier's promise: whatever was coming, whatever she was, the Broken Oath would not be the ones who ran. He went back to the fire, sat down, and began, methodically, to sharpen his sword.

By dawn, he'd need a plan. By dawn, he suspected, the whole valley would need one.

Because the eye of the storm was standing in the yard, glowing softly, learning her own name.
