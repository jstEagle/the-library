## 6. The Ashen Choir

The candles in the Cathedral of Ash were never lit by human hands.

Prelate Sarelle Voss had counted them once, early in her novitiate, before she understood that counting was a form of doubt. Nine hundred and forty. They burned without wick-trimming, without guttering, without the small mortal indignities of wax and smoke. They had burned for six years now—since the night Cindral first answered—and they would burn until the world did.

She walked the central nave at the hour before dawn, when the choir slept and the cathedral belonged to her alone. Her footsteps made no sound on the ash-strewn floor; that was doctrine, not accident. The Choir had learned to walk quietly. Loudness was for gods who had something left to say.

At the altar she knelt, as she did every morning, and waited for the voice.

It came the way it always came—not in words but in warmth, a pressure against the inside of her skull like a palm laid over a fevered brow. *My hands,* it seemed to say. *My eyes. My hunger.* Six years of that, and she had learned to translate. Every prelate before her had received silence or riddles or the maddening half-dreams of a god surfacing from long sleep. Sarelle received purpose. She had been chosen for clarity, and she intended to be worthy of it.

The warmth withdrew. She rose, knees cracking—she was fifty-one years old and had spent thirty of them on stone floors—and turned to face the cathedral's one concession to ugliness: the map.

It covered the eastern wall behind the altar, forty feet of treated hide stitched from a hundred smaller skins, and upon it the realm was rendered not in ink but in fire. Cities glowed as embers of varying brightness. Roads were threads of dull heat. And scattered across it, like sparks thrown from a struck flint, were the answered prayers—each one marked by a novice with a pin of red glass, each one a place where the divine silence had cracked and something had spoken back.

Six years ago there had been one pin. Then three. Then eleven. Now the map bristled with them, thick as a pox, and Sarelle read it the way a general reads terrain: where the fire fell thickest, where it clustered, where it refused to fall at all.

And there—one valley in the northern hill country, a blank space shaped like a cupped hand—the fire would not go.

Hollowmere.

* * *

She studied it while the cathedral woke around her. Novices filed in for matins, gray-robed, ash-marked across their brows, their voices rising in the Dawn Canticle—that strange, beautiful liturgy the Choir had composed in the years when no god answered anyone, sung to a deity who might have been dead, sung anyway, because faith that requires an audience is only performance. Sarelle had written three of its verses herself. She no longer remembered which.

"Mother Prelate."

Brother Cassian stood at her shoulder. He was her best instrument: young enough to be molded, clever enough to be dangerous, loyal enough—for now—to be trusted. He carried a leather folio under one arm the way other men carried weapons.

"The report from the hill country," he said. "You'll want to see this."

"I always want to see everything, Brother. That is why I am Mother Prelate." She took the folio without looking at him. He had learned not to mind. That too was a kind of training.

The report was from a lay-brother stationed in the market town of Fenlow, two days' ride south of Hollowmere's snowline. It was dictated, transcribed in a novice's careful hand, and it began with the sort of hedging language that Sarelle had ordered flayed out of all correspondence years ago: *Your Eminence will forgive the strangeness of what follows.*

She read it twice.

A peddler had come down from the valley. Ordinary man, ordinary goods, tin mirrors and salt and dyed thread. But he had told a story in the taproom of the Drowned Bell, and three separate witnesses confirmed it, and the lay-brother had done his duty and written it down before the story could grow its usual coat of tavern embellishment.

Twenty years, the peddler said, since anything holy had happened in Hollowmere. Twenty years of prayers falling into a well with no bottom. And then—a candle. A farmer dying of the lung-rot, his family gathered round, and someone's prayer—no one would say whose—and the tallow stub on the table had relit itself. Not blown out and relit. *Relit*, from nothing, flame arriving like a guest who had simply been waiting in the next room.

"And here is the part Your Eminence will find significant," Cassian said, because he could never resist narrating. "The peddler swears the family didn't rejoice. They went quiet. The widow pulled the candle away from the bed like it was a snake. As if they were afraid of it."

"As if," Sarelle said, "they remembered what it cost them the last time."

"You think the valley remembers?"

"I think valleys remember better than people do. People forgive. Valleys just wait." She set the folio down on the altar rail. "There's more. Read me the last passage."

Cassian turned the page. His voice dropped, the way voices drop in this cathedral even when there is nothing to disturb. "The peddler also spoke of a woman. A mercenary—he thought she was local, though she traveled with outsiders. He saw her only once, at the shrine road tollhouse, and he says—" Cassian hesitated, and Sarelle let him hesitate, because the shape of a hesitation often told her more than the words that ended it. "He says the frost melted where she stood. Just a little. Just around her boots. And he says when she passed the tollhouse shrine, the votive there—the one that's been dark for twenty years—it *leaned*. The flame bent away from her. Like wind. Except there was no wind."

Silence, except for nine hundred and forty flames burning their soundless burn.

"Fire bending away from a person," Sarelle said softly. "Not toward. Away."

"You find that significant."

"I find it the most interesting thing I have heard in six years, Brother, and if you repeat that assessment to anyone I will have you reassigned to the tanneries." She smiled to take the edge off it, the way she always did; Cassian relaxed, the way he always did. He still believed her smiles meant warmth. One day he would learn they meant arithmetic. "Every prayer answered since the Silence broke has been fire answering fire. Candles leaping. Coals refusing to die. Burnt offerings consumed whole. Our lord's voice carries on heat, and heat *recognizes* him. So tell me what it means when heat recoils."

Cassian considered. He was, to his credit, actually considering rather than guessing, which put him ahead of most of the Curia. "That whatever stands there isn't empty ground. Empty ground just stays dark. This—this is opposition. Something that pushes back."

"Or something that *consumes*." Sarelle turned back to the map. Her reflection ghosted across it, faint, superimposed over the hill country, so that for a moment her face hung above Hollowmere like a small pale moon. "We have spent six years asking what woke the gods. We have asked it wrong. The better question—the question our lord burns us to ask—is what put them to sleep. Because something did, Brother. The old texts are very clear, once you stop reading them devotionally. There was a Silence before this Silence. The gods have gone quiet before and woken again, and every tradition we've collected agrees on one detail: the last time, it wasn't gradual. It was *caused*."

"You believe the valley caused it."

"I believe the valley is where causation goes to hide." She touched the blank space on the map, the cupped-hand shape, and the hide was cool under her fingertip—cooler than anywhere else on the wall, as though even the map's faint residual heat declined to gather there. "And now something in it has begun to leak."

* * *

The Curia convened at midmorning in the Chamber of Cinders, a round room floored with compacted ash from the first year's burnt offerings, when the faithful had come streaming in with everything they owned and fed it to the flames. Sarelle presided from the Ember Seat—iron, unupholstered, deliberately uncomfortable. Doctrine held that comfort was a debt owed to the body and debts weakened prayer. Privately she suspected the seat existed because some earlier prelate had wanted the others to squirm.

Twelve faces looked up at her: the archdeacons, the master of relics, the mistress of inquisition. Old friends, mostly. Old rivals, entirely.

"The hill country," she began, without preamble. "I want it combed."

Archdeacon Ovist, who was seventy and had survived four prelacies by mastering the art of the useful objection, cleared his throat. "Combed for what, Mother? The pins fall everywhere now. Fenlow alone has reported nine answered prayers this season. We cannot send inquisitors chasing every candle."

"We can and we do. But this is different." She gestured, and Cassian moved through the room distributing copies of the peddler's report. "Read the third page. All of you. I'll wait."

She let them read. She watched their faces change in sequence, like windows going dark at dusk. Ovist's jaw tightened. The Mistress of Inquisition, a narrow woman named Hesk with ink-stained fingers, went very still.

"A null-tendency," Hesk said at last. "If it's true."

"If it's true, it is the first verified instance since the Rending." Sarelle let the word sit. *The Rending*—the catastrophe the old chronologies circled in black, the reason the Choir's oldest vaults were sealed, the event every text described in the passive voice, as though history itself had been too frightened to name an actor. "For twenty years the valley swallowed prayer. Now prayer works again everywhere in the world except there—and now, at its border, fire bends *away* from a living person. Those two facts are not separate, my friends. They are the same fact wearing two coats."

Ovist set down the report. "You're proposing an inquisition into Hollowmere. Into a valley that has spent two decades proving it wants nothing from us. The people there—"

"The people there are sheep who have forgotten they live near a wolf den, and one of them may be the wolf." Sarelle kept her voice level, almost gentle; gentleness was more frightening than force in that room, and everyone present knew it. "Archdeacon, I have read every account of the Rending that survives. Do you know what they share? Everyone writing about it was certain it was over. Certain the danger had passed, been contained, been buried. Certainty is how the last age died. I decline to inherit it."

"And if you're wrong?" Ovist's voice was dry. "If it's a sick woman and a drafty tollhouse?"

"Then we lose a season and some coin, and I will kneel publicly at the Ember Seat and confess my error before the Curia." She paused. "But I am not wrong. I have not been wrong about the fire yet, and our lord has not punished me for presumption, which in this cathedral is the closest thing to a signature we receive."

That silenced him. It was true, and they all knew it was true, and truth in the Chamber of Cinders was the one currency that could not be counterfeited. Six years ago, when the first candle had relit in a fisherman's hut on the southern coast, it had been Sarelle—then merely First Voice, then merely ambitious—who had ridden three days through storm to kneel beside it, who had pressed her palms to the warm air and heard, for the first time in the modern age, a god clear his throat. Every doctrine of the Awakening was built on her certainty. They could not easily dismantle her now without dismantling themselves.

"Mistress Hesk." She turned. "Choose your inquisitors. I want three—no more. Quiet ones. Travelers, not processions. If the valley holds what I believe it holds, the worst thing we can do is arrive loudly and teach it to run."

"And what do we tell them to look for?" Hesk asked, already writing.

Sarelle walked to the chamber's single window. Beyond it the city of Vhal spread out in its winter grays, chimney smoke rising straight in the still air, and somewhere among those ten thousand rooftops a thousand small fires burned in a thousand hearths, obedient, domestic, utterly unaware of what they were.

"Tell them to carry a flame," she said. "An everburning one, from the altar, blessed. Tell them to watch how it behaves around strangers. Fire leans toward its kin. Toward our lord's works, toward the newly answered, toward anything that carries his warmth." She turned back to the room. "And if it ever leans *away*—if it cowers—they will have found what we're looking for. Then they send one word south. One word, by fast rider, and nothing else. No arrests. No sermons. No martyrdoms. Is that understood? Whatever walks in that valley, I want it unaware that it has been found until the moment it is *taken*."

Hesk wrote. Ovist frowned. Somewhere beneath the floor, in the reliquary vaults, the Choir's oldest prisoners—relics that whispered, relics that wept, relics that had to be fed—shifted in their sleep, and Sarelle felt them the way she felt weather coming.

"One more thing," she said. "No one speaks of this outside this room. Not to the novices, not to the confessional, not to your own reflections if you can help it. The peddler talked in a taproom, and within a month half the hill country will have heard some version of his story. Let them talk. Rumor is a fog, and fog is cover. But if a single syllable leaves this chamber that points to *us*—" she looked at each of them in turn, unhurried, letting the iron seat's discomfort remind her body of its doctrine while her mind stayed cold and bright— "then I will know the leak came from inside, and I will treat it as heresy against the Awakening itself. We are the only institution alive that remembers what the Silence cost. I will not have our one advantage spent on vanity."

They bowed. They dispersed. Ovist lingered a moment at the door, and she caught his eye and gave him the smile she reserved for men who thought they were her equal, and he left.

* * *

Alone, she returned to the cathedral and to the map, and stood before Hollowmere's blank space for a long time.

The others thought her ambition was for the Choir—for Cindral's dominion, for the restoration of the old order with the Ashen Choir as its spine. They were wrong, and she permitted them to be wrong, because wrong subordinates are obedient subordinates. Her ambition was simpler and older than any of that.

She had been fourteen when the Silence fell. She had been kneeling at her village altar, mid-prayer, hands open, heart open, when the god she served had stopped existing—not gradually, not like a lamp dimming, but like a door closing between one breath and the next. She had finished the prayer anyway. She had finished it into nothing. And she had spent the following thirty-seven years learning what filled the space where a god used to be: nothing, nothing, nothing, and then, six years ago, *warmth*.

Cindral had come back speaking to her first. She had never told the Curia that. She had never told anyone that the first thing the returning god had said, in that fisherman's hut, with his voice like a banked fire finding air, was not a blessing.

It had been a question. *What closed the door?*

And Sarelle, kneeling in the fish-scale stink of that hovel, tears freezing on her cheeks, had understood with total clarity that this was the work she had been spared for. Not worship. Worship was for people who had never lost anything. Her work was the question. Find what closed the door. Find it, and open it wide, and stand in the doorway when everything that had fled came pouring home—and be the hand on the latch of the world.

The peddler's story was a thread. Fire leaning away from a woman was a thread. A valley that had swallowed twenty years of prayer was a loom.

She reached out and pressed her fingertip to the blank space on the map, and held it there, and waited to feel something—heat, revulsion, recognition, anything.

The hide stayed cold.

"Cold as a closed door," she murmured. "Hold on, little valley. Whatever you're hiding—I'm sending people to knock."

Behind her, unnoticed, one of the nine hundred and forty altar candles guttered—the first guttering in six years—and steadied, and burned on, and no one saw.

Except, perhaps, the thing it leaned away from, far to the north, walking through snow with a dead god's medallion warming against her breastbone like a second heartbeat.

Sarelle did not know her name yet.

She would.
