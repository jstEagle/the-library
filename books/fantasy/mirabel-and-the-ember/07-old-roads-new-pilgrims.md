## 7. Old Roads, New Pilgrims

The snow came down soft as ash for three days before the first of them arrived.

Vesper saw them from the ridge above the mill road—a line of dark figures picking their way up the frozen track from the south pass, single file, patient as ants. She counted eleven. Then more behind them, a second cluster, then a third, until she stopped counting because the numbers stopped meaning anything. They carried bundles and lanterns and children too small to make such a journey, and they moved with the particular slowness of people who believe that what waits at the end of the road is worth the walking.

"Pilgrims," Doss said beside her, spitting into the snow. "In the dead of winter. Half of them will lose toes."

"Half of them will say it was worth it."

"That's the part I hate about holy people." He pulled his scarf up over his nose. "They'll suffer anything and call it proof."

She had been on the ridge checking the boundary stones—old habit, old contract, the company's standing arrangement with the valley council to walk the perimeter twice a month whether anyone paid or not—and now she stood a long moment watching the line crawl northward toward Hollowmere's heart, toward the tavern where a candle had lit itself for a dying man, toward the well where someone swore the water tasted of honey, toward every small rumor that had leaked out past the snowline like blood through a bandage.

"They're coming for miracles," she said.

"They're coming for stories," Doss corrected. "Miracles are just stories you can't take home."

Below them, the first pilgrim in line paused at the boundary stone, knelt, and pressed both bare hands flat against the snow. Even at this distance Vesper could see the shape of the gesture—the unmistakable posture of prayer. The figure stayed there a long time. Nothing happened. The snow did not glow; no voice came down out of the grey sky. But when the pilgrim rose, they rose weeping, and Vesper could not have said why, and that frightened her more than anything else she'd seen all week.

Because she knew why. She knew exactly why. It was the same reason she'd felt that searing heartbeat under her ribs ten days ago, the same reason her medallion lay warm against her sternum even now, warm as a held coin, warm as something alive.

The valley was waking up. And everything awake was turning its face toward her.

* * *

By week's end there were two hundred of them camped in the commons below the chapel, and by the week after that, four hundred, and the village Vesper had grown up in stopped being a village.

It happened gradually enough that she could pretend otherwise for a while. A few tents became a few dozen. A cookfire became a cookfire row. Someone strung lanterns between the alder trees along the millstream, and someone else built a rough pulpit from a fallen door laid across two barrels, and by the time the first real preacher arrived—a lean man with frostbitten ears who called himself Brother Aldous and spoke of the *returning*—the commons had become a town square, and the town square had become a congregation, and Hollowmere had become a destination.

Vesper walked through it on market day and did not recognize a single street.

Not literally—the streets were the same streets, the same crooked lanes she'd run down as a girl with stolen apples in her sleeves, the same gutters that flooded each springmelt, the same leaning chimney on the cooper's house that had needed repointing for twenty years and would need it for twenty more. But they were buried. Banners hung from the eaves, white cloth torn into strips, because white was the color of the silent gods and someone had decided silence was worth mourning in public. Stalls sold tallow candles by the fistful, blessed or unblessed depending on the price. A woman sat outside the bakery selling knots of red thread guaranteed to hold a prayer tight until it was answered, and the queue for her little table was longer than the queue for bread.

The air smelled different. That was the thing that unsettled her most. Hollowmere had always smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and the mineral cold off the high snowfields, a smell so constant it had stopped being a smell and become simply the fact of the place. Now there was incense underneath it, cheap resin burning in a dozen braziers, and beneath the incense something else—something Vesper had no name for except that it made the fine hairs on her arms stand up and made her medallion shift temperature against her skin like a thing breathing.

"You look like a woman walking through her own funeral," said Tam, falling into step beside her. He had a sack of salted pork over one shoulder and the expression of a man deeply enjoying someone else's catastrophe. "Look at them. They've got a shrine to the *well*, Ves. The well. I've drunk out of that well my whole life and the only miracle it ever performed was not killing me."

"People are frightened," she said. "Frightened people reach for anything warm."

"Frightened people reach for torches, is what they reach for. Warm comes later, if you're lucky." He shifted the pork sack. "Doss wants us out. He won't say it straight, but he's been pricing mules."

"I know."

"He asked me if you'd go. I told him you'd go when the job was done and not a day sooner, and he said that's what he was afraid of." Tam glanced sideways at her. "What job, Ves? There's no job. There's just you, walking the perimeter like the valley's going to leak out the bottom if you stop."

She didn't answer. Ahead of them the crowd thickened around the chapel steps, where Brother Aldous stood in the thin sunshine preaching to perhaps sixty souls, his voice pitched to carry, worn smooth as river stone:

"—and the god did not die, brothers and sisters, the god only *slept*, and what sleeps can wake, and what wakes will ask us what we did in the long dark—did we keep faith? Did we keep our lamps trimmed? Did we—"

Vesper stopped listening. Not because the words were empty—they weren't, quite—but because as Aldous swept his gaze across the crowd, his eyes passed over her face and snagged there, half a heartbeat, the way a fishhook snags on a submerged branch. He didn't know her. He couldn't know her. But something in her face made him pause mid-sentence, and the pause rippled outward, and other faces turned to follow his gaze, and for three full seconds Vesper Thackeray stood in the middle of her own village while strangers looked at her like she was a door they'd been told existed somewhere.

Then Tam elbowed her gently and said, loudly, "Come on, this pork won't salt itself," and the moment broke, and the crowd went back to its hunger, and Vesper breathed again.

But her hands were shaking, and they didn't stop shaking until she'd put two streets between herself and the chapel.

* * *

Her mother's house still stood at the end of Tanner's Row, which was not really Tanner's Row anymore but Pilgrim's Way, renamed by consensus sometime in the last fortnight without anyone consulting the actual tanners. The house had been empty for six years—not hers, never hers, but the house of the woman who'd taken her in at fourteen after the orphanage and before the company, a widow named Hesta who'd fed her and taught her letters and never once asked about the blank medallion she wore. Hesta had died in the spring, quiet as she'd lived, and the house had gone to a nephew in the lowlands who didn't want it, and so it stood shuttered and patient, holding its dust.

Vesper let herself in with the key she'd never returned. She told herself she'd come for Hesta's good blanket, the heavy grey one, because the nights were getting colder and the company's quarters above the smithy leaked heat like a sieve. This was even true. It was also true that the house was the last place in the valley where nobody was praying, preaching, selling, or watching, and she wanted—she needed—one hour inside that silence.

She got twenty minutes.

She was folding the blanket—it smelled of cedar and disuse, of a life closed up tidy—when she heard the front door creak, and turned, hand already at the knife she wore even indoors these days, and found an old woman standing in the doorway with a basket of eggs against her hip.

For a moment neither of them moved. Then the old woman's face did something complicated—surprise folding into recognition folding into something older and stranger than either—and she said, in the cracked, unhurried voice of the very old:

"Well. Look at that. The foundling's come home."

Vesper's mouth went dry. "I think you've mistaken me, gran. I'm just—Hesta kept this house. I'm collecting some things for the family."

"Mistaken." The old woman came in uninvited, the way old women do, setting her basket on the table with the authority of ownership. Up close Vesper could see she was well past eighty, small and brown and wrinkled as a winter apple, with eyes gone pale as skimmed milk. "I don't mistake eyes. I delivered half this valley and buried the other half, and I remember every pair that ever opened in my hands." She looked up, and those milk-pale eyes fixed on Vesper's face with terrible precision. "Grey-green. Like slate under water. Like nobody else's. You're the foundling. The one Maren Ostwick brought up the mountain road in the dead of winter, twenty-three years back, wrapped in a cloak with no name sewn in it."

The room tilted, very slightly, and righted itself.

"I don't know what you're talking about," Vesper said, and heard how it sounded—too fast, too flat, the voice of a liar who'd been honest too long to do it well.

"No," the old woman agreed placidly. "You wouldn't. You'd have been days old. I wasn't there—I only heard, like everyone heard, because Maren paid me to keep my mouth shut and I took his coin and kept it, all these years, because there was nothing in it worth saying." She began unpacking her eggs one by one onto the table, unhurried, as though she intended to stay. "But now there's pilgrims on every road asking after omens, and preachers asking after signs, and yesterday a man in grey with a Choir pin asked me if I remembered any unusual births in the valley. Any foundlings. Any children who came from nowhere." She set down the last egg and looked up again. "And I thought, well, here's a thing worth saying at last."

"Why tell me?" Vesper managed. Her voice sounded far away, underwater, slate-colored.

"Because you've got her eyes."

The words landed like ice water. "Whose?"

"The mother's." The old woman said it simply, terribly, the way you'd report the weather. "The woman who left you. I saw her once, the night before she went back down the mountain—came to my door wanting milk for a babe she wouldn't show me, hood up, hands burned red like she'd reached into a fire. But she pushed the hood back to drink, and I saw her face, and I saw those eyes. Grey-green. Slate under water. I never forgot them, because I'd never seen their like before, and I've never seen their like since—" she tapped the air in front of Vesper's cheekbone with one crooked finger, gentle as a moth, "—until today, standing in a dead woman's kitchen, wearing a soldier's coat."

Outside, distantly, a bell was ringing. One of the new bells, bought by subscription, hung in the chapel tower for calling the faithful. It rang and rang and Vesper stood inside its sound like a thing drowned.

"You should leave the valley," the old woman said, and there was no malice in it at all, only the flat kindness of the very old delivering bad news they've outlived the strength to soften. "Whatever Maren hid you from, it's coming up the mountain roads now. I can smell it. Same smell as the year the fever came. People get hungry-looking." She picked up her empty basket. "I'll not speak your name. I never learned it, and I'm too old to start learning names. But eyes are eyes, child, and there's a hundred strangers in this valley, and some of them look *hard*."

She let herself out. The door swung shut behind her with a sound like a period at the end of a sentence.

Vesper stood a long time among Hesta's dust and cedar-smell, the folded blanket clutched to her chest like a shield, doing arithmetic she didn't want to do: a man in grey with a Choir pin, asking after foundlings. Asking door to door. Working methodically through a valley that had spent twenty years forgetting how to be interesting, and finding, at last, in a dead woman's kitchen, an old woman with nothing left to sell but memory.

Home had been the hiding place. That had been the whole architecture of her life—Maren's design, she understood now, or part of it: bury the spark in the one place fire couldn't reach, bury the girl inside the valley, bury the valley inside silence. Twenty-three years of snow on a sealed room.

And she had walked out of the sealed room herself. She had stood in crowds and let preachers' eyes catch on her face. She had flared—gods, she had *flared*, snow melting in a perfect circle around her boots while pilgrims watched—and told herself it would fade, that attention was weather, that it would blow over.

It wasn't weather. It was a hunt, and she was the scent.

She left by the back door, over the garden wall, through the tanner's yard, moving the way she moved on contracts, quick and quiet and forgettable. But halfway down Tanner's Row she stopped, because ahead of her, at the corner by the old pump, stood a man in grey robes with a silver pin shaped like a flame, and he was talking to the baker's boy, and he was holding up something for the boy to look at—something small, something that caught the light—and as Vesper watched, the boy shook his head and pointed.

He pointed south. Down the row. Toward her.

The man in grey turned, unhurried, and looked straight down the street at the place where Vesper Thackeray was standing in plain sight with nowhere left in the world to hide.
