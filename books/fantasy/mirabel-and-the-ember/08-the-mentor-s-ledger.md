## 8. The Mentor's Ledger

The records-house of Hollowmere had always smelled the same to Vesper: dust and lamp oil and old vellum, the sweet rot of paper that had outlived everyone who wrote it. As a child she had loved that smell. It meant Maren was near—Maren who kept every name in the valley like a shepherd keeps count of his flock, Maren whose ink-stained fingers could find any birth or death or marriage going back four hundred years without opening a single drawer.

She stood across the lane from it now, hood up, snow gathering on her shoulders, and hated herself for what she'd come to do.

*You could knock,* said the reasonable voice, the one that sounded like Doss after too much ale. *He raised you. He'd tell you anything you asked.*

Would he? That was the question that had been eating her since the hedge-priest went pale at her touch outside the valley, since the pilgrim woman grabbed her wrist in the market square and whispered about foundlings' eyes. She had asked Maren nothing yet. She had watched him instead, three evenings running, and what she'd seen had brought her here in the dark with a lockpick kit and a liar's heart.

She'd seen him burn something.

Not papers—he burned correspondence openly enough, feeding the stove in his study with letters from the diocese, from the Choir's provincial office, from half the archivists in the lowlands. But two nights ago, passing his window on her way home from the Broken Oath's lodgings, she had seen him standing over the brazier with a book open in his hands, and the look on his face—

She knew that look. She wore it herself sometimes, in the years after Vael went silent, when she caught her reflection wearing the medallion. It was the look of a man destroying something because keeping it had become unbearable.

Maren Ostwick did not have an unbearable face. He had the mildest face in Hollowmere, round and soft as a winter pear, framed by white hair he never bothered to cut straight. He had taught her letters on slate, taught her the difference between grief and mourning (one is weather, the other is work), taught her that every record is a promise made to people not yet born. He had held her hand at fourteen when the god died in her chest, and he had not once, not ever, lied to her.

So far as she knew.

The lane was empty. The valley's new population of pilgrims slept in barns and under hedges and in the shrine-house, dreaming of miracles, and none of them dreamed of the plain stone building with its green door and its leaded windows where the whole history of Hollowmere lay sleeping in drawers.

Vesper crossed the lane.

* * *

The lock was old and honest, a ward-and-tumbler her mentor himself had shown her how to pick when she was eleven—"in case you're ever locked out, child, and also because a girl should know that all doors are opinions." The pick found the tumblers like an old friend's handshake. The door swung inward on hinges that needed oil, and the dark of the records-house folded around her, familiar as a coat.

She didn't light a lamp. She knew the room by heart anyway: the long reading table scarred by generations of students' knives; the map of the valley on the west wall, its rivers faded to ghost-threads; the great cabinet of ledgers along the north side, each drawer labeled in Maren's careful hand—*Baptisms 1580–1620, Marriages, Deaths, Land Grants, Foundlings.*

Foundlings.

Her breath fogged in the cold air. She stood in front of that drawer longer than she'd stood anywhere in years.

There were only ever three foundling entries in Hollowmere's modern memory. A boy left at the shrine-house in the famine year, taken south by relatives. Twin girls abandoned on the high road, raised by the tanner's widow. And one more—the one nobody spoke of, because there was nothing to speak of. No story. No basket on a doorstep. Just a priest-archivist who came down from the pass one spring morning twenty-three years ago with an infant wrapped against his chest and a smile that answered no questions, and a valley too polite, too grateful, too tired to ask why a celibate archivist had suddenly acquired a daughter.

Vesper had built her whole childhood on that silence. She'd told herself stories: a dead sister's baby, a nobleman's by-blow, a refugee from some southern war. She'd stopped telling them stories somewhere in her twenties, because Maren had given her something better than answers. He'd given her a life with no hole in it. Lessons and chores and the medallion of Vael warm against her skin, and a trade, and a crew, and a valley that knew her name and expected nothing strange of her.

Until the prayers started being answered again. Until the candle relit itself before a dying farmer's eyes. Until something turned over inside her ribs like a sleeper disturbed, and knew her name.

She opened the drawer.

The ledgers inside were bound in green leather gone brittle at the spines. She lifted the most recent—*Foundlings & Registrations, 990–1013*—and carried it to the reading table, and set it down, and for a moment simply rested her palms flat on the cover, as if the book might feel her heartbeat through it and decide to be merciful.

Then she opened it to the year of her birth, or near enough. The pages were ruled in faded red, each entry in Maren's upright, unhurried script. She read them the way you read a familiar road at night, knowing every turn. A stillbirth in the miller's family, recorded with unusual tenderness. The famine-year boy. The twins.

And then—

Nothing.

No. Not nothing. Worse than nothing.

There was an entry. She could see where it had been: the ruled lines waiting, the page numbered, the sequence unbroken. Entry forty-one, between the twins in early spring and a legitimate adoption in autumn. The space where the words should have been was a wound in the paper—a rough-edged oval where the surface of the page had been burned away entirely, taking the ink with it, leaving behind a scab of browned, blistered fiber that crumbled slightly when her fingertip hovered over it.

Someone had cut—or burned—an exact piece out of the ledger and replaced it with this scorched patch, pressed flat, aged to match. Not torn out. *Erased.* Made to look, at a careless glance, like damage. Like accident. Like damp, or fire, or time.

But Maren Ostwick did not have accidents in his ledgers. Maren Ostwick had once spent three days reconstructing a water-damaged marriage record from parish memory and marginalia, because "a blank line is a lie we tell the future."

Vesper stood very still in the cold room and felt the first taste of something she would carry for a long time afterward.

It wasn't rage. Rage would come later, and grief later still, and a terrible clarity after that. What came first was smaller and sharper: the specific humiliation of realizing that a person you loved had been managing you. Curating you. Deciding, entry by entry, decade by decade, exactly how much of yourself you were permitted to know.

All those evenings by the stove while he transcribed records. All those times she'd asked, casually, idly, about her own beginnings—and watched him give her the same gentle non-answer, the shrug, the "the past keeps its own counsel, child"—and she had accepted it because accepting it was easier than suspecting him. He hadn't failed to answer. He had *answered*, again and again, with silence shaped to look like kindness.

And he had done this. He had sat at this very table—she could see him so clearly it hurt, lamplight on his bald crown, spectacles pushed up into his hair—with a knife and a flame and her name in his hands, and he had burned her out of the book.

*Why?*

The word had no echo in her. It just fell into the hollow where the entry used to be.

She became aware, gradually, of her own breathing—too fast, too shallow, fogging the cold air in little ghosts. She forced it slow. Mercenary discipline. Breathe, assess, act. There might be other evidence. There was almost certainly other evidence; a man who erased one line erases everything connected to it. Think like a thief breaking in, because that's what you are tonight, and don't think like a daughter, because that girl doesn't live here anymore.

She searched.

She started with the drawer itself, feeling along its underside, its back panel, the joinery where a flat packet could hide. Nothing but mouse droppings and a fossilized apple core from some student's sin decades dead. Then the adjacent drawers—baptisms for the same years, deaths, land grants. In baptisms, the same year held another small wrongness: a page where the ruling lines continued but the entries jumped oddly, as if one had been removed and the rest shifted up to disguise the gap. In deaths, nothing. In land grants, a property transfer dated the spring after her arrival: the shrine-house cottage and its acre, deeded to *M. Ostwick, archivist*, from an estate name she didn't recognize—*Halloway Trust*—with no donor listed.

A trust. Someone had paid for her keep. Someone had endowed a cottage for a celibate archivist and his inexplicable infant, and then arranged for even that to look ordinary.

Vesper closed the land-grant drawer with exaggerated gentleness, the way you close a door on a sickroom.

The study was through the arch at the back of the building—Maren's private room, technically off-limits to everyone including her, though as a child she'd spent hundreds of hours in there doing sums at his knee. The door stood ajar. Of course it did. This was Hollowmere. Nobody locked doors against their neighbors, and the one man with secrets had relied for twenty-three years on the valley's decency and his own invisibility.

His desk was neat as a sermon. Inkwell centered. Quills in a jar. The brazier cold in the corner, and beside it, in the iron pail where he let ash cool before carrying it to the midden heap—

Paper. Not fully burned. Whoever destroys evidence in haste makes mistakes, and Maren, for all his care, had been working frightened. Vesper knelt at the pail and lifted out the fragments with a thief's delicacy, piecing them on the desk's blotter under the gray light of the window.

Most were ash and suggestion. But three pieces survived enough to read.

The first was a fragment of heavy cream stock, official, bearing part of a seal she recognized with a lurch: the sunburst-and-chalice of the Ashen Choir. Around the seal fragment, in a formal ecclesiastical hand: *...renewed inquiry concerning persons resident within the valley of...* and then nothing, the rest consumed.

The second fragment was thinner, older, the ink browned with age. Only a phrase survived: *...the vessel must remain where the fire cannot follow...* and beneath it, in a different hand—Maren's hand, younger, less certain—*I understand. I will keep her safe. I will keep her ignorant.*

*I will keep her ignorant.*

The words sat on the blotter and looked at her.

The third fragment was smallest of all, a corner really, but the handwriting on it stopped her heart mid-beat, because it was not Maren's and not the Choir's and not any clerk's hand she knew. It was slanted, urgent, written fast:

*...if they come before she is grown, burn everything, and tell her nothing—not even her n—*

The rest was gone. *Not even her n—*. Her name. Her *name*. Not Vesper—that was the name Maren gave her, she'd always known that, a foundling's name chosen from a saint's calendar. Some other name. A name someone had been willing to see burned rather than spoken.

Vesper sat down slowly in her mentor's chair, in his study, surrounded by his careful lies, and put her head in her hands.

Outside, the wind moved through the valley, and somewhere a pilgrim cried out in sleep, or prayer—it was getting hard to tell the difference in Hollowmere these days—and the medallion of Vael lay against her breastbone, cool and dead as it had been for twenty years, and she found herself talking to it anyway, the way she used to at fourteen, before she learned better.

"Did he know?" she whispered. "Did you know what he was doing? Were you in on it too?"

Silence. Of course silence. Silence was the one honest thing anyone had ever given her.

She gathered the fragments, folding them into the oilskin pouch inside her coat. She straightened the chair. She closed the study door to the width she'd found it. On her way back through the reading room she paused at the great ledger, open still to entry forty-one, to the burned oval where her beginning used to be.

She didn't take the book. Taking it would tell Maren, the moment he noticed, exactly what she knew and where she'd looked. Let him keep his curated ignorance a while longer. She had a different use for him now. She wanted to watch his face when she finally asked the right question—one he couldn't shrug away, couldn't archive, couldn't burn.

She had a list forming, actually, of questions, and it grew even as she stood there: Who was Halloway? What vessel? Whose hand wrote *not even her n—*? Why had the Choir's letter come *now*, after twenty silent years—unless whoever wrote it had also heard that the gods were waking, and had known, all along, exactly what slept in Hollowmere?

What slept in *her*.

The hedge-priest's pale face rose in her memory, his hand jerking back from hers as if from a stove. *Magic recoils from me like water from hot iron.* She had thought herself untouched. Empty. Unchosen. She had built a whole identity on the absence, worn it like the medallion, made a virtue of the void.

But voids didn't make hedge-priests go pale. Voids didn't need to be hidden in the one valley where "the fire cannot follow." You didn't hide an emptiness, Maren. You hid a *thing*.

Vesper blew out the breath she'd been holding and pulled her hood up.

The snow had thickened while she worked. She slipped out the green door and relocked it, and the lock clicked shut behind her with a sound like a period at the end of a sentence. The lane lay blue-white under the clouds, pilgrim-less, silent. Across the way, the shrine-house bell tolled the third hour, and the sound rolled down the valley and was swallowed.

She was halfway home before she understood that she was shaking—not from cold, though the cold was real enough, but from the effort of holding one particular thought at arm's length, the thought underneath all the others, the one the burned page pointed at like a finger of char:

*Maren didn't erase your history to protect you from the past. He erased it to protect the past from you.*

She reached the crossroads by the old well, where the lane bent toward the lodgings, and stopped.

Because the frost on the well's stones—ordinary frost, valley frost, the magic-dead frost of Hollowmere that had never once in her lifetime done anything but sit there and be cold—was moving. Curling. Writing itself in fine white threads across the curved granite, letter by letter, in a hand she recognized with a violence of longing that buckled her knees.

She knew that hand. She'd spent her girlhood watching it form in candleflames and frost and the steam off soup, before it stopped coming. Before the silence.

The letters finished themselves, delicate as breath on glass:

**VESPER.**

Just that. Her false name, her foundling name, written in the ice by the god who had gone quiet for twenty years—the god whose medallion, she realized distantly, was no longer cold against her skin but warm, warming, burning gently through her shirt like a coal remembering what it was.

The frost-script trembled. Held. And began to write again.
