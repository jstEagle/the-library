## 9. Voice in the Frost

The dream came again that night, and this time it did not let her go.

She was fourteen again, kneeling on the cold flagstones of the shrine at Alder's Rest, her palms pressed flat against stone worn smooth by a century of other children's palms. The candle before her had guttered low. Outside, snow fell in the patient, endless way it fell over Hollowmere—snow that never melted, never deepened, just kept falling like a hush laid over the whole valley. And Vael's voice had been there, warm as a hearth through a window, saying her name.

*Vesper.*

Not the way people said it. Not the way Maren said it when calling her to supper, or the way the other foundlings said it when they wanted something. The god said it the way fire says *burn*—as if her name were simply what she did, what she was made of.

*You are seen, little wick. You are known.*

And then—mid-prayer, mid-breath—the warmth had gone out of the world like a door closing. She had knelt there until her knees ached and the candle drowned in its own wax, and the silence that answered her was not empty. It was full. It was the silence of a room where someone has just stopped breathing.

Twenty years later, she woke in the loft above the Drowned Bell with frost on the inside of the window and her heart slamming like a fist against a locked door.

The medallion was hot.

Not warm. Hot—hot enough that she felt it through her shirt, hot enough that when she pulled it out into the moonlight the blank silver face of it shimmered, faintly, like water about to boil. She had worn the thing since she was fourteen. For twenty years it had been exactly what everyone teased her for: a dead god's coin, cold as the valley, mute as stone. Doss called it her "tombstone." Tam called it her "retirement plan." She had laughed along because laughing was cheaper than weeping.

Now it burned against her sternum like a swallowed coal.

"No," she whispered to the empty loft. "No, no—you don't get to do this. Twenty years. You don't get to come back now."

The medallion pulsed. Once. Twice. Like a heartbeat that wasn't hers—or worse, like one that had always been hers and had only now decided to admit it.

She should have thrown it across the room. She thought about it, honestly considered it, her fingers actually closing around the chain—and then the voice came, and every thought of throwing anything went out of her like breath from a struck chest.

It did not come through her ears. That was the first horror of it. It came up through the floorboards, through the frost on the glass, through the marrow of her own bones, as though the whole world were a bell and something enormous had finally decided to strike it.

***Vesper.***

Her name in that old fire-warm cadence, and twenty years collapsed like a rotten floor. She was fourteen and forty at once, kneeling on flagstones and sitting bolt upright in a hired bed, and the grief she had spent two decades paving over with pragmatism cracked straight down the middle.

"You left," she said aloud. Her voice came out small and vicious. "You left mid-prayer. Do you know what that does to a child? Do you know what I built on top of that?"

*I know,* said Vael, and his voice was not warm anymore. *I know what you built. I have watched you build it, stone by stone, prayer by unanswered prayer. I am sorry for it. I will be sorry past the end of things. But I did not wake to apologize.*

"Then why?" She was standing now; she didn't remember standing. The frost on the window had begun, very slowly, to melt—not all of it, just a handprint-shaped patch directly across from where she stood, as though the glass itself were listening with its forehead pressed to hers. "Why now? Why after everything? The farmer's candle—that was you, wasn't it. The stirrings. All of it."

*Yes.*

"Why?" she demanded, louder, and somewhere below her a dog began to bark, and she didn't care. "Gods don't wake up out of kindness, Vael. I carried your silence for twenty years. I earned the right to an answer."

A pause. In that pause she felt him gather himself, the way a man gathers himself to deliver news he has rehearsed and still hates.

*Because they are coming for what burns in you.*

The words landed in the silent loft and did not echo. They sank. They went down through her like a stone down a well, and the well was deeper than she was, and she heard them hit water a long, long way beneath the person she had believed herself to be.

"What burns in me," she repeated.

*You felt it. When the candle relit. When my attention passed through you like light through a lens. You are not untouched by the divine, Vesper. You are not a valley in miniature, magic-dead and safe.* A sound moved through her then that might have been a god drawing breath. *You are the reason the valley is dead. You are the silence. Everything holy flees you, and everything holy has finally noticed why.*

The frost-melt on the window had spread. Water crawled down the pane in branching veins, and outside, the snow over the rooftops of Hollowmere had begun, almost imperceptibly, to slump and slide from the eaves.

"That's not possible," she said. "I'm a mercenary. I'm nobody. I take escort contracts and drink bad ale and count my wages twice. I'm—" Her voice failed, restarted. "Maren raised me. Maren found me. He told me I was left at the shrine door in a basket like any other—"

*He told you what kept you alive,* Vael said, and there was something in the god's voice she had never heard in it before, not even in the old days, not even at fourteen. It took her a moment to name it, because she had no practice hearing fear from anything larger than herself.

It was shame.

*Maren Ostwick is a good man who did a terrible necessary thing, and I have guarded his secret as I have guarded you, badly, from too far away, for too long. But the seals are thinning. The Ember stirs in you because the world's prayers are being answered again, and answered prayers are heat, and heat finds fuel. There is a spark sleeping under your ribs older than every god whose bells are ringing tonight. Older than me. They will smell it soon, if they have not already. The Choir. The winter thing in the north. Every hungry thing that ever called itself divine.*

"The Ember," she said. The word tasted borrowed, secondhand, like a word from a story told in another country. "I don't know what that is."

*You are the only living thing that does not need to know,* said Vael. *You have been carrying it since before you could carry anything else. Ask your archivist. Ask him what he burned out of the ledger. Ask him why the valley forgot its magic, why my voice died here and nowhere else, why prayers slid off Hollowmere like rain off oiled wool. You were not hidden in a quiet place, child. The quiet place was built around you.*

Vesper sat down on the edge of the bed because her legs informed her they were done negotiating. The medallion cooled against her skin by slow degrees, but the voice remained, closer now, or perhaps she had simply stopped being able to tell the difference between inside and outside.

"I prayed to you," she said, and hated how young she sounded. "Every day. For years after you went quiet. I prayed until praying was just a habit, like biting my nails. And you were *here*? You were watching? You could have—"

*If I had spoken to you, they would have heard me speaking to you.* The words came flat and fast, a man walking quickly past a danger. *Prayer is not private, Vesper. It never was. The Choir reads answered prayers the way you read tracks in snow. Every time you knelt at that shrine, I held my tongue so hard I cracked. Do you understand what I am telling you? Your whole life has been a held breath.*

"So the oath meant nothing."

*The oath was the truest thing in this valley.* And there—he softened, or something in him did, the old hearth-warmth flickering up through the dread like flame through a grate. *I never broke faith with you, little wick. I broke faith with everyone else, to keep faith with you. Remember that, in the days coming, when you hate me. And you will hate me. You should start now, if it helps.*

"I already do," she said, and meant it, and felt it land somewhere in him, felt him accept it without flinching, which was somehow worse than any argument would have been.

Outside, the barking dog had gone quiet. The whole valley lay under the moon in its usual shroud of falling snow—but the snow was wrong tonight. Slower. As if the sky were thinking about each flake before letting it go. Down the street, a lamp flickered in the Drowned Bell's front window, and Vesper watched it gutter, steady, gutter, steady, keeping time with the pulse of the medallion against her breastbone.

"How long?" she asked.

*Before they know? Days. Before they come? Perhaps a season, if the snows are kind. They will not be kind. Snows never are, when something rides them.*

"Isolde," Vesper said. The name surfaced from tavern talk, from caravan gossip, from the kind of stories mercenaries told with their voices lowered. *The Long Winter. Lady Isolde.* Even in the magic-dead valley the stories had reached them, wearing the disguise of fable. "The stories say her winter walks."

*The stories undersell it.* A pause, and when Vael spoke again, the warmth was entirely gone, and what remained was older than warmth, older than comfort: a god telling the truth because he had run out of everything softer. *Listen to me, Vesper Thackeray, and hold these words tighter than you have ever held anything. What sleeps in you is the last ember of the fire that lit the gods. If Cindral's Choir takes it, their god becomes the only god, and every soul that ever prayed will pray to him or burn. If the Winter Court takes it, the world becomes Hravn's law—still, white, obedient, eternal. And if you give it back to me—*

He stopped.

Vesper waited. The melted patch on the window refroze, slowly, feathering white again from the edges inward.

"If I give it back to you," she said quietly, "what?"

*Then I become whole,* Vael said, *and I do not know what I become whole enough to do. That is the truth, little wick. I am asking you to trust a god who fled his own altar, and I have no right to ask it, and I am asking anyway, because the alternative is that they find you unprepared.*

"And if I destroy it?"

Silence. Longer this time. Long enough that she wondered if he'd gone—if he'd retreated back into whatever far place gods go when they are ashamed—and the loneliness of that possibility hit her with such stupid, childish force that her eyes stung.

*Then divinity dies,* he said at last, *and the world goes on, and no one will ever be seen the way I saw you. No one will say anyone's name the way I have said yours. It will be ordinary. It will be free. And some nights, Vesper, some nights you will miss being known that much, and there will be nothing anywhere in creation to answer you.*

She laughed—a short, broken sound with no humor in it whatsoever.

"You realize," she said, "that you've just described my last twenty years. I'm already an expert in missing it."

*Yes,* said Vael, and for one heartbeat the grief in his voice was so vast and so gentle that she understood, suddenly and completely, that everything he had said tonight was true, and that truth was the cruelest thing he could have brought her. *I know. That is why I came back. That is why I risked the Choir's ears and the Winter's frost and the thin places between. Because you of all souls deserve to choose with open eyes. And because—*

The voice frayed. Flickered. Somewhere far above the valley, thunder rolled across a cloudless, freezing sky, and the medallion went abruptly, terribly cold.

*They are listening,* Vael said, and now his voice was rushing, compressed, a man whispering through a closing door. *I have said too much, stayed too long. Vesper—Maren knew. Maren chose. Ask him what he chose, and why, and what he burned. Do it tomorrow, before the pilgrims come, before the roads fill. And Vesper—when the strangers arrive, and they will arrive, men with relics and women with rings of frost—do not let them touch you. Whatever truce they offer, whatever courtesy, whatever blessing—your skin is the only wall left, and walls fall when they are touched.*

"Vael—"

*Little wick. One more thing, and then I must be silent, and you must forgive me for the silence, all of it, every year of it.*

The frost on the window bloomed. Not melted—bloomed, fern-patterns spreading outward from the center in spirals too regular to be weather, and in the heart of the pattern, for less than a heartbeat, she saw fire. A hearth-fire. A shrine-candle. Something burning in a high place with screaming in the wind around it, and a woman's face turning toward—

Gone. Frost. Moonlight. The ordinary dark of an ordinary valley.

*Your mother did not abandon you,* Vael said, barely a breath now, a god's voice worn down to an ember's glow. *Whatever Maren tells you, whatever the ledger says, whatever the world concludes—she put you in that basket running toward something, not away. Remember that when you hate us both.*

And then the silence came back.

But it was not the old silence. It was not the silence of a dead god or a magic-dead valley or a life built on being unchosen. It was the silence of a room after someone has left it—the shape of a presence still hanging in the air, the warmth still fading from a chair.

Vesper sat in the loft until the moon set, the medallion resting cold and quiet in her palm, watching the frost patterns on the window slowly return to their natural disorder. Below her, the Drowned Bell slept. Down the lane, Hollowmere slept. Somewhere north, beyond the pass, something walked inside the snow. Somewhere south, behind incense and iron, something read prayers like tracks.

*They are coming for what burns in you.*

She got up, dressed in the dark, and buckled on her sword out of pure habit, and stood a moment with her hand flat against the door, listening to the valley breathe.

Tomorrow she would find Maren. Tomorrow she would ask him what he burned out of the ledger, and watch his face while he lied, and ask again.

Tonight, she opened the shutters one last time and looked out at the falling snow—the slow, deliberate, thinking snow—and made herself a promise in the language she trusted most, the plain grammar of a hired sword:

Whatever came over those passes looking for her, it would have to bleed for the finding.

Far to the north, past the White Pass, past the frozen falls, a light appeared on the mountainside that was not a star, did not flicker, and did not move.

It was waiting, too.
