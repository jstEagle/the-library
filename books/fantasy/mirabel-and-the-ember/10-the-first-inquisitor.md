## 10. The First Inquisitor

The truce banner arrived before the man did.

Vesper saw it first from the ridge above the mill road—a square of white cloth on a black pole, carried by a rider whose horse picked its way through the slush with the careful, expensive gait of an animal that had never known hunger. Behind him came six more riders in ash-grey cloaks, and behind them a wagon with barred windows, and behind *that*, strung out along the frozen road like beads on a grey cord, pilgrims. Dozens of them. Then hundreds.

She had been mending a fence rail with Doss when the banner crested the hill, and she stood there with a hammer still in her hand, watching the white cloth snap in the wind off the pass.

"Truce flag," Doss said, spitting into the snow. "That's either very good manners or very bad news."

"Both," Vesper said. "It's the Choir."

* * *

They came to the village green at midmorning, when the market stalls were just being uncovered and the smell of woodsmoke and frying onions hung low under the grey sky. The inquisitor dismounted at the well, and Vesper got her first good look at him.

He was younger than she expected. She had imagined some ancient, leather-faced instrument of the Choir, all scar and scripture, but this man was perhaps thirty-five, with a scholar's soft hands and a face that might have been handsome if it had ever learned to be anything other than attentive. His cloak was fine wool, undyed, and at his throat he wore the Choir's sigil: a circle of iron with a single coal set in its center, glowing faintly, never quite going out. Everyone knew what that coal was. Everyone pretended not to know.

"I am Brother Corvane," he said, and his voice carried without his raising it, the way voices do in empty churches. "Of the Ashen Choir, sent by the grace of Cindral, who answers." He let the last word sit in the cold air like a coal itself. "I come under truce. I come to test, not to take. Any soul who wishes may be blessed; any soul who wishes may pass by. The fire compels no one."

"The fire compels everyone," muttered Tam Wickerby from beside his stall of salted fish, too loudly, and then went pale and busied himself rearranging fish that needed no rearranging.

Corvane smiled as if he hadn't heard, which was its own kind of hearing.

Vesper stood at the edge of the crowd with her arms crossed and her medallion tucked inside her shirt, where it had lived for twenty years, and told herself the cold feeling in her stomach was just the weather.

* * *

The testing took place on the green, in the open, because Corvane understood—better than the villagers understood—that secrecy would frighten them more than spectacle. He set up a trestle table draped in grey cloth, and upon it he laid his relics one by one, each with a small ceremony of unwrapping, each drawing a collective intake of breath from the gathered villagers.

There was a candle stub, black with age, that was said to have burned in Cindral's first temple. There was an iron ring, pitted and dark. There was a small clay vessel, sealed with wax, that no one could see into and everyone could feel—the air around it was warm, like standing near an oven door.

"Come," Corvane said, and held out his hands, palms up. "Hold the ring. Speak a wish, or speak nothing. If the fire knows you, you will know it too."

The line formed slowly, the way lines form for things people are afraid of. Old Greta Hobb went first, chin up, daring anyone to comment. She held the ring, closed her eyes, and nothing happened, and she walked away looking vaguely insulted. Tam Wickerby held it and nothing happened and he looked relieved. A pilgrim woman with a baby on her hip held it and the ring grew briefly, faintly warm, and she wept, and Corvane blessed her in a voice like falling ash, and the crowd murmured, and the line grew longer.

Vesper watched all of it from the shadow of the smithy's awning, and she watched something else besides: she watched Corvane watch. He wasn't looking at the people holding the ring. Not mostly. His gaze kept sliding along the edges of the crowd, patient and systematic, the way a man reads a page line by line. Looking for something that didn't want to be found.

"He's counting," Doss said quietly beside her. He had appeared the way he always did, without sound, a big man moving like a rumor. "See how he pauses on some folk? He's marking them. Warm ones, cold ones."

"Why?"

Doss shrugged, but it was a shrug that meant *you know why*. "Rumor came down the pass two days ago. Pilgrims talk. They say the Choir's hunting someone born wrong. A null-born, they're calling it. A person magic can't touch—or can't abide." He looked at her sideways. "You'd know about that better than most."

"I don't know what you mean."

"No," Doss said. "Course you don't." And he moved off into the crowd, a head taller than everyone, positioning himself between Vesper and the table without making it look like positioning.

Vesper should have left. Every instinct she had honed over twenty years of contracts and ambushes said: leave now, quietly, be elsewhere when the net comes down. But her feet stayed rooted in the trampled snow, because leaving would be a confession, and because—she admitted it only to herself, in the smallest room of her heart—some part of her wanted to be tested. Wanted the ring to stay cold. Wanted proof, finally, after twenty years of silence, that whatever was wrong with her was nothing at all.

* * *

The morning wore on. The line shortened. Children were brought forward by mothers who crossed themselves with the old sign, the pre-silent sign, the one that had gone out of fashion when the gods stopped listening. One boy, the cooper's son, made the ring glow so bright it threw shadows, and Corvane knelt and spoke to him gently for a long moment, and the mother glowed harder than the ring did.

And then the line was gone, and the green was emptying, and Corvane began to pack his relics with the same unhurried ceremony, and Vesper turned to go—

"Hold, if you please."

She stopped. Turned back. He was looking directly at her, and up close his eyes were a pale grey, almost colorless, like ice over deep water.

"You've been standing there half the morning," he said pleasantly. "Watching every blessing. Never once approaching. In my experience, sister, there are two kinds of people who watch and don't approach: those who fear the fire, and those whom the fire fears."

"I'm neither. I'm a mercenary. I'm paid to watch things."

"Ah." His smile deepened by a degree. "A soldier of Hollowmere. Of course. Forgive me—I forget that this valley breeds its own kind of devotion." He gestured at the table. "Indulge me anyway. It costs nothing, and the truce protects you. Hold the ring. Speak a wish or speak nothing. Thirty seconds, and you may go about your violence in peace."

Around them, the lingering villagers had gone quiet in that particular way crowds go quiet—not silent, but *attentive*, leaning inward. Vesper felt their attention on her skin like sunburn. Doss had drifted closer again, close enough that she could smell the tallow on his coat.

Every rational part of her said *walk away*. You owe this stranger nothing. Truce cuts both ways.

But refusing would be worse than any result. Refusing would be a story told in taverns tonight and repeated to other grey-cloaked men tomorrow. *The mercenary who wouldn't touch the relic.*

"All right," she said. "But I don't wish for anything."

"Most honest souls don't," Corvane said. "Wishes are for people who believe they'll be refused."

He lifted the iron ring from its cloth and held it out to her on his open palm.

Vesper reached for it.

The moment her fingers touched the metal, she felt it—the thing she had spent twenty years pretending she couldn't feel, the pressure of magic against her skin like a hand pressed flat against a windowpane. The ring was full of Cindral's borrowed warmth, a thin thread of living fire, and against her it behaved exactly as everything magical had always behaved in her presence, from hedge-charms to the fever-blessings that failed at her bedside as a child: it *shrank*. She felt it recoil, curl inward, a flame flinching from a draft.

*Cold,* she thought. *Let it read cold. Cold is nothing. Cold is just a valley girl with bad circulation.*

For three seconds, it worked. The ring lay dead in her grip, colder than the air, and Corvane watched it with his colorless eyes, and she began to compose the shrug she would give him, the mercenary's bored little lift of the shoulders—

Then he gripped her hand.

His fingers closed over hers, over the ring, trapping it between their palms, and he leaned in slightly and said, softly, for her alone: "Sometimes the fire needs help finding what hides from it."

The ring flared.

Not warmed—*flared*. Heat bloomed between their hands, sudden and violent, and Vesper heard herself make a sound she didn't authorize, a short sharp breath, and she tried to pull back and found his grip like a shackle. And then—

The crack was small. A sound like an icicle breaking, like a knuckle popping, like the world clearing its throat.

The ring split.

A hairline fracture ran across the iron, and through it leaked a thread of light—not red, not gold, but a white so hot it looked blue—and the light *hissed* where it touched her skin, and Corvane snatched his hand back as if bitten, and the broken halves of the relic dropped into the snow, where they smoked and hissed and went dark.

The green was absolutely silent.

Steam rose from Vesper's palm. That was the detail she fixated on, later, in the long sleepless dark: steam, rising off her own hand in the freezing air, as though she were the hot thing, as though *she* were the ember. There was no mark. No burn. Her palm was unbroken, pink, ordinary, steaming gently like fresh bread.

Corvane stared at the ruined ring. Then at her hand. Then at her face.

What was in his expression was worse than triumph. It was worse than suspicion. It was *recognition*—the look of a man who has spent years searching a crowded room for one face and has finally, finally found it, and finds it utterly unremarkable, and is delighted by the unremarkableness, because it means no one else will ever see it coming.

"Well," he said lightly, to the crowd, to the frozen silence. "Old iron. Twenty centuries old, in point of fact. It was bound to fail eventually." He laughed—a warm, easy, generous laugh—and the crowd laughed with him in relieved fragments, because laughter offered by a man like that is not really optional. "Forgive me, sister. I have handled that relic ten thousand times and forgotten it is mortal, like everything the gods touch." He bowed to her, shallow and correct. "You have my thanks. You've taught me humility today, and humility is the first lesson of the fire."

Vesper heard herself say, "No charge for the lesson," and heard the crowd chuckle, and heard her own voice come out steady, and marveled at it from a great distance, the way one marvels at a stranger walking calmly out of a burning house.

* * *

She didn't remember crossing the green. She remembered pieces: Doss's hand landing heavy on her shoulder, steering her; the smell of the smithy's coal smoke; the sound of her own blood in her ears like surf. Then they were in the company's rented rooms above the Wayfarer's Rest, door barred, shutters drawn, and Doss was pressing a cup of something raw and spiritous into her good hand and saying nothing, which from Doss was a speech.

"He knows," she said finally. Her voice sounded scraped.

"He suspects."

"He *knows*, Doss. Did you see his face? That wasn't doubt. Doubt looks like a question. That looked like—" She stopped, because she couldn't find the word, and then found it and wished she hadn't. "That looked like a receipt."

Doss crouched in front of her, forearms on his knees, and looked at her the way he looked at maps before a bad contract. "Here's what I saw," he said. "I saw a man break his own relic."

Vesper blinked. "What?"

"Think. That ring survived twenty years of pilgrims, children, farmwives, every sweating true believer in three valleys. It survives *them* fine. Then a hollowmere-born sellsword touches it and it cracks like a dropped plate?" He shook his head slowly. "Either your hand is a miracle or his theater is. And I've watched enough Choir men work crowds to know they travel with breakable props. Maybe he wanted a reaction. Maybe he wanted to see who flinched."

"And if he didn't?"

"Then we have a problem." Doss stood, joints cracking, and went to the shuttered window and peered through the gap with one eye. "He's in the square still. Talking to the reeve. Smiling. Being gracious about his broken toy." A pause. "He's asked for lodging at the temple-house. Said he'll stay 'a few days.' Said he wants to bless the sick, since he's here." Another pause. "Vess. The sick list starts with the Harrow farm. Fevered twins. Maren Ostwick's been tending them."

The name landed in her chest like a stone dropped down a well. Maren. Who had raised her. Who had taught her letters and prayer and how to bind a wound, who had given her the blank medallion at fourteen and told her *it holds whatever you bring to it*, who had never once, in twenty-three years, answered any question about where she came from with anything but a gentle deflection.

"Maren has nothing to hide," Vesper said.

"Nobody has nothing to hide from a man who breaks relics for a living." Doss turned from the window. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow I want you visible, boring, and surrounded. Market at dawn. Buy turnips. Be the dullest woman in Hollowmere."

"Doss."

"Yeah?"

"What if the ring didn't break because of theater?" She looked down at her palm, still faintly pink, impossibly unmarked. "What if it broke because of me?"

For a long moment Doss said nothing. Outside, the wind moved through the valley, and somewhere below a pilgrim was singing—one of the new songs, the answered-prayer songs, sweet and simple and wrong.

"Then we'll deal with that too," he said at last. "One problem at a time. That's the whole trade, Vess. Everything else is just noise."

He left. She sat alone in the lamplight with her untouched cup and her blank medallion, and she became aware, gradually, of a warmth against her sternum. Small. Steady. Growing.

She pulled the cord over her head and opened her hand, and the medallion lay in her palm, and it was warm. Not the false warmth of a body's heat trapped in metal. A warmth from inside, like a stone from a hearth, like a sleeping animal, like—

Like something that had heard a cracked ring hiss against her skin, and had stirred in its long sleep, and was pleased.

Down in the square, faint through the shutters, she could hear Brother Corvane's voice, blessing the evening crowd, warm and gracious and patient.

And beneath it, so quiet she might have invented it, another voice—the first syllable of a word twenty years unsaid, rising through her ribs like heat through ash:

*They are watching. Let them watch. They cannot find what does not yet know itself.*

Vesper closed her fist around the medallion until the warmth bit, and in the dark behind her eyes she saw the inquisitor's colorless gaze, patient as frost, already turning toward the temple-house, toward the fevered twins, toward Maren Ostwick and every carefully curated lie of her childhood.

Three days, he had said. He intended to stay three days.

She had two.
