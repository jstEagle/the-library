## 11. Doss's Bargain

The rain came to Hollowmere in the wrong season, and that was the first thing that told Doss the world outside the valley had gone mad.

Snow should have been falling—had fallen for three days straight before it turned—and now it fell as cold grey water that hissed where it struck the coals of the watchfire and left the whole camp smelling of wet ash and iron. He sat under the awning of the supply wagon with his boots propped on an anvil-stone, cleaning a blade he had already cleaned twice, and watched Vesper across the fire.

She was mending a strap. That was all. Sitting on an upturned bucket with her sleeves rolled to the elbow despite the cold, working the awl through leather with the same unhurried patience she brought to everything, her breath steaming, her dark hair escaping its braid in wisps that the damp curled against her neck. The blank medallion at her throat caught the firelight and gave nothing back, as it always did. Twenty years Doss had known her, and the medallion had never once reflected so much as a spark.

Tonight, he could have sworn it held a faint warmth, like a stone pulled from a hearth.

He told himself he was imagining it. He was getting old. Old men imagined warmth in cold things and meaning in empty ones.

* * *

The Choir's man came at dusk, when the light went thin and blue and the sentries' eyes were at their worst.

Doss saw him first from the wagon—a figure walking up the old drover's road out of the west, alone, unhurried, no horse and no pack, which in this weather meant either a fool or a man who did not feel the cold. By the time he reached the picket line Doss had already counted the ways he could be killed and found four of them, which was two more than most travelers offered.

He was young, or young-looking, in the way Choir men were: smooth-faced, grey-robed, with ash rubbed into his hairline in the shape of a crown. His eyes were pale as river ice and just as still. When he stopped at the edge of the firelight and lifted both hands, palms out, Doss noted that they were uncalloused. A man who had never held anything heavier than a censer.

"Serjeant Doss." Not a question. "Of the Company of the Broken Oath."

"That depends who's asking."

"A friend of the faith." The man smiled. It was a well-made smile, fitted to his face like a good boot, and about as warm. "Prelate Sarelle sends her regards to a soldier of reputation."

"The Prelate of the Ashen Choir knows my name." Doss set down the blade he wasn't cleaning and let his hand rest on his knee instead, loose, ready. "I'm flattered and frightened in equal measure."

"You should be neither." The Choirman glanced past him, toward the fire, toward Vesper, and Doss felt something cold walk down his spine on small feet. "I'm here about your contract. I understand you hold one for escort work through the valley. I also understand your company has been in Hollowmere a long time. Roots, serjeant. A house in Thistle Row. Burial rights in the churchyard at Lowmere. A man with roots thinks carefully before the ground shifts under him."

"The ground hasn't shifted."

"No?" The pale eyes came back to him. "Prayers are being answered again, serjeant. All over the realm. And here in this valley—the one place in the world where the gods went silent—there are rumors of a woman whom magic cannot touch. Whom magic *flees*. They call her the null-born. And my Prelate would very much like to meet her."

Behind Doss, someone laughed at the far fire. Brannic, probably, telling one of his ugly stories. The sound of it was obscene against the silence between the two men.

"There's no null-born," Doss said. "There's a valley full of frightened pilgrims and a tavern full of liars. You've ridden a long way for gossip."

"I haven't ridden at all." The Choirman tilted his head. "And I haven't come for gossip. I've come with an offer, because the Prelate prefers to buy what she could otherwise take. It saves wear on everyone's souls."

* * *

They walked, because the Choirman asked for privacy and Doss wanted him away from the fires and away from Vesper's ears, out along the woodline where the dripping pines stood black against the last grey light. The rain had thinned to a fine mist that beaded on wool and didn't fall so much as hang.

"Ten thousand crowns," the Choirman said.

Doss kept walking.

"Gold, minted in Cindral's own house, weighed before witnesses. Ten thousand. Enough to buy the company a keep. Enough to buy every sword in it a farm and every widow it ever made a pension. Enough that none of you would ever again have to stand in the rain and count the ways a stranger might die." The Choirman's voice was soft, reasonable, almost kind. "All you must do is bring us the woman. Alive. Unharmed. She will be *honored*, serjeant. Whatever she is, whatever burns or doesn't burn in her, the Choir does not intend her harm. We intend her *veneration*."

"And if I say no?"

"Then we will find her anyway, and you will have refused ten thousand crowns for the privilege of standing next to her when we do." The Choirman stopped walking. In the half-dark his ash-crowned head seemed to float above his shoulders like something severed. "I don't say that as a threat, serjeant. I say it as arithmetic. She is going to be found. The only question left in the world is whether you profit from it."

Doss looked at him for a long moment. Rain dripped off the pine boughs between them, counting seconds.

"You know what I was doing twenty years ago?" Doss said. "Before the company? I was a levy-man in the border wars. Stood in a shield wall at Carrow Ford and watched a lord spend four hundred lives to take a bridge nobody needed. I learned something there about men who talk about veneration while other people bleed." He rolled his shoulders, felt the old scar across his back pull tight. "No. Find your own null-born. This one's not for sale."

The Choirman studied him with those still eyes, and then, horribly, he smiled again—that fitted smile—and inclined his head as if Doss had passed some examination.

"So I was told you'd say." He reached into his robe and produced a small object: a coin, heavy gold, stamped with a flame. He set it on a stump between them, deliberate as a man laying down a card. "Keep it. Consider it not payment but a *promise*. The offer stands until the woman is in our hands, however she comes to them. And serjeant—" the pale eyes lifted, mild as milk, "—the Prelate knows about the house on Thistle Row. She knows about the burial rights. She knows you took no fee for the winter you sheltered the Kellam orphans, and she knows why. A man with that much to lose should think very carefully about what refusing buys him."

Then he turned and walked back down the road into the mist, and the dark took him the way water takes a stone, without a ripple, and Doss stood alone among the pines with his heart going like a drum and the gold coin gleaming on the stump like a single lit eye.

* * *

He didn't take the coin. That mattered to him, though he couldn't have said why, and he told himself it didn't matter more than the rest of it.

But he didn't throw it away either. He stood over the stump for a long time, long enough for the mist to soak through his cloak and find the old wound in his shoulder, and then he picked the coin up because leaving Choir gold sitting on a stump in a valley filling with pilgrims and zealots was its own kind of madness—and he put it in the bottom of his saddlebag, under the spare socks and the whetstone, in the place where a man puts things he has decided not to think about.

Ten thousand crowns.

He tried to make the number mean nothing. It wouldn't. Numbers were honest that way; they didn't care about honor. Ten thousand crowns was the company freed of contracts forever. It was Brannic drunk and happy in a hall he owned instead of hired. It was little Tam with a surgeon's schooling instead of a scar collection. It was himself, old and gray and done, with a roof that didn't leak and no one's war to fight.

It was also, said a quieter voice, the price of Vesper Thackeray.

Doss stood in the dark and hated the Choirman very precisely, the way you hate a blade that has slipped between ribs without cutting the skin—no wound to show, no blood, just the cold thing inside you that wasn't there before.

Because that was the filth of it. Not the threat. The threat he could spit on. It was the *arithmetic*. The Choirman hadn't lied to him, hadn't pretended the woman would be safe, hadn't even pretended much veneration. He had simply laid out the truth like a merchant lays out cloth: she will be taken; the only variable is you. And beneath the truth, folded small as a knife in a sleeve, the other truth: *you love her, and love can be priced.*

Did he? Twenty years, and he had never once said it, never once let it take a shape that could be named and therefore lost. It lived in smaller things—in the way he always took the watch that ended nearest dawn because she slept badly before light; in the fact that he knew how she took her tea and how she took her wounds and which of those she'd refuse help with; in the way the word *home*, which had meant nothing to him for forty years, had quietly come to mean wherever she was standing. If that was love, it was the kind that grew in the dark, like a root under a floor, invisible and load-bearing, and you didn't learn it was there until you tried to move the house.

He walked back into camp. The fires had been fed; the company was settling into its evening rhythms, Tam arguing with Brannic over dice, Wren oiling her bowstring with the concentration of a priest at prayer. And Vesper still on her bucket, still mending the strap, except she wasn't. She had stopped. She was looking at the fire with an expression Doss had never seen on her face—not fear exactly, and not wonder, but the place where those two things share a border.

Her medallion, in the firelight, was glowing. Faintly. Like a coal remembered rather than seen.

She pressed her palm flat against it, and Doss watched her lips move around words too quiet to carry, and something in his chest went tight and cold, because he understood suddenly that the world the Choirman had described—the shifting ground, the rumor, the hunt—was not coming toward Vesper Thackeray.

It was coming *out* of her.

* * *

Later, when the camp slept, Doss sat awake in the wagon with the coin in his fist.

He had decided, somewhere between the pines and the fire, what he would do, and the decision had surprised him by being easy. He would refuse. He had refused. There was no version of any world, he was fairly sure, in which he handed Vesper to the Choir for gold; the thought fit his hands as poorly as another man's gloves. That part was settled, and settling it felt like setting a bone—painful, then clean.

What was not settled, what sat in his stomach now like swallowed river-stone, was the rest of it. The part where he said nothing.

He turned it over and over, the way he'd turned the coin. Telling her was dangerous—the Choirman had known things, known names and houses and kindnesses, which meant the Choir had eyes in the valley, which meant anything Vesper knew might reach Sarelle's ears, and Vesper with that information might do something brave and stupid, which was the only kind of thing she did. Silence protected her. Silence was tactics.

Silence was also the Choirman's arithmetic wearing his own voice. Because if he told her, she would know that she was hunted, and she would know that her own sergeant had been offered her price, and she would look at him—at all of them—with that new arithmetic behind her eyes, and something between them would calcify forever whether he deserved it or not. He had watched soldiers learn their comrades had been bought. It never washed out. Trust was a rope; you could splice a frayed rope, but everyone who used it afterward tested it with their weight.

So: silence. For her protection. For the company's cohesion. For a dozen good reasons arranged in a tidy row like gravestones, and underneath them, deeper than all of them, the true one, which was that Doss was a coward in exactly one respect and had been for twenty years—he could face a shield wall, a blizzard, a Choir inquisitor's smile, but he could not face Vesper Thackeray knowing she might one day learn what she was worth, and wonder, even for a heartbeat, even in the safety of knowing he'd refused, whether anyone had ever been tempted.

He could not bear to be counted in that number, even falsely. So he would carry the secret, and the secret would sit in him beside the coin in the saddlebag, two small weights he had chosen over honesty, and he would tell himself every morning that they were armor and not rot.

Outside, the rain had stopped. Frost was forming on the wagon canvas, feathering the seams white, and through the gap in the flap Doss could see the valley lying under the stars, the old roofs of Hollowmere silvered and still, smoke rising straight from a hundred chimneys into windless air. Somewhere out there the Choirman walked without feeling cold. Somewhere beyond the snowline, armies and pilgrims and things worse than either were turning toward this valley like iron filings toward a lodestone. Somewhere in the camp, a woman slept with a god's warmth against her breastbone and didn't yet know what she was.

Doss closed his fist around the coin until the flame-stamp bit his palm.

He was still holding it when he heard the footsteps—soft, deliberate, stopping just outside the wagon—and the flap lifted, and Vesper stood there in the frost-light with her cloak over her shoulders and her eyes wide and dark and shaken, and she said, in a voice scraped down to the quick:

"Doss. Something spoke to me. Out of the frost. It knew my name."

The coin slid from his open hand into the straw, and lay there gleaming between them like a confession neither of them had made.
