## 12. Maren's Confession, Half-Given

The records-house smelled the way it always had: dust and lamp oil and old paper gone soft as bread. Vesper stood in the doorway a long time before she went in, her hand on the frame, letting the smell of it move through her like water through a cracked jar. Twenty years of evenings in this room. Twenty years of Maren Ostwick bent over his ledgers, ink to the second knuckle, humming hymns he no longer believed in because the habit of them was comfort, and here she was at last with blood on her sleeve and a question that would not stay swallowed.

He hadn't seen her yet. He sat at the long table beneath the high window, where the afternoon light came down gray through the snow-frosted glass, and he was writing—of course he was writing—the pen moving in small careful strokes, his shoulders curved around the page like a man sheltering a candleflame from wind.

She had rehearsed this on the walk up from the tavern. She had rehearsed it on the walk down from the pass, and on the walk back into the valley, and in the dark hours before dawn when sleep would not hold her and Vael's voice still rang in her skull like a struck bell: *They are coming for what burns in you.* She had rehearsed fury. She had rehearsed calm. She had imagined setting the ledger on the table between them, opening it to the burned-out entry, and saying nothing at all, letting the absence speak.

What she actually said, when she finally pushed the door shut behind her and the latch clicked like a held breath released, was:

"You lied to me."

Maren's pen stopped. He did not look up. For three heartbeats the only sound in the room was the tick of cooling ink drying in the nib, and then he set the pen down across the well of the desk with the same unhurried precision he brought to everything, and folded his hands, and looked at her.

"Vesper." Her name in his mouth sounded like an apology already half-spoken. "You're bleeding."

"It's not mine." Mostly. The hedge-priest outside the valley had grabbed her arm when the magic recoiled from her, and his nails had been sharp as a frightened animal's, and she had wrenched away too fast. "Sit there. Don't get up. Don't do the thing you do where you make tea and everything becomes gentle and nothing gets answered."

"Vesper—"

"The ledger, Maren." She crossed the room in six strides and put both palms flat on the table, leaning over him, close enough to see the age spots on his hands and the tremor he was trying very hard not to have. "The adoption registry. Year of the Long Frost. I've read every entry in that book since I was nine years old. I used to fall asleep reading it. You know that. You *know* that. And there is a hole in it. A burned-out hole, right where a foundling girl should be, right where *I* should be, and you have watched me read that book my whole life and never once—" Her voice cracked and she hated it, hated the crack, hated him for hearing it. "Never once said a word."

He looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the wind moved through the bare orchard behind the records-house, and somewhere down in the village a pilgrim was singing—a thin, off-key thread of sound, one of the new songs, the ones about answered prayers—and Maren closed his eyes as if the song hurt him.

"Sit down," he said quietly. "Please."

"I'd rather stand."

"Yes," he said. "I imagine you would." He rose anyway, slowly, joints protesting, and went to the shelf where he kept the ledgers—not the public ones, the ones any villager could consult for births and deaths and land disputes, but the private row, the ones bound in gray leather with brass corners gone green with tarnish. His fingers walked along their spines and stopped. He did not take one down. He stood with his hand resting on the spine of the third book as though it were the shoulder of an old friend he was about to disappoint.

"Do you remember," he said, "the winter you turned eleven? When you fell through the ice on the millpond?"

Vesper's jaw tightened. "Don't do this. Don't soften me with memories. I know every trick you have, Maren. I learned them all at your table."

"I'm not softening you." He turned then, and his eyes were wet, which startled her more than anger would have; she had never once seen Maren Ostwick weep, not at funerals, not at the mass grave-digging after the fever year. "I am trying to find the place to begin, and every place I can find is a memory, because the truth does not begin anywhere. It has no beginning I can point to. It begins before you. It begins before me. So let me start with the ice, and you may hate me while I talk, but you will listen, because you have always listened even when you pretended otherwise."

She did not sit. But she stopped leaning over the table, and she crossed her arms, and she waited, and that was its own kind of answer.

"You fell through the ice," he said again, "and you were under for longer than anyone could survive. Four minutes. Brann Hallow counted. He counted out loud because counting kept him from panicking, and afterward he swore to me he'd miscounted, because four minutes is impossible, and yet you came up through the hole coughing and furious and cold to the bone but *alive*, and by evening you had no mark on you at all. Not even chilblains." He paused. "I went home that night and I drank half a bottle of plum brandy and I sat with the third ledger open in front of me until dawn, and I thought about telling you. You were eleven. You were so angry about being pulled out—you wanted to know why everyone was making such a fuss, why they all looked at you strangely afterward. And I told them all it was shock. I told them the cold had simply not had time to bite. And they believed me, because I was the archivist, and archivists are believed, and that is a terrible power, Vesper. That is a power I have abused for twenty-three years."

The word landed in the room like a stone dropped down a well. Twenty-three years. She was twenty-three years old.

Her arms uncrossed slowly. "Say the number again."

"Twenty-three years." He lifted the third ledger down at last and carried it to the table, and laid it open—not to the burned page, but to a page near the front, a page of ordinary entries in his younger handwriting, neat as stitching. "Because that is how long ago this entry was made, and I made it, and I have regretted the making of it every day since, and I have never once undone it, because undoing it felt more dangerous than living with it. Read it."

She read it.

*Received, by night, without escort or papers, one infant, female, approximately three weeks old. Brought by a woman who would not give her name, who was burned about the hands and face and who refused healing, saying she had earned the burning. The child was warm—unnaturally so, as though fresh from a fire, though her clothes were snow-wet and she had traveled far in winter. The woman said only: keep her where the fire cannot be seen. Then she asked me to write nothing, and I disobeyed her, because I am an archivist, and what is written cannot be hunted.*

Below that, in different ink, darker, added later: *The woman did not survive the week. Buried in the potter's field under the rowan, unmarked, per her request. The child thrives. She laughs at flames. God forgive me, she warms my hands when I hold her, and I have told no one.*

Vesper read it twice. The second time her lips moved without sound. The room seemed to tilt gently, the way the millpond had tilted under her feet a lifetime ago, right before the ice gave.

"She laughed at flames," Maren said softly. "You did. Three weeks old, and you would fix your eyes on a candle and laugh like it was telling you a joke. I thought it was a trick of the light. I told myself a great many things were tricks of the light."

"This isn't—" Vesper's voice came out strange, flattened. She pressed her hand flat against the table to steady herself against something that wasn't moving. "This doesn't say what I am. This says how I arrived. That's not the same thing."

"No," Maren agreed. "It isn't."

"Then say the rest."

And here he faltered. She watched it happen—watched the man who had faced down debt-collectors and bandit-chieftains and once, memorably, an entire delegation of the Salt Synod with nothing but a ledger and an immovable expression, watch him stand there with his hand flat on the open book and *falter*, his mouth opening and closing, the words visibly failing him like birds refusing a barren branch.

"I don't know the rest," he said at last, and the honesty of it was somehow worse than a lie. "That is the truth, Vesper, and I know how it sounds, after everything, but it is the truth. Your mother—I assume she was your mother; she called you nothing, named you nothing, left nothing—your mother told me three things in the hour I knew her. Keep her where the fire cannot be seen. Do not let priests baptize her. And if ever the valley's silence breaks—if ever the god of this place speaks again—take her and run, and do not look back, and do not believe anything the voice tells you, no matter how kind it sounds."

The room went very cold. Or perhaps it was Vesper who went cold, from the inside out, frost blooming along her ribs where a heartbeat ago something had stirred and spoken her name.

*They are coming for what burns in you.*

*Do not believe anything the voice tells you.*

"Maren." She heard her own voice as if from a distance, level and terrible. "When did Vael go silent?"

His face changed. She saw him understand, all at once, what she was asking and why, and she saw him decide—she watched the decision move through him like weather—to lie to her one more time.

"Twenty years ago," he said. "You know that. Everyone knows that."

"When exactly?" She stepped closer. "What day? What hour? Think carefully, old man, because I will know if you're lying. I've known you twenty-three years. I know what your lies look like. They look exactly like your face looks right now."

He closed his eyes. When he opened them, he looked older than she had ever seen him, older than the records-house, older than the valley, a man standing in the ruins of a decision made long ago by a frightened young priest with an infant warming his hands.

"The day you took your oath," he whispered. "The day you swore yourself to Vael at fourteen. Mid-prayer. Mid-*word*. One moment the whole congregation felt Him the way you always feel warm sun through a window, and the next—nothing. Silence. And everyone said the god had abandoned us, the god had died, the god had turned His face away, and I let them say it." His voice broke on the last word. "Because I knew. God help me, I *knew*. The silence didn't begin that day. It began the moment you were carried through that door as an infant, and it deepened every year you grew, and on the day you swore yourself to Him—gave Him your voice, your service, your *soul*, pressed your hand to His altar with whatever burns in you beating behind your ribs—on that day the silence became total. Because you didn't come to the altar empty-handed, child. No one comes to a god carrying fire, and the fire does not care whose altar it is. It only knows it is not home."

Vesper sat down. She didn't decide to; her legs simply ended their argument with her, and she found herself in the chair across from him, the chair she'd sat in a thousand evenings, with the ledger open between them like a wound neither of them could stop looking at.

"You raised me," she said. Her voice sounded far away to herself. "In the valley where my presence was smothering a god. You raised me *inside the cage*."

"I raised you in the only place in the world where no one could see what you are," Maren said, and now the tears did come, tracking slow through the seams of his face, and he did not wipe them away. "Your mother chose this valley. She was dying and she chose it the way a soldier chooses a fortress. Magic cannot follow here. Prayers die on the air. Whatever sleeps in you could not be woken, could not be found, could not be *used*. I told myself for twenty-three years that this was mercy. Shelter. I built you a life on top of a prison and I called it a home, and you thanked me for it, and every thank-you was a knife."

Outside, the pilgrim's song had stopped. In its place, faintly, came the sound of many voices in the square—the low murmur of the crowd that had been gathering for days now, growing, pressing against the valley's edges like water against a dam. Maren turned his head toward the window, listening, and something in his expression shifted from grief to fear.

"They're asking questions," he said quietly. "The ones who came last week. Polite people, well-dressed, asking after records. Asking whether any children were received in the winter of the Long Frost. Asking after *foundlings*." He looked back at her, and his eyes were fierce suddenly, the old fierce archival ferocity she had loved and feared all her life. "I burned nothing, Vesper. I want you to understand that. I hid the true entry in the private ledger and I burned a false one into the public copy—a fake hole, a decoy wound—so that anyone who came hunting would find the absence and think it meant nothing. But they are not fools. Whoever sent them knows a record was kept. And they will keep coming, politely, well-dressed, until someone puts your face beside the word *foundling* and the arithmetic finishes itself."

Vesper looked down at her own hands. They lay in her lap, ordinary hands, scarred knuckles, a mercenary's hands—and beneath the skin, beneath the bone, something that gods fled from and hedge-priests blanched at and her dying mother had carried through the snow with her flesh on fire.

"The voice," she said. "Vael. He spoke to me. Two nights ago. First time in twenty years."

Maren went white. Actually white, the blood leaving his face like tide off a sandbar, and his hand came out across the table and gripped hers, hard, an old man's grip with desperate strength in it.

"What did he say?"

"That they're coming for what burns in me." She met his eyes. "And your dead woman's advice was not to believe anything the voice says. So tell me, Maren—which of you do I trust? The god who went silent the day I was born into his valley, or the priest who lied to me for twenty-three years and is only talking now because the hunters are at the door?"

He flinched as if she'd struck him. But he didn't let go of her hand.

"Neither," he said hoarsely. "Trust neither of us. Trust the ledger. Trust the ink. Trust the woman who died in the potter's field under the rowan, who carried you through a winter that killed her, who would not let me heal her burns because she said she had *earned* them." His grip tightened. "There is more, Vesper. There is more and I have kept it for twenty-three years and I swear to you on her unmarked grave that I will give it to you—all of it—but not here, not today, not with those polite well-dressed questioners walking the village and the square filling with strangers. Tonight. After dark. There is a place—"

The door exploded inward.

Not opened—*exploded*, the latch tearing free of the frame, snow and cold air swirling in with two figures in gray traveling cloaks, and Vesper was on her feet with a knife in her hand before her mind caught up to her body, old training moving faster than thought. But the figures weren't reaching for weapons. The taller one held up a sealed scroll, wax glinting, and spoke in a voice like oiled hinges:

"In the name of the Ashen Choir, this house of records is seized for examination. No one leaves."

Behind them, in the square, the crowd's murmur swelled—and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, Vesper heard singing. The Choir's hymn. Coming closer.

Maren's hand found hers in the chaos, and pressed something small and hard into her palm, folding her fingers over it with the speed of a lifetime's sleight-of-hand, and his eyes said what his mouth could not: *run, and tonight, the rowan tree, and everything.*

She closed her fist around whatever he had given her, and smiled at the inquisitors the way a blade smiles.
