## 13. The Winter Court Wakes

The hall had no doors.

That was the first thing visitors always noticed, when they were permitted to notice anything at all: the Long Winter's court had no doors, only archways of blue ice so old they had stopped being cold and started being *true*. Lady Isolde had ordered them carved that way a century ago, or her predecessor had—she no longer troubled herself to remember which, since the distinction had ceased to matter the day she drank from Hravn's chalice and became something that did not age so much as *accumulate*, the way snow accumulates on a mountain, layer upon patient layer, until the mountain is only a shape the snow permits.

She sat at the head of a table of black glass and listened to a man lie to her.

He was good at it. She would grant him that much. A courier out of the southern passes, frost-bitten at the fingertips, his breath steaming in the hall's perpetual autumn-cold, he spoke of roads and tolls and the price of grain in the lowlands, and threaded through all of it, delicate as wire through wax, the lie she had already tasted: that he had come to the Winter Court for no reason but commerce.

"Finish," she said, "the part you rehearsed on the stairs."

The man's throat moved. Around them, forty courtiers in white furs went very still, the way a forest goes still when it hears something walking.

"I—my lady, I don't—"

"You counted your steps. I heard you counting. Sixty-one from the outer arch to my table, and somewhere around the fortieth you began saying your speech under your breath, the way frightened men do, as if the words might wear smooth with practice." Isolde turned her goblet a quarter-turn on the glass. The wine inside had frozen weeks ago; she drank it anyway, because frozen things obeyed her. "So finish it. Whatever someone paid you to say. You have my full attention, which is more than most men earn in a lifetime."

The courier knelt. His knee cracked against the black glass like a branch breaking under snow.

"They say," he whispered, "that prayers are being answered again. In the south. In Hollowmere."

*There it is.*

Isolde did not move. Movement, in her experience, was a confession—a tell for lesser creatures who still believed their bodies belonged to them. Instead she let the silence stretch until the courier began to tremble in earnest, his breath coming in little ragged flags of steam, and she watched the fear bloom in him the way frost blooms on a window: slowly, then all at once, then everywhere.

"Hollowmere," she repeated. The name lay on her tongue like a stone pulled from a riverbed, cold and smooth and older than the water. "The valley where nothing answers."

"Yes, lady."

"And what else do they say?"

"That—" He swallowed. Somewhere behind his eyes, self-preservation warred with greed, and greed was winning, which told her everything about who had sent him. "That a god woke there. That a candle relit itself before witnesses. That the dead silence of twenty years has broken, and every relic, every priest, every zealot in the realm is turning north like birds before a storm."

"Every relic," Isolde said softly. "Every priest." She rose. The court rose with her, forty bodies moving as one, because they had learned—or been taught—that when Lady Isolde stood, standing was simply what air did. "And yet they are wrong. All of them. Chasing echoes. Do you know why, little courier?"

"Lady?"

"Because gods do not wake in places where gods have been made silent." She descended the three steps from her dais, her hem whispering across the glass, and stopped an arm's length from the kneeling man. This close, she knew, he could see what most saw only once: that her eyes were not grey but glacial—that deep in each iris something pale and patient drifted, the way silt drifts under river ice. "Silence is not absence. Silence is a held breath. And a held breath means whatever was silenced is still *there*."

She reached out and touched his forehead with one finger.

It was not cruelty. It was arithmetic. The cold took him between one heartbeat and the next—not violently; she was never violent, violence was so *loud*—but gently, frost feathering outward from her fingertip across his skin, silvering his lashes, stilling the tremble in his shoulders mid-shiver so that he knelt forever after as a sculpture of his own terror, beautifully preserved, honestly rendered. Fear, she had found, made the finest portraiture.

The courtiers did not flinch. Good courtiers never flinched. They had watched her do this to ambassadors, suitors, one unfortunate bard, and twice to her own chamberlain, whose replacement had proven such an improvement that she sometimes thought of the execution as an act of patronage.

"He came from Sarelle," said a voice at her shoulder—the only voice permitted at her shoulder. Vane, her seneschal, a thin man with a scholar's stoop and a killer's hands, wrapped in wolf-fur gone grey as his own hair. "The Ashen Choir. The message was in his boot heel. Coded, of course. Sarelle does love her ciphers."

"Of course she does." Isolde returned to her dais and seated herself, arranging her skirts with the care of a woman setting a chessboard. "Read it."

Vane produced a slip of vellum no larger than a playing card. He read aloud, his voice dry as paper:

"*To the Throne of the Long Winter, greetings and frost. Our lord Cindral has woken first, and firstness is a kind of ownership. We have word of a vessel in Hollowmere—a null-born, untouched by any working, whom even hedge-priests cannot read. We believe the old fire sleeps in her. We go to take it. We send this courtesy so that Your Ladyship need not waste a winter's march on a prize already claimed.*"

A pause.

"There's more," Vane said. "*Consider this charity between great powers.*"

Isolde laughed.

It was not a warm sound. Nothing about her was warm, and her laughter least of all—it came out bright and ringing and utterly without mirth, like ice cracking on a lake at midnight, and the courtiers bowed their heads beneath it as though under falling snow.

"Charity," she said. "Between great powers. Oh, Sarelle. Twenty years building your ash-and-incense machine, twenty years teaching your inquisitors to smile while they burn, and you still think courtesy is a weapon anyone fears." She looked down the length of her court, at the pillars of ice veined with frozen light, at the banners of white silk hanging motionless in air that never stirred unless she wished it to. "They think I am a rival to be managed. A season to be waited out. They think winter is what happens when the fire goes away."

"And isn't it?" Vane asked. It was not insolence; Vane had earned the right to ask her true questions, which was the rarest currency in the court. "Winter, lady. Isn't that exactly what it is?"

"No." She let the word fall and shatter. "Winter is not the absence of fire. Winter is what fire *answers to*. Every flame in this world burns on borrowed time and calls the borrowing warmth. Every hearth, every forge, every prayer-candle—it all runs back to the same source, the same first burning, the same stolen light. And everything that burns—" she smiled, and forty courtiers pretended not to see it, "—can be put out."

* * *

Afterward, alone—or as alone as a god's avatar ever is, with Hravn murmuring along the edges of her mind like wind along a ridge—Isolde walked the long gallery of her palace where the trophies hung.

Not trophies of conquest. Those bored her; conquest was a young person's vice. These were older. Here, suspended in blocks of flawless ice, hung the last prayers of dead gods.

She paused before the largest: a shard of something that had once been a voice, salvaged from a temple her lord had unmade four centuries ago. Even now, if she pressed her palm to the ice and listened, she could hear it faintly—a syllable repeating, over and over, the final word of a dying divinity. She had never learned its meaning. She suspected there was none. That was the lesson of the gallery, the lesson she had built her whole long life upon: *gods end. All of them. They end screaming a word no one translates.*

Hravn had endured by understanding this. While the other gods quarreled over worship like gulls over fish, Hravn had studied the one force older than any of them—the first fire, the ember from which every divine spark had been struck—and had understood that whoever held the source held the leash on everything lit from it. The other gods called Hravn cold. Hravn had simply done the reading.

But the Ember had vanished. Before Hravn could reach it, before any of them could, it had slipped into mortal keeping and been hidden so thoroughly that even gods went blind looking for it. Twenty-three years of silence. Twenty years of the world's prayers falling unanswered, because the gods had panicked—they had *fled*, Isolde knew, fled the valley where the lost fire slept, abandoning their faithful rather than risk touching whatever nullified them. Cowardice, dressed up as mystery. She had watched it happen with something close to contempt.

And now a candle had relit in Hollowmere. Now the held breath was ending. Now every power in the realm was turning north like wolves catching a scent on the wind.

And Sarelle—clever, ambitious, fatally sincere Sarelle—believed the prize was a vessel to be cracked open and drained.

"She thinks it's a well," Isolde murmured to the gallery, to the frozen voices, to the god coiled in her blood. "A thing you draw from. Dip your ladle, drink your fill, become sole god. As if the first fire were a *resource*."

*It is a crown,* Hravn answered, in the voice that was also her voice, that had been her voice for a hundred years until she could no longer find the seam between them. *And crowns are worn, not drunk. Tell me, my winter. What do you feel?*

She considered the question honestly, because Hravn hated flattery and loved precision.

"Hunger," she said. "But not mine."

*No. Not yours.* Something vast shifted in the cold of her, the way a glacier shifts—imperceptible, inevitable, capable of erasing valleys. *For twenty-three years I have starved politely. I have kept my winter small. A valley here. A pass there. A court of mortals who mistake patience for weakness. And all the while the Ember slept, and I could not sleep, because I remembered what I was before I was anything—remembered the first morning, the first light, the fire we were all struck from.* A pause, long as an ice age. *I want to go home, Isolde. Gods are not supposed to say such things. But I am telling you, because you are the part of me that walks.*

"Then we walk north," Isolde said.

*The Choir moves first. Sarelle has inquisitors on the road already.*

"Let her. Let her spend her faith and her fanatics prying at the valley. Let her find the vessel and discover that vessels bite." Isolde resumed walking, past trophy after trophy, her reflection sliding across the ice-blocks like a ghost attending its own funeral. "We will not race her. Racing is for creatures who fear losing. We will arrive *after*—when the Choir has dug up the ground and the vessel has burned whatever hand reached for it, and everyone in that miserable valley has learned exactly how dangerous the thing in their midst truly is. Fear prepares a feast better than any cook. We will simply sit down at the table."

*And if the vessel cannot be taken? If it must be given?*

"Then we will make giving look like the only mercy left." She smiled again, and this time she let the courtiers' reflections see it. "I am very good at mercy, my lord. I have practiced on a century of people who begged for it."

* * *

She gave her orders at dusk, which in the Long Winter's court was a formality—the light outside the high windows changed color on schedule, a courteous fiction her sorcerers maintained, because even tyrants understood that mortals needed to believe in evenings.

Vane stood before her dais with a ledger open, quill ready, recording her commands in his small precise hand.

"The court rides in nine days," Isolde said. "Not the whole host—I am not marching an army south to fight over scraps with ash-priests. Three hundred. My knights of the Rime, the choir of frozen voices, and the sleighs. We travel as pilgrims. White cloaks, open hands, hymns on our lips."

"Pilgrims," Vane repeated, quill pausing. "Lady, the Rime Knights haven't worn pilgrim's cloth since—"

"Since the Sack of Corvel. Yes. Which is precisely why it will terrify everyone who remembers it." She rose, drifting down the steps, trailing cold like a comet trails its tail. "Send riders ahead—not to Hollowmere, not yet. To the shrine-towns on the northern roads. Let it be known, quietly, in the way such things are always known, that the Lady of the Long Winter has received a vision. That she comes south seeking the child of the new miracle. That she comes in peace." Her mouth curved. "Peace, Vane, is the most frightening rumor a ruler can spread. It makes everyone imagine what the alternative would be."

"And the vessel herself? When we find her?"

Isolde stopped before the gallery's greatest trophy—the dead god's last syllable, ticking away inside its ice like a heart that hadn't been told to stop.

"Everything Sarelle will do to that girl, she will do with knives and rites and scripture," she said. "Extraction. Excision. She'll treat the Ember like a tumor to be cut out, and she'll be surprised—genuinely surprised—when the girl screams in a language older than scripture. No." She touched the ice, and far away, in a snowbound valley she had never seen, she imagined a candle guttering. "We will offer the vessel a chair at our table. An education. A purpose. Everyone wants to matter, Vane; it is the softest spot on any creature, and the deepest. We will not carve the fire out of her."

"Then what, lady?"

"We will ask her to hand it over," Isolde said, "and mean it kindly. And when she refuses—as she will, they always refuse—we will show her a winter so complete, so patient, so *reasonable*, that surrender begins to look like spring." She turned back toward the dais, robes whispering, the temperature of the hall dropping degree by degree as her mood tightened like a fist around the world. "Sarelle wants to consume a god. Hravn wants to reclaim one. But I—" she settled onto her throne, and the frozen wine in her goblet crackled softly, listening, "—I intend to be *understood*. There is a difference. Consumption leaves ruins. Understanding leaves converts."

Vane bowed and withdrew, ledger under his arm, footsteps soundless on the black glass.

Alone at last—or near enough—Isolde looked south, through walls of ancient ice, toward a valley she had never visited and already despised. Somewhere down there, a candle had relit itself. Somewhere down there, a woman walked around with the first fire sleeping in her chest, ignorant as a sleeper ignorant of the avalanche gathering above the mountain. Somewhere down there, the held breath of twenty years was almost finished.

"Little vessel," Isolde said to the distance, gently, the way one speaks to something already caught. "Burn quietly a while longer. We are coming, and we are in no hurry, and that should frighten you far more than haste ever could."

Outside the high windows, the courteous fiction of dusk failed all at once, and true dark poured over the Winter Court like ink into water.

In nine days, they rode.
