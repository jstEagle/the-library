## 14. Emberlight

The snow had been falling for three days without stopping, and Hollowmere had gone soft with it—the way the valley always did in deep winter, its edges rounded off, its sounds muffled, as though the whole world were being tucked under a blanket and told to sleep. Vesper walked the high road from the shrine at Coldbrook with her collar up and her hood drawn, and she tried very hard not to think about the way the snowflakes landed on her shoulders.

They melted.

Not all of them. Not dramatically. But enough of them that if you looked closely—if you were the kind of person who looked closely—you would see that the dusting of white that settled on every other traveler's cloak simply did not stay on hers. It beaded, shivered, and slid away, leaving the wool dark and damp, as though she carried summer folded up inside her coat like a letter she'd forgotten about.

She had been telling herself for two weeks that it was nothing. That the forge-heat of the taverns clung to her. That she walked faster than other people and worked up warmth. That wool held heat differently depending on—she didn't know—on the phase of the moon, on the alignment of the stars, on anything, anything at all besides the thing she could not say out loud because saying it would make it real.

The medallion hung against her sternum, warm as a stone left too near a fire. She had stopped touching it in public. She touched it now, through her shirt, the way you press a bruise to check whether it still hurts.

It still hurt.

* * *

The trouble with Hollowmere in winter was that there was nowhere to be alone. The valley was a bowl, and the roads all funneled down into it, and lately everything funneled down into the village of Mirrow's Edge, where the shrine stood and where the pilgrims gathered like snow against a fence line.

Vesper came down into the village at dusk and stopped at the top of the last hill, and what she saw made her breath catch in a way she resented.

There must have been four hundred people camped in the fields below. Fires dotted the white ground like embers shaken loose from some enormous hearth. Tents of waxed canvas and patched blanket-cloth huddled together in clusters organized by region—she could tell by the cut of the coats, the shape of the hoods: lowland farmers from the Weald, river-folk with their shell-charms, mountain clans in grey, even a knot of Choir pilgrims in their ash-grey vestments, standing apart from everyone else, singing in a slow unison that carried up the hill like smoke.

Four hundred people who had come to a valley where magic did not work, hoping it would start working again. Hoping the silence would break.

*It broke,* she thought. *You just don't know what broke it.*

She pulled her hood lower and started down.

The company had taken rooms at the Drowned Bell, the inn nearest the shrine, because Doss said if trouble came it would come to wherever the crowds were thickest and he preferred to already be there when it arrived. "Forewarned," he liked to say, "is forearmed, and I like my arms where they are." The Bell was full past capacity; travelers slept three to a bed and others slept in the stable among the horses, and the common room roared every night with the particular fervor of people who had traveled far to want something very badly.

Vesper slipped in through the kitchen door, as she had been doing, avoiding the common room, avoiding eyes. Brenna the cook, a broad woman who had known her since she was small enough to steal tarts off this very counter, looked up from her pots and gave her the nod that meant *I see you and I'll say nothing*, which was, Vesper thought, the rarest and most valuable currency in the valley these days.

"You look half-froze," Brenna said.

"I'm fine."

"Sit by the oven anyway." Brenna ladled something out of a pot without being asked—barley and lamb, heavy on the pepper—and set it in front of her. "Your sergeant came through an hour ago looking like a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like."

"Doss is always doing arithmetic he doesn't like."

"This arithmetic had your name in it." Brenna wiped her hands on her apron. "Vesper. Sweetling. You've been coming in through my kitchen for eleven days. You used to walk in the front door."

"The front door has four hundred pilgrims behind it."

"The front door has people who knew your mother," Brenna said, and then went quiet, and Vesper understood that the sentence had been aimed carefully, the way you aim a thrown stone—not to kill, but to land close enough to make a point.

"My mother," Vesper said slowly, "was a laundress in Coldbrook who died of lungfever when I was six."

"Mm," said Brenna, and turned back to her pots, and offered nothing else, which was somehow worse than anything she might have said.

* * *

She should have stayed in the kitchen. She knew that later, turning it over in the dark like a coin she kept finding heads-up no matter how many times she flipped it. But Maren Ostwick had sent a boy with a note—*Come to the records-house after vespers. Alone. There is more I can tell you now.*—and whatever else Maren was, whatever the burned page in his ledger made him, he was the only person alive who seemed to know what she was becoming, and hunger for answers is its own kind of stupidity.

So she went out again, into the blue-black cold, up the lane toward the records-house with its squat stone tower and its windows glowing amber. Snow squeaked under her boots. Her breath plumed. And around her, always, the small treacherous melt, the flakes dying an inch above her skin, so that she walked inside a faint private thaw, a moving circle of wet air that she told herself nobody could possibly notice.

The records-house sat at the edge of the shrine square, and the square was where the pilgrims gathered for evening devotions, because the shrine's bell rang at vespers and four hundred hopeful hearts are not going to miss a bell. Vesper meant to skirt the square along the colonnade. She almost managed it.

What stopped her was the sound.

A voice, thin and cracked with age, rising over the murmur of the crowd—a woman's voice, praying aloud, the way people had begun to do here, testing the silence each time like children poking a sleeping animal. Vesper paused in the shadow of the colonnade, meaning only to let a clot of pilgrims pass, and heard the words.

"—and my Tam, holy ones, my Tam is coughing blood since the frost came, and the physicker says his lungs are full of water, and I am asking, I am *begging*, anyone who's listening—" The old woman knelt in the snow before the shrine steps, hatless, her white hair plastered to her skull, holding a candle that guttered in the wind. "Anyone. I don't care which god. I don't care if you're new or old or dead twenty years. He's seven years old. He's *seven*."

The crowd had gone quiet around her, the way crowds do—not reverent, exactly, but hungry. Four hundred faces turned toward one kneeling woman and one failing flame, and Vesper felt the attention of all of them like a physical pressure, like standing too near a falling wall.

And beneath her breastbone, something answered.

It was not a word. It was not even a feeling, exactly. It was *heat*—a single pulse of it, deep and vast and intimate, the way the memory of sunlight is warm on skin long after sunset—and the candle flame stood straight up, doubled in size, and burned white.

The crowd gasped as one animal.

The old woman stared at her candle. Then at the sky. Then she began, softly, to laugh and weep in the same breath, rocking forward onto her hands in the snow, saying *thank you, thank you, thank you* to no one and everyone, and the crowd broke into motion—people pressing forward, people falling to their knees, people shouting questions at the sky—and above it all the candle burned steady and tall in a wind that should have killed it.

Vesper backed into the colonnade's shadow with her heart slamming and her hand pressed flat over the medallion, which was no longer warm.

It was hot. Hot enough that she felt it through linen and wool both, hot enough that when she pressed her palm to it she expected to smell her own skin scorching. And inside her chest, in the space behind and beneath her heart where the pulse had come from, something settled back down like a great beast folding itself smaller, and she heard—or imagined she heard, or dreamed, or remembered—a thread of something that was almost a voice:

*Not yet. Not here. Hold.*

Then nothing. Just her own heartbeat, and the roar of the crowd, and the candle burning white on the shrine steps.

She ran. She was not proud of it. She pulled her hood down and went along the colonnade and cut through the tithe-barn yard, and she was three streets gone before she realized she wasn't running toward the records-house anymore. She was running away—from Maren's summons, from the note in her pocket, from the word *more*, from everything. Her feet took her up the mill road instead, out of the village, toward the high pasture where she'd played as a child, because when the world becomes unbearable the body goes looking for the places it was small enough to be safe in.

* * *

The pasture lay white and empty under a sky clearing at the edges, the clouds tearing open to show hard stars. The millpond wore a lid of ice. The willows along the stream were furred with frost, each twig cased in glass, and when the wind moved them they chimed faintly, like distant bells made of something colder than bronze.

Vesper stood in the middle of the field and finally let herself shake.

It had been building for weeks—the warming medallion, the melting snow, the hedge-priest outside the valley who had looked at her and gone the color of tallow and refused her coin. The stirrings. The voice that was almost a voice. She had been carrying it all the way you carry a full cup across a crowded room: carefully, breath held, watching the surface tremble and never spill.

But tonight the cup had sloshed. Tonight a child's fever and an old woman's candle had pulled a response out of the thing inside her, and the thing inside her had *answered*, and four hundred people had seen a miracle, and none of them knew that the miracle had been paid for out of her chest.

"Hold," she said aloud, to it, to herself, to the empty field. "You said hold. So hold."

The medallion pulsed against her hand.

"No," she whispered. "No, no—we're not doing this here, there are people, there are *always* people now—"

The heat rose anyway.

Later she would try to reconstruct the order of it and fail. Whether the heat came first or the light, whether she cried out or whether the cry was only in her ears. What she remembered—and she would remember this, in fragments, for the rest of her life—was the sensation of something vast turning over in its sleep beneath her ribs, stretching once, luxuriously, the way a cat stretches, without any malice at all, without any awareness of what its claws might catch.

The snow around her did not melt gradually.

It vanished.

In a perfect circle, thirty feet across, every flake of snow within it simply ceased—no puddle, no slush, no steam curling up, just bare brown grass appearing as though winter had never reached that ground, as though someone had cut a hole in the season itself with a knife. The frost on the willows at the circle's edge sublimated into glittering mist. The ice on the millpond groaned, a long cracking note, and a seam of open black water opened down its middle, steaming gently in the starlight.

And Vesper stood at the center of it, lit from below and within, because the light had come from her—from her skin, from the seams of her, from everywhere the blood ran close to the surface. She glowed the color of coals breathing. She could see the bones of her own fingers through her gloves. When she screamed—and she did scream, she was almost sure she screamed—it came out as steam.

It lasted perhaps ten heartbeats. Perhaps five. Time went strange and syrupy, and she had the sense of being watched from inside her own body by something patient and enormous and *sorry*, and then the heat withdrew, folding down and down and down into that place behind her heart, and the glow guttered out, and she was standing in a circle of bare earth in a white field, steaming like a kettle, alone.

Except she wasn't alone.

She heard them before she saw them—voices on the mill road, lantern-light bobbing between the trees. Pilgrims. Of course pilgrims. The whole valley was a pilgrimage now; there was no hour of day or night when the roads were empty; there was no *empty* left anywhere in Hollowmere. They had seen the light. Everyone within a mile had seen the light, because a pillar of ember-glow rising out of a dark pasture on a winter night is not a subtle thing, and by the time Vesper got her legs under her and her hood up, there were already a dozen of them spilling through the gate at the field's edge, and more coming behind them, and lanterns swinging, and voices calling out—

"Here! Here, look—"

"What is it? What happened?"

"The snow's *gone*—look at the pond, the pond's open—"

"Someone's standing there. Someone's standing in the middle of it."

Vesper did the math with terrible clarity: the gate was forty yards away. The field was flat. The snow was gone, which meant no cover, no shadows, no ditch, nothing but her and thirty feet of naked brown grass and a ring of frozen willows catching the lantern-light like chandeliers. She could run for the stream, for the mill, for the tree line—but running confirmed everything, and besides, her legs felt like poured candle wax.

So she stood still, and pulled her hood down over her face, and became, with the desperate discipline of twenty years' practice, nobody. Shoulders rounded. Weight settled. A tired traveler pausing on the road, nothing more, nothing to see.

It did not work. For the first time in her life, it did not work.

Because the evidence was all around her, written in the earth. Thirty feet of bare ground in a world of white, centered precisely on the spot where she stood, and no wind explains that, and no lantern, and no trick of the eye. The pilgrims spread out along the circle's edge the way people approach a shrine, cautious and hungry, and one of them—an old man with a shepherd's crook—walked right up to the boundary and stopped, and looked at the grass, and looked at her, and looked at the grass again.

"It's warm," he said. His voice shook. "The ground's warm. I can feel it from here." He raised his head, and his eyes found her hood, found the shadow beneath it, and did not let go. "Woman. Woman, take down your hood."

"She's just someone on the road," another pilgrim said uncertainly. "Aren't you? Traveler?"

"Take down your hood."

More of them were arriving. Lanterns multiplied along the fence line. Somewhere behind them, someone began to run toward the village, shouting as he went, and Vesper understood with a sick lurch that whatever happened in the next minute would happen twice: once here, and once again in every mouth in Mirrow's Edge before midnight.

Her hand found the medallion. It was warm again—not burning now, but warm, steady, like a hand resting over hers. And in the quiet underneath her fear, in the cellar of herself where the voice lived, she felt it again, that enormous patience, that sorrow:

*It cannot be undone. Only borne.*

"That's easy for you," Vesper breathed. "You're not the one they'll burn."

Nobody heard it. Or somebody did—one of the pilgrims, a young woman with a babe wrapped against her chest, tilted her head as though catching a whisper on the wind—but by then the old shepherd had stepped across the invisible line into the warm bare circle, crossing himself clumsily with a hand that didn't know the proper signs, and knelt in the dead grass at Vesper's feet.

"I knew it," he said, and his voice cracked wide open. "I knew when I saw the candle on the shrine steps tonight—that wasn't any god answering. Gods answer *down*. This came up. This came up out of the ground, out of *someone*." He lifted his face to her hood. "It's you. Whoever you are—it's you. Show us. Please. Twenty years we prayed into a hole in the world, and you're what's been down there listening."

"Get up," Vesper said hoarsely. "Old man, get up, I'm a mercenary, I escort wool carts, I'm nobody—"

"The snow," said the young woman with the babe, stepping into the circle too, and then the others, one by one, crossing the line, until Vesper stood in the center of a congregation she had never asked for, on ground she had made with her own unbearable body. "The snow doesn't touch her. Look—look, it's landing on us and it won't land on her."

And it was true. She saw it herself then, unmistakable, undeniable: snowflakes drifting down through the lantern-light, settling on every upturned face around her, catching in eyelashes and wool and the babe's blanket—and dissolving, every single one, an inch above her shoulders, so that she stood in a fine bright rain of vanishing stars while everyone around her whitened.

The hood was not going to save her. Nothing was going to save her. Invisibility died not with a shout but with a snowflake, and it died right there, in front of forty witnesses, irreversibly, the way all true deaths do.

Vesper closed her eyes. And then—because there was nothing left to hide behind, and because some stubborn core of her, the same core that had sworn an oath at fourteen and mourned a god for twenty years, refused to be caught cowering—she reached up and pushed the hood back.

The lantern-light found her face. The falling snow found it too, and died there, and in the strange double illumination—lanterns gold from without, coal-glow seaming up her throat and cheekbones from within—the forty pilgrims of the mill pasture drew one collective breath.

"It's the Thackeray girl," someone said at the fence line, in a voice of pure disbelief. "That's Vesper Thackeray. Doss's company. She grew up in Coldbrook—I sold her father turnips—"

"She's not anybody's girl," the shepherd whispered. "Look at her. *Look at her.*"

And far off across the fields, back toward the village, a bell began to ring—not the shrine's vespers bell, which had long since rung, but the alarm bell at the Drowned Bell, quick and urgent, and Vesper knew with absolute certainty whose hand was on the rope, and that he had seen the light, and that he was coming for her with the whole company behind him, and that it was already too late for all of them.

Down the lane, torches. Many torches, moving fast.

And beside her ear, so close it might have been her own thought, the voice that was not a voice said, clear at last, and gentle, and terrible:

*Now they know. Run.*
