## 15. The Valley Turns

The snow had not melted where Vesper stood. Everyone in Millbrook agreed on that much, and on almost nothing else.

It had been three days since the flare—three days since the pilgrims crowding the tavern yard had watched a perfect circle of bare brown earth open around the mercenary woman like a wound in winter, steam rising off it, the snow retreating from her boots as though afraid. Three days since she'd looked down at that circle of her own making with an expression no one could agree to describe. Some said horror. Some said shame. Old Tam Wicker, who'd been drunk since midmorning and was therefore the only honest man present, said it looked like a girl finding out her house was haunted by herself.

By the second day, the circle had frozen over again, but the ice there was different—clearer than the rest, glass-bright, and when the light hit it at evening it glowed faintly gold, as if something were buried beneath it, still warm.

They called it the Hearthmark now. Children skated on it when they thought no one was watching. Pilgrims knelt around its edge and pressed their palms flat to the ice and wept.

And Vesper Thackeray's company had moved to the far end of the village, near the old tithe barn, and posted watches, and stopped drinking in the common room, and would not say why.

* * *

Brammel Hetch sold turnips, and had for thirty years, and considered himself a fair judge of what things were worth.

He stood at his stall in the market square on the third morning and watched the pilgrims buy nothing and pray everywhere, and he did the arithmetic that had kept his family fed through two famines and a plague: a miracle this size, in a valley this poor, meant money coming in—and money coming in meant someone would come to take it away. The Choir's gray wagons had been seen on the south road. Everyone knew what the Choir paid for information about the god-touched. Everyone also knew what happened to villages that lied to the Choir.

He looked across the square at the tithe barn, where the tall sergeant with the scarred jaw stood smoking, watching everyone back.

Then he looked at the Hearthmark, glowing faintly through its ice like a coin at the bottom of a well, and he thought about his daughter's bad hip, and the Choir's reputation for healing their faithful first.

He didn't decide anything, he told himself later. He just walked to the south road after dark, because it was on his way home. It wasn't on his way home. He went anyway.

The inquisitor who met him wore ash-gray robes gone stiff with road salt, and a mask of white ceramic shaped like a calm face. When Brammel told him—a mercenary woman, a null-born, the melting snow, the medallion—the masked face tilted, listening, and Brammel felt the strange vertigo of a man selling something he hadn't known he owned.

"Her name," the inquisitor said. His voice was gentle, muffled slightly by the ceramic, like a voice heard through a wall.

"Thackeray," Brammel said. "Vesper Thackeray."

"And the one who raised her? The archivist?"

The question landed wrong somehow, cold in the gut. "Ostwick," Brammel said. "Maren Ostwick. But he's nobody. He just keeps the records."

"Yes," the inquisitor said softly. "They usually do." He pressed a small iron token into Brammel's palm—a flame stamped in gray metal, cool as a dead coal. "You will be remembered as faithful."

Brammel walked home the long way, past the Hearthmark, and found he couldn't look at it. The token sat in his fist all night like a stone he couldn't put down or throw away. In the morning his daughter said her hip felt better, and he wept into his porridge, and hated himself, and went to the stall anyway, and sold turnips, and smiled at everyone, and told no one.

That was how it started. Not with betrayal, exactly. With arithmetic.

* * *

Sela Marsh had known Vesper Thackeray since they were girls stealing apples off the widow Crane's tree, back when Vesper was all elbows and silence and a stare that made grown men hand over whatever they were holding. She'd known her through the oath-taking at fourteen, through the years of contracts and long absences, through every homecoming where Vesper stood in Sela's doorway looking like someone checking whether the door still opened for her.

So it was Sela—not the pilgrims, not the priests—who climbed the hill behind the tithe barn on the fourth morning, past Doss's pickets, who let them take her knife but not her temper, and stood in front of Vesper Thackeray in the raw wind and said what half the valley was thinking.

"You could have told us."

Vesper was sharpening a blade that didn't need sharpening. She didn't look up. "Told you what?"

"Don't." Sela's breath came out white between them. "Don't do that thing where you go quiet and make me feel stupid for asking. There are forty people camped around your snow-circle singing songs, Ves. Songs. About you. Old Mother Ansel says your eyes are the foundling's eyes, whatever that means, and now half the village thinks you're a saint and the other half thinks you're going to burn us all in our beds, and you're sitting here *sharpening a knife.*"

"I'm good at knives," Vesper said. "It's the other thing I never got practice at."

Sela wanted to laugh. She didn't. That was the worst part—that Vesper could still make her want to laugh while everything came apart. "What is it? What's inside you?"

"I don't know yet." And that, at least, was true; Sela had known her long enough to know true from brave. "Something old. Something the whole world seems to want. I don't know if it's mine or if I'm just... where it was put."

"Like a hiding place," Sela said quietly.

Vesper's hands stilled on the whetstone. The wind moved the dead grass along the fence line, and somewhere below, the pilgrims' song rose again, thin and tuneless and terribly sincere.

"They're lighting candles to me, Sela. Did you know that? Little ones, in their windows. They think I can't see." Her voice stayed level, which was how Sela knew it was costing her. "Twenty years I lived here and I was just the Thackeray girl, the one with the dead god, the one who took escort work because she was too strange to marry and too stubborn to care. Now I melt some snow and I'm a *window.* People don't look at me anymore. They look *through* me at whatever they need."

"So leave," Sela said. "Take your company and go north over the pass before those gray wagons arrive. You've run contracts in worse weather."

"It's not the weather." Vesper finally looked up, and Sela saw the thing under the tiredness, the thing that had been growing in her friend's face for days like frost on glass. "It's Maren. If I run, they'll take it out on him. And it's—" she stopped. Started again, lower. "There's a voice. Under my ribs. It's been quiet my whole life and now it won't stop talking, and half of what it says I don't understand, and the other half scares me worse than the Choir does."

Sela reached out and gripped her shoulder, hard, the way you grip someone standing on ice. "Whatever it says—it's still you in there?"

"That's exactly what I'm afraid isn't true."

Sela held on anyway. Then she went back down the hill, and told no one what had been said, and lit no candle, and slept badly, and in the morning found a candle burning in her own window that she had no memory of lighting.

* * *

Doss counted wagons the way other men counted money.

From the loft of the tithe barn he could see the south road for two miles, gray in the gray afternoon, and on it, just cresting the last rise, the first of the Choir's wagons—six of them, then eight, drawn by pale mules, escorted by riders in ash-gray with the white masks slung at their saddles, unhurried, unhidden. They weren't sneaking. They didn't have to. That was the part that turned Doss's stomach: the patience of them. They moved like men collecting a debt already owed.

"Twelve riders per wagon," said Ferren beside him, squinting. "Plus outriders. Call it a hundred and ten, maybe more behind that rise."

"We've got nineteen swords fit to fight and a barn full of pilgrims who'd stand in front of them out of *love,*" Doss said. "Love dies fast when the masks start swinging. Nineteen against a hundred and ten isn't a battle, it's a funeral with extra steps."

"So we run."

"She won't run while Ostwick's in the valley. And now—" he exhaled smoke through the gap in the boards— "half the village won't run either. They've built her a shrine out of a puddle. You can't evacuate people from their own religion."

Ferren was quiet a moment. Then, carefully: "Sarge. The offer. You ever going to tell her?"

Doss looked at him. Ferren had been with the company nine years and had the sense of a man who checked locks twice; of course he'd worked it out, or enough of it. The Choir's rider had come at dusk two nights ago, alone, unarmed, with a purse heavy enough to buy the company three times over and a simple request: deliver the null-born, alive and unspoiled, to the reliquary at Grayfall. No witnesses required. Payment on delivery.

Doss had said no. Had said it flatly, without haggling, which had surprised even himself—he was a bargainer by nature, a man who believed everything had a price and most things should be negotiated down. But some prices you paid by accepting them.

"No," he said now. "I'm not."

"She'll find out. She always finds out."

"Aye." He ground the cigarette out under his boot. "And when she does, I'll tell her myself, and she'll have every right to put me through a wall. But she'll hear it from me, not from a Choir interrogation, and not from some turnip-seller with silver in his pocket. There's a difference between a lie and a debt. I owe her the second kind."

Below them, the wagons rolled into the outskirts of Millbrook and stopped, precisely, at the edge of the market square, as though an invisible line had been drawn that they would not cross until invited. The masked riders dismounted. One of them unfurled a banner: a gray flame on white.

And from the shrine at the Hearthmark, the pilgrims began to sing louder.

Doss watched the two sounds meet over the frozen circle—the hymn and the silence of the masked men standing in rows, waiting—and understood with cold clarity that the valley had already turned. The only question left was which way, and how fast, and who would be standing on Vesper Thackeray's side of the line when it finished turning.

He went down to set the watch for night.

* * *

They came for Maren Ostwick at dusk, when the lamplighters were working and the lanes smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool.

There were six of them, masked, polite, terrifying in the way of men who have never once needed to raise their voices. They did not break his door. The inquisitor in front knocked, waited, and knocked again, and Maren opened it himself because he had spent twenty-three years knowing this knock would come and had promised himself he would meet it standing.

"Inquisitor," Maren said.

"Archivist." The white mask caught the lamplight. Behind it, the voice was young—younger than Maren had expected, and that frightened him more than cruelty would have. "We are given to understand you keep the records of this valley. Births, deaths, oaths, adoptions."

"All of it. Thirty-one years."

"May we see the ledgers?"

"They're public record," Maren said. "Any citizen may read them. That has always been the law here."

"Of course." The mask tilted, courteous. "We are not citizens."

They went in. They were gentle with the books—gentler than Maren expected, handling each ledger with gloved care, reading by his own fire as though they owned it, and Maren stood in his own kitchen with his heart going like a trapped bird and understood that gentleness was simply what efficiency looked like when it had no opposition.

It took them less than an hour. He watched the young inquisitor's finger travel down the adoption rolls of the year of the burning temple, and stop, and press flat against the page where the entry should have been—where a scorched gap sat in the ink like a missing tooth.

"Burned out," the inquisitor said. Not accusing. Almost admiring. "With a taper, from the reverse side, so the scorch would look accidental. Very careful work." He looked up, and though the mask showed nothing, Maren felt the gaze settle on him like a hand. "Who taught you to destroy a record so cleanly, Archivist? Only another archivist knows paper that well."

"The fire that burned the temple burned many things," Maren said. "Some of them traveled."

"Mm." The inquisitor closed the ledger with a soft, terrible finality. "You will come with us. Not as a prisoner"—the word *prisoner* was pronounced with exquisite delicacy— "as a guest of the Choir. We find that those who curate ignorance often possess knowledge worth... recovering. You will be comfortable. You will be asked questions. Eventually, you will answer them. They always do."

One of the masked men stepped forward to take Maren's arm, and Maren—old, dry, sixty-eight years of quiet ink-stained life—did the only defiant thing available to him. He picked up the ledger of adoptions and held it against his chest.

"This comes with me," he said. "It's mine. I kept it. Whatever's been burned out of it, I'm the one who burned it, and I'll carry the weight of it myself."

A pause. Then the inquisitor inclined the white mask, amused or respectful, impossible to tell. "As you wish, Archivist."

They walked him down the lane through the falling dusk, past windows where faces watched and lamps guttered and no door opened. Past the Hearthmark, where the pilgrims had gone silent, sensing something. Past the tithe barn, where a lamp burned high in the loft and Doss stood watching with his fists on the rail, and Maren looked up at him and gave the smallest nod—*don't*—because he had done his arithmetic too, and nineteen swords against a hundred and ten was a funeral, and he had spent twenty-three years keeping one child safe, and he would not spend her company's blood on the opening move.

At the wagon he paused, one foot on the step, and spoke to the inquisitor conversationally, as one archivist to another.

"When you find her—and you will, you're thorough—I hope you're prepared for what she is. Because I've read every book in this valley, son, and there's no entry for her anywhere. Not in any ledger. Not in any scripture. Whatever you've been sent to collect, it isn't in your catalogues." He smiled, thin and fierce. "I made sure of that."

The mask regarded him for a long moment.

Then the wagon rolled south toward Grayfall, carrying the records of Hollowmere and the man who had guarded them, and behind it the valley settled into its longest night—pilgrims praying harder, villagers bolting doors, candles lit in windows for reasons their owners couldn't name.

And up on the hill above the tithe barn, Vesper Thackeray stood alone in the wind, watching the wagon lights shrink down the south road, her hand pressed flat over the place beneath her ribs where something ancient and patient whispered, over and over, in a voice like embers settling:

*They have taken the keeper of your name. Come and take it back.*

She was moving before she finished deciding, already shouting for Doss, already awake.

* * *

In the market square, long after midnight, Brammel Hetch stood at the edge of the Hearthmark with his iron token in his fist and his daughter's easy stride in his memory, and he knelt down on the clear ice among the pilgrims' offerings—candles, flowers, a lamb's wool cap, a wooden toy—and he set the Choir's flame-token down on the frozen circle as if returning stolen goods.

The ice beneath it glowed once, softly, gold as bread.

He walked home feeling neither forgiven nor forgiven-for, which was, he suspected, simply what it felt like to live in a valley that was turning—and in the morning, when the company rode out of Millbrook with swords wrapped and lanterns hooded, bound south after the wagon, half the village lined the road to watch them go.

Some wept. Some prayed. Some spat in the snow and turned their backs.

And one small girl at the end of the lane, too young to know which side of anything she stood on, waved until they were out of sight.
