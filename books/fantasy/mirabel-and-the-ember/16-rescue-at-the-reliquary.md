## 16. Rescue at the Reliquary

The reliquary had been a granary once, back when Hollowmere still grew things worth storing. Now the Choir had hung it with chains of blessed iron and called it holy, and Vesper stood in the shadow of the woodshed across the lane, watching lamplight move behind its narrow windows like something trapped and swimming.

Snow fell in that particular way it did before dawn—not falling so much as hanging, undecided, the whole valley holding its breath. She counted guards by their shadows. Four on the door. Two walking the perimeter with censers that trailed smoke the color of old bone. The Choir's incense was supposed to sanctify; here it just made the cold smell like a sickroom.

"You're doing the thing," Doss murmured beside her, "where you count things nobody asked you to count."

"Somebody has to."

"I did. Four and two, plus the one inside who never comes out, because he's the one who likes his work." Doss shifted, and the snow creaked under him like a floorboard in an empty house. "We could wait for the changing of the watch."

"We could also grow old waiting." Vesper kept her eyes on the door. Three days since they'd taken Maren. Three days since the villagers had stood in the square and watched the prelate's soldiers drag the old archivist through the mud, and no one—not one soul Vesper had known her whole life—had stepped into the road. She understood why. Understanding didn't help.

"Vess." Doss's voice dropped lower, into the register he used for things he'd been chewing on all night. "Before we go in. I need you to hear me say it plain. Whatever's in you—the thing the hedge-priest saw, the thing that cracked Brannic's relic—we're not here because we understand it. We're not here because we're brave. Half my people ran already, and I don't blame them. We're here because you pulled Tam out of the river at Marrow Ford, and you carried Wren eleven miles with an arrow in her shoulder, and you've never once left anyone on the field. So whatever happens in there—whatever comes out of you when you touch those locks—you should know none of us are going to look at you different."

Vesper turned her head. In the blue half-dark, Doss's face was a landscape she knew better than her own: the scar through his eyebrow from a fight he'd started defending someone else's honor, the gray coming in at his temples like frost claiming high ground.

"That's the nicest thing you've ever said to me," she said.

"It's the only time I'll say it, so savor it." He pressed his palm briefly against her shoulder—armor against armor—and turned back to the others crouched along the woodshed wall: Tam with his two short swords and his terrible grin, Wren with her bow strung and wrapped in wool to keep it quiet, and young Pell, sixteen and freckled, who had refused twice to be left behind and would have followed them anyway if they'd tried.

"The locks," Wren said quietly. "You're sure about this, Vess? The Choir's iron is blessed six ways. If your touch unmakes it, it unmakes it *loudly*. Everyone in the valley will feel it."

"Then everyone in the valley will know I came for her." Vesper flexed her bare fingers. She'd taken off her gloves a mile back. Her hands looked ordinary in the dark—scarred knuckles, bitten nails, a mercenary's hands—but she could feel something under her skin now, ever since the candle relit itself for the dying farmer, ever since Vael's single heartbeat of voice. A warmth like a coal wrapped in ash. Like something patient that had waited twenty years and was done pretending to sleep.

"Positions," Doss said. "Tam, the east window. Wren, the bell tower base—if the alarm goes up, you make sure it doesn't ring twice. Pell—" 

"I'm going in with her," Pell said.

Doss closed his eyes as if asking some dead god for patience. "Pell—"

"He can't carry Maren alone if her legs are bad. And if the locks scream, someone needs to watch the corridor while Vess works." Pell's jaw was set in a way that reminded Vesper painfully of herself at fourteen, swearing oaths to gods who were about to go silent. "I'm going in."

A long breath through Doss's nose, fogging the air. "Fine. But if you die, I'm telling your mother it was quick and heroic, and then I'm telling her it was your idea."

* * *

They went over the wall in the last dark hour, when even the faithful sleep deepest, and the Choir's faith turned out to be no exception. Vesper took the sentry on the north corner with a forearm across the throat and the flat of her knife against his temple until his knees folded—he'd wake with a headache and a story he'd never dare tell—and then they were inside the yard, and the reliquary loomed up black against the blacker sky, breathing smoke from its censers, humming faintly with the sound of chained holiness.

Up close, the door was worse than she'd expected. It wasn't wood or iron but both, planks banded with scripture-stamped metal, and every band was engraved with prayers she could almost read—almost, the way you can almost remember a dream. The letters seemed to shift when she wasn't looking directly at them. Ward-script. The kind that bit.

"Pell. Eyes on the lane." She set her palm flat against the nearest band.

The warmth under her skin rose to meet it.

She had braced for pain—for the recoil the hedge-priest had described, water from hot iron, the world flinching away from her. Instead the opposite happened. The ward-script *leaned* toward her hand like a plant toward light, and then, with a sound like a long-held note finally released, it simply... stopped being blessed. The engraving faded to plain grooves. The metal cooled. Somewhere deep in the door, a chain that had been taut for three days went slack.

It didn't hurt. That was the horrifying part. It felt like *breathing out*.

"Vess?" Pell whispered.

"It's fine. Keep watch." She moved her hand to the next band. Again the soft exhalation of unmaking, the prayer dissolving like sugar in warm water. She thought of the hedge-priest going pale, of Brannic's relic cracking in her grip, of twenty years of feeling like a hole in the shape of a person—and now, standing in the cold, she understood that she had never been a hole at all. She was a fire that other fires recognized, and feared, and could not help reaching toward.

The last band released. The door swung open on hinges that had been oiled recently, thoughtfully, by jailers who expected a long tenancy.

Inside: darkness, incense thick enough to chew, and the smell beneath it that Vesper knew from too many campaigns—the smell of a person kept too long in one small room. Fear-sweat and old straw and the mineral tang of unwashed stone.

* * *

The corridor ran straight back into the building's heart, cells on either side, most of them empty. The Choir hadn't filled this place with prisoners. They'd built it for one.

Maren Ostwick sat in the last cell, back against the wall, hands folded in her lap with the composure of a woman who had spent forty years cataloguing miracles and had always known she might end up on the wrong side of one. Her white hair was matted. There was a bruise along her jaw. When the lamplight found her face, she blinked once, twice—and then her expression did something Vesper had never seen it do in twenty-three years of knowing her.

It broke.

"Oh," Maren said. Just that. Just *oh*, in the smallest voice Vesper had ever heard her use, as if the word were all that remained of a much longer sentence.

"Maren." Vesper's hands were already on the cell lock—a great wheel of blessed iron, wards crawling across it like frost. She put her palm to it and felt the prayers sigh out of existence, one by one, like lamps being blown out down a long hall. "Can you walk?"

"I walked here, didn't I? Under escort, at spear-point, past everyone I've buried and baptized." Maren hauled herself up the wall, joints cracking like green wood in a fire. Her eyes went to Pell, to the open corridor, to the dark doorway where Doss now stood silhouetted. "You brought the company. You absolute fools. You beautiful, doomed fools."

"Save the eulogy," Doss said. "We're leaving the same way we came, which is quickly and with property that isn't ours."

But Vesper had stopped. Because in the moment the lock died, something else had stirred—not the wards, not the god, but the room itself. Every relic in the reliquary's antechamber, every saint's fingerbone and vial of holy oil the Choir had confiscated from Hollowmere's shrines, had begun, very softly, to glow. Not burning. Not answering. Just... turning toward her, the way sunflowers turn, the way drowning men turn toward shore.

Maren saw it too. Her eyes widened, and for the first time since the cell, she looked afraid—not of the Choir, not of capture, but of Vesper.

"You really don't know," Maren whispered. "You truly don't know what you are."

"I know what I'm told, which lately is nothing. Move."

* * *

They made the wall without raising the alarm, which should have been impossible, and Vesper spent the entire run trying not to think about why it hadn't been. The sentries' lanterns had guttered as she passed. The dogs in the Choir's kennel had not so much as whimpered—they'd pressed themselves flat to the bottoms of their pens, silent, the way animals go quiet before an earthquake. Even the snow seemed to fall more gently around her, reluctant, deferential.

Wren met them at the treeline above the millrace, breath steaming, eyes wide. "The bell tower," she said. "The rope snapped when I cut it loose. Clean snap, like it was rotted through. It wasn't rotted through yesterday."

"Later," Doss said. "Talk later. Run now."

They ran. Down through the birch stand where Vesper had learned to track hares, along the frozen millrace, up the goat path toward the abandoned shepherd's hut below the crags—two miles of night and snow and Maren's increasingly labored breathing, until Pell finally simply picked the old woman up and carried her, and Maren, to her credit, stopped protesting after the first hundred yards.

The hut was a low stone beehive with a roof of turf, abandoned since Vesper was a child. Doss got a fire going from damp peat while Wren saw to the door and Pell laid Maren down on the one intact pallet. Outside, the sky was turning the color of pewter. Dawn would come, and with it horns, and with the horns, the hunt. They had perhaps two hours.

Vesper knelt by the fire and held her hands to it, and noticed—again—that the flames leaned toward her. Gently. Affectionately. Like dogs greeting their master.

"Give her water," she said, not looking around. "And then somebody tell me how bad it is."

"Bad," Doss said cheerfully. "But we've had worse. Remember the siege of Kettlebridge?"

"We lost the siege of Kettlebridge."

"We lost it *together*," Doss said, "which is the part I'm celebrating." He passed around hard bread and colder cheese, and for a few minutes there was only the sound of eating and the wind worrying the turf roof, and Vesper let herself have those minutes, because she was learning that peace came in smaller portions these days and you had to eat what was served.

Maren drank the water Pell offered, and then set the cup down with the deliberate care of a woman arranging her affairs.

"You want the truth," Maren said. "All of it."

"I want enough of it to survive the day."

"No." Maren shook her head slowly. "That's the coward's arithmetic I've been doing for twenty-three years—giving you truth in spoonfuls, measuring it out like medicine, always deciding you couldn't swallow the whole dose. Look where my arithmetic got us. Burned ledgers. Arrested archivists. A girl who unmakes holiness with her bare hands and doesn't know why." She drew a breath that shuddered at the bottom of it. "No more spoonfuls, Vesper. But not here, and not all at once, because what I have to tell you starts with a fire, and I can only say so much of it before my voice gives out. Ask me one question. The one you've carried longest. And I swear to you on every name in every ledger I ever kept—I will answer it whole."

The hut went quiet. The fire popped. Outside, the dawn was coming up gray and merciless over the crags, and somewhere far below, faint as a memory, a horn sounded in Hollowmere—the Choir waking, discovering the empty cell, beginning to understand what had passed through their blessed iron like light through glass.

Vesper looked at the woman who had raised her. Who had taught her letters and herbcraft and how to hold a sword so it didn't hold you. Who had burned her own name out of a ledger to keep a secret, and let a child grow up believing she'd been abandoned rather than *hidden*.

One question. She had a thousand, and they all collapsed, in the end, into one.

"What am I?" Vesper said. "Not what do I carry. Not what was done to me. What *am* I?"

Maren closed her eyes. When she opened them again, they were wet and steady and terribly old.

"You," she said, "are the last living piece of the fire the gods were lit from. And child—there are two armies marching on this valley, and neither of them is coming for *you*. They're coming for what burns in you. So before I tell you the rest, you need to decide something no one should ever have to decide at any age: when they come—and they are coming—do you intend to give it to them?"

The horn sounded again, closer now. Two notes, then three. Not an alarm anymore. A summons.

Doss rose, unhurried, and began checking the edges of his blades. "For the record," he said, "I vote we decide while walking."
