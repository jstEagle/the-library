## 18. Why the God Went Silent

The fire in the shepherd's hut had burned down to a red eye by the time Vael finished gathering himself.

Vesper sat on the floor with her back against the cold stone of the hearth, her knees drawn up, her hands wrapped around a cup of melted snow that had long since gone cold. She had not drunk from it. It was something to hold, and holding things was how she kept from shattering—she had learned that much about herself in the last three days, since the candle relit itself for a dying farmer, since the hedge-priest went pale at her touch, since the medallion she'd worn for twenty years began to warm against her sternum like a second heartbeat.

*They are coming for what burns in you.*

That was all he'd said, that night in the frost. Four words, delivered in a voice like a bell rung underwater, and then silence again—or what she had thought was silence. Now she understood it had not been silence at all. It had been the sound of a god standing very still in a dark room, afraid to speak.

"Say it again," she said to the empty air. "All of it. Every piece you've been chewing on for twenty years."

The fire shifted. Not the way fires shift when wind moves through a chimney—there was no wind—but the way a body shifts in sleep, turning toward a voice it knows. The coals brightened. Shadows leaned.

*I am old,* Vael said, and his voice filled the hut the way water fills a cup, from the bottom up, until there was no space in her skull that wasn't him. *Older than the temples they built to me. Older than the names they gave me. Do you know what I was before I was Vael?*

"No," Vesper said. Her voice came out flat and hard, which surprised her. She had imagined this moment a thousand times over twenty years—the return, the reunion, the explanation—and in every version she had wept or raged or fallen to her knees. She did none of those things. She sat very still with her cold cup and waited, the way you wait across a table from someone who owes you money. "I don't know anything. That was rather the point."

*Before I was Vael I was a flame in a hearth that no one tended,* he said. *We all were. Every god you have ever heard prayed to—we were sparks off one fire, the First Fire, the Ember, the thing your archivists call the pre-divine light because they cannot bear to write down what it actually was.* A pause, and in the pause the coals dimmed, as if he were flinching. *It was not a god, Vesper. It was the thing gods are made FROM.*

Outside, the snow came down without sound. Somewhere in the village a dog barked twice and stopped, as if it too were listening.

"The Ember," Vesper said. "The thing Maren wouldn't name. The thing the Choir is hunting. You're telling me it's real."

*It is sleeping in your chest,* Vael said. *Has been since before you could walk.*

She set the cup down. Carefully. Because if she didn't set it down carefully she was going to throw it, and the wall was old plaster and Doss would make her pay for it out of her share, and there was something obscene about breaking crockery while a god told you that you were a lantern.

"Then explain," she said, "why you left me."

* *

He told it the way old men tell stories they've rehearsed at funerals—slowly, with pauses where the grief was supposed to go, and Vesper recognized the technique because she used it herself when she talked about the day her oath died.

*Twenty-three years ago,* he said, *the temple at Karvath burned. You know the story they tell—the Choir tells it every solstice. The Temple of the First Flame, consumed by heretics' fire, its priests martyred, its relics lost. A tragedy. A cautionary tale about faithlessness.*

"It's not true."

*It is true that it burned,* Vael said. *Everything else is a lie built on top of the smoke. The gods burned it, Vesper. We burned our own cradle. Because the fire at the heart of that temple was waking up, and it remembered what we had done, and we could not bear to be remembered.*

The coals had gone very low now, almost black, and his voice came thin off them like heat off a dying forge.

*You have to understand what we were. We were thieves who got comfortable. The First Fire made us—it lit us the way a hearth lights a hundred candles, each one believing itself the origin of warmth. And when the Fire showed signs of wanting to take back what it had given—when it began, slowly, over centuries, to draw its sparks home—we panicked. We are made of borrowed light, child. Extinguishment is not death to us. It is unmaking. We would not merely stop existing. Everything we had ever been would be unwritten.*

"So you killed it."

*We tried.* The word came out cracked. *We tore the temple down around it. We scattered its priests. We broke the great spark into fragments and hid them across the world—in relics, in rivers, in the marrow of mountains. But fire does not die because you disapprove of it. It waits. It finds wicks. Twenty-three years ago the largest fragment woke in Karvath, and it was angry, and it began calling its pieces home, and we felt ourselves starting to be gathered up like dropped coins.*

"And so you burned it again." Vesper heard her own heartbeat, slow and enormous. "And the fragment escaped. Carried out. Smuggled." Her mouth was dry. "In an infant."

*Yes.*

"Me."

*You.*

* * *

The silence after that lasted a long time. Long enough for the fire to rebuild itself, coal by coal, into something like a face—not literally, never literally, but the suggestion of one, the way clouds suggest things to people who want them to.

Vesper found she was thinking about small things, stupid things, the debris of a life suddenly revealed as scaffolding. The way Maren had always checked her temperature with the back of his hand instead of a thermometer. The way the valley's few hedge-witches had refused, politely but absolutely, to teach her even the simplest cantrips—"not for you, dear, nothing personal"—while teaching Doss's youngest sister to charm milk fresh. The way her mother, whoever she had been, had chosen Hollowmere: the one place in the world where magic did not work, where prayers fell dead as stones, where a living fragment of the divine fire could sleep undetected because the whole valley was a blanket soaked in nullity.

Or not a blanket. A cage.

"Why here?" she asked. "Why Hollowmere?"

*Because the valley is a wound,* Vael said. *When we shattered the First Fire, the place where we did it—the first temple, older than Karvath, older than names—was scarred so deeply that divinity cannot exist there. Magic dies crossing the snowline the way sound dies in deep water. We did not make the valley a refuge for you, Vesper. The valley was already a prison. Your mother simply chose the strongest cell.*

"My oath." The words came out of her before she could stop them, and once they started they came fast, twenty years of them, a landslide. "I swore myself to you when I was fourteen. In the shrine above the mill, with the whole village watching. I gave you my name, my service, my—everything. And two years later you went silent. Mid-prayer. I was sixteen years old, kneeling in the snow, talking to a god who had stopped existing, and everyone in Hollowmere watched me do it for another hour because I couldn't believe—I thought I'd done something wrong. I thought I'd said the wrong word. I spent YEARS thinking I had broken it somehow, that I was the flaw, that I was the reason—"

*You did not break it.*

"You let me believe I did!"

*I could not speak to you.* His voice had gone strange, strained, like a man talking through clenched teeth. *Do you understand what I am telling you? I did not abandon the valley. None of us abandoned it. We FLED it. The moment the Ember crossed the snowline, this valley became poison to us. Every god in the pantheon lost their voice here overnight. We could not enter. We could not answer. We could not even hear—do you know what it is to be a god and go deaf? To feel ten thousand faithful praying into a hole where you used to be?*

"So you LEFT."

*We fled,* he said, and the distinction sounded, even to him, even in his own mouth, like nothing at all. *And we told ourselves it was strategy. Containment. The Ember must not wake; therefore no god may come near it; therefore the valley must remain silent; therefore the girl raised inside it must never learn why. It was cleaner that way. It was safer. If she knew—if any of them knew—*

"If I knew, I might have left." Vesper was on her feet now. She didn't remember standing. The cold cup lay tipped on the floor, spreading a dark stain across the boards. "If I knew, I might have walked out of the valley someday, and the moment I crossed the snowline every god within a thousand miles would have felt me like a brand against the skin. So you kept me ignorant. You let Maren keep me ignorant. You let me swear an oath to you—a REAL oath, a binding, the kind that shapes a soul—you let me swear it knowing full well you'd never be able to answer it, knowing the whole arrangement was—"

She stopped. The word arrived in her chest like a swallowed coal.

"A leash," she said. "My oath wasn't devotion. It was a tether. You couldn't watch the Ember directly—it would burn you blind. So you bound yourself to the vessel. Through me. Through a fourteen-year-old girl who wanted so badly to be chosen that she'd have sworn herself to a fencepost if the priest asked nicely. My faith wasn't worship. It was a surveillance contract."

*Vesper—*

"Don't." Her voice cracked on it, and she hated that, hated the crack, hated the wet heat behind her eyes. "Don't say my name like that. Don't you dare be gentle with me. I built my entire LIFE around being unchosen. Do you understand? I made it my identity. Dead-god Vess, the crew calls me. The mercenary with the blank medallion. I turned the worst day of my life into armor because armor was better than grief. And the whole time—THE WHOLE TIME—you weren't dead. You were outside the walls. Watching. Listening through me like I was a keyhole. You let me mourn you for twenty years because mourning was quieter than truth."

The fire said nothing. Coals do not have the luxury of defensiveness.

*Maren did not know everything,* Vael said at last, quietly. *He knew you carried something that had to stay hidden. He did not know what. He believed—he chose to believe—that he was saving a child. Which, in the small hours, is also true. He smuggled you out of Karvath under a wagon of salt pork with the Ember's cry muffled against his heart, and he aged ten years on that road, and he has never once slept a whole night since. Whatever else he curated out of your life, he did it with love and terror in equal measure.*

"And you?" Vesper's laugh came out jagged. "What did you curate me with?"

*Care,* he said. *As much as I could pour through a leash. When you prayed, I listened. When you trained with the company, I watched. When you took the wound at Grey Ford—the arrow, the fever—I held the infection back as far as the valley's silence allowed. You were never alone, Vesper. You were never unwatched. I know that is not the same as being loved, and I know—* the coals guttered, flared, steadied— *I know that from where you stand, care delivered through a chain looks exactly like ownership. Perhaps it IS ownership. I am a thief, child. Thieves do not become honest men. They only get better at explaining the theft.*

* * *

She walked out of the hut afterward, because staying felt like agreeing, and the night was the kind of clean cold that scours a person down to their decisions.

Snow to her ankles. Stars in their winter constellations, sharp as nailheads. The village below her dark except for one window where old Sefa was surely awake, as she was always awake these days, keeping vigil for a son who'd gone north with the pilgrim roads and not written. Vesper stood in the white street between the shuttered houses and breathed until her breath stopped shaking.

A leash. Twenty years of prayer, and she had been a length of rope tied between a god and his prisoner.

But here was the thing that kept snagging her, the splinter under the skin: it had WORKED both ways. She had felt him. All those years of dead silence, and yet on the worst nights—the nights after Grey Ford, after the contract that went wrong at Marrow Bridge, after her seventeenth birthday which she'd spent alone in a rented room getting drunk enough to forget she'd ever been chosen—on those nights there had been something. Not a voice. Not comfort. Just a warmth at the base of her sternum, right where the medallion lay, like a hand pressed flat against a door from the other side.

She had called it imagination. She had called it memory. She had called it anything except what it was, because what it was meant the god hadn't left, and if the god hadn't left then the silence was a choice, and if the silence was a choice—

Then she had been imprisoned by kindness. Contained by care. Raised, fed, trained, armed, and loved—all of it, every bit of it, in service of a lock.

"You could have told me," she said to the stars, to the snow, to the warmth behind her breastbone. "At fourteen. At sixteen. Any year of the twenty. One sentence. 'You are not mine, little one; you are the thing I guard.' I'd have hated it. I'd have raged. But I'd have had the TRUTH, and I could have built something on truth. Instead you let me build a self on a lie, and now I have to tear it down in the middle of a war, with zealots on the road and winter walking, and I don't even know who I am when the lie's gone. Do you understand that? I don't know WHO I AM."

The medallion warmed against her skin. Not words. Just presence—the weight of a great creature lying very still, taking the blow because it has decided it deserves the blow.

Good, she thought. Stay still. Take it.

But underneath the rage, in the cellar of her where she kept the things she didn't say out loud, another thought moved on soft feet, and it was this: *he stayed.* The other gods fled the valley entirely—fled it so completely that twenty years of answered prayers had dried up across half the realm. Vael had stationed himself at the border of his own poison, pressed against the snowline like a face against glass, for two decades, listening through a leash he was ashamed of. That was not the behavior of a jailer with a comfortable conscience. That was the behavior of a man doing penance at a bedside.

It didn't forgive him. Nothing forgave him tonight, maybe nothing ever would. But it complicated him, and complications were harder to hate than simple monsters, and Vesper suspected—with the weary certainty of someone who had forgiven worse for less—that in a month or a year she would find a way to live beside this too, the way you live beside a scar.

Not tonight, though. Tonight the rage was hers, and she was going to keep it.

* * *

She was still standing in the street when the warmth spoke again—quieter now, humbled, a god who had learned to knock.

*There is more,* Vael said. *The part I have not yet been able to say. The part that will make you hate me properly, if the rest has only made you hate me halfway.*

"Then say it."

*The Choir is not hunting you to destroy the Ember, little one. And the Winter Court is not hunting you to freeze it. Hear me: Cindral means to EAT it. Hravn means to WEAR it. Every power moving toward this valley intends to consume the First Fire and become, alone among all things, the source of every flame—including ours, including mine, including the small fire I have left. And they will use YOU to reach it, because the vessel opens from the inside, and vessels can be persuaded, or broken, or bargained with.*

The cold seemed to deepen around her. Down the valley road, far off, she saw it—a smudge of orange against the black, where no orange should be at this hour. Torchlight. Many torches, moving in formation, disciplined as a procession.

*They are closer than I said,* Vael admitted. *I did not want to frighten you before you had finished being angry at me. Anger keeps a soul warm, and you will need your warmth for what comes. But the time for telling you things gently is over. They are coming for what burns in you, Vesper. All of them. And the first of them will be at the valley's edge by morning.*

Vesper looked at the distant torchlight for a long moment. Then she turned and walked toward the inn where Doss kept the ledgers and the company slept in shifts, and her boots printed a line of dark holes in the fresh snow, and behind her the medallion burned steady as a coal against her heart.

Wake the others, she thought. Wake Maren. We are done hiding, and we are done being lied to—and whatever comes down that road by morning is going to find out what a leash is worth when the thing on the other end stops being held.
