## 20. Crossing the White Pass

The pass had no name on any map worth trusting. The old drovers called it the White Stair, because it climbed in switchbacks like a ladder laid against the sky, and because by midwinter it was nothing but white — white stone under white snow under white cloud, until a traveler could not say where the world ended and the sky began.

They climbed it at dusk, twenty-three of them, and the cold came up to meet them like something that had been waiting.

Vesper felt it before she saw anything: a pressure against her chest, deep beneath the breastbone, where the Ember sat coiled and quiet and burning its slow secret burn. It was the same feeling she'd had all her life in Hollowmere when a storm rolled over the rim of the valley — a flattening, a hush, as if the air itself were being pressed flat by a hand. But this was different. This was colder. This was *interested*.

Behind them, far below and miles south, the horizon glowed.

Not sunrise-glow. Not firelight. A pale luminescence, blue-white and utterly still, like moonlight that had forgotten to move on. It lay along the southern sky the way frost lies along a windowpane, and it did not flicker, and it did not fade, and Vesper knew — the way you know a wound is yours — that it was looking for her.

"Keep moving," Doss said, low, at her shoulder. He didn't point at the light. Nobody pointed at the light. They had all agreed, somewhere in the last two days without ever saying it aloud, that pointing at it might be an invitation.

The company moved. Twenty-three figures in grey wool and oiled leather, breath streaming behind them like scarves, boots crunching a rhythm into the frozen scree. Ahead of them the Stair rose through the gloom, switchback above switchback, and above that the notch they called the Shepherd's Tooth — a granite fang split by wind into a narrow saddle, the only way north into the high country where, Maren said, there were caves, and hot springs, and maybe, if the maps were honest, a road down the far side before true winter closed everything.

Maren walked near the middle of the column, wrapped in three cloaks and none of them enough. He was sixty-eight years old and he had spent his life among ledgers and candle-smoke, and he was climbing anyway, because the alternative was the Choir, and because — though he had not said this either — he believed he owed Vesper a debt that could only be paid in distance put between her and her hunters.

"You should rest," she told him, falling back a step.

"I'll rest when we're over the Tooth." His voice came out in white puffs. "Resting on a mountainside in Isolde's weather is how you become a landmark."

Despite everything, despite the ache in her chest and the light on the southern sky, Vesper almost smiled. "You've been spending too much time with Doss."

"Twenty years of it. It wears off never."

They climbed. The wind found them at the third switchback and began its long argument — first tugging at hems, then shoving, then screaming through the crags with a sound like a choir of the drowned. Vesper pulled her hood low and counted heads out of habit. Wren was ahead, small and quick, finding the path where the path vanished under ice, her short bow slung across her back with the string wrapped in waxed cloth to keep it dry. Brannic lumbered behind her with half the company's food on his back and complaining about it in a steady, cheerful mutter that was, everyone knew, the sound he made instead of fear. The twins — Sefa and Ilka — moved together as always, flanking the column's weak spots without being asked. Old Kettrin brought up the rear with the mule, because the mule trusted her and nobody else, and because Kettrin had been rearguard for so many years that putting her anywhere else would have been like moving a hearthstone.

Twenty-three. Vesper counted them again. She had started counting after the reliquary raid, after the night the valley turned, and she could not stop. Every head was a name. Every name was a weight she carried alongside the other one, the burning one, the one the whole wintering world wanted.

*They are coming for what burns in you.* Vael's voice still lived in her skull like a splinter worked under the skin. She had not heard him since. He was there — she felt him sometimes, a vast attention circling her like a hawk over a field — but he did not speak, and she did not ask. There had been no time for gods. There was barely time for breathing.

At the fifth switchback, the light behind them brightened.

It happened between one glance and the next. One moment the southern glow lay distant and diffuse; the next it had sharpened, gathered, drawn itself into a single luminous line along the horizon — and the cold changed. It stopped being weather and became intention. Vesper felt it slide up the mountain toward them like water finding a crack, patient, thorough, certain of its destination.

"She's found us," Sefa said quietly. She hadn't turned around. None of them needed to.

"No," Vesper said. Her mouth was dry. "She's *reached* us. There's a difference."

There was. Because in the next breath, snow began to fall from a sky that held no clouds.

It fell upward-first, or seemed to — flakes rising off the rocks around their boots, hanging, turning, then drifting down again in patterns too deliberate for wind. Each flake caught what little light remained and threw it back wrong, blue-white, cold as a dead man's smile. And where the flakes touched living things, they did not melt.

Kettrin's mule screamed.

It was not a sound Vesper had heard an animal make before and hoped never to hear again. The beast reared, and where its breath should have steamed, it crystallized — a plume of tiny knives — and frost raced up its legs like ivy growing in fast-forward, silvering hide, stiffening hair, climbing. Kettrin cut the lead rope with one stroke of her belt knife and shoved, hard, both hands flat against the animal's shoulder, and the mule bolted back down the switchback, shrieking, already glassy to the knee, already more statue than creature, and the mountain took it and the sound faded and then there was only the wind again.

Nobody spoke. Kettrin stood with her empty hands open at her sides, watching the place where the mule had gone, and her breath came out in ragged clouds, and she was alive, and that was the whole of the eulogy anyone had time for.

"It's testing us," Maren said. His scholar's voice shook only a little. "A god's winter doesn't kill indiscriminately — it *searches*. It's casting a net made of cold, and everything it touches, it reads. It's looking for heat that doesn't belong to flesh."

Looking for me. Vesper's hand went to her chest without her permission. The Ember pulsed once beneath her palm, warm, defiant, a coal refusing the dark — and every flake within ten feet of her shivered and turned away, curling aside like moths from a flame, and she understood with sick clarity that she was the one thing on this mountain the winter could not read, and therefore the one thing it would never stop hunting.

"Then we give it nothing to read," Doss said. "Wren. How fast to the Tooth?"

Wren looked up the Stair, squinting through the impossible snow. "Two hours. Less if we drop the packs."

"We don't drop the packs." Brannic, tightening his straps. "We drop the packs, we starve in the caves, and I refuse to be eaten by my own friends. Bad manners all around."

A few of them laughed. It was thin laughter, frightened laughter, but it was laughter, and Vesper loved every one of them for it, and hated herself for leading them here.

"Move," she said. "All of you. Move like the cold has teeth, because it does."

* * *

They climbed through the falling snow, and the snow climbed with them.

That was the horror of it — that it kept pace. However fast they went, however brutal the grade, the blue-white fall hung at their backs like a tide that had decided, rather than a tide that merely was. Twice Vesper looked back and saw it clearly: a wall of glittering stillness filling the pass below them, hundreds of feet tall, advancing without hurry, and inside the wall — shapes. Figures. Frozen mid-stride, mid-reach, mid-scream. Pilgrims who had fled the valley by the same road. A carter with his team. A woman with her arms raised, and a child beside her, both of them perfect, both of them glass, both of them *kept*.

Isolde did not kill them, Veper realized. She collected them.

"Don't look," Doss said, and put his body between her and the sight, and she let him, because she was tired and because some mercies are easier to accept than to name.

The cold found the stragglers first. It always finds the stragglers; that is the oldest law of retreats and the reason old soldiers walk in the middle. A hired spear named Ollis, who had joined the company only that spring and laughed too loud at Brannic's jokes, began to lag at the seventh switchback — just a little, just a stumble, just a man whose legs had given what legs had. Sefa went back for him. Then Ilka. They got him up, got him moving, got him going again, and lost him again forty minutes later, and this time when they reached him he was standing very still, staring at his own hands, which had gone the color of pewter at the fingertips.

"Frostbite?" Doss asked, gripping his shoulder.

Ollis shook his head slowly. His lips were blue. "It's not cold like cold," he whispered. "It's cold like *asking*. Like something asking me a question, over and over, and I can't remember the answer—" His eyes rolled up. Frost feathered across his lashes.

Sefa slapped him. Hard. Once, twice. "Stay with us. You stay *with us*—"

And Ollis blinked, and swore, and came back — for now. Vesper watched Sefa and Ilka haul him up the switchback between them, each of them giving him their own warmth without hesitation, and thought: *this is what the Ember is supposed to be. This. Not thrones. Not wars. Two women carrying a freezing boy uphill because no one else will.*

The thought steadied her. She held onto it like a handrail.

* * *

The Shepherd's Tooth rose against the last of the light, black granite streaked with ice, and the saddle at its shoulder was perhaps thirty feet wide with drops on either side that the eye refused to measure. Wind funneled through it with a sound like the end of the world played backward. Beyond it, the ground leveled into the high plateau, and somewhere out there — Maren swore it, map in mittened hand — there were caves with hot springs, and shelter, and a chance.

They reached the base of the final climb as full dark came down, and that was when the winter stopped searching and began to take.

It came up the Stair all at once, the whole glittering wall of it, no longer patient. The temperature dropped so fast that breath froze in the air and fell as crystals that rang on the stones like spilled glass. The snowfall thickened until the world was a white roar. And through it, Vesper heard it — faint, beautiful, appalling — a voice like a bell rung inside a glacier.

*"Give me what burns, and the rest of you may live."*

Every one of them heard it. She saw it land on their faces, one by one, as they turned to look at her — not with blame, not yet, but with the terrible arithmetic of people doing sums they don't want to finish.

"No," Vesper said, loud, into the wind, to all of them. "No one is trading me. No one is *being* traded. We go through the Tooth together or not at all. That's the whole speech. Now move."

They moved.

The final pitch was a scramble, hands and boots, ice under everything. Wren went first, driving pitons with numb fingers, calling holds down to the others. Brannic pushed Maren from below. Doss hauled. The twins passed Ollis up hand over hand like cargo, and Ollis, gray-lipped, wept without knowing he wept, tears freezing on his cheeks.

Vesper climbed last but one, with Kettrin at the rear, because Kettrin would not come off the rear and arguing with her cost time nobody had.

Halfway up the pitch, the winter reached them.

It came not as cold now but as *presence* — a pressure, a regard, vast and elegant and utterly without mercy, pouring up the rock face like ink poured up a page. Vesper felt it touch the edges of her and recoil, hiss, circle, try again, the way it had tried since the valley — and she felt, beneath her terror, the Ember answer. Heat bloomed in her chest, spread down her arms, and where her bare fingers gripped the stone, the ice *melted*, ran, steamed. She was leaving a trail of bare warm handprints up the frozen face of the world, and she knew Isolde could see them, and could not stop making them.

"There," the glacier-voice said, delighted, close now, closer than sound should allow. "*There* you are."

"WREN!" Doss bellowed from above. "THE TOP! GET THEM TO THE TOP!"

Wren was already there — small, quick Wren, who could shoot a sparrow's whisker off at eighty yards and who had once, drunk, taught the entire company a song so obscene that Doss fined them all a day's wages just to have an excuse to hear it again. She gained the saddle, drove a piton, fixed a rope, and leaned back over the drop with her arm out, hauling climbers over the lip one by one, and she was laughing, actually laughing, because that was what Wren did when the world went to hell: she laughed at it, defiantly, until it blinked first.

The last of them came over — Maren, Ollis, the twins, Brannic, Doss — and the count on the plateau was twenty-two, and Vesper turned to pull Kettrin up, and that was when the winter closed its hand.

It did not strike the plateau. It struck the Stair. The whole route below them — the switchbacks, the scree, the frozen waterfall of the final pitch — went white in a single silent instant, a bloom of absolute cold, and the rope in Vesper's hands turned to brittle iron, and Kettrin, ten feet below the lip, fingers hooked into a crack, looked up at her with clear eyes and no fear at all.

"Go on, then," Kettrin said. Her breath did not steam anymore. Frost was climbing her collar like lace. "You're letting the heat out."

"*Kettrin—*"

"Listen to an old woman." The words were slowing, coming from further away. "I've been rearguard thirty years. Best post there is. You see everything. You see *them* get clear." Her hand was silver to the wrist now, fused gently to the stone, and still her eyes were calm, and still they were on Vesper, and there was something in them that was not calm at all — something fierce and proud and unbearably tender. "You listen to me, girl. Whatever's burning in you — it isn't a curse and it isn't a crown. It's a *hearth*. Hearths are for keeping people alive. Don't let them make you forget which."

The cold took her voice. It did not take her eyes. For a long moment — three heartbeats, four — Vesper knelt at the edge of the plateau with her hand stretched down into the killing white, and Kettrin looked back at her from inside the ice, upright, unbroken, a soldier at her post at the end of the world, and her frozen face was smiling.

Then Isolde's winter withdrew down the mountain, taking its collected dead with it, and the snowfall thinned, and the stars came out over the northern plateau, indifferent and clean.

They buried nothing. There was nothing to bury. Doss stood at the edge of the drop for a long time with his fists on the rope, and then he cut it loose and let it fall, because he was not a man who kept things that hurt to carry. Brannic did not make a single joke. The twins stood shoulder to shoulder, and Sefa was crying silently, and Ilka stared north with an expression Vesper recognized because she wore it herself: the expression of someone adding a name to a debt.

Twenty-two. Vesper counted them twice, hating the number, hating the counting, unable to stop.

"We keep moving," Doss said finally, hoarse. "Warm ground by dawn. That's what she bought us. We don't waste it."

They moved. All except Vesper, who stood one moment longer at the edge of the plateau, looking south, where the pale light still burned along the horizon — closer now, patient now, certain now.

*Hearths are for keeping people alive,* Kettrin had said.

Vesper pressed her hand flat against her chest, against the small fierce heat that had cost a good woman her life to protect, and made a promise to the ice and the dark and the elegant, endless winter watching from the south:

*I will decide what this is. Not you. Not any of you. Me.*

Then she turned north, into the wind, and followed her people toward the caves and the hot springs and whatever came next — and behind them, far below, the winter settled over the White Pass like a lid closing on a pot, and waited, and did not need to hurry anymore.

Because ahead of them, on the far side of the plateau, where the maps said there should be shelter — there were lights. Dozens of them. Moving.

Torches. A column of men in ash-grey robes, marching out of the north.

The Choir had crossed the pass first.
