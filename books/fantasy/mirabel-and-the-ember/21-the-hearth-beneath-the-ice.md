## 21. The Hearth Beneath the Ice

The storm found them an hour past the pass, as if it had been waiting all this time for the mountain to stop shielding them, and it came down on the company like a judgment no one had prayed for.

Vesper walked at the front because walking at the front was the only thing she knew how to do when her hands wanted to shake. Behind her, eleven survivors of what had been twenty-two moved in a huddled line through snow that rose past their knees and kept rising, wind-driven white erasing the road, the sky, the distinction between up and down. Somewhere behind them, back at the throat of the pass, Brenna lay under a cairn of stones they had built with bleeding hands in the space of ten minutes while the cold chewed at their fingers, because there had been no time for anything better and no ground soft enough for anything deeper.

She had held the pass. That was the sentence Vesper kept turning over in her mind like a coin she couldn't identify. *Brenna held the pass.* As if holding were a thing a person could do against the Long Winter, against Lady Isolde's hunting frost that froze men mid-stride with their mouths still open around a shout. Brenna had stood on the narrow spine of stone where only one could come at a time, and she had swung her axe until the ice took her, and the last thing she said—not to Vesper, not to Doss, but to the pass itself, to the whole white howling world—was *"Not today."*

It hadn't been today. It would be another day. But not today.

"Vess." Doss's voice came out of the white beside her, hoarse with wind. "We can't make the tree line. Look at them."

She looked. The line behind them had gone loose and staggering, the way a rope goes loose before it snaps. Tamlin was carrying Fenn's pack along with his own and had been for two hours without saying so. Old Wren, their quartermaster, sixty years old and tough as boot leather, was leaning on a spear she should have been using to test the snow ahead. And little Ilsa—the newest of them, seventeen, who had signed on for bread and adventure and gotten neither—had stopped moving twice already and needed someone's shoulder both times.

"There," Vesper said.

She almost didn't believe it herself. Down and to the left, half-drowned in the storm, a shape in the mountainside that wasn't rock. Too regular. Too deliberate. A slope of snow with a seam in it, straight as a mason's line, and at the bottom of the seam, just visible, the corner of something carved.

Doss squinted into the white. "That's nothing. That's your eyes playing tricks."

"My eyes have been playing tricks my whole life," Vesper said. "Lately they've gotten interesting."

* * *

The door was stone, and it opened the way doors open in old stories: reluctantly, with a grinding complaint, exhaling a breath of air that was impossibly, obscenely warm.

Warmth. Actual warmth, rising out of the dark like the memory of a hearth, and the company pressed toward it the way drowning men press toward light. Vesper went first, because she always went first, sword drawn, into a passage that sloped down and down beneath the mountain, walls of fitted stone on either side, and the strange thing—the thing she noticed even before the relief on her crew's faces, even before Doss's hand closed gratefully on her shoulder—was that the warmth did not touch her.

She felt it the way you feel heat through a window. Present, visible in its effects, but held at a distance. Around her the others were stripping off gloves, gasping, flexing white fingers back to life, and Vesper stood among them in her cold like a woman standing in another room.

*You are the opposite of it,* the hedge-priest had told her, weeks ago, before any of this, back when she had still believed she was merely unlucky. *Magic doesn't fail near you, girl. Magic flees.*

And now she carried the last spark of the fire that had made the gods, and warmth parted around her like water around a hot iron.

"Down," she said, and led them down.

* * *

The temple—if temple it was—opened without warning. One moment the passage was shoulder-width stone, and the next it bloomed into a hall so vast that the lanterns they lit could not find its ceiling. Pillars marched away into the dark, each one carved from base to capital in figures that the flickering light made move: dancers, mourners, kings, things with too many arms and too few faces. The floor was mosaic, thousands upon thousands of tiles, and under the grime of centuries Vesper could see that the tiles formed a pattern—a river, maybe, or a road, flowing inward from every wall toward the center of the hall.

And at the center, sunk into the floor like a hearth sunk into a great room's heart, was a basin of black stone.

It was empty. Whatever had once burned there had burned out ages ago. But the air above it shimmered faintly, the way air shimmers over coals, and the warmth of the whole place poured outward from it in slow, patient waves, and Vesper understood, with a certainty that arrived in her body before her mind, that this was why the temple was warm. This was why the snow had never quite buried the door. Something down here remembered how to be a fire, even after the fire was gone.

"The Hearth," Maren said quietly.

She turned. She hadn't heard him come up beside her—he moved like an archivist, apologetically—but there he stood, wrapped in his ruined priest's coat, his face lit orange and shadow by the lantern he held. His eyes were fixed on the black basin with an expression she had never seen him wear. Not grief. Not awe. Something closer to recognition. The look of a man reading his own handwriting in a place he'd never been.

"You know this place," Vesper said.

"I know the word." Maren's voice was careful, the voice of a man stepping onto ice he'd been warned about. "In the oldest records—the ones the Choir burned, the ones I copied as a young man because no one thought them worth burning yet—there is a name that appears before the names of gods. *Hearth-mother. First-fire. The one who burned before naming.* The translations disagree about everything except this: whatever she was, she came first."

"And the gods?"

Maren looked at her then, and Vesper saw him decide—saw the small internal war of a man who had spent twenty-three years rationing truth, deciding how much of the store to spend tonight.

"The gods came after," he said. "Everything holy came after."

* * *

They made camp in the temple's side chapels, if chapels they were—small rooms off the great hall, dry, defensible, warm enough to sleep in without fires, which was a mercy, since fires would show through cracks to anything hunting them on the mountain. Doss set watches at the entrance passage with the grim thoroughness of a man who had lost too many people in too few days. Tamlin and Fenn got Ilsa's boots off and her feet warming against Wren's middle, and the old woman complained about it in the tone that meant *hold still, child, let me help you,* and Vesper listened to the familiar music of it from the doorway and felt the strangeness of being warm-adjacent in a buried god-house while the world above froze.

Then she went back into the great hall alone, because she couldn't help it. Because the murals were calling her.

She'd seen them at the edges of the lantern light and told herself she'd examine them later, when the crew was safe, when the watches were set. But *later* was a lie she'd been telling herself since Hollowmere, and she was done with it. Later was how Maren had hidden her whole life from her. Later was how gods went silent.

So she took a lantern and she walked the walls.

The murals were painted in pigments that should have died centuries ago—ochre and rust-red and a deep blue made, she suspected, of crushed stone that no longer existed anywhere—and they told a story in procession, panel after panel, winding around pillar after pillar, and Vesper followed it the way pilgrims follow stations, except that she was the only pilgrim this temple had seen in a thousand years, and the story it told was not the story any living faith taught.

The first panels showed darkness. Not empty darkness—darkness with depth, darkness alive, and within it a single point of light, small as a seed.

The second panel: the seed opening. Fire unfolding from it like a flower made of flame, and around the flame, reaching toward it, shapes. Figures. They were crude compared to the craftsmanship elsewhere, deliberately so, as if the artist had wanted them to seem less than the fire—and Vesper felt her breath go shallow, because she knew those shapes. She had grown up on their icons. The antlered silhouette of Hravn. The sun-disc halo of Cindral. The grey-cloaked figure with the broken crown that every child in Hollowmere learned was Vael, god of thresholds and quiet promises, the god whose medallion hung against her chest even now.

They reached toward the fire. In the third panel, they touched it.

In the fourth, they tore it apart.

Vesper stood very still. The lantern trembled in her hand, and she made it be still, and she looked at what the ancient painter had rendered with a clarity that no theology had ever permitted: the figures of the gods, crowding around the burning seed, and their many arms not reaching in wonder but *pulling*, wrenching the fire open, tearing flame from flame, each god seizing a piece and turning away with it clutched to its chest while the source—the Hearth-mother, the First-fire, the figure at the center who until this moment had been only a shape of light—

Vesper stepped closer.

The figure at the center had a face. Every other figure in every other panel was stylized, symbolic, generic in the way sacred art is generic. But the Hearth-mother had a face, and it was rendered with terrible specificity: a woman's face, young and old at once, and her expression was not rage. That was the worst of it. It was not rage, not even in the panel where the gods drove their stolen flames into their own bodies and kindled themselves divine. Her expression was grief. The specific, exhausted grief of a mother watching her children do an unforgivable thing and loving them anyway, and knowing, even as they do it, exactly what they will become.

Below her feet, in the final panel of the sequence, the artist had painted the aftermath. The gods ascending, blazing, crowned in stolen fire, scattering into the heavens. And below them, at the bottom of the mural, small and almost hidden, a single ember falling. Falling away from the ascension. Falling down and down into darkness, one last unclaimed spark of the First-fire, dropped or lost or—Vesper leaned close, her breath fogging the ancient paint—*hidden*. Because around the falling ember, painted so faintly you'd miss it unless you were looking, was a ring of tiny figures with their backs turned to the ascending gods, arms raised, catching it. Shielding it. Carrying it away.

Her hand rose without her permission and pressed flat against the wall.

The stone was warm. Of course it was warm. Everything here was warm. But under her palm, deep in the wall, something answered—a pulse, faint as a heartbeat heard through a door, and inside her chest the Ember stirred for the first time since the pass, since Brenna, since the frozen shout, and it did not stir like fear or like power. It stirred like recognition.

*Home,* said something that was not a voice. *This is where they took me from.*

* * *

"They killed her."

Vesper didn't know how long she'd been standing there when Maren spoke. Long enough that the lantern had burned low. He stood a few paces behind her, hat in his hands, an old man's habit of reverence in a place that had never been his to revere.

"Don't soften it," Vesper said. Her voice came out flat and strange. "I've read your sanitized records, Maren. I grew up on the Choir's version—*the gods kindled the world's first flame together in harmony*, all of it. Say what the wall says."

Maren was silent for a long moment. When he spoke, each word cost him.

"They killed her," he said. "Or they did whatever gods do instead of killing, which may be worse. They took the fire that burned before them, the fire that made their making possible, and they lit themselves with it, and they left her—" He gestured at the grieving face, the falling spark. "And then, do you understand, they wrote the story? They wrote all the stories. Every scripture in the world is a confession rewritten as a creation hymn. *In the beginning, the gods brought light.* Yes. Brought it. Carried it out of a murdered house in their hands."

Vesper's medallion was hot against her sternum. Not warm—hot, the way it had been getting hotter for weeks, and she pulled it out and looked at it, the blank disc of grey metal that had represented Vael's silence for twenty years, and the blankness suddenly meant something different. A god's icon wasn't blank because the god was absent. It was blank because everything the god was had been taken from somewhere else. There was nothing of Vael in Vael. There never had been. He was a shape wearing stolen fire, and the fire remembered, and somewhere in the deep places of him, the theft ached like a wound that divinity could not close.

"That's why the valley went silent," she said slowly. "That's why magic dies at the snowline. It's not that Hollowmere is cursed, or forgotten, or blessed with peace. It's that I'm there. The Ember nullifies the gods—it nullifies everything lit from her. The gods didn't abandon my valley, Maren. They *fled* it. All those prayers going unanswered for twenty years, all those people thinking themselves forsaken—" Her laugh came out cracked. "I was the answer. I was the reason. Every unanswered prayer in Hollowmere was answered by keeping me hidden."

"Yes," Maren said simply. There was no more rationing in him tonight. The ledger was open. "I helped hide you, and I told myself it was protection, and some nights I even believed it. But I knew what the valley was. I knew why your presence made the priests uneasy and the charms go dead. I raised you in a cage and called the cage a cradle, and I will answer for that for whatever remains of my life."

Vesper didn't turn around. She was looking at the Hearth-mother's face, at the grief that was not rage, and she was thinking about Vael's voice in the frost, *they are coming for what burns in you,* and about Sarelle's Choir wanting to carve the Ember out and feed it to Cindral, and about Isolde's winter wanting to swallow it and make the cold eternal, and about Vael himself, gentle Vael, asking—soon, he would ask soon, she could feel the question gathering in the silence like weather—to be made whole again.

Every god in the world wanted the fire back. None of them wanted to give it back.

"She caught it," Vesper said. "Look." She pointed at the tiny figures at the bottom of the final panel, the ones with their backs to heaven. "Someone caught the last spark and ran. Someone decided that what the gods stole, someone should keep out of their hands. Twenty-three years ago that someone was my mother, and she paid for it with a burning temple, and now every power in creation is marching across a frozen world to finish the job." She finally turned to face Maren, and her eyes were dry, which surprised her; she had expected tears and found something harder. "They're not coming for me, Maren. They never were. They're coming for her. I'm just the hearth she's hiding in."

Behind them, across the vast dark hall, the black basin shimmered over its dead coals, patient as it had been patient for a thousand years.

* * *

It was Ilsa who came running, boots in hand, barefoot on warm stone, with the look on her face that Vesper had learned to read as *bad news delivered at speed.*

"Riders," the girl panted. "On the ridge above the south slope. Doss counted nine. They're not attacking—they've stopped, just stopped, right at the edge of the storm line, like they're waiting for something."

"Choir?" Doss asked, arriving behind her, sword already drawn.

Ilsa shook her head. "White banners. Grey cloaks. And—" she hesitated, glancing at Vesper with an apology already in her face for what she had to say next, "—they've planted a pole. There's a flag on it. A *parley* flag."

The word dropped into the room like a stone into a well.

Isolde. It had to be Isolde. The Long Winter did not besiege; it negotiated, the way a glacier negotiates, patiently, with total confidence in the outcome. Nine riders at the edge of her own storm, a parley flag snapping in wind that belonged to her god—she was inviting Vesper up out of the warm dark, out of the murdered mother's house, into the snow, to talk.

Vesper looked down at the medallion in her palm. Blank metal, stolen fire, a god's whole nature reduced to a disc with nothing on it. Then she looked back at the mural, at the Hearth-mother's grieving eyes, at the small fleeing figures carrying the last spark into the dark.

*Whatever they offer you,* the Ember seemed to say, in the wordless way it said everything, *remember whose fire it is.*

Vesper put the medallion away, took up her sword, and started for the stairs.

"Get the others ready to move," she told Doss. "If I'm not back by dawn—"

"You'll be back by dawn," Doss said, in the voice that ended arguments. "Because I'm coming with you, and I don't take orders from dead women."

Above them, muffled by a hundred feet of mountain, the storm went on howling. And beneath the howl, so faint that Vesper alone might have heard it, something in the buried temple began—very softly, for the first time in a thousand years—to hum.
