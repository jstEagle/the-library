## 22. Isolde's Parley

The flags went up at dawn.

Vesper watched them from the ridge above the frozen millpond—two white banners on black poles, planted in the dead field between the tree line and the ice, and she thought they looked like two small surrendering ghosts waiting to be claimed by whoever walked toward them first. White was not a color Hollowmere wore in winter. White here meant snow, snow meant burial, and anything that stood against that whiteness and announced itself was either very brave or very certain it could not be killed.

She suspected Lady Isolde of the Long Winter was both.

"They'll want you alone," Doss said beside her. He had not slept; she could see it in the gray under his eyes, in the way his thumb kept finding the edge of his sword hilt and worrying it like an old scar. "Parley's parley, but with her kind, alone means *alone*. No blades within a hundred paces. Her terms, not ours."

"I know."

"You could refuse."

"She'd come anyway." Vesper pulled her gloves tighter. The cold had teeth this morning, sharper than the season should have allowed, and she knew why. The winter court had been circling the valley for six days now, patient as frost forming on glass, and every hour it waited the air grew a degree colder, as if the world itself were being slowly tuned to Isolde's key. "Better to hear what she wants from my own mouth than find out from what's left of the valley."

Doss grunted. It was not agreement. It was the sound a man made when he had run out of arguments and hated it. Behind him, half-hidden among the pines, Brenna and Sef and old Tam had their crossbows trained on the field despite the truce-terms, because Doss's crew obeyed the letter of a bargain and kept its spirit only when the spirit suited them.

"You come back," Doss said. Not a request. "Or I burn whatever's left of that woman's winter myself, truce or no."

"Doss."

"I mean it, Vess."

"I know you do." She almost smiled. "That's why I'm coming back."

* * *

The field between the banners had been swept clean of snow.

That was the first thing Vesper understood, walking down through the drifts with her hands empty and her heart hammering a soldier's rhythm against her ribs—the ground beneath her boots was bare brown earth, dry as August, in a circle thirty paces wide while everything beyond it lay buried to the knee. Isolde had not cleared the snow. She had simply decided it would not fall here, and the sky had agreed. The power of it sat in Vesper's stomach like swallowed river-stone. This was what divinity looked like when it was awake and paying attention: not lightning, not glory. Just weather, obedient.

Isolde stood at the center, at the second banner, and she was smaller than Vesper had imagined.

The stories painted her as a giantess, a figure of rime and iron eight feet tall with a crown of hanging icicles, and there were elements of that—the crown was real, delicate spars of ice woven through dark hair like frost ferns on a window—but the woman herself was slight, narrow-shouldered, dressed in furs so pale they seemed cut from the belly of the winter itself. Her face was young and old at once, the way a portrait ages without moving. Her eyes were the color of deep ice over deep water, and they did not blink often enough.

"Lady Vesper Thackeray," Isolde said. Her voice carried strangely, without echo, as though the air took dictation from her. "Or may I say Mirabel? I'm told that was the first name. Before the foundling name. Before the mercenary name." A pause, precise as a scalpel laid down on cloth. "I've read your ledger entry. What remains of it."

Vesper kept her face still. It cost something. "You broke into Maren Ostwick's records-house."

"I broke into nothing. Cold opens locks more honestly than thieves do. Water finds its way into every cellar; I am merely water that remembers." Isolde gestured to the swept earth between them. "Sit, if you like. I've warmed the ground. It seemed courteous. You'll forgive me if courtesy is not my native tongue—I've had three hundred years to forget it."

There was no chair. There didn't need to be; the earth itself held a faint warmth now, absurd and wrong in the frozen field, and Vesper understood it was an offering, a demonstration, a *look what I can give*. She stayed standing. So did Isolde, and something flickered across the winter-woman's face that might, in anyone else, have been approval.

"Your god sent you," Vesper said.

"My god does not send. My god *is*, and I am the part of him that walks." Isolde tilted her head. "But yes. Hravn knows what burns in you. He has known since the Choir's inquisitor cracked his relic against your hand and ran home weeping about null-borns and abominations. Men are loud even when they whisper. Especially when they're afraid."

"And you're not afraid."

"Of you?" For the first time, Isolde smiled, and it transformed her face into something terrible—not cruel, exactly, but vast, the way a glacier is vast, indifferent to the villages in its path. "Child, I have watched empires freeze. I watched the first temples burn and the second ones be built on their ashes. I have been the last warm thing in rooms full of dying kings. Whatever sleeps inside you, it is a candle. I am the season. Candles interest me. They do not frighten me."

"But you came to parley."

"I came," Isolde said, "because candles can be carried."

The wind moved through the pines behind Vesper, and she heard, threaded faintly in it, voices—her crew, watching, praying in the wordless way soldiers pray. And beneath that, deeper, in the marrow of her own bones where Vael had lived silent for twenty years and woken searing: a pressure, a warning, a god holding his breath.

*Careful,* the silence said, which was how Vael spoke now, mostly. *Careful, little oath.*

"Why am I here?" Vesper asked.

"Because you are tired." Isolde said it simply, without cruelty, and that was the worst of it—that she said it the way a physician names a wound. "Do not reach for your sword-hand. I am not attacking you. I am only going to tell you the truth, and you may do with it what you will. You are tired. You have been tired since you were fourteen years old and knelt before an altar to swear yourself to a god who had already stopped listening—or rather, who had already *fled*, and let a child believe his silence was her failure." She began, slowly, to walk the circumference of the cleared circle, and Vesper turned with her, the two of them orbiting each other like cautious moons. "I know what it is to be unchosen. I was mortal once. Did you know that? Everyone forgets. They look at the crown of ice and think I was poured out of winter fully formed. But I was a girl in a village much like this one, in a country much colder than this one, and I prayed for forty years to gods who never answered, because they were busy devouring each other's fire and had none left over for girls in villages."

Vesper's throat was tight. "That doesn't make you what you are."

"No. What I chose afterward makes me what I am." Isolde stopped. The banner above her stirred without wind. "And that is precisely what I offer you, Vesper Thackeray. The choice. The one no one else will give you. The Choir wants to carve you open and pour you into their god's mouth. Your Vael wants—" she laughed softly, and the laugh frosted the air between them, "—well. Ask him sometime what he wants, and watch how gently he lies. Even your dear archivist wanted you hidden, contained, a secret kept in a valley where nothing divine could ever touch you. Everyone who has ever loved you has loved you *as a vessel*. I am the only one offering to love you as a woman."

"That's a lie."

"It is an offer," Isolde said. "Test it against the others. That is all I ask."

* * *

They walked, after that. Isolde suggested it—said negotiations went better in motion, said sitting was for judges and she was not judging—and so they paced the dead field together under the two white flags while the sun climbed thin and colorless behind the eastern ridge, and Isolde talked, and Vesper learned the shape of the trap, which was that it had no walls.

"Immortality," Isolde said. "Not the vulgar kind—not the Choir's kind, where they stitch souls into relics until the soul forgets its own name. The true kind. Hravn's winter does not end, and those who stand at its heart do not end with it. You would stop. Do you understand what a gift that is? To simply *stop*? No more winters of the soul. No more watching everyone you might love grow old because you were too afraid to love them—you've done that already, haven't you? The sergeant. Doss. You've spent eleven years standing one careful step away from that man because everyone you've ever been close to becomes either a hostage or a grave."

Vesper said nothing. Her silence was answer enough, and Isolde received it without gloating, which somehow made it worse.

"Purpose," Isolde continued. "You were made an inheritance, not a person—it's written all over you, in how you move, in how you keep accounts of everything, debts and favors, as though existence itself were a ledger someone might audit. Fine. Then be given a purpose you *choose*. Stand at my right hand. The Ember in you, the fire all gods were lit from—under my winter it would be safe forever. Contained, yes, but contained the way a hearth is contained: warm, honored, *home*. No Choir with its knives. No resurrected priests with their ledgers. No god whispering warnings in your bones and calling it guardianship. Just you, and work worthy of you, and a world finally quiet."

"And the price," Vesper said quietly.

"The price is that the winter becomes law." Isolde met her eyes, and did not flinch, and Vesper gave her credit for that. Most liars flinched. Isolde believed every word. "Eternal winter. Yes. I will not dress it as anything else. The world under Hravn's season is a world without war over the divine—because there will be nothing left to war over. One god. One law. One long, clean, *quiet* peace. You have seen what faith does when it multiplies, child. You watched the Choir take hostages in your valley. Ten villagers a day. You walked into their camp with bound hands to buy strangers' lives. Imagine a world where no one can ever again do that to anyone, because there is nothing left worth burning for." She spread her hands, fur and frost and terrible sincerity. "I am not offering you cruelty. I am offering you the end of cruelty, purchased once, paid for once, by everyone but felt by no one. Winter does not hate what it freezes. It preserves it. Perfectly. Forever."

"And the people in it?"

"Asleep," Isolde said. "Dreaming. Safe."

"Safe isn't asleep."

"No," Isolde said softly. "It isn't. But I stopped being able to tell the difference around my second century, and I am offering you the chance to skip that particular lesson."

* * *

Here was the thing Vesper would carry out of that field, the thing that would sit in her chest for years after like a splinter too fine to draw: she understood.

She understood Isolde completely, and that was the danger. Twenty years of being unchosen had built a hunger in her that had no honest name, and Isolde was setting a feast in front of it course by course. An end to hiding—she had not realized how badly she wanted that until it was spoken aloud, until the words *no more hiding* landed in her like a struck bell and rang through every hollow year. To be seen. To be known entirely and kept anyway. To stop being a secret wearing a woman's body. Isolde offered it with the confidence of someone who had already seen everything there was to see of Vesper and had not recoiled, had not gone pale like the hedge-priest, had not cracked like the inquisitor's relic. The winter did not fear the fire. The winter *wanted* it.

And purpose. Gods, purpose. All her life she had been a sword looking for a hand, and every hand that had held her had lied about why.

"If I refused," Vesper said, "you'd hunt me."

"Yes."

"You'd freeze my friends on some pass somewhere. You'd bury this whole valley in a season that never lifts. You'd do it patiently, politely, the way you do everything, and you'd call it preservation."

"I would," Isolde said. "And I would grieve it. Both things are true. That is the oldest truth I know—that grief and necessity can share a bed as comfortably as any married couple." She stepped closer, close enough that the cold coming off her raised the fine hairs on Vesper's arms, close enough that Vesper saw her own reflection doubled in those deep-glacier eyes, small and warm and impossibly far away. "So refuse, if you must. Refuse loudly, if it helps. But know that I am not your enemy, Vesper Thackeray. I am your future, offering to be gentle about it. Every fire goes out. Every ember cools. I am simply asking whether yours would rather cool in a hearth or in the snow."

For a moment—one heartbeat, maybe two—Vesper let herself stand in the offer. Let herself feel its full weight, the way you test a blade's balance before deciding whether to trust it. Immortality. Purpose. An end to hiding. Everything she had ever wanted, arranged on white snow like a merchant's goods.

Everything except herself. Because the woman Isolde wanted at her right hand was not Vesper Thackeray, sergeant of the Broken Oath, woman who liked bad wine and worse jokes and the smell of Doss's pipe smoke. It was the vessel. The container. The Ember-bearer, preserved forever in a museum of ice, warm and honored and *gone*—the way a candle is preserved inside a lantern that is never, ever opened.

Her mother had named her Mirabel. *A wonder to behold.* Not a wonder to be kept.

Vesper exhaled, and her breath hung white between them, and she watched Isolde watch it—watched the winter queen track that small mortal warmth with something that was almost tenderness, almost envy.

"No," Vesper said.

Isolde closed her eyes briefly, as if in prayer, or farewell.

"I thought perhaps," she said. "One hopes. Three hundred years, and one still hopes." She stepped back, and the warmth died out of the ground between them so suddenly Vesper felt it like a door closing. When Isolde opened her eyes again they were only ice, and the tenderness was packed away somewhere deep, the way you pack away a dress you intend to wear again. "Then hear the terms of refusal, lady of the ember. Under the old laws—which I keep, because I am the last creature alive who keeps anything—you have refused a parley-gift freely given. That is your right. And it invokes mine."

The temperature fell. Not gradually—all at once, like stepping through a wall, and the snow at the edges of the cleared circle groaned and shifted, and far off across the valley Vesper heard something vast exhale.

"I mark you for the Hunt," Isolde said, and her voice was still gentle, still almost kind, and that was the most frightening thing yet. "Not today. I am patient; patience is the only virtue winter has ever needed. But from this hour, every wolf of the white, every frozen thing that walks, every cold that finds you in the night belongs to my pursuit. Your friends will scatter because they must. Your valley will learn what a long winter means. And you will run, Vesper Thackeray—run north, run to your ruined temple, run wherever the fire in you tells you to go—because running is the only choice left that I have left you."

She turned away toward her banner, then paused, half-turned, the ice crown catching the colorless light.

"For what it is worth," Isolde said, "when you stood just now deciding—I saw you consider it honestly. Few mortals manage honesty. It was the finest thing I have seen in a century." A pause. "It changes nothing. Go home, Mirabel. Say goodbye to your ordinary life. Winter is coming to collect its answer."

Vesper stood alone in the dead field between the two white flags, breath steaming, heart steady, and behind her in the treeline she heard Doss shout her name—and ahead, far to the north, past the pass, past the frozen rivers, she felt the Ember turn over in her chest like a sleeper waking, and begin, very softly, to burn.

* * *

She told them at the millpond, all of it, every word, because Doss had earned the truth and because she was done letting people curate her ignorance—even kindly, even for her protection. Brenna swore. Sef went gray. Tam, who had survived four companies and two wars, said only, "Then we'd best get to the passes before her weather does."

"We scatter," Vesper said. "Safehouses. The old network. If we stay bunched, we freeze bunched."

"And you?" Doss asked.

Vesper looked north, where the mountains stood white and patient as an open page.

"I go to the First Hearth," she said. "Where the fire was born. It's the only place left where I might learn what I actually am—before she takes that from me too."

Above the valley, unseen, the first flakes of the new snow began to fall—and they did not melt.
