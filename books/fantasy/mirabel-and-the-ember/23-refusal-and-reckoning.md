## 23. Refusal and Reckoning

The parley tent smelled of pine smoke and something older beneath it—cold stone, deep water, the breath of a glacier that had never once melted. Lady Isolde sat across the brazier from Vesper like a portrait that had learned to breathe: silver hair braided with frost-pearls, a gown the color of first snowfall, hands folded in her lap as though she had all the time the world could offer her. Which, Vesper supposed, she did.

Between them, the fire burned blue-white and gave no heat.

"You haven't touched your wine," Isolde said.

"I watched it freeze."

"Ah." A small smile, patient as ice forming on a pond. "Yes. That would happen near me, at first. It eases, in time. Everything eases, in time. That is rather the point of what I'm offering you."

Outside the tent, the wind moved through the pass like a slow procession. Vesper could hear her people beyond the canvas—Doss's low voice, the nervous stamp of horses, Brennick coughing into his scarf. Eleven of them had come to the parley ground under white flags, and eleven of them were listening to the silence inside this tent and hating every second of it.

Vesper wrapped her hands around the frozen goblet anyway, just to have something to hold. The cold bit through her gloves and she welcomed it. Pain was simple. Pain told the truth.

"Say it again," she said. "All of it. I want to hear how it sounds when you say it plainly."

Isolde tilted her head, amused. "You know how it sounds. I said it plainly an hour ago."

"Say it again."

The lady of the Long Winter studied her for a long moment, and then—perhaps because she was ancient enough not to be proud, or perhaps because she genuinely believed she had already won—she obliged.

"The Ember sleeps in your chest," Isolde said. "A spark of the First Fire, older than every god who ever took a name. Hravn wants it. Cindral wants it. Vael, if he has found his voice again, wants it too. Whoever holds it whole will shape what divinity is for the next ten thousand years—or until someone pries it from their cold dead hands, whichever comes first. You cannot carry it forever, Vesper Thackeray. You are mortal meat around an immortal coal. Eventually it will burn through you, or someone will cut it out of you, and either way the world will decide its future without your consent." She unfolded her hands, and frost crept delicately across the table between them, feathering out from her fingertips like fern leaves. "I am offering you the only version of that future in which you are not a victim. Sit beside me. Share the winter. Your years stop counting. Your enemies become my enemies. And when the Ember passes from you to Hravn—as it must—it passes by *choice*, witnessed, honored. You will be remembered as the woman who ended the age of thieves instead of dying as one more stolen thing."

The brazier crackled. Somewhere outside, a horse screamed briefly and went quiet, and Vesper's stomach tightened—no, just the cold, just horses being horses in a frozen pass. Probably.

"And if I refuse?" Vesper asked, though she already knew. She'd known since the moment Isolde's heralds had appeared on the snowline riding elk the size of barns, their antlers hung with prayer-flags frozen stiff as boards.

Isolde sighed, and it was almost tender. Almost motherly. That was the worst part—that everything cruel about her came dressed in kindness, the way frost comes dressed in beautiful patterns across a window while it kills the crop behind the glass.

"If you refuse," Isolde said, "then you are prey. Not because I am cruel, child. Because the Ember cannot be allowed to wander unclaimed. Cindral's Choir will take it and burn the world down to a single faith. Hravn—" she spread one elegant hand, "—will take it and make the world quiet and orderly and *still*, which I confess I would prefer, but even stillness requires that it be taken *first*. There is no path where you keep it and no one comes for it. So I mark you for the Hunt—not in malice. In mercy. Better my hunters find you than Sarelle's inquisitors. My people kill cleanly. Hers do not."

"You've thought about this a lot," Vesper said. "The speech. The framing. 'Not in malice, in mercy.'" She set down the frozen goblet. "Did you practice it? In front of a mirror? Did the mirror freeze?"

Something flickered behind Isolde's eyes—the first true expression Vesper had seen there, and it looked almost like delight. "Oh, you are *wonderful*. Maren Ostwick hid you well, but he couldn't hide the spine, could he. That's the Ember's doing, I think. Fire makes people obstinate." She rose, smooth as snow sliding off a roof, and the temperature in the tent dropped so sharply that Vesper's breath came out in solid curls. "Last chance, Vessel. I am offering you eternity, purpose, an end to running. I am offering you a seat at the only table that will matter. Every god who ever lived stole his fire from something smaller than himself. I am offering you the chance to be the one who wasn't robbed—or the one who robs back. Think of your crew. Think of Doss, growing old on escort contracts. Think of the girl you were at fourteen, kneeling in the snow, praying to a god who never answered. I can give you a god who answers *everything*."

And there it was. There was the hook, sunk deep and turned gentle. Vesper felt it catch in the place where Vael's voice had lived, that hollow behind her sternum where twenty years of silence had grown scar tissue over longing. Isolde had seen it. Of course she had. She was old enough to read hearts the way Doss read weather.

Vesper stood up slowly. Her knees ached from the cold. She was thirty-seven years old, she had a bad shoulder that ached before rain, and she was standing in a tent at the top of the world being offered immortality by a woman made of winter.

"No," she said.

Isolde waited.

"No," Vesper said again, louder, and heard her own voice crack on it, and didn't care. "You're right about one thing. Every god stole his fire. From the murals in that buried temple—from the First Hearth—you killed your maker, all of you, and you've been spending the theft ever since and calling it holiness. And now you want me to hand over the last piece of what was stolen so the theft can be finished properly. So it can be *notarized*." She laughed, and it came out ragged. "My whole life, everyone decided what I was for. Maren decided I was a vault. Vael decided I was a leash. The Choir decided I was a relic. Now you decide I'm a crown jewel. Well, here's my decision, lady. I'm not for anything. I'm not a vessel, or a vault, or a vessel of vengeance, or whatever pretty word you want to put on it. I'm a person. I'm a mercenary with a bad shoulder and eleven people waiting outside this tent who followed me up a frozen mountain because they're idiots, and I intend to keep being a person for exactly as long as I've got, and then I intend to die, and whatever happens to the Ember after that—" she jabbed a finger at her own chest, "—happens *over my body*, and let me tell you, there are easier bodies."

The silence that followed had weight. Frost bloomed up the tent poles. The brazier's blue flame shrank to a bead.

Then Isolde smiled, and it was the smile of a woman closing a ledger.

"So be it," she said softly. "Vesper Thackeray. Mirabel, if the old records speak true." Vesper flinched—she couldn't help it—and Isolde noted the flinch the way a falconer notes a wingbeat. "I name you quarry. I loose the Hunt upon your scent. Hear me, winter: wherever she runs, follow. Whatever shelters her, remember it. Whoever loves her—" and here her voice gentled into something truly terrible, "—give them time to reconsider."

She drew one finger through the air, and the cold itself wrote something—a sigil hanging luminous between them, a branching shape like a deer track crossed with a lightning bolt—and then Isolde flicked her wrist and the sigil flew, impossibly fast, and struck Vesper's breastbone above the medallion.

It didn't hurt. That was the horror of it. It sank in like a snowflake landing on warm skin, and Vesper felt it settle somewhere beneath her ribs, a small cold star planted in the dark, and she knew—with the same certainty she'd known when Vael's voice had returned—that she would feel it every day for the rest of her life. A compass needle in reverse. Not showing her the way. Showing the *hunters*.

"There," Isolde said, sitting back down as though nothing had happened, resuming her folded hands, serene as a chapel. "Now we understand each other. Go home, little ember. Burn bright. It only makes the tracking easier."

Vesper walked out of the tent. Her legs held. She was quietly proud of that.

* * *

Nobody spoke on the ride down from the pass.

That was the thing about Doss's company—they were veterans of enough bad contracts to know the sound of a parley gone wrong, and Vesper's face coming out of that tent had been legible at fifty yards. They formed up around her without being asked, Brennick on her left with his hand resting easy on his axe, young Petra ahead scouting the switchbacks with more diligence than the terrain required, old Halvard humming a marching song from a war nobody remembered, off-key, deliberately, because Halvard hummed when things were worst and everyone knew it and nobody ever told him to stop.

They made camp that night in the lee of a granite ridge, fires low, watches doubled. Vesper sat apart, as she always did, working a whetstone along a blade that didn't need it, and listened to her company pretend she wasn't the reason the temperature dropped every time she exhaled too hard.

Doss came and sat beside her eventually. He didn't ask what happened. He never asked what happened; he asked what she needed, and tonight what she needed was for no one to talk, so he sat in silence and shared her fire and passed her the flask of plum brandy that had been making the rounds since the border wars.

"They'll come at night," Doss said finally. "Her hunters. Cold ones, if the stories are true. Walk right through a picket line."

"I know."

"We can't outrun winter, Ves. Not with wagons. Not with the wounded."

"I know."

He turned the flask in his big hands. In the firelight Vesper saw lines on his face she hadn't noticed a year ago—no, that was a lie, she'd noticed them and pretended she hadn't, the way you pretend not to notice a roof sagging. "There's a place," he said. "Old smuggling cache, three days south, under the ruined watchtower at Grayfen. Stone all the way around, half-buried, one entrance. We used it in the Harrow campaign. If anything can hold off the cold, it's that."

"It won't hold off the Choir."

"No. But the Choir marches on roads, and roads take time, and time is what we buy." He looked at her sideways. "We split up. Small groups, separate routes, different safehouses. Anyone following the mark on you follows *you*—so you don't go to Grayfen. You go north, high, into the fells, where nothing tracks well. The rest of us scatter and draw whatever eyes we can."

Vesper stared into the fire. "You're splitting the company."

"I'm keeping the company alive. Different thing." He drank, offered her the flask, and she took it. The brandy burned going down, a small hearth in her chest right next to the cold star Isolde had planted, and she hated how much she needed it. "Been twenty-two years, Ves. Men have come and gone. You and me and Halvard are the only ones left from the founding, and Halvard mostly remembers the songs." He was quiet a moment. "Company doesn't scatter because it's broken. It scatters because staying together is how you all die in one hole."

"When do we regroup?"

"Midwinter. Grayfen, if the tower stands. If it doesn't—" He shrugged, eloquent in the way soldiers are. "Then whoever's left finds whoever's left."

* * *

They broke camp before dawn, in the blue hour when the snow gave back the last of the starlight, and the scattering was worse than any battle Vesper had ever walked away from.

Battles had noise. This was quiet. Brennick gripped her forearm hard enough to bruise and said only, "North, sister. High ground. Don't be brave; be annoying. Annoying survives." Petra hugged her quick and fierce and was crying and pretended not to be, and Vesper pretended not to notice, and both pretenses were gifts. Halvard pressed something into her hand—a carved wooden bird, crude, a child's whittling, one of a dozen he kept in his pack and never explained—and said, "For luck. Bring it back worn smooth." The twins, Osk and Ilse, saluted her with parade-ground formality that was entirely mockery and entirely sincere. One by one, eleven became four became two became one, riders peeling off at trail junctions into the gray morning, each departure a small door closing.

Doss rode with her to the fork below the fells, because of course he did, because he would ride her to the gates of whatever hell came next and then argue with the gatekeeper about the toll.

At the fork he reined in. Snow was starting again, fine and dry, the kind that erased tracks in minutes and would erase hers whether she wanted it to or not.

"Doss." She looked at him, and found that everything she wanted to say was too large for the space between them, so she said the smallest true thing instead. "Twenty-two years. Thank you."

He grunted, embarrassed, and reached over and gripped the back of her neck the way he had when she was nineteen and had taken an arrow meant for him. "Midwinter," he said. "Grayfen. Don't make me come find you."

"You wouldn't survive the fells."

"No," he agreed. "But I'd die annoyed, and you'd never forgive yourself." He let go. Turned his horse south. Then stopped, and for a moment—just a moment—he looked back at her with an expression she couldn't read, something caught between love and guilt, his mouth opening slightly as if a word were balanced on his tongue.

"What?" she said.

The word balanced. Teetered. The snow fell between them.

"Nothing," Doss said. "Ride safe, Ves."

And he was gone, down the southern trail, and Vesper told herself the look had been nothing, told herself it twice, told herself a third time, and then stopped telling herself anything and rode north into the fells alone.

* * *

The fells were a country of bone and wind. No trees past the tree line, just granite teeth and drifts that swallowed a horse to the belly, and a sky so wide and white it felt less like weather than like judgment. Vesper walked the last day—she sent the horse back down toward the valley with a slap on its rump, because a horse's tracks told stories and her own boots told fewer—and climbed until the world below was a smear of white and the world above was nothing at all.

She holed up that night in a shallow cave with a fire the size of two cupped hands, and she took out Halvard's wooden bird and turned it over, and she took off her medallion and turned that over too. Blank as ever. Twenty-three years blank. The sigil Isolde had planted sat beneath her sternum like a splinter of midnight; when she breathed deeply, she could feel it, faintly cold, patiently pointing. Not at her. Through her. Toward her, for anyone with the eyes to read it.

"They are coming for what burns in you," Vael had told her, in the frost, in the dark of the records-house, his voice like a bell rung underwater. She had thought that warning was about the Choir. About Sarelle's inquisitors with their cracking relics and their patient smiles.

It hadn't been only about them.

"Are you watching?" she said aloud, to the cave, to the blank medallion, to the god who had stayed silent for twenty years and then come back speaking warnings instead of comfort. "Can you even see this far north? Or did you flee the valley too, like the rest of them?"

No answer. Of course no answer. Gods answered prayers now, the taverns said, the pilgrims said, the whole thawed world said—but apparently not hers, not here, not unless it suited some plan she wasn't party to. She was done being part of other people's plans. That was the whole speech she'd given Isolde, and she'd meant it, and meaning it cost exactly what Isolde had promised it would cost: everyone. Eleven doors closing in the gray morning light. Midwinter at Grayfen, if there was a midwinter, if there was a Grayfen, if there was anyone left to stand in it.

Choosing yourself, Maren had once said, over wine she'd stolen from the temple stores, is the most expensive purchase a person ever makes. Vesper had been fifteen and hadn't understood. She understood now. The price was loneliness, paid in full, non-negotiable.

She fed a twig to her tiny fire and watched it catch, and in the flame she saw—for half a heartbeat, the way you see a face in smoke—the burning temple again, the mural fire, a woman's face turning toward her through the blaze with an expression that was not fear but *urgency*, as if trying to hand her something.

"Who were you?" Vesper whispered.

The fire popped. The cold star under her ribs pulsed once, gently, like a second heartbeat learning its rhythm. And far to the south, down where the trails forked and the roads ran toward Hollowmere, she felt—did not hear, *felt*, the way you feel a door open in a distant room—something else begin to move. Not winter. Winter she knew now, knew its patience and its elegance. This was hotter. This was incense and ash and a hymn sung by throats that used to belong to men.

The Choir had found the scattered company's trail. Or one of them. Or—

Vesper closed her fist around the medallion until the edges bit her palm, and made herself a promise in the language she trusted best, the plain soldier's grammar of next steps: survive the night, move at dawn, stay annoying.

Behind her, at the mouth of the cave, the snow lay unbroken in every direction.

Except one. Where, sometime in the last hour, without any wind to explain it, a single line of footprints had appeared—leading in.
