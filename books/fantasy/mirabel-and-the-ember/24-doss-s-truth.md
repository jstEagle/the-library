## 24. Doss's Truth

The safehouse was a shepherd's hut above the tree line, the kind of place that existed on no map because maps were made by people who had never needed to disappear. Doss had found it years ago, back when the company was four hungry souls and a mule named Bishop, and he'd paid the old widow who owned it in salt and stories until she agreed to leave the door unlocked and the hearth swept for anyone who knew to knock twice and then once.

He knocked twice. Then once.

Inside, it smelled of cold stone and old smoke and, faintly, of Vesper—the particular clean ash-and-iron scent she carried now that the Ember lived in her like a coal banked in a grate. She sat on the floor with her back against the wall, boots off, sword across her knees, cleaning a blade that did not need cleaning. The firelight moved on her face and made her look, for one unguarded moment, like the girl who'd signed on with him at seventeen, all elbows and stubbornness and a dead god's medallion swinging from her neck.

She wasn't that girl anymore. None of them were.

"You're late," she said, not looking up.

"Isolde's pickets are thick along the pass road. Had to come around by the scree." He set down his pack, shrugged off his wet cloak, hung it by the fire. His hands were steady. He'd made sure of that before he came in. A man's hands could betray him faster than his tongue.

"Anyone follow you?"

"Snow followed me. Snow and nothing else." He crouched at the hearth and fed it a knot of pine. The flames took it greedily, and he watched them instead of her, because watching her was about to become very difficult. "Vesper."

"Don't." The whetstone stopped. "Whatever speech you've climbed up here to give me, don't. I'm tired, Doss. I'm tired in my bones, in my blood, in whatever's burning in me that isn't blood. If you're going to tell me the company's done, just say it plain so I can sleep."

"It's not that."

"Then what?"

He turned around. He looked at her—really looked, the way he hadn't let himself since the night the Choir's messenger had slid a purse across a tavern table heavy enough to bend the wood—and what he saw was a woman who had lost everything except herself, and was holding even that by her fingernails. Her eyes had changed since Emberlight. There was light behind them now, literal light, a gold flicker deep in the iris like sun through amber. When she was calm it slept. When she was afraid it woke.

She was afraid now. She was always afraid now. She'd gotten very good at making it look like anger.

"I have to tell you something," Doss said, "and after I tell it, you're going to want me gone. Maybe worse than gone. And I'll take whatever you decide, but I need you to hear all of it before you decide. Can you do that? Can you sit still for the whole of it?"

Vesper set down the blade. Slowly. The way she did everything when she was most dangerous—slowly, deliberately, giving herself time to choose the sharp thing instead of the quick one.

"Say it," she said.

* * *

He started with the tavern, because that was where it had happened and lies lived longest when they were dressed as geography.

"Three days after your snow melted in front of two hundred pilgrims—after the whole valley saw what you are—a man found me in the Drowned Ox. Not Choir. He said he wasn't Choir, and I believed him, which should have been my first warning, because men who need you to believe they aren't something usually are exactly that thing. Grey coat, soft voice, hands like a clerk's. He knew the company's numbers. Knew we hadn't been paid since the reliquary job. Knew Tam's sister was sick in Lowmarsh and Fenner owed gambling debts in three towns. He'd done his reading."

Vesper said nothing. The fire popped. Outside, the wind worried at the loose shutter the way a dog worries a bone.

"He offered me a fortune," Doss went on. "Not company money. My money. Enough to retire every blade under my command, settle every debt, buy Tam's sister a physician and Fenner a new life. All I had to do was deliver you. Not harm you—he was very clear about that, almost gentle about it. Just bring you to a certain crossroads at a certain hour, and walk away. They'd handle the rest. He called you 'the null-born' like it was a delivery manifest. Like you were a crate of salt pork."

"How much?" Vesper asked. Her voice was flat. That flatness frightened him more than shouting would have.

"Does it matter?"

"It matters. I want to know what I'm worth."

Doss closed his eyes. "Four hundred crowns. Plus a pension for the company, five years guaranteed, drawn on a bank in Cael Saren."

Silence. Long enough that he heard the wind change direction, heard an owl somewhere below the hut ask its one question of the dark.

"And you refused," Vesper said at last. It wasn't a question either, but it was close to one. "You're telling me this because you refused."

"I refused," Doss said. "And then I didn't tell you. And those are two different things, Vesper, and only one of them is forgivable, and I've spent every day since trying to convince myself it's the same thing. It isn't. I know it isn't."

"Why didn't you tell me?"

"Because—" He stopped. The true answer sat in his chest like a swallowed stone, and he'd carried it up a mountain to be rid of it, and now that the moment came he understood why men lied until their graves. Because the truth was small and ugly and entirely human. "Because for about ten seconds, I considered it."

Her head came up. The light behind her eyes woke—not blazing, not yet, but a banked glow, a forge at rest.

"Not seriously," he said quickly. "Gods, not seriously. But for ten seconds I stood there with that man's voice in my ear doing arithmetic. Four hundred crowns. Five years of wages. Tam's sister breathing easy. Fenner out from under. Everyone fed through the winter, everyone safe, everyone *alive*, which is more than I can promise them following you—and I stood there and I let myself count it. Ten seconds. Maybe twelve. And then I told him no, and I spat at his feet, and he smiled like he'd expected exactly that, and he left the purse anyway. Left it on the table. Said, 'Keep it. Consider it a retainer. Men change their minds when the people they love start burning.'"

Vesper rose. She didn't seem to decide to; she simply rose, the way water rises when heat gets under it. The firelight bent toward her. He would swear afterward, to himself, alone, that the flames actually leaned.

"You kept the purse," she said.

"I gave it away. Every coin. To the villagers whose homes the Choir burned when they took Hollowmere. I gave it out in the night so no one would know where it came from, least of all me." He laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I couldn't keep it and I couldn't throw it in a river—that felt like throwing *you* in a river. So I laundered my own temptation into charity and told myself that made it clean. It doesn't. I know it doesn't."

"Doss—"

"Let me finish. Please. You said you'd hear all of it." His voice shook now and he let it. He was done being steady; steadiness had been the lie's uniform. "Here's the rest, then. The part that matters. Every day since that tavern, I've been watching you sideways. Watching whether you noticed me noticing. Flinching when you looked at me too long. Saying yes when I meant maybe. And I watched the others do it too—Tam, Fenner, little Wren—they all got approached or they all got scared, and none of us said a word to you, because we were all protecting ourselves from the same question: *what if she asks us to die for her, and what if we say yes, and what if we say no?* We made you carry us, Vesper. You, with the whole world hunting you—you carried our fear too, without ever knowing its weight. That's my crime. Not the ten seconds. The silence after. The silence was the betrayal."

The glow behind her eyes brightened. Frost crept up the inside of the window glass in feathering patterns, though the hearth burned hot. The temperature in the room was dropping, or rising—he couldn't tell; the air itself seemed confused, the way it always got around her when she felt too much.

"You want absolution," she said quietly.

"No."

"You want me to say it's fine. You want the scene where I'm angry and then I'm not, where forgiveness comes fast and cheap and we drink to it and ride on. You've rehearsed it. I can see the rehearsal all over your face."

"Yes," he admitted. "That's exactly what I want. And I know better than to want it, and I climbed a mountain anyway to ask for it, because wanting things you shouldn't have is apparently the whole of my character."

Something moved through her then—something that might have been laughter, strangled at birth. She pressed the heel of her hand against her sternum, hard, the way she did when the Ember stirred, and breathed, and the frost on the window stopped growing.

"Do you know what the worst part is?" she said. "It's not the offer. It's not even that you hesitated. Doss—I *am* dangerous. Whatever's in me, it melts snow and cracks relics and makes gods wake up after twenty years of silence, and every person near me is standing closer to a fire than they ever agreed to stand. If someone offered *me* four hundred crowns to walk away from all of you—from Maren, from the crew, from every soul Isolde and Sarelle will burn through to get at me—do you think I wouldn't count? Do you think I haven't counted? Some nights I've stood watch and done the arithmetic myself. How many lives is one life worth when the one life is the thing everyone's dying over?"

"Vesper—"

"The worst part," she said, stepping toward him, and the firelight leaned again, "is that you decided. You decided in that tavern, and you decided right, and then you took the decision away from me. You decided I was too fragile to know someone had priced my head. You decided I'd break. Twenty-three years, Doss—Maren deciding what I could know, the valley deciding what I could be, the gods deciding what I could carry—and now you. Everyone keeps deciding *for* me, and calling it protection, and the protection always costs me the truth."

The words landed like blows, and he took them standing, because sitting down would have been mercy and he hadn't earned mercy.

"So here's what happens now," Vesper said. "I'm not casting you out. Not because I forgive you—I don't, not yet, maybe not soon—but because casting you out would be me deciding for you one more time. Deciding what you get to be: the traitor, the coward, the cautionary tale. No. You stay. But we do it differently. From tonight, no more decisions made *about* each other. Everything spoken. Every offer, every fear, every ugly arithmetic done out loud where the whole company can hear it. If another grey-coated clerk slides a purse across a table, you bring me the purse and the man's description and the exact words, and we decide together what it means. If you're afraid, you say you're afraid. If I'm dangerous—and I am—you say that too, to my face, and I will listen, because I'd rather hear it from you than learn it from a frozen village."

She put out her hand. Her palm, he noticed, was warm—visibly warm, the skin flushed like she'd been holding a cup of tea, though her fingers were bare to the mountain cold.

"Pact," she said. "Honest and ugly and spoken aloud. Trust rebuilt on confessed failure, not on pretending the failure never happened. Can you live under those terms? Because they cut both ways. I'll be held to them too. Every secret I keep from here on, I keep *with* you, not from you."

Doss looked at her hand. Then he did the hardest thing he'd done since the tavern, harder than refusing four hundred crowns: he took it, knowing the warmth of it, knowing what lived behind her ribs, knowing she was offering him the fire instead of the ashes.

"Pact," he said.

They stood like that a moment, grip firm, and outside the wind dropped all at once, the way wind does when it has listened long enough.

* * *

Later, when the fire had settled and Vesper slept in the corner with her sword in reach and the Ember's glow dimming in her closed eyes like embers under ash, Doss sat up and wrote by candlelight. Not a letter. A list. Names, dates, sums—the whole ledger of the company's compromised honor, every approach, every fear, every silence, in his own blunt hand. In the morning he would read it aloud to all of them, per the pact, and let the words fall where they fell.

At the bottom of the page, before he could stop himself, he wrote one more line:

*She forgave me before I deserved it. That's not weakness. That's the strongest thing I've ever seen anyone do, and I've seen her crack a holy relic with her bare hand.*

He stared at the line a long while, then didn't strike it out. Some truths earned their ink.

He was snuffing the candle when he heard it—footsteps on the scree below, many of them, unhurried, deliberate. Not the soft scramble of hunters. The measured tread of soldiers who wanted to be heard.

He crossed to the window. Down the mountainside, torches moved in a column through the dark, dozens of them, and above the foremost torch flew a banner he knew from bad dreams: white ash on black cloth, the sigil of the Ashen Choir.

But it was not the banner that stopped his breath. It was what marched beneath it—prisoners, roped in a line, stumbling in the torchlight. He counted them twice because the first count could not be true.

Twenty. Villagers of Hollowmere. And at the end of the rope, gray-faced and upright as a fencepost, walked Maren Ostwick.

Below the window, a herald's voice rose through the cold, pitched to carry, pitched for her:

"Null-born! Prelate Sarelle sends greetings, and a promise: ten lives for every day you hide. The counting begins at dawn."

Behind him, in the corner, Vesper opened her eyes—and they were full of fire.
