## 25. The Choir Takes Hollowmere

The ash came before the soldiers.

Prelate Sarelle stood at the mouth of the valley road and watched it drift down on the wind like a second snowfall, grey and fine and smelling of burnt vellum. It was the ash of burned prayers—she had ordered a thousand of them fed to the fire that morning, petitions gathered from villages along the march, each one read aloud and then consigned to flame so that Cindral might taste what his faithful wanted most. The god had answered with warmth against her sternum, a slow approving heat, and Sarelle had smiled for the first time in days.

Hollowmere lay below her in its bowl of white, chimneys smoking, bells silent. A valley without magic. She had walked through a hundred such places in her life—the world's dead teeth, its blind spots—and had always felt in them a kind of insult. Here, she thought, is where faith goes to be forgotten.

But not today.

"Form the column," she said. "White banners forward. No blades drawn until I give the word. We are not conquerors." She let the pause stretch, let her voice carry down the line of grey-robed inquisitors and steel-capped soldiers. "We are an answer."

Behind her, Brother Callum adjusted the reliquary chains across his chest and said nothing. He had been quiet since the pass. He had been quiet since the relic cracked in a foundling mercenary's hand, though he believed she did not know he'd reported it. Sarelle knew everything that happened under her banner; that was the whole architecture of her faith. A god who saw all required servants who did likewise.

They descended into Hollowmere as the light went long and gold, and the people came out of their houses to watch, and Sarelle thought: *look how easily they gather. Look how hungry they already are.*

* * *

Tam Wicker saw them first from the roof of the tithe-barn, where he'd gone to mend storm-loosened shingles and mostly to get away from his wife's silence. The column came up the old salt road in perfect order, white banners snapping, and the sight of them did something strange to Tam's chest—not fear exactly, or not only fear. He was fifty-one years old and he had never seen a soldier of any god, because gods did not send soldiers to Hollowmere. Gods did not come to Hollowmere at all.

He climbed down faster than a man his age should and ran to the square, where others were already gathering with that same half-ashamed eagerness he felt in himself. Old Bess Halloway was there, gripping her prayer beads—beads she'd kept hidden in a floorboard for twenty years, back when praying out loud could get you laughed off your own land. The miller's boy who'd been born with a withered arm. The widow Crane, whose daughter had died last winter of a fever no candle and no herb could touch.

"They're coming *here*," Bess whispered, and her voice cracked on the word *here*, and Tam understood. All those years of silence, all those years of being the place the divine forgot—and now the answer had finally come walking up the salt road wearing white.

Then the white banners parted, and the burning began.

It started with the records-house. Sarelle herself gave the order—a single lifted finger, almost gentle—and four soldiers broke from the column with torches already lit. Tam watched Maren Ostwick come running down the lane in his nightshirt, an old man suddenly young with terror, screaming words that made no sense: "The ledgers! The ledgers, you can't—" And then the doors were shouldered open and the torches went in, and within minutes smoke was rolling black and greasy from every window of the building where Hollowmere kept its births and deaths and marriages and debts, its whole remembered self.

"Why?" someone cried. "Why the records? What harm—"

Sarelle turned to the crowd. She was smaller than Tam expected, narrow as a reed, with a face like a saint carved by someone in a hurry. When she spoke, her voice was soft enough that everyone leaned in to hear it, which Tam would later understand was the entire trick.

"The past is a wall between you and your god," she said. "We have only come to help you climb over it."

Maren Ostwick lunged toward the burning door and two soldiers caught him by the arms, and when he struggled they did not strike him—they simply held him, patiently, while the records-house roared behind him like a living thing. Tam saw the moment the fight went out of the old archivist. It left him all at once, the way breath leaves a body, and what remained standing in the soldiers' grip was just a small grey man in a nightshirt, watching twenty years of paper curl into the sky.

"Take him," Sarelle said. "Gently. He will be our guest."

And then, as if the burning of a building were merely the opening of a conversation, she began to speak to the crowd about miracles.

* * *

Vesper heard the bells—or rather, heard where the bells weren't.

She was two miles up-valley at the Fenn farmstead, finishing the ugly business of a contract, when the sound reached her: not ringing but stopping, the chapel bell cut off mid-swing, and underneath it a smell on the wind that her body understood before her mind did. Smoke. Not hearth-smoke. Paper and pitch and something older, something that made the medallion against her breastbone go hot as a coal.

Doss found her at a trot, face grey. "They took the square," he said. "Took Maren. Burned the records-house to the foundation stones." He swallowed. "Ves. They put up a list."

"A list."

"Ten names a day," Doss said. "Starting tomorrow at dawn. Ten villagers, hanged in the square, every day, until the null-born walks into their camp and gives herself up." His jaw worked. "They don't know your name. They know you exist. The inquisitor wrote it all down after he left here—that relic business, the way magic won't touch you. Sarelle's had scouts in the valley for a week. They've been asking after a woman the fire doesn't want."

The snow around Vesper's boots had begun, very quietly, to melt.

She looked down at it—at the dark circle spreading beneath her, green showing through where grass remembered itself in midwinter—and made herself stand still inside it. Around her, the Fenns' yard lay white and untouched. Only here. Only ever here. Twenty-three years of hiding in plain sight, and it had taken the world three weeks to find her anyway.

"Doss," she said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "How many names are on the list?"

"Forty. First ten go up at dawn." He wouldn't look at her. "Bess Halloway. The widow Crane. Tam Wicker. The miller's boy." He recited them like a man reading casualty rolls, and maybe that was what they were. "They took hostages out of the square this afternoon. Twenty of them, chained in the tithe-barn under Choir guard. Sarelle says—" His voice caught and steadied. "Sarelle says the null-born has until dawn to decide how much Hollowmere's blood is worth."

The medallion burned. Inside her chest, deeper than bone, something else burned too—that banked ember-heat that had lived in her since before she had words for it, stirring now like a sleeper turning over. *They are coming for what burns in you,* Vael had told her, weeks ago, in a voice like frost forming. Well. They had come. And they had brought arithmetic.

Ten lives a day. Forty names. One of her.

"Get the crew together," Vesper said. "Everyone we trust. Everyone who'll still follow me knowing what I am." She was already walking, snow hissing to water in her wake, and she did not look back at the trail she was leaving. There was no point. From tonight on, anyone in Hollowmere could find her by following the bare ground.

"And Ves?" Doss said behind her. "What are we going to do?"

Vesper looked north, toward the square, toward the smoke still rising off the ruins of the only building in the valley that had ever told the truth about her.

"I don't know yet," she said. "But nobody hangs at dawn."

* * *

In the tithe-barn, chained to a post among nineteen neighbors, Maren Ostwick counted the guards and failed.

There were too many. There had been too many since the moment they marched him in here, and he had known even then—he had known, watching the ledgers burn, watching twenty years of careful erasure go up in honest flame—that there was no clever plan waiting in the wings of this story. He had spent a lifetime believing that knowledge was armor, that a secret well-kept was a wall well-built. Now he sat in the wreckage of both, listening to Bess Halloway pray out loud for the first time in twenty years, and understood that he had built his wall around a child instead of around the truth, and walls like that always, always fall inward.

"You knew her," Bess whispered beside him. Her wrists were raw where the chains bit. "The null-born. The one they want. You knew her, didn't you, Ostwick? Everyone says the foundling girl grew up under your roof."

Maren closed his eyes. Through the barn's warped boards he could see a sliver of evening sky going the color of a bruise, and somewhere beyond it, somewhere up-valley, a woman he had raised from infancy was deciding whether forty strangers were worth more than whatever burned inside her.

"I knew her," he said.

"Will she come?"

And here was the crucible of it, Maren thought. Here was the question he deserved to choke on. Because he knew Vesper Thackeray—knew the orphan who'd sworn herself to a dead god at fourteen out of sheer stubborn hunger to belong to something, knew the mercenary who took escort contracts at starvation rates whenever the client was a farmer, knew the girl who still, at twenty-three, left wildflowers on the grave of a dog she'd owned for one summer. Of course she would come. She would come the way water comes downhill, the way fire comes to a wick. That was never the question.

The question was whether he could live with what coming would cost her.

"She'll come," Maren said, and Bess nodded and went back to her praying, satisfied, and Maren bowed his head over his chained hands and added his own prayer to hers—the first he had dared in decades, sent up to a god who had fled this valley long ago and for very good reason.

*Vael,* he prayed, into the silence where Vael had once lived. *I kept your secret. I kept it so well I nearly buried her alive in it. If you can hear anything anymore—if any part of you stayed—*

The medallion-warmth he remembered from his priesthood did not come. But the chains, briefly, went cold against his wrists. Cold as frost. Cold as a held breath.

Or perhaps, Maren thought, opening his eyes, simply cold as iron in a barn in winter, and an old man's wishful blood.

He could no longer tell the difference. That, he suspected, was the truest punishment of all.

* * *

Sarelle worked by lamplight in the miller's front room, writing.

She wrote the morning's orders. She wrote the disposition of pickets on the valley rim. She wrote a careful account of the records-house fire for the Choir archive—*the site cleansed; the false history consumed; the ground prepared*—and then she sat for a long time with the pen dry above the page, listening to the house settle and the wind move down the valley, and admitted to herself what she had not written anywhere.

Cindral was getting louder.

Not in the way the scriptures promised—not the gentle suffusion of divine approval, the warm weight of a god's gaze. This was appetite. All day, as the hostages had been chained, as the list had been nailed to the chapel door, she had felt him leaning through her like heat through a stove-wall, vast and patient and *hungry*, and twice today she had caught herself thinking thoughts in a cadence that was not quite her own. *Burn the barn with them in it,* the thought had said, reasonable as a ledger entry. *Why wait for dawn. Fire does not keep schedules.*

She had driven the thought out with liturgy. It had come back before compline.

Brother Callum knocked and entered, and stood with his ruined hand cradled against his chest—the hand that had gripped the null-born's, whose relic had cracked like an eggshell in his fist. He had not slept properly since. Sarelle found that interesting. Faith, untested, was a lamp behind glass; faith tested and cracked was something else entirely, and she had not yet decided what.

"The scouts report movement up-valley," Callum said. "The mercenary company. Armed, but holding position at the Fenn farmstead. They're not running."

"No," Sarelle said. "She won't run." She set down the pen. "That is what makes her dangerous, Callum. Fanatics run or they charge. She will do neither. She will *think*, and then she will do the thing we have not imagined, and I confess I am looking forward to it."

Callum stared at her. "Looking forward—"

"To meeting her. Yes." Sarelle rose and went to the window. Below, in the square, the gallows frame stood black against the snow—erected in an afternoon by carpenters who had wept while they worked, and she had let them weep, because tears were honest and honesty softened crowds better than terror did. Ten ropes hung from it, coiled and waiting, pale as garlands.

"You think I enjoy this," she said quietly, to the glass, to the reflection of her own narrow face. "I have read the histories, brother. I know what happens when divinity returns to a world that has learned to live without it. War. Every time. God against god, faith against faith, and the faithful burn as fuel. Unless—" she turned, and the lamplight made her eyes look hollowed, "—unless one flame consumes all the others first, quickly, cleanly, and ends the argument forever. Cindral devouring the Ember is not cruelty, Callum. It is *mercy*. It is the only mercy large enough to hold this world."

"And the ten at dawn?"

"Investment," Sarelle said, and heard, distantly, how easily the word came, and chose not to examine that. "Go and sleep. Dawn comes early."

When he was gone she knelt at the bedside with her hands folded, and reached for her god, and felt him reach back—a heat like a furnace door opening—and for one vertiginous instant Sarelle could not tell whether she was kneeling before the fire or being fed into it.

She finished her prayers anyway. She had always been very good at not examining things.

* * *

Up-valley, in the cold dark of the Fenn barn, the company of the Broken Oath sat in a ring of lamplight and argued in whispers, and Vesper stood apart with her back to the wall and listened to men she loved decide what she was worth.

"—forty hostages, Doss. Forty. Against thirty of us and whatever she's got cooking in her chest." Rennick, arms crossed, jaw set. "I signed on to guard caravans. I didn't sign on to fight a church."

"You signed on to Vesper," Doss said flatly. "Same thing, near enough, these last six years."

"That's sentiment, not strategy—"

"It's the only strategy this company has ever had, and it's why we're still alive, so sit down."

Vesper let it go on. She had learned, in the worst years, that a crew needed to argue its way into a fight or the fight would break it later. But under the argument she could feel the other thing—the thing that had changed since the melting snow, since the cracked relic, since the list on the chapel door. They kept glancing at her when they thought she wasn't looking. Not with fear, most of them. With something worse. With hope.

They wanted her to be the answer. And she had spent her whole life learning that she was, in fact, the opposite of an answer—she was the question the gods had fled.

Outside, the wind shifted, and carried up the valley the faintest thread of woodsmoke and hymn-singing from the occupied square below, and Vesper pressed her hand flat against the medallion at her breastbone and felt it pulse against her palm like a second heart.

*Dawn,* she thought. *Ten ropes. Forty names.*

She pushed off the wall and stepped into the lamplight, and the arguing stopped.

"Rennick's right," she said, and the surprise in the room was total. "Thirty of us can't take that camp. So we're not going to take the camp." She looked around the ring of faces—Doss, Rennick, little Wren with her crossbow, old Harl who'd taught her to throw a knife, all of them watching her with that terrible hope. "Tomorrow at first light, ten people die unless I walk down that hill. So here's what happens instead. Wren, Harl—you're the best quiet pair we've got. The tithe-barn's rear wall faces the creek. Tonight, while they're singing their hymns, you cut those hostages loose and take them up the shepherd stairs. Nobody dies at dawn, because there's nobody left to hang."

"And you?" Doss said quietly.

Vesper looked at the door, at the darkness beyond it, at the valley where she'd been born twice—once in blood and fire, once in Maren Ostwick's careful lie.

"Me," she said, "I'm going to go have a word with my god."
