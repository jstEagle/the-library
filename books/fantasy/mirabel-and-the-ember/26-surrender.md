## 26. Surrender

The snow had stopped falling by dawn, as if even the sky were holding its breath.

Vesper stood at the edge of the tree line above Hollowmere and looked down at what had been done to her home. The Choir's camp filled the valley floor like a wound that would not close—rows of white tents stitched together with ash-grey banners, braziers burning a fire that gave no warmth, only light. The reliquary wagons sat in a ring around the old market square, and from this distance she could see the wooden platform they had raised there, and the ten figures kneeling on it in a line, roped at the wrists, their breath rising in small white flags of surrender.

Ten villagers. The first day's tithe.

She counted them twice, because she could not make herself believe it once. Old Tam Wicker, who had taught her to gut a fish before she could write her name. The miller's daughter, Pell, sixteen years old and still growing into her hands. The cooper's twins, who had never done anything in their lives more dangerous than steal apples. They knelt in the snow with their heads bowed, and beside each of them stood an inquisitor in ash-white robes, and behind each inquisitor stood the thing the Choir called a censor—a hooded shape carrying a censer that swung without wind, trailing smoke the color of burnt bone.

"They'll do it," Doss said beside her. He said it flatly, the way he said everything now, as though flatness were armor. "Sarelle doesn't bluff. I watched her burn a village in the Marches for harboring one relic-thief. She read the charges aloud first. Gave them time to weep." His jaw worked. "She'll give these ones time too."

"I know."

"Then you know there has to be another way. We go in tonight, quiet, through the drainage culverts under the tannery—"

"The culverts are warded. I felt it from here." She turned her hand over and looked at her palm, where the skin still prickled with the wrongness of consecrated ground. Everything the Choir touched now itched against her like wool on a burn. "They knew we'd come back for Maren. Sarelle planned for us. She's been planning since the moment her relic cracked in that inquisitor's hand."

Doss was silent a long moment. Below them, a horn sounded—one long note, thin and clear, the kind of note meant to carry into houses, into cellars, into the ears of anyone hiding. Then a voice, amplified by some artifice of the Choir's making, rolled up the valley walls:

*"The null-born has three days. At dusk each day, ten will answer for her silence. Bring her bound and living, and the valley will be spared. This is the mercy of Cindral. This is the only mercy there is."*

The voice faded. The smoke kept rising from the censers, straight up, unbothered by any wind, as though the air itself had been told to stand still.

"She knows you're already here," Doss said quietly. "That message wasn't for the valley. It was for you."

"Yes," Vesper said. "It was."

* * *

She made her decision the way she did most things: quickly, and then slowly, all at once—the blade drawn in a heartbeat, the bleeding taking days.

She did not tell the crew all of it. She told Doss, alone, in the ruined shepherd's hut where they'd sheltered, while the others slept in shifts around a fire no larger than two cupped hands. She told him she was going down at dusk. She told him he was not to follow. And when he opened his mouth to argue, she said the thing she had been saving, the thing she had carried since the pass, since Isolde's winter had taken Brennick mid-stride and left him standing frozen like a monument to their failure:

"Doss. If you come after me, everything I do today means nothing. Do you understand? The only reason Sarelle will honor a bargain is if she believes there is no one left to bargain *for*. If my people are in those trees, she'll take me anyway—and take the hostages too, and take you, and use every one of you to carve me open slower." She held his eyes. His were wet, and he hated that they were, and she loved him for hating it. "I need you to let me be alone. That's the last order I'll ever give you. Let me be alone."

"That's not an order," he said hoarsely. "Orders are things you can survive disobeying."

"Then it's a request. From Vesper Thackeray, hired sword, to Sergeant Doss of the Broken Oath." She almost smiled. It hurt her face to try. "You took my coin for eleven years. Take this instead."

He looked at her for a long time. Outside, the light was beginning to fail, going blue and then grey and then the color of ash, which seemed fitting. Somewhere below, the Choir's horns were sounding again—not the summons this time but something else, something rhythmic and low, a procession-song, the sound of a faith warming up its instruments for sacrifice.

"If there's anything left of you in there," Doss said finally, "any piece they don't take—" His voice broke and he rebuilt it, brick by brick, the way he'd rebuilt the company after every bad contract, every dead friend. "—you send it back to us. However you can. A dream. A bird. A feeling. We'll know. We'll *know*, Vesper."

"There won't be anything left," she said gently. "But I'll send it anyway."

* * *

She walked down into the valley as the sun went red behind the western ridge, and the snow crunched under her boots with a sound like someone slowly grinding their teeth.

She had dressed for it. That was the strange part, the part she would remember afterward, if there was an afterward: how much care she had taken. Clean shirt. Hair braided back tight, the way Maren used to braid it when she was small and feverish, so that nothing would pull. Her medallion—the blank disc of dull metal she had worn since she was fourteen, the medallion of a god who had gone silent—she had polished until it caught the last of the light. Not because it mattered. Because it was hers, and whatever they took from her today, they would not take the fact that she had chosen how to arrive.

Her hands she bound herself, in front, with soft cord, so that the ropes would not cut. There was a dignity in small preparations. It was the dignity of the condemned, and she wore it like her father's—like *Maren's*—old coat, too big and warm anyway.

The pilgrims came out of the houses to watch her pass. That was the worst of it. Not the inquisitors, not the censers, not the white tents—but her own neighbors, standing in their doorways with their faces half-hidden in scarves, watching the monster walk out of the woods to save them. Some of them had tied ash-grey ribbons to their doors overnight. Some had painted the Choir's spiral sigil in lye on their shutters. She could not blame them. Fear makes converts faster than faith ever did; fear had always been the Choir's best missionary.

An old woman near the well did not hide her face. Widow Sorrel, who had once paid Vesper a copper to scare crows from her garden and had paid her a kiss on the forehead besides. Her eyes followed Vesper down the lane, and her lips moved—not in prayer, Vesper thought, or not exactly. In apology.

Vesper nodded to her, once, the way you nod to someone across a crowded room when there is no time to say goodbye.

At the edge of the market square, the inquisitors were waiting. They did not take her arms. One of them simply looked at her bound hands, looked at her face, and said, in a voice trained to be gentle the way a scalpel is gentle: "State your name for the record."

"You know my name."

"For the record," he repeated.

She breathed out, and the breath hung white between them, and inside her chest—deep, deep down, beneath the fear and the grief and the terrible calm—something stirred. It had been stirring for weeks now, that buried heat, that banked coal. Vael's presence, or the Ember's, or both, tangled past separating. It pressed against the inside of her ribs like a child pressing against a door.

"Vesper Thackeray," she said. "Born Mirabel. Foundling of Hollowmere. Sworn of the Broken Oath." She lifted her chin. "And the thing you want is not for sale, so let's not pretend this is commerce."

The inquisitor's mouth twitched—almost respect, or its imitation. "Prelate Sarelle will see you now."

* * *

Sarelle had made her court in the temple of Vael.

Of course she had. Of course she had chosen that building—the stone church where Vesper had sworn her oath at fourteen, where the pews still bore the knife-scratches of two hundred years of restless children, where the altar stood bare because the god it served had gone quiet. The Choir had hung its banners from the rafters and set its censers in the corners, and the old sanctuary smelled of incense laid over the ghost-smell of candlewax, and it was the violation of it, more than anything, that made Vesper's hands shake inside their cords.

Sarelle stood at the altar rail, reading. She was smaller than Vesper remembered—smaller than her voice, certainly, which filled valleys. Grey hair cropped close. Robes of undyed wool, plain as a farmer's, because the Choir wore poverty like a uniform and called it honesty. Only her eyes were rich: pale, patient, absolutely certain, the eyes of a woman who had never once doubted anything and called that condition grace.

"You came bound," Sarelle said, without looking up. "Interesting. Most who come to us come arguing. You've saved us both a great deal of noise." She closed the book. "Do you know what this is?" She held it up. It was a ledger, old, its spine cracked. Vesper's stomach dropped before she understood why—and then she saw the burned-out entry, the scorch-shadow of a name removed, and she knew. Maren's ledger. Pulled from the records-house before the fires.

"You burned the records-house," Vesper said.

"We burned a lie," Sarelle corrected mildly. "Ledgers are just lies with dates attached. But this page interested me. An infant, entered into the parish rolls with no origin, no family, no cause. And the priest who wrote the entry later tried to erase it—which tells me he was hiding something worth twenty-three years of silence." She set the ledger down and finally looked at Vesper fully, and her gaze went through Vesper like a lance through water. "Null-born. That's what my inquisitors call you. Magic dies at your skin. Relics crack in your grip. Gods cannot look at you." She smiled faintly. "I have a different word. I call you the *vessel*. Because that is what you are, child. You are a cup, and something very old has been poured into you, and cups are not people. Cups do not get bargains."

"The hostages," Vesper said. "First. Before anything else. Ten lives for one. That was your own proclamation. Honor it in front of your whole choir, or I turn around and walk out, and you spend the rest of the winter chasing a rumor through snowdrifts."

Something flickered in Sarelle's eyes—irritation, quickly filed away. "You are in no position to dictate terms."

"No," Vesper agreed. "I'm in the only position that matters. I'm the one holding the thing you want. Positions don't get more powerful than that—they just get less comfortable." She heard her own voice and marveled at it: steady, dry, almost bored. Eleven years of mercenary work, teaching her to negotiate with bandits and barons alike, and it had all been practice for this. "Free them. All ten. Unharmed, fed, walked to their doors. Then I kneel wherever you like."

For a long moment the only sound was the censers, breathing their bone-colored smoke.

Then Sarelle turned to the inquisitor at her shoulder. "Release them. Publicly. Let the valley see that Cindral keeps his word." And then, softer, to Vesper, with something that might have been genuine curiosity: "You would have made a fine prelate, vessel. You understand that faith is mostly bookkeeping."

"It isn't," Vesper said. "That's the difference between us. I've met the real thing. It doesn't keep books. It just burns."

* * *

They freed the hostages at dusk, in front of everyone, as promised. Tam Wicker wept into his rope-burned wrists. Pell ran to her mother and nearly knocked the old woman into the snow. The cooper's twins clung to each other and looked back at Vesper across the square with an expression she would carry into whatever came next: not gratitude, exactly. Horror, wearing gratitude's clothes. They had seen what she was worth. Ten of them for one of her. No one could look at that arithmetic and feel clean.

Vesper stood in the falling dark and watched them go, and felt the strangest peace settle over her, like snowfall.

No rescue was coming. She had made sure of it—had spent Doss's love like coin, had spent her own pride, had spent the company itself, buying this one clean transaction. Whatever happened next happened to her alone, and there was a terrible freedom in that. Twenty-three years of being a hidden thing, a guarded thing, a secret wearing a girl's body. Tonight the secret ended. Tonight the cup would be emptied, one way or another, and whatever was left afterward would at least be honest.

Inside her chest, the Ember burned low and steady, and she thought she felt—she could not be sure—something like a hand laid over hers. Vael, perhaps. Or just her own heart, trying to comfort itself.

*I'm sorry,* she told it, whichever of them was listening. *I couldn't find a third way.*

*There is always a third way,* said a voice like frost forming, like embers breathing, like a god remembering how to speak. *But you were never given time to look for it. That was their crime, little oath-keeper. Not yours.*

"Don't," she whispered, barely moving her lips. "Don't watch this. Please. Go somewhere far away and don't—"

*I stayed when every other god fled,* the voice said, and it was gentle, and it was grieving. *I will stay for this too.*

They led her into the temple. They laid her on the cold stone before the desecrated altar, and the inquisitors began to draw the ritual circle around her in lines of ash and salt and something darker that smoked where it touched the floor. Sarelle stood at her head, sleeves pushed back, hands bare, murmuring the opening words of the extraction rite in a language older than any nation—a language, Vesper realized with a distant, floating horror, that the Ember itself seemed to recognize, because it flared inside her at every syllable like a memory being dragged into the light.

The pain, when it began, was not like pain. It was like being *opened*.

Vesper screamed, and the sound echoed off the stones where she had prayed as a child, and above her Sarelle's voice rose, calm and triumphant, reciting the words that would unmake her—

—and far away, at the edge of the valley, in the dark beyond the tree line, something answered.
