## 27. The Extraction Rite

They did not bind her to the altar.

That was the first cruelty Vesper noticed, and she would remember it longest. There were iron rings set into the stone, old ones, worn smooth by centuries of struggling wrists, and Sarelle's acolytes looked at them, and then at Vesper's hands—still raw from the rope they'd cut away at the camp's edge—and chose not to use them. It was a message. She had come freely. She would stay freely. The Choir wanted no coercion in this, no stain of force on the miracle they intended to perform. They wanted her to lie down on the cold basalt slab with her arms at her sides like a woman taking her own death as medicine, and they wanted her to understand that she could have walked away at any moment, and that walking away would have cost ten lives a day until she didn't.

So Vesper lay down.

The ritual space was a hollowed granary at the heart of the Choir's camp, its grain long since burned or stolen, its walls whitewashed over the old soot-stains of some previous century's fire. Someone had hung it with ash-grey banners stitched with Cindral's sigil—a flame inverted, burning downward, devouring rather than giving light—and someone else had laid a circle of white sand on the floor around the slab, fine as flour, raked into patterns that hurt Vesper's eyes when she tried to follow them. The patterns moved. Not visibly, not while she watched directly, but every time her gaze slid elsewhere she felt them rearranging themselves behind her, patient as insects.

Candles ringed the space. Hundreds of them, all lit, none flickering. Their flames stood perfectly still, like a congregation holding its breath.

Sarelle came to stand over her, and for a moment the Prelate simply looked down, and Vesper looked back, and neither of them spoke. Up close, without the distance of a parley ground between them, Sarelle was smaller than Vesper had imagined—all that cold authority folded into a narrow frame, a face like a knife that had been used so often it had gone thin. Her eyes were the color of banked coals, and they were not cruel. That was the worst thing about them. They were the eyes of a woman who believed, completely, that what she was about to do was mercy.

"You are frightened," Sarelle said.

"No."

"A lie." The Prelate's voice was soft, almost gentle. "Good. Fear is honest. Fear is the soul telling the truth about itself. We will need your honesty before this is done—the Ember answers truth, not flesh. Steel cannot carve it out. Only confession can open you wide enough."

Vesper stared at the ceiling, where the whitewash had begun to blister over some older stain. "You said ten a day. I've paid. Let the villagers go."

"They will be released as the rite proceeds. Each stage completed buys their freedom. You have my word on Cindral's name."

"Your god ate his own priests," Vesper said. "I heard what happened at the reliquary. I heard what your vial of my blood showed you."

Something flickered across Sarelle's face—there and gone, quick as a fish under ice. "I know what he is," she said quietly. "I have known since the night the blood spoke to me. And I serve him anyway, because the alternative is a world where divinity dies in a valley and no one ever again hears an answer when they pray. You think you are offering the world peace, null-born. I think you are offering it silence. I have stood in the silence. Twenty years of it. I will burn the whole world before I go back."

She turned away before Vesper could answer, which was perhaps wise, because Vesper had no answer—or too many—and lifted her hands.

"Begin," Sarelle said.

* * *

The acolytes sang.

It was not a hymn. Hymns rise; this descended, note by note, a slow stairway of sound going down and down into registers Vesper felt in her teeth, in the roots of her hair, in the wet dark of her lungs. Twelve voices, hooded, standing at the compass points of the sand circle, and as they sang the candle flames began—at last—to move, all of them leaning inward toward the slab as if drawn by a wind that blew from everywhere at once.

The first touch of the rite was cold.

Not the cold of the stone beneath her back, though that was there too, seeping through her shirt. This was a cold *inside*, a precise and surgical cold that began at her sternum and spread outward along lines she had never known her body contained—lines that were not veins, not nerves, but something older, something laid down before her flesh was flesh. She felt them the way a riverbed feels water: as shape, as history, as the memory of flow.

And along those lines, moving toward her center from every direction at once, came the sensation of being *opened*.

There was no blade. She had expected a blade. But the Choir's craft worked from within, and what it did was find the seams of her—not the body's seams but the soul's, the places where Maren Ostwick had sewn a hiding place into an infant twenty-three years ago—and unpick them, stitch by stitch, with a patience that was worse than violence. Each seam that parted rang through her like a plucked wire. Her back arched off the slab. Somewhere far away an acolyte's voice wavered and steadied. The cold poured in through the gaps, and where it poured, things were revealed.

She saw herself from underneath.

That was the only way she could describe it afterward, and she never managed to describe it well. She saw the architecture of Vesper Thackeray laid open like a dissected flower: the oath to Vael at fourteen sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone; the twenty years of silence layered over it in strata, grief compressed into pragmatism, pragmatism compressed into habit; Doss's gruff loyalty wrapped around her ribs like a bandage; the medallion, blank and warm, hanging at her throat even now, humming against the skin above her sternum. All of it visible. All of it legible to the singing, patient dark.

And at the center, deeper than any of it, wrapped in the oldest and tightest seams of all—

*the Ember.*

It woke as the first seam parted. Not gradually. All at once, like a sleeper struck, like a hearth kicked open, and the cold of the rite met the heat of it and the two screamed against each other inside her ribcage, and Vesper screamed too, because there was nowhere else for the sound to go.

It did not feel like pain. Pain was what the body did. This was what the *soul* did when it was pried apart—it was agony in a direction she hadn't known existed, a north and south of suffering she had never had coordinates for, and she understood in one white instant why the rings in the stone were worn smooth, why some other captive centuries ago had needed binding after all. She clawed at the slab. Her nails left no mark. The sand circle held her the way a bowl holds water.

"Confess," Sarelle's voice said, from very far away, from very close. "The Ember seals itself in lies. Open your mouth and let the truth out with the blood."

"I don't—" Vesper gasped. "I don't know what—"

"You do. You have always known. Say what you are."

The cold found another seam. The wire-snap of it went through her jaw, her spine, her knees. And the Ember flared in answer, and in the flare—

* * *

—in the flare, she fell.

Not out of her body. Into it. Down through it, down past the layers the rite had opened, down the shaft the acolytes' song had bored through the center of her, and the Ember rose to meet her falling the way fire rises to meet a dropped torch, and they met somewhere that was not a place, and the meeting was memory.

Heat first. Always heat. A temple, but not like any temple she had seen—vast beyond sense, its pillars grown rather than built, living wood turned to living stone, and every surface alive with flame that gave light but consumed nothing. The First Hearth. She knew its name the way she knew her own heartbeat, because it *was* her heartbeat, had been since before her birth. Fire moved through the great hall in slow rivers, and the fire was not orange or gold but every color that has ever meant warmth: the brown of bread, the red of blood in sunlight, the amber of honey, the pink of a fever breaking at dawn.

And in the middle of it, kneeling at the base of the oldest pillar, a woman.

Her mother.

Vesper had never seen her mother's face. She had prepared herself, in the dark hours of twenty-three years, for never seeing it—for the possibility that the Ember held nothing, that Maren's secrets were all there were. But here the face was, turned up toward the pillar, lit from below, and it was *her own face*. Older. Softer at the jaw. Scarred across one eyebrow in a way Vesper's was not. But hers, unmistakably, the way a stream is unmistakably the river's.

Her mother was praying. No—she was doing something more than praying. She was *holding* the fire. Both hands plunged into the slow river of flame at the pillar's base, and her expression was not ecstasy but effort, the grim straining effort of a woman holding shut a door against a flood, and Vesper understood, with the terrible clarity of memory that is not one's own, what she was looking at.

Her mother was a vessel too. The first vessel. The keeper of the last spark of the fire that had made the gods—the fire the gods had killed their maker to steal, the fire they had fed on for an age until it guttered low and they fled the places where it slept. Her mother had carried it. Had hidden it in the one place no god could reach: inside a child not yet born. Inside the valley where magic died. Inside *her*.

And now the memory tilted, and the temple was burning.

Real fire this time, hungry fire, fire that consumed—the theft made manifest, divine hands tearing at the pillars, gods in their glory ripping the First Hearth apart to take what remained, and her mother running through the collapsing hall with a newborn pressed to her chest, and the newborn was screaming, and the fire was screaming, and her mother looked back once at the ruin of everything holy and her face was not afraid.

It was *furious*.

That was the thing Vesper carried out of the memory like a coal cupped in bare hands: not fear, not sorrow, but fury. Her mother had run not to save herself, nor even only the child, but to save the fire *from them*—from the thieves, from the eaters, from the beautiful radiant powers who had murdered their own source and called it destiny. She had smuggled divinity out of heaven the way a slave smuggles letters, and she had hidden it in the safest place in the world, which was inside a person, because a person can be loved and a treasure can only be taken.

The last thing the memory showed her was her mother's mouth, close, so close, whispering against the infant's ear words that Vesper felt more than heard, words that sank into the marrow of her like rain into drought:

*A wonder to behold. My Mirabel. Grow up ordinary. Grow up free.*

Then the vision closed like a hand, and Vesper was on the slab again, and she was weeping, and the rite was only half done.

* * *

She came back to the granary in stages: the song first, still descending, thinner now as if the singers tired; then the cold, retreating sullenly from a center that blazed unharmed; then Sarelle's face above her, closer than before, intent, hungry, *afraid*.

"It showed you something," the Prelate said. "What did it show you?"

Vesper laughed. It came out broken, half sob, and she didn't care. "You want to know what your god is?" she whispered. "He's a thief. They all are. Every god that ever answered a prayer—they stole the fire they're made of, and they killed the one who lit it, and your Cindral was first through the door." She dragged breath into her ruined chest. "You're not serving a god, Prelate. You're serving a burglar with a cathedral."

"Blasphemy," an acolyte breathed.

"The Ember doesn't lie," Vesper said, and the candles flared as one, every flame in the granary standing suddenly tall, and twelve voices faltered in the song. "It *remembers*. And it just remembered me."

Sarelle's hand shot out and gripped Vesper's jaw, forcing her face up. The Prelate's coal-dark eyes were wide, and in them Vesper saw the exact moment faith cracked—not broke, not yet, but cracked, a fault line running through certainty the way lightning runs through a wall of ice. "Hold the circle," Sarelle said, and her voice did not shake, which must have cost her everything. "The rite continues. Deeper seams. We are close—I can see it burning through her skin—"

She was right. Vesper could feel it too: the Ember rising in her chest like a second heart, pushing light out through the seams the rite had opened, thin gold lines glowing along her collarbones, her throat, the backs of her hands, as if she were a lamp beginning to come apart at its joins. The cold poured in harder. The song climbed—or descended—toward its final movement, and the sand circle began to turn, slowly, grains lifting and drifting in a pattern that spelled something in a language older than prayer.

And deep beneath the pain, beneath the memory, beneath the fury and the grief and the name her mother had given her—Mirabel, a wonder to behold—Vesper felt something else stir at the edge of the valley.

Something vast. Something that had stayed when everything else fled. Something ashamed, and faithful, and *coming*.

The blank medallion at her throat went hot as a forge-stone.

Far to the south, beyond the camp's edge, beyond the snowline, every candle in Hollowmere—every candle in the whole silent valley—flared upright at once.

And Vael's voice, when it came, did not speak to her ears at all. It spoke to the Ember, and the Ember translated, and the translation shook the granary to its rafters:

*Let her go. Or I will remind you what fire was, before you stole it.*
