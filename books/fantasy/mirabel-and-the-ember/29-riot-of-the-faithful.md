## 29. Riot of the Faithful

The world came apart in the space between one heartbeat and the next.

Vesper knew it first as sound—a tearing, a groaning, like a glacier calving into the sea—and then as light, white and total, pouring out of her own chest through the half-carved wound in her soul. The ritual circle beneath her was a wheel of ash and salt and Choir-script, and it was *breaking*, its lines guttering like wicks in wind. Sarelle's acolytes staggered back from their stations, hands pressed to their ears, their mouths moving around words that no longer held.

She could not stand. Her wrists were still bound to the iron frame at the circle's heart, and every breath dragged fire through her ribs. But she could see. Through the cathedral of smoke above the camp—through the torn roof of the granary the Choir had consecrated for the rite—she saw the sky over Hollowmere turning the color of a bruise, and she heard, rising from beyond the palisade, a sound she had never heard in twenty years of soldiering.

It was not a battle cry. It was a congregation.

*They're coming,* said something inside her that might have been Vael and might have been the Ember and might have been only her own blood, singing. *They are coming for you.*

The last thing she saw before the light took her under was Prelate Sarelle's face—upturned, ash-smeared, no longer cold with ambition but slick with something Vesper had never expected to see there.

Fear.

* * *

Doss had fought in eleven sieges, and he had learned that every one of them began the same way: with a door that should have been guarded and wasn't.

The Choir's camp sprawled across the commons of Hollowmere village like a wound on white cloth. Tents of grey silk, braziers burning ash-scented coals, penitents kneeling in rows in the snow until their knees froze to the ground. Doss had watched it grow for six days from the tree line above the mill, counting sentries, counting hours, counting the ten villagers who were marched out each dawn to stand against the wall while a Choir crier read the terms of surrender in a voice like poured honey. Six days. Sixty lives hanging by a thread of pride.

On the seventh night, the sky split open over the granary, and every sentry on the palisade turned to look.

"Brann," Doss said quietly, "the east gate."

His crew had been waiting three days for exactly this—for the enemy's eyes to be dragged upward by something too large to ignore. They came down through the orchard in the dark, twelve of them, no armor that would ring, blades wrapped in wool until the moment of use. Brann went over the east gate with a grapnel and a silence learned in worse wars than this one. The two sentries there never made a sound; Tamra caught one and Doss the other, and they laid them down in the snow almost gently, the way you'd put down something sleeping.

Inside, the camp was chaos already. The rite had failed—or half-failed; Doss didn't pretend to understand what Vesper was or what the Choir had tried to carve out of her, he only understood that the light coming off the granary was wrong, holy-wrong, the kind of light that made his old knee ache and his dead wife's name rise unbidden to his lips. Acolytes ran in every direction. Some knelt where they stood and prayed. One tore off his grey robe and simply walked away into the dark, and nobody stopped him.

"The pens," Doss said. "Hostages first. Everything else is noise."

The villagers were held in the sheep pens behind the tithe-barn, forty-odd of them, hollow-eyed, frostbitten, waiting for their mornings against the wall. The guard there was thin—half the Choir's strength had been pulled to the rite, and the other half was now staring at the sky. Doss put an axe through the padlock rather than waste time on subtlety, and the sound of it—the *clang* of iron breaking—did something strange to the crowd inside. It did not make them flee.

It made them turn toward him.

An old man Doss recognized—the cooper, Aldous, who had once repaired a wagon axle for the company without charging them because Vesper had carried his sick daughter twenty miles through a storm—gripped the bars with both hands.

"Is she alive?" he asked. "The girl. Is Vesper alive?"

"She's in that building," Doss said, jerking his chin at the burning light. "And we're going to get her out. Now move."

But they didn't move—not away. The pen gate swung open and the villagers poured *out*, all of them, and then they kept going, past Doss, past his crew with their wrapped blades, toward the granary and the light and the Choir. Aldous picked up a broken tent-pole as he went. A woman whose name Doss didn't know pulled a burning brand from a brazier. Behind them, more shapes were emerging from the tents where pilgrims had been penned since the occupation began—hundreds of them, the ones who had come to Hollowmere seeking miracles and found instead a cordon and a crier and ten executions a day.

Doss had seen riots before. He had even started a few. But he had never seen one born whole in a single breath, the way a fire is born when a spark finally finds the dry place.

"To Vesper!" someone shouted, and the word went down the crowd like current down a wire, changing shape as it traveled—to the null-born, to the vessel, to the girl in the valley, to *Mirabel*, though Doss didn't know yet why that name made people weep when they said it—and then the whole mass of them was moving, and the Choir's camp broke like ice under a spring flood.

* * *

Sister Cerys of the Ashen Choir had entered the order at nine years old, and in nineteen years she had never once doubted.

She doubted now.

She had been stationed at the western brazier when the sky tore open, and she had felt the failure of the rite in her own body—a snapping, deep in the sternum, as if a string inside her had been cut. Around her, the faithful were crying out that this was the birth-pang, that Cindral was coming, that the pain was purification. She had opened her mouth to say the same.

Then the hostages' pen broke open, and the villagers came out not fleeing but *advancing*, and among them she saw the face of the cooper's daughter—the sick girl, the one the mercenary woman had carried through the storm, the one whose recovery the Choir had called a false miracle, a devil's trick. The girl was maybe sixteen now. She was holding her father's hand. And she was looking at Cerys—at Cerys specifically, at the grey robe and the ash-marked brow—with no hatred at all, which was somehow infinitely worse.

"You burned the records-house," the girl said. Not accusing. Just stating it, the way you'd state that snow was falling. "Maren Ostwick wrote down everything that ever happened in this valley so nobody would be forgotten. And you burned it."

"We burned lies," Cerys heard herself say, in the trained cadence of the catechism, and heard how hollow it rang in her own ears. "The prelate will explain—"

"Where is your explanation?" the girl asked. "Where is your prelate?"

And Cerys turned, along with everyone else, toward the granary, and saw Prelate Sarelle emerge from the ruined doorway with blood on her sleeves and a vial clutched in one fist, running—not marching, not withdrawing with dignity, *running*—toward the horse lines, and understood in one long, cold instant that everything the Choir had taught her about certainty was a costume, and the person wearing it had just fled the stage.

The brand in the sick girl's hand was still burning. Cerys looked at it. She looked at her own ash-marked hands.

She stepped aside, and let the crowd pass.

* * *

The bindings were Choir-work: cold iron and consecrated rope, designed to hold a body that might otherwise burn like a struck match. Vesper had felt them as anchors, and now, as the rite collapsed, she felt them become shackles on a drowning woman—because the thing inside her was no longer quiet, was no longer a banked coal, was *rising*, and there was nothing left to contain it and nothing left to guide it, and she was tied to a frame of iron in the middle of it like a lightning rod.

*Let go,* said the voice that was Vael, or wore Vael's voice. *You are fighting the tide with your hands.*

"If I let go," she gasped, "I don't know what's left when the water goes down."

*That,* said Vael, and there was something in the god's voice she had never heard before—something small and ashamed and terribly gentle—*is the question I should have answered for you twenty years ago.*

The first binding snapped. Not cut—*unmade*, the iron going to rust-red flakes, and Vesper remembered the reliquary locks crumbling under her fingers, Maren's weight on her shoulder, Brann laughing in the dark. Her right arm came free.

The second binding held, and through the smoke she saw why: an acolyte, young, terrified, both hands on the rope, chanting the holding-liturgy with tears streaming down his face. He wasn't looking at her. He was looking at the crowd pouring through the camp below, at the fires starting in the tent rows, at the world his faith had built coming apart around him, and he was holding the rope the way a man holds a rail in a shipwreck.

"I'm sorry," he said to her. "I'm sorry, I'm sorry, the prelate said—if we finish it, if we give Cindral his fire, everything will be *mended*, everything—"

"It isn't mending," Vesper said. Her voice came out rougher than she meant it to, scraped raw by the rite. "Look at me. Look at my arms. Does this look like mending?"

He looked. At the half-carved wound the rite had opened along her sternum, still glowing faintly, still bleeding light instead of blood. His chant faltered.

The third thing that happened, happened all at once: the granary wall burst inward, and Doss came through the wreckage with an axe in one hand and Tamra at his shoulder and the whole roaring tide of Hollowmere at their backs, and the acolyte dropped the rope and ran, and Vesper's arm came free, and she slid down the broken frame into Brann's waiting grip, and the last binding fell away in flakes of rust.

"Got you," Brann said, absurdly calm, as if catching her out of a river instead of a collapsing miracle. "You're heavy for a god."

"I'm not a god," Vesper managed.

"No," said Doss, cutting the air with his axe toward the crowd, toward the fires, toward the sky still bruised and seething overhead. "You're something worse. You're *ours*. Can you walk?"

She could. Barely. She got her feet under her, and the crowd saw her—saw the null-born, the vessel, the girl from the valley standing upright in the ruins of the thing they'd tried to carve her open for—and the roar that went up was not a cheer. It was bigger than a cheer. It was the sound of a valley remembering it had a voice.

And underneath the roar, faint but unmistakable, Vesper felt a pull. Southward. Faint and fading, like a scent on wind.

Blood. Her blood. Going somewhere fast.

"Sarelle," she said.

"Gone," Tamra confirmed, jogging up with a stolen horse's reins in her fist. "South road, hard ride, minutes ahead. She's got something of yours in a vial—I saw it glint."

Vesper closed her eyes. She could feel it even now, that thread of herself being carried away into the dark—a compass needle pointing at her own heart, in the fist of a woman who served a god that ate its believers. Half-finished, the rite had left the Ember unstable; she could feel it in her chest like a cracked bell, ringing wrong. Whatever Sarelle meant to do with that vial, whatever Cindral meant to do, the crack would only widen.

"Doss," she said. "How many horses?"

"Enough."

"Then we ride tonight." She looked south, at the snow and the dark and the distant smudge of the pass, and felt the Ember shudder in its broken housing, and felt, far to the north, something else stirring—an old cold, patient and vast, turning its attention toward the valley like a head turning in sleep. Two hunters now. Maybe three.

Behind her, the riot burned on, and the freed hostages sang, and Sister Cerys of the Ashen Choir stood alone by a dead brazier with ash on her hands, watching the mercenaries ride out and wondering, for the first time in nineteen years, what it was she actually believed.

The snow began again, soft and indifferent, covering everything.

Southward, the thread of blood pulled tight as a bowstring, and Vesper rode after her own heart into the dark.
