## 30. What Sarelle Carried Out

The vial was warm.

That was the first thing Sarelle noticed, and the last thing she would ever be permitted to forget: the vial of blood she had torn from a half-carved girl in a burning camp did not cool. It sat against her sternum, tucked into the reliquary case chained beneath her cassock, and it held its heat like a small stubborn heart that refused to acknowledge it had been removed from any body at all.

Three days north of Hollowmere, in the saddle, through sleet, across a river the horses balked at crossing—the warmth persisted. She checked it each night by candlelight, unclasping the case with hands gone clumsy from cold, and each night the blood glowed faintly amber, thick as honey, moving when she was not looking at it. Moving toward her fingers. Toward her skin. Toward the pulse point at her wrist where Cindral's mark had been burned into her at ordination, twenty-two years ago, when she had been young enough to weep with joy about it.

*It wants something,* she thought, on the third night, in a waystation chapel with a leaking roof and a congregation of one. *And I have been too much a coward to ask what.*

She knelt before the altar as she had knelt ten thousand times before. The altar was bare—Cindral's icon had been stripped from every roadside shrine since the Silence, carted off or melted down or simply abandoned to rot, because what use was an image of a god who no longer answered? For two decades Sarelle had prayed to emptiness and called it discipline. *Faith is not transaction,* the Choir's founders had written. *We do not serve for reward.* She had believed it. She had built her whole self out of that belief, stone by patient stone, the way a mason builds a cathedral over a lifetime knowing he will never see the spire finished.

Then prayers had begun to be answered again, and Sarelle had learned what she truly was—not a mason but a miner, someone who had spent her life digging in the dark on the faith that ore lay somewhere below, and who now heard, far down in the shaft, something enormous beginning to move.

She prayed. Not the formal liturgies, not the ordered canticles of the Choir, but the raw stumbling prayer of a woman alone in a cold room with a stolen miracle chained to her chest.

*Cindral. First-Fire. Lighter of Lamps. I have carried you through the years of ash. I have done terrible things in your name and called them necessary. Tell me what you want of me.*

The silence stretched. The candle guttered. And then—

Not a voice. Nothing so merciful as a voice. What came instead was heat: a slow bloom of warmth spreading up through her knees, her spine, her jaw, as if she were a wick being lit from the inside. The flame on the altar stood up straight and tall without flickering, impossibly still, and in its stillness Sarelle saw reflected—not her own face, but a shape behind her own face, vast and orange-eyed and *attentive*, the way a wolf is attentive to a sound in the grass.

*I want,* said Cindral, in the language of fire, which is not words but consumption, *what is mine.*

Sarelle's breath left her in a thin white ribbon. She had waited twenty years for this moment. She had imagined it a thousand times: the god returning, the reunion, the purpose restored, herself standing radiant at the right hand of the First-Fire as he rekindled the world. In her imaginings there had always been gratitude. There had always been *her*, seen at last, chosen at last.

There was no gratitude in the heat that filled the chapel now. There was appetite.

*The Ember,* she whispered. *You mean the Ember. The spark in the girl.*

*The spark,* agreed the fire, and the word was warm as a mouth closing around her, *and the vessel, and the priestess, and all the little lamps I lit and forgot to tend. All of it comes home to me. All of it burns in me. That is what fire is, Sarelle. That is the only prayer I have ever answered.*

She stayed kneeling long after the presence withdrew, long after the candle sank back into ordinary trembling, long after the cold crept back into the chapel and made itself at home in her bones. Outside, snow fell on the waystation roof with a sound like sand in an hourglass, measuring out something she could not yet name.

Her hands, when she finally looked at them, were steady. That frightened her more than shaking would have. Twenty-two years of discipline, and her body would not even permit her the honesty of fear.

* * *

They made the border fortress of Karrow Vale by dusk on the fifth day, a squat grey keep where the Choir maintained its northern garrison, and Sarelle locked herself in the scriptorium with the door barred and gave orders that she was not to be disturbed for anything short of the world ending.

She had brought back archives from Hollowmere. Records seized from the burning records-house before the flames took them—Maren Ostwick's ledgers, the archivist's meticulous hand marching across page after page, dates and names and entries in a code Sarelle had partially broken on the road north. And interleaved among them, older documents, older than Ostwick, older than the Choir itself: fragments of the pre-Silence liturgies, the true ones, the ones the Choir's founders had edited when they compiled the Canon of Ash.

She had read those fragments a hundred times in her life and understood them as poetry. Tonight, with the vial warm against her breastbone and the god's appetite still echoing in her teeth, she understood them as testimony.

*In the beginning was the Hearth, and the Hearth was not worshipped, for worship had not yet been invented. The gods were not born; they were lit. Each god a lamp kindled at the First Fire, each carrying away a portion of the flame, and the Flame remaining whole behind them, for fire is not diminished by the lighting of other fires.*

*But the gods looked back at the Hearth from which they had been lit, and they were afraid. For as long as the Hearth burned, they were not sources but branches. They were not creators but inheritors. And so they conspired together—Vael of the oaths, Hravn of the winter, Cindral of the forge-flame, and all their shining kin—and they smothered the First Fire, and they divided its last living spark among themselves to hide it, each burying his portion deeper than the last, until none remained above ground and the Hearth went dark.*

*And the gods declared themselves the beginning, and wrote new songs, and taught them to men.*

Sarelle set the page down. Her vision swam. The scriptorium's single lamp threw her shadow huge against the shelves of canon, and for a moment she saw the shadow for what it was—a silhouette shaped like devotion, hollow at the center, cast by nothing.

Twenty-two years. She thought of the novices she had trained, the villages she had purified, the heretics she had questioned in rooms with good drainage. She thought of Brother Callen, who had doubted, and whom she had broken on the wheel of doctrine until he recanted everything he loved, and who had hanged himself in the penitent's cell anyway, and whose death she had recorded in the annals as *returned to the Flame*. She had fed all of it—all of it, the whole grinding machine of her life—into the furnace of a god who had not been betrayed by the Silence at all, who had engineered the Silence, who had hidden the Ember precisely so that no one would ever learn what he was: not a source, but a thief. A branch pretending to be the root.

And now the Ember had surfaced. Now a girl in a frozen valley carried the last living piece of the truth in her chest. And Cindral wanted it back—not to restore it to the Hearth, not to relight the First Fire for the sake of the world, but to swallow it, to make certain that no one, ever, could hold him accountable to what he had been before he was a god.

*All of it comes home to me,* he had said. *All of it burns in me.*

Sarelle opened the reliquary case.

The vial sat in its cradle of black velvet, glowing softly, patient as a coal. Up close she could see movement in it now—slow convections, currents turning like weather in miniature, and at the center of the blood a brightness that pulsed in rhythm. She counted without meaning to. Four beats to the interval. Rest. Four beats again.

A heartbeat. Not hers. Herself, she realized, had been only the courier. The blood remembered its body. Somewhere south, bleeding and bound and half-unmade by a rite Sarelle had ordered performed, the null-born girl was still alive, and this stolen portion of her was still listening for her, still turning toward her across the miles like a sunflower toward a sun it could not see.

*She is not a vessel,* Sarelle thought, and the thought arrived with the force of a blow, flat and undeniable. *She is a person. She has a heartbeat. I am carrying a piece of a person in a bottle and calling it a relic.*

She had told herself, riding north from Hollowmere, that the extraction rite had been mercy—that the girl would have died anyway, that the Choir's surgeons were gentle, that the Ember belonged to heaven and heaven's claims superseded flesh. She had told herself the hostages would be spared, that the valley would be brought into order, that order was love wearing armor. Every step of the road home she had dressed the thing she'd done in finer and finer vestments, and now, alone in a barred room with a lamp and a ledger, the vestments caught fire and burned away, and underneath was just a woman holding a stolen heart.

The vial warmed under her gaze. Welcoming her attention. And in the warmth she felt again the vast orange attentiveness, the wolf-patience, and understood—with the clarity that comes only too late, the clarity that is the punishment rather than the insight—that Cindral was not speaking *through* the vial to her. He was speaking *through her* to the vial. She was not the priestess at the rail. She was the bellows. She had always been the bellows, and the fire had never once mistaken her for anything else.

*You knew,* she said aloud, to the empty room, to the shadow on the shelves. *You always knew what you were. You let us build you a cathedral of lies and you sat in the dark at the bottom of it, hungry, waiting for us to dig deep enough to find the thing you buried.*

The lamp flame leaned toward her, though there was no draft.

*And when you are whole again,* she continued, and her voice did not shake, because her body had never once been permitted the honesty of fear, *when you have swallowed the Ember and become the sole fire in a world of ashes—what will you need with priests?*

The flame straightened. Stilled. And in its stillness she saw the answer, plain as scripture, plain as the mural truths Maren Ostwick had died protecting: a god does not consume his rivals first and his servants later out of cruelty. He consumes them out of *order*. The near fuel burns before the far. The hand that holds the torch is the closest wood of all.

Sarelle closed the case. Chained it. Sat back in the dark with her hands folded in her lap like a novice awaiting instruction, and began, very quietly, to laugh—a dry, airless sound with no joy anywhere in it, the laughter of a woman reading her own obituary and finding it accurate.

* * *

Near midnight, a knock at the barred door. Three soft strikes. Then, low, through the iron: "Prelate. It's Acolyte Ferren. The scouts have returned from the pass."

Sarelle rose. Composed her face into the mask the Choir required—serenity over certainty over steel—and lifted the bar.

Ferren was young, earnest, and terrified, which was the natural condition of anyone who had served Sarelle longer than a month and paid attention. He stood in the corridor with sleet melting on his shoulders and a dispatch tube clutched in both hands like something that might bite.

"From the White Pass, Prelate. The winter host has moved. Lady Isolde's forces are descending on the lowlands—whole companies frozen mid-march, rivers locked to the bedrock. And—" he hesitated, swallowed, "—there are reports from the south. From Hollowmere. The prisoner escaped during the riot. The rite was interrupted. And the girl—"

"The girl," Sarelle said.

"They say she walked into the Choir's camp with her hands bound and bought forty lives. They say the god Vael poured himself into the valley to break the rite. They say—" Ferren's voice dropped, and what he said next he said the way men say things they do not believe, hoping disbelief will protect them. "They say she broke the Ember. Shattered it. Gave it away. To everyone. There isn't an Ember anymore, Prelate. There are thousands of them. Every person who stood in that temple carries a spark now. Bakers. Farmers. Children."

The corridor was silent except for the drip of sleet off Ferren's shoulders.

Sarelle felt the vial shift against her sternum. Not cooling. *Quickening.* The last fragment of the shattered whole, the piece she had carried out of the burning camp in a black velvet cradle, and it was beating faster now, beating like something that had heard its name called from very far away, beating like something that intended to be found.

*Thousands of sparks,* she thought. *Scattered into mortal hands. Unownable. Ungovernable. No throne worth seizing remains.*

Except one.

Because the shattering could not have been perfect. Because nothing mortal ever is. Because somewhere in that crowd of newly-lit ordinary people, one spark had fallen short—one fragment had landed in blood instead of breath, had been cut away before the giving, had been carried north in a reliquary case by a woman who had spent twenty-two years feeding a lie and had only tonight learned the taste of it.

Cindral did not need the whole Ember anymore. He needed only what she carried. One seed. One last ember in the possession of one last believer, and believers, she understood at last, were the only currency gods had ever truly accepted.

"Prelate?" Ferren said carefully. "Your orders?"

Sarelle looked past him, down the long stone corridor, toward the chapel where the garrison's stripped altar stood bare in the dark. She thought of Brother Callen hanging in the penitent's cell. She thought of the girl in the valley with her hands bound, buying strangers' lives with her own body, and how Sarelle had watched her do it and called it surrender.

She thought of the flame leaning toward her in an empty room, though there was no draft.

"Burn the dispatches," she said. "All of them. And wake the garrison—we ride south within the hour."

Ferren blinked. "South? Prelate, the Choir's command expects the relic delivered to the Cathedral of Ash. The manifestation rites are prepared. His Radiance—"

"I know what His Radiance has prepared." Her hand rose, without her willing it, to rest flat against her cassock, over the chain, over the case, over the small stubborn heart that beat four times and rested and beat again. Over the last piece of stolen fire in the world, and the last priestess foolish enough to think she had ever been anything more than kindling. "And I know, now, exactly who it is prepared *for*."

Outside, the snow had stopped. The sky over Karrow Vale was clearing for the first time in days, and the stars came out hard and cold and indifferent, and Sarelle Vane—who had been named for a saint who burned, who had given her whole life to a fire that meant to eat her—stood in the doorway of the scriptorium and felt, beneath the terror and the grief and the ruin of everything she had believed, one small and dangerous thing take light in her chest that did not come from any god at all.

It felt, astonishingly, like her own.

"We ride south," she said again, and this time her voice carried the edge of something Ferren had never heard in it before, and would spend the rest of his life trying to name. "Not to deliver the Ember."

She turned back to the darkened room, to the ledgers of thieves lying open on the desk, to the truth she had carried out of Hollowmere heavier than any relic.

"To return it."
