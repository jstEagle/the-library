## 32. Council of the Broken

The buried temple had a name once. Maren said it was called the House of Quiet Hands, raised by an order of monks who believed the gods were best served by silence, and that every prayer was a stone thrown into still water. They had carved their sanctuary deep into the mountain's root so that no wind, no pilgrim, no voice from above could reach them. Now the wind had followed us down anyway—or something like wind, the long cold breath of Hravn's winter pressing through the pass behind us—and we sat in their silence and argued about who should be allowed to own the fire.

There were eleven of us left. Eleven, out of a company that had crossed the White Pass with thirty-two. I counted them the way you count your own ribs after a fall, one by one, to learn what's broken.

Doss sat nearest the fire we'd built in the old offering trench, his hands wrapped around a tin cup he wasn't drinking from. Beside him, Ferro and the twins—Aldous and Merrit, gray-faced, frostbitten at the fingertips where they'd carried Tam's body down off the ice. Tam was folded in canvas by the far wall because none of us could bear to look at him and none of us could bear to put him outside. Behind them, Maren Ostwick, my mentor, my jailer, my father-in-everything-but-blood, his ledger open on his knees as if the book itself might defend him. The defected acolyte, Ilenne, sat apart from everyone, her Choir-gray robes traded for one of Ferro's spare shirts three sizes too big, her shaved head wrapped in wool. And at the edge of the light, wrists still raw where her own people had bound her before we cut her loose, Prelate Sarelle of the Ashen Choir, first voice of Cindral, architect of everything that had burned behind us.

She should have been dead. Sarelle herself seemed half-convinced of it, the way she kept touching her own throat as though checking whether the pulse there belonged to someone else.

The firelight did strange things in this place. The monks had polished the walls for centuries, and the stone held the flame and gave it back doubled, tripled, until the whole chamber seemed to breathe with a hundred small fires. Every shadow moved when no one moved. I kept catching shapes at the edge of my vision—the outline of a woman standing where I stood, a girl kneeling where Ilenne knelt—and when I turned, nothing. Only the Ember, banked low in my chest, keeping its own counsel.

It had been quiet since the extraction rite. Quiet the way a wound is quiet.

"We start with what we know," Doss said, because Doss always started with what we knew, even when what we knew was a knife with no handle. "The winter's moving south. Not weather—*him*. Hravn, through his lady. We saw it take the pass. We saw it take Tam." His voice caught on the name and he pushed through it, jaw tight. "Rivers are freezing in the lowlands. Ilenne says prayers are freezing with them. People pray and nothing comes back, not even silence—just cold."

"It is not that prayers die," Ilenne said softly. She spoke the way she did everything, carefully, as if words were relics she might crack. "It is that they are *kept*. Frozen mid-flight. In the north, the faithful say they can see their prayers hanging in the air, glittering. Waiting for a spring that Lady Isolde will not permit."

"And the only thing that melts it," Ferro said, "is sitting inside our captain."

Everyone looked at me. I let them. It was easier than looking away first.

"So," Doss said. "Three roads. We've all said it a hundred times in whispers, on watch, over the dead. Time to say it out loud, all together, where nobody can pretend they didn't hear." He held up one finger. "We give the Ember to Vael. Our captain's god. He stayed when the others ran. He asks for it—gently, she tells me—and maybe a god made whole again can unmake a winter." A second finger. "We give it to Hravn. Or trade it. Isolde offered terms once already. Winter ends, or winter becomes law, depending on which end of the bargain you're standing on." A third finger, and here his hand hesitated, the way it never hesitated over maps or pay ledgers. "Or we destroy it. Kill the last spark of the old fire. Gods fade to stories, miracles go back to being luck, and whatever comes after—we all just have to live in it."

"No fourth road?" Merrit asked. His voice was hoarse. He hadn't spoken much since the pass.

"There's a fourth road," Aldous said. "We do nothing. We hide down here until the world finishes arguing without us."

"The world doesn't finish arguments," Ferro said. "It just bills the survivors."

I looked at Maren. He had been silent since we came down into the temple, and his silence had a texture I knew from childhood—the particular stillness of a man deciding how much truth a room can hold. He had curated my ignorance for twenty-three years. He was curating this room now, I realized. Deciding what we would be permitted to know.

"Maren," I said. "You've read everything. Every ledger, every mural, every scrap the records-house ever smuggled out of the burning places. Tell them what the Ember actually *is*. Not what it does. What it is."

He closed his eyes. When he opened them, he looked older than the mountain around us.

"The murals in the inner shrine," he said. "The ones Vesper found. You've heard her tell it, but hear it from me, because I verified it against sources the Choir burned two hundred years ago and would burn again." He drew a breath. "Before the gods, there was the fire. Not a god. Older than godhood. The first warmth, the first light, the thing that taught the dark the difference between itself and everything else. And the beings that would become gods—they were not its children. They were its thieves. They came to the first hearth, and they killed the fire's keeper, and they lit themselves from the coals, each of them taking a flame and calling it theirs. Cindral took fire most of all, and called himself its lord. Hravn took the cold that fire leaves behind and called that holy too. And Vael—" Maren's eyes found mine, and I made myself hold them. "Vael took less than any of them. Perhaps because he took least, he remembered most clearly what the theft cost. What it made them. What it would keep making them, forever, unless the original spark was destroyed or returned."

"And the Ember is the original spark," Ilenne whispered. "The part that was never stolen. The part that stayed home."

"The part that remembers being murdered," Sarelle said.

Every head turned to her. She had not spoken since we freed her, except once, to warn us of a crevasse hidden under snow—a warning that saved Merrit's leg, though he had not thanked her and she had not expected him to. Now she rose, slowly, joints stiff from her bindings, and walked to the fire, and stood over it with her hands open at her sides like a woman approaching an altar she no longer believed in.

"You want to know why I am here," she said. "Why I do not run, though you leave me unguarded. Why I do not preach, though preaching is the only craft I have." Her voice was steady, but underneath it I heard the same sound I'd heard in the ritual tent, when the rite went wrong and her god's presence poured through the stolen blood and she understood, all at once, what she had spent her life feeding. "Because I have seen the face of Cindral. Not his icon. Not his glory. His *appetite*. I carried a vial of this woman's blood across half the world, and when my god manifested through it, the first thing he reached for was not the Ember. It was me. His prelate. His most faithful servant. He looked at me the way a man looks at a candle he has decided to eat." She smiled, and it was the worst smile I have ever seen, grief wearing ceremony's clothes. "I built the Ashen Choir to give the world one god instead of many quarreling ones. I thought monopoly would mean peace. I understand now that I was building a mouth."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above us, deep in the rock, the mountain ticked and settled, ice expanding in some unseen seam.

"So you agree," Doss said carefully. "None of them gets it. Not Cindral, not Hravn—"

"I did not say that." Sarelle turned. Firelight moved on her face like water. "I say: be honest about what you are choosing. If you give the Ember to Vael, you are not restoring balance. You are crowning the thief who feels guiltiest. Guilt is not innocence. Ask yourselves—if a god swallows the last living piece of the thing his kind murdered, does the murder heal? Or does he simply become strong enough to ensure no one ever speaks of it again?"

"That's not—" Aldous started.

"It is exactly what happened," Maren said quietly. "Twenty-three years ago, the old faiths agreed to hunt the Ember—not to destroy it. To destroy it would have been mercy, and mercy was never on the table. They hunted it to *control* it. Each church wanted it locked where only they held the key. The temple that sheltered it was burned by a coalition of four faiths acting in concert. Your gods, Vesper. All of them. They burned a building full of innocents to reach one infant."

I had known this. Maren had told me, in fragments, on the road. But hearing it spoken aloud in front of my crew—in front of people who had bled for me believing I was a woman, not a relic—was different. The Ember stirred in my chest, a slow turn like a sleeper shifting, and the fire in the trench leaned toward me, every flame in the chamber bending my way at once, and eleven people watched it happen and pretended not to.

"And Vael?" I asked. My voice came out flat. I was proud of that, in a distant, sickened way. "Where was my god, Maren? While four churches burned a nursery?"

"He was there." Maren didn't flinch. "That's the horror of it. He was there, and he did not stop it, because stopping it would have meant revealing himself, revealing *you*, and the valley was the only place on earth where the Ember could sleep undetected. So he did the arithmetic every coward does. One temple against one world. He chose the world." He paused. "And then he went to Hollowmere and fell silent, because he could not bear to speak to the child he had failed, and silence felt like penance, and penance felt like virtue, and twenty years passed."

"*Penance*," I repeated. The word tasted like copper. "He let me swear an oath to him when I was fourteen. He let me pray to him every night. He let me believe I was abandoned—that something was wrong with *me*—and all along I was his prison, his vault, his little box in the mountains where he kept the evidence of his crime. And now he wants his reward. Now he asks *gently*."

"Vesper." Doss's voice, low. Warning. Not at my anger—at the fire. The flames had risen to shoulder height without fuel, burning white at their hearts, and the temperature in the chamber had climbed enough that frost was running off the walls in threads of water. I pulled it back. I was getting better at pulling it back. It cost more every time.

"Say your piece about Vael," I said. "Then we move on."

Sarelle studied me with those pale, ash-colored eyes. "When the rite broke," she said, "and your god poured himself into the valley—he did not strike me. He could have. I was kneeling in the ruins of my own working, holding your blood in my hands, and he was everywhere, in every snowflake, in every breath, and he did not touch me. He shielded *you*, and he shielded the hostages, and he let me walk away." She tilted her head. "Either he is merciful beyond his kind, or he is saving me, because he knows I am the only person alive who understands what Cindral has become, and he may need that understanding. Gods do not waste pieces on the board."

"Or," said Ilenne, barely audible, "he is ashamed, and shame makes gods gentle, and gentleness is not the same as goodness."

Silence. The good kind and the bad kind, tangled together.

Doss cleared his throat. "All right. Road one: Vael. Objections stand—she's named them better than I could. Road two: Hravn." He looked around the circle. "Anyone going to argue for the winter?"

No one spoke. Then, to my surprise, Merrit did.

"My sister lives in Lowmark," he said. "On the coast. You all know the story, I've told it enough times drunk." He rubbed his frostbitten fingers. "Isolde came to us on the pass. Offered the captain immortality, purpose, an end to hiding—I heard the terms secondhand, but I heard them. And here's the thing nobody wants to say out loud." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious. "Her winter doesn't lie. That's what frightens me. Cindral's church promised salvation and delivered an appetite. Vael promised protection and delivered twenty years of silence. But the Long Winter—it is exactly what it says it is. Cold, endless, honest. My sister's prayers froze in the air, and they're still *there*, still hanging, still hers. Nothing else in this cursed story kept a single promise it made. Maybe the ice is the only honest god left, and maybe honesty is worth something, and maybe I'm so tired and so frozen that I'm mistaking surrender for sense." He dropped his gaze. "But somebody had to say it."

"Thank you for saying it," I told him, and meant it, and hated that I meant it, because he was right in the way that exhausted people are right—right the way drowning men are right about the water.

"Here is what Isolde's honesty buys," Sarelle said. "Eternal law. No spring, anywhere, ever. No growth. No change. Every prayer preserved like a fly in amber, every living thing paused at the moment of its greatest suffering, forever, because forever is the only shape winter knows. She offered you a place at her side, Vesper. Did she offer the world a place in her heart?"

"She offered the world rest," I said. "That's what she called it. Rest."

"Rest," Sarelle repeated, "is what the dead get. The living get morning."

Ferro snorted. Even Merrit almost smiled.

"Road three, then," Doss said. "Destroy it." He looked at me, and I saw what it cost him, because Doss had lost a wife to a plague the gods ignored and a son to a war the gods sponsored, and destroying divinity outright was the dream he'd nursed through twenty bitter years of selling his sword to whoever paid. "Kill the fire. All of it. Gods starve, miracles stop, the world belongs to people. Clean ending. Simple."

"It is not simple," Maren said. "It is not even possible, perhaps—but suppose it is. Suppose we find a way to extinguish the last spark of the first warmth. Understand what you are extinguishing. Not gods. *Warmth*. The Ember is not merely the seed of divinity—it is the original heat that matter itself was kindled from. Burn it out, and you may unmake far more than heaven. You may unmake the difference between the fire and the dark. Every hearth. Every fever that breaks. Every body that stays warm against the cold because something in the world's first morning said *yes* to warmth and never fully retracted it." He spread his hands. "The gods stole from the fire. But we—mortal things—we were lit by it too. Freely. Before the theft. The fire loved us first. That is in the murals, and I have checked it against every source, and I believe it with everything left in me. Destroy the Ember, and you do not kill the thieves. You kill the inheritance."

"The inheritance," I said, "has been used as a leash, a weapon, a hostage, and a prize. Forgive me if I don't weep for it."

"You don't have to weep," Maren said. "You only have to decide. That's the cruelty of it. There is no clean hand here. Every road runs through someone's annihilation. Vael's wholeness requires the Ember's death-as-itself—it ceases to be the fire and becomes merely his power. Hravn's eternal law requires everything the fire warmed. Destruction requires—" he stopped, and his voice frayed, "—requires gambling that the world survives its own funeral. And doing nothing requires the deaths of everyone Isolde freezes while we deliberate in comfort underground."

"Then we're back to the fourth road," Aldous muttered. "Hide."

"For how long?" Ferro asked. "Winter's coming down the passes. It'll find this mountain. It finds everything."

I stood. I hadn't planned to. My legs decided, and the Ember decided with them, and when I rose, the fire rose with me, and this time I let it, because I was tired of pretending I was only a woman in a room.

They all looked up at me. Broken crew, broken priest, broken prelate, broken old man with his broken ledger. The monks who built this place had believed the gods were best served by silence. Looking at their faces, I finally understood what they'd gotten wrong. The gods aren't served by silence. Silence is what serves the gods—silence and ignorance and people too frightened or too tired to ask what the fire in their chest is actually for.

"Here's what I know," I said. "Not decide—know. The Ember isn't a crown. It isn't a key. It isn't a weapon, though everyone who's ever held it has tried to make it one. It's the part of the first fire that never got stolen. The part that stayed home and kept the hearth. And every option on this table—every single one—is a way of handing the hearth back to the burglars." I looked at Sarelle. "Yes, including destruction. Destruction is just the burglary of tomorrow." I looked at Maren. "And yes, including giving it to Vael. Especially that. Because he'd be gentle with it. He'd be so gentle. And in a thousand years, gentle possession looks exactly like ownership."

"Then what?" Doss asked. "You've shot down every road. Give us a fifth."

"I don't have a fifth." I crouched, so I was eye-level with them, with all of them, the way you crouch to speak to a child or a wounded friend. "I have a direction. The Ember was born somewhere. The first hearth. The temple before temples, the one the murals show, the one the gods burned and buried and lied about for ten thousand years. Every answer anyone in this room has offered is secondhand—scripture, ledger, rumor, the self-serving memories of thieves. I want to go to the place where the fire began and ask the fire itself. Not Vael's version. Not Isolde's. Not the Choir's ashes. The original."

"And if the fire answers?" Sarelle asked quietly. "What then?"

"Then it will be the first time in this entire war that anyone asked it," I said.

Nobody spoke. Above us, muffled by a hundred feet of stone, the wind changed its note—from howl to whistle to something almost like singing, the sound Hravn's winter made when it was thinking about a place. We all heard it. We all pretended, for one more moment, that we hadn't.

Then Doss drained his untouched cup, set it down, and said, in the voice he used for contracts: "How far?"

Maren opened the ledger to a page near the back—one I'd never seen, covered in his cramped cipher—and turned it toward the fire.

"Nine days' march," he said. "If the winter lets us."

* * *

We drew lots for watches and settled into the temple's cold hospitality, and I took the last watch alone, because I always did now, because sleep and I had come to a difficult arrangement.

Near dawn—or what the mountain guessed was dawn—I felt Vael stir. Not words. Just presence, vast and careful, the way a large animal stands very still at the edge of firelight. And beneath the care, unmistakable, the thing I had refused to name since the ritual tent:

He was afraid.

My god, who had watched temples burn and said nothing, who had guarded his prison for twenty years and called it love—my god was afraid of where I was going, and of what the fire might say about him when I got there.

Good, I thought, wrapping my cloak tighter, watching my breath hang silver in the dark like a tiny frozen prayer.

Let him be afraid. Let him wait nine days, like the rest of us, to find out what he is.

Far to the north, the wind sang on—and this time, I was almost certain, it sang my name.
