## 33. Vael's Request

The council had ended the way all councils end when no one can agree: in silence, and in the smell of tallow.

Vesper stayed behind after the others filed out of the buried temple's antechamber—Doss rubbing his jaw where an old scar ached in cold weather, Maren leaning on her staff with the careful dignity of a woman who refused to be carried, the acolyte Brenna with her Choir-gray robes still smelling faintly of incense and shame. Even Sarelle had gone, escorted to a cell they'd agreed was a cell only because she'd asked for it. *I need somewhere my god cannot see me think,* she'd said, and no one had laughed.

That left Vesper alone with the murals.

She stood beneath them now, in the long gallery where the first hearth's keepers had painted their truth before the truth became heresy. Torchlight moved across the plaster and made the figures seem to breathe. The maker-god, vast and radiant, pouring fire into the vessels that would become the gods. The vessels turning. The knives. The theft. The maker burning down into a single spark—the Ember—and the gods scattering like thieves from a house whose owner had woken.

*They killed their maker to steal it.* She had read those words in pigment and ash two nights ago, and they had not stopped echoing since.

"You are staring at it again."

The voice came from everywhere and nowhere, the way it always did now—not the searing heartbeat of that first stirring in Hollowmere, not the frost-voiced warning, but something quieter. A voice like banked coals. Like a fire that had learned gentleness out of necessity.

"I'm trying to understand you," Vesper said aloud. Her breath fogged. "All of you. What you were. What you did."

"And what have you concluded?"

"That I was raised by a thief." She said it flatly, testing the weight of it. "That everything I prayed to at fourteen was built on a murder older than language."

The silence that followed was not empty. It was the silence of someone choosing their words with care, and Vesper had spent twenty years learning the difference between a god's absence and a god's restraint. This was restraint. It hurt worse.

"May I sit with you?" Vael asked. "As much as I sit anywhere."

"The whole temple is yours, apparently. You've been pouring yourself into its stones since we arrived. The walls are warm, Vael. In a ruin under a glacier, the walls are *warm*."

"I know. Forgive me. Old habits. Gods warm what they love; it is nearly all we know how to do."

So he came—not as a shape, not yet, but as a presence settling into the gallery like light through a window, and where he settled the torch flames stood up straighter, and the cold retreated to the edges of the room as though politely excused. Vesper wrapped her arms around herself anyway. Not from chill. From the old reflex, the one carved into her at fourteen on the altar-stone at Hollowmere, when she had sworn herself to this voice and meant it with every scrap of her foolish, faithful heart.

He formed slowly, the way heat forms above coals: a suggestion of shoulders, a suggestion of a face, features that never quite resolved because, she suspected, he had never quite decided what he looked like—or because what he looked like depended too much on who was looking. To her, he had always been tall and gray-eyed and kind around the mouth. A father made of ember-light.

"You've been avoiding me," he said.

"I've been *thinking*," she corrected. "There's a difference. Though I understand how a god might confuse them."

A low sound, almost laughter. Almost. "You have Maren's tongue."

"Maren taught me to question everything except her. You taught me nothing at all for twenty years, so I had to learn the rest myself." She turned back to the mural, to the maker's painted face, serene even as the knives rose around him. "Why did you stay?"

"We have spoken of this."

"We spoke of it while running from inquisitors. I was bleeding. You were dramatic. I don't remember it clearly, and I want to remember it clearly." She faced him. "The other gods fled the valley because of me. Because the Ember nullifies them. They abandoned their faithful wholesale—whole nations went silent, prayers dropped into nothing, and your fellow gods simply... left. But you didn't. You stayed near me. Near *it*. Why would a god do that? It should have been agony."

"It was."

"Then why?"

The ember-light of him flickered, and for one unguarded moment his form thinned so badly she could see the mural through his chest, the painted knives, the painted fire. When he spoke again, his voice had lost its warmth and found something rawer underneath.

"Because I was ashamed."

Vesper waited. She had learned waiting from him, ironically—from twenty years of silence, from the discipline of unanswered prayer. She could out-wait a god. She had, in a sense, been doing it her entire life.

"Shame," he said at last, "is not an emotion I was designed for. We were made to be loved, Vesper. That is what the fire made us *for*—vessels shaped to hold devotion the way a cup holds water. When the other gods fled the valley, they told themselves it was strategy. Containment. Quarantine of the Ember until some solution could be found. But I stayed because I could not stop looking at what we had done. And I could not stop looking at *you*."

"Me."

"The child the Ember was hidden in. An infant, smuggled into the one place in the world where divinity could not reach her, and left there to grow up ordinary." His gaze, if a god's gaze could be said to fall, fell to the medallion at her throat—the blank disc of dead steel she had worn since fourteen. "Do you know why your oath never worked? Why the medallion stayed cold, why the prayers went nowhere, why every other acolyte felt the fire of vocation and you felt only stone?"

"Because you were silent. Because the valley eats magic."

"Because I *refused* you." The words came out quiet and terrible. "You knelt on that altar at fourteen years old and gave yourself to me wholly, and I sat in my exile outside the valley and listened to every word, and I did not answer. Not because I could not reach you. Because answering would have bound you tighter to a god, and I had sworn—privately, cowardly, in the dark where no one could hear—that no god would ever bind you. Not even me. *Especially* not me. Not after what binding had already cost you."

Vesper's hand had risen without her permission to the medallion. Her fingers pressed against the cold metal until it bit. "You let me believe I was unchosen."

"You were *protected*."

"Don't." Her voice cracked like ice on a pond. "Don't you dare dress it up. Do you know what it is to spend your whole life believing your god looked at you and found you wanting? I built myself out of that. I became a mercenary because soldiers don't need to be chosen. I learned to fight because blades answer everyone equally. Every hard thing I ever did, I did to make peace with being *passed over*—and you're telling me it was mercy. That it was love."

"Yes."

"Then your love is indistinguishable from abandonment, and I have no use for it."

The words hung between them, and the torches guttered, and for a moment Vesper thought she had finally, truly driven him away—the way she had driven away every soft thing she'd ever been offered, Doss's loyalty, Maren's sheltering lies, the company's easy affection. But Vael did not withdraw. He dimmed, the way a man dims when struck, and then he steadied.

"Good," he said softly. "Keep that anger. You will need it for what I came here to ask."

And Vesper felt it then, before he said a word—a pulling sensation behind her sternum, gentle as a hook set in wool, and she understood with sickening clarity why he had come to her alone, after the council, in the hour when the others slept.

"No," she said.

"You have not heard the request."

"I don't need to hear it. I watched Sarelle try to carve it out of me with a rite. I watched Isolde offer me eternity to hand it over. I watched Cindral try to *eat* it. Everyone wants the Ember, Vael. Everyone has wanted it since the maker burned. And now it's your turn, and you've come at night, when I'm tired, when the others aren't watching, because you hoped I'd say yes to you when I said no to them." She laughed, and hated the sound of it. "You hoped being loved would work where fear failed."

"Is that what I am doing?" He drifted closer, and the warmth of him touched her face like a hand. "Or is this what it sounds like when a god asks honestly, having watched every other claimant ask dishonestly?"

"Ask, then."

He was silent a long moment. The murals watched them both—the maker pouring out his fire, the gods lifting their knives, the whole history of heaven rendered in flaking paint.

"Give me the Ember," Vael said. "Let me become whole."

* * *

The gallery seemed to lean in around the words.

Vesper did not move. Somewhere far above, through fathoms of stone and ice, the wind off the Long Winter moaned against the temple's broken crown, and the sound came down the corridors like a mourner walking.

"Whole," she repeated.

"You have seen what the world becomes otherwise. Hravn's winter swallows armies. Cindral manifests in stolen blood and burns whatever holds him. The Ember sits in your chest like a lit lamp in a room full of starving men, and every day more of them find the door." His voice was gentle, terribly gentle, the voice he must have used once on frightened worshippers in temples that no longer stood. "If I take it—if you give it freely, which no one else can do, which only you can do—I become what the maker was. Complete. Whole fire. And with that wholeness I can end the winter, unmake Cindral's stolen manifestation, scatter the Choir, heal the torn fabric of the divine order. One act of trust, Vesper, and the world is saved."

"And you become a god with no rivals. No equals. No checks." She held up a hand before he could speak. "I've heard this sermon. Sarelle preached a version of it. So did Isolde, in her way—eternal winter as eternal law, suffering solved by making suffering impossible, freedom included in the price. Every one of you has a plan to save the world that ends with one will standing over it."

"I am not Cindral."

"No. You're the god who stayed. The god who was ashamed. The god who protected me by letting me break my heart on a cold medallion for twenty years." She stepped closer to him, close enough that the ember-light washed her shadow huge against the mural wall, so that her silhouette seemed to stand among the knife-bearing gods, and she hated that. "Tell me the rest, Vael. The part you rehearsed not saying. Tell me what happens to me."

The silence stretched. And Vesper had known this man—this god—for the length of a lifetime's grief, and she watched him decide, watch the truth come up in him like blood through a bandage, and she knew, before he opened his mouth, that whatever he said next would cost him something real.

"If I take the Ember," he said, "the vessel is emptied."

"Meaning what? I die?"

"No. Death would be simpler." He closed his eyes—or made the gesture of closing eyes, a courtesy of form. "The Ember has been part of you since before you drew breath. Your soul grew around it the way a tree grows around iron. To draw it out cleanly—to draw it out *whole*, which is the only way it can be given—I must unmake that growth. The spark leaves. What remains is..." He searched. "Less. Not dead. Not damaged, exactly. But the part of you that has burned all these years—the part that felt me stir, that unmakes enchanted locks, that melted the snow in front of three hundred pilgrims—that part goes with me. You would be mortal, Vesper. Only mortal. Entirely, permanently, irretrievably mortal."

For a moment she couldn't speak. Then, absurdly, she almost laughed.

"That's your offer? Vael. *Vael.* That's what I've been begging the universe for since chapter one of my own miserable life. Ordinary. Do you know how many nights I lay in barracks praying—praying to *you*, God help me—to be ordinary? To be a woman instead of a wound waiting to happen?" She pressed her palm flat against her sternum, against the banked heat she had carried for twenty-three years without knowing its name. "You're offering me the one thing I actually want."

"I know." His voice had gone very quiet. "That is why I hate asking."

And there it was. The crack in him. She saw it open—the god-shaped composure falling away like ash off a log, and underneath, something smaller and older and more afraid. Something that had watched a fourteen-year-old kneel on an altar and had chosen silence over binding, and had regretted it every day since, and had now come in the dark to ask her for the only thing that could undo his shame or complete it, depending on how it ended.

"Say the other part," Vesper whispered. "The part you admitted to the council. Say it here, where it's just us. Say it the way you'd say it if you weren't trying to be answered yes."

The torches burned. The wind mourned overhead. And Vael, last of the staying gods, bowed his ember-lit head.

"I may simply be another thief," he said.

The words fell into the cold like stones into a well, and Vesper heard them hit bottom, and heard the echo, and knew the well was deep.

"Look at the wall, Vesper. Look at what we were. Vessels. Cups. The maker poured himself out to fill us, and we measured our fullness, and we decided we deserved it, and we cut him down and called it ascension. Every god you have met on this road—Cindral devouring his own faithful, Hravn freezing the world into a law no one voted for, the Choir burning records so that theft could be remembered as destiny—we are all of us the same crime wearing different vestments. And I tell myself I am different. I tell myself I *stayed*, I *guarded*, I *loved*. But when I imagine the Ember in my chest—when I let myself feel, even for a moment, what wholeness would be—" His form shuddered, flame guttering. "I want it. Not to save the world. The world-saving is true, but it is not the *reason*. The reason is that I have been hollow for ten thousand years and I cannot bear it anymore. That is the truth. I am a cup asking to be filled, and the wine is your life, and I have dressed the asking in salvation because that is what my kind does. We always have."

Vesper's eyes were burning. She wiped them with the heel of her hand, angry at the tears, angrier that they came anyway.

"Do you know what the worst part is?" she managed. "It's not that you might be a thief. It's that I *believe* you. Every word. The shame, the staying, the hollow—I believe all of it, because I've spent my whole life loving you, and loving someone means you can't lie to them, and you just didn't. Twenty years of silence and then you come back and the first honest thing in my spiritual life is a god confessing he might be a monster." She laughed wetly. "Maren hid the ledger entry. You hid the silence. Everyone I love curates my ignorance, and then hands me the truth like it's a gift."

"Would you rather I had lied beautifully?"

"Yes," she said. "Obviously. And then I'd have given you the Ember, wouldn't I? That's the horror of it, Vael. If you'd lied well enough, I'd have said yes. I'd have walked to that altar upstairs and let you hollow me out and thanked you for the privilege. So thank you for telling the truth. Thank you for making it *hard*." She turned away from him, toward the mural, toward the maker's serene painted face. "Because now I have to judge you. My own god. The voice I swore to at fourteen. And judging means weighing, and weighing means I have to look at what you were and what you might be and decide whether wholeness in your hands saves the world or just completes the oldest robbery in creation."

"And what do you weigh?" he asked softly. "What tips the scale?"

Vesper was quiet for a long time.

"You stayed," she said finally. "When every other god ran, you stayed next to the thing that burns you, for twenty-three years, out of shame and love and whatever tangled knot those make together. That's evidence. Thieves don't stand guard over the treasure they're planning to steal. Thieves don't wait a generation." She turned back to him. "But Cindral waited too, in his way. Hravn has waited an age for his winter. Patience isn't innocence, Vael. Sometimes it's just appetite with discipline."

"So the scale hangs even."

"The scale hangs even." She looked down at her own chest, at the faint glow that had begun, these past days, to show through her skin when she was tired or afraid—as though the Ember knew the world was closing in and wanted, in its mute pre-divine way, to be ready. "Which means I can't decide tonight. And I won't decide tonight. I promised the council I'd hear every claimant, and I've heard yours, and it was honest, and honesty buys you time. Nothing more."

"That is more than I expected," Vael said. "More than I deserve, perhaps."

"Don't fish. Gods fishing for absolution is obscene."

This time he did laugh—a real one, brief and startled out of him, and for a heartbeat he was only the gray-eyed kindness she had prayed to at fourteen, and her heart broke a little more, because that was the cruelest trick of all: that the thief and the father wore the same face, and she would never be able to separate them, not entirely, not ever.

"One thing more," he said, as he began to thin back into the stones, into the warmth, into the everywhere-he-was. "Whatever you decide—give me to another, keep the spark, shatter it, burn it out entirely—know that I will abide by it. Not because I am good. Because you are the only judge in ten thousand years whose verdict I would accept. The maker never got a trial, Vesper. His children took his fire and never once asked him. Let me at least stand before yours."

And he was gone, and the walls cooled by degrees, and the torches leaned back into their ordinary flickering, and Vesper stood alone in the gallery with her hand pressed over the small unbearable sun in her chest.

*Only mortal,* he had said. *Entirely, permanently, irretrievably.*

She should have felt relief. Instead she felt the specific vertigo of a woman who has just discovered that the exit she dreamed of all her life leads to a door she may have to lock behind her forever.

Above her, muffled through stone and glacier, the wind changed its note—deeper now, colder, carrying a sound like vast wings beating somewhere beyond the pass. The Long Winter, moving. Isolde's host, or Hravn's law itself, coming down out of the white country toward the First Hearth, toward the altar, toward her.

They were out of time for deliberation. Whatever she decided about her god, she would have to decide it on the march—and the snow was already at the doors.
