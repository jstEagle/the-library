## 34. The March to the First Hearth

The world ended at the pass and began again as something white and merciless.

Vesper had known winter her whole life—Hollowmere winters that sealed the valley like a jar, snow to the eaves, breath freezing in the wool of your collar. But this was not winter. This was Hravn's will made weather, a cold with intention behind it, and it did not fall so much as *press*, the way a hand presses on a wound. The sky was the color of old pewter. The sun rose each morning as a pale coin behind gauze and set without ever once warming the snow. Even the wind had stopped being wind and become something more like breathing—the long, slow respiration of a god leaning close over the world to watch them walk.

They walked anyway.

Forty-one of them when they left the buried temple behind the White Pass. Vesper counted them each dawn and each dusk, the way Maren had taught her to count sheep in the ledger-house—not because the number changed often, but because counting was a way of promising. *You are all still here. I am still responsible for you.* Doss walked at the column's head with his sword slung across his back like a pilgrim's staff. Brenna carried the maps, inked on vellum that cracked if you breathed on it wrong. Sef, who had lost two fingers to frostbite at the pass and refused to speak of it, carried the food, which meant he carried everyone's tempers along with it.

And Vesper carried the Ember.

It did not weigh anything. That was the strangest part. It sat behind her sternum like a second heart, warm when everything else was cold, and the warmth was not comfort—it was *attention*. All day, every day, she felt it listening through her. Feeling for the road ahead. Feeling, sometimes, for the things that followed.

Because they were followed. Of course they were followed.

* * *

On the ninth day out of the temple, the snow began to sing.

Vesper heard it first—a thin, high tone at the edge of hearing, like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass. She stopped mid-stride and held up a fist, and forty men and women halted behind her in a silence so complete she could hear the blood moving in her own ears.

"What is it?" Doss asked quietly.

"You don't hear that?"

He cocked his head. Around him, the others did the same, faces blank under rime-crusted hoods. Nothing. To them the world was only wind and the creak of leather and the groan of glaciers far off in the dark.

But Vesper heard it, and worse—she *felt* it, a pulling sensation behind her ribs, as if the Ember were a fish on a line and something on the other end had begun to reel.

"It knows where we're going," she said. "Cindral. He's found the thread."

Doss's jaw tightened. In the three weeks since the council of the broken, since Sarelle had stood among them with her god's blood in a stolen vial and her certainty in ruins, Doss had aged ten years. Not in his body—he was iron, he would be iron in his coffin—but around the eyes, where the skin had gone thin and papery from squinting into a future none of them liked.

"Then we move faster," he said.

"We can't." Brenna unrolled a corner of her map with numb fingers. "The First Hearth is six days north across the Rimefield. There's no faster. There's only the road the old pilgrims cut, and half of it's under ice now."

"Then we take what rest we can," Vesper said, "and we pray Isolde's host is slower than a song."

No one laughed. They had stopped laughing somewhere around the frozen river, where they'd found the first of Isolde's stragglers—or rather, the first of Isolde's *victims*: a company of freeholders turned soldier, thirty of them, caught mid-march by the advancing winter and left standing in the ice like a garden of gray statues. A woman at the front still held a banner. A boy near the rear had been turning to look back when the cold took him, and his face, preserved perfectly under a skin of clear ice, wore an expression Vesper could not stop seeing when she closed her eyes.

Not terror. *Surprise.* As if even at the end, he hadn't believed the winter would actually arrive.

That night they camped in the lee of a fallen waymarker, one of the ancient pillars that had guided pilgrims to the First Hearth in the years before the gods learned to be ashamed of their own cradle. Vesper took first watch, as she always did, sitting with her back against the stone while the company slept in a huddle of shared blankets and borrowed warmth.

Maren came to sit with her. The old archivist moved slowly now; the Choir's prison had taken something out of him that the rescue had not given back. His hands, once so quick with pen and seal, trembled unless he pressed them flat against his knees.

"You should sleep," he said.

"I'll sleep when we get there."

"You said that at the river. And at the pass. And before that, in the reliquary." He smiled faintly. "You've been saying it since you were seven years old, when you'd fight bedtime like it was an assassin."

Vesper almost smiled back. Almost. "Do you remember much? From before?"

"I remember everything." His voice went quiet. "That's my curse, Vess. Archivists don't get the mercy of forgetting. We just get better shelves."

She looked out over the snowfield. Far to the south, past the black teeth of the pass, there was a glow on the horizon that should not have been there—amber, deep, pulsing slow as a heartbeat. Cindral's manifestation, walking. The Choir's god had poured himself out of Sarelle's stolen vial into the world's wounds, and what had come through was not a man and not quite a fire but something between: a walking conflagration wearing the suggestion of a crown, melting a road of bare earth through the ice wherever he chose to go. Behind him marched what remained of the Ashen Choir—hundreds strong, their white robes gone gray with ash, singing as they walked. The hymns drifted north on the wind some nights, and Vesper hated how beautiful they were.

"He's not hurrying," Maren said, following her gaze. "Have you noticed? Cindral could cross this distance in a day if he wished. He's keeping pace with us. Like a cat with a mouse it isn't hungry enough to eat yet."

"I've noticed."

"And Isolde?"

"Worse." Vesper flexed her fingers inside her gloves. Since the parley, since the mark Isolde had burned into the air above her—an invisible brand only Vael's lingering presence kept dormant—she had been able to feel the Winter Court the way you feel a storm in an old scar. "Isolde doesn't want to catch us on open ground. She wants us tired. She wants us at the Hearth, with nowhere left to run, when we finally understand there was never anywhere to run *to*."

"That sounds like her."

"You knew her?" Vesper glanced at him. "Before?"

Maren was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. Then: "Everyone who served the old order knew of Lady Isolde. She was mortal once, you know. A queen in the far north whose children starved in a winter her own prayers couldn't end. When Hravn finally answered, he didn't send spring. He sent an offer. And she took it, because grief makes excellent kindling." He shook his head. "I met her once, at a conclave, a hundred years ago. She was the most reasonable person in the room. That's what frightens me about her still."

* * *

On the eleventh day, the Ember spoke.

Vesper had learned its voice by then—not words, exactly, but pressures, images, tides of meaning that broke over her from the inside. That morning, as the column picked its way down into the Rimefield proper, a plain of blue ice so vast it curved visibly with the world, the pressure came sharp and insistent, and with it a knowing: *left.*

"Left," she said aloud, and Doss, without question, called the turn.

An hour later they understood why. The rightward route ran along a shelf of ice above a crevasse field—and as they watched from safe ground, the entire shelf calved away with a sound like the sky cracking, sliding down into darkness in a slow white avalanche. Had they been on it, forty-one would have become eleven, or none.

After that, no one questioned the Ember's nudges. Left. Right. Stop. *Dig here*—and beneath two feet of snow, a pilgrim shelter, roofed in ancient stone, dry and empty and waiting, as if the road itself remembered them.

"The fire remembers the way home," Maren murmured that evening, watching Vesper rub her chest through her coat. "Of course it does. It walked this road when the world was young. You're not navigating, Vess. You're *reminiscing*."

But the Ember's memory had a cost, and Vesper paid it nightly. Each time she let it guide her, the visions came stronger—the burning temple, the theft, the faces of the first thieves wreathed in stolen light. And each time, the mark Isolde had left on her burned a little hotter, as if the Huntress could feel the Ember quickening and pressed closer to the flame.

On the twelfth night, the singing started again—closer now, close enough that the words resolved:

*Burn away, burn away,*
*the chaff of the world—*
*one flame alone shall remain,*
*one throne, one name.*

Sef spat into the snow. "I'm going to have that hymn stuck in my head for the rest of my life. However long that turns out to be."

"However long that turns out to be," Brenna agreed grimly, "is looking shorter by the day."

"They're two days back," Doss said. He'd climbed the waymarker pillar with a spyglass. "Maybe less. The fire melts the road ahead of them—they travel fast and sleep warm. We freeze and crawl." He lowered the glass. "And there's something else on our flank. Northeast. Moving parallel."

Vesper felt it before he finished speaking. The cold in her scarred-over mark had shifted, sharpened, become directional—like a compass needle finding north, except the north was *her*.

"She's not following us," Vesper said. "She's herding us. Look at the geometry. Cindral pushes from the south. The Choir sings us forward. And Isolde rides the eastern flank, bending our path, keeping us on the ice, keeping us in the open—" She stopped. Her breath came out ragged.

"Keeping us where?" Doss asked.

"Where the First Hearth always was." Vesper stared north, where the Rimefield ended against a range of jagged peaks, and among them one summit stood apart—black, bare, untouched by the endless snow, as though the winter itself feared to settle there. "There's only one approach to the temple. One road. One door. She doesn't need to catch us. She just needs to make sure we arrive."

* * *

They paid for the thirteenth day in blood.

It came at the narrows, a defile between two walls of glacier ice where the old pilgrim road squeezed down to six abreast. The Ember had gone quiet all morning—not calm, Vesper realized too late, but *holding its breath*—and the ambush sprang when they were half-committed, the column strung out like beads on a wire.

Not Isolde's knights. Worse: her *made* ones. The frozen dead of the Rimefield, the freeholders from the river, the boy with the surprised face—all of them up and moving now, eyes glazed blue-white, frost pouring off them like smoke, marching in perfect silence with weapons of black ice grown from their fists. Isolde did not waste her own people when the dead marched for free.

"Hold the mouth!" Doss roared, and the company turned and became, in the space of three heartbeats, the thing Vesper had spent twenty years building them into: not heroes, not zealots, just professionals. Shields locked. Pikes down. The narrow defile that threatened to trap them became their fortress, because six abreast meant the dead could only come six at a time, and six at a time, the Company of the Broken Oath had never lost.

But the dead did not tire. Did not bleed. Did not break and run. And every minute the line held, the cold worked deeper into the living—into Sef's ruined hand, into old Tam's lungs, into the gaps in armor and resolve alike.

Vesper fought in the center, and it was there, with an ice-blade skidding off her shield and the dead boy's surprised face inches from hers, that she stopped fighting like a mercenary.

She didn't decide to do it. The Ember decided, or she and the Ember decided together, the line between them worn thin as parchment after thirteen days of shared heartbeat. Light came off her skin—not fire, nothing so crude—a radiance like the moment before dawn, and where it touched the dead, the ice that animated them simply *remembered being water*. The boy crumbled into snow at her feet. Then the woman with the banner. Then rank upon rank, whole lines of the risen falling apart into drifts, and the company surged forward through the meltwater shouting her name—

—and Vesper staggered, blind, the mark on her soul flaring white-hot, because somewhere northeast, close enough now to touch, Lady Isolde had *felt* that. Felt her power spend itself. Felt the exact bearing of the flame.

"Move," Vesper gasped. "Everyone move, *now*, she knows precisely where we—"

They ran the last mile of the defile as the temperature plummeted around them, the air itself crystallizing, frost feathering across eyes and eyelashes, and behind them the snow began to rise—not drifting, *rising*, gathering itself into shapes. Shapes with antlers. Shapes with spears.

Tam didn't make it.

He was sixty-one years old and he had marched with Doss since before Vesper joined the company, and his lungs had never recovered from the Choir's prison. At the mouth of the defile he stumbled, caught himself, stumbled again—and turned, deliberately, planted his pike, and set his back to the rock.

"Go," he said. Not shouted. Said, the way you'd say it across a tavern table. "Someone has to teach the new recruits to drink properly. Go on, then."

They went, because he ordered them to, and because stopping meant his death bought nothing. Vesper looked back exactly once, from the ridge beyond. Tam stood alone against the rising shapes, small and straight-backed, and the last thing she saw was him raising two fingers in a lazy salute before the white closed over him like a curtain.

Doss did not look back at all. But that night, in the shelter the Ember led them to, he sat apart from the fire with Tam's pike across his knees, and he did not sleep, and no one spoke to him, because some payments are private.

* * *

On the fourteenth day, they reached the foot of the mountain, and Vesper understood at last why the winter would not land on it.

The First Hearth did not look like a temple. It looked like a wound that had healed wrong—a great black dome of fused stone rising from the summit, veined with channels of glass where, ages ago, heat had run like blood. No snow touched it. No ice grew on it. The air around it shimmered faintly, the way air shimmers over a forge, though whatever forge had burned here had been cold for longer than nations had existed.

And the road up was exactly what Vesper had known it would be: single file, switchbacked, walled in by cliffs of naked rock. An army could not flank it. An army could not surround it. An army could only follow, one by one, into the throat of the mountain.

"She doesn't need to beat us here," Vesper said, staring up the switchbacks. "She needs to arrive *after* us. Let us open the door. Let the Ember wake fully, the way it must, at the place of its birth. And then she takes it at the moment of its greatest brightness—with Hravn's whole strength behind her, and Cindral arriving from the south to make it three-way, and us—" she gestured at the thirty-nine remaining, exhausted, frostbitten, grieving— "us holding the altar."

Doss studied the road for a long time. Then he said, quietly, "Then we hold the altar."

"Doss—"

"No. Listen to me." He turned, and in the strange shadowless light of the ember-warmed mountain, his eyes were very steady. "Twenty-three years ago, a priest smuggled a burning baby out of a temple and hid her in a valley where the gods couldn't see. Everyone who helped him died or scattered or forgot. And the baby grew up and became the best thing this company ever had." He shrugged, awkward, a man handling something fragile. "We didn't march fourteen days across the end of the world to watch you give up at the doorstep. We hold the altar. All of us. For as long as it lasts."

Behind him, thirty-eight soldiers of the Company of the Broken Oath began, one by one, to check their gear.

Vesper looked at them—at Brenna rolling her maps for the last time, at Sef wrapping his ruined hand, at Maren leaning on his staff with his trembling hands folded flat—and felt the Ember stir behind her sternum, warm and grave and attentive, like a child waking in the dark.

Far to the south, the amber glow pulsed. Closer than yesterday. Closer than an hour ago.

And on the wind, faint but unmistakable, carried up the mountainside from the plains below, came the sound of hooves—hundreds of them, riding through snow that did not crunch, silent as falling frost. The Winter Court had arrived at the foot of the mountain, and dismounted, and begun, with terrible patience, to climb.

Vesper turned toward the door of the First Hearth, drew her sword, and led them up into the light.
