## 35. Battle of the Burning Cradle

The temple rose out of the ice like a ribcage out of a whale's corpse.

Vesper had seen it first from the ridge, three days' march behind them now, and even then—half-blind with wind, her breath freezing in the wool over her mouth—she had known it for what it was. Not because Maren had described it. Not because the murals in the buried temple beneath the pass had shown its silhouette in faded ochre. She had known it the way you know your own heartbeat: the Burning Cradle, the First Hearth, the place where fire had been stolen from something older than gods. The snow refused to settle on it. Around its broken colonnade the drifts lay in a perfect ring, as if the world itself had flinched away.

Now she stood inside that ring, on cracked black stone that was warm through her boots, and watched the horizon fill with white.

"They're not marching," Doss said beside her. He had his spyglass up, though there was no need. You could see the winter host with the naked eye—a tide of pale figures moving across the glacier plain without disturbing the snow, without leaving tracks, without any rhythm a living army kept. "Marching has order to it. This is just *weather*."

"How many?"

"Does it matter?" He lowered the glass. His face was gray with cold and something else. "We can't kill weather, Vesper. We can only stand in front of it for a while."

She looked back at the temple doors. Behind them, in the heart of the ruined nave, the Ember waited—or rather, *she* waited, the thing that had lived in her chest for twenty-three years, the spark every god alive had been lit from and every god dead had been murdered for. Vael's voice was quiet in her now, quieter than it had been since he'd poured himself into the valley to break Sarelle's rite. He had spent most of what he was doing that. What remained of him felt less like a god and more like an old man leaning on her shoulder, whispering directions.

*The altar room,* he had said. *It will try to seal itself against you. It remembers being stolen from.*

"Then we hold the colonnade," Vesper said. "As long as we can. Long enough for me to reach the altar."

"And after the altar?"

"After the altar," she said, "there doesn't have to be an after. That's the point."

Doss looked at her for a long moment. Twenty years he'd carried her in his company—first as a foundling with a blank medallion who ate more than any two recruits, then as the best blade he'd ever commanded, then as whatever she was now, a girl-shaped hearth with a god cooling in her chest. He didn't say *be careful*. He didn't say *I'm proud of you*. He said:

"Brenna's got the east arch. Tam and the twins are on the rubble slope. I put Yeva in the nave with Maren—if anything gets past us, they're the last door." He rolled his shoulders, and Vesper heard the old injury in his left one click, the same sound it had made the day he'd pulled her out of a burning waystation when she was nine. "Company of the Broken Oath. Worst name in the mercenary rolls. Best company in the world."

"Worst name," Vesper agreed. Her hand found his forearm, gripped once. "Best company."

Out on the plain, the white tide reached the ring of bare stone—and stopped, all at once, like a wave meeting glass.

Then it began to climb.

* * *

Doss had fought in eleven battles, and he had learned that every battle had a sound. The first was always the worst: not the screaming, which came later, but the sound before—the creak of leather, the rattle of breath, the small human noises men made when they were trying to convince their bodies to stay where their minds had ordered them. He stood on the great stair of the Burning Cradle with forty-one souls around him, listening to that sound, and thought: *this is the last time I'll hear it.*

Because this wasn't a battle. Battles ended. This was a wall standing in front of a flood, and everyone on the wall knew it.

They came up the stair without footfalls. That was the horror of them—not the frost-rimed faces, not the eyes like chips of blue glass, but the silence. Isolde's host were the frozen dead of a hundred winters, villagers and soldiers and pilgrims taken mid-step by the Long Winter and set walking again, and they moved the way snow moved: total, patient, indifferent. Doss put his axe into the first one's throat and felt the blow land like chopping river ice. The thing did not fall. It took his axe with both hands and held it there, frozen into its own flesh, while the second one came past it with a rusted spear.

Tam saved him. Tam, who was nineteen and had joined the company for three meals a day and a coat, drove his sword through the spearman's spine and screamed the whole time—not a war cry, just noise, just a boy making noise so he wouldn't hear how quiet everything else was.

"Hold the stair!" Doss roared, and the company of the Broken Oath held the stair.

They held it for an hour. Doss would remember later that it felt like a year and also like nothing at all, the way the worst hours do. Brenna fought on the east arch with two swords and no fear, laughing—actually laughing—until the moment she stopped laughing, which was the moment four of the dead took her arms and legs and walked her backward off the arch into the dark below, and her laughter cut off like a snuffed candle. The twins held the rubble slope by rolling boulders, which worked beautifully until the boulders ran out, and then it worked the way everything else worked: for a while.

Yeva died at the nave door. Doss saw it happen from thirty feet away and could do nothing—was pinned, was bleeding, was holding a line with six people where twenty had stood that morning. Yeva went down under five of the frozen dead with Maren's name in her mouth, because Maren was behind her, because Yeva had promised the old archivist she'd keep the last door, and she kept it. She kept it until she couldn't, and then Maren closed it himself, from inside, and barred it, and Doss understood with a clarity that felt like cold water that the old man meant to die in there if it came to that.

And still they came. And still the company held.

It was somewhere in the second hour, spitting blood onto black warm stone, that Doss saw the light change on the plain below—and saw, walking untouched through her own army, a woman in white with a crown of frost, coming up the stair herself.

Isolde had come to watch the door close.

* * *

Sarelle had been dead for some time before her body agreed to notice.

She knelt at the edge of the battlefield, in the shadow of a fallen colossus whose carved face had been worn to anonymity by ten thousand winters, and she watched Cindral walk through Isolde's host, and she finally—finally—understood what she had spent her whole life serving.

He wore Prelate Ondrus today. Yesterday he had worn a choirboy she remembered teaching to read. The god of the Ashen Choir moved from body to body the way a man tries on gloves, and each one lasted a little less long, burned a little greyer, and Sarelle had told herself for years that this was holy—that to be worn by Cindral was the highest sacrament, that the greying corpses left behind were vessels emptied into glory. She had written the liturgy for it. She had composed the hymn.

But she had carried a vial of Vesper Thackeray's blood out of Hollowmere, and she had used it to call her god fully into the world, and she had seen, in the moment of his manifestation, what was actually inside the glove.

Hunger. Nothing else. No love of ash, no grief for the burnt world, none of the beautiful sorrow the Choir sang about at evensong. Just appetite wearing her god's name, the way Cindral wore Ondrus's face. The Ember was the fire all gods were lit from. Cindral did not want to worship it. Cindral wanted to eat it, and then eat Hravn, and then eat the world down to the wick—and Sarelle understood, kneeling in the snow with her hands too cold to feel, that when the last thing was eaten, the eater would turn to the nearest remaining fuel.

Her. Her faith. Her self. The hymns she'd written would make fine kindling.

"Cindral," she said aloud, and her voice cracked on it, and she almost laughed at that—twenty-three years of devotion and her voice broke like a girl's.

The god turned. He was fifty feet away, wearing a dying acolyte named Pell, and Pell's face was already going grey, and the eyes that opened in it were furnace-cracks, orange light through black iron.

*Little flame,* he said, and his voice was everywhere, in the snow, in her teeth, in the vial of blood against her breastbone. *You brought me to the threshold. Come. Be the first consumed. It is an honor I have reserved for you.*

Every doctrine she had ever taught said: kneel. Every hymn said: yes. Sarelle knelt—but not the way the liturgy meant it. She drew the knife she had used to bleed a null-born in a ritual tent in Hollowmere, and she unstoppered the second vial, the one no one knew about, the one filled not with the girl's blood but with her own, given freely, blessed with every rite of consecration the Choir possessed.

A vessel, the doctrines said, must be pure. A vessel must be willing. She had never understood why those two lines sat side by side in the canon, until now, when she finally saw what they were *for*.

"You want a door," Sarelle said. "Here is one."

She drove the knife into her own heart, and she poured herself out—not her blood only, but everything, her ordination, her certainty, her twenty-three years of ash-colored prayer, all of it offered through the oldest rite in the Choir's hidden canon, the rite of *vessel-taking*, the one that let a consecrated soul bind a god to the shape of her own dying.

Cindral screamed.

It was not a sound. It was a *stopping*. Across the whole glacier plain, the winter host froze—truly froze, mid-stride, mid-slaughter—as the god wearing Pell's corpse convulsed and shrank and was dragged, howling, down into the small burning prison of Sarelle's sacrifice. The orange light guttered. The snow for a mile around went red as sunset.

In that stopped moment, on the great stair, Doss raised his head and shouted the only order that mattered:

"NOW! Vesper—*go!*"

* * *

Vesper ran.

She ran through a battlefield gone suddenly silent, over ice glazed with her enemy's blood-red light, past Tam kneeling over the twins, past Brenna's empty arch, past Yeva's body at the nave door with Maren's bar rattling as the old man threw it open from within. She ran into the heart of the Burning Cradle, into the nave where the murals climbed the walls—the theft of fire, the murder of the maker, the gods with their hands red in the oldest painting in the world—and at the far end, past the broken pews of a congregation dead ten thousand years, the altar waited.

It was a hearth. Of course it was a hearth. A great black hearthstone, cracked down the middle, cold as the grave, and above it an empty iron cradle-hook where the first fire had once hung.

Behind her, faintly, the silence broke. Cindral was tearing free of Sarelle's binding—it would take him minutes, perhaps only one—and beyond him Isolde's voice rose over the plain, furious and ringing, calling her host back into motion. Minutes. She had minutes.

Vesper knelt at the hearthstone and pressed both hands flat against the crack.

*I'm here,* she said—to the Ember, to the temple, to whatever was listening. *You know me. You've been in me since before I had a name.*

The stone was warm. Then hot. Then—

The crack under her palms widened, breathing out a smell of woodsmoke and milk and something older than either, and Vael's voice came thin and tired at her ear, almost a whisper, almost an apology:

*Mirabel. Whatever you choose in the next few moments—choose it as yourself. Not as my vessel. Not as anyone's.*

"I know," she said.

The hearthstone split.

And out of the dark below the altar—out of the cradle where fire had been born and stolen and mourned—rose the Ember, whole and blazing, the last living spark of the pre-divine flame, hovering in the air before her like a heart torn from the world's chest. It was smaller than she had imagined. It was the size of a coal from a home fire. It was the most beautiful thing she had ever seen, and it beat in time with her pulse, because for twenty-three years it *had* been her pulse.

Behind her, at the far end of the nave, the doors burst open in a bloom of frost.

Isolde stood in the doorway, alone, her host stilled at her back, her crown of frost glittering, her eyes fixed on the fire with an expression Vesper had seen only once before—on her own face, in a tavern mirror, the night she learned her god's voice was real.

"Sarelle is dead," Isolde said. Her voice did not carry; it simply arrived, the way cold arrives. "She bound your devourer for the length of a death, and it cost her everything, and she did it so that you could stand exactly where you are standing." She stepped into the nave, and the frost came with her, climbing the pillars, reaching for the murals. "So. Null-born. Thief-child. Hearth-girl. Whatever you are—" her hand rose, and the temperature of the world began to fall, "—the fire comes with me, or it comes with no one. Choose."

Vesper rose to her feet, the Ember burning between her hands, and chose.
