## 36. Isolde at the Altar

The temple had been burning for twenty-three years, and it was still burning.

Not with flame—Vesper understood that now—but with memory. The fire that had made the gods had never gone out here; it had only sunk so deep into the stone that the world above forgot to look for it. She felt it through her boots as she climbed the last of the shattered stair, a warmth like standing on the lid of a sleeping heart, and every step closer to the altar made the thing inside her chest lean toward it the way a child leans toward a lit window in winter.

Behind her, far below, the Battle of the Burning Cradle roared and cracked through the ruined colonnades—steel, screaming, the shriek of ice meeting fire. Doss's voice somewhere in it, hoarse and bellowing orders. Sarelle's acolytes chanting themselves hoarse against Hravn's white host. She could not help them from here. She had known that since the pass, since the frozen dead, since Maren's blood on the snow: everything below was bought time, purchased in names she loved, and the only coin she carried worth spending was herself.

So she climbed.

The altar chamber opened at the temple's crown like a wound in the sky. The roof had fallen centuries ago; snow drifted down through the broken vault in long, slow spirals, and none of it melted until it touched the floor—and there it did not melt either. It lay in drifts around a circle of black basalt, untouched, because the cold that guarded this place was not weather. It was older than weather. It was *hers*.

Isolde stood at the center of the circle, at the altar itself.

Vesper had imagined this moment a hundred times down the long march north, and in none of those imaginings did Isolde look like this. She had pictured armor, frost, the terrible pageantry of the Long Winter's avatar. Instead the woman wore plain grey wool, travel-stained, her white hair loose down her back, and she was looking at the altar the way Vesper had once looked at Vael's silent medallion—with the particular grief of someone addressing a thing that would not answer.

"You came alone," Isolde said, without turning. Her voice carried strangely in the broken vault, neither echoing nor fading, as if the room were holding its breath around it. "I wondered if you would. The others fight so loudly for you. I thought you might let them die for the privilege of keeping you."

"They're not dying for me." Vesper came down into the circle, boots crunching frost that crunched *wrong*, too loud, too sharp, like walking on old glass. "They're dying because I asked them to hold a door. You're the thing behind it."

"And yet here you are, behind it with me." Isolde turned then, and up close her eyes were the worst part—not white, not blue, but the pale grey of a sky before snowfall, eyes that had watched centuries accumulate like drifts. "Do you know how long I have waited at altars? Do you know how many chosen vessels I have outlived? They all come eventually. Hope is patient. So am I."

"I'm not here to hope."

"No," Isolde agreed softly. "You're here because you've finally understood what everyone who carries fire must understand—that you cannot keep it, and you cannot give it away, and the only mercy left is to choose whose hands it burns." She lifted one hand, and frost bloomed across her knuckles like lichen across stone. "I have watched your kind for six hundred years, null-born. Every god still breathing stole something to be alive. Hravn took his winter from the corpse of the first fire and called it law. Cindral took flame and called it faith. Even your quiet Vael—" something flickered across her face, contempt or envy, hard to say, "—took silence, and wore it like a virtue. And you. You are the theft itself. The last unspent piece of the original crime."

"Then you know what I'm going to say."

"I know what you *think* you're going to say." Isolde began to walk the circle's edge, unhurried, and where her footsteps fell the frost thickened, climbing the basalt in feathered plates. "You'll offer me truth. They always do, at the end. As if truth were a blade and I an unbaptized peasant. I have read the murals, child. I helped *carve* some of them, in centuries when I still bothered to lie to myself about what my god was. I know Hravn killed the firemaker. I know he drank the first hearth dry and froze what remained. I have made my peace with being the heir of thieves."

"Have you?" Vesper stopped walking. The warmth under her feet was rising now, climbing her shins, her ribs, pooling behind her sternum where the Ember sat like a second heart. "Because I stood in the chamber below this one three days ago, and I saw something the murals don't show unless you kneel in the ash and look with your hands."

Isolde paused.

"There's a figure in the frieze," Vesper went on, quietly, letting the words come the way Vael's voice used to come—slow, inevitable, terrible in its gentleness. "Everyone always looks at the thief with the torch. That's the story the gods left standing. But there's a second figure. Smaller. Behind him. And she isn't fighting him. She's *holding the door open*. Holding it open while he carries the fire out, and weeping, because she knows what taking it will cost, and she does it anyway, because she loves him more than she loves what's right."

The silence that followed had weight. Snow spiraled down through the broken roof between them.

"That's not in any version I know," Isolde said at last, and her voice had changed by a single degree—the way ice changes just before it decides whether to hold or crack.

"It's in the oldest layer. Under the paint the priests added later. Maren showed me how to find it—you scrape back the gilt and there she is. The doorkeeper." Vesper took a step closer, and the warmth in her chest flared; frost retreated from the basalt between them in a slow, widening ring, and Isolde looked down at it and did not retreat. "And do you know what the doorkeeper's name is, carved beneath her feet in the first-cut letters? Not a title. A name. *Hrim.*"

Something moved behind Isolde's grey eyes—something vast and very old shifting its weight in a small room.

"Hravn's first avatar," Vesper said. "His first chosen. The woman who held the door. He didn't steal the fire alone, Isolde. He couldn't have. And when the theft was done, when the other gods rose up hunting the arsonist, he didn't shield her. He *froze her*. Sealed her in the first winter so no one could ever question her about what they'd done together. Your god didn't make you his heir. He made you his *accomplice*, over and over, century after century, and he kept you cold so you'd never thaw enough to remember why that word feels wrong in your mouth."

"You're lying." It came out flat, almost bored, and that was how Vesper knew it had landed. "This is the oldest trick of vessels—to flatter, to divide, to plant doubt in the servant so the master stumbles."

"Then touch me."

The words hung in the vault air.

"You told me on the White Pass," Vesper said, "that your god speaks through your skin. That anything you grip, you can freeze from the inside. So grip me. Read me. The Ember doesn't hide its memories—I've seen them, Isolde. The burning temple. The door. *Her face.* If I'm lying, you'll know in a heartbeat, and you can take the fire out of my corpse at your leisure. But if I'm telling the truth—"

"If you're telling the truth," Isolde said slowly, "then everything I am has been a leash."

"Now you understand what my whole life felt like."

For a long moment neither of them moved. Below them the battle thundered on, muffled, distant, another world. Snow fell through the broken sky. And Vesper stood with her arms open at her sides, palms out, offering the only thing she had ever truly owned—the truth of what burned in her—and waited to see whether the avatar of the Long Winter was brave enough to look at it.

Isolde crossed the circle.

Her bare hand closed around Vesper's wrist, and the cold came in like a tide—Vesper gasped, knees buckling, frost racing up her forearm, the Ember flaring wildly in answer, two ancient fires colliding in the narrow channel of her veins—and then Isolde went *still*.

Vesper watched it happen. Watched the certainty drain out of that grey face the way color drains from a dying man. Because Isolde wasn't reading Vesper's memories. She was reading the Ember's, and the Ember remembered everything, and the Ember had been *there*—had been the fire in the torch, had seen the door held open, had felt the doorkeeper's tears land hot on its surface as Hravn carried it into the dark.

"*Hrim,*" Isolde whispered. Her hand shook. Frost cracked along her sleeve and fell away in flakes. "That was—she was the first of us, the first avatar, they told us she *failed*, they told us she was punished for weakness—"

"She was punished for knowing." Vesper's voice was rough; the cold in her arm ached down to the bone. "They always punish the ones who know. Ask Maren. Ask me."

Isolde released her wrist and staggered back a step—an avatar of an elder god, staggering—and pressed both hands over her mouth, and for one unguarded moment she looked exactly like what she presumably had once been: a woman from a village that stayed too long in winter, chosen because she was strong enough to survive it, kept because she was useful, frozen because she was dangerous.

"He let me believe," she said through her fingers. "Six hundred years. Six hundred years of avatars, and he let every one of us believe we were heirs. Legate. Daughter of the law of winter. And all along we were—" She lowered her hands, and her eyes were wet, and the wetness froze on her lashes and glittered like the whole cruel beautiful court she had built. "We were the door, held open. Held open so *he* wouldn't have to look at what he'd done."

"Yes."

"And you're offering me—" She laughed, and it was a terrible sound, thin as wind off a glacier. "What? Absolution? I have frozen children in their beds, null-born. I have ended bloodlines. I marched an army across the world to carve the fire out of your chest. There is no absolution large enough—"

"I'm not offering absolution." Vesper flexed her fingers, feeling feeling flood painfully back into the frostbitten arm. "I'm offering you the same choice everyone gets, starting tonight. That's all the Ember ever was, Isolde. Not a throne. Not a weapon. A *choice*, taken away from the world by thieves who wanted to be the only ones making it. Hravn took it. Cindral took it. Vael hid from it. And you—" she stepped forward, into the space between them, close enough to see the frozen tears, "—you've spent six hundred years mistaking obedience for purpose. I know that road. I walked it for twenty years wearing a dead god's medallion, calling my leash a calling."

Below them, the battle's sound changed—a great cracking boom, a chorus of shouting—and neither of them turned to look.

"So what happens now?" Isolde's voice had gone quiet. Dangerous still, but quiet—the quiet of a drawn bow deciding. "You think one mural unmakes an eternal winter? My god is coming. He felt you touch his memories through my skin. He is coming *here*, and he will pour himself into this valley, and when he arrives I will either be restored to him or I will be the first thing he freezes, and I no longer know which I want—"

"Then choose," Vesper said. "Right now. Before he comes. Choose which one you are—the door again, or the woman who walks through it."

Isolde stared at her.

And high above the broken vault, past the falling snow, past the ragged edge of the ruined sky, the air itself began to pale—to whiten, to thicken, to fill with a pressure like the whole weight of a season leaning on one point of the world. The temperature plunged so fast that Vesper's breath tore white from her lips. Frost raced inward across the basalt from every direction at once, converging on the altar, converging on them, and in the heart of that convergence Vesper felt the Ember recoil like a living thing before a predator, and she knew, with absolute clarity, that Hravn had heard everything.

Isolde looked up at the whitening sky. Then down at her own hands—hands that had held a door open for six hundred years without once being asked.

When she spoke, her voice was barely audible over the rising roar of the coming winter, and it was not addressed to Vesper at all.

"*No,*" she said—to the sky, to the cold, to the god who had made her. "*Not this time. Find your own door.*"

And the Long Winter's avatar stepped to Vesper's side, and turned to face the descending god with her.

* * *

The cold arrived like a verdict.

It did not fall; it *descended*—a column of absolute white punching down through the broken roof, filling the vault, erasing the snowfall, erasing the stars, erasing the distinction between air and ice. Vesper threw her arms across her face and felt the Ember blaze up her throat, melting a bubble of survivable warmth around them both, and through the white glare she saw him.

Hravn did not wear a body so much as consent to one—a towering figure of packed snow and black ice, crowned in auroral light, eyes like the gap between stars. When he spoke, it was not sound but the sensation of deep lakes freezing, heard from underneath.

***YOU SHOWED HER THE DOOR.***

"He showed me nothing," Isolde shouted into the gale, standing shoulder to shoulder with Vesper, her own frost rising to meet her god's. "I *looked*. There's a difference, old one. You taught me that much and never knew it."

***SHE IS MY FIRST AND MY LAST. SHE WILL KNEEL OR SHE WILL BE THE FOUNDATION OF MY NEW WINTER.***

"She was never yours." Vesper planted her feet on the warm stone. The Ember roared in her chest, and for the first time in her life she did not try to quiet it. "None of us were. That's what you never forgave the firemaker for understanding—that fire divided is not diminished. You stole it because you couldn't bear that. And you froze her because she could."

Hravn raised one colossal hand, and the world went white—

—and Vesper reached for the only thing left, the thing she had sworn in the ashes of Hollowmere she would never use as a weapon, and poured everything into it: not fire, not force, but *memory*. The Ember's memory, flung wide open, blazing outward like a beacon—the burning temple, the theft, the doorkeeper's tears, the gods' long guilty flight, every stolen thing laid naked in the light for the descending god to see himself in.

Hravn stopped.

The column of winter hung motionless above them, and in its frozen silence Vesper heard—or felt—the vast slow grinding of a mind six thousand years old encountering, at last, the one thing it had built an eternity of cold to avoid looking at.

The reflection lasted three heartbeats.

Then the white column *shattered*—not in rage but in something worse, something like a god flinching—and the pressure lifted, and the aurora guttered, and the presence withdrew upward and outward, retreating from the temple, from the valley, from the sight of its own hands, leaving behind a silence so complete that Vesper could hear the snow landing.

She sagged against the altar, laughing breathlessly, half sobbing. Beside her, Isolde stood very straight, watching the sky where her god had been, and her frozen tears were melting now, running freely down a face that had not wept in six centuries.

"He ran," she said wonderingly. "He actually ran. From a memory."

"Not from a memory." Vesper pushed herself upright. Below them, faintly, the sounds of battle were faltering—dying, changing, the white host losing its animating will as its god fled the field. "From what comes next. Because he knows what I know now, Isolde. He knows I'm not going to give the fire to anyone. Not to him, not to Vael, not to Cindral, not to the Choir. Which means there's only one thing left for me to do with it—and every god still standing is about to realize it at the same moment."

Isolde turned to her, and in her melting-grey eyes there was something new—not allegiance, not yet, maybe never, but the raw beginning of a question she intended to spend whatever remained of her immortal life answering.

"What are you going to do?"

Vesper looked down at her own hands—at the ordinary, scarred, mercenary hands that had carried the first fire of the world up a mountain and refused every throne offered for it.

"I'm going to give it back," she said. "To everyone. All at once."

And far below, in the smoking ruins of the Burning Cradle, the Ember's light began—gently, irresistibly, like dawn through a broken roof—to spread.
