## 2. Wren's Empty Eyes

I told myself the light in the shrine had been a trick of altitude. Cartographers are prone to that — we spend so long bending the world onto paper that when the world refuses to bend, we assume the fault is ours. Three days I'd spent descending from the Thornfell passes, and for three days I'd been composing increasingly elaborate explanations. Reflected sunlight off a mica seam. Marsh gas with theatrical ambitions. A trick of the smugglers who used the shrine as a cache point, some new lantern-oil that burned colors oil has no business burning.

By the time Mirabel's Garden came into view below me — a scatter of slate roofs in a green fold of the mountains, smoke rising straight and domestic from a dozen chimneys — I had almost convinced myself.

Almost. Which is the cartographer's curse. *Almost* is the space between the map and the territory, and it is where every bad decision I have ever made was born.

The village hadn't changed. That was the first wrong thing. Villages change; they lean, they sag, they grow a new barn or lose one to fire. But Mirabel's Garden sat in its valley exactly as it had six weeks ago, when I'd passed through on my way up into the high passes, and it sat there with the stillness of a thing holding its breath.

* * *

I'll be honest about what happened six weeks ago, since honesty is apparently the theme the gods have chosen for my season.

Six weeks ago I'd stopped at the Garden to resupply. Old habits: I always stop there, because the village sits at the mouth of three unmapped valleys and because Marta Hale keeps the only honest ale within two days' walk. And while I drank that ale, a child had come and stood beside my table and watched me draw.

Her name was Wren. I knew that much because everyone in the Garden knew everyone, and children there were raised to speak when spoken to but not otherwise. She didn't speak. She stood at my elbow, thin as a winter branch, maybe nine years old, with a smudge of ash on her cheekbone that she either didn't know about or had decided was permanent, and she watched my pen move across the paper with an attention most adults never manage.

"Something you want?" I'd asked her, not unkindly, but not kindly either. I was working. There were passes to chart before the snows.

She shook her head. Then she pointed at the blank space on my map where the Thornfell interior went — the great white nothing at the center of every map ever made of those mountains — and she tapped the table twice, deliberate, like a knock on a door.

Then she walked out, and I finished my ale, and I left the next morning without thinking about her again.

I want you to understand that this is the part I keep returning to. Not the shrine. Not even what came after. The tap on the table. Twice. Like a knock. Children do strange things, I told myself then. Children are weather; they blow through and mean nothing by it. I had roads to chart and a reputation to maintain as the woman who doesn't get distracted.

Six weeks later, I would have given both hands for the chance to ask her what door she'd been knocking on.

* * *

Marta Hale's tavern smelled the same — woodsmoke, spilled ale, the ghost of last night's stew — but the silence in it was new. A tavern at midday should have murmur in it, the low commerce of talk. This one had twelve people sitting in it drinking like men drinking through a funeral, which, I was beginning to suspect, they were.

Marta saw me and her face did something complicated. Relief and dread fighting for the same ground.

"Vesper Reyes." She said it loud enough for the room. "Back down from the mountain."

"Down and mostly intact." I set my pack against the bar. "Pour me something and tell me why your village looks like someone died."

Nobody laughed. In a healthy tavern, somebody laughs at that line even if nobody's died. The silence got worse.

Marta poured. Her hands weren't steady. "You came through six weeks back," she said. "You remember."

"I remember."

"You remember Wren Hale." My stomach did something slow and cold, because Marta's surname was Hale too, and I suddenly understood whose child I'd ignored over my ale. "My sister's girl. She took sick in the spring. Fever, three days, wouldn't break. Then it broke, all at once, and she got up out of bed fine as rain."

"That sounds like good news."

"She got up fine," Marta said, "and she hasn't been *right* since. You've seen her. You saw her yourself, standing at your table like a little ghost."

"I thought she was shy."

"Wren was never shy in her life. Wren talked the ears off a stone." Marta's voice cracked and she pressed it flat again. "She walks around. She does her chores. She eats what you put in front of her. But there's nobody home, Vesper. You look in her eyes and it's like looking down a well with no bottom and no water. Just depth. Her mother prays over her every night at the old shrine up the ridge. Whole village prays. Nothing."

"And now something," I said. Because I had seen the something. I had seen it burn impossible colors three days ago and spent the descent lying to myself about it.

Marta went very still. "What did you see?"

* * *

They took me to see Wren before they'd let me tell them anything, which was fair. If I'd been them, I'd have wanted proof the stranger wasn't mad before trusting her with madness.

Wren was in the yard behind her mother's house, stacking firewood. Stacking it perfectly, each piece aligned, each row true, with the mechanical diligence of a machine built for exactly one purpose. Her mother — Sera, small and gray-faced, with the particular exhaustion of someone who has cried herself out months ago and now just watches — stood in the doorway and didn't call out to her.

"Wren," Sera said finally, softly. "There's a visitor."

The girl turned. And there it was, the thing Marta had tried to describe and couldn't: the eyes. They were dark and clear and utterly vacant, not empty the way a fool's eyes are empty but empty the way a house is empty when the family has moved out and taken everything, even the curtains. Something was operating behind them. It knew how to stack wood. It knew how to breathe and blink and turn its head when addressed. But whatever had once looked out of those eyes — the quickness, the mischief, the child — had gone somewhere else.

Or been taken somewhere else.

"Hello, Wren," I said. "Do you remember me? I drew maps at your aunt's table."

She looked at me. She looked at me the way you look at furniture. Then her gaze slid past my shoulder, up the ridge, toward the shrine, and stayed there. Fixed. Hungry, almost, if hunger can be expressed by a total absence of expression.

"She does that," Sera whispered. "Every day since the fever broke. She looks up there like she's waiting for something to come down."

Three nights ago, something had come down. Or lit up. Or answered.

I crouched to the girl's level, which put me in the direct line of that vacant stare, and I did the thing I do instead of praying, which is observe. Her breathing was even. Her pulse, when I took her wrist — gently, and Sera flinched but allowed it — was steady and slow. Warm skin. Living body. Every sign of health except the one that matters, the one no physician has a name for because physicians deal in bodies and this was a problem of tenancy.

On the inside of her wrist, below the pulse point, there was a mark. Small, pale, like an old scar in the shape of a knot or a seal — a circle with lines running through it, precise as engraving. I'd have called it a birthmark if birthmarks came with margins that neat.

"When did that appear?" I asked.

Sera stared. "That's — she was born with that. We always thought—"

"No," I said quietly, because I was looking at the edges of it, and edges are my trade. Birthmarks blur. This had been cut clean, recently, into skin that remembered being unmarked. "No, she wasn't."

* * *

They gathered in Marta's tavern that evening — the whole village, or near enough, packed in among the tables with their fear worn openly now that a stranger had named it. I told them about the shrine. The light that burned upward and inward at once, the color I couldn't name because it kept refusing to stay one color, the way the air had tasted afterward: metallic, sweet, like licking a coin. I told them I'd spent three days pretending I hadn't seen it.

"Why lie?" asked the miller, a broad man named Corren.

"Because I'm a mapmaker, and mapmakers don't believe in things that aren't on maps." I turned my cup on the table. "It's a professional failing. We decide what the world is allowed to do."

An old woman near the fire spoke up. Tam, they called her; she kept the shrine, swept it, left offerings nobody had believed in for generations. "The old gods answered," she said simply. "First time in two hundred years. Maybe more. My grandmother's grandmother said the shrines went quiet in her day, all at once, everywhere. We kept tending them anyway. Habit. Respect." She looked at me with wet, fierce eyes. "Sera prayed hardest. Every night. Give her back, give her back, give her back. And the god heard, and the god answered."

"Answered how?" I asked. "The girl's still hollow."

Tam's face folded. "That's what I keep saying. What kind of answer is this?"

And there it was, the question underneath everything, the one I could feel the whole village leaning on: what kind of god answers a prayer by taking the thing you prayed for?

I had no answer. What I had was a mark on a child's wrist, a shrine that burned wrong, and a memory I'd been avoiding for six weeks: a thin girl tapping twice on my table like a knock on a door, pointing at the blank heart of the Thornfells, at the white space where no map had ever gone.

Children are weather, I'd told myself. They mean nothing by it.

But what if she hadn't been weather? What if she'd been a warning, delivered the only way a soulless child can deliver one — by finding the one person in the village whose entire profession was paying attention to places other people ignore?

I had walked past her. I had finished my ale.

"Grief makes people see patterns," Corren said heavily. "Maybe the fever just... broke something. Maybe there's no god in it at all."

"There's a god in it," I said. My voice came out flatter than I intended. "I don't know which one, and I don't know why it answered cruelly, but the mark on her wrist isn't a sickness and the light on that ridge wasn't marsh gas. Someone answered Sera's prayer. Someone is holding whatever used to live inside your daughter, and they're holding it under a seal." I looked around the room, at the faces lit orange by the fire, at the hope and horror tangled together in every one of them. "And I think I know how to find out who."

"How?" Marta asked.

"The hard way." I drained my cup. "I'm going to go ask it."

* * *

Later, past midnight, I climbed the ridge alone. Professional habit: I wanted to see the shrine again, in the dark, without witnesses, and take its measure properly this time. No lies about mica seams. No professional skepticism. I brought paper and charcoal, because that's how I pray.

The shrine was a low stone structure, older than the village, its walls furred with moss, its lintel carved with figures worn nearly smooth. Inside, the offering bowl still held ash from the impossible fire — ash that glittered faintly, like snow made of embers. The air tasted of coin again. Of bargains.

I knelt — awkwardly; kneeling is not in my repertoire — and spread my paper on the cold floor, and began to draw the shrine. The walls. The worn carvings. And as my charcoal moved, I noticed the figures on the lintel resolving under my attention: not decorative spirals, as I'd assumed years ago, but mouths. Row upon row of small carved mouths, open, singing or speaking or screaming, and beneath them, in a script older than any alphabet I knew, a single line of text that my craft translated without my permission, the way craft sometimes does when it's older than the craftsman:

*WHAT IS GIVEN IN FEAR IS OWED IN FULL.*

I sat back on my heels. Somewhere below, the village slept, and in it a girl stacked perfect firewood with borrowed hands, and somewhere above, the moon hung in its sky doing whatever moons do when they're counting.

Because that was the other thing I'd noticed, the thing I hadn't said in the tavern. The moon had a hairline of shadow on its lower edge tonight. A sliver. As if something had begun to eat it, patiently, one turn at a time.

Thirteen turns, said something in the back of my skull, in a voice that sounded uncomfortably like my mother's. Thirteen turns until it's whole again. Thirteen turns to make things right.

I didn't know yet whose voice that was, or why it knew things I didn't. I only knew that I had ignored a child once already, and that ignoring things was a debt too, and debts — the lintel was very clear — are owed in full.

I rolled up my drawing, and behind me, in the empty shrine, one of the carved mouths exhaled.
