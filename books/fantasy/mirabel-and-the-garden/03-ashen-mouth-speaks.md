## 3. Ashen-Mouth Speaks

I did not sleep that night. I want to say it was because of the shrine, the light, the impossible geometry of a prayer being answered in real time — but honestly, half of it was the tea. Sefa's tea. She'd pressed a pouch of it on me at the crossroads two weeks back with the solemnity of a woman dispensing relics, and I'd been drinking it out of politeness ever since. It tasted like boiled pine needles and regret. It kept me awake the way guilt keeps you awake: persistently, and without offering anything useful in return.

So I sat in my camp above the pass with my maps spread around me like a losing hand of cards, and I told myself stories.

The shrine-light was marsh gas. The shrine-light was a reflection off mica. The shrine-light was some smuggler's new lantern-oil, cut with something strange, and tomorrow I'd find the still and laugh about it over brandy. These were good stories. Professional stories. I had built a career on them — *there is always a road, and every road has an explanation* — and they had never once failed me.

They failed me at dawn, when I came down to the shrine and found the stone still warm.

Not warm like sun-warmth. Warm like a hand that has just been holding yours. The little cairn of stacked rocks — one of hundreds scattered through Thornfell, the kind travelers add a pebble to for luck and thieves knock over for sport — was glowing faintly at its seams, as if the mortar between the world's stones had gone luminous. And pressed into the ash at its base were footprints. Bare feet. Human-sized, human-shaped, leading away down the slope toward the valley.

Toward Mirabel's Garden.

I stood there for a long moment, doing the arithmetic no cartographer wants to do. Distance from shrine to village: six miles. Time since the light: perhaps nine hours. Speed of a walking god, or whatever walked like one: leisurely. Which meant that whatever had answered Wren Hollis's prayer had arrived in Mirabel's Garden while I'd been up here telling myself comforting lies about marsh gas.

I packed my camp in four minutes flat. A personal record. I did not look back at the shrine, mostly because I was afraid it would be looking at me.

* * *

Mirabel's Garden at midmorning looked exactly as it always did, which was the problem. Smoke from breakfast fires. Chickens conducting their ongoing coup against the miller's fence. Old Marta beating a rug with the rhythm of a woman who has been beating rugs since before the war and intends to continue after the next one. Life, proceeding. Unaware or unbothered.

I went straight to the Hollis house, because where else does a debt point?

Wren was in the yard. That was the first wrongness — not something dramatic, just a child standing in a garden at an hour when children are usually underfoot in kitchens. She stood among the bean rows with her arms at her sides, face tilted up toward the sun, and she did not move. Not swaying, not shifting weight, not doing any of the hundred small motions living bodies do when they think no one is watching. She was still the way furniture is still.

"Wren," I said, from the gate.

Her head turned. Smoothly. All at once, like a weathervane finding wind. Her eyes found mine and I felt the bottom drop out of my chest, because I had seen those eyes three weeks ago — bright, suspicious, quick to narrow at strangers asking questions on smuggler business — and what looked out of them now was nothing. Not sadness. Not emptiness in the poetic sense. Emptiness in the literal sense, the way a window is empty when the lamp behind it goes out. Something was looking through her, or she was looking through everything, and either way Wren wasn't home.

"Hello," she said. Her voice was fine. That was somehow worse. "Are you lost?"

"No," I said. My mouth had gone dry enough to store grain in. "No, I'm not lost."

"That's nice." She turned back to the beans.

I stood at that gate for what felt like a year. I am not a man who freezes — freezing gets smugglers killed — but I froze. Because I remembered, with the terrible clarity that only arrives too late to be useful, standing in this same yard three weeks ago, haggling with Wren's father over a route fee, while Wren had sat on the step tracing shapes in the dust. Praying-shapes. Little spirals and open hands, the old gestures, the ones from before the gods went quiet. And I had noticed. I had noticed and thought, *children and their games*, and thought, *not my business*, and ridden on, because noticing things is my trade but caring about them was never in the contract.

The signs had all been there. I'd read them and filed them under *irrelevant*. A cartographer who misreads a map can blame the map. There is no one else to blame for this.

Wren's mother found me eventually — found me standing at the gate like a scarecrow that had given up — and pulled me inside with the desperate grip of someone who needed to tell somebody, anybody, before she came apart. The story poured out of her in pieces. Three nights ago Wren had started praying at the old cairn up the ridge. Every night. Begging, the mother said, begging anyone who might still be listening to make her father's cough better, to make the winter shorter, to make the tithe-collectors go away. Small prayers. Child prayers. And then two mornings ago Wren hadn't woken. Had gotten up, rather — walked, eaten, spoken, done her chores with perfect obedience — but hadn't *woken*. The healer had come and found nothing wrong. The priest — what remained of the faith sent a circuit priest twice a year — had found even less.

"She says she feels fine," the mother whispered. "She says she doesn't feel anything at all. She asked me what sad was like. She used to know what sad was like, Vesper. She cried when the dog died."

I held the woman's hands and said the only true thing available to me: "I'm going to fix this."

I want to note, for the record, that I had absolutely no plan. I said it anyway, because sometimes a sentence is a rope you throw to someone drowning, and you figure out the other end later. Also because the alternative — saying *your daughter's soul has been taken and I don't know by whom or how or whether it can be returned* — would have killed her on the spot, and I already had enough deaths on my conscience that morning.

* * *

The burned chapel sat a mile east of the village, up where the tree line thinned. It had been a proper chapel once, back when the old gods still took collections; now it was a roofless shell of blackened stone with weeds growing through the altar, which struck me as theologically apt. Smugglers used it as a waypoint cache. I'd stashed rope and salt-pork under its floor slabs more than once. Gods' houses make excellent warehouses precisely because everyone believes they're haunted, and nobody believes in anything strongly enough to check.

I didn't go there on purpose. I'll swear to that. I went because the footprints from the shrine led that direction — bare feet in the frost, unhurried, spaced like a stroll — and following tracks is reflex, not choice. But somewhere along that mile I became aware that I wasn't tracking anything anymore. The tracks weren't ahead of me. They were beside me. Matching my stride. And the frost stopped crunching under my own boots, because apparently the ground had decided to hold its breath.

The chapel doorframe — no door left, just the frame — stood against the grey sky. I stepped through it because stopping felt worse than continuing.

Inside, the air was warm and smelled of woodsmoke and something older underneath, something like hot iron and incense and the inside of a bank vault. The weeds had died in a neat circle around the altar stone. Sitting on the altar stone, quite comfortably, legs crossed, was a man made partly of ash.

I want to describe him properly and I'm going to fail, because he was the sort of sight the eye files under *later, deal with this later*. He wore a coat the color of burnt paper, and his skin beneath it was grey and finely cracked like charcoal that had decided to stand up and take an interest. His eyes were embers — not glowing dramatically, just banked, patient, the coals at the heart of a fire that has been burning a very long time and intends to keep going. When he smiled, small flakes fell from the corners of his mouth.

"Vesper Reyes," he said. His voice was quiet and vast at once, like wind through a chimney. "Cartographer. Debtor."

"I think you have the wrong Reyes," I said, because bravado is cheaper than terror and I've always been frugal.

"Ashen-Mouth does not have the wrong anyone." He unfolded himself from the altar and dropped to the ground without sound. Up close he was taller than he'd seemed, and less shaped — edges of him smoldered gently, giving off a heat I could feel on my face like an opened oven. "Ashen-Mouth keeps accounts. Yours is overdue."

"I don't have accounts with gods." I crossed my arms, which is a stupid gesture to make at a fire. "Nobody does. That's the whole arrangement. You went quiet, we went practical. Everyone signed off on it centuries ago."

"Gods do not go quiet." He drifted closer, and where he passed, the weeds crumbled to fresh ash. "Gods go *unpaid*. There is a difference, though I grant it is subtle from your side of the ledger." He stopped an arm's length away, close enough that I could see the cracks in him were not random — they formed patterns, dense and interlocking, and it took me an obscene amount of time to recognize them as writing. Script. He was covered in contracts. "Your mother knew the difference."

The cold that went through me then had nothing to do with the mountain.

"My mother," I said carefully, "died eleven years ago, in a bed, of a bad chest, surrounded by people who owed her money. She was a smuggler and a fence and a magnificent liar, and she did not bargain with gods, because she taught me gods were the first con ever run on frightened people."

"Maren Reyes taught you that?" Ashen-Mouth's ember-eyes did something that might have been amusement. "Maren Reyes, who walked into this chapel on the night you were born?"

I didn't answer. My body answered for me, going very still, the way prey goes still.

"You were dying," the god said, conversationally, terribly. "Born too early, in the wrong month, with lungs full of the wrong weather. The midwife had already begun the washing of the dead. And Maren came up here through the snow with you wrapped against her chest, and she knelt where you're standing, and she prayed to whoever still listened. Not the polite gods. Not the tithed ones. She prayed to the hungry ones. To me." He raised one grey hand, and in its palm lay a small curl of ash, delicate as a fern. "And I listened. And I said: the child will live. And in exchange —"

"Don't," I said.

"— she promised a debt unnamed, to be called when I chose, upon the child who lived." The ash-flake rose from his palm and hung in the air between us, turning slowly. "She wept. She thanked me. She named you for the road home. And then she went back down the mountain and told no one, and raised you on skepticism, because a debtor who doesn't believe in the creditor sleeps better. Clever woman. Cruel, a little. Loving, in the way of people who pay for things secretly so their children never see the price."

The chapel was very quiet. Somewhere outside, a crow said something rude and left.

"What do you want?" I asked. My voice came out level. I was almost proud of it.

"Three things. First: acknowledgment. Say aloud that the debt exists. Debts die in silence; that is why your kind loves silence so."

"The debt exists," I said. It cost less than I expected. Perhaps because some part of me — the part that had watched Wren's eyes and seen no one home — had already signed.

"Second: service. There is a soul in the court beneath the mountain that does not belong there. A child of Mirabel's Garden, sold by intermediaries, purchased in error — or in someone's error, which the court does not distinguish, the court being very fond of paperwork. The moon turns thirteen times to full. On the thirteenth turn, what the court holds, the court keeps. Permanently. Irrevocably. Filed."

"And third?"

"Third," Ashen-Mouth said, and the embers of his eyes brightened, "you bring witnesses. The court beneath the mountain respects only three currencies: debt, truth, and sacrifice. You will need all three, and you cannot carry them alone." He gestured with his burning hand, and across the chapel floor a line of ash drew itself — a road, a map, drawn in the residue of burned things. It ran from the chapel door down through the valley, through a pass I had mapped and three I had not, and then it went somewhere no line of mine had ever gone: it bent *downward*, impossibly, folding through the page like a river running under the world. "This is the road. No one has charted it. That, I suspect, is why he chose you."

"He? Who's he?"

"The Regent keeps his own counsel. I keep mine." The god straightened, and for a moment his outline flickered — I saw, or thought I saw, something vaster underneath, a shape like a mouth full of mountains. "Understand me, Vesper Reyes. I am not kind. Kindness is expensive and I am owed. But I am *honest*, which in the current market is rarer and worth more. Your mother bought your life with a blank promise. I am calling it in the only way that honors the purchase: the life I saved will spend itself saving another. Symmetry. The ledger loves symmetry."

"And if I refuse?"

He smiled again, wider, and more flakes fell. "Then the debt remains, and I call it differently. Debts unnamed are debts interpreted, and I interpret... thoroughly. Walk the road freely, or be walked. Those are the terms. Maren accepted similar ones. It runs in families, apparently."

I looked at the ash-map on the floor. Down through the valley, past Mirabel's Garden, past a child standing motionless among the bean rows asking her mother what sad was like. Six miles of ordinary ground and then a fold in the world where no map of mine had ever reached.

Here is the thing nobody tells you about obligation: it doesn't feel like chains. It feels like gravity. You've been falling your whole life; someone simply finally tells you what you've been falling toward.

"Fine," I said. "I'll walk it. But I set the pace, I pick the company, and when this is done, the ledger closes. All of it. Written off, witnessed, burned. Whatever your word is worth."

Ashen-Mouth extended his charred hand, and I — fool, debtor, son of a clever cruel loving woman — shook it. His grip was warm as a hearthstone and closed around my hand like a decision.

"The ledger closes," he agreed, "when the account balances. Not before. Never before." He began to come apart as he spoke, pleasantly, the way a fire settles — flakes of him lifting on a wind that wasn't blowing, his voice thinning to smoke. "One gift, freely given, since you bargained well: the child's soul hangs in the court's high gallery. Lantern-lit. Look for the smallest flame. And Vesper —"

The chapel was nearly empty of him now, just warmth and drifting grey.

"— ask your Tally what she traded, thirty years ago. Ask her before the river asks for you."

And then he was gone, and the ash-map on the floor began, slowly, to blow apart at the edges — except the road itself, which held its shape like it was waiting for me to memorize it.

I stood in the ruined chapel for a long time, holding the hand he'd shaken, feeling the warmth fade out of it.

Thirty years ago. Tally. The woman who found me half-frozen in a smuggler's cache at fourteen and fed me, armed me, taught me every hidden road in Thornfell, and never once — not once in twenty years — mentioned knowing my mother.

I took out my notebook. I copied the ash-road from memory, line by fold, until my hand stopped shaking enough to write the last entry:

*Debt acknowledged. Road accepted. Company required.*

Then, underneath, smaller:

*Ask Tally. Before the river.*

Outside, the moon was three days from full, and the count to thirteen had already begun.
