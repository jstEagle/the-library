## 4. The Company of Borrowed Names

There is a particular art to asking for help, and I have never mastered it. Asking implies need. Need implies weakness. And weakness, on the smuggler roads of the Thornfells, is the sort of thing that gets written down in ledgers and called in later with interest.

But a god had called in my mother's debt, and the interest was a child's soul, and so I spent the two days after Ashen-Mouth burned his road into my memory doing the thing I hated most: asking.

I started a list. Cartographers love lists. It gives chaos the illusion of having been measured.

*One: someone who still believes in gods, because apparently they've started answering again and I'd like a professional opinion before I trust one.*
*Two: someone who knows the under-road, because the mountain has doors and I only know the ones above ground.*
*Three: Old Tally, because there was never any version of this where I didn't tell her.*

I looked at the list for a long time. Three names. For an errand into realms no map had ever held, against a court beneath a mountain, on a deadline measured in moon-turns.

It would have to do. It always does. That's the dirty secret of every impossible thing ever accomplished — nobody qualified said yes.

* * *

Sefa lived — lives, I should say; she outlived all my expectations and most of my hopes — in a lean-to half a mile below the shrine at Coldwell Gap, where the pilgrim road gave up on dignity and became a goat track. She grew vegetables in soil that had no business growing anything, and she'd built her roof from the shattered door of the very chapel that had cast her out, which told me everything about her sense of humor before she'd said a word.

She was splitting firewood when I came up the track. She saw me coming — everyone sees me coming; I walk like a woman reading terrain, because I am — and she didn't stop splitting. Just watched me over the top of the woodpile with eyes the gray of old slate.

"You're the mapmaker," she said. "Vesper Reyes. The one who charts the Thornfell runs."

"Reputation travels."

"So does heresy." She set the axe down. "You're standing in the yard of a woman the Concord excommunicated eleven years ago. Whatever you want, it isn't a blessing."

"It might be," I said. "That's the problem."

I told her all of it. The shrine at the pass igniting like a struck match against the dark. Wren walking Mirabel's Garden with nothing behind her eyes but weather. Ashen-Mouth in his burned chapel, patient as rot, talking about debts and roads and thirteen turns of the moon. I told it plainly, the way I'd write it in a field journal, because if I let myself feel it while telling it I'd have to stop and sit down in her vegetable patch and not get up for a while.

Sefa listened without interrupting, which is rarer than honesty. When I finished, she wiped her hands on her apron and asked the question I'd been dreading.

"Why me? You could find priests who kept their vows. Whole choirs of them, if you want people who sing nicely about gods from a safe distance."

"Because they'd tell me the gods are distant and safe," I said. "And I've seen otherwise now. You were thrown out for saying what I saw with my own eyes. That makes you the only expert I know."

Something moved behind her face — not a smile, exactly. More like a crack where a smile had been bricked over.

"They said I was mad," she said quietly. "Eleven years ago. I stood up at convocation and said the silence was ending, that the old ones were stirring under the mountains, that we'd better learn their names again before they learned ours. They took my vestments and my name from the rolls and put me on a hill to grow cabbages." She looked past me, up toward the peaks, where the last light was going copper. "I prayed every day they were right to cast me out. And every day some small part of me hoped I wasn't mad."

"And now?"

"Now I'm afraid I was right," she said, "and rightness is heavier than madness ever was." She turned back to me. "The gods that answer after centuries of silence aren't gentle, Vesper Reyes. A god who ignored your grandmother's prayers doesn't wake up kind. He wakes up hungry."

"I know. I met him."

"Then you understand why I'm coming." She pulled a canvas bag from inside the lean-to — already packed, I noticed, though she couldn't have known I was coming. Perhaps she'd packed it years ago. Perhaps exile is just waiting with extra steps. "Not because I believe in your errand. Because someone in that company has to know how to talk to things like Ashen-Mouth without flinching, and flinching is the one sin I never managed."

We shook hands. Her grip was a farmer's grip, honest and strong.

"One condition," she said as I turned to go. "When we meet whatever waits beneath that mountain — when it offers you something sweet, and it will — you listen to me before you listen to it. Promise."

"I promise," I said. And because I'm a cartographer and therefore superstitious about words, I wrote it down that night in the margin of my field book: *Sefa listens first. Remember.*

* * *

Bramble found me, which should have been my first warning.

I was in Lowmark, buying rope and lying about its purpose to a shopkeeper who knew perfectly well I was lying and charged me accordingly. I came out into the alley behind the chandlery and there he was, sitting on my saddlebags like they were furniture he'd inherited, a wiry young man with a face designed by committee to be forgettable and fingers that wouldn't stay still.

"Vesper Reyes," he said. "Thornfell's finest. Heard you're going under."

"Going under what?"

"The mountain. The deep way. Don't do that face — everyone in Lowmark's heard something. The shrines lit up. The gods are awake. Half the town's praying and the other half's drinking, and I don't pray." He hopped off the bags. "You need a guide for the under-road."

"I need a guide for the under-road," I agreed, "which means I need someone who isn't a thief."

"Those categories overlap more than you'd think." He grinned. "Name's Bramble."

"That's not a name, that's a shrub."

"It's the name I use, which makes it a name. Names are just handles, mapmaker. You of all people should know — you put them on maps and suddenly places answer to them." He said it lightly, but there was something underneath, the way there's water under ice. "Look. You want the under-road, you want me. I've run it four times. Well — run it three times and crawled out of it once, which is the more educational trip."

"Who sent you?"

"No one. I heard you were asking around and I came to volunteer, which frankly should worry you more than being sent. People who volunteer for trips under haunted mountains have reasons." He held up a hand before I could speak. "My reason: there's a debt of mine that got sold. Not a soul-debt. A money-debt, to a man named Corvess, who holds paper on me and would very much like me dead or indentured, whichever's cheaper. Under the mountain is the one place his paper doesn't reach. You take me along, I work free, and when we come out the far side I disappear into whatever country we land in. Everyone wins."

"Except Corvess."

"Corvess can weep."

I studied him. Thieves lie — it's the trade — but the best liars build their lies on true foundations, and everything structural in his story rang solid. What worried me wasn't the lies. It was how much he wanted in. Nobody volunteers to go beneath the Thornfells out of boredom.

"Three times through and once crawling out," I said. "What did you learn on the crawling trip?"

His grin faded into something more careful. "That the under-road isn't a tunnel. It's a throat. And the thing it's a throat *of* notices when you swallow wrong." He shrugged, making light of it too late. "Geography down there runs on attention. You look away from a passage, it moves. You doubt a bridge, it thins. Your maps are going to be useless, mapmaker. Down there, belief is terrain."

"Then I'll bring a priestess."

He blinked. "You're serious. You've actually got a priestess."

"A disgraced one. Best kind."

For a moment he looked at me with something almost like respect, or possibly indigestion. Then he stuck out his hand. "Bramble. At your service until the far side or the swallowing, whichever comes first."

I shook it. His palm was calloused in the pattern of a climber and pickpocket both, and I noticed — because noticing is my whole profession — that he'd already lifted my spare knife somewhere between the handshake and the goodbye. He returned it an hour later, sheepish, when Sefa made him empty his pockets as a condition of traveling together. Found family, assembling. Every family starts with somebody getting caught stealing.

* * *

Old Tally was the hardest, because Old Tally was home.

Her house sat where it had always sat, at the crook of the Miller's Run where the smuggler path dropped into Mirabel's Garden valley — a low stone house with a blue door she painted fresh every spring, though no one had used that door honestly in forty years. Smoke from the chimney. Lamplight in the window. The smell of her cooking reaching me before I reached the gate, onion and rosemary and woodsmoke, the smell of every safe evening of my childhood.

She opened the door before I knocked. She always did. Said she could hear my footsteps apart from anyone else's, that I walked like someone apologizing to the ground.

"You look terrible," she said, which in Tally meant *come in, I love you, sit down.*

"Tally—"

"In. Soup first. Terrible news keeps better on a full stomach."

So I sat at her table, at my spot, the bench worn smooth by twenty years of my sitting there, and ate soup while she watched me with those pale sharp eyes that had priced contraband and read men's lies across three decades of the Thornfell routes. She'd raised me from ten years old, after my mother went into the mountains and didn't come back. Taught me to read trails, read faces, read the space between what people say and what they mean. Everything I am, I am because of her. Which is why what I had to say next felt like cutting into living wood.

I told her. All of it — the shrine, Wren, Ashen-Mouth, the debt, the road beneath the mountain, the thirteen turns. Sefa and Bramble. The wager I hadn't even made yet with my own life.

Tally listened the way stone listens. When I finished, she was quiet for a long time, turning her spoon in the empty bowl.

"Wren Halloway," she said finally. "Little girl with the red boots."

"You know her."

"I know everyone in this valley. That's the job." But something crossed her face when she said it — quick, gone before I could fix its coordinates. I've spent my life reading terrain, and I know a landslide scar when I see one, even healed over. "You're going under the mountain for her."

"For her. For my mother's debt. For—" I gestured vaguely at the world, which lately contained gods. "All of it."

"And you came to tell me goodbye."

"I came to ask you something worse than goodbye." I made myself look at her. "I came to ask you to come with us."

The lamplight flickered. Outside, the Run muttered over its stones the way it had muttered my whole life.

"Vesper—"

"Don't say no yet. Hear the shape of it. I need someone who knows the old bargains — the real ones, the pre-Concord ones, the ones the smugglers kept alive when the priests burned the rest. You taught me that the mountains remember debts older than any ledger. If anyone alive knows what the court beneath the Thornfells honors, it's you." My voice cracked and I hated it. "And I don't want to walk into the dark without the person who taught me to walk at all."

Tally looked at me for a long moment. And here is the thing I will turn over for the rest of my half-souled days: in that moment, before she spoke, I saw her decide something. I saw her choose, deliberate and fast, the way a card player chooses which truth to show. I saw it and I named it nothing, because I loved her, and love is the great cartographer's error — the blank space we draw over our own maps.

"All right," she said. "I'll come."

I let out a breath I felt I'd owed since the chapel.

"But hear my terms," she went on, brisk now, already rising to pack, because Tally grieved and decided and packed at the same speed. "On the road, I'm your scout and your quartermaster and nothing else. You lead. Whatever we find down there, whatever old business surfaces — you lead, and I follow, and we don't relitigate it in front of the others." She pulled her old travel cloak from a chest, the gray one, the one I'd never seen her wear anywhere near the smuggling routes. "Agreed?"

"Agreed," I said slowly. "What old business?"

"Every family has some," she said, not looking at me. "Yours has a mother who bargained with a god. Mine can keep."

I should have pressed. Any competent version of me would have pressed. Instead I helped her bank the fire and latch the blue door, and the four of us — the mapmaker, the heretic, the thief, and the matriarch — set out from Mirabel's Garden two hours before dawn, when the moon hung low and fat and one night shy of its second turn since Wren stopped dreaming.

Eleven turns left. Three borrowed names, walking into country no map had ever held.

I remember looking back once, at the valley, at the village where a soulless child slept in a house with red boots by the door. And I remember thinking — with the warm, foolish confidence of a woman surrounded by people she trusted — that whatever waited beneath the mountain, we would face it together, and together would be enough.

The mountain, I suspect, had already read my list.

And it knew about the fourth name. The one Tally hadn't given me yet.
