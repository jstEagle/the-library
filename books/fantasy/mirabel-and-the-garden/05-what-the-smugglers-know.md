## 5. What the Smugglers Know

I've been in this trade long enough to know that the worst news never arrives shouting. It comes quiet, on ordinary evenings, wearing an ordinary face. The night Vesper came back down from the pass with that look on their face — the one they get when the world has handed them something their maps can't fold flat — I knew before they said a word. I was salting a haunch of mutton and my hands went still, and salt doesn't forgive stillness. It just keeps eating.

"Old Tally," Vesper said, standing in my doorway with the wind still on them and something terrible folded up small behind their eyes, "we need to talk about Wren."

I put down the salt. That's how you know it's serious. In forty years I have not once put down the salt mid-job, because salt waits for no one and neither does spoilage, and a smuggler who abandons her preserves is a smuggler preparing for a funeral — hers or somebody else's.

"Aye," I said. "Sit down, then."

They didn't sit. Vesper Reyes has never once sat when they ought to; it's why they're alive and half the clever ones are dead. They stood there in my kitchen with the lamplight making shadows of them and said, "Her soul's gone. Sold. And the old gods are answering prayers again, and one of them answered *hers*, and Tally — I need to know if you've ever seen anything like it."

And here is the thing I will carry to whatever grave the mountain gives me: I said yes too fast.

Not a lie, exactly. A truth with its pockets turned out. Because yes, I had seen it before. Yes, decades ago. But there are ways of saying yes and ways of saying yes, and I chose the way that didn't open the door all the way. I chose the way that let me keep my hand on the latch.

* * *

Let me tell you what I told them, since I'm telling you everything else anyway, and shame is a debt like any other — it only grows interest the longer you sit on it.

I told them about the winter of the Long Gray, thirty-odd years back, when the passes froze early and the routes died and every family on the Thornfell side of the mountain watched their stores go down to crumbs and their children go down to silence. Hard winters make hard bargains. That much is true and always has been. What I did not tell them — what I kept folded up small in the pocket of that truth — was whose hand signed the bargain, and what exactly was traded, and how well I knew the woman who held the pen.

I told them about a girl called Merrin. Not her real name — we didn't use real names for things like that, even then, even before we understood why. She came walking out of her mother's house one gray morning breathing and blinking and answering to her name, and she was not there. You know how you can tell? Most folk can't. Most folk see a child walking and think *child, walking*, and go on about their porridge. But I'd raised enough children by then, mine and borrowed both, to know that a soul shows in the eyes the way water shows under ice. There's a shine to it. A living thing looks at you like it's *looking*. Merrin looked through me like I was weather.

"Walking dead," Vesper said, when I described it. "Husked."

"Worse than dead," I said, and meant it. "Dead leaves a body you can bury and a grief you can carry. This leaves you setting a place at the table for a girl who eats and doesn't taste, sleeps and doesn't dream, and smiles — oh, she smiled, Vesper, that's the part that curdles me still. Somebody had taught her the shape of smiling and she wore it like a coat that didn't fit."

Vesper's face did something complicated. I watched them decide not to ask the obvious question — *who took her? where did she go? did anyone try to bring her back?* — because they already knew the answer to the last one. Nobody tried. Nobody knew how. The gods were silent in those days, properly silent, the way gods are supposed to be, and a soul sold beneath the mountain was a soul gone past recall. The village learned to stop setting her place at the table. Her mother learned it last, and hardest, and I held her hand while she learned it, and that is another thing I have not told anybody, and I am telling you now only because a confession that stops halfway is worse than none.

Vesper asked me how I recognized Wren's condition so fast. Down in Mirabel's Garden, the day we found the girl standing in the shrine-yard with her empty winter eyes, I'd known inside three breaths — said so out loud, plain as a price tag. Vesper wanted to know how.

"Because I've seen it," I said. "Once. Thirty years ago. A girl called Merrin, in the winter of the Long Gray." And then I gave them the shape of the story without the spine of it, the way you give a customs man a sample of the cargo without opening the whole crate.

They bought it. Gods forgive me — and I mean that literally now, in a way I didn't used to — they bought it clean.

* * *

Here's what I didn't say, laid out honest so you can judge me, since judging me is clearly on tonight's menu:

In the winter of the Long Gray, the routes froze and the families starved, and I was not yet Old Tally but Tally of the Nine Names, young-ish and hungry and meaner than I am now, which is saying something. And a thing came up out of the under-road — not a god, nothing so grand, more like a god's errand-runner, a broker, a middleman with a face like a closed ledger — and it offered terms. Routes opened. Winters softened. The mountain would look away from our carts and our cargo and our little crimes, all of it, in exchange for payment rendered below.

Payment rendered below. You hear the phrasing and your mind does you the courtesy of not finishing the sentence.

It was Merrin's mother who signed. I stood beside her. I carried the pen. I told her — and I believed it, that's the rot at the center of it, I *believed* it — that the court beneath the mountain wanted only "a tithe of the household," some small portion of spirit, a trimming, a nail-paring, nothing a child would miss. The broker smiled its ledger-face smile and said words to that effect, and I repeated them to a starving woman in a freezing house, and she signed, and her daughter walked out into the gray morning two days later with a stranger's weather behind her eyes.

The routes opened. The winter broke early, that very week, which should have told us everything. Debts that come due conveniently are debts that were never fair.

And I stayed silent. Thirty years I stayed silent. I watched Merrin's mother die of it slow, the way mothers do, and I helped carry her coffin, and I said the words over her grave with a straight face, and every year after I sent coin to nobody because there was nobody left to send it to. I told myself I'd been tricked too. I told myself the broker lied to me as surely as I lied to her. Both true. Neither sufficient. A lie you repeat in good faith is still a lie coming out of your mouth, and the mountain does not care what you believed when you said it.

Then, thirty years on, along comes Wren. A child in Mirabel's Garden, husked, hollowed, smiling wrong. And Vester Reyes — sharp-eyed, sharp-tongued, raised at my own knee, the closest thing to a child I ever let myself claim — comes asking me how I knew so fast.

How did I know so fast?

Because I helped build the machine that did it. That's how fast.

* * *

The evening wore on. Vesper sat eventually — I told you they don't, but grief makes even Reyeses sit — at my table, turning a cup of tea they weren't drinking, and I watched them assemble the company in their head the way they assemble maps: Sefa first, because a disgraced priestess is the only kind worth having when the gods wake up angry; Bramble second, because the under-road needs a thief the way a lock needs a pick; and me third, though they were polite enough to list me first out loud.

"You'll come," they said. Not asking. Vesper doesn't ask when they already know, which is a habit they got from me, which is a debt I suppose I'm also owed repayment on, in the cruel currency of seeing your own faults walk around wearing your face.

"I'll come," I said.

"Tally." They looked up, and for a moment they were eight years old again, sitting on this same table's edge, asking me why the tax men always found the north route and never the south. "If you know something — anything — about how souls get sold, about who buys them, about how this happened to Wren —"

"I've told you what I know," I said.

And here is the craft of it, the smuggler's craft, the oldest craft there is: I almost believed myself. That's the trick that keeps liars alive. You rehearse the partial truth until it sits in your chest like a full one, until your hands stay steady and your eyes stay level and even the gods, were they listening, would mark it down as honest testimony. I have passed contraband past a hundred inspectors with less conviction than I passed that sentence across my own kitchen table.

But Vesper set down the cup they weren't drinking from, and looked at me a moment too long, and said, quietly, "You put down the salt."

"What?"

"When I came in. You were salting the mutton and you stopped. You never stop mid-salt. I've watched you do it my whole life. You salt through arguments, through funerals, through the raid on Harrow's Ford — you salted *through the raid*, you had the jar in one hand and a knife in the other." A pause. "So whatever you're not telling me, it's older than the raid. It's older than most things."

I laughed. I actually laughed, a real one, because terror and laughter live next door to each other and share a wall thin as paper. "Reyes," I said, "you've been reading too many of those novels you pretend not to read. Sometimes a woman puts down the salt because her knees hurt."

"Your knees hurt every day."

"And yet I salt through it daily. So tonight must be special." I stood, gathered the cups, made busy work of the basin, and gave my back the job of lying that my face couldn't manage. Behind me, silence — Vesper's particular silence, the cartographer's silence, the sound of someone measuring a room for a door that isn't drawn on any plan yet.

They left near midnight. At the door they stopped and said, "We leave for the Thornfell Gate in three days. Whatever you're carrying, Tally — leave it, or tell me. Either one. But don't carry it up the mountain, because the mountain hears everything now. The gods are answering prayers again." A beat. "And somebody prayed *against* Wren, or near enough. Somebody sold her. And I'm going to find out who, if it's the last thing my maps ever hold."

*I'm going to find out who.*

I stood in my kitchen after the door shut, with the mutton unsalted and the lamp burning low, and I thought about Merrin's mother's hand under mine on the pen, cold as the winter outside. I thought about the broker's ledger-face. I thought about the fact that the court beneath the mountain does not forget a signature, and neither, apparently, does fate — because what is Vesper's quest but an audit arriving thirty years late, with interest?

There's a version of me that tells them tomorrow. I've met her. She lives in the same house as the version of me that runs from the raid on Harrow's Ford, and the version that spends the stolen tax money on medicine instead of hiding it, and all the other better Tallies who exist for exactly the length of a cowardly heartbeat. She's a fine woman. She'd be welcome anywhere. She has never once survived the night.

What I did instead was this: I went to the loose stone behind the hearth, the one every smuggler has and no smuggler admits, and I took out the thing I have kept there for thirty years without once looking at it. A contract. One page, vellum that isn't vellum, ink that isn't ink, signed in a starving woman's hand and countersigned — countersigned, witnessed, *guaranteed* — in mine.

I held it over the lamp.

Paper burns. Everyone knows that. But this page didn't burn. The flame went around it like water around a stone, and in the firelight I could read the clause I have spent thirty years not reading, the one in the small script at the bottom, the one the broker pointed to with its ledger finger while Merrin's mother wept: *Guarantor binds herself jointly, in soul and station, unto the term of the debt.*

Jointly. In soul and station.

Which means that somewhere beneath the Thornfell mountains, in a gilded dark where souls hang like lanterns, there is a hook with my name on it, and it has been waiting thirty years, patient as salt, for the debt to be called.

Three days. The Thornfell Gate. Vesper's company, assembled from borrowed names, walking straight down the throat of the thing I built.

I put the contract back behind the stone. I picked up the salt jar. And I finished the mutton, because whatever else I am — liar, guarantor, ghost-in-waiting — I am still Old Tally, and the preserving gets done, and the road goes up the mountain in three days whether my courage comes with it or stays home sulking by the fire.

The salt ate into the meat the way truth eats into a liar: slowly, thoroughly, and with plenty of time to change your mind.

I didn't change my mind.

God help me — and He won't, they're all awake now, and awake gods keep ledgers of their own — I didn't change a thing.
