## 7. Sefa's Broken Faith

I was cast out of the Cloister of the Listening Ear on a Tuesday, which I have always felt was an insult added to injury. Excommunication should happen on a Sunday, at minimum. There should be bells. There should be weather — rain at the very least, ideally thunder. Instead it was a bright, indifferent morning in late spring, and the Abbot dismissed me in the herb garden while he was deadheading roses, as though my soul were another bloom past its season.

"Sefa," he said, snipping off a browned blossom with more tenderness than he'd shown me in three years, "the gods are silent. They have been silent for four hundred years. This is doctrine. This is *settled*."

"And yet," I said, "the prayers still work."

He did not even look up. That was the worst part. Not the anger — there was no anger. Anger would have meant I'd said something worth being angry about. He simply went on with his roses, the way you'd go on weeding while a child insisted the well was haunted.

"The prayers work because the liturgy works," he said. "Because repetition shapes the heart. Because the act of asking is itself the grace."

"Then explain Marisol."

Marisol. I knew even then that invoking her name would end me, and I invoked it anyway, because I had never once in my life let prudence interrupt a good point. Marisol was a lay sister, sixty years old, who had prayed every evening for forty years for her brother, lost on the Thornfell passes, and who had stopped praying the winter before last because she said — quietly, ashamedly, the way people confess to crimes — that she could feel someone listening now. And she was frightened.

"Explain how a woman who prayed faithfully for four decades suddenly finds the silence *changed*," I said. "You can't shape a heart into hearing things, Abbot. Either the line was always dead or someone picked up the receiver."

The shears paused. Just for a moment. Then they resumed, and he said, "Gather your things by vespers," and that was that.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about heresy: it is not dramatic. You do not get dragged through the streets. You pack a satchel, you sign a ledger (they make you sign a ledger; the Church loves a ledger more than it loves God, if I'm honest), and a lay brother walks you to the gate and says "safe travels" with the hollow courtesy of a man who has done this eleven times before and will do it eleven times again. The eleventh heretic before me had insisted the saints were still taking requests. The one after me, I heard later, claimed the Virgin appeared to him in the form of a mule. I like to think I was the reasonable one in that lineup.

The truth is, I didn't leave angry either. I left *vindicated*, which is a far more corrosive thing to carry. Anger cools. Vindication ferments. For six years I carried it through the low country like a skin of wine gone to vinegar, tasting it every morning, telling myself: *you were right, you were right, you were right.* I took work where I could find it — hedge-blessings, deathbeds, the occasional wedding for couples too poor or too scandalous for proper clergy. I kept the old forms out of habit. I did not pray. Praying felt like writing letters to someone who had moved without leaving a forwarding address, and I had too much pride to be the fool at the empty house.

And then Wren.

* * *

I need to go back a little further, because the reason I agreed to this mad errand isn't the reason anyone thinks.

When I was noviced — sixteen, pious in the way only the truly stubborn can be — my grandmother died. She raised me after my mother went into the mines at Cinderhollow and did not come out, and she was the only person alive who loved me without requiring documentation. On her last night I sat by her bed and I prayed. Not the liturgy. Something rawer, something I'd never been taught: *let there be someone on the other end. Let her not walk into the dark alone. If anyone is listening, please, please be listening.*

She died at dawn, peacefully, holding my hand.

And I want to tell you I felt peace. I didn't. I felt *nothing come back*. Forty years later I can still map the exact shape of that nothing — the way a prayer goes out of you like breath in cold air and just disperses, unclaimed, unacknowledged, a letter posted into a burning house. I told myself the liturgy stories: the grace is in the asking. I told myself she walked into the dark accompanied by angels who simply hadn't introduced themselves. I built a whole career on the architecture of that consolation.

But some part of me, some load-bearing part, never forgave the silence. Never trusted it. And when I began to suspect — Marisol, and the others, the little anomalies I collected like contraband — that the silence was *ending*, I should have rejoiced. Instead I felt something I'm only now able to name:

Terror.

Because I had made my peace with an empty heaven. An empty heaven is at least honest. It cannot lie to you. It cannot bargain with you. It cannot take your grandmother's death and fold it into some vast inscrutable design that requires you to be grateful. A heaven that answers — a heaven that has been *choosing* not to answer for four hundred years and has now changed its mind — that is a heaven with intentions. And I have read enough scripture, and enough history, to know what happens to small people standing under large intentions.

So when Vesper Reyes stood in the ruins of the chapel at Mirabel's Garden and told me a god had spoken to them — Ashen-Mouth, the Burned One, the god whose name we were taught to say quickly and quietly, like a debt — I said yes before my courage caught up with my mouth.

Not because I wanted vindication.

Because if the gods are waking, someone needs to be there who remembers how to talk to them. And someone needs to be there who is *afraid of them properly*. Reverence without fear is just enthusiasm, and enthusiasm is how congregations get eaten.

* * *

The night before we crossed the Thornfell Gate, I couldn't sleep. None of us could, though Bramble pretended to, snoring theatrically against a root as though sleep were a performance he was giving for our benefit. Old Tally sat by the fire turning her pipe over and over without lighting it, wearing the face of a woman doing arithmetic she doesn't like the answer to. And Vesper sat apart, sketching by moonlight — always sketching, that one, as though paper were a place they could hide.

I went and sat beside them.

"Can't sleep either?" they said, not looking up.

"I'm praying," I said.

Vesper's pencil stopped. "You're praying? To *him*?"

"To whoever's on duty." I shrugged. "It's been a long time. I'm out of practice at being picky."

"What do you pray for? Now. Knowing what you know. Knowing what happened to Wren." There was something raw under the question, something that wasn't really about me. Vesper had seen the child's empty eyes. We all had. But Vesper took other people's wounds the way their maps took rivers — personally, as errors to be corrected.

I considered lying. I was, after all, very good at theology, and theology is mostly lying with footnotes.

"I pray that they've gotten kinder in four hundred years," I said finally. "Or that they haven't, and we find out early enough to run."

"That's a terrible prayer."

"It's an honest one. Honest prayers travel better." I pulled my knees up. "You know what I was cast out for? Saying the gods still listened. Everyone thought I was a fanatic. A zealot. Poor mad Sister Sefa, shouting at an empty sky and calling it a conversation." I laughed, and it came out wrong, bitter as I'd feared. "Nobody ever asked me the obvious question."

"Which was?"

"*What if I'm right?* What if the sky isn't empty? What if it's full?" I looked up at the stars, those old indifferent punctuations, and felt the vinegar of six years turn to something colder. "An empty heaven is a tragedy, Vesper. A full one might be a catastrophe. Everything that listens also wants. Everything that answers also keeps accounts. And we are walking toward the biggest account-keeper there is, to steal from its ledger, armed with a thief, a smuggler, and a priestess nobody wanted."

"You forgot the cartographer."

"I never forget the cartographer. You're the only one of us with a skill. The rest of us are just ballast with opinions."

That got a huff out of them — almost a laugh. Vesper Reyes laughs the way most people apologize: briefly, reluctantly, and only when cornered.

"Why did you come, Sefa?" they asked. Quietly. Genuinely. "Really."

And here is the thing I have not told them. Here is the thing I will carry until the mountain makes me set it down.

"When I was sixteen," I said, "I prayed for my dying grandmother's soul, and nothing answered, and I built my whole life on top of that nothing. If the gods are answering now — truly answering — then maybe somewhere in that great ledger of theirs there's a record of a girl's prayer from thirty years ago. Maybe it was heard and filed and *kept*. Maybe nothing is ever wasted, not even the prayers that fell into silence." I wrapped my arms around my knees. "I came because I need to know if the silence ate my prayers or saved them. That's all. That's the whole of it. Not faith. Bookkeeping."

Vesper looked at me for a long moment, and I braced for pity, which I would have forgiven, or mockery, which I would have answered with interest.

Instead they said: "Then we want the same thing. I keep maps. You keep receipts."

"Amen," I said, and meant it for the first time in years.

* * *

Morning came gray and cold, the kind of dawn that seems less like a beginning than a withholding. We broke camp in silence. Tally finally lit her pipe and immediately put it out again, which from Old Tally is a sign of omens worse than any comet. Bramble checked his knives twice, then a third time, then pretended he hadn't. Vesper unfolded the pass-map one last time — the last map that would mean anything, though none of us fully understood that yet — traced the route with one finger, and folded it away like a letter to someone who had died.

At the treeline, where the Thornfell Gate waited — a cleft in the rock older than the roads, older, some said, than the idea of roads — I stopped and did what I had sworn off doing for six years.

I prayed aloud.

Not to Ashen-Mouth. Not yet. I wasn't ready to open negotiations with the Burned One; a wise woman reads the contract before she speaks to the creditor. I prayed the old opening, the dawn-liturgy, the words I'd said ten thousand times into a silence that never once said anything back:

*Light before us, dark behind us, ears above us—*

And the wind came down through the cleft, cold and purposeful, and it smelled of ash and incense and something underneath both, something mineral and patient and *attentive*, and every hair on my body stood up like a congregation rising.

Six years I had shouted at an empty sky.

The sky had heard every word.

"Come on, then," said Vesper, already walking, because of course they were, because cartographers meet terror and their first instinct is to survey it. Bramble followed, whistling something tuneless. Tally followed, eyes forward, jaw set, carrying whatever she was carrying like a stone sewn into her coat.

I stood one moment longer at the threshold, and I made myself one promise, bitter and clean as the vinegar I'd drunk so long:

*I will not flinch. Whatever is listening up there in the dark — however cruel, however hungry, however much worse than silence — I will stand in front of my company and I will not flinch.*

Then I stepped through the Gate, out of the mapped world, into the attention of gods —

and behind us, faint but unmistakable, the wind through the rocks shaped itself, almost politely, into something very like a voice saying: *you're late.*
