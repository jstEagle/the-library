## 8. The Market of Small Mercies

The under-road did not lead to the Market so much as it eventually admitted to containing one.

We had been walking for what felt like two days and might have been four hours — time down here had the consistency of porridge — when the tunnel simply gave up pretending to be a tunnel. The walls peeled back like a curtain drawn by a bored stagehand, and there it was: a cavern the size of a cathedral's bad dream, hung with lanterns that burned a color I refuse to call purple because I have some professional standards, and filled with more people than the geography could possibly have been hiding.

"Cartographer's note," I said, mostly to keep from screaming. "The Thornfell mountains contain a city. The Thornfell mountains have always contained a city. Every map ever made is a lie by omission."

"That's most maps," Sefa said.

"I know. It's usually on purpose."

Bramble was already grinning. That should have been my first warning. Bramble grinning is like weather turning green — technically possible, never good.

"The Market of Small Mercies," he announced, with the reverence other men reserved for their mothers' graves. "Best trade below the sun. Better than above it, honestly. Above it you've got to worry about witnesses."

"What do they sell?" Old Tally asked. She'd been quiet since the Gate — quieter than usual, which for Tally meant her silence had furniture in it, rooms you couldn't see into.

"Everything," Bramble said. "But everything costs the same thing."

He held up his hand and tapped his temple.

I didn't like it. I want that on the record, in ink, in triplicate. I am a professional skeptic; skepticism is not a mood, it's a discipline, and my discipline said: a market where the currency is memory will find out exactly what your memories are worth, and it will not be gentle about it.

But we needed passage. The under-road forked ahead into passages that Sefa said were "prayer-locked" — doors that only opened for travelers carrying something true enough to spend — and the only alternative was a side tunnel Bramble described as "the one where the walls listen back," which nobody wanted.

So we went down into the Market.

* * *

It smelled like rain on hot stone and something sweeter underneath, like fruit going soft in a bowl nobody was watching. Stalls crowded the cavern floor in crooked rows, lit by those violet lanterns, each one burning at a slightly different height as though the light itself were breathing. The merchants were every kind of person and several kinds of almost-person. A woman with riverweed in her hair sold bottled afternoons. A thing shaped like a tall man but arranged wrong traded in lullabies. Somewhere a bell rang, and everyone flinched except me, because I hadn't yet learned what the bell meant.

"What's the bell?" I asked.

"Transaction completed," Bramble said. "You'll hear it a lot."

Sefa walked close to me. Close enough that I could feel her praying, if that makes sense — a warmth off her like a banked fire. She hadn't prayed openly since her exile, but down here she kept her lips moving, and I pretended not to notice, and she pretended to believe I hadn't.

Tally stopped at the first stall and read the price list the way she used to read tariff schedules before a run: fast, flat, memorizing. Then she went very still.

"Vesper." Her voice had dropped into the register she used for bad news about people we loved. "Look at the exchange rates."

The list was chalked on slate in a hand too neat for this place:

*One afternoon of childhood: passage through three gates.*
*A face remembered: one night's lodging.*
*Your mother's voice saying your name: negotiable.*
*First love: grossly overpriced, still worth it.*

And at the bottom, smaller, in different chalk:

*Souls purchased whole or in part. Fair weight given. Inquire within.*

"Inquire within," I repeated. "Within what?"

Bramble pointed with his chin at the far end of the cavern, where the stalls gave way to something grander — an archway of black stone carved with scales, and beyond it a building that managed to look official the way tax offices look official: clean lines, no joy, terrible competence.

"The Brokerage," he said. "That's where the big trades happen."

"You've been inside?"

A pause. Just long enough.

"No," said Bramble, who was lying, and wasn't even good at it today.

* * *

Here is what I learned about the Market in the first hour, and I learned it the way I learn everything, which is by watching who pays and how much:

Nobody haggled over memories the way they haggle over bread. They offered them up like apologies. A farmer-type fellow — broad hands, mud still on his boots, which raised fascinating questions about local agriculture — traded away the memory of his wedding day for a cure for his daughter's cough. He walked away lighter. Not metaphorically. He walked differently, like a man who'd set down a load he'd forgotten he was carrying, and kept checking his hands to make sure they were empty.

His daughter would live. He would go home to a wife whose face he knew the way you know a stranger's face from a description. And the bell would ring somewhere behind him, and the Market would tuck his wedding day into a jar and sell it to someone who'd never earned it.

"This place is obscene," I told Sefa.

"Yes," she said. She was looking at the Brokerage archway with an expression I couldn't file anywhere. "It's also honest. Do you know what the temples charged for miracles? Everything you had, and then they told you it was your fault you were poor. At least here the price is written on slate."

"That's the most damning endorsement of honesty I've ever heard."

"I was exiled from a dishonest institution. I have opinions."

Old Tally had drifted toward the Brokerage. I watched her stand before the black archway for a long moment, reading the carvings — the scales, and beneath them a line of small figures, sellers and buyers, and between them a third figure holding the balance beam. An intermediary. A broker.

She turned away from it faster than a woman turns away from something she has no reason to fear.

"Tally," I called. "What's the rate for souls?"

"Depends on the soul," she said, without turning around. "Come on. We need the gatekeeper's stall, and it's this way, and you shouldn't ask questions in this market that you don't already dread the answers to."

Which was, I'll grant her, excellent advice, and advice I ignored within the quarter hour.

* * *

The gatekeeper was a small old man — or a large child; the Market played games with proportions — who sat behind a counter made of stacked ledgers, each ledger fatter than the last. His eyes were the violet of the lanterns. When he looked at you, you felt itemized.

"Four travelers," he said. "Three gates to the deep road. Passage requires payment in truth held dear." He smiled, and the smile was correct in every technical respect. "What will you give?"

Sefa stepped forward first. She set both hands flat on the ledger-stack.

"My ordination day," she said. "The moment they laid hands on me and I believed — truly believed — the gods heard me. I've been trying to get it back ever since. Take it."

The gatekeeper studied her. Something in the lantern-light bent toward her, the way iron bends toward a lodestone.

"Accepted," he said softly. "Though I'll tell you what I tell few sellers: that memory is worth more than passage. You are undercharging."

"It's not for sale at its real price," Sefa said. "It's for sale at the price of getting us to Wren."

The bell rang. Sefa swayed. For half a heartbeat her face went smooth as a closed door — and then she blinked, and frowned faintly, as if she'd forgotten why she'd been about to cry, and stepped aside. She looked at her own hands. She didn't seem to recognize them as belonging to a priestess.

I hated this market. I want that noted too.

Old Tally paid next. What she gave, I don't know — the gatekeeper leaned close and listened, and whatever she whispered never touched the air between them. But afterward she came back to us moving carefully, like a ship that had taken water below the line, and when Bramble asked her what she'd traded she said, "None of your business, love," in a voice that ended conversations.

Then it was Bramble's turn, and Bramble — cheerful, light-fingered Bramble, who had picked my lock twice for practice and once as a joke — reached into his shirt and drew out a small tin whistle on a cord.

"Oh no," I said.

"Oh yes," he said.

"Bramble—"

"It's nothing," he said, which was the first lie, because his face was doing something complicated. "It's just a memory. One memory. The gatekeeper can have it."

"Which memory?" Sefa asked quietly. She'd recovered enough of herself to ask the right question, which is the whole job of a priestess, exiled or otherwise.

Bramble looked at the whistle. Turned it over. The tin was dented, worn silver-bright at the mouthpiece by years of a mouth.

"When I was small," he said, "before the streets, before any of it, I had a sister. Nell. She taught me this tune on this whistle — well, on the original whistle; this is the same one, actually, I stole it back from the man who stole it from our house, took me six years, but that's another story. She died when I was nine. Fever. And the memory I'm selling is the last night she played it. Both of us on the roof, whole city smoking below us, her teaching me the fingering, laughing every time I squeaked." He shrugged, elaborate and unconvincing. "It's a heavy memory. Heavy ones fetch good rates. I checked."

"You checked," I repeated.

"I always check. It's how you survive markets." He turned to the gatekeeper. "One memory. Full and complete. For four passages."

"Three," the gatekeeper said. "Her ladyship paid her own way."

"Fine. Three passages and my friends' dignity intact."

The gatekeeper extended a hand that was too many-jointed to be comfortable looking at. Bramble set the whistle-memory into it — I never saw how; the transfer happened the way transfers happen in dreams — and the bell rang, and Bramble staggered, caught himself on the ledger-stack, and stood there blinking at the tin whistle still hanging around his neck.

"Why do I have this?" he asked. His voice was genuinely puzzled, and it was the worst sound I'd heard all week, and I had recently heard a god speak.

"It's just an object now," the gatekeeper said. "The music is mine. You may keep the tin."

Bramble stared at it. Turned it over once. Then he tucked it back inside his shirt, next to his heart, which was either the saddest thing I'd seen in the Market or the most stubborn, and knowing Bramble it was both.

"Right," he said brightly, too brightly, the brightness of a lamp turned up to hide a draft. "Where's these gates, then?"

* * *

I paid last, because I am a coward about certain things and disciplined about the same things, and those turn out to be identical lists.

What did I give? I'll tell you, since everyone else's secrets are spilling all over this chapter anyway. I gave the gatekeeper the memory of the first map I ever finished — the one I drew at fourteen, of the smuggler pass above Mirabel's Garden, the one Tally pinned up in the common room and called, in front of everyone, *the work of a true road-witch.* It cost me three gates' passage and it was worth it, because that memory had done its work. It had made me. Maps don't need to be remembered to be true.

Or that's what I told myself, walking away, while somewhere behind me a bell rang and someone bought fourteen-year-old Vesper Sterling's proudest hour out of a jar.

Don't think about it. There's a road. Walk the road.

* * *

We were nearly to the first prayer-lock — I could see it ahead, a door of woven shadow that Sefa approached the way you approach a horse that hasn't decided about you — when I made my mistake.

I asked a question I dreaded the answer to.

The Brokerage archway was on our route whether I liked it or not, and passing it, I slowed, and looking at the carved figures — seller, buyer, broker — I said, to no one in particular:

"Who sells to them? Up top, I mean. Souls come from somewhere. Somebody on the surface is the supplier."

The gatekeeper had followed us — or rather, the Market had produced him again, the way it produces everything you need and nothing you want. He materialized at my elbow with the punctuality of a bill collector.

"Intermediaries," he said pleasantly. "Always intermediaries. The court beneath the mountain does not stoop to surface commerce. It prefers respectable channels. Brokers who move goods without asking what the goods are. Smugglers, often. Smugglers are ideal — they already know the hidden roads, they already ask no questions, and they already believe everything has a fair price."

The word *smugglers* went through the company like a knife through wet canvas.

Bramble stopped walking. Sefa's lips stopped moving mid-prayer. And Old Tally — Old Tally looked at the carved intermediary on the archway, the little figure holding the balance beam between buyer and seller, and her face did something I had never seen it do in twenty-two years of being raised by her.

It went guilty.

Not startled. Not angry. Guilty — the specific, practiced guilt of a person who has seen a thing before and hoped never to see it again.

"Tally," I said. My voice came out very level, which is what my voice does instead of shaking. "How does the Brokerage set a soul's weight?"

"By measure," she said, still not looking at me. "Standard rates. Child souls weigh light but fetch high. Everyone knows—"

She stopped. Too late. The sentence was already out in the world, wearing its boots.

"*Everyone knows,*" I repeated. "Everyone who?"

The gatekeeper smiled his technically perfect smile and consulted a ledger that had not existed a moment before.

"There was a sale registered through this brokerage," he said, "some years past. Intermediary-based. Surface origin. The commodity—" he ran a too-many-jointed finger down the page, savoring it, or performing the savoring, hard to say with gatekeepers, "—was sourced from a village called Mirabel's Garden. Registered to a child. Held in trust by the court beneath the mountain until maturity of contract."

The violet lanterns breathed. Somewhere a bell rang — someone else's loss, someone else's wedding day — and none of it mattered, none of the whole breathing market mattered, because the arithmetic had finished itself in my head and arrived at the only answer it had.

Wren's soul hadn't been stolen. It had been *sold*. Sold through channels. Sold by an intermediary who knew the hidden roads and asked no questions and believed everything had a fair price.

Sold by someone who could walk into a market like this one and whisper terms to a gatekeeper without the air between them ever hearing a word.

I turned to Old Tally. She was already looking at me. Had been, maybe, since the word *smuggler* left the gatekeeper's mouth.

"Who brokered Wren's soul?" I asked.

And Old Tally, matriarch of the Thornfell routes, the woman who taught me to read stars and tariffs and men, opened her mouth — and closed it — and said, in a voice I will hear for the rest of my half-mapped life:

"Not here, Vesper. Please. Not here."

Behind us, the prayer-locked door sighed open, waiting. Ahead, the deep road went down into the dark toward the court that held a child's soul in trust.

And beside me, my family fell silent, every one of them doing the same terrible math I was doing, arriving at the same terrible sum.

*Not here* meant *somewhere.* And *somewhere* meant *soon.*
