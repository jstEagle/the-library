## 9. Bramble's Ledger

There are three kinds of silence in the world, and I've made a modest living off all of them. There's the silence of a locked house at night, which is just an invitation with better manners. There's the silence of someone deciding whether to kill you, which you learn to hear in your teeth. And then there's the silence of four people sitting around a fire after learning that souls can be bought and sold like wheelwright's nails, and that one of them might have been delivered by *you*.

That third kind was the one sitting on our camp like a wet dog nobody would claim.

I'd been holding my tongue for two days. Two days of watching Vesper turn their maps over and over like a man looking for the fold where the lie lives. Two days of Sefa praying with her eyes open, which is the worst way. Two days of Old Tally smoking her pipe down to nothing and packing it again without lighting it, which for Tally is the equivalent of screaming.

And me? I'd been doing what I do best, which is nothing, beautifully.

"Bramble." Vesper said it without looking up from the map they were pretending to study. "You've checked that satchel eleven times since noon."

"Twelve," I said. "I'm thorough."

"What's in it?"

"Rope. Flint. A book of poetry I stole from a man who deserved it."

"And?"

I looked at the fire. The fire looked back, being fire and therefore having no shame whatsoever. Somewhere above us the Market of Small Mercies was still doing its night trade — you could hear the memory-brokers calling their prices in voices like warm oil, *memories of first snows, memories of mothers' kitchens, gently used griefs, going cheap* — and every sound of it sat on my chest like a cat that knew something.

"You know," I said, "in my line of work, there's a rule. Never confess to anything you can't be hanged for twice."

"We're not hanging anyone," Sefa said.

"That's what worries me. Hanging's quick. It's the *forgiveness* that takes forever."

Old Tally finally lit her pipe. The match flared and went out, and in that half-second of light her face did something old and tired and terrible. "Bramble," she said, in the voice she used when the ledgers didn't balance, "did you carry something to Mirabel's Garden?"

And there it was. The question I'd been swallowing whole for two days, laid out on the ground between us like a dead bird.

* * *

Let me tell you how a thief comes to be carrying souls, because I promise you it doesn't happen the way you're imagining. Nobody hands you a lantern full of somebody's self and says, mind the corners. That's not how evil works. Evil works like a courier service. Evil has excellent rates and never loses a package.

Three years ago — maybe four, the winters blur — I was broke in a way that had stopped being funny. I'd been running messages between the Thornfell passes, the usual smuggler traffic: letters, small parcels, bribes wrapped in oilcloth. Good work if you don't ask questions, and I have never once asked a question in my life that I couldn't also steal the answer to.

The broker found me in a tavern in Lowmark. He called himself Master Quillon, and he looked like what happens when a tax collector marries a funeral director. Soft voice, soft hands, and eyes that counted things even when he was looking at people. He paid triple the going rate for deliveries along one particular route: up through the Thornfell passes, to a series of shrines and way-stations, always at night, always alone.

The packages were small. Heavy for their size. Cold — not winter-cold, but cellar-cold, meat-locker cold, the kind of cold that gets into your knuckles and stays. I told myself it was candle wax. Religious goods. Some sects pay well for consecrated tallow, and who am I to judge a man's tallow?

I carried them for a year.

Do you know what the worst part is? Not the guilt. Guilt I can manage; guilt is just a debt, and I understand debts. The worst part is that I was *good* at it. Never lost a package. Never opened one. Never let the cold bother me past the second mile. I took pride in it. I remember standing on a ridge above the passes with one of those parcels against my ribs, watching the sun come up over Thornfell, feeling like a professional.

Somewhere inside that parcel was a person. Or the part of a person that makes them a person instead of meat that talks. And I was admiring the view.

* * *

"I carried for a broker named Quillon," I said, when I could make my mouth work. "A year, maybe more. Small parcels. Heavy. Cold."

Nobody said anything. The fire popped, showing off.

"I told myself it was wax. I want that on the record. I told myself it was wax, and I believed myself, because believing yourself is the one theft nobody can prosecute."

"Mirabel's Garden," Vesper said. Not a question. They'd done the arithmetic already — of course they had, they're a cartographer, arithmetic is their native tongue. "One of your deliveries came to Mirabel's Garden."

"Two years ago. Autumn." I pulled the ledger out of my satchel — the actual ledger, the one I'd been checking eleven times since noon, though checking it couldn't change a single line of it. I keep records. Thieves who don't keep records end up hanged or famous, and I've got no head for fame. "Route nine. Delivery to a house on the shrine road. The one with the blue door."

Sefa made a sound like she'd been struck.

"The Hollis house," she said. "The blue door. That's—" She stopped. She's a priestess, or was, or is again depending on how you count these things, and I watched her do the priestess math: Wren Hollis, soulless, walking around Mirabel's Garden like a coat left on a hook. "Wren was eight."

"She'd have been six," I said. "When I carried it."

I put the ledger down between us, open to the page, because hiding it now would be like drowning after the ship's already gone down. The entry was in my own hand, neat as scripture: *Quillon pkg., route 9, blue door, night delivery, paid in full.* Below it, in the margin, in ink I barely remembered using: *cold.*

I'd written *cold* in the margin. Some part of me had known even then, and had filed it away under weather.

* * *

Here's what happened next, and I want it understood that I am not proud of any of it, including the parts that sound noble.

Vesper closed the ledger. Gently. That was somehow worse than if they'd thrown it in the fire.

"When did you know?" they asked. "Actually know. Not suspect. Know."

"The market," I said. "Yesterday. When the memory-broker mentioned the court buys from intermediaries on the surface. Intermediaries." I laughed, and it came out wrong, the way laughter does when it's carrying too much. "I was an intermediary. I was the last pair of hands. You don't sell a soul across a counter, do you understand? Too many questions. Too much weight. You send it by courier, parcel-wrapped, marked *fragile*, and the courier tells himself it's wax."

"Why didn't you say anything?" Sefa's voice cracked on it. "Two days, Bramble. We walked two days with you knowing."

"Because saying it makes it true." I heard my own voice go flat and hard, the tavern voice, the street voice, the one I use so I don't have to feel anything while I'm using it. "As long as I kept my mouth shut, I was a man who maybe carried candles. The moment I opened my mouth, I was the man who delivered Wren Hollis to hell in a parcel marked fragile. And I liked being the candle man. He was a good man. I'd known him for years."

Tally drew on her pipe, and the smoke went up straight in the still air, like something climbing toward a judgment it wasn't going to reach in time.

"Quillon," she said quietly. "Describe him again."

"Soft. Careful. Paid in old coin." I frowned, because describing him properly meant actually looking at him in memory, and I'd spent three years not doing that. "Old coin, Tally. Pre-Regency marks. Who pays couriers in pre-Regency marks?"

"Someone whose money's older than the country," Tally said. She wasn't looking at me anymore. She was looking at the dark past the firelight, up toward Thornfell, and her face had gone to stone, and I thought — not yet, I didn't understand yet, I wouldn't understand until the river — but I saw that she recognized the name, or the shape of the name, and that recognition frightened her more than my confession frightened me.

Which, given what my confession contained, is saying something.

* * *

I want to tell you about the night after, because the confession isn't the story. The confession is just the door. The story is what people do in the room.

Sefa didn't speak to me for an hour. Then she sat down next to me — close, closer than she needed to, close enough that her shoulder touched mine — and said, "In the temple we had a word for this. *Anathema-carried*. It means you transported a sin without committing it. The elders argued for a hundred years about whether the carrier shares the guilt."

"And what did they decide?"

"They decided whatever was convenient, same as everyone." She almost smiled. "But the rite exists. For carriers. It's a washing, mostly. Water and words. The words are the important part." She looked into the fire. "They cast me out before I ever learned whether I believed it worked."

"That's not much of a comfort, Sefa."

"No," she agreed. "It's not. But it's company, and company is most of what comfort ever was."

Vesper took longer. Vesper sat with the ledger until the fire burned down to coals, reading my own handwriting back to me like a sentence being composed. And then, near midnight, they said:

"You kept records."

"It's what I do."

"You kept records of every delivery. Dates. Routes. Payments." They turned to look at me, and in the coal-light their face was unreadable, which for a cartographer means the opposite of blank — it means there's a whole map there and they haven't decided whether to show you. "Bramble. Your ledger is a trail. Every soul Quillon moved, every route, every drop point, for a year. Do you understand what you've been carrying?"

"A load of guilt with excellent penmanship?"

"A map." They tapped the ledger. "The court beneath the mountain has been buying from the surface through intermediaries, and nobody has ever held a record of the traffic. Because brokers burn their books. But couriers—" and here something came into their face that I will call hope only because I don't have a better word, though hope seemed like a rich thing to be feeling about me just then, "couriers keep receipts."

I stared at him. "You're telling me my shame is useful."

"I'm telling you everything honest is useful eventually. It's the lies that depreciate."

Old Tally laughed then — one short bark, smoke and gravel — and reached over and cuffed the back of my head, gentle, the way she did when I was small and had stolen the wrong thing. "Took you long enough, boy. I've watched you circle that confession for two days like a moth round a lamp."

"You knew?"

"I suspected you were carrying something heavier than rope. I didn't know what." Her face went old again, and quiet. "There's more than one person in this company who's been circling a lamp, Bramble. Remember that when the judging starts."

* * *

We broke camp late the next morning, because none of us had slept much and because the road down from the market wound through country that didn't reward haste — hills that rearranged themselves when you weren't praying at them, which is a sentence I never expected to write, and less expected to mean.

I walked beside Vesper. My ledger rode in my satchel, and for the first time in three years it felt less like evidence against me and more like a tool I hadn't known I was holding. Every entry was a thread. Every thread ran back to Quillon, and Quillon ran back to — somewhere. Something. The court bought from the surface; the surface sold through men like Quillon; and men like Quillon needed routes, and routes get mapped, and maps, as a certain person kept reminding us, are just prayers drawn to scale.

"Do you hate me?" I asked, somewhere past the second ridge. "For the waiting. For the lying. Pick a crime, there's a menu."

Vesper considered it honestly, which is the thing about them I both admire and find deeply inconvenient.

"I hate that you waited," they said. "And I understand why you waited, and I'd probably have waited longer. Both of those are true, and I'm learning to hold true things that fight each other. It seems to be the local custom." They glanced sideways at me. "Besides. If we're keeping score of secrets, I've a mother-shaped hole in mine, Tally's got a silence the size of a valley, and Sefa prays like she's apologizing to the gods individually. You're in respectable company, Bramble. We're all forged documents."

I laughed — a real one this time, surprised out of me — and it echoed off the hills, and one of the hills, I swear on every god living and otherwise, echoed it back a half-beat late, like it was checking the words for contraband.

By afternoon we could see it ahead: the river, wide and slow and silver-grey, moving without any wind to move it, running through the valley like a vein of mercury through flesh. The River That Remembers. Sefa had gone quiet as we crested the rise. Tally had gone quieter.

There was a ferryman's post on the near bank, and a sign nailed to it in three languages and one language that wasn't a language at all, just a spiral that made my teeth ache to read. The sign said, in the trade-tongue: *Drink deep. No refunds.*

"Crossing at dusk," Vesper said, consulting a map that I noticed, with a cartographer's private horror, had begun annotating itself. "We drink, we cross, we keep moving."

I looked at that grey water, and I thought about margins. About the little note in my own hand, *cold*, filed under weather for three years. About what the river might file under my name, and whose faces it might keep in its banks.

"Vesper," I said, as we started down toward the water. "If it shows you something about me worse than what I confessed—"

"It won't," they said. "It shows truest memories, not worst ones. Those aren't always the same thing." A pause. "Though in your case, I'll admit the odds aren't comforting."

"Thank you. Truly. Your pastoral gifts are wasted on cartography."

The river waited, patient as a debt, and somewhere behind us the moon was already counting.
