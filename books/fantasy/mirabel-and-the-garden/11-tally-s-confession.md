## 11. Tally's Confession

There are two kinds of lies a smuggler tells. The small ones keep you alive — the ones about cargo, about routes, about who you were seen drinking with last night. And then there are the big ones, the ones you tell so long they stop feeling like lies and start feeling like furniture. You lean on them every day. You dust them off. You'd notice their absence the way you'd notice a missing wall.

I had been leaning on mine for eleven years.

The river knew it before I opened my mouth. That was the cruelty of the thing — the River That Remembers didn't ask for confessions. It just held up its water like a mirror and waited, patient as a debt collector, while each of us drank or didn't drink and found out what we were made of.

Vesper drank first. I watched my child — because that's what they were, whatever name the years had given us, my child in every way that mattered and several ways that hadn't been legal since before either of us was born — I watched them go still as a dead man's compass over the water. Then they looked at me. Just looked. And I knew the river had shown them my face beside their mother's, and I knew there was no furniture left to hide behind.

"You were there," Vesper said. Not a question. Questions give a person somewhere to stand. This was just a fact laid out on the riverbank between us like a body.

"I was there," I said.

Sefa made a sound low in her throat, half prayer and half curse, which for Sefa is practically a hymn. Bramble took one step backward, then another, the way he does when he's calculating exits. Old habit. Even now, even here, with nowhere in any world to run that the river wouldn't remember.

"Sit down," I said. "All of you. If I'm going to be flayed, I'd rather it be done proper, with an audience."

* * *

You have to understand what Mirabel's Garden was, eleven years ago, if you're going to understand what I did. It wasn't the soft little village Vesper maps with such tenderness these days. It was dying. The passes had closed — rockfall on the north road, soldiers on the south, and the Thornfell routes, our routes, choked with tolls from the garrison at Kestrel Ford. Three winters of bad harvest stacked on top. Children with hollow cheeks. Wren's mother grinding bark into bread flour and calling it supper.

And then a man came down from the mountain. Or something wearing the shape of a man. He called himself Corvane, and he dressed fine, and he spoke soft, and he sat in my kitchen the way only someone very old sits — like the chair was a courtesy he was granting *it*.

He made an offer. Routes reopened. Tolls lifted. Soldiers struck blind at the ford, though he phrased it prettier than that. All he wanted in trade was small things, he said. Trifles. A memory here, a year of someone's luck there. And once — only once, he said, and never again — a soul. Not a whole one. A child's, unformed, light as a coin. The court beneath the mountain collected them, he said, the way we collect rent. It was simply how the world balanced its books.

I told him no. I want that written down somewhere, in whatever ledger the gods keep: I told him no, and I meant it, and I went to bed believing I would die poor and honest on the right side of that particular line.

But he came back. They always come back. And the third time, he brought proof — showed me the north pass open again, clear as spring water, because a village two valleys over had paid. Showed me their granaries full. Their children pink-cheeked. And he said the words I've spent eleven years trying to drown:

*"Someone will pay, Old Tally. The only question is whether it's your people or someone else's."*

That's the arithmetic of evil, if you want to know. It doesn't roar. It doesn't tempt. It just hands you an abacus and waits for you to do what merchants do.

* * *

"I didn't choose Wren," I said, and my voice came out wrong, scraped thin, like rope dragged over stone. "I want that understood too. I gave him a list of three names. Families gone, families moving through, families nobody would miss enough to make trouble. His mother's name was on it because she'd told me — told me, Tally, take anything of mine you need, we're all drowning together — and I thought... saints forgive me, I thought a family already that far under wouldn't feel the cut."

Bramble spoke first, which surprised me. Bramble, who trusts nothing and no one and least of all himself.

"You *listed* them." His voice cracked in the middle. "You sat at your table with your tea and you wrote down children."

"I did." There was no version of this where I flinched away from it. Flinching was how I'd survived eleven years; it could not be how I faced it. "I sat at my table with my tea, boy, and I wrote down three children, and I told myself the court only ever takes one, and I told myself the odds were mercy, and I poured myself a second cup and slept that night. That's what I did. Every word of it true, and every word of it damning, and I won't insult you by dressing it in grief now."

The fire had burned down to coals. Nobody fed it. Above us the Thornfell peaks wore the moon like a wound that wouldn't close, thirteen nights short of full, and somewhere under all that stone a child's soul hung in a lantern and my name was stitched into the chain.

Vesper hadn't moved. That frightened me more than shouting would have. Vesper angry is weather — loud, dramatic, over in an hour, followed by rain and forgiveness. Vesper quiet is a landslide deciding which way to fall.

"The debt," they said finally. "Ashen-Mouth's debt. Your bargain with my mother. Was that part of it? Was I on the list too?"

"No." And this I could say clean, because it was true, and after so many years of furniture-lies the true things felt strange in my mouth, new-minted. "Your mother came to me after. After Wren. She'd heard whispers — she always heard everything, that woman, half witch if she wasn't careful — and she worked out what I'd done before I confessed it. She came to my door at night with her eyes like knives and she said: *you'll not touch my child, and you'll not touch theirs, and if the mountain wants a price from this family ever again, I'll pay it myself.*"

"And Ashen-Mouth?"

"She went looking for him. Found him. Bargained with him — I don't know the terms, Vesper, I swear it on the road itself, I don't know what she promised or why. I only know she came back gray-faced and older and told me the debt was hers now, and if it ever came due, it would fall to her blood. She made me swear to watch over you if anything happened to her." My voice went, then. Eleven years of furniture and it chose that moment to collapse. "She died six months later and I told myself the debt died with her. Lies are comfortable beds. You can sleep in them a long time."

* * *

Sefa stood. I expected fury from her — she'd been cast out of her order for believing gods still listened, and here was proof positive that when they listened, what they mostly heard was women like me selling children. But her anger, when it came, was quiet, which is the worst kind.

"I prayed for Wren," she said. "The night we found her. Before I knew any of this. I knelt in that shrine and I begged whatever was left listening to look at that empty little girl and *do something*." Her hands were shaking. "And the god answered. And the answer was you. The answer has been you the whole time, sitting at our fire, mending our socks."

"Sefa—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't you dare say my name like we're friends tonight. I came on this road because I believed the silence of the gods was the cruelest thing in the world." She looked at me a long moment, and there was something worse than hatred in her face — there was pity, which no sinner survives well. "I was wrong. The cruelest thing is what people do in the silence *before* the gods come back."

She walked to the far edge of camp and sat with her back to us, and I let her go, because chasing her would have been one more theft — stealing comfort I hadn't earned.

Bramble stayed by the fire, turning something over in his fingers. One of his lockpicks. He does that when his thoughts get loud.

"My delivery," he said quietly. "The sealed box I carried to Mirabel's Garden, the winter I was fourteen. Paid in silver, no questions asked." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious. "That was yours. That was your bargain, coming down the mountain in my hands."

"I didn't know you, boy. I'd never have—"

"You didn't need to know me." He laughed, and it was an awful sound. "That's the whole trick, isn't it? Nobody needs to know anybody. Names on lists. Boxes without labels. Everyone innocent, everyone just carrying, and a little girl walks around with holes where her soul should be." He stood, shoved the pick back in his sleeve. "I'm going to check the perimeter. Don't follow me. I might say something I mean."

And then it was just Vesper and me and the coals and the moon counting down its turns like a moneylender tapping a ledger.

* * *

"You should banish me," I said. "From the company. Tonight. It's what the law would do, if the law reached this far up the mountain, and it's what I'd do, sitting where you sit."

Vesper said nothing. They were doing cartographer-things with their face, which is to say measuring me, taking bearings, fixing my position relative to everything else they loved. I've watched them survey impossible terrain with that same expression. I knew I was being mapped. I knew I would not survive the drawing.

"Wren's mother," Vesper said at last. "When she made you swear to watch over me. Did she know what you'd done?"

"Yes."

"And she stayed anyway. Broke bread with you. Left me to you."

"She did." I swallowed. "Your mother believed in second chances the way other folk believe in gravity. Said a person was a route, not a destination — that you couldn't judge the whole road by the worst mile of it." I laughed, and it hurt. "I used that line for years, Vesper. Used it to forgive myself. Stole her mercy and wore it like a coat."

"It fit poorly," Vesper said. "It always hung wrong on you. I noticed. I just didn't know why."

There it was. The knife, turned gentle. Of course they'd noticed. Vesper notices everything — it's why their maps are worth killing for. They had felt the lie in me the way a good navigator feels a current under a calm sea, some wrongness in the pull, and they had loved me anyway, and loved me anyway, and loved me anyway, and every one of those anyways was a weight I'd added to the scale myself.

"I'm not asking forgiveness," I said. "Understand that. Forgiveness is yours to give or not, and either way I'll carry what I carried up the mountain. What I'm asking is simpler and harder. Let me finish the road. Let me stand outside that court when you go in. Let me be the one who carries the lantern home if — when — you bring her soul back. Not because I've earned it. Because someone in this company owes that child a debt that can't be paid in memories or riddles, and I am that someone, and I will crawl the rest of the way on my knees if my legs give out."

The wind came down off the peaks and moved the coals, and for a moment the fire leapt and threw our shadows huge against the rocks — mine bent and crooked, Vesper's straight as a surveyor's line.

"You lied to me my whole life," Vesper said.

"Yes."

"You raised me on lies."

"On the best lies I had. Which is to say, badly."

Something moved at the corner of their mouth. Not a smile. The ancestor of a smile, maybe, some fossil of the kid who used to steal my tobacco and blame the goats. Then it was gone, and what remained was harder and sadder and more honest than anything I'd taught them.

"I need to think," Vesper said. "And you need to be somewhere I'm not, until I have."

I nodded. I gathered my bedroll. And as I turned to go, Vesper spoke once more, quiet enough that the river almost kept it:

"Tally. The list. The other two names. Who were they?"

And that — that question, asked in that voice — was the moment I understood that the confession wasn't finished. That the river had shown Vesper my face beside their mother's, but rivers show what they please, and there was more in me yet, more furniture, more ledgers, one more entry I had sworn on my mother's grave and my own damnation never to speak aloud.

Because the truth was this: there had been a fourth name. Written smaller, crossed out, but written in my hand. And the reason I had crossed it out — the reason I had begged Corvane to take anyone, *anyone*, but that one — was a reason that would burn what was left of this company to ash and salt the ground behind it.

I looked at Vesper, my child, my judge, my last chance at being anything better than the woman who wrote lists of children, and I opened my mouth to lie one final time.

The river laughed.
