## 12. Exile on the Under-Road

I want to tell you that I banished her with a steady voice.

I want to tell you that the words came out clean, that I said *go* the way a judge says it, the way a god says it, with the weight of law behind me and no tremor in the hand. But there is no law out here. That's the whole trouble with the under-road. No magistrates, no gallows, no village stocks to lock a woman in while the town decides what she's worth. There's only the company, and the company is whatever I say it is, and what I said was:

"You can't walk with us anymore."

Tally took it the way she took everything — standing up, arms crossed, chin set at the angle she used when the toll-collectors got greedy and she wanted them to know she'd counted every coin twice. She was smaller than I remembered her being. That's the trick of a lifetime, I suppose. You build someone up out of campfire stories and hard bread and the hundred small rescues of a childhood, and then one river shows you her face beside your mother's at a bargain, and all that architecture comes down at once, and underneath it there's just an old woman with river-damp boots and knuckles like walnut shells.

"You're going to need me," she said.

"I'm going to need a lot of things. Sleep. A straight road. A mother who didn't sell children." I heard my own voice go sharp and was, in some distant, cartographer's part of my mind, interested by it. Sharpness is what I reach for when the ground gives way. You draw the line harder when you're not sure the line will hold.

"Vesper." Sefa's hand on my arm. Gentle. Priestess-gentle, the kind of touch that had probably talked a hundred grieving families off a hundred ledges before the Church decided her faith was a liability. "You don't have to do this now. You can—"

"I can and I have." I pulled my arm free, not unkindly, but free. "She looked at me on the riverbank and she lied. She let me drink that water and she stood next to me and she *waited* to see if I'd see it, and when I saw it she said, *Vesper, it isn't what it looks like.* It is exactly what it looks like. It has been exactly what it looks like for eleven years."

Bramble had gone very still by the pack-mule, which for Bramble was a kind of honesty. He knew what brokers looked like. He'd carried their letters. He knew the shape of a woman deciding how much truth a room could hold.

"Eleven years," Tally said quietly.

"Eleven years since you brokered Wren's soul. Since you sat across from whatever thing wears a face down here and shook on it. Since my mother—" My voice did something I didn't authorize. I let it. "Since you and my mother sat down together and decided that a road was worth more than a child."

"It was worth *every* child," Tally said, and there it was, the old iron in her, the thing that had kept forty smugglers fed through three famines and a war. "You think I don't know what I did? You think I've slept? The routes were dying, girl. The Thornfell passes were closing one by one, the tolls were bleeding us, and the thing under the mountain offered me a road that never closes. Never floods. Never freezes. One soul. *One.* It gave me the arithmetic and I did the arithmetic, and I have been doing that arithmetic every night for eleven years and it has never once come out clean."

"Then you should have stopped counting."

"I should have," she agreed, and that agreement was somehow worse than any argument. "But I didn't. And now you're standing here doing sums of your own, and I'd wager my last tooth you're not coming out clean either."

I looked at Sefa. Sefa looked at the ground. Bramble examined a buckle on the mule's harness with the concentration of a man defusing a bomb.

Because she was right, you understand. That's the filthy heart of it. Banishment is easy when the banished is a stranger. When it's the woman who pulled you out of a snowdrift at seven, who taught you to read contour lines by candlelight, who fed you through the winter your mother didn't come back from — when it's her, then banishment is just another arithmetic, and nobody's sums come out clean.

* * *

We made camp that night in a hollow of the under-road where the walls glowed faintly with something that wasn't quite moss and wasn't quite lichen and wasn't quite interested in being named. Sefa built a fire from wood that had never been a tree — down here, fuel was another thing geography couldn't be bothered to explain — and we ate in silence, the four of us, or the three of us and the woman who was no longer one of us and had nowhere else to be.

That's the cruelty of exile on the under-road. Above ground, you banish someone and the world receives them. There are other villages, other roads, other fires. Down here there is only the road we're on and the dark it runs through, and the dark, we had learned, has opinions about people who travel alone.

"She can't walk back," Sefa said to me, low, by the fire. We'd stepped away from the camp, far enough for privacy, not far enough for safety. There was no far enough for safety. "The Thornfell Gate won't open backward for her. You know that. The geography here runs on — on *intention*, Vesper. On prayer, almost. She can't intend her way home. None of us can. We can only go forward."

"Then she goes forward behind us. Alone."

"Alone, in a realm where the last lone traveler we met had been walking for so long he'd forgotten his own name and asked us if we'd seen it."

I said nothing. The fire popped. Somewhere far off in the dark, something made a sound like a bell being unmade.

"I'm not asking you to forgive her," Sefa said. "Gods know I'm not sure I have. I'm asking you to remember that mercy isn't forgiveness. Mercy is just refusing to be cruel when cruelty would be *tidier*."

"You sound like a priestess."

"I was a priestess. It's a disease. It flares up."

I almost laughed. It came out as something broken and adjacent to laughing, which is, I've found, how most laughter comes out when it matters.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat at the edge of the firelight with my map case open on my knees, and I did the thing I do when the world won't hold still: I drew.

The under-road unspooled behind us and ahead of us, and I charted what we'd passed — the Gate, the market, the river that remembers, the long galleries where the walls sweated light. And then, because my hands were honest even when my heart wasn't, I charted forward. The route to the court beneath the mountain. The approaches. The places where the road narrowed and the dark thickened and the walls grew faces, or grew things that wished they had faces.

And I found, somewhere in the middle of the third sheet, that I was drawing a second route. A branch. A way for one traveler, alone, to follow at a distance — to keep the firelight of the company just in sight, to walk the same dark without walking in it. A road for someone who had been banished but not abandoned, and who needed never to know the difference, or needed to know it exactly, I couldn't decide which.

That's the thing about maps. People think they record the world. They don't. They record what the mapmaker can't stop caring about.

I worked until the false-moss dimmed toward what passed for dawn down there, and when I was done I had two documents. The first was the company's route, clean and true. The second was a single sheet, folded small, and on it I had drawn the under-road from the river crossing to the court's outer approaches — every safe hollow, every water-seep, every stretch where the dark thinned enough to rest in. And at the places where a lone walker would need to make a choice, I marked the right choice. Not in words. In the old smuggler's shorthand Tally herself had taught me, the one we'd used for years to mark routes the toll-men must never find: a hook for shelter, a slash for danger, a circle for water, a star — her star, the one she put on every map she ever drew, the one I'd copied without thinking my whole life — for *this way, and trust it*.

I marked the way with her own star. Eleven times. Once for each year.

* * *

Morning — the under-road's version of it, the dimming and re-brightening of the walls that we'd agreed to call morning because calling it anything else was worse — came gray and slow, and I walked to where Tally had made her own fire apart from ours, because she was a proud woman and proud women exile themselves a little further every hour.

She was awake. Of course she was awake. She'd probably never slept.

"You'll want to keep to the high gallery after the second water-seep," she said, before I'd even stopped walking. "The low route looks faster but the dark pools there. Sits like a fog. Eats resolve. I've been watching how this place works. It runs on—"

"Intention. I know." I held out the folded sheet. "Take this."

She looked at it and didn't take it. "What is it?"

"A map."

"I know what a map looks like, girl. I taught you maps. What's *on* it?"

"Your route. Behind us. At a distance." I heard my own voice go flat and official, the voice I used for clients, for strangers, for anyone I didn't want to know that my hands were shaking. "You'll follow us. Half a mile back, no closer. You'll see our fire and we won't see yours, or you'll be clever enough to hide it, which you are. You'll not speak to us. You'll not come into camp. And when this is done — when Wren's soul is back under the sky where it belongs — then we'll see what's left to say to each other. Maybe nothing. Probably nothing. But you'll be alive to hear it, and you'll be alive because I put every safe hollow between the river and the court on that page, and because I marked the choices with your own star, because I am apparently incapable of learning anything."

Tally took the map. Her hands, those walnut-knuckled hands that had taught me to hold a stylus, that had pulled me from snow and fire and one memorable flood, closed around that folded sheet like it was the last warm thing in the world, and for a moment her face did something I had never seen it do in nineteen years.

It came apart.

Not weeping, exactly. Tally didn't weep; she *cracked*, the way stone cracks, along old lines, quietly, in a way you'd miss if you blinked. "You're a better woman than me," she said. "That's the whole of it. That's the sum. You're a better woman than me, and I made you, and I don't know how that happened."

"You didn't make me. You *mapped* me." I turned away before she could see what my face was doing. "There's a difference. Maps can be redrawn."

I walked back to the fire. Sefa looked up. Bramble looked up. The mule, who throughout this entire ordeal had displayed more emotional intelligence than any of us, looked up.

"She follows at a distance," I said. "She stays out of sight. And if she dies back there in the dark, I'll—" My voice went. I stood there with three faces turned toward me and a god's debt around my neck and half a company and a whole broken heart, and I finished it anyway. "I'll carry it. That's all. Some things you just carry."

Sefa nodded slowly, and there was something in her face that was not quite approval and not quite pity and was probably, in the end, the closest thing to blessing I'd get. Bramble reached over and squeezed my wrist, once, hard, the way you'd check a rope before trusting it with your weight.

We broke camp. We walked. The under-road unspooled ahead of us, down and down, toward the gilded dark, toward the court where souls hung like lanterns and one of them, eleven years burning, was Wren's.

And half a mile back, just at the edge of what the eye could hold and the heart could deny, a small fire kindled and followed. I didn't look at it. I looked at it the whole time.

We walked through the morning that wasn't morning, and the walls around us began, very slowly, to change. The rough dark of the under-road gave way to something smoother, something worked, something *finished* — stone dressed by hands that had never been human, veined with gold that moved like it was alive. The air grew warm and thick and sweet, sweet like offerings, sweet like the inside of a temple, and far below us — or perhaps far ahead, distance being a suggestion down there — I began to hear it.

Music.

Thin, and patient, and old. The kind of music that has been playing so long it has stopped being entertainment and become architecture.

Sefa stopped walking. Bramble stopped walking. Even the mule stopped, ears flat, and I stood at the front of my small, broken, loyal company with a banished woman at my back and a stolen soul ahead, and I listened to the court beneath the mountain welcome us the way a spider welcomes — silently, beautifully, and with every thread already in place.

The road bent downward one last time, and opened, and showed us the light.
