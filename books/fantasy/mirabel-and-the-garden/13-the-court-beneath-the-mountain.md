## 13. The Court Beneath the Mountain

There is a rule in cartography that I have lived by since I was old enough to hold a compass: the map is not the territory, but it should at least *argue* with the territory. A good map disagrees with the world politely — here a ridge where the eye sees none, there a river that has wandered since the last survey. The argument is the whole point. It keeps both parties honest.

The road down into the court beneath the mountain did not argue with anything. It simply declined to exist on any terms I understood.

We had been descending for the better part of a day through Bramble's under-road, which was less a tunnel than a grudge the mountain had against itself — passages that folded back on each other like a liar's story, staircases that went down so long they came out going up. Bramble navigated by touch and superstition, trailing his left hand along the wall and muttering apologies to things I chose not to look at too closely. Sefa walked behind him with her prayer-beads wrapped around her fist like brass knuckles. Old Tally — exiled, disgraced, following us anyway at a distance of exactly forty paces, close enough to hear, far enough to claim she wasn't with us — brought up the rear with a lantern she refused to let anyone else carry.

And me? I had stopped drawing four hours ago.

I want you to understand what that means. I have drawn maps during rockslides. I have drawn maps while being shot at by men whose tolls I'd declined to pay. My hands shake less than most people's opinions. But somewhere around the third hour of descent, when the passage widened into a gallery whose ceiling was studded with what looked like fossilized stars, I looked down at my page and found that I had been drawing the same corridor over and over — eleven times, identical, down to a crack in the floor that appeared in every sketch whether or not it existed in front of me.

The paper had given up before I had. That takes a special kind of failure.

"Your face is doing something ugly," Sefa said, without turning around.

"I'm reconciling accounts," I said. "The ledger's coming up short."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning everything I know about how space works is a rumor, and we're walking through the correction."

Bramble snorted. "Welcome to the under-road, cartographer. Down here, the only honest map is the one you draw after you've survived."

"That's not a map," I said. "That's a confession."

"Same thing, mostly."

He wasn't wrong, which annoyed me more than usual, because I needed him to be wrong about something, anything, or else the last twenty years of my life were an elaborate joke told by geography at my expense.

* * *

The change came as a smell first: beeswax and cold iron and something sweeter underneath, something like fruit left too long in a sealed room. Then a warmth that had no source. Then light — not the lantern-light we carried, but a gold glow rising from below like the memory of a sunset that had gotten lost and decided to settle underground.

Bramble stopped so suddenly I nearly walked into his back.

"We're here," he said. His voice had gone strange — flat, careful, the voice of a man standing very still near a sleeping animal.

"Here where?" Sefa asked. "I thought the market was the—"

"The market's a porch." He swallowed. "This is the house."

The passage opened.

I have stood at the lip of the Thornfell gorge at dawn and felt small. I have seen the sea once, from a cliff, and understood why people drown themselves. Neither prepared me for the court beneath the mountain.

It was a cavern, but *cavern* is a word for empty places, and this place was not empty. It was full — full of gold, yes, veins of it running through the walls like the mountain had been gilded from the inside out, but that wasn't the half of it. Stairways spiraled up the cavern walls into darkness above, hundreds of them, carved with a precision no human hand could hold steady enough for. Bridges arched between towers of black stone. And everywhere, everywhere, hung lights.

Lanterns, I thought at first. Thousands upon thousands of lanterns, drifting at every height, casting that warm gold across everything, swaying gently in a wind I couldn't feel.

Then one drifted closer, and I saw what was inside the glass.

Not flame. A light that moved. That pressed its palms — and it had palms, small ones, child-sized — against the inside of the glass. That looked at me.

"Oh," said Sefa, very quietly. Her beads slipped from her fist. She didn't pick them up. "Oh, they're *people.*"

"Souls," Bramble said. "Told you. They hang them."

"How many?" I asked, though I already knew the answer was a number that made the question obscene.

"All of them," Bramble said. "All the ones nobody came back for."

One of the lanterns drifted near enough that I could see the soul clearly: an old woman's shape, folded small, her light dimmed to a coal-glow. She wasn't beating on the glass. She was watching us with the patience of someone who had watched ten thousand travelers pass and knew the odds. As I met whatever served her for eyes, she raised one hand — slowly, deliberately — and pointed down the great central stair, toward the heart of the court.

Toward the throne.

"She's giving us directions," I said, and heard my own voice come out thin.

"They do that." Bramble's jaw was tight. "Some of them remember being helpful. Being useful. It's all they've got left." He started down the stairs without waiting, and I noticed he kept his eyes fixed on the steps and never, not once, let his gaze linger on any single lantern. There was a discipline in it. A practiced grief.

I made myself look. Someone should. I took out my notebook and I drew them — quick sketches, dozens, each lantern with whatever detail I could catch: the old woman pointing; a young man whose light flickered in rhythms like a heartbeat; two souls hanging side by side, holding hands through the glass, their lights pulsing together. If the world above had forgotten these people, then at least there would be a record. Maps are arguments with the world. This one was an accusation.

Sefa fell into step beside me. "What are you doing?"

"My job."

"Vesper." She touched my elbow, gentle. "You can't map them home."

"No," I agreed. "But I can make sure somebody knows they were here."

She didn't answer, but she walked a little closer after that, and her shoulder stayed near mine the whole way down.

* * *

The etiquette began before we saw anyone who could enforce it.

It started with the stairs. Halfway down the great spiral, the steps changed — the rough-hewn stone gave way to marble so polished it showed our reflections, and our reflections were wrong. They walked a half-step behind us. They bowed when we didn't. Mine smiled at me with an expression I have never worn in my life and hope never to wear: deference. Eager, hungry deference.

"Don't look at them," Bramble murmured. "And when we get to the bottom, don't speak until spoken to. Don't touch the gold. Don't accept anything — not food, not drink, not a compliment offered as a gift. And whatever happens—" he glanced back at Tally, forty paces up, "—nobody mentions debts out loud unless they mean to pay one."

"Why?" Sefa asked.

"Because down here a debt isn't a figure of speech. Say you owe someone and the court hears it, the court *holds* it. Words are contracts. Names are signatures." He paused at a landing where the marble bore an inscription in a script that hurt to read — the letters seemed to rearrange themselves whenever I blinked, always just short of legible. "Everything here is older than kings. Kings are a *fashion*. The etiquette is the law."

"And if we break the etiquette?" I asked.

"Then something enforces it." He resumed walking. "You won't see it coming. Nobody sees it coming. That's rather the point of it."

"What kind of something?"

"The kind that was old when the old gods were young," Bramble said, "and thought they were terribly promising."

I decided not to ask follow-up questions.

At the base of the stairs stood a gate — not locked, not even closed, just *there*: two pillars of black stone topped with lanterns whose souls had gone dark as spent coals. Between the pillars, the air shimmered faintly, like heat off summer stone. Bramble stopped before it, straightened his ragged coat, ran his fingers through his hair, and became — I watched it happen — someone else. His slouch smoothed into a bow. His thief's shuffle became a gliding walk. He looked, briefly, like a man who owned things.

"When we're inside," he said, "I'm Master Quill, a licensed procurer of curiosities. Sefa is my associate. You're my surveyor. Tally—" he raised his voice slightly, without turning, "—is nobody. She waits outside."

"The hell I do," Tally's voice floated down, gravel and iron.

"You can't come in. Your name's known here, Tally. *Sold* things here." A silence. "You know what they do to sellers who come back."

Another silence, longer. When Tally spoke again, her voice had gone quiet in a way I'd only heard twice before, both times over graves. "I'll wait at the gate. Forty paces. Like always."

Something in my chest twisted at that — *like always*, as if exile were a habit we'd always had — and I filed the feeling away to examine later, or never.

We passed through the pillars. The shimmer slid over my skin like water that had opinions about me, and for one vertiginous moment I felt *read* — turned over, weighed, catalogued — and then we were through, and the court received us.

* * *

Inside, the gilded dark opened into streets.

That's the only word for it. Streets, avenues, boulevards of black stone lit by drifting souls, lined with buildings that leaned together overhead like conspirators. And the inhabitants — I use the word loosely — came out to look at us.

They were beautiful, which was the first terrible thing. Not human-beautiful; beautiful the way a blade is beautiful, the way ice on a lake is beautiful. Tall figures in robes the color of deep water, faces smooth and ageless, moving with a grace that suggested they had never once stumbled, never hurried, never wanted anything badly enough to sweat for it. Courtiers. They watched us pass from balconies and doorways with expressions of mild, ancient interest, the way one watches weather.

A few of them were carrying lanterns on silver poles, the way footmen carry candles, and the souls inside leaned toward the warmth of the courtiers' robes like moths, or children.

"Keep walking," Bramble breathed. "Eyes forward. We're boring. Boring is safe."

We crossed a plaza where a fountain played — not water but light, liquid gold pouring upward from a basin and dissolving into motes that drifted up to join the lanterns overhead. Around the fountain sat figures playing a game with pieces I couldn't identify, and as we passed, one of them looked up.

Its face was a mirror. Not reflective — an actual mirror, flat and silver, showing me my own expression instead of any features of its own. It showed me looking afraid, and then it arranged my fear into something polite, and wore it.

I looked away so fast I nearly tripped.

"Master Quill," said a voice beside us, silken and amused, and I nearly jumped out of my boots. One of the tall courtiers had materialized at Bramble's elbow — I would swear there had been no one there a heartbeat before. "How unusual. A procurer of curiosities, come all this way, and bringing *guests*."

"Business associates," Bramble said smoothly. "Survey work. The upper realms are poorly documented."

"Mm." The courtier's smile was a crescent of white in its smooth dark face. Its eyes were the color of coins at the bottom of a well. "Documentation. How quaint. And where does your survey conclude, Master Quill?"

"At the Regent's threshold," Bramble said. "We've a petition."

The word *petition* moved through the nearby courtiers like a ripple through still water. Heads turned. Conversations paused mid-syllable. Somewhere above us, a lantern flared bright — a soul reacting, remembering, perhaps, what petitions meant here.

"A petition," the courtier repeated, tasting it. "How brave. Or how desperate. They so often wear the same coat." It gestured with one long hand toward the far end of the plaza, where a staircase broad as a highway climbed toward a structure that made my breath catch: a palace built of glass and gold wire, translucent as frost, and within it — at its heart — a light so vast and pale that I understood immediately it was not a lamp, not a fire, nothing so honest as either.

The Pale Regent. I hadn't been told, but I knew it the way you know a storm by the pressure in your ears.

"The Regent receives petitioners at the turn of the hour," the courtier said. "You have time to compose yourselves. I do recommend composing yourselves." The smile widened a fraction. "The last party who petitioned arrived weeping and were escorted out weeping, which everyone agreed showed admirable consistency."

It drifted away, and the crowd's attention withdrew with it like a tide.

"Bramble," Sefa whispered, "what happened to the last party?"

"No idea," Bramble said. "There's never been a last party. He makes them up. Keeps petitioners frightened." He wiped his palms on his coat, and I noticed his hands were shaking despite the performance. "It's working, isn't it."

"It's working," I admitted.

We climbed the great stair toward the palace of glass, and the lanterns drifted alongside us the whole way — dozens of them now, drawn to us, pressing close to the glass of their prisons. Watching. Some of them mouthed words I couldn't hear. One — a soul with the shape of a soldier, still wearing the ghost of armor — held up a hand in what might have been a salute, or a warning, or a plea.

At the top of the stair, the palace doors stood open onto a hall of impossible height, and there, enthroned in that vast pale light, sat a figure I could not look at directly — not because it blazed, but because my eyes kept sliding off it, the way they slide off the rearranging script, the way a map slides off a place that refuses to be mapped.

The Regent's voice reached us before we reached it, and it sounded almost kind.

"Petitioners," it said, "you are late, and you are few, and you are carrying grief like contraband. Come forward. Let us discuss what you believe you are owed."

*Believed* we were owed. I felt Sefa's beads click in her fist beside me, felt Bramble go rigid at my left, felt, forty paces and a gate away, Tally waiting in the dark like a debt of her own.

I stepped forward into the light, and every lantern in the hall turned to watch.
