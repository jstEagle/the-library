## 14. An Audience of Lanterns

There is a particular etiquette to being led to your own execution by things that used to be people, and I will spare you the manual, because I learned it in real time and would not wish the footnotes on anyone.

The ushers of the court beneath the mountain were called the Courteous, which should have been my first warning. They were tall in the way that architecture is tall — not bodies so much as load-bearing decisions — and they wore faces that had been worn by other faces before them, like coats passed down through a family with poor luck and excellent posture. Mine walked slightly ahead of me, its hand hovering near my elbow without touching, the way you'd guide someone through a garden you were proud of.

"Mind the third step," it said. Its voice was the sound a coin makes when it stops spinning.

I minded the third step. The first two had been marble; the third was something softer, and I did not ask what.

Behind me came Sefa and Bramble. Sefa walked like a woman returning to a house she'd been thrown out of — chin up, knuckles white on her prayer beads, daring the walls to comment. Bramble walked like a thief in a temple, which is to say he was trying very hard to look like a pilgrim and fooling no one, least of all the ceiling, which I am fairly sure was watching him.

The corridor opened, and there it was: the court beneath the mountain.

I have drawn maps of places that do not exist. I have charted smuggler roads that shift overnight, passes that appear only under certain moons, bridges that charge tolls in years instead of coin. I thought I understood that geography could lie. I did not understand that it could *grieve*.

The court was a cavern the size of a cathedral's ambition, and hanging from its vaulted dark were souls. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. They hung like lanterns — that is the only honest word for it — each one a small warm light in a glassy shell, swaying gently in a wind that came from nowhere, or from everywhere, or from the breathing of the thing we were walking into. Some burned gold. Some burned blue. One, off to the left, flickered in a pattern I recognized before I could stop myself from recognizing it: the rhythm of a child skipping rope. Two hops, a pause, two hops.

My stomach did something unprofessional.

"They're not suffering," Sefa murmured beside me. She said it like a prayer and looked like she didn't believe it. "They're kept. There's a difference."

"There is," I said. "One of them is smaller than the others."

She followed my eyes to the far end of the hall, where a single lantern hung apart from the rest, lower, closer to the floor, as if it had been set down within reach of small hands once and then lifted again out of caution. It burned a color I can only describe as *stubborn*. Wren's soul. Even down here, even sold and shelved, it refused to hang quietly.

Bramble made a sound low in his throat. "That's her?"

"That's her."

"She's — " He stopped. Started again. "She's bright."

"Yes," Sefa said softly. "They usually are. That's rather the point of what happens next."

* * *

The Pale Regent received us at the far end of the hall, upon a dais that was less a throne than an argument the room had agreed to lose. The Regent itself was difficult to look at directly, not because it was blinding but because it was *patient* — a figure of pale, unhurried geometry, robed in what appeared to be folded silence, its face a smooth oval with features suggested rather than committed to. Looking at it felt like reading a map with the names rubbed off. You knew what everything was. You couldn't say any of it aloud.

We stopped at the proper distance. The Courteous arranged themselves behind us with the soft finality of doors closing.

"Petitioners," the Regent said. Its voice was not loud. It arrived inside your head already seated, like a guest who'd let themselves in. "You stand in the Court of Kept Debts. Speak what you have come to speak, and be brief, for the lanterns are listening, and they are owed their quiet."

I had rehearsed this. On the under-road, through the market of small mercies, past the river that remembers — I had rehearsed it in every spare hour, polishing the words like a blade. Petition the throne. Name the debt. Demand the return.

What came out of my mouth was none of those things, because the hall was full of hanging children and my rehearsed speech died somewhere around my sternum.

"My name is Vesper Reyes," I said. "I'm a cartographer. I map roads that don't want to be mapped. And eleven days ago, in a village called Mirabel's Garden, a girl named Wren woke up without her soul because somebody sold it to you." My voice held. I was almost impressed with it. "I've come to buy her back. Or take her back. I'm flexible on method. I understand flexibility isn't popular here, but I thought I'd open strong."

Silence. The kind with weight to it. Somewhere above us, ten thousand lanterns swayed, and the light moved across the Regent's blank face like weather.

"You speak of purchase," the Regent said at last. "Do you bring payment?"

"I bring a counteroffer."

"The court does not haggle."

"Everyone haggles," I said. "Some just call it law."

Something shifted in the Regent's stillness. I could not have told you what. But the temperature of the room changed by a degree, and every usher behind us leaned in by exactly one inch, and I understood that I had either amused the thing or offended it, and that the difference might not matter.

"State your counteroffer," it said.

"Wren's soul was sold by a broker acting in bad faith. We have proof the sale was fraudulent — that the one who brokered it did so under false pretenses, trading a child's soul to protect her own interests. Your court prizes true debts. This one is rotten at the root. Void it. Return her. No payment required, because no true debt ever existed."

I heard myself finish and felt, briefly, magnificent. Then the Regent tilted its head — a slow, terrible degree of tilt, like a glacier considering a valley — and spoke.

"You are correct," it said.

I blinked. Beside me, Sefa's breath caught. Bramble whispered, "Is that good? Is that — "

"It changes nothing," the Regent continued, "but you are correct. The debt was fraudulently brokered. The contract is tainted. The court has known since the ink dried."

The floor did not open up beneath me, though I gave it a moment to consider it. "You've *known*?"

"The court knows everything written in it. Fraud is common. Souls are commonly sold by liars to pay the debts of worse liars. If we voided every tainted contract, the court would be empty by morning, and the lanterns would go out, and the dark below the world would have nothing to burn in it." The Regent's voice did not rise. It didn't need to. "You mistake what this place is, cartographer. It is not a marketplace. It is a *ledger*. And a ledger does not care whether its entries are just. It cares only whether they are *true*. The debt is written. Wren of Mirabel's Garden was sold, and the selling happened, and truth does not unwind itself because a stranger finds it ugly."

"So that's it?" My voice cracked on it, and I hated the crack, and I let it stand anyway because some things deserve cracks. "A child hangs in your rafters because paperwork is sacred?"

"Precisely," said the Regent, and if a voice made entirely of patience could be described as warm, this one managed it. "You begin to understand."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about despair: it is quiet. All my life I'd imagined the worst moments arriving with thunder — collapses, betrayals, the ground giving way. But standing in that gilded dark, watching a soul the size of a heartbeat sway in a wind that wasn't wind, the despair that settled over me was perfectly silent, like snow filling in a road I'd just finished mapping.

Sefa stepped forward. I hadn't seen her decide to. She simply moved, the way water moves downhill, and planted herself before the dais with her beads wrapped twice around her fist.

"Then hear a petition older than your ledger," she said. Her voice shook and did not stop. "I was cast out of my order for saying the gods still listened. I was wrong about nothing except how much it would cost to be right. So I am telling you now, in front of your lanterns and your ledger and whatever you keep underneath that face: the old gods are awake again. Ashen-Mouth walks. Prayers are being answered for the first time in three hundred years, and you sit here enforcing contracts while the sky remembers how to speak." She lifted her chin. "Your writ runs beneath the mountain, Regent. It does not run over it. And the ones above are waking up."

For the first time, the Regent paused.

It was a small pause. A hairline fracture in the patience. But I have spent my life reading terrain, and I know a fault line when I see one, and I saw it: the court was afraid of something. Not of us — we were four ragged mortals with bad boots and worse odds. Of *them*. Of the old gods, stirring in their shrines, answering prayers that had gone unanswered for centuries. The ledger beneath the mountain had been built in the long quiet, when no one above was listening. The quiet was ending.

And then the Regent did the most dangerous thing it could have done.

It smiled. Or produced the local equivalent — a thinning of the smooth face, a suggestion of teeth that may have been metaphorical. "You are more interesting than your paperwork," it said. "Very well, cartographer. The court offers a wager."

"We're not wagering," Bramble said immediately. "Ves. Ves, we're not — "

"Hear the terms," the Regent said, and the whole hall leaned in, lanterns and ushers and all, ten thousand small lights tilting toward us like a field of flowers turning toward a sun that had never loved them.

"Thirteen nights remain until the moon completes its thirteenth turn, when the ways between worlds close and your petition dies unwritten. In those thirteen nights, you shall answer thirteen riddles posed by the court. Answer all thirteen truly, and the debt of Wren shall be voided — not paid, *voided*, struck from the ledger as though it never was, and the child's soul returned whole. Fail a single riddle—" the smile deepened by a suggestion, "—and you forfeit a soul of equal weight to the one you seek. The court will select which of yours balances best."

The silence afterward had texture. I could feel Sefa's fear coming off her like heat off stone. I could feel Bramble's hand hovering near his knife, which is Bramble's answer to most theological questions.

"And if we refuse the wager?" I asked.

"Then you leave the way you came, and the ledger stands, and Wren hangs until the end of her usefulness. Souls are fuel here, cartographer. They burn a very long time." The Regent folded its hands — or the idea of hands. "But consider: you have already lost everything except hope. Hope is the only currency the court cannot mint. Spend it."

I looked back at the lanterns. At the little stubborn light burning low and apart, keeping its own time, two hops and a pause. Somewhere above us, a village was holding a child who breathed and blinked and laughed at nothing, hollow as a bell, and every day we spent traveling was a day the hollow grew calluses.

Hope is the only currency the court cannot mint. Fine. Then let's find out what it buys.

"We accept," I said.

Sefa closed her eyes. Bramble swore with real artistry, under his breath, in three languages. And the Regent rose from its argument of a throne, taller than the hall had seemed able to hold, and spread its arms, and every lantern in the cavern flared at once — ten thousand souls burning brighter for a single instant, like a city holding its breath.

"Then let the first riddle be spoken," said the Pale Regent, "and let the counting of your nights begin."

The dark opened its mouth. And the first question came down like a stone dropped into a well, falling and falling, and I understood — too late, always too late — that we had not been offered a chance.

We had been offered a countdown.
