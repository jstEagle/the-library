## 15. The Wager of Thirteen Riddles

The Pale Regent did not raise its voice. I suspect it had never needed to. When you are older than the mountain you live under, volume becomes a vulgar habit, like wearing shoes indoors.

"Debts are sacred," it said again, and Wren's soul-lantern swayed gently on its hook among the thousands of others, a small gold moth in a cathedral of moths. "The child was sold. The sale was witnessed. The coin was counted. I do not unmake what has been made."

"We didn't come all this way to hear about your accounting practices," Bramble said. He had been saying things like that since we entered the court, which is either courage or a death wish with better posture.

The Regent turned to him. It had no face, exactly — more of an agreement that a face had once been there and both parties had agreed to move on. "You would prefer I unmake *you* instead? It would balance nothing, but it would amuse me."

"Sefa," I said quietly, "a little help."

Sefa stepped forward. She had spent three days in the under-road rehearsing this moment, and I could see her discarding her prepared speech line by line as she looked up at the thing on the throne. What she said instead was simpler and, knowing Sefa, far more dangerous.

"You're right. Debts are sacred. So are wagers."

The Regent went very still. Stillness, in that hall, was not the absence of motion; it was the presence of attention. Every lantern flickered at once, ten thousand souls holding their breath because something old had begun to listen.

"The gods used to wager," the Regent said. "Before your kind learned to write contracts. You propose to bargain with me in the old style?"

"I propose," Sefa said, "that you give us a chance to win what we cannot buy."

I want it noted, for the record, that I did not plan any of this. My contribution to the negotiation was standing slightly behind Sefa and looking like a man who had already done the arithmetic and found it ugly. This is a legitimate diplomatic technique. Old Tally taught it to me. But that's another wound, and we're not there yet.

The Regent descended from the throne. I say *descended*, though its feet never touched the floor; it simply became lower, the way fog becomes lower. It stopped before Sefa and regarded her with whatever it used instead of eyes.

"A soul of equal weight," it said. "That is the price of failure. Answer my riddles across thirteen nights, one each night, or forfeit a soul of equal weight to the child's. Thirteen questions. Thirteen turnings of your moon above us. Fail, and I take from one of you what hangs beside her." A pause, which in Regent-time might have been a century. "Or I take from all of you, piecemeal, until the scales agree. I am flexible. It is one of my few virtues."

"And if we win?" I asked.

"If you win, the debt is voided. Not forgiven — voided. As though it had never been struck. The ledger forgets. The lantern comes down." Its head tilted, an angle that made my teeth ache. "Understand what you accept. No one has answered thirteen. Many have answered nine. One answered eleven, and I still think about him. You will find the experience educational in ways you do not request."

Bramble, beside me, whispered: "Ves. We have a soul between four of us and a moon that doesn't wait. Say yes anyway?"

"Say yes anyway," I said.

Sefa said, "We accept."

And the Pale Regent smiled. I use the word loosely. Something in the region of its face rearranged itself toward warmth the way a knife rearranges itself toward a throat.

"Then let the first night begin."

* * *

They gave us quarters. Of course they did. The court beneath the mountain is nothing if not hospitable; hospitality is how it fattens you for the ledger. Our rooms were carved into the living rock and furnished in a style I can only describe as *expensive regret* — velvet gone soft with age, mirrors that showed you a half-second behind yourself, a washbasin whose water tasted faintly of every promise you'd ever broken. Mine tasted mostly of ink and one specific apology I've never managed to say out loud. I drank it anyway. Thirst is thirst.

Bramble flopped onto the bed in his room — he'd claimed the largest one through a process of aggressive sitting — and declared the whole arrangement a trap.

"It's obviously a trap," I said, leaning in the doorway.

"Then why are we in it?"

"Because the alternative was leaving Wren hanging on a hook forever, and because Sefa asked, and because — I don't know, Bramble, why does anyone walk into a trap? Because the door out is on the far side."

He rolled onto his back and stared at the ceiling, where someone had painted constellations that matched no sky I've ever charted. "Thirteen nights. Thirteen riddles. And if we flub one, the Regent starts carving pieces off our souls until the weight balances." He said it lightly, the way he says everything, but his hand had drifted to his coat pocket, where he kept the small tin of memories he'd been spending in the Market of Small Mercies. He'd traded away his grandmother's kitchen already. I knew what was left in that tin, roughly. There wasn't enough tin in the world.

"One wrong answer," I said, "and we negotiate. Souls come in weights. Maybe ours weigh less than we think."

"That's the most depressing cartography you've ever done."

"I'm a professional."

He laughed, briefly, and then stopped laughing, and then said, quietly, without the performance: "Ves. If it comes down to it — take mine first. It's got holes in it already. Less to carry."

"Don't be stupid."

"I'm never stupid. I'm *efficient*. There's a difference and it's measured in tins."

I left him staring at the false stars. In the corridor, the lantern-light came through the high windows in long gold bars, and somewhere below us the court went on with its endless, patient business of weighing things.

* * *

Sefa was in the chapel.

There shouldn't have been a chapel. Nothing in the court's architecture suggested worship — worship requires the possibility that the god might not show up, and everything here was built on certainty. But there it was, a small alcove off the eastern gallery, with a stone altar worn smooth by hands that predated the mountain, and Sefa kneeling before it with her palms open the way she used to before they took her vestments and her name off the rolls.

I stood in the doorway like a coward and watched her pray.

She wasn't asking for anything. That was the strange part. Her lips moved, but there was no begging shape to it, no transaction. She was just talking, low and steady, the way you talk to someone you've missed for years and finally gotten alone in a room. After a while she noticed me — priests always notice you, it's a occupational hazard of being watched over — and beckoned without turning around.

"How long has it been?" she asked. "Since you prayed, I mean. Properly. Knees and everything."

"I don't kneel. Bad knees. Bad faith. Combination package."

"Mm." She rose, joints cracking, and faced me. In the lantern-light her exile-brand — the thin scar across her collarbone where they'd cut her ordination away — looked almost fresh. "The Regent gave us the terms. Thirteen riddles. Did you notice anything about them?"

"I noticed we're going to die of them."

"Anything else?"

"Sefa, I've been awake for thirty hours and I've negotiated with a faceless monarch. My noticing budget is spent."

She smiled, and it was the smile she wore when she was about to ruin someone's evening with theology. "The Regent said the riddles were old. Older than the court. It said it collected them 'from the mouths of the desperate.' Vesper — prayers. The riddles are *prayers*. The same ones the gods have started answering."

I felt it land in my chest like a dropped anchor. "Say that again slowly."

"The shrine that lit when Wren prayed. The ones after — you told me, on the road, that Ashen-Mouth said the gates were open, that the old gods were answering again after centuries of silence. Why now? Why *these* prayers? Because someone is collecting them. Someone is taking the prayers of the desperate and setting them as locks." She tapped the altar. "The Regent isn't testing our cleverness. It's testing whether we can answer the prayers ourselves. Whether mortals can do what the gods have begun doing."

"That's insane."

"It's theology. Same thing, better posture."

"But — why? Why would it build its wager out of the exact prayers the gods are answering?"

"Because the Regent is afraid." She said it simply, without triumph, and somehow that was worse. "Think about it. For centuries the court bought souls through brokers like Tally, and no god ever intervened, because the gods weren't listening. Now the gates are open. Now prayers get answered. Now a company of four nobodies walks into the court demanding a soul back, and the Regent — instead of simply refusing, which it *can* do, it refused us already — offers a wager. A game. Thirteen chances." She met my eyes. "It wants us to lose slowly. Or it wants to learn something. Either way, Vesper — the riddles aren't just a test. They're a map. Every prayer the gods have been answering is a door the Regent is trying to understand. And we're going to be handed them one per night, with time to study."

I sat down on the altar step, because my legs had opinions. "You're saying the wager is rigged."

"I'm saying it's rigged in a direction nobody's checked yet." She knelt again, suddenly tired, suddenly just an old woman with a scar and a stubborn streak a mile wide. "When the first riddle comes, don't just listen to the words. Listen for who's praying underneath them. That's where the answer lives."

* * *

The first night came the way tides come — not announced, simply arrived.

We were brought to a smaller hall, round, with a single lantern hanging at its center: Wren's. Below it stood a table of black glass, and on the table lay a card of pale vellum that hadn't existed five minutes earlier. The Regent did not appear in person. It didn't need to. Its voice came from everywhere the light touched, which is to say everywhere.

"*Night the first.*"

Bramble picked up the card. Read it. Went pale in a way I'd only seen him go pale once before, in the market, when he understood what the memory-broker actually wanted from him. He passed it to Sefa. She read it. Her breath caught. She passed it to me, and I read:

*I asked the dark to keep what I could not carry, and it was kind, and now I cannot remember what I lost. What did I trade, and to whom, and was the kindness real?*

Signed, at the bottom, in a spidery hand: *a prayer, offered by a thief, in the year the roads closed.*

Bramble made a sound like a man swallowing glass. "That's mine," he whispered. "That's — Ves, that's *my prayer*. I said that. Years ago. On the under-road, the night before the job that got everyone caught. I prayed to anything listening, and I asked the dark to hold what I couldn't carry, and I never—" His voice cracked clean through. "I never remembered making the trade. Until the market. Until I saw the tin."

The dark had been kind. The dark had taken the memory of the taking. And the prayer had lain in some ledger for years, waiting, until a Regent with too much time and too much fear set it as the first lock on a door.

"Answer it," the light said, patiently, terribly. "You have until the moon completes its turn. Speak truly, or forfeit."

Sefa put her hand on Bramble's shoulder. "Who did you pray to?" she asked him, gentle as a bandage. "Not the words. The *direction*. Where were you facing?"

Bramble stared at the card. At the lantern. At his own empty hands. And slowly, horribly, wonderfully, understanding began to dawn across his face — the same understanding Sefa had reached in the chapel, arriving now in the place it hurt most.

"I was facing the shrine," he said. "The little one. Under the thorn tree. The one everybody said was dead."

"Then that's your answer," Sefa said. "Not *what* you traded. *To whom.* The rest follows."

The lantern overhead flickered — once, twice — and I swear on every map I've ever drawn that the light leaned toward him. Listening. The way the gods had begun to listen. The way they'd listened to Wren, and to me, and to every desperate soul whose prayers had lately started getting answers.

The Pale Regent's voice filled the hall, and for the first time since we'd arrived beneath the mountain, I heard something in it I hadn't expected.

Haste.

"Speak your answer, thief. The moon is already turning."

And in the gold light of a stolen child's soul, Bramble opened his mouth — and I realized, with a cartographer's cold clarity, that we hadn't walked into a trap at all.

We'd walked into a race.
