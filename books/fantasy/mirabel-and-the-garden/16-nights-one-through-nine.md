## 16. Nights One Through Nine

**NIGHT ONE — VESPER**

The Pale Regent's herald delivered the first riddle the way a butcher delivers meat: wrapped in cloth, cold to the touch, and with an air of *you'll be wanting this soon*.

We'd been given a cell in the guest wing of the court beneath the mountain — gilded dark, as I've said, all filigree and shadow, where the walls breathed faintly and the floor was warm as a sleeping animal. Our quarters were generous by any surface standard. There was even a window, though it looked out on nothing but the slow drift of soul-lanterns far below, like fireflies that had once been people.

I unrolled the cloth on the table. Inside lay a strip of pale bark, and on it, in letters that moved when you weren't looking:

*What is carried empty, filled without hands, and spent the moment it is kept?*

"A riddle," Sefa said flatly.

"Thank you," I said. "I'd have mistaken it for a recipe."

Bramble leaned over my shoulder, chewing something he'd found somewhere, which was how Bramble acquired most things. "Carried empty. Filled without hands." He squinted. "A grave?"

"That's cheerful."

"You wanted optimism from a thief?"

Sefa took the bark from me and held it up to the lantern-light. Her face did something complicated. "It's not a riddle," she said quietly. "It's a prayer. A harvest prayer — old form, older than the chapel rites. *Fill us without our asking; we spend what we are given.*" She set it down like it might bite. "The answer is a life. Or a day. Depends how pious you're feeling."

The Regent's wager had seemed abstract in the throne room: thirteen riddles across thirteen nights, or forfeit a soul of equal weight to Wren's. Abstractions have a way of developing teeth. This one already had.

"We answer tonight?" Bramble asked.

"We answer at moonrise," Sefa said. "That's when prayers go up. That's when they'll be listening for ours to come down."

So at moonrise — or whatever passed for it down there, a dimming of the lantern-glow that felt rehearsed — I stood before the little alcove the herald had indicated (a niche in the wall, shaped suspiciously like every wayside shrine I'd ever mapped) and spoke the answer aloud.

"A day," I said. "The answer is a day. Carried empty until it fills itself, spent the moment you try to keep it."

The silence stretched. Then the bark in my hand warmed, and the letters rearranged themselves into a single word: *TRUE*.

Bramble whooped. Sefa pressed her hand to her mouth. And I stood there in the gilded dark feeling something I hadn't felt since I was a child kneeling at my mother's knee: the specific vertigo of having been heard by something vast.

One down. Twelve to go.

* * *

**NIGHT TWO — BRAMBLE**

Right, so. Second riddle. Let me tell you about the second riddle, because the second riddle cost me something, and I want it on record that I paid voluntarily, mostly.

*What do the dead own that the living borrow and never return?*

Easy, you'd think. Names, maybe. Breath. Vesper thought breath. Sefa thought names. Both wrong — the bark went cold as river stone and stayed cold, and the herald's voice came through the wall like water through a crack: *"You have until moonset."*

Moonset gave us maybe four hours. Four hours to stand around a table in a breathing room while a child's soul hung in a lantern-court downstairs, weighing our guesses against her neck, so to speak.

It was Sefa who figured out we were reading it wrong. "It's not asking what the dead possess," she said. "It's asking what they *own*. Ownership's different. Ownership means title. Deed. Something with your name on it that others use."

"Borrowed and never returned," Vesper murmured. "Not stolen. Borrowed. Which means it was given."

And there it was. I knew the answer before anyone said it, because I'd given mine away twice already — once in the Market of Small Mercies, and once before that, back when I ran messages for a man whose deliveries I should have opened. The dead own their forgiveness. The living borrow it every time we say *they'd have wanted us to move on*, every time we light a candle and feel better about ourselves, and we never give it back. We just keep spending it.

"I know this one," I said, and my voice came out strange. "But I need to pay for it first."

They looked at me. Vesper has this look, cartographer's eyes, always measuring distances — and I could see them measuring the distance between what I was about to say and what it would cost.

"The court doesn't take answers free," I explained. "You noticed that, yeah? Every true thing down here has a price tag. So." I closed my eyes and went digging in the only vault I had left.

I gave them my mother's laugh.

Not the memory of it — I'd sold memories before, whole scenes, whole afternoons. This was different. This was the *sound*, the thing underneath the memory, the way her laugh started in her chest like a wagon starting downhill. I don't know how you sell a sound. But the court knew how, and the moment I offered it, the room accepted, and now when I try to remember her laughing I get the shape of it without the music. Like a letter describing a song.

Then I said the answer out loud — "Their forgiveness" — and the bark burned *TRUE*, and Sefa put her hand on my shoulder, and Vesper said, quietly, "Bramble—"

"Don't," I said. "Don't make it heavy. Heavy things are harder to carry, and I'm carrying enough."

Here's the thing nobody tells you about solidarity: it isn't standing shoulder to shoulder singing songs. It's watching someone pay a price you can't pay for them, and staying in the room anyway. They stayed in the room. That counted. Down here, I suspect it counts double.

* * *

**NIGHT THREE — SEFA**

They let me write this account later, so I will set down Night Three honestly, though honesty has never been my strongest devotion.

The third prayer-riddle was this: *What grows heavier the more you share it, until sharing is the only way to hold it?*

Grief. Obviously grief. Any priestess worth her salt knows that one — it's in the funeral liturgies, dressed up in prettier language. I answered it inside a minute and then sat with the terrible knowledge that I had answered easily, because grief was the one subject on which I had a doctorate.

Let me explain what exile does to a person. You spend years saying the gods are silent. You build a life on that silence the way you build a house on rock. And then you find yourself in hell — actual hell, gilded and polite — discovering that the gods were never silent at all. They were listening. They have *always* been listening. And worse: they answer.

On Night Three, for the first time since they cast me out of the chapel at Thornhollow, I prayed. Not the answer — the answer I gave the bark, plain and dry. I mean I prayed. Kneeling in that alcove built for older purposes, hands open the way Mother Cassia taught me before she handed me to the mob, I prayed.

*I don't ask you to be kind,* I said. *I asked that once and look where it got everyone. I only ask you to be true. If you're cruel, be cruel where I can see it. I can serve a cruel god. I cannot serve a lie.*

Nothing answered. Of course nothing answered. That's the arrangement with the divine: you speak into the well and the well keeps its own counsel, and faith is the decision to keep talking anyway.

But here is what I will swear to, because I felt it through my palms on the warm stone: the court heard me. The whole mountain shifted its weight, the way a large animal does when it notices something small. And the bark turned *TRUE* faster than it had for either of the others, eager, almost — as if my answer had pleased something.

Vesper found me still kneeling afterward. They didn't say anything clever, which I appreciated. They just sat down on the cold floor beside me, in the dust, in the gilded dark, and stayed until my knees complained louder than my heart.

That's the company I keep now. An exiled priestess, a thief missing his mother's laugh, a smuggler-cartographer half in love with a debt, and an old woman we'd left behind on the road with a marked map and her crimes. None of us qualified for this. All of us showed up anyway.

Solidarity, I have decided, is just love with its sleeves rolled up.

* * *

**NIGHT FOUR — VESPER**

The fourth riddle nearly killed us, and not in the dramatic way. In the tedious way, which is how most things nearly kill you.

*What road exists only where two walkers agree upon it?*

I should have had that instantly. Roads are my trade, my scripture, my entire self. But I kept answering *a border* and *a smuggler's path* and *the under-road*, and the bark stayed cold, and Bramble started pacing, and Sefa started praying again, which by Night Four had become less a spiritual discipline than a nervous habit, like nail-biting with extra steps.

The problem, I finally understood, was that I was thinking like a mapmaker. Maps show roads as things — lines, features, facts. But every smuggler knows better. A route only exists while two people trust each other enough to walk it together. Burn that trust and the path doesn't vanish from a chart; it vanishes from the world. Tally taught me that when I was nine. *A road is a promise wearing boots,* she said.

*A promise,* I told the bark. "A road is a promise two walkers make together, and it exists exactly as long as they both keep it."

*TRUE.*

I sat down hard after that one. Not from relief. From recognition. Because the word *promise* had come out of my mouth sounding like Tally's voice, and I wasn't ready for how much I missed her, betrayal notwithstanding. Exile on the under-road, alone with a marked map I'd drawn her — mercy and justice tangled up so tight I couldn't tell which thread was which.

"She'd have known that answer straight off," Bramble said.

"Yeah," I said. "She would."

Nobody said *maybe we shouldn't have sent her away.* Nobody said the opposite either. Some silences are load-bearing. We left that one standing and went to bed.

* * *

**NIGHT FIVE — BRAMBLE**

Fifth riddle, and I'll be brief, because the fifth one I'm not proud of.

*What can you give away forever and still possess?*

Your word. Your name. Your past — no, wait, you lose your past when you give it. Love, maybe. The bark liked *your word*: give your word away and you still have it, spoken and kept. *TRUE* on the first guess, fastest yet.

Fastest yet, and it made me sick, because I realized the court was warming to us. The Regent's game wasn't purely adversarial. Something down here *wanted* us to win — or wanted something else entirely, and winning was the shape it took. Sefa muttered that night about prayers being answered from unexpected directions. She was right. More on that later, because later is when it got strange.

Anyway. Fifth night, easy answer, and I celebrated by eating something I found. Consistency is a virtue.

* * *

**NIGHT SIX — SEFA**

The sixth prayer arrived wet.

That's the only way to describe it. The bark came damp, and the letters bled slightly, and the question read: *What waits at the bottom of every well, and is it kind?*

"It's a trick," Vesper said. "Riddles don't usually ask two questions."

"This one's not asking," I said. My mouth had gone dry. "It's offering. Wells are how you reach the dead, in the oldest rites. It's asking whether we believe what's down there is kind."

"And what do we believe?" Bramble asked.

What did we believe. What a question to hand three people standing in hell. I thought of the shrine-light over Thornfell pass, the first answered prayer in centuries, and how it had lit up the sky like a wound healing. I thought of Wren's empty eyes. Kindness and cruelty, arriving in the same post.

"We believe," I said carefully, "that what waits at the bottom of every well is *listening* — and that listening is the beginning of kindness, even when what comes back hurts."

The bark considered. Genuinely considered; I watched the letters argue with themselves. Then: *TRUE*, but grudgingly, the way a judge says *not guilty* to a defendant he dislikes.

Afterward Vesper looked at me and said, "You realize these aren't tests. They're confessions. The court's making us describe ourselves."

"Yes," I said. "That's what prayer is."

* * *

**NIGHT SEVEN — VESPER**

On the seventh night, the answer didn't come from us.

The seventh riddle was: *Who lights the lamp that finds the lost, if the lost refuse to be found?*

We fought it for hours. Bramble proposed searchers, shepherds, mothers. Sefa proposed God, in several grammatical configurations. I proposed lighthouse-keepers, because I am sometimes an idiot with a professional bias. Cold bark, all of it. Moonrise came and went. Moonset loomed. And Wren's soul hung downstairs in its lantern, and I could feel the hours peeling off it like paint.

Then, near midnight, the alcove lit up.

Not our alcove — *all* of them. Every niche in the guest wing, every shrine-shaped hollow in the breathing walls, flared gold at once, and through the stone came a sound like a bell being struck underwater, and the bark in my hands burned *TRUE* before anyone had spoken a word.

Because someone had answered. Somewhere above us — on the surface, in the world of mud and weather — someone had knelt at some roadside shrine and prayed the answer to the seventh riddle without knowing they were doing it. Sefa had been saying it for days: the riddles were prayers, the same ones the gods had begun answering when the old covenant woke. The court wasn't testing us against its own wisdom. It was testing whether the surface and the deep still spoke the same language.

And they did. Someone up there had said, essentially, *you light it yourself, or you light it for each other* — the lamp that finds the lost is lit by whoever loved them first — and the mountain had accepted it on our behalf.

Bramble stared at the glowing niches. "Did we just win that round by proxy?"

"Somebody's grandmother just won it for us," I said.

Sefa was crying. Quietly, efficiently, the way she did everything devotional. "They're answering," she said. "All of them. The shrines are awake. Do you understand what that means? It means the silence was never the gods'. It was ours."

I understood something else, too, something colder. If the riddles drew on the prayers of the living, then the thirteenth riddle — the last one, the one due on the night the moon completed its turn — would draw on the deepest prayer in the chain. And I had a bad, cartographer's certainty about chains: you follow them long enough, they lead home.

* * *

**NIGHT EIGHT — BRAMBLE**

Eighth riddle. This one cost me again, and I'm keeping the receipt.

*What remains of a man when everything he remembers is taken?*

Charming. Very charming. Whoever writes the Regent's material should be pushed down a stairs.

I want to state clearly that I volunteered. Nobody made me. But I'd sold my mother's laugh on Night Two, and half my childhood in the Market of Small Mercies, and the eighth riddle looked at me across the table like it knew precisely which drawer I kept my spare keys in.

The answer, once Sefa unpicked it, was *his debts* — what remains of a man when memory goes is what he owes, the shape other people remember for him. But knowing the answer wasn't enough. The bark wanted payment, and the payment it named — I felt it name me, the way a teacher's finger finds you in a crowd — was another memory.

I gave it the rain on the under-road. The first job I ever ran solo, seventeen years old, sleet coming sideways, and the pure stupid joy of being trusted with something. Gone now. I know I had it the way you know you had a tooth.

Vesper tried to stop me. Grabbed my wrist. "There has to be another way."

"There isn't," I said, "and even if there were — Wren gets her soul back or I stop being the kind of man who helps. Those are the options. I know which one I pick."

*TRUE*, said the bark. And Sefa wrote my name in her little book of intentions, which is a priestess thing, and Vesper poured me a drink from the flask they pretended they didn't have, and we sat in the gilded dark, three people pretending a fourth was still with us, holding the rope for each other.

Eight nights. Eight truths. Five to go.

* * *

**NIGHT NINE — VESPER**

The ninth riddle came at dawn instead of dusk, which broke every pattern, and patterns breaking is how mountains warn you.

*What did you bring with you that you did not pack?*

Personal, suddenly. The earlier riddles had been universal — grief, roads, wells. This one looked at *me*. At us. And the bark waited, patient as debt.

I inventoried. Guilt — packed that myself, years ago. Hope — Sefa's contribution, smuggled in against regulations. Tally's absence — brought that, definitely, though I'd have sworn I left it on the under-road with her. And underneath all of it, something older, something I'd been hauling since before I could name it, the thing my mother's bargain and Ashen-Mouth's debt and Tally's confession had all been stitched onto—

"Oh," I said.

"What?" Bramble asked.

"The answer's *belonging*," I said slowly, working it out as I spoke. "None of us packed it. We came here as exiles and debtors and thieves. But somewhere between the gate and the ninth night we started belonging to each other, and none of us put it in a bag. It just — accumulated. Like mileage."

*TRUE.*

The bark cooled. The alcoves dimmed. And in the quiet after, Sefa said what all of us were thinking, in the gentle voice she saved for bad news:

"Four nights left, Vesper. And the riddles are getting personal. The twelfth and thirteenth won't be about grief or roads or wells. They'll be about *you*. About why you came. About what you asked for, once, before you could afford the answer."

Outside our breathing walls, far below, the soul-lanterns drifted. Somewhere among them, small and steady, hung the light that was Wren — and beside it, I now noticed for the first time, a second lantern had appeared, dimmer, flickering, as if it were still traveling.

As if someone were still walking toward us through the dark.

I knew the stride before I knew the silhouette. Broad, deliberate, favoring the left knee.

"Tally," I said.

The lantern stopped beneath our window and lifted, like a face looking up.
