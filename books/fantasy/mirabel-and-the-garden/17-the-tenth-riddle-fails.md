## 17. The Tenth Riddle Fails

The court beneath the mountain kept time the way a miser keeps coins: counted twice, spent never. We had been in the gilded dark for nine nights when the tenth riddle came to find me.

It didn't arrive on parchment. Nothing in that place arrived on parchment — paper was too honest a material for the Pale Regent's tastes. Instead, at the hour the lantern-souls dimmed to embers, a servant of older-than-king vintage glided into our cell with the riddle folded into its posture, written in the way it held its too-many hands, and I understood it the way you understand a headache: directly, and against your will.

*What is the road that widens as it is walked, narrows as it is forgotten, and ends where the walker began?*

I knew the answer before the last clause finished settling into my skull. That should have been my first warning. In nine nights of riddles, none of them had come easy. The fourth had taken Bramble six hours and two sacrificed memories to even *hear* properly. The seventh had made Sefa weep into her sleeves until she realized the answer was her own name spoken backward in a dead liturgy. Easy was not what this place did.

"It's a life," I said aloud, because the court demanded answers be spoken, witnessed, weighed. "A life is the road that widens as it's walked—"

Sefa's head came up sharply from her prayers. Bramble stopped mid-fidget, a stolen candle-end halfway to his pocket.

"—and narrows as it's forgotten," I continued, "and ends where the walker began."

The silence that followed had texture. It had *weight*. It pressed down on us like snowfall on a roof that was never built for snow.

"Vesper," Sefa said carefully, in the voice she used for the dying and the stubborn, which in my experience overlapped considerably, "the riddle asked what the road *is*. Not what walks it."

I opened my mouth to argue and found, to my considerable professional embarrassment, that she was right. I could feel it the way I felt errors in a map — that faint wrongness in the contour lines, the river flowing uphill. A life wasn't the road. A life was the walker. The road itself was something else. Something that widened with every step taken upon it, narrowed when people stopped remembering it, and ended —

"Memory," Bramble whispered. "It's memory. Or — no. A *story*. A story about someone. It widens every time it's told, thins when folks forget it, and it ends where it started, back in the mouth of whoever—" He looked up at me with an expression I'd learned to dread over nine nights: the expression of a man watching someone else step onto thin ice. "Vesper. You already answered."

"I know I answered."

"You answered *wrong*."

"I'm aware of the situation, Bramble."

The servant unfolded slightly, like a flower deciding whether to bloom or bite, and extended one hand. In its palm lay a small ledger bound in something I chose not to identify. The pages were blank. The pages were always blank until they weren't.

"The debt is noted," it said, in a voice like dust settling in an empty house. "A soul of equal weight shall be drawn against the account of Vesper Reyes. The court thanks you for your custom."

"I'd like to speak to a supervisor," I said, because wryness is the last muscle to atrophy and I intended to flex it to the end.

The servant regarded me with the patience of a geological era. "The Regent does not hear appeals on riddle-nights. The Regent finds appeals *flavorful*, and flavors are for feasts." It paused. "You have until the ledger opens."

And then, because the court beneath the mountain believed in ceremony the way drowning men believe in air, the walls of our cell began to open.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about losing ten years of your own memories: it doesn't hurt. That's the obscenity of it. The court's ledger worked like a cartographer's eraser — clean, precise, leaving no smudge where the line used to be. One moment I had a decade. The next moment I had a decade-shaped absence, smooth as fresh vellum, and everyone around me insisting there had ever been anything drawn there at all.

They let me choose the decade. That was the mercy, if you could call it that, and the Regent absolutely could and did, loudly, in the formal proclamation. *Choose freely,* the summons said. *The court honors the sovereignty of the debtor.*

So I sat in the weighing chamber — a room like the inside of a bell, all curved silver surfaces and a floor that hummed when you lied on it — and I considered my life the way I'd once considered the Thornfell passes: looking for the stretch I could afford to lose.

There were years I couldn't touch. The years Tally raised me, after my mother went into the mountains and didn't come back — those were load-bearing. Remove them and the whole structure of who I was would come down. The years learning the craft, apprenticed to a half-blind surveyor named Odile who taught me that a map is a promise you make to strangers: load-bearing. The last three years, mapping the smuggler roads, finding Wren's village, answering Ashen-Mouth's call: load-bearing, obviously, though a treacherous part of me noted that the decade I was about to lose was probably going to include tonight, which meant I might lose the memory of losing it, which was either a comfort or a horror depending on how much sleep I'd gotten.

That left the middle. My twenties, roughly. And here was the humiliating truth I discovered sitting on the humming floor of the bell-chamber, with Sefa's hand on my shoulder and Bramble pretending very hard to study the ceiling:

My twenties were mostly road.

Not bad road. Good road, some of it. The year I crossed the Saltmere alone and learned the stars well enough to navigate by their *absence*. The winter in Kettlebridge when I'd drawn maps for bread and gotten fat on both. A woman in a river town whose name I suddenly clutched at like a man grabbing a railing — no. Not her. I wouldn't give them her. There were things in that decade that were mine, that had made me, that no court of gilded dark had any right to—

"Vesper." Sefa's voice, low. "Whatever you keep, keep it whole. They'll take the seams if you leave seams."

Of course she was right. She was always right; it was her most irritating quality after the praying. A decade cut cleanly was a decade lost. A decade cut raggedly was a decade *wounded*, and wounds fester in ways absences don't.

So I chose whole years. Three of them, the emptiest ones, the ones where I'd been running — running from Tally's expectations, from my mother's ghost, from the question of why a person who hated staying put kept drawing maps of places she'd never return to. Three years of footprints and nothing planted in them. I told myself they were the lightest weight. I told myself the ledger would barely notice.

The ledger noticed everything. That was rather its entire nature.

"Confirmed," said the servant, and opened the book.

I want to describe what it took, and I want you to understand that I am describing it from the outside, the way you'd describe a scar on someone else's arm. There was a sensation like a page turning behind my eyes. There was a moment — I will swear this to my grave, whichever grave that turns out to be — a moment where I saw all three years at once, spread out like a chart, every road and river town and starless night, and I thought, with the clarity of the doomed: *this is the finest work I've ever done.*

Then the ink lifted off the page.

* * *

Afterward, we sat in our cell, and I tried to take inventory of myself the way I'd inventory a satchel after a rough crossing.

"Tell me what's missing," Sefa said. She'd appointed herself my cartographer now, which was either kind or cruel, and I suspected with Sefa it was both, professionally.

"You tell me," I said. "You're the one who'll notice the gaps."

She frowned. "Your left hand. You keep looking at it."

I looked at my left hand. There was a scar across the palm, old and white, a rope-burn by the look of it. I had no memory of earning it. I turned the hand over twice, feeling the way you feel when your own house has been rearranged by someone else — everything functional, nothing familiar.

"Kettlebridge?" Bramble offered helpfully. "You said — before, you said there was a rope bridge over the gorge there, and you nearly—"

"I don't remember saying that."

He shut up. Bless him, he actually shut up, which for Bramble was an act of heroism worthy of its own ballad.

The gaps were strange in their shape. I still knew *that* I had crossed the Saltmere — the fact remained, filed under accomplishments — but the crossing itself was gone, the cold and the terror and the dawn I'd supposedly wept at. I knew I'd been lonely in my twenties. I no longer knew *how* lonely, or why, or what it had cost me, or what it had taught me. It was like being handed a summary of a book I'd lived through and discovering the summary was accurate and useless in equal measure.

"They took the walking years," I said finally. "The ones where I wasn't going anywhere."

"That's not how it feels, though," Sefa said quietly. "Does it."

"No," I admitted. "It feels like they took the years I became someone worth following on a road."

Bramble, to his credit, tried humor, which in the court beneath the mountain was an act of defiance comparable to arson. "Look at it this way. You've got three blank years now. Fresh vellum. You could draw anything in there. Dragon attack. Romance. A brief but passionate career in interpretive dance."

"Bramble."

"A *very* brief career."

"Bramble, I swear by every god still answering—"

But I couldn't finish the threat, because a thought had just walked into the middle of it and refused to leave, and the thought was this: the riddles were prayers. Sefa had figured it out around the sixth night — that each riddle the Regent posed was a prayer some god had once answered, dressed in courtly clothes. Which meant the tenth riddle, the one I'd flubbed, the one about the road that widens as it's walked and ends where the walker began—

Someone had prayed that. Someone had stood at a shrine and asked the old gods a question about stories, or roads, or memory, and the gods had *answered*, and the court had taken the answer and turned it into a trap for me specifically.

Which raised a question I didn't like at all: had the riddles been designed to be answerable, and I'd simply failed? Or had the tenth one been designed — by the Regent, by Ashen-Mouth, by whatever games gods play with their debts — to fail *me*?

"Get some sleep," Sefa said. "Two nights left. We need you sharp."

"We need me *anything*," I said. "Sharp would be showing off."

* * *

Sleep didn't come, so I did what I always do when sleep won't come: I drew.

They'd let me keep my kit — the court found it amusing, I think, the little human with her little instruments, mapping a realm that rearranged itself out of spite. I sat cross-legged on the cell floor by lantern-light and drew the only thing I had left to draw: myself. A self-portrait in map form. Rivers for veins, ridgelines for old scars, settlements for the people I loved, blank spaces where the ledger had been.

There were more blank spaces than three years' worth. I kept finding them. Small ones. The name of Odile's second apprentice — gone, though I remembered the woman's face perfectly. The taste of a particular stew in a particular town — gone, though I remembered loving it. Memory, it turns out, isn't stored in neat decades like grain in a granary. It's stored the way water is stored underground, in connected pockets, and when the court drained three chambers it lowered the water table of my whole interior country.

I was halfway through labeling the blank space where my mother's face should have been — it was *gone*, do you understand, not faded, *gone*, and I hadn't given them that year, I hadn't *chosen* it, unless choosing three years near it had pulled it down like a landslide pulls down the slope below — when the door of the cell opened without a sound.

I assumed it was the servant. I assumed riddle eleven had come early, or a summons, or another elegant cruelty.

It was Old Tally.

She stood in the doorway wrapped in her traveling cloak, gray-haired and iron-spined and impossibly, impossibly real, and behind her the corridor glowed faintly with the light of a marked map — *my* marked map, the one I'd left for her on the under-road, the one I'd told myself was mercy and knew was cowardice and had left anyway.

"Tally," I said, and my voice cracked on it, and I found I didn't have three blank years' worth of pride left to care. "How in the names of every god listening — how are you *here*?"

The old woman looked at me for a long moment, the way she used to look at ledgers that didn't balance, and whatever she saw in my face made her jaw tighten.

"Vesper," she said slowly. "Who's Vesper Reyes?"

And I understood, with the particular horror available only to cartographers, that the map I'd drawn of myself had a flaw I hadn't charted: somewhere between the cell door and the weighing chamber, between the question and the answer, the ledger had taken more than I'd agreed to sell.

Tally stepped inside, closed the door, and said the words that turned my blood to ice-water:

"Because the girl I raised — the one who left me a map with a heart drawn on it — she's standing in that corridor right now, talking to a priestess and a thief. And she says she's come to pay a debt." Tally's eyes, sharp as a surveyor's needle, fixed on me. "So either she's lying, or you are. And Vesper — *whoever you are* — you've got until the eleventh riddle to tell me which."
