## 18. Tally Returns

I have walked every smuggler road in the Thornfells twice over and half of them a third time for spite, but I never once thought I'd die on one. Smugglers don't die on roads. We die in beds we've paid for honestly with money we haven't, or we don't die at all, we just get slower and meaner until the mountain itself gets embarrassed and takes us.

So it was a strange thing, following my own girl's map toward the place where souls go to be kept like winter apples.

Vesper had left it tucked under the flat stone by the shrine at Hollowmere — the marked map, the one I wasn't supposed to find, or was supposed to find, depending on which version of Vesper you believe, and I've raised that child through four versions and liked none of them better than the others. The map was drawn in their careful hand, every line precise as a confession: *the under-road begins where the river forgets its name.* And beneath it, small enough that I almost missed it, a note:

*If you're reading this, I'm sorry. If you're reading this anyway, follow the red marks. Don't trust anything that offers to carry you.*

Well. That's Vesper all over. Apologizing and giving orders in the same breath.

I sat on that cold stone a long while, longer than an old woman should sit anywhere, because sitting too long is how you decide you're done. The village of Hollowmere slept around me, smoke rising from chimneys straight as plumb lines, honest people dreaming honest dreams. Somewhere south of here, in Mirabel's Garden, a child named Wren was walking around with her eyes like unlit windows, and I'm the reason.

Fifty years of running cargo and I never once got caught. Turns out the thing that finally catches you isn't the law. It's a little girl's empty face.

* * *

The under-road is not a road. That's the first lie of it. It's more like a wound the mountain never bothered to close, and walking it is like walking through the memory of a tunnel — the walls are there when you need them to be and absent when you'd rather they weren't. My lantern burned steady, which frightened me more than flickering would have. Down there, nothing behaves the way it should, and behaving perfectly is its own kind of wrong.

The red marks led true, though. Vesper's marks always do. I taught them cartography myself — taught them that a map is a promise you make to strangers, that if your line lies, someone dies in a snowdrift believing you. They took the lesson harder than I meant them to. Most of what I taught that child, they took harder than I meant.

On the third day under, I came to the river.

I knew what it did. Vesper had told me before they banished me from the company — told me in front of Sefa and Bramble, told me with a voice like a door closing, that the river shows drinkers their truest memory, and that mine had been standing beside their mother at a bargain. As if I needed a river to tell me that. I've carried that memory fifty years the way you carry a stone in your boot: painfully, constantly, and without ever once taking the shoe off, because taking it off would mean admitting it's there.

But the river was between me and the court, and I am too old to go around things.

I knelt at the bank. The water was black and patient, the way debt is patient.

"Go on," I said to it. "You can't show me anything worse than I show myself every morning."

I drank.

And there she was — Mara Reyes, Vesper's mother, twenty-six years old and already dying of the sickness that would take her two winters later, standing in my kitchen with her hands flat on my table like a woman bracing against weather. And there I was, younger, still handsome in the way that hard women are handsome, counting out coin that wasn't mine to count.

"You're sure," Mara said, in the memory, in her voice, in the voice I have heard every night since. "Tally. You're *sure*."

"The routes are dying," I heard myself answer. "The passes are closing. The family starves in five years, ten if we're lucky. The court pays for souls, Mara. Clean payment. No blood on it."

"It's a child's soul."

"It's a *promise*," I said, and God forgive me, I believed it. "They only keep what's given freely. She'll walk, she'll breathe, she'll grow. She won't miss what she doesn't know she had."

Mara looked at me across my own table, and this is the part the river showed me truest, the part I'd spent fifty years blurring: she didn't argue. She was too sick and too scared and too tired, and her daughter was three years old and needed feeding, and the court's broker had made the offer sound like mercy. So she signed. She pressed her thumb in wax, and she wept, and she said, "Then it's on both of us, Tally. Whatever comes of it. Both of us."

And I said — and here I lifted the water from the river and let it run out of my cupped hands, because I could not bear to hear it again — I said, "Nothing will come of it. Nobody will ever know."

Somebody knew. Somebody always knows. That's the whole of smuggling and the whole of sin, and I'd have saved us all fifty years of grief if I'd learned it sooner.

I stood up from the riverbank an old woman with wet knees and a dry throat, and I walked on toward the court, and I did not look back at the water, because looking back is how rivers get you.

* * *

The last stretch of road climbed, which felt like a joke somebody down there was telling. A road to the underworld ought to descend; instead it rose through rock that glowed faintly gold, like the mountain had swallowed a sunset and kept it. The air grew warm and thick, and the walls began to glitter, and then the walls stopped being walls and became something else — shelves, I'd have called them, if shelves held anything but light.

Souls. Hanging in their thousands, soft-glowing, each one a little different — some white as new paper, some gold, one or two a deep bruised violet that I chose not to think about. They hung in rows that receded farther than the eye could follow, and the quiet among them was enormous. Not silence. Quiet. The difference being that silence is empty, and that quiet was full — full of everything those souls remembered, all of it held just behind the glow, like words behind a shut door.

I had sold one of those. One of those lights, hanging in the dark, belonged to a seven-year-old girl who liked to catch frogs and couldn't remember why she'd liked it.

"Keep walking," I told my legs, and they did, because my legs have always been more obedient than the rest of me.

The court opened ahead like a cathedral that had gotten ideas above itself. Gilded pillars. A floor of polished black stone that reflected the hanging lights so the whole space seemed lit from below and above at once. And there upon a throne of pale mineral sat the Pale Regent, whom I will describe as best I can, though description fails the way a rope bridge fails — suddenly, and with everyone on it.

It was tall. It was thin. Its face was beautiful the way frost on a window is beautiful, which is to say: intricate, and indifferent, and gone by morning. When it spoke, its voice arrived inside your chest rather than your ears.

"A surface-dweller," it said. "Alone. Uninvited. How novel. The last century produced no uninvited guests at all. You people have become so *polite*."

"I've been called worse things than polite," I said, and my voice cracked on it, because terror does that to a throat my age. "Old Tally. Of the Thornfell routes. I've come about a soul."

"They all come about a soul." The Regent's head tilted, slow as a door in a house nobody lives in. "Yours?"

"No." I planted my boots on that black glass floor and made myself stand straight, the way I used to stand when the tax collectors came through the pass and I had contraband in every pocket. "A child's. Wren, of Mirabel's Garden. Sold nine years ago through a broker whose name your records will hold. Sold by me."

Something moved in the great hall — a stirring among the hanging lights, the way a field of wheat stirs when wind finds it. The Regent regarded me for a long moment, and I had the distinct feeling of being read. Not watched. *Read*, cover to cover, footnotes and all.

"You confess," it said. "Here. In this place. Where debts are weighed to the grain."

"I confess."

"Do you understand what confession costs here? In your world, a confession is a performance. You say the words, your god nods, your neighbors whisper, and by spring you're respectable again. Here, a confession is a *transaction*. Once spoken truly, it cannot be unsaid. It becomes part of the ledger. Forever."

"Good," I said. "That's exactly what I'm hoping."

The Regent's frost-face did something I couldn't parse — a tightening, perhaps, or the beginning of curiosity, which in a creature that old might be the same thing. "Speak, then. But know that truth spoken here binds the speaker. If your truth serves the ledger, the ledger keeps it. If it breaks a debt falsely held—" the Regent paused, and the pause had weight, like a hand set gently on a scale, "—then the ledger must correct itself. And corrections are never gentle."

"I'm seventy-one years old," I told it. "Gentle had its chance with me. It declined."

* * *

I told it everything.

I told it about the year the passes closed — how the imperial patrols sealed the northern routes after the Salt War, how the smuggling families who'd fed three valleys for generations faced ruin, how the court's broker had appeared one autumn night at my fire wearing a merchant's smile and carrying a ledger bound in something I hadn't wanted to examine too closely. I told it the terms he'd offered: souls given freely, purchased clean, in exchange for routes reopened, patrols blinded, roads kept open that should by every law of men and mountains have stayed shut. I told it how I'd reasoned — and reasoning is the right word, God help me, I *reasoned* — that a soulless child who walked and laughed and grew was no poorer than a starving one, and that the family's survival bought bread for forty children who kept their souls entire.

I told it about Mara. About her thumb in the wax. About the words *both of us*, and how I'd spent fifty years pretending she'd said *only you*.

"And the child Wren," the Regent said, when I finished. "Her sale. Was it freely given?"

I made myself say it plainly. "No. Her mother prayed for the child's safety from a fever. The prayer was answered crooked — the fever broke, and the broker came within the month, and I told the mother the sale was the price of the answered prayer, which was a lie. The prayer was free. The gods were answering again after centuries of silence, and I knew it, and I used it. I dressed a bargain up as a miracle and sold a child to pay for my roads."

The hall went very still. Even the hanging lights seemed to lean in.

The Regent rose from its throne. It did not stand so much as *unfold*, the way winter unfolds over a valley, and when it spoke again its voice filled the whole gilded dark.

"This debt is false."

Two words. They landed on that black floor like dropped coins.

"The consent was manufactured. The prayer was weaponized. The brokered sale rests on fraud, and fraud, in this court, voids the instrument entire." The Regent descended one step, and the light of ten thousand souls bent toward it. "Do you comprehend what you have done, surface-dweller? For nine years this court has held a soul it has no right to hold. Our ledger does not err. Our ledger *does not err*. And now it must be corrected, and the correction will be heard in every realm that touches this one, and there will be consequences, and they will not land softly."

"Good," I said again. My knees had gone bad somewhere in the middle of my confession, and I was leaning on a pillar that was warmer than a pillar should be, but I made my voice come out level. "Let it be loud. Let every smuggler and broker and clever old woman on the surface hear it. That's the price I want paid. Not coin. Not years. Just this: say it aloud, before your whole court, before every soul you've got hanging in the dark. Say that the sale was fraud. Say that the child Wren was taken unjustly. Say it so it can't be unsaid."

"That is not a price," the Regent said. "That is a correction, and corrections are owed regardless of your wishes. What you ask costs you nothing."

"Everything costs something down here. I've read enough of your kind's bargains to know the arithmetic." I pushed off the pillar and stood on my own two feet, wobbling like a newborn foal, seventy-one years old and terrified clear down to the marrow. "So name my fee. The correction happens either way — fine, good, let it. But I'm not walking out of here having cost your precious ledger nothing. I sold a child. I'll not have even the fixing of it come free."

The Regent studied me. Frost studying fire, maybe. Or the other way round.

"You are an unusual criminal," it said at last. "Most who defraud this court spend their remaining years constructing elaborate reasons why they did not."

"I tried that for fifty years. It's heavy lifting, and the view doesn't improve."

One long finger, pale as moonstone, extended toward me. "Then hear the terms. The soul of the child Wren will be released to the petitioners who came before you — the cartographer and their company, who wagered for it fairly and whose wager stands. The original contract is void. The broker who facilitated it—" and here the Regent's voice acquired an edge that made the hanging lights shiver, "—will be named in the correction, publicly, in perpetuity. Your name, Old Tally of the Thornfells, will hang in this hall alongside the souls you trafficked. Every petitioner, every merchant, every god who passes through this court will read it. That is your portion."

"And mine?" I asked. "My soul. You'll want it. Fair's fair — I've got one, it's worn but it holds a charge."

The Regent was quiet a moment. Then, and I swear this on every road I ever ran, it sounded almost weary.

"Your soul is collateral," it said. "Not forfeit. You will remain in this court as surety against the correction — for if the surface realms contest the voiding of the contract, if brokers and their patrons raise claim against our ledger, we will require... leverage. A living witness. A confessed criminal of standing, whose testimony cannot be dismissed as self-interest, because her interests are already forfeit." The pale finger curled closed. "You will stay, Old Tally. Perhaps a year. Perhaps a century. The correction must be seen through, and you are its instrument now."

I looked up at the hanging lights. Thousands of them. Somewhere in those rows, one of them was a frog-catching girl from Mirabel's Garden, and somewhere above us all, that girl was walking around empty-eyed because a hard old woman had done arithmetic with her life.

"Well," I said, and my voice finally broke properly, and I let it, because some things shouldn't come out smooth. "Seventy-one years I've been telling folks the road goes where I say it goes. About time one of them said it back."

The Regent inclined its frost-white head — the closest thing to respect, I suspect, that a creature like that can manufacture.

"Then it is witnessed," it said, "and entered, and binding. Go to the guesting-hall. Your companions will be brought to you, for the cartographer's wager concludes on the twelfth night, and the court judges—" it paused, and the pause was almost, almost gentle, "—judges that you will wish to see them. There is a matter of a final riddle. And a matter of a flight. And I suspect, old woman, that your roads run swifter than anyone in this hall believes."

I turned to go, and my legs shook, and I let them.

Behind me, in a voice that filled the gilded dark from floor to unreachable ceiling, the Pale Regent began the correction — began to speak my crime aloud into the permanent dark, my name entering the ledger of the underworld forever, and every hanging soul in that vast bright hall turned slowly, slowly toward the sound, like flowers finding a sun they'd forgotten existed.

I walked toward the guesting-hall with my chin up and my eyes streaming, an old smuggler making one last delivery.

This time, the cargo was the truth.
