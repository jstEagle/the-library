## 19. Truth Before the Throne

The court beneath the mountain had a way of making silence feel like an audience.

When Tally walked in — old boots on gilded stone, her gray braid swinging like a pendulum that had finally run out of patience — every lantern-soul in that vast dark turned its light toward her. Thousands of them, hanging from chains that vanished upward into a ceiling no eye could find, and all of them leaning, the way wheat leans toward weather.

I had spent my whole life believing maps were honest because they were made by hands. Standing there, I understood for the first time that honesty is not a property of documents. It's a property of debts. And this court kept its books better than any cartographer alive.

Tally stopped three paces before the Pale Regent's dais and did not kneel.

I loved her for that. I also wanted to shake her until her teeth rattled, because kneeling might have bought us a kinder sentence.

"Old woman," the Regent said. Its voice was the sound a glacier makes when it decides to become a river — patient, enormous, faintly amused. "You are not summoned. You are not expected. You are not *owed* passage here."

"That's the point," Tally said. "I've come to un-owe something."

Bramble made a small sound beside me, half gasp, half prayer. Sefa's hand found my sleeve. Wren's soul-lantern hung at my hip where I'd carried it since the tenth riddle failed, warm as a sleeping bird, and I felt it stir against my side like it knew.

"You cannot un-owe," said the Regent. "Debt is the only law older than me."

"No," Tally said. "You're wrong, and I'm the proof. Debt can be *falsified*. A debt built on a lie isn't a debt. It's a theft wearing debt's clothes." She reached into her coat — slowly, deliberately, the way you move around things older than kings — and drew out a folded scrap of paper so worn it had gone soft as cloth. "And I have come to say the true words out loud, before your throne, where lies are supposed to curdle."

The Regent went very still.

I want to be clear about what stillness means in that place. When a living person goes still, it's a pause. When the Regent of the court beneath the mountain goes still, it is a geological event. The lanterns dimmed. The etiquette-things that patrolled the aisles — tall, faceless, dressed in the manners of extinct courts — stopped mid-stride. Even the gold on the walls seemed to hold its shine.

"Speak," the Regent said.

And Tally, who raised me, who taught me to read stars and fences and men's intentions, who fed me when my mother couldn't and lied to me about why she never spoke of her — Tally looked up at that throne of gilded dark and told the truth.

* * *

"I brokered the sale," she said. "Thirty-one years ago. A child of Mirabel's Garden, sold to intermediaries of this court, carried down through the under-road by a courier who didn't know what he carried." Her voice didn't shake. That was somehow worse than shaking. "The routes were dying. The passes were closing. The Thornfell families were starving, and something came to me in the winter with an offer: one soul, freely given by contract, and the roads would stay open. Warm winters. Full cellars. I signed. I told myself the child was unwanted, unclaimed, that no one would miss what no one had named." She paused, and her jaw worked like she was chewing on glass. "That was a lie I told myself so I wouldn't have to tell anyone else."

"The child," the Regent said, "was unclaimed."

"She wasn't," Tally said. "She was hidden. There's a difference, and I knew it, and I signed anyway. Her mother hid her from a father who'd have sold her himself. Hiding isn't abandonment. I knew that too." She unfolded the soft old paper and held it up. On it, I could see the ghost of ink, a signature gone brown with decades. My mother's name was written above Tally's, both of them witnesses. Both of them complicit. "And thirty-one years later, when the court came collecting on the *next* generation — when it took Wren's soul through a prayer answered crooked — I recognized the work. Because I'd done it before. With my own hands. With Vesper's mother standing witness beside me."

Every eye in the company turned to me. I kept mine on Tally, because if I looked at the Regent just then I would have done something unforgivably stupid, and we needed one more hour of forgivable stupidity-free conduct.

"I let Vesper believe the debt was theirs alone," Tally went on. "I let them carry it up the mountain. I followed anyway, because following was the least I owed and the most I could bear." She lowered the paper. "There. It's spoken. In front of the throne. Let it curdle."

* * *

The court did something I will spend the rest of my life failing to map.

It *weighed* her.

Not metaphorically — or not only metaphorically. The air thickened. The lanterns brightened and dimmed in waves, thousands of souls flickering like a heartbeat the size of a cathedral, and I understood that each one was being consulted. Every soul the court had ever bought, every stolen light in that hanging dark, was being asked a question I couldn't hear. Judging Tally. Judging the truth of her.

Sefa gripped my arm hard enough to bruise. "It's true," she breathed. "Vesper — the truth is *verifiable* here. It's not a matter of belief. It's a substance. They're testing whether her confession weighs true."

"And if it does?"

"Then the original sale was fraudulently brokered." Sefa's voice cracked on the word *fraudulently*, and under the crack was thirty years of exile, of priests telling her the gods were silent, of her insisting otherwise and paying for it. "A false debt. And this court — this horrible, beautiful, terrible place — prizes true debts over everything. Over mercy. Over kings. Over *itself*. If the debt is false, it must void it. Its own law will force it."

The Regent knew that too. I watched it know that. And I watched fury move across that pale, unfinished face like weather over water — not the hot fury of a man cheated, but the cold fury of a ledger that has found an error in its own arithmetic.

The weighing ended. The lanterns settled.

"*True,*" the Regent said, and the word fell into the hall like a stone into a well. "*Fraudulently brokered. Witnessed falsely. Signed under concealment.*" Its gaze moved from Tally to the soft old paper to me. "The sale of the child's soul, thirty-one years past, is hereby declared void. The debt does not exist. It never existed. The court does not collect on thefts."

The hall exhaled. Bramble actually sat down on the gilded floor, right there, legs simply giving out. Somewhere in the hanging dark, one lantern flared bright gold and then, gently, began to rise — drifting upward toward a ceiling none of us could see, toward whatever release looks like from inside a jar. Thirty-one years late. But rising.

Wren's soul-lantern grew warmer against my hip.

I should have been triumphant. Part of me was — a fierce, singing part that wanted to laugh and cry and kiss Old Tally on both cheeks and then strangle her, possibly in that order. We had done it. Thirteen riddles, nine nights survived, one decade of my own memories already forfeit to their ledger, one confession dragged up the under-road by a woman too stubborn to die and too guilty to stay home — and the court's own sacred law had turned on itself.

But the Regent was still looking at me. And it was smiling.

I have seen smugglers smile when the deal turns. I have seen Ashen-Mouth smile, which is a thing I hope never to describe even under torture. The Regent's smile was neither. It was the smile of an accountant who has found the error, corrected the books, and now intends to discuss the *fee*.

"Do not," the Regent said softly, "celebrate yet, cartographer."

"We won," Bramble whispered. "Didn't we? Tell me we won."

"The debt is void," the Regent said. "The child's soul is returned to your keeping — that lantern you carry so carefully, as though warmth were a thing you could steal. Take it. Go. Climb back to your thin air and your short lives." It leaned forward on its throne of gilded dark, and the whole hall leaned with it. "But understand what you have cost me tonight."

"Cost *you*?" Sefa's voice came out sharp with disbelief. "We came to *you*—"

"You have unmade a transaction," the Regent said, "that stood for thirty-one years. Do you imagine this court runs on gold? On prayer? On the little fires of human faith?" It gestured upward, one pale hand, at the thousands of hanging lights. "This court runs on *balance*. Every soul purchased is a weight entered. Every weight entered holds up a wall, seals a door, keeps something older than me asleep beneath the deepest floor of this place. You have struck one weight from the scale. The scale remembers. The scale *tilts*."

The temperature dropped. Not gradually — all at once, like walking into a river in winter. The lanterns nearest the dais guttered. Far below us, beneath the floor, beneath the foundations, something shifted in its sleep, and the sound it made was not a sound but a *pressure*, a slow tectonic sigh that I felt in my teeth and in the half-map of my own memory where my childhood used to be.

"What's down there?" I asked. My voice came out steadier than I deserved.

"Nothing you need to know," the Regent said, "if you pay."

"There it is," I said. "There's always a price."

"There is always a price," the Regent agreed, and for one moment its fury softened into something almost like respect. "You think I am cruel. I am not cruel. Cruelty is imbalance. I am the opposite of cruelty: I am *arithmetic*. The voiding of the debt is law, and law I honor — take the child's soul, go free, my word on it, and my word is the oldest currency in this hall. But the *error* must be paid for. Not by the child. Not by the old woman — her penance is her own long life, which she will now live knowing what she knows, which is payment enough even for me." Its eyes fixed on mine. "By the one who petitioned. By the one who wagered. Debts follow the debtor, cartographer. You opened this account. You will close it."

I thought of the tenth riddle. Of the decade the court's ledger had already taken — my mother's voice, gone; the smell of our kitchen, gone; the reason I flinch at the smell of pine smoke, present but inexplicable. I thought of the wager's terms still standing: *a soul of equal weight*. I thought of Wren's lantern, warm against my hip, nearly whole again after thirteen nights of riddles and shrines and prayers answered by gods who had finally remembered how to listen.

"How much?" I asked.

The Regent smiled again, and this time there was nothing cold in it at all, which frightened me more than the glacier-voice ever had.

"Not a how much," it said. "A *what*. The scale requires a weight, and the weight must be chosen freely, and it must be *yours*. Bring me your offering before the moon completes its thirteenth turn — you have one night, cartographer, and the moon is already climbing. Choose well. Choose truly. Because the scale does not weigh size." It settled back, and the pressure beneath the floor sighed once more, and went still. "It weighs *willingness*."

* * *

They gave us a chamber for the night — a small vaulted room off the great hall, gilded, windowless, lit by a single lantern-soul that hummed to itself in an old dead language. Bramble lay flat on the floor and stared at the ceiling. Sefa knelt in the corner and prayed openly, out loud, for the first time since her exile, her voice steady and her hands shaking.

Tally sat beside me on the stone bench and didn't touch me, and the not-touching was louder than any apology she'd given on the under-road.

"You heard it," she said finally. "What it wants. A willing weight."

"I heard."

"It'll ask for your soul. All of it. Equal weight for equal weight — that's how its arithmetic works. And you'll be tempted to give it, because you've got that look, the one your mother had, the one that mistakes sacrifice for strategy." Her old hands were folded tight in her lap. "Don't give it all. Whatever you do, whatever noble fool thing you're building in that head — don't give it all."

"Tally." I looked at her. Thirty-one years of lie between us, burned away in five minutes of truth before a throne, and she was still the woman who'd fed me, taught me, followed me into hell with a marked map clutched in her fist. "Why did you really come?"

She was quiet a long time. The humming lantern filled it.

"Because the river showed me myself," she said at last, "and I couldn't drink and walk away. Because you left me a map, and a map is a question, and I've spent my whole life answering questions." She looked up at me, and her eyes were wet and furious and relieved all at once. "And because someone had to say it out loud where the truth could weigh it. I just wish—" Her voice caught. "I wish it hadn't cost you a decade to buy me the courage."

"It didn't buy your courage," I said. "It bought the court's attention. The courage was yours. Ugly, late, and yours."

That almost got a laugh out of her. Almost.

Then the door of the chamber opened without being touched, and Ashen-Mouth stood in it — all smoke and ember and the smell of chapels after fire, filling the frame and somehow not entering, because even gods observe etiquette here.

"Little cartographer," the god said, and its voice was warm ash settling. "One night remains. The moon climbs. And I have come to remind you of an old bargain, made by a woman with your same stubborn jaw, in a chapel that no longer stands." It extended one burning hand, palm up, the way you offer a coin or a knife. "Your mother promised me a witness. When you go before the scale tomorrow — whatever you choose to lay upon it — I will stand beside you and bear witness that the offering is freely given."

"Why?" I asked. "What does a god get out of witnessing?"

Ashen-Mouth's embers brightened, which I had learned was its version of a grin.

"A debt honored," it said, "is the only prayer a god ever really answers."

Outside the windowless room, somewhere far above us, the moon began its thirteenth and final turn.
