## 21. The Twelfth Night

The court began to die the way a liar dies — all at once, and loudly, and insisting to the very end that it had always meant to.

We were three levels up from the throne when the first lantern went out. I felt it more than heard it: a cold spot opening in the air behind us, like a door left ajar in winter. Then another. Then a sound I will spend the rest of my life trying not to hear again — the sound of a soul going dark, which is not silence at all but a kind of thin, high *unmaking*, like a note played on an instrument that no longer exists.

"Vesper." Sefa's hand closed on my arm, hard enough to bruise. "The ceiling."

I looked up. The gilded dark above us — that beautiful, suffocating canopy of hanging souls that had lit our descent — was flickering. Not all of it. Just one vein of it, running from the throne dais toward the far wall, guttering like a candle in a draft.

"The Regent's fury," Bramble said. He had Wren's lantern slung across his back with a smuggler's rigging, wrapped in three layers of cloth against the cold, and he was already moving, because Bramble had never once in his life waited for permission to run. "Or the moon. Or both. Doesn't matter. *Move.*"

We moved.

* * *

You would think a court beneath a mountain would have stairs you could trust. You would be wrong. The under-road through the court had been built by things older than kings, and older than kings means older than straight lines. Corridors bent where they should not have been able to bend. A gallery we had crossed on the way down — the Hall of Small Debts, Sefa had called it, voice tight with old grief — now opened onto a chamber I had never mapped and did not want to name, its walls hung with contracts written on skin so old it had gone the color of candle tallow.

And everywhere, the light was failing.

"It's the wager," Sefa gasped as we ran. She had torn her skirt to the knee somewhere above and did not care, and her bare feet slapped the obsidian floor with a rhythm I found oddly steadying. "The Regent said it himself — debts are sacred here. Vesper, we *voided* a debt. We unmade something this place considers holy. The court can't hold its own shape around a wound like that."

"So it's collapsing," I said.

"So it's *dying*," she corrected, "and dying things grab."

She was right, of course. She usually was, which was insufferable, which was why I kept her close. Twice in the next hundred yards the floor reached for us — not metaphorically; the black stone flexed upward like a tongue testing the air — and twice Bramble's knowledge of the under-road saved us, cutting left through service passages so narrow we had to turn sideways, so old their walls were slick with something that might have been wax or might have been time.

Old Tally would have loved these passages. The thought arrived uninvited and I put it away the way you put away a knife you're not ready to use.

Tally was two days behind us, following my marked map up from the surface with whatever escort Ashen-Mouth had grudgingly provided her. Two days. If the court died before she reached the throne room — before her confession could be spoken into the ledger permanently, witnessed and sealed — then everything we had won would unravel. The sale would stand. Wren's soul would hang here forever in a lantern among lanterns, and the court's death would only make it harder to find.

So we ran. And behind us, level by level, the gilded dark went out.

* * *

We surfaced onto the Long Stair — the great ascending gallery that spiraled up through the mountain's heart toward the Thornfell Gate — just as the twelfth night reached its deepest hour.

I knew the hour the way a cartographer knows anything: by the moon. Through a fissure in the mountain's flank, far above, a sliver of the night sky showed, and in it hung the moon, swollen and strange, one turn short of complete. Thirteen turns since Wren's prayer had been answered. Thirteen turns since a child in Mirabel's Garden had stopped being entirely a child.

One turn left. When it completed, the wager ended — answered or forfeit, settled or damned.

Twelve riddles stood answered in the ledger of the court. Nine we had solved together in those grinding early nights, trading memories and prayers and pieces of ourselves across the Regent's table. The tenth had cost me a decade — my mother's face, most of what remained of it, scraped out of me like marrow from a bone. The eleventh Sefa had answered alone, kneeling in the wreck of a shrine with her hands open and her faith, impossibly, rekindled.

The twelfth had answered itself tonight, half an hour ago, in the most terrifying way possible: the moment the court began to collapse, every remaining lantern in the upper galleries had flared at once, and a voice that was not the Regent's — older, wearier, almost amused — had spoken through the dark:

*A house that burns itself down to keep its promises: what is it?*

And before any of us could open our mouths, the answer had simply been *true*, the way answers are true in places where geography obeys prayer. *A home.* The word had landed in the ledger like a coin in a well, and the thirteenth riddle had begun.

That was the trouble. The thirteenth riddle had *begun*, and it had not finished beginning. It hung in the air of the whole mountain like weather about to break. I could feel it pressing against the inside of my skull, patient and enormous, waiting for me to understand a question I hadn't even heard yet.

"What does it say?" Bramble asked. "The last one. Can you hear it?"

"No." I took the lantern from him — gently, the way you take a sleeping child from someone whose arms are tired — and held it against my chest. Inside the glass, Wren's soul burned steady and gold, the only calm thing in the collapsing dark. "It hasn't said anything yet. It's just... waiting."

"For what?"

"For me, I think." I started up the stair. "It's been waiting for me for a long time."

* * *

Here is what I have not told anyone, not Sefa, not Bramble, not the Regent, not the god who holds my mother's debt in his ash-grey palm.

When I was nine years old, the winter my mother disappeared into Tally's debt-wagon and did not come back, I climbed to the ruined chapel above Mirabel's Garden — the same burned chapel where Ashen-Mouth would find me years later — and I prayed. I didn't know how to pray. Nobody had taught me; the gods were dead, everyone said so, and prayer was for fools and priests, and I was going to be a mapmaker, and maps don't need gods.

But I knelt in the ash anyway, because I was nine, and I was alone, and the village below had never once felt like mine. Tally fed me and taught me and called me *little compass*, and I loved her the way you love a fire — grateful and careful and always slightly afraid of getting burned. But belonging is not the same as being kept. And I knelt there in the cold and I asked — I *begged* — whoever might still be listening:

*Let me belong somewhere. Let me belong to something larger than a road.*

I had forgotten it. That's the truth. I had forgotten it so completely that when Sefa told me the riddles were prayers — the same prayers the gods had begun answering again — some part of me had flinched and refused to do the arithmetic. Twelve riddles, twelve prayers, each one answered by someone: the widow at the pass, the priestess in exile, the thief who wanted to forget. It had been easy, so easy, to believe the pattern belonged to other people.

But the thirteenth prayer was mine. It had always been mine. And the gods, who had slept for centuries and woken hungry and strange, had taken my childish bargain seriously — they were answering it right now, tonight, in the only way gods ever answer anything: literally, terribly, and at a price.

*Let me belong to something larger than a road.* So they had given me a road between worlds. They had given me a company of borrowed names who chose to stay. They had given me a debt, a wager, a half-soul, and a child's lantern to carry home. Every terrible gift of this journey had been an answer to a prayer I made in the ash when I was nine years old and did not know what I was asking.

The question of the thirteenth riddle, when it finally came, would not come as words. It would come as a choice. I could feel it gathering.

* * *

The Gate was sealed.

We came around the final spiral and found the Thornfell Gate — the threshold we had entered weeks ago, the door between the mapped world and the unmapped one — fused shut with darkness that was not shadow but absence, a hole in the world shaped like a door. Beyond it, faintly, I could smell pine and rain and the honest dirt of Thornfell, and the smell made me want to weep.

"It's closing with the rest of it," Sefa said quietly. "The court is dying, and the doors die with it."

"Then we go out through the under-road," Bramble said. "The smugglers' way. There are a dozen—"

"Bramble." I touched the lantern. "Look at it."

He looked. Wren's soul had begun to lean — the flame inside the glass tilting, straining, toward the sealed gate like a plant toward sun. Toward home. And the closer the flame leaned, the dimmer it grew, as if the distance between it and Mirabel's Garden were a thing drinking it.

"She doesn't have weeks," Sefa whispered. "She doesn't have days. The soul knows where it belongs, and every hour it spends on the wrong side of that door, it... pours out. Looking for the way back."

Behind us, far below, another gallery went dark. The cold breath of the dying court climbed the stair after us like floodwater.

And the thirteenth riddle finally spoke.

It did not speak in words. It spoke in the oldest language there is, the language of doors and thresholds and the moment your foot hangs in the air between one step and the next. It said:

*You asked to belong to something larger than a road. Here is the road entire, dying around you. Here is the door. Here is the child. Here is the price. What will you give to open it?*

And I understood — finally, completely, with the terrible clarity that only arrives at the worst possible moment — what the answer was.

Not memories. I had few enough left. Not blood; the court had no more claim on my blood. The door needed what all true doors need: a key made of the thing it opens for. The gate between worlds would open for a soul freely given — not sold, not brokered, not bargained away in a wagon-deal by frightened women, but *given*, the way you give a gift, the way you light a lantern for someone else's road.

Half my soul was already the court's fee, promised and pending. What remained of me was small. A decade gone, a childhood mostly spent, a self worn thin as tracing paper.

It would be enough. It would have to be enough.

"Vesper." Sefa had gone very still. She was looking at me the way you look at someone standing on a ledge. "What is it asking?"

"It's asking what I'm willing to pay," I said. "And I know the answer, and you're going to hate it."

"I already hate it."

I set the lantern down on the stair, gentle as setting down a sleeping child. I knelt beside it. And I pressed my palm flat against the cold glass, and I spoke — not to the gate, not to the riddle, but to the small gold burning thing that had once been a girl named Wren.

"Listen to me," I said. "I'm a mapmaker. I've spent my whole life drawing lines around the world so people wouldn't get lost in it. And I'm telling you: there is a way home, and it goes through me, and I am not afraid. Do you hear me, Wren? Whoever you are in there — however much of you is still listening — *I am not afraid.*"

The flame leaned into my palm. It was warm. Of course it was warm. Souls are warm; it's bodies that go cold.

I turned to my company — my impossible, borrowed-named, chosen family — and I saw Sefa already praying, lips moving, hands open, and Bramble already crying and pretending it was dust, and I thought: *this. This is the something larger than a road. This was the answer all along, and the gods only wanted me to say it out loud.*

"I give it freely," I said to the door, to the riddle, to whatever ancient patience was listening. "Not sold. Not owed. Given. Open."

* * *

The gate did not explode. Nothing so honest.

It *remembered*. The absence shaped like a door filled in with the memory of itself — pine and rain and moonlight pouring inward like water finding its level — and through the widening seam I saw the Thornfell mountains under the near-full moon, real and ragged and mapped, and beyond them, impossibly far and impossibly close, the lights of Mirabel's Garden.

The price was taken gently. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger keeps such things: it did not hurt. It was like setting down a pack I had carried so long I'd forgotten its weight. Something in me loosened and flowed out through my palm, through the lantern, through the door — and I felt myself become, in the most literal sense, half-mapped. Half-charted. Half of me walking home and half of me staying behind to hold the door.

Wren's soul rose out of the lantern like a struck match rising, passed through the seam of light, and was gone — home, ahead of us, faster than grief.

The gate stayed open exactly long enough.

We ran through it — Sefa laughing and sobbing, Bramble clutching his few remaining memories like coins — and the mountain exhaled behind us, and the Thornfell Gate closed with a sound like a book shutting, and the moon above us hung one turn short of full, and I stood on the wet grass of the mapped world feeling lighter and emptier and fuller than I had ever been in my life.

"We made it," Bramble said. "Thirteenth turn's tomorrow night. We made it with a night to spare."

"Yes," I said. And then, because the ground chose that moment to remind me it was still ground, I sat down in it rather hard.

Sefa crouched beside me. "Vesper. What did it take? What did you give?"

I considered lying. I was, historically, very good at it. But my company had earned better than my lies, and besides — I found I genuinely didn't know. I patted my pockets, that instinctive cartographer's inventory, and found them all present: hands, scars, the ache in my left knee, the habit of wryness. What was missing was subtler. A color had gone out of things. Or perhaps a color had come *in* — I couldn't tell yet. Half a soul is a strange inheritance; you don't discover its terms until later.

"I'll let you know when I figure it out," I said. "Ask me again in Mirabel's Garden."

Bramble helped me up. And that was when we all saw it — down the mountainside, where the smuggler path bent toward the valley — a light coming up toward us. Not a lantern of the court. Not the cold gold of souls. A plain, honest, oil-fed flame, carried in a plain, honest hand, attached to a plain, honest woman walking fast through the night with an escort of smoke at her heels.

Old Tally had reached the throne room. Old Tally had spoken her truth into the ledger. And now Old Tally was climbing my marked map toward me, carrying the only light she'd ever trusted, and I had one night — one turn of the moon — to decide what I would say to her when she arrived.

The smoke at her heels took the shape, briefly, of a mouth full of ash.

Ashen-Mouth had come to watch the last turn fall.
