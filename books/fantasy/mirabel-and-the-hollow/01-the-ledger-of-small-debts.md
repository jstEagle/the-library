## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The candle had burned down to a stub the color of old teeth, and Wren Okafor was still three columns short of balanced.

He sat in the back room of the Guild of Impossible Things — a building that had once been a granary, then a chapel, then a smuggler's warehouse, and now housed an organization dedicated to the pursuit of endeavors no sane person would fund. The guild's charter, framed above the hearth, read: *We undertake what reason forbids and fortune denies.* The charter did not mention the ledgers. The ledgers were Wren's contribution, and they were, he felt privately, the only truly impossible thing the guild had ever accomplished: making the accounts add up.

"Three coppers," he muttered, tapping the quill against his lip. "Three coppers missing from the ink budget."

Someone had bought something. Someone always had. Master Ferrow, the guild's resident alchemist, had a habit of purchasing "reagents" that were transparently brandy. Dame Osla, who claimed she could speak to storms (she could not; she was simply very loud), kept requisitioning candles for séances that never seemed to produce anything but wax drippings and mild embarrassment.

Wren wrote *miscellaneous* in the margin, as he did most nights, and moved on.

Outside, rain worked at the shutters with patient fingers. The guildhouse stood on Tallow Lane in the free city of Vessarin, wedged between a chandler's shop and a house of ill repute whose proprietor, Mistress Bell, was one of Wren's few genuine friends. It was not a grand location. But rent was cheap, the neighbors asked no questions, and the roof only leaked in two places, both of which had been diplomatically assigned to members nobody liked.

He finished the last page, blotted it, and allowed himself the small ceremony of closing the ledger with both hands, like shutting a door on a noisy room.

Then he opened the window, and got to work on the real accounting.

* * *

The rats came first. They always did.

They arrived along the rafters in a rustle of small ambition — six of them tonight, led by a scarred female Wren had named Duchess, mostly because she refused to answer to anything else. She settled on the windowsill with the composure of someone arriving at a dinner party she had technically invited herself to.

*You're late,* said Duchess.

*I'm never late,* said Wren. *You're early.*

*Those are the same thing from where I sit.*

*From where you sit,* said Wren, *everything is either food or a threat. I've made my peace with being neither.*

Duchess considered this. *Barely.*

This was how it went, most nights. Wren spoke to animals the way other men hummed while working — quietly, constantly, and with a persistent awareness that if anyone caught him at it, everything he loved would burn.

Because that was not a figure of speech. His village had burned. Ashfen-edge, nineteen years ago, when he was nine years old and had made the mistake of telling his mother that the miller's dog was lonely. He hadn't known the word for what he was. He'd only known that the words came out of him sideways, into ears that shouldn't have understood, and that the dog had answered — plainly, cheerfully, in a voice like gravel rolling downhill.

Someone had heard. Someone had told the wrong person. And the wrong person had told the kind of men who carried torches and warrants in equal measure, and by morning there was nothing left of Hollowbrook but smoke and a black tooth of chimney standing in the ash like a grave marker.

His mother had put him on a grain cart heading south and told him never to speak to anything that wasn't human again. Not *don't get caught*. Never *speak*. As if the gift were a habit, like biting nails, rather than the shape of his own mind.

He had obeyed her for eleven years. Then, one winter night, starving and half-frozen under a bridge in Vessarin, he had heard a gull complaining about the weather, and the loneliness had cracked something open in him, and he had answered before he could stop himself.

The gull had been so startled it dropped its fish.

They had been friends ever since. Well — acquaintances. Gulls held grudges about fish.

*Well?* Duchess prompted. *Are we doing business or aren't we?*

*We're doing business.* Wren fished in his desk drawer and produced the evening's payment: a rind of hard cheese, saved from supper. He broke it into pieces on the sill. *I need eyes.*

*You always need eyes. What is it this time?*

*A man named Brannick. Debt collector. Works for the Lamplight Consortium. Grey coat, grey beard, walks like his knees are settling out of court.*

*I know him,* said Duchess, around cheese. *He kicks cats.*

*Does he now.*

*The orange one on Cooper Street. Twice. Unprovoked.*

*I'll note it in the file,* said Wren gravely, and Duchess made a sound that in a larger animal would have been a laugh.

But underneath the banter, his stomach was tight. Brannick had come to the guildhouse yesterday — ostensibly collecting on a debt owed by Master Ferrow, who had borrowed forty crowns from the Consortium to fund an experiment involving turning lead into gold, which had succeeded only in turning forty crowns into smoke. Brannick had stood in the guild's front hall, counting heads, and when his gaze had passed over Wren, it had snagged. Just for a moment. Like a fishhook catching cloth.

And then this afternoon, a rat from the harbor district had found Wren at the market and delivered a message in the breathless shorthand of rodents: *Grey-coat man asking questions. About you. About your family. Where you came from.*

Nobody asked where Wren Okafor came from. That was the entire point of Wren Okafor. He was the guild's bookkeeper, a man so unremarkable that new members routinely forgot his name, which suited him down to the ground. He had spent nineteen years constructing a personality so beige that even inquisitors' eyes slid off it. If Brannick was pulling at that thread, it meant someone had paid him to.

*Find out who he talks to,* Wren told Duchess. *Where he sleeps. Who he drinks with. Don't get close.*

*What do you take me for?*

*A rat.*

*Exactly. We're excellent at close.* She took the last of the cheese with dignity and poured herself off the windowsill into the dark.

Above the rooftops, the gulls wheeled in the storm wind, screaming their opinions at the city. Wren leaned out into the rain and listened to them the way other men listened to news-criers. Most of it was gossip — a fight behind the fish market, a broken crate of eels, the eternal scandal of the lighthouse lamp going unlit again. But one young gull, a ragged thing Wren called Cob because of the color of her feathers, swept low past the window and left him with something else:

*Big ships in the outer roads. Black sails. They don't fly any colors.*

Ships without colors, riding at anchor off a free city, in the first cold week of autumn. Wren filed that away next to the name Brannick, in the drawer of his mind where he kept things he didn't yet know how to be afraid of.

* * *

Morning brought fog, and with the fog, Brannick himself.

Wren saw him coming from the counting room window — the grey coat, the deliberate walk, the way he paused at the guildhouse door to look up at the facade, reading the carved motto with the expression of a man pricing a coffin. Wren had time to tuck his ledger away, smooth his face into pleasant administrative blankness, and put the kettle on before the knock came.

The Guild of Impossible Things operated on the principle that all visitors should be offered tea, because tea bought time, and time was the guild's principal currency. Wren poured two cups and met Brannick in the front hall, which was furnished with mismatched chairs, a stuffed crocodile of disputed provenance, and a sign reading PLEASE DO NOT FEED THE PHILOSOPHERS.

"Master Okafor." Brannick's voice was soft, which Wren distrusted more than shouting. Soft voices were for people who already knew they didn't need volume. "Consortium business."

"Master Ferrow's account," Wren said. "Yes. We've arranged payment — installments over four months, beginning—"

"I'm not here about Ferrow." Brannick accepted the tea anyway, holding it without drinking, which struck Wren as a professional discourtesy. "Forty crowns is small money. I'm here about a rumor."

"Rumors are outside my department. My department is arithmetic."

"Is it?" Brannick smiled. He had good teeth, which somehow made it worse. "Funny thing. A man in my trade hears stories. Stories about a boy from the north country, years back. Village called Hollowbrook. Terrible fire. One survivor, a child, carted south with the grain." He sipped — finally, delicately. "And now here's a bookkeeper in Vessarin, northern vowels under the city polish, no family, no history before age twenty. A man starts adding columns, Master Okafor. It's a disease of the trade. We can't help it."

The hall was very quiet. Somewhere upstairs, Dame Osla was attempting to negotiate with the weather, loudly and without success. Wren became aware of the stuffed crocodile looming at the edge of his vision and thought, absurdly, that it looked like it was listening too.

"You've mistaken me for someone," Wren said mildly. "It happens. I have one of those faces."

"No one has that face." Brannick set down the untouched tea. "Here's how this goes. The Consortium has clients in the capital. Certain clients pay well for certain names. A beast-speaker, alive and employed, in a free city — that's worth more than every debt on my books combined. Now, I could sell that name tomorrow." He leaned in, and his breath smelled of mint, carefully masking something older. "Or you could make it worth my while to forget I ever added those columns."

There it was. Not an accusation — an invoice.

Wren looked at him for a long moment. Nineteen years of practice kept his hands still and his pulse somewhere behind a locked door. Inside, though, something old and tired turned over, the way a body turns over in a bed it has lain in too long.

"What are you asking?"

"Fifty crowns. Now, and quarterly. Consider it a subscription to your continued obscurity."

Fifty crowns. Quarterly. Wren did the arithmetic without meaning to — it was his disease too — and found that it consumed nearly everything he'd saved across nine years of careful, gray, invisible living. Every coin hidden in the loose floorboard. Everything set aside against the day the guild failed, or the war came, or the world simply closed its hand.

He could refuse. He could run. He could deny it and gamble that Brannick's evidence was thinner than his confidence. All of those were moves on the board, and he examined each one the way he examined a ledger line that wouldn't balance — patiently, coldly, looking for the error.

There was no error. Brannick had done his sums. And a man who sold debts for a living knew exactly what a secret was worth, and exactly how much his buyer could pay.

"All right," Wren said.

Brannick raised his eyebrows, pleased.

"But not fifty," Wren went on, and heard his own voice go flat and quiet, the voice he used for closing accounts. "Sixty. And you'll take it in silence instead."

"I don't follow."

"It's simple bookkeeping." Wren folded his hands. "You'll receive sixty crowns — today, and quarterly, for as long as I draw breath. In exchange, you will never speak of Hollowbrook, of beast-speakers, or of me, to anyone, ever. Not to your clients. Not to your wife. Not to yourself, alone, in the dark. You will forget those columns exist. Because here's what your arithmetic missed, Master Brannick: a secret sold isn't yours anymore. It belongs to whoever buys it. And the people who buy names like mine don't pay collectors afterward. They use them up. You'd be selling me a rope and hanging yourself with it."

Brannick's smile flickered — just at the corner, just for a heartbeat. Long enough.

"And if I refuse your terms?"

"Then I'll pay you the fifty," Wren said, "and spend the rest of my life waiting for the knock at the door. Which is, I believe, the trade you're offering. I'm merely proposing better terms for both of us." He rose, went to the loose floorboard beneath the crocodile's plinth, and came back with a purse that weighed what nine years weighed. He counted sixty crowns onto the table between them, coin by coin, letting each one ring.

"The first quarter," he said. "The rest follows on the same day each season. You'll find me reliable. It's the one virtue of my profession."

Brannick looked at the money. Then at Wren. Whatever he saw in Wren's face seemed to settle the account, because he swept the coins into his coat without another word, nodded once — a merchant's nod, sealing terms — and let himself out into the fog.

Wren stood in the empty hall for a long time. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Upstairs, Osla had given up on the weather and was arguing with the philosopher instead.

Sixty crowns. He'd have to tell Duchess the cheese ration was cut. He laughed at that — a small, cracked sound with nothing much behind it — and then pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes and breathed until the shaking stopped.

That was the cost of hiding. Nobody warned you it wasn't paid once. It was paid quarterly, forever, in the only currency that mattered.

* * *

That night the rain came back, harder, with a wind behind it that made the shutters jump. Wren sat by the window with the ledger open but unread, watching water silver the street below, when Duchess returned — wet, breathless, and moving with a urgency that made his chest tighten before she said a word.

*Found him,* she said. *Your grey-coat. He met someone tonight. Down by the salt warehouses. A stranger off one of the black-sailed ships.*

Wren's mouth went dry. *What did they say?*

*Couldn't get close enough for words. But the harbor rats heard the tail end.* Duchess shook the rain off her fur, and her voice dropped into the register rats use for things that frighten even rats. *They were talking about a search. Not for you, bookkeeper. For something the storm washed in. Something walking up from the south road toward the city gates.*

*Walking?*

*A child,* said Duchess. *Alone. Barefoot. Mud to the waist. The gate guards tried to turn it away and it just... looked at them. And they stepped back. Both of them. Like it had spoken.*

*Did it?*

*No,* said Duchess. *That's the thing. It never opened its mouth. But every animal within ten yards heard it plain as bells. Saying a word none of us knows. Old word. Older than walls.* Her whiskers trembled. *A word for something buried.*

The candle guttered. Out in the dark, the rain redoubled, hammering the city like a fist on a door, and far to the south — Wren couldn't have said how he knew — something answered.

He was already reaching for his coat.
