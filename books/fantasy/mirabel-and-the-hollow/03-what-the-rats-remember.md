## 3. What the Rats Remember

The trouble with asking rats for favors, Wren had learned over eleven years of secret practice, was that they always wanted to be paid in cheese, and they always lied about how much they knew.

Not maliciously. Rats simply had the same relationship with truth that merchants had with weights — everything could be negotiated if you pressed hard enough. A rat would tell you the cat slept all afternoon because it wanted you to believe the kitchen was safe, even if the cat was, at that moment, three feet behind your head.

So Wren took precautions.

He sat cross-legged on the cold flagstones of the guildhouse cellar at an hour when even Corvane's lamp had gone dark, a wedge of hard yellow cheese from the guild stores balanced on his knee, and he waited for the cellar's resident parliament to assemble. They came in ones and twos, out of cracks in the mortar and gaps beneath the wine racks, until perhaps thirty of them ringed him in a loose half-moon, whiskers twitching, eyes like wet black beads in the lamplight.

"Well," said the eldest, a scarred grey female who had held the cellar for six years and whom Wren privately thought of as the Dowager. "The soft-footed one returns. With *cheese*." She said the word the way bishops said *heresy* — with relish.

"Good evening, Grandmother."

"Don't flatter me. You only call me Grandmother when you want something."

"That's not true." It was entirely true. "I called you Grandmother last spring when I warned you about the new terrier."

"You warned us about the terrier," the Dowager said, "because if we'd been caught, the exterminators would have come, and then who would you talk to down here? Sentiment is just strategy wearing a nicer coat, ledger-keeper. We know this about you."

Wren sighed. There was no winning an argument with a rat; there was only losing slowly, with dignity. He broke off a corner of the cheese and set it on the stones between them. "I need to know about the child."

The parliament went still. Thirty pink noses stopped working at once, which among rats was roughly equivalent to a room full of men dropping their tankards.

"The small one," Wren pressed gently. "Mud to the eyebrows. Came to our door two nights ago in the storm. Doesn't speak — or speaks something no one knows. The gulls saw it come up from the river gate. I want to know where it walked *from*."

Silence stretched. Then a young male near the back — Wren didn't know his name, but he had the nervous energy of a first-year clerk — blurted: "It came out of the Ashfen."

"*Hush,*" snapped the Dowager.

But it was too late; the word was out, and words, once loose among rats, ran like spilled grain.

"The Ashfen," Wren repeated. His mouth had gone dry. "That's not possible."

"Nothing lives in the Ashfen," said the Dowager, with the flat finality of someone reciting scripture. "Everyone knows this. Everyone has known it since before my grandmother's grandmother. The ground is ash, the water is poison, the birds will not fly over it. We do not go there. Our kind does not go there. No kind goes there."

"And yet," said the young male, unable to help himself, "it walked out of it. On its own feet. Through the dead country. Alone."

"How do you know this?" Wren asked. "You're cellar rats. You've never been past the tanneries."

"We have cousins," said the Dowager stiffly, as though admitting to embarrassing relatives. "River rats. Dock rats. One or two disreputable types who live near the east wall and have opinions. Word travels. And word says—" she paused, and Wren heard, for the first time in all their years of bargaining, actual fear in her voice— "word says the small one did not walk alone."

Wren's hands had gone cold around the cheese knife. "What does that mean?"

"It means things followed it," said the young male, in a rush now, the way people talked when they'd been holding a secret too long and it burned. "Out of the Ashfen. Things that the cousins couldn't name. Not wolves — the cousins know wolves, wolves are honest. Not crows — crows are rude but they're *alive*. These were... other. Things that moved wrong. That moved *slow*, like they had all the time in the world, like nothing could ever catch them because they weren't really *there*. The dock rats watched them pass along the seawall at night, three nights running, always behind the child, never catching up. Never giving up. And then—" he swallowed— "and then they stopped at the edge of the city. All together. Like they'd hit glass."

"They're waiting," said the Dowager quietly. "At the boundary. My cousin says the whole east wall stinks of them, though there's nothing to see. The cats won't hunt there anymore. The dogs howl at nothing. And the child walked straight through the middle of the city, through the storm, to *this door*."

Every black eye in the cellar turned toward Wren, and he felt the weight of thirty small judgments settle on his shoulders.

"Why this door?" he asked, though his heart already knew, the way you know the answer to a question you haven't dared ask aloud.

"Why do you think, ledger-keeper?" said the Dowager. "Rats don't choose doors. Doors are chosen *for* us. Something told that child where to go. And whatever it was, it knew this house." She fixed him with her wet black gaze. "Now. About that cheese."

He gave them all of it. Every crumb. It seemed suddenly, urgently important to be generous.

* * *

Sleep did not come, which was fine, because Wren had work to do anyway.

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its records in the undercroft behind the counting room — three centuries of ledgers, contracts, debts, and disputes, stacked on shelves that groaned like old ship timbers. Most guilds burned their dead paperwork. The Impossible Things kept everything, on the theory that an impossible thing might become possible again later, and then you'd want the receipts.

Wren lit a lamp, pulled the oldest volume from the deepest shelf — a ledger so ancient its leather cover had gone the color of dried blood, its spine repaired twice with cord that predated the current dynasty — and carried it to the table.

The guild's founding records. Before the free cities had been free. Before Veyran, before the wars anyone still named. The handwriting inside changed hands every few pages, scribe after scribe across the generations, but the earliest entries were in a script so old that Wren could barely parse it, and what he could parse made strange reading:

*Received: one promise, sealed in iron. Paid out: one hundred years of quiet.*

*Received: one grief, too large for any single bearer. Held in trust.*

*Debt outstanding: the debt beneath. Do not collect. Do not wake. Do not forget that we must not forget.*

And beside certain entries — the oldest ones, the ones in that pre-dynastic hand — marks. Small annotations in the margins, drawn with a steadier instrument than quill, scratched maybe, or carved with a stylus and rubbed with ash.

Spirals.

Wren stared at them until his eyes watered. Tight-wound spirals, each one slightly different, coiling inward like things falling into deep water. He'd seen them a hundred times over eleven years and never thought twice — old books collected odd marginalia the way old coats collected lint.

Then he climbed the stairs, crossed the dark counting room, and pushed open the door to the linen closet where he'd hidden the child two nights ago, wrapped in a tablecloth and a lie about a sick nephew.

The child was awake. Of course the child was awake. Children who walked out of dead countries did not seem to sleep so much as rest with one ear open, like soldiers, like prey animals, like Wren himself.

"Hello," Wren said softly, and then, because it had become habit in two days' time, "I brought bread."

The child took the bread. The child always took the bread, ate it with the methodical thoroughness of someone who had known real hunger, and then — this was the part that undid Wren every time — looked at him with those grave dark eyes and *waited*, as though bread were merely the opening move in a conversation the rest of which the world had lost the grammar for.

"I need to show you something," Wren said. "And I need you to tell me — any way you can — if it means anything."

He opened the great ledger on the floor between them, found the page with the margin-spirals, and turned the lamp low so the light fell across the old ink.

The child went rigid.

Not startled — Wren knew startled; he'd raised a younger sister and survived it. This was different. This was the stillness of recognition, of a lock feeling its key turn in the dark. The child leaned forward, laid one small mud-freckled finger on the spiral in the margin, and traced it, round and round and inward, following the coil to its center.

Then the child spoke.

It was the same language as before — that flowing, layered speech that sounded less like words than like weather, like wind moving through a canyon with intent — but softer now, almost tender, and Wren's gift, the cursed, buried, unspeakable gift that had gotten his village burned when he was nine years old, reached out without his permission and caught the shape of the meaning the way a net catches fish:

*A circle that goes down. A remembering that goes down. The mark of the ones who keep the going-down place.*

Wren sat back hard against the shelving. A jar of quills rattled.

"You know these," he whispered. "You *know* these."

The child looked up at him. And then, deliberately, the child did something that made the hair on Wren's arms stand up: it took his hand, turned it palm-up, and drew on his skin with one fingertip — a spiral, tight and perfect, exactly matching the ones in the three-hundred-year-old ledger.

Then it pointed at itself.

Then, after a moment, with terrible gentleness, it pointed at Wren.

"No," Wren said. His voice cracked on the word. "No, you've got it wrong. I'm a clerk. I count beans and chase debts and argue with rats about cheese. I'm nobody's anything. Whatever you think I am—"

The child tilted its head. And in the silence, unbidden, unwelcome, Wren's gift translated the look perfectly, because looks were a language too, and some meanings needed no words at all:

*The door was chosen. You are the door.*

* * *

He fled to the roof.

It wasn't dignified. Eleven years of careful, invisible living, and here he was at four in the morning scrambling up the guildhouse stairwell like a man chased by bees, needing sky, needing distance, needing anything other than the weight of a child's finger drawing spirals into his skin.

The gulls were already up, wheeling over the harbor in the grey pre-light, screaming their eternal grievances at the fishing boats. Wren leaned on the parapet and let the wind hit his face until his heart slowed.

"You look terrible," observed a gull perched on the chimney pot. Her name was Brine, though she refused to acknowledge it. "Have you been crying?"

"Gulls can't see tears at this distance."

"We can see *everything*. It's why we're insufferable." She ruffled her feathers and sidled closer, angling for whatever food he might be carrying, which was none. "The river birds say you've been asking after the small one."

Word traveled fast when thirty rats had cousins on every dock. "They told you?"

"They told the pigeons, the pigeons told everyone, because pigeons are gossips with wings. So let me save you the second round of interviews, clerk, because unlike the rats I don't charge cheese — I charge attention, and you pay badly." Brine fixed him with one yellow eye. "I flew the east road two nights ago. Storm or no storm, I fly it every night; it's my territory, and territory is the only thing that matters." She paused, and for once the theatrical self-regard dropped away. "Something is standing at the edge of the Ashfen. Many somethings. They don't fly, they don't eat, they don't sleep. They just stand. Facing the city. Facing, if you ask me — and you did — facing *here*."

"What are they?"

"If I knew, I'd be charging you considerably more." She snapped her beak. "But I'll tell you what I know, which is this: my mother's mother remembered stories from her mother's mother, from before the cities were free, from the time of the first sealing. Stories about watchers. About wardens. About a bargain struck so far underground that even the sky forgot it. And I'll tell you what I *saw*, which is this: when the small one walked out of that dead land, every one of those standing things bowed its head. All at once. Like courtiers. Like mourners." Her voice dropped to something almost human. "Like soldiers saluting a general they'd follow into hell."

The wind came off the harbor, cold and salt-sharp, and Wren hugged his arms around himself and thought: *my village burned because of what I am. Because one man heard me talking to a horse and decided the world was safer with forty houses reduced to ash.* He had spent his entire adult life being small, being careful, being a man who paid blackmail in silence and counted other people's impossible things because he could not afford his own.

And now the impossible things had come looking for him. Walking out of a dead country. Bowing to a child. Drawing spirals on his hands.

"There's more," Brine said, and now she sounded genuinely reluctant, which from Brine was unprecedented. "The standing things aren't alone anymore. Since last night, the birds that fly the east road — the brave ones, the stupid ones — say the ground behind them has started to *move*. Slowly. Toward the city. Not walking. Sinking and rising, sinking and rising, like something enormous breathing under the ash." She looked at him directly. "Whatever your small guest woke up walking through that place, clerk, it's still coming. And it isn't stopping at the wall."

Below him, muffled by floors and stone, Wren heard the guildhouse begin to stir — the cook lighting the stove, the porters grumbling awake, and, distinct beneath it all, the measured tread of Guildmaster Corvane crossing the counting room below, pausing, Wren would have bet his last coin, outside the linen closet door.

Corvane, who noticed everything. Corvane, whose smile never reached his eyes. Corvane, who had been asking very gentle, very reasonable questions about the child since dawn yesterday — *where did you find it, has anyone claimed it, you'll bring it to my office when it wakes, won't you, Wren?*

Wren looked east, past the rooftops, past the walls, toward a horizon where the ash met the sky in a smudge the color of old bruises.

"Thank you, Brine."

"Cheese next time," she said, recovering her dignity, launching herself into the wind. "Aged. Don't insult me with the hard yellow stuff again."

He stood alone on the roof a moment longer, listening — really listening, letting the gift run free for once — to the city waking beneath him. Ten thousand heartbeats. Mice in the granaries. Dogs in the yards. Horses stamping in the stable rows. And underneath it all, faint as a pulse through stone, from the direction of the Ashfen: a slow, vast rhythm, sinking and rising.

Sinking and rising.

Like breathing.

Downstairs, Corvane's tread stopped outside the linen closet, and stayed there.
