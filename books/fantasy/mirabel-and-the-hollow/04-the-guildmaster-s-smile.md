## 4. The Guildmaster's Smile

Guildmaster Aldous Corvane believed in three things: the sanctity of contracts, the medicinal value of a good brandy, and the principle that every problem, no matter how large, could be solved if one simply refused to be present when it arrived.

He was practicing the third of these on the morning the child came to his attention. He had risen early — earlier than he liked, which put him in a temper before the sun had properly committed to anything — because a shipment of "agricultural implements" from the eastern passes was due at the docks, and agricultural implements, in Corvane's long experience, did not unload themselves, did not pay their own tariffs, and very rarely were agricultural at all.

He stood at the window of his study on the guildhouse's third floor, a room he had furnished with deliberate modesty: plain oak desk, two chairs, a single shelf of ledgers bound in sober grey. Visitors always underestimated him in that room, and underestimation, Corvane had found, was the most reliable currency in the free cities. It never devalued.

Below, in the courtyard, the morning's business had already begun. Porters hauled crates. The gatekeeper, Brammel, a man built like a wardrobe with opinions, argued cheerfully with a fishwife about the weight of her baskets. Ordinary life, ordinary noise. Corvane had spent eleven years building this ordinariness, brick by careful brick, and he loved it the way a miser loves coins — not for what it was, but for what it could buy.

Which was why the mud-caked child standing at the gate, small and dripping and utterly unaccounted for, annoyed him before it ever worried him.

* * *

He watched from above for a quarter hour, which was his habit. Watch first; act second; apologize never, or only when apology was cheaper than silence.

The child stood at the gate as though it had grown there. It did not beg. It did not weep. It simply looked at the guildhouse with an attention that Corvane found — he searched for the word while sipping his brandy — *inconvenient*. Children who arrived at doors usually wanted something obvious: bread, coin, shelter from rain. This child wanted nothing visible. It only watched, water streaming off its hood, as though it were reading the building the way Corvane read a ledger.

And then Wren Okafor appeared.

Corvane's clerk. His ledger-keeper. A thin, quiet young man with ink-stained fingers and the general air of someone apologizing for taking up space. Corvane had hired him four years ago for two reasons: he was flawless with numbers, and he asked no questions. Both qualities were rarer than honesty among thieves, and considerably more valuable.

Wren crossed the courtyard, spoke briefly to the gatekeeper, crouched before the child, and then — here Corvane leaned closer, breath fogging the glass — did something odd. He went still. Perfectly still, the way a man goes still when listening to something very far away. Then he took the child's hand and drew it through the gate, past Brammel, into the kitchens, moving with the smooth urgency of a man smuggling contraband past customs.

Corvane set down his glass.

Interesting, he thought. And then, because he was honest with himself in the privacy of his own skull, if nowhere else: *Trouble.*

* * *

The Guild of Impossible Things had a public face and a private ledger, and Corvane kept both himself.

The public face was beloved throughout the free cities. The guild found lost heirs and authenticated miracles, brokered peace between feuding merchant families, and once — this story was told in every tavern from here to Saltmere — reunited a widow with her husband's ghost, or at least with a convincing imitation thereof. People brought the guild their impossible problems and paid handsomely, and the guild solved perhaps one in five, and everyone agreed this was a fine bargain, because hope, like brandy, was best purchased in small quantities at inflated prices.

The private ledger was simpler. It recorded what the guild would do, and for whom, and at what price, and it contained entries Corvane would burn before letting anyone read them. Entry after entry, year after year, all in his neat, narrow hand:

*To the office of the Lord Protector Veyran: six crates, marked grain, contents as discussed. Paid in full.*

*To the same: passage for two gentlemen of the capital through the smugglers' tunnels, no names requested. Paid.*

*To the same: the guild's silence regarding movements at Ashfen. Price negotiated. Paid.*

Corvane was not a cruel man. He wished that counted for more than it did. He fed the courtyard cats. He remembered his clerks' birthdays. He had once spent a ruinous sum buying freedom for a debt-bonded girl because her mother had wept in his office in a manner he found impossible to ignore. But cruelty and survival were different trades, and Corvane had chosen survival early, back when survival was the only thing on offer, and he had never seen reason to change professions.

When Veyran had taken the capital — not with armies at first, but with loans, favors, arrests, and the slow patient strangulation of every institution that might oppose him — the free cities' councils had wrung their hands and drafted strongly worded resolutions. Corvane had sent a courier with a bottle of good wine and a note offering the new regime whatever services the guild could discreetly provide.

People called this treachery. Corvane called it weather. You did not argue with a storm; you made sure your roof was the last one standing, and you charged the neighbors for shelter afterward.

But storms changed direction. And this morning, watching Wren Okafor shepherd a nameless child through his kitchen door, Corvane felt the wind shift against his neck.

* * *

He descended to the kitchens at midmorning, wearing his smile.

It was a famous smile, or ought to have been. Corvane had calibrated it over decades: warm enough to reassure, modest enough to disarm, with just a flicker of self-deprecating humor at the corners, as though to say *I know I am a foolish old man, please do not look too closely.* He deployed it now on Cook, who was stirring something that smelled aggressively of onions, and on Wren, who sat at the far table with the child beside him, feeding it bread and honey with the concentration of a man defusing a trap.

"Ah," said Corvane. "New staff?"

Wren started so visibly that the honey spoon clattered against the bowl. "Guildmaster. I — she was at the gate. In the storm. I didn't think you'd want—" He stopped, visibly reorganizing his sentence. "She was freezing."

"Mm." Corvane studied the child. Girl, he judged, seven or eight years old, though it was hard to say beneath the mud. Her hair had been fair once; the Ashfen dust had turned it the color of old bone. She stared back at him without fear, which was itself remarkable. Children looked at Corvane and saw either a grandfather or a wallet. This one looked at him as though counting his teeth.

"Where are her people?" he asked pleasantly.

"Don't know," said Wren. "She doesn't speak. Any tongue I've tried."

"Perhaps she's simple," Corvane suggested gently.

Something flickered across Wren's face — quick, quickly buried. "Perhaps," he said. "But she doesn't seem simple."

No, she did not. Corvane crouched, joints protesting, to bring himself level with the girl. "Hello," he said, in Common, then in High Trade, then in the river-tongue of the southern barges. He even attempted a phrase of Old Meridian, learned badly in his youth from a woman he preferred not to remember. The child watched his mouth move with polite, unnerving patience, the way one watches a dog perform tricks.

Then she spoke.

It was one word, or what sounded like one word, and it was nothing like any language Corvane knew. It was low and resonant, almost a hum, and it seemed to come up through the floor rather than out of her chest. The fire in the kitchen hearth guttered. Cook's ladle stopped mid-stir. For one absurd moment Corvane had the impression that the whole building had leaned slightly, like a ship correcting course.

Then it passed, and the child returned to her bread, and Cook swore softly at the fire, and everything was ordinary again except for Corvane's heart, which had begun conducting business at a considerably accelerated rate.

"What," he said, rising carefully, "was that?"

"I don't know," said Wren. And there it was again, that flicker — guilt, Corvane thought, or its close cousin, concealment. Four years of flawless service, and Wren Okafor could lie with numbers beautifully, but his face remained a scandalously open book.

Corvane smiled his smile. "Well," he said warmly. "We'll take care of her, of course. The guild looks after strays. That's rather our entire reputation." He patted Wren's shoulder on his way out, feeling the young man flinch beneath his hand like a plucked string. "You did right to bring her in, Wren. Very right."

In the corridor, alone, he let the smile fall off his face like a mask set down on a table.

* * *

That afternoon he wrote three letters.

The first was to the harbormaster, confirming the tariff arrangements for the eastern shipment, and required no courage whatsoever.

The second was to his tailor, and also required no courage, though it did require some diplomacy regarding the matter of sleeves.

The third he wrote slowly, on the guild's finest paper, in his most respectable hand, and addressed to an office in the capital that officially did not exist: the Bureau of Inquiry, whose agents wore grey and carried warrants the way other men carried knives.

*To the Honored Bureau,* he wrote. *It has come to the guild's attention that a child of unusual character has sought refuge at our house. She speaks no known tongue. There are those among my staff who believe her gift may be of the old kind — the kind your office concerns itself with. I report this in the spirit of cooperation that has long distinguished our relationship, and in the sincere hope that any inquiry may be conducted with discretion, so that the guild's reputation, and the peace of this city, may be preserved.*

He read it twice. He thought about adding a line asking that the child be treated gently, and decided against it. Sentiment in official correspondence was like saffron in stew: expensive, and it changed nothing.

He thought instead about the arithmetic, because arithmetic was where Corvane lived, comfortably, the way other men lived in houses.

If the child was nothing — a mute orphan, a stray — then reporting her cost the guild nothing and bought goodwill with the capital, which was never a bad investment. If the child was something — and the hearth-fire had flinched, Corvane had felt it flinch — then the guild stood at a crossroads of the sort that came along once a decade, and crossroads, handled correctly, were where fortunes were made.

There was a third possibility, of course. That the child was dangerous. That whatever slept beneath the free cities — and every schoolchild knew the stories, the Hollow, the first war, the sealing — had something to do with a girl who spoke in words that moved firelight. Corvane did not believe much in stories. But he believed firmly in the behavior of powerful men, and powerful men, when they smelled power, stopped paying for discretion and started demanding loyalty, and the price of loyalty was always quoted in other people's blood.

One stray life, weighed against the guild. Eleven years of work. Forty souls under this roof, from Cook to Brammel to Wren Okafor with his open face and his closed secrets. Against one child who had walked out of the Ashfen, where nothing lived, carrying a language older than any kingdom.

The scales were not subtle. They never were. That was what Corvane liked about scales.

He sealed the letter with the guild's wax — the impossible knot, the guild's sigil, a joke nobody outside the trade understood — and rang for Tam, his fastest courier, a boy with legs like a heron and a face too honest for smuggling, which was why Corvane only ever used him for letters, never cargo.

"Capital," Corvane said. "Bureau of Inquiry. Ride through the night, change horses at Marrow Bridge, and speak of this to no one, including especially yourself."

Tam grinned, saluted, and was gone, hoofbeats fading down the wet street.

Corvane stood in the doorway a moment, watching the rain come back over the rooftops. Somewhere below, faintly, he could hear Wren's voice in the kitchen, talking to the child in that soft, patient tone he used with frightened animals and difficult accounts. Talking to her as though she could answer.

*Poor boy,* Corvane thought, without malice, which was somehow worse. *You've gone and cared about something. That's how it starts.*

* * *

Dinner that evening was roast fowl and turnips, and Corvane presided over it in the great hall as he always did, at the head of the long table, dispensing warmth by the ladleful. He complimented Cook twice. He asked after Brammel's bad knee. He told the story of the widow and the ghost, embellished in the usual places, and the junior clerks laughed in the usual spots, and the hall glowed with candlelight and belonging, and Corvane basked in it the way he basked in all his creations.

Only two seats were empty. Wren had taken his supper in the kitchen, with the child. Corvane noted this, filed it, forgave it in advance.

After dinner he climbed to his study, poured two fingers of brandy, and opened the private ledger to a fresh page. His pen hovered.

This was the part of the trade nobody saw: not the selling, but the writing. Every entry was a small funeral for some version of himself — the young Aldous Corvane who had joined the guild believing it actually did impossible things, good ones, the kind that couldn't be priced. That young man had died by degrees, one ledger line at a time, each death painless, each reasonable, until nothing remained but a smiling old man with excellent taste in brandy and a talent for being elsewhere when problems arrived.

*Entry,* he wrote. *Child of unknown origin, female, approx. seven years. Found at guild gate during storm. No documentation. Possible old-gift speaker. Reported to Bureau per standing arrangement. Discretion requested.*

He paused, pen dripping.

Then, because he was not entirely dead yet, and because some small stubborn organ in his chest insisted on beating out of time, he added one final line in smaller script:

*Request gentle handling. Cost of request: negotiable.*

He blotted the page, closed the ledger, and locked it in the drawer beneath the window, where the key lived in his breast pocket, next to his heart, or where his heart would have been if he hadn't traded it away in installments.

Outside, the rain thickened. Down in the city, lamps kindled one by one, and beyond the walls the dark roads ran east toward the capital, toward Marrow Bridge, toward a grey-clad rider who would arrive within the week with a warrant and questions.

Corvane finished his brandy, smiled his smile at the empty room — practice, always practice — and went to bed.

He did not hear the rats in the walls that night, running westward in unusual numbers, as though carrying news of their own.
