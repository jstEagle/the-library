## 5. Inquisitor Grey

The rain had followed Sera Grey from the capital like a debt collector, and she was in no mood to be polite to it.

She stood in the courtyard of the Guild of Impossible Things and looked up at the guildhouse — a crooked, many-gabled building that seemed to have been designed by three architects who hated one another and had never met. A weathervane in the shape of a fish pointed stubbornly into the wind. The sign above the door read *Impossible Things, Reasonable Rates*, which was either a joke or a mission statement, and Grey suspected it was both.

"Charming," said Corporal Denny beside her, shaking rain off his warrant-case for the fourth time. He was twenty-three, earnest, and carried his convictions the way other men carried spare coins — loosely, and always jingling.

"Don't be charmed," Grey said. "Charm is how places like this survive."

She had read every file the Office of Inquiry kept on the Guild of Impossible Things, which was not a long file but was an interesting one. A brokerage house for the strange: they found lost heirs, authenticated miracles, negotiated with things that lived under bridges, and once — according to a single uncorroborated line in a marginal note — located a man's shadow after he'd gambled it away in Saltmarket. The guild survived because it was useful to people who mattered, and it stayed small because it was useful to people who shouldn't be noticed.

And somewhere inside it, if her information held, there was a ledger-keeper named Wren Okafor who had been asking animals questions.

Grey touched the warrant in her coat pocket. It was heavy paper, sealed in red wax, signed by the Hand of the Usurper himself. *By order of Veyran, rightful sovereign of the free cities:* hunt the beast-speakers. Root them out. The gift was forbidden twice over now — once by old law, once by new fear.

She did not need the warrant to want this work. She needed it only to justify wanting it.

Her brother Tomas had died eleven years ago with his throat opened by something that wore a wolf's shape and spoke in a dead shepherd's voice. She had been fourteen. The inquiry had called it a wild animal attack and closed the file in nine days, because the truth — that a man had poured his soul into a beast and used it to settle a land grudge — was inconvenient to the magistrate who owned the land. Grey had spent her whole career since then becoming the person who did not close files in nine days.

Beast-speakers had done that to Tomas. Whatever else was true, whatever excuses the soft-hearted made about gifts and natures and misunderstood souls — someone with that gift had worn her brother's last scream like a borrowed coat.

She climbed the steps and knocked.

* * *

The gatekeeper was a woman built like a dock crane, with forearms that suggested a history of settling arguments physically, and a name badge that read *MRS. HOBBS* in letters so large they could probably be read from the road.

"Inquisitor Grey," Grey said, showing her seal. "Office of Inquiry. I have a warrant concerning all persons resident in this guildhouse. I'll need access to your registers, your common rooms, and each member of your staff."

Mrs. Hobbs looked at the seal. Then at Grey. Then at the two soldiers dripping on her steps.

"You'll want towels first," Mrs. Hobbs said. "Can't interrogate people properly when you're leaving puddles. Bad for morale, puddles. Makes everyone think of drowning." She stepped aside and bellowed into the house, "GUESTS! WET ONES!"

Grey had planned for obstruction, evasion, legalistic stonewalling. She had not planned for hospitality as a weapon, and she felt, uneasily, that she had just lost the first exchange of something.

The entrance hall smelled of ink, beeswax, and — faintly, underneath — wet stone and animal. A staircase corkscrewed upward into shadow. Doors led off in directions that seemed geometrically unkind. Somewhere above, a floorboard creaked, and Grey noted it without looking up. Someone was listening. Good. Listeners were useful; listeners could be caught.

A man came down the stairs taking them two at a time and arrived at the bottom already talking. He was broad, cheerful, bald as an egg, and wearing an apron that said *KISS THE COOK, HE KNOWS WHERE THE KNIVES ARE.*

"Inquisitor! Wonderful! You're just in time, I've got a stew on that would make a grown magistrate weep. Bramwell Tuck, cook, quartermaster, and unofficial keeper of the guild's moral compass, such as it is." He shook her hand before she could decide whether to allow it. His grip was warm and floury. "You'll take lunch. Everyone takes lunch. It's policy."

"I'm here on official business, not—"

"Lunch is official business. Ask anyone." He turned and shouted up the stairs again, "WREN! BRING DOWN THE RESIDENCY LEDGER! AND THE GOOD INK, NOT THE CHEAP STUFF, WE'RE BEING INSPECTED!"

There was a pause from above. Then footsteps, unhurried, descending.

Grey arranged herself.

* * *

Wren Okafor came down the stairs carrying a ledger against his chest like a shield he was pretending was a book.

He was younger than she expected — mid-twenties, narrow through the shoulders, with the careful hands of someone who handled valuable things. Dark skin gone ash-pale at the knuckles from years of scrubbing ink. He set the ledger on the hall table, opened it to the correct page without being asked, and said, "Residency register, Inquisitor. Current staff, boarders, and recent visitors. If you tell me what you're looking for, I can save you some reading."

Confident. Helpful. Slightly too helpful — the way a card player is slightly too quick to show you a hand.

"Thank you, Master Okafor." She let the name sit between them and watched his face. Nothing moved in it. Well-trained face. "You keep the ledgers?"

"All of them. Debts, dues, inventories, grudges." A small smile. "We're thorough about grudges. They appreciate being written down. It makes them feel legitimate."

Denny snorted. Grey did not.

"And how long have you been with the guild?"

"Six years. Since I was nineteen."

"Where were you before?"

"A village called Marrow's End. It doesn't exist anymore." He said it evenly, the way you'd report the weather in a place that no longer had weather. "Fen fever took most of it. The rest scattered."

Grey knew about Marrow's End. It appeared in exactly one file in the capital archives, and the file was thin, and thin files about burned villages were the kind of thing Grey collected the way other people collected stamps. Fen fever, the record said. She had read the word *fever* and heard the word *fire* underneath it, the way you sometimes hear a second voice under a singer's.

"There was a fire," she said.

"Fever," Wren said. "Fire's quicker. Fever lets you watch."

Something cold walked down Grey's spine, because that was not a sentence a liar says. Liars say *terrible tragedy, we don't speak of it.* Only someone who had actually watched would say it that plainly, with that flatness, daring her to flinch.

She wrote nothing down, and saw him notice that she wrote nothing down, and understood that they had just begun playing properly.

* * *

Lunch was served in the common room, because Bramwell Tuck regarded interrogation without food as a human rights violation, and Grey discovered that resisting him required more energy than she wished to spend.

The staff assembled at the long table like a jury that had already decided to acquit everyone including themselves. Mrs. Hobbs the gatekeeper. Tuck the cook. A pale young archivist named Pell who kept glancing at Grey's warrant-case as though it might hatch. An elderly mapmaker called Sorrel who was nearly deaf in a way that Grey suspected was selective. And Wren at the far end, pouring tea for people, quiet as furniture.

Grey stood, warrant in hand, and read it aloud. The words filled the room: *beast-speakers, traffickers in the forbidden gift, concealers thereof.* She watched faces the way a jeweler watches stones under lamplight — for flaws, for flashes, for the fracture lines that only appear under pressure.

Pell went white. But Pell went white at everything; Tuck confirmed later that the boy had once fainted at a moderately aggressive goose. Mrs. Hobbs looked bored, which was its own kind of armor. Sorrel asked her to repeat the entire warrant, louder, and then commented on the weather.

And Wren — Wren passed the honey.

That was the moment Grey knew she had her man, or at least her thread. Because when she read the words *the gift of speaking with beasts,* every person at that table had reacted, and Wren's reaction had been to pick up the honey pot and offer it to Sorrel, smoothly, kindly, at precisely the moment a guilty man would most want something to do with his hands.

It was good tradecraft. It was very good tradecraft.

It was also, she thought, the tiniest bit too smooth. Honest men are clumsy. Guilty men who've practiced for years are graceful, and grace leaves a signature, and Grey had spent eleven years learning signatures.

"Master Okafor," she said pleasantly. "You didn't react to the warrant at all."

"I'm reacting right now," he said. "I'm horrified. Look at my face."

Tuck choked on his stew. Mrs. Hobbs said, "Boy," in a warning tone, but there was laughter hiding in it, and Grey felt the room tilt toward Wren the way rooms do around people they love. That was another signature. Innocent men don't usually collect that much affection in one room. Neither do liars. Men who carry secrets carefully, though — men who make themselves quietly indispensable — those men collect rooms full of people willing to lie for them.

She filed that away and pressed on.

* * *

The afternoon was methodical. Grey interviewed each staff member alone in the guild's map room, surrounded by coastlines of places that might not exist. Denny took notes. Outside, the rain kept up its drumming, and Grey found herself grateful for it — rain covered sound, and sound was how she worked best. Not confessions. Sounds. The pause before an answer. The breath drawn through teeth. The creak of a floorboard overhead that had creaked twice already today in the same spot, above the same corridor, where someone was evidently keeping out of sight.

"Who is staying in the east attic?" she asked Mrs. Hobbs, third interview of the day.

"No one."

"The floorboard above the linen corridor disagrees."

"Floorboards disagree with everybody," Mrs. Hobbs said. "They're a contentious lot. My late husband was the same." She folded her enormous arms. "Inquisitor, I'll say this plain, since you seem the type who likes it plain. This house is full of odd folk doing odd work for odd reasons, and half of them would cry at the mistreatment of a spider. If any of them had a forbidden gift, they'd be terrible at hiding it, because they can't even hide their feelings about soup. Now. Are we finished, or shall I put the kettle on again?"

Grey finished. But she wrote in her notebook, in her private shorthand: *Hobbs — loyal past reason. Protects something specific, not general.* General loyalty was institutional. Specific loyalty had a face, and a size, and — she was beginning to suspect — a height of perhaps four feet.

* * *

She found the child's traces at dusk, and she almost missed them.

It was Wren himself who showed her, which she thought about afterward for a long time. She had requested to see the residency ledger's recent entries, and he handed it over open, obliging, and there on the page — in a neat clerk's hand — was an entry dated four days ago:

*One (1) child, sex indeterminate, age approx. 7–9. Found at gate, storm. No name given. No kin claimed. Fed, bathed, bedded. Debt: none. Some things are not debts.*

Below it, smaller, as if the pen had hesitated: *Language unknown. Speaks no tongue on record.*

"Why wasn't I told of this immediately?" Grey asked.

"You didn't ask about children," Wren said. "You asked about residents and visitors. I wasn't sure which the child counted as yet. Still working on it." He paused. "Also, honestly? I forgot you'd want to know. We get strays. Last winter it was a goat with opinions. The winter before, a retired assassin. Nobody writes warrants about goats."

"Where is the child now?"

"Asleep, I hope. Second floor, blue room. Mrs. Hobbs is sitting with—" He stopped.

Grey waited. She was very good at waiting. Waiting was most of the job; the rest was paperwork and grief.

"—with the door open," Wren finished, "because the child doesn't like closed doors."

Everything in that sentence was true except, she judged, the middle of it. Mrs. Hobbs was not sitting with an open door. Mrs. Hobbs was sitting as a wall. And Wren Okafor had just lied to an inquisitor of the Office of Inquiry, badly, sweetly, in front of God and the honey pot — and Grey realized with a small cold shock that he had done it *on purpose*, that the clumsy lie was a gift to her, a bone thrown so she'd chase it and believe she'd won.

Which meant the real thing he was protecting was somewhere else entirely.

She thanked him. She smiled. She even accepted a second helping of stew, because refusing Tuck's stew was apparently a prosecutable offense in this house. And all the while she was thinking: *you lie well, Master Okafor. Better than well. But nobody lies perfectly, and the flaw in you isn't in your words — it's in your kindness. You can't stop feeding people. You can't stop standing between them and the door. And someday, Inquisitor Grey promised the rain and the dark and her brother's memory, someday that kindness will stand exactly where I need it to stand, and it will not move fast enough.*

* * *

Night settled over the guildhouse. Denny slept in the cart. Grey sat by the window of the guest room she'd been given — they'd given her a guest room, the absolute audacity of it, with a quilt and a candle and a sprig of dried lavender on the pillow — and wrote her report by inches.

*Guild of Impossible Things. No evidence of forbidden gift among staff. One unregistered child, origin unknown, language unrecorded. Recommend continued observation.*

She stopped. Read it back. It was true, and it was incomplete, and the gap between those two things was exactly the width of a clerk named Wren Okafor.

She blew out the candle and listened instead. Rain. Wind in the eaves. And beneath it all — faint, intermittent, patient — the creak of a floorboard in the east wing, moving now, softly, back and forth, back and forth, like someone pacing with a child who couldn't sleep.

Or like someone whispering to something that wasn't a child at all.

Grey lay in the dark with her warrant under her pillow and did not sleep, and somewhere below her, in the blue room or near it, Wren Okafor was making the same mistake his whole hidden life had been building toward:

He had let her see where he kept his heart. And Sera Grey had never once, in eleven years, lost track of a thing once she'd seen where it was kept.

Tomorrow she would ask to speak with the child.
