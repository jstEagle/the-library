## 6. The Word Beneath Words

The child slept in the ledger room, curled beneath Wren's own winter cloak on a pallet of folded sacking that smelled of ink and tallow. Wren had told himself it was temporary. Three nights now he had told himself that. The lie was wearing thin as an old coin.

He sat across the room with the oldest ledger in the guild's possession open on his knees — *Foundations & Precedents*, its spine cracked like river ice, its pages soft as moth wings. He had inherited the keeping of it along with every other debt no one wanted to count. Most of it was dull beyond bearing: candle allowances from two hundred years past, a dispute over a smuggled goose, the guildmaster's great-great-predecessor fining himself three coppers for laughing during a funeral. Impossible Things, it turned out, were mostly small.

But there were other pages.

Wren turned to the section near the back where the handwriting changed — older, slanted, pressed hard into the paper by someone writing in bad light with a bad pen and something urgent in their chest. Marginalia, mostly. And in the margins, over and over, in different hands across different decades, the same mark:

A spiral. Drawn once carelessly, once carefully, once so many times it had worn a pale groove through the vellum.

The same spiral the child had scratched into the hearthstone with a fingernail. The same spiral the child traced now, even asleep, one finger moving slowly against the floorboards, patient as water finding its level.

"You keep drawing that," Wren said softly, though he knew the child couldn't hear him yet — not in any way that counted. "I'd like to know why."

The child's answer came anyway, because answers always did at the worst hours: a sound in sleep, low and layered, like wind through a reed bed. Not crying. *Speaking.* Words in that language with no name that Wren's gift heard not as noise but as meaning, the way you feel heat before you understand fire.

*Below. Below-below. The door that is also a mouth. Keep it shut. Keep it fed with remembering.*

Wren set down the ledger very carefully, the way you set down something that might be listening.

* * *

Morning came grey and dripping. Wren left the child with Mirabel — the cook, who had decided the child was hers now with the swift inevitability of all her decisions — and went out into the yard to conduct interviews.

His first informant was a sparrow with a chipped beak who lived in the eaves above the stable and considered herself owed a standing debt of millet for services rendered during the affair of the magpie counterfeiter. Wren paid up, scattering seed while she hopped the length of the fence rail, scolding.

"The little one," he said. "Where did they come from? You birds see everything."

"Roads," said the sparrow, around a mouthful. "Roads and roads and roads. Walking walking walking. Never stopping, never eating enough. Birds don't walk. It was embarrassing to watch."

"From which direction?"

"Away." She fluffed, aggrieved. "Away-ward. From the dead place. We don't fly over the dead place. Nothing to eat, nothing to nest, air tastes wrong. Even the crows won't, and crows would attend their own funerals for the pickings."

The Ashfen. Wren felt the word settle in his stomach like a swallowed stone. The Ashfen, where nothing lived, where the old maps simply stopped drawing and wrote *here was war* instead.

"And did anything follow?"

The sparrow stopped eating. That alone frightened him more than her answer.

"Things," she said. "Slow things. Heavy things. They walked behind the little one for nine days and then they stopped at the river and stood in the water and the water went around them and they are still standing there. I saw them from high up. They look like men if men were made of waiting."

Wren thanked her, gave her double millet, and stood in the yard watching gulls argue over the midden heap until his hands stopped shaking.

* * *

That evening he tried again with the child, and this time he brought tools: bread and honey, a stub of charcoal, and the old ledger open to the spiral pages.

The child ate with the desperate politeness of someone who has learned meals can be taken away, then watched Wren's face with those grave dark eyes that seemed to hold about forty years more life than the body carrying them. Wren dipped the charcoal and drew the spiral on the tabletop.

The child went still.

Then small hands covered his, guiding his wrist, correcting the spiral — inward, not outward. Not a mark radiating out but one winding *down*. A descent. A door seen from above.

"Yes," Wren breathed. "Yes, that's what I thought. Show me more."

What followed was the strangest conversation of his life, and Wren had once spent an entire afternoon negotiating a rent dispute between a harbor seal and a fishmonger who both claimed the same stretch of dock.

The child could not speak common speech. But the child could draw, and the child's language — spoken in fragments, half-dreamed, offered shyly like gifts that might be refused — came to Wren's gift translated into shapes of meaning rather than words. He learned to stop listening with his ears. He learned to listen the way he listened to rats: with the part of him he kept locked in a box under his ribs.

Piece by piece, over honey-bread and charcoal dust, the story assembled itself.

A power beneath the earth. Old — old the way stone is old, the way grief is old. It had woken once, long ago, in the years the free cities' histories called *the first war* and described, Wren now understood, entirely wrongly. The histories spoke of kings and armies. The child's language spoke of a wound in the world that armies had merely walked around.

It had been sealed. Not killed — sealed. Bound beneath the ground on which the free cities were later built, because the wardens who bound it understood something the histories never recorded: that the safest place for a sleeping power is directly beneath the feet of everyone who might be foolish enough to dig for it, where ten thousand hearthfires and market-day quarrels and children's laughter would press down on it like a warm blanket, teaching it, year by year, century by century, what ordinary life sounds like.

"It's not chained down there," Wren said slowly, the realization arriving like cold water. "It's *tucked in*. Like a feverish child."

The child made a sound that might have been agreement, or laughter, or both.

And the wardens — the speakers. Wren's hands went cold around the charcoal. The binding needed voices. Voices that could speak to what slept below, sing it back toward dreams when it stirred, remind it of the warmth above. Speakers of beasts and birds and growing things, generation after generation, each one taught in secret, each one hidden, because the gift that could soothe a buried god could also wake one, and there had always been men like Veyran who preferred waking.

*Like your village,* the child's language said, unbidden, and showed him without pictures: fire, running, a mother's hand pushing a small body into the reeds, a last word pressed into a small mind like a seal pressed into wax. *Carry it. You carry it now. Run until you find the ones who still remember.*

The ones who still remember. Wardens. Speakers. People like Wren, scattered and hiding, most of them dead or burned or quiet as stones — and the child had walked out of the Ashfen following nothing but that pressed-wax word, and of all the doors in all the free cities, had knocked on the one belonging to a man who spent his nights trading gossip with sparrows.

Wren sat back. His eyes stung.

"I'm not a warden," he said. "I'm a bookkeeper. I hide. Hiding is my whole profession. I balance accounts for people who traffic in miracles and I've never once balanced anything bigger than a goose dispute."

The child looked at him with terrible patience, took the charcoal, and drew one more thing on the table before yawning and surrendering to exhaustion: a small figure, stick-thin, standing beside a spiral. Then a second figure beside the first. Two marks. Together.

Then the child pointed at the first figure, then at Wren.

Wren did not sleep much that night.

* * *

Near dawn, unable to bear the ceiling of his room, he climbed to the guildhouse roof with a blanket and the old ledger, and sat among the chimney pots where the city spread out below him grey and gold in the early light — rooftops and smoke and the market square already rumbling awake, carts and cries and somewhere a dog with strong opinions about a cat.

All of it, he thought, sitting on top of the Hollow. Every street. Every cellar. The whole bright, bickering, impossible sprawl of the free cities, built over a sleeping thing and never knowing, kept safe by speakers who had been burned out of village after village until almost none remained.

Almost none.

He heard footsteps on the roof ladder and didn't startle; he knew the tread. Mirabel hauled herself up through the hatch with the groan of a woman who regretted several of her life choices but not this one, and settled beside him with two cups of chicory.

"The little one's drawing on my good tablecloth," she said. "Spirals. I let them, mind. But I want it known I noticed."

"Noticed what?"

"That you haven't slept. That you've got the old ledger up here. That you look like a man who's read something he wishes he hadn't." She blew on her cup. "I'm a cook, Wren Okafor. I notice things. It's how I know when the fish is off and when my boarders are lying."

"Is the fish off?"

"Don't change the subject." She sipped. "Corvane asked me yesterday about the child. Casual, like. Asked where they slept, whether they'd spoken, whether anyone had come asking." She paused. "Casual questions from Corvane are never casual. I've cooked for that man eleven years. He asks casually exactly when he's being careful."

Wren's blood went slow and heavy. "What did you tell him?"

"What you'd expect. That the child's mute and simple and sleeps in the kitchen like a stray cat." She shrugged. "Might even be true someday. Might buy time." She looked at him sideways. "Whatever you've found in that book, whatever the little one's been saying to you in whatever tongue it is — I don't want to know it. Understand? Knowing things is dangerous. But I'll tell you this for free: Corvane sent a pigeon out yesterday evening. Fast bird, capital-banded. And Guildmaster Corvane does not send pigeons to the capital about geese."

She drained her cup, heaved herself up, and paused at the hatch.

"Whatever's coming," she said, "the child eats breakfast at my table. That part isn't negotiable."

Then she was gone, and Wren sat alone among the chimneys with the ledger in his lap and the whole weight of a buried age pressing gently up through the soles of his boots.

Far below — deeper than cellars, deeper than sewers, deeper than the bones of the first war — something slept, and dreamed, and stirred in its dreaming. And somewhere in the capital, a pigeon was landing on a roost, carrying a message whose shape Wren could already guess: *a speaker lives. A warden's heir walks. Come quickly.*

He looked down at the spiral drawn in charcoal on the last page, winding inward, downward, patient as water finding its level.

"We're going to need help," he told the city below him, quietly. "And I have the distinct feeling we're going to need it soon."
