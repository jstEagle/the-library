## 7. Ashes of the Fen

*The child could not speak a word of common tongue when they came to us. But words are not the only way a story arrives. Some nights, sitting cross-legged on my cot with a stub of candle between us, I understood them without understanding — and what follows is what I heard, set down as best I could in a language the child never learned. Forgive the seams. Grief does not translate cleanly.*

— W. Okafor, marginal note, Guild Ledger 114

* * *

Before the machines came, Ashfen smelled of rain on warm stone.

The child remembered this first, always this first: the smell after a storm, when the whole village seemed to exhale at once. The fen-water steamed. The reeds shook themselves like dogs. And Grandmother Sefa would stand in the doorway of the warden-house with her arms folded and say, in the old tongue that had no word for hurry, *"Now the world is washed. Now we begin again."*

They began again every morning, really. That was the whole of the wardens' work, though no one outside the fen would have believed it. The child's people did not guard the Hollow with swords or walls. They guarded it with attention.

Every dawn, the wardens walked the nine circles. Every dawn, they spoke to the ground.

Not *to* the ground the way farmers do, coaxing and complaining. They spoke *with* it, in the old tongue, which was not made by people at all. The old tongue was older than people. It was the language the world had used before anything walked on it, and a few bloodlines still carried it the way rivers carry silt — quietly, invisibly, until you looked closely at what grew along their banks.

The child was one of those banks.

They had known since they were very small. There was no ceremony. One morning, four years old, mud to the elbows, the child had told a beetle — in the beetle's own idiom, which was mostly patience — that the path under the woodpile was blocked, and the beetle had thanked them, and gone around. The child had run to Grandmother Sefa with the news, and she had laughed until she had to sit down, and then she had cried a little, which confused the child terribly, because it was good news.

"You'll understand when you're older," she said, wiping her eyes. "A gift is only good news until someone wants to take it from you."

The child thought about that sentence for eleven years. They were still thinking about it when the machines came.

* * *

But before the machines — before all of that — there were the ordinary days, and the child hoarded those the way other children hoarded river-glass.

There was Uncle Brannic, who kept the fen-bees and talked to them so much that they'd begun to answer in a sort of buzzing sigh, and who swore the bees gossiped about him. ("They know about the honey I steal," he confided to the child once, gravely. "They forgive me. But they *know*.") There was Cousin Ilo, who could speak with eels and considered it a deeply annoying gift, because eels were, in his professional opinion, terrible conversationalists. "All gripes," he said. "No gratitude. You ask an eel how its day went, you get a *list*."

And there was the singing.

Every evening at dusk, the whole village gathered at the ninth circle — the innermost one, where the ground hummed faintly if you pressed your palm flat against it — and they sang the wardens' song. It had no beginning anyone remembered and no ending anyone feared. Each generation added a verse. The verses were small things: a birth, a good harvest, a joke someone had made that deserved preserving. The song was how the village remembered itself, and how the thing below remembered being loved.

Because that was the secret at the bottom of everything, the one the free cities had forgotten, the one written nowhere: the Hollow was not a weapon.

The Hollow was a wound that had learned to sleep.

Long ago — longer than kingdoms, longer than the word *kingdom* — something vast had been hurt here, hurt so badly the earth itself had scarred over it, and the first wardens had done the only merciful thing anyone had ever done for it. They had stayed. They had sung to it. They had promised it, night after night, generation after generation: *You are not alone down there. You are not forgotten. Sleep, wounded thing. We will remember for you.*

And it slept. Mostly. Sometimes, on bad nights, the humming under the ninth circle turned restless, and the wardens sang louder, and Grandmother Sefa would say, "It dreamed of the war again. Sing the harvest verse. It likes the harvest verse."

The child liked the harvest verse too. Everyone did. It was the funny one.

* * *

The strangers came in the spring of the child's twelfth year, and they came politely, which should have been the first warning.

Politeness, Grandmother Sefa said later, was how power introduced itself when it intended to stay. A raider roars. An empire knocks.

They arrived in three long barges flying no banner at all, which meant they flew the banner of whoever paid them. Their leader was a soft-spoken man with ink-stained fingers and a surveyor's smile, and he spread maps across the warden-house table and explained, with great courtesy, that the Crown — *the new* Crown, he said, with a small hesitation around the word *new* — wished to conduct certain surveys in the fen.

"Surveys," said Grandmother Sefa.

"Soundings," the man agreed. "Deep ones. There are... geological interests."

"There are," said Grandmother Sefa, "nine circles of warding older than any crown, and beneath them a sleeping thing your maps cannot weigh, and if your soundings wake it, no geological interest will save you."

The man smiled his surveyor's smile and rolled up his maps. "We'll begin at the outer fen," he said. "Nothing intrusive. You have my word."

He gave his word the way men give coins to beggars: without expecting to see it again.

The child stood in the corner during all of this, silent as children are expected to be, and listened — not to the man's words, which were smooth as river stones, but to the barges tied at the dock. Barges creak. Ropes groan. And aboard those barges, in cages under tarpaulin, something was whimpering.

The child slipped out and looked.

Fen-creatures. Marsh-wrens, a mud-lion cub, a family of otters, all drugged and cramped and catalogued, little brass tags wired to their ears. Specimens. The surveyors were collecting the fen's speakers — because of course they were. If you wanted to find the Hollow without waking it, you needed creatures that could hear it. And if you found a creature that could hear it, you followed the creature home to whatever two-legged thing had been talking to it.

The child freed the mud-lion cub that night. It was the only cage the child could open before the watch turned, and the cub fled into the reeds, and the child ran home through the dark with their heart hammering, and told Grandmother Sefa everything.

She listened without interrupting, which was her way. Then she lit the warning lamp in the warden-house window — the one that hadn't been lit in three generations — and sent riders to the other fens, and began teaching the child the deep verses of the song. The ones for emergencies. The ones no one had needed since the first war.

"Why me?" the child asked. "I'm not finished learning."

"No one is finished learning," Grandmother Sefa said. "That's why we sing together. Go to sleep now. Tomorrow we add a verse."

They never got to write it down. The child has been carrying it ever since, unwritten, unfinished, a verse with no words yet — just the shape of it, waiting.

* * *

The machines came at moonset, forty-one days later.

The child would count the days afterward, obsessively, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. Forty-one days of polite surveyors and polite soundings, and then the barges brought things that were not polite at all: great iron excavators on caterpillar tracks, dredging engines, drilling rigs tall as watchtowers, and soldiers in grey-and-crimson livery — Veyran's colors, though the child didn't know the name yet. Names came later. First came the noise.

The noise was the worst part. Worse than the fire, even. The machines screamed into the earth day and night, and the screaming went down through the nine circles like knives through cloth, and the child could feel it in their teeth, in their bones, in the place behind the sternum where the old tongue lived. Every drill-bit was a question shouted into a sleeper's ear. Every dredge was a hand shaking a wounded thing awake.

The village sang back. That was the plan, the only plan: the full warden-song, all nine circles, all hundred and six voices, day and night in shifts, pouring the old tongue into the ground like water onto a fire. Grandmother Sefa led the first shift herself, standing on the ninth circle with her arms raised, her voice — a voice that had sung the Hollow to sleep for sixty years — cracking against the engine-roar and holding anyway.

For six days, it held.

On the seventh day, the soldiers stopped pretending to be surveyors.

The child was on the eastern circle when they came, mid-verse, Ilo's off-key harmony in one ear and the bees' alarmed drone overhead. The soldiers moved through the reeds in a line, quiet as the machines were loud, and the first the singers knew of it was a horn-note from the dock, and then it was too late for anything but the song.

They did not stop singing. That must be said. Whatever else the child remembers — and they remember everything, that was the curse of it, they remembered every face, every voice, every verse cut off mid-word — they remember that no one stopped singing. Not when the first arrows came. Not when the warden-house caught. Uncle Brannic sang to the end with bees boiling out of his hives to die stinging around him. Cousin Ilo sang while the eels rose from the fen-water in hundreds, thousands, flinging themselves at the soldiers' boots in a frenzy of grief, and Ilo was laughing and crying and singing all at once, saying, "Good eels! Good eels! Best eels!" — and the child has never decided whether that memory is the most terrible or the most beautiful thing they carry, and suspects it is both.

Grandmother Sefa died on the ninth circle, standing up, mid-verse.

The child knew the exact moment, because the song faltered — one heartbeat, one gap in the harmony — and from far below, from deeper than drills had reached, something answered the gap. A sound. Not a roar. A *grief*, rising through forty feet of fen-mud, so vast and so wounded that half the soldiers dropped their weapons and covered their ears, and one of them wept openly and would not say why.

The Hollow was waking. Not raging. Mourning. It had felt Sefa's voice go out the way you feel a hand let go of yours in the dark.

And the child — twelve years old, ash in their hair, standing in a circle of dying singers — did the only thing left. They knelt on the scorched earth of the ninth circle, pressed both palms flat, and sang the emergency verses alone. All of them. In a voice too small for the work, cracked and sobbing and stubborn, they sang the deep song, the promise-song, the oldest words in the world:

*You are not alone down there. You are not forgotten. Sleep, wounded thing. I will remember for you.*

And the grief below paused. Listened. And slowly, slowly, like a storm deciding to become rain, it settled.

The child sang until dawn. When they finally lifted their hands from the ground, the village was smoke and the soldiers were gone — called away, the child learned later, because the excavation had been declared a failure, the site "unstable," the wardens "an obstacle removed." A ledger entry. That was all the child's people were, in the end. A line in someone's ledger.

The child knew about ledgers even then. The old tongue had a word for them. It was not a kind word.

* * *

Grandmother Sefa had prepared for this, the way she seemed to prepare for everything. In the ruined cellar of the warden-house, under a floor-stone shaped like a turtle, the child found the bundle she had left: dried food, a knife, a map of the smugglers' ways east toward the free cities, and a strip of birchbark with a message burned into it in the old script.

The message said: *The language lives in your blood. Where you go, it goes. Keep it moving. A song they cannot catch cannot be killed.*

And beneath it, smaller, in her handwriting, the closest she ever came to a joke: *Also, do not argue with geese. You will lose.*

The child almost smiled. Almost. Then they climbed out of the cellar into the grey morning, and looked back once at the smoking fen, and did not look back again, because Grandmother Sefa had also taught them this: *Grief is fuel, little bank. Burn it forward, not backward.*

So they burned forward. Through the marshes, past the drowned road, along the smugglers' ways, sleeping in culverts, trading small kindnesses with whatever creatures would listen — and many would, once they heard the old tongue; word travels fast among the small and hunted that someone has come who speaks the world's first language. A heron carried them a river-crossing on its back, which was cold and undignified and wonderful. A fox walked three nights beside them for no reason except company. A rat in a granary town pressed a stolen crust into their hands with the solemnity of a coronation.

Twenty-nine days. Nine cities' worth of roads. And everywhere, more signs of the grey-and-crimson: muster-fields, requisition carts, proclamations nailed to doors, the new Crown's face on coins and banners, smiling its surveyor's smile. The machines were coming here too. The child could hear it already, faint under the world, a distant drilling at the edge of sleep. Veyran had tasted the fen and found it wanting, and he was looking elsewhere for his door into the deep.

Which meant the child had to get there first. Had to find whoever was left who could hear — the scattered bloodlines, the hidden banks of the old river — and warn them, and gather them, and finish the verse that had no words.

On the twenty-ninth evening, rain hammering the cobbles, the child stood at last before a building unlike any in the fen: crooked, crowded, glowing, with a sign that read *Guild of Impossible Things* — which, the child thought, exhausted and soaked and grieving, was either exactly right or exactly wrong, and they were too tired to work out which.

They knocked.

Inside, footsteps. A latch. A door opening onto candlelight and the smell of ink and bread, and a young man with tired eyes and a ledger under his arm looking down at them — and the child opened their mouth and spoke the only words they had left, the old tongue, the world's first words, the words that were a key:

*"Something buried is stirring. I am the last bank of the river. Help me hold the door."*

And the young man — this the child would understand later, would treasure later — went pale, and gripped the doorframe, and whispered back, in accented but unmistakable old tongue:

*"Come in out of the rain. We need to talk."*

* * *

*That is the story as the child tells it, in fragments, over many nights, usually with their hands as much as their voice. I have smoothed nothing that matters and invented nothing that doesn't. I checked the dates against the guild's shipping records — the Ashfen "survey" contracts were routed through our own house, under Corvane's seal, three years running. I sat with that knowledge for a long time last night. The rats tell me the guildmaster has been awake, walking the halls, and that he stopped outside my door twice without knocking.*

*I think I am about to learn what my silence costs.*

— W.O., Ledger 114, final annotation
