## 9. The Free Cities at Dusk

The road to Vell had become a river, and rivers do not apologize for what they carry.

Wren stood at the crest of the hill with the child on his shoulders — Pip, he'd taken to calling her in his head, though she had not yet given him a name of her own choosing — and watched the flood of them come: carts stacked with hens and grandmothers and the good silver nobody would ever use again, oxen with their heads down, children trotting beside wagons holding ropes because there was no room left on the boards. Behind them all, far to the east, the sky over the Ashfen glowed a dull orange that was not sunset. It did not go out at night anymore.

"They're running from it," Wren murmured, mostly to himself.

Pip's small hands tightened in his hair. She said something in the old tongue, three syllables, soft as river stones. He still understood only fragments, but he was learning, the way you learn a song by hearing it wept rather than sung. *Farther,* she might have said. Or *more.* Or perhaps simply *yes* — yes, they are running from it, and so are we, little warden, and I have not yet decided if that makes us wise or merely early.

A woman pushing a handcart paused beside them at the top of the hill. She looked at Wren's mud-stained coat, at the child, at the road, and made the quick arithmetic of refugees everywhere: *another mouth, another stranger, another grief.*

"Vell's closed its gates," she said, not unkindly. "Third day now. They say the council can't decide who to let in."

"Then where is everyone going?"

She shrugged, and the shrug contained an entire philosophy of survival. "Wherever the gates open last." She moved on, cart wheels squeaking a complaint older than either of them.

Wren adjusted Pip's weight and started down into the crowd. The rats had told him about the roads days ago — *long lines of feet, warm and frightened, crumbs everywhere but no one dropping any, even the mice are hungry* — but hearing it from a rat and seeing it were different things. Seeing it meant understanding that the free cities were not one thing that could fall. They were seven things, seven proud, quarrelsome things, and every one of them believed the danger would knock on someone else's door first.

He kept his hood up. A man who spoke with beasts could vanish among ten thousand strangers; a man seen speaking with beasts could not. And there were ears on these roads now. Grey's agents worked the refugee columns like fisherwomen working a net, patient and thorough, asking questions in gentle voices. Twice already Wren had felt the particular prickle of being watched by someone pretending not to.

Pip tugged his ear and pointed down the slope, toward where the road bent around a shrine of some forgotten god — the god of thresholds, perhaps, or lost keys; the statue's face had been worn smooth by weather and indifference. A dog lay there, ribs like a washboard, watching the human river pass with the weary judgment only dogs can manage.

"What's it saying?" Pip asked — in Common, her newest word-shape, rough as a foal standing up for the first time. She had begun demanding translations of everything, and Wren had discovered that teaching her Common while she taught him the old tongue was like two people building a bridge from opposite banks, meeting in the middle over increasingly complicated sentences.

"He says the humans are very loud," Wren translated, "and that the ones carrying chickens owe him an apology."

Pip laughed — a real laugh, bright and startling, the first one since the Ashfen — and something in Wren's chest that had been clenched for weeks loosened half a turn. Then he saw the poster nailed to the shrine's post, and it clenched again.

It was a fine engraving, better paper than most refugees would ever touch. It showed a child, age and sex conveniently vague, and beneath it, in letters meant to be read aloud by town criers:

**BY ORDER OF THE USURPER'S JUSTICE — REWARD FOR THE RECOVERY OF THE ASHFEN HEIR. ALIVE. INFORMATION CONCERNING BEAST-SPEAKERS TREATED WITH EQUAL FAVOR.**

*Alive*, this time. That was new. That was worse. Dead children made martyrs; living children made witnesses.

Wren walked past without breaking stride, the way you walk past anything dangerous, and peeled the notice off the next post they came to, crumpling it into his pocket where it couldn't frighten anyone else.

* * *

Fifty miles east, beyond the Greytooth Pass, Inquisitor Sera Grey stood on a ridge and counted campfires.

She had stopped counting at four hundred. She started again out of professional discipline and got the same answer, which told her what she needed to know: the army below was not a raiding force, not a punitive column, not any of the tidy instruments the warrants in her satchel had been written for. It was a nation sharpened into a point, and the point aimed west, at the free cities, at everything her oaths theoretically protected.

Her aide, a young man named Pell whose enthusiasm had survived three months of her company through sheer incomprehension, shifted beside her. "Shall I report the numbers to the capital, Inquisitor?"

"The capital wrote the numbers," Grey said. "They ordered the columns. They know how many fires their own army lights."

Below, the excavator-machines sat in their transport cradles like enormous iron beetles, tarped against the mountain wind. She had seen one uncovered at the Ashfen dig site, before. She had told herself then what the reports told her: mining engines, salvage operations, legitimate recovery of strategic resources. She had been telling herself things for a long time. Lately the things were getting harder to lift.

"Inquisitor?" Pell again.

"Report the numbers," she said. "Omit the excavators."

Pell blinked. "But they're part of the order of battle—"

"*Omit the excavators.*" She heard her own voice do the thing it did, the flat cold thing that made grown men confess to crimes they hadn't committed, and she watched Pell decide, wisely, not to ask why. He went down the ridge to the dispatch riders, and Sera Grey stayed alone with the fires and the wind and the growing, unspeakable suspicion that she had spent twenty years serving a law that had quietly changed masters while she wasn't looking.

In her saddlebag, wrapped in oilcloth where a warrant should have been, was a copy of a requisition ledger she had stolen from Corvane's guildhouse — dates, payments, signatures, the Guild of Impossible Things selling passage and silence to Veyran's men for years. She had not filed it. She had not destroyed it either. It sat there like a stone in her boot, and every mile it hurt more.

Somewhere ahead of her, in that river of refugees, was a child and very likely the beast-speaker who had lied to her with almost perfect skill. Almost. She remembered the exact moment his lie had slipped — a half-second pause when she mentioned the Ashfen, a flicker of his eyes toward the stairs — and she had let him go anyway, telling herself she wanted to follow the trail, knowing even then that she had simply chosen not to take him.

She was beginning to suspect that choice had been the first honest act of her career.

* * *

In Vell, in the marble council chamber that smelled of beeswax and fear, the Council of Seven Voices argued about surrender for the eleventh hour running.

Speaker Aldous Marrow, oldest member of the council and owner of the finest collection of surrender proposals in the free cities, tapped the table with a ring worth more than the district he came from. "Veyran asks only tribute and acknowledgment. Acknowledgment costs nothing. We acknowledge him, pay the tithe, and in five years—"

"In five years we pay a larger tithe," cut in Speaker Yeva Thorn, who represented the dock districts and had the voice of a woman who had personally thrown smugglers off piers. "And in ten years we discover that acknowledgment was the first installment of ownership. I've read that contract before. My grandmother read it too, in the first war. It ended with our harbor full of iron bells."

"A legend," Marrow sniffed.

"A ledger," Yeva said. "Which your kind respects more than legends."

The seventh chair, reserved for the Voice of the Guilds, sat empty. Its occupant, Guildmaster Corvane's appointed proxy, had pleaded illness — which everyone present correctly translated as *the guild is currently negotiating with both sides and cannot be seen choosing*. The empty chair bothered Speaker Yeva more than the army beyond the passes. Armies you could fight. An empty chair sat there agreeing with everyone.

Outside the chamber windows, the city glowed with evening lamps, and beyond the walls the refugee camps spread like lichen, thousands of hearth-fires of people who had run from the east only to arrive at a city deciding whether to bar its own doors. A petition had come up from the camps that afternoon, written in six languages on the back of a sailcloth: *Let us in. We will work. We will remember.*

Marrow had called it presumptuous. Yeva had read it aloud twice, slowly, letting each language land.

"We should vote," Marrow said, for the eleventh time.

"We voted eleven times."

"And arrived at eleven ties."

"Then we shall arrive at a twelfth," Yeva said, "and I will keep voting until one of us dies of old age, at which point I will vote twice."

It might have been funny, in another season. Nobody laughed. The vote came: five to two for sending envoys to discuss terms. Not surrender — the word *surrender* was banned by council custom, replaced with the phrase *pragmatic accommodation*, which everyone understood perfectly and no one said aloud. Two voices against. Yeva Thorn's was one of them.

That night, walking home through streets where shopkeepers were quietly pricing their goods in both currencies, Yeva passed the old bell tower at Harbor's Edge — the tower that held the iron bells of the first war, recast from the weapons of the defeated enemy, rung once a year in remembrance. She stood beneath it a long while.

The bells hung silent. But the wind moved through the tower, and the great bells stirred on their beams without ringing, a low groan of metal like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

Yeva Thorn, who did not believe in omens, went home and wrote three letters she did not send.

* * *

They slept that night in a hay barn outside Vell's walls, in the refugee sprawl, among forty other families. Wren lay awake listening to the barn talk — the horses grumbling about the quality of the hay, the mice conducting their eternal audit of the grain stores, an ancient mouser cat named Bracken delivering a running commentary on human foolishness that would have gotten him exiled from any respectable household.

"You hear all that?" Wren asked Pip quietly.

"Some." She frowned, concentrating. "The cat is... angry?"

"The cat is *opinionated*. There's a difference. Angry means something happened. Opinionated means something hasn't happened yet and he intends to complain about it in advance."

Pip considered this with the gravity of a scholar. "You are opinionated."

"I am wounded," Wren said, "and also correct."

Around them the barn settled, breath by breath, into sleep. Somewhere a baby cried itself quiet. Somewhere a man muttered numbers in his dreams — debts, probably, or distances, the two things refugees count. And under it all, faint as a pulse through floorboards, Wren felt the thing he had been feeling for three days now and had not said aloud, because saying things aloud gives them weight and this weight was already enough.

The ground here hummed.

Not the ordinary hum of earth and water. Something deeper, slower, patient — a vast attention turned inward, like a sleeper listening to its own heart. The Hollow. It ran beneath the free cities like a root system beneath a garden, and the child sleeping against his side was its heir, and somewhere east an army carried machines built to dig down to it, and the councils of the free cities were debating whether to hold the door open politely.

"Pip." He touched her shoulder. She woke instantly, completely, the way wardens' children presumably learned to. "Do you feel it? Under us. The humming."

She pressed her palm flat to the packed earth. Her eyes went wide, then soft, then wet. When she answered, she used the old tongue, and for once she didn't translate down to Common, because there was nothing in Common for it. But she tried anyway, for him, word by careful word:

"It knows... we came back. It has been... alone a long..." Her voice caught. "Long time. It thought everyone forgot."

Wren lay back and stared up at the dark rafters, where swallows nested in the eaves, and listened to the swallows' small sleeping sounds, and thought about ledgers. He had spent years keeping accounts for impossible things — favors, debts, secrets, the invisible economy that actually held the free cities together while the councils argued about visible ones. Every entry balanced. Every debt found its debtor eventually.

And the account coming due now was not his, and not the child's, and not even Veyran's, really. It belonged to everyone who had ever promised the Hollow it would be remembered, and then gone home and forgotten. Seven cities built on top of a sealed wound, spending the interest on a debt none of them knew they owed.

The stakes had changed shape. He had fled the guildhouse thinking he was saving one child from one inquisitor, and that had been true, and it had been small. Now the roads were rivers, the passes were burning, the councils were counting votes instead of courage, and the thing beneath their feet was waking up lonely and angry and impossibly strong, and a usurper meant to put his hand on it first.

One child. One gift. One very tired clerk with a talent for talking to rats.

Against all of that.

Wren laughed quietly, so as not to wake anyone, and Bracken the cat opened one eye across the barn and gave him a look of profound feline agreement, as if to say: *Yes. Ridiculous. Do it anyway.*

"I don't suppose," Wren whispered to the rafters, to the humming earth, to nobody, "you know any thieves in this city who owe me money?"

The earth did not answer. But the next morning, at the edge of the refugee camp, a rat with a torn ear sat up on its hind legs in front of him, waited until he crouched, and said, in the matter-of-fact tone of a creature reporting a delivery:

"There's a fence in the Saltmarket who owes you for the pigeon business. She wants to know if you're finally calling it in."

Wren looked at the rat for a long moment. Then at Pip, who was watching with her head tilted, catching maybe one word in three and understanding all of it.

"Yes," he said, standing, brushing straw from his coat, feeling the strange lightness of a man stepping off a ledge. "Tell her the ledger's open."

Behind them, eastward, the sky over the passes glowed, and did not go out.
