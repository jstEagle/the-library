## 10. Thieves Who Owe Him

The fence kept a shop that sold nothing, which was the safest kind of shop to keep.

Wren found him where he always was: behind a counter stacked with candlesticks that had never held candles, rings sized for fingers no one had, and a small brass telescope through which one could see, if one squinted, absolutely nothing at all. The sign above the door read HALLORAN & SONS — FINE GOODS. There was no Halloran. There were no sons. There was only Odile Halloran, seventy-one years old, sharp as a paper cut, and currently pretending to be asleep in her chair so she could watch how Wren entered her shop.

"You snore," Wren said.

"I was meditating." She opened one eye. "You look like something the tide refused."

"Thank you. I've been sleeping in a cellar with a child who talks in spirals and a rat who thinks he's my landlord."

"That tracks." She rose, joints crackling like green firewood, and poured two cups of tea without asking. It was the good tea, the smuggled tea, which meant she was either pleased to see him or preparing to soften bad news. With Odile it was usually both. "Sit down before you fall down. And put your hood back — there's a man across the street who counts foot traffic for the inquisitors. Pays him three coppers a day. I pay him four to count wrong."

Wren sat. The child — Mirabel, though Wren had taken to thinking of her as *the little warden* when he wanted to frighten himself — sat too, solemn as a small judge, and drank her tea with both hands wrapped around the cup. She had learned six words of common speech in nine days. *Yes. No. Hungry. Safe.* And two more she'd invented: *listen* and *under*, used together to mean something Wren didn't yet have the heart to translate fully.

"Odile," he said. "I need help. The kind that can't be bought."

"Good thing you came to me, then. Everything here can be bought. That's the point of everything here." She sipped. "What kind?"

"The kind that's owed."

She set down her cup. For a long moment the only sound was the rain on the shutters and Mirabel carefully turning her cup so the chipped side faced away from them both, because even children fresh out of catastrophe understand manners.

"Ah," said Odile. "That kind."

* * *

Here is what the ledgers knew, and what Wren knew better than anyone alive, because he had written them:

In the year the old guildmaster died, a shipment of fever-medicine bound for the river districts was seized at the Salt Gate by customs men working for a magistrate who liked his bribes rare and his cruelty routine. The medicine sat in a warehouse rotting while children coughed themselves into graves. The Guild of Impossible Things did not do charity — the guild did *improbable arrangements*. So a junior ledger-keeper named Wren Okafor discovered, through three weeks of patient bookkeeping, that the magistrate had been double-billing the crown for guards he'd never hired, and that this fraud was recorded, in the magistrate's own hand, in a ledger the magistrate believed had been burned.

It had not been burned. It had been sold to a scrap dealer, who sold it to a fence, who kept it in a drawer under the nothing-telescope.

Wren did not blackmail the magistrate. Blackmail was crude and left stains. Instead he *visited*, laid the ledger open on the man's desk, turned the pages slowly, and asked whether the magistrate felt the medicine might find its way to the river districts after all, purely as an administrative correction. The medicine moved within the week. Forty children lived who would not have. The magistrate retired to the coast, where he took up fishing and, reportedly, honesty.

Odile Halloran had supplied the ledger. She had never once mentioned it since. But debts like that don't sleep; they wait, patient as seeds.

"There was also the matter," Odile said, staring into her tea, "of the year I nearly hanged."

"The Holloway affair."

"Don't say that word in my shop." She rubbed her eyes. "I moved a cargo for a client. The client was a liar. The cargo was not candlesticks. The constables found my name in a book that should never have contained it, and the only reason my neck is still the width it has always been is that a certain ledger-keeper noticed the constable's report had been *filed twice* — filed twice, mind you, which means someone wanted it found twice — and quietly arranged for the second copy to be misplaced into the archive of a parish that flooded every spring."

"You misfiled a document into a flood zone," Odile said, "and let the water do the perjury."

"It seemed tidy at the time."

"It was the single most elegant crime I have ever witnessed," said Odile, "and you committed it with a rubber stamp." She looked up at him then, and beneath all the rust and irony there was something older and softer, the thing she hid the way he hid his gift. "You've come to collect."

"I've come to ask."

"Worse. Asking is worse." She stood and began pulling shutters closed against the grey afternoon, and her voice dropped into the register she used only for true things. "There are soldiers in the city who weren't here last month. Real ones, not garrison boys. Veyran's iron bells on their harnesses. They're asking after a child. They're asking after *you*, Wren Okafor, and they're doing it politely, which is how you know they're serious." She latched the last shutter. "So. What do you need?"

"A circle," Wren said. "Small. Loyal. People who owe the ledger or love it. People who can hide us, move us, and lie for us. And eventually—" he paused, because saying it made it real— "eventually, people who can get us into the surrendered city."

Mirabel looked up at the word *surrendered*. She set down her cup and drew, on the table's dusty surface, a spiral. Then she pointed down. *Under.*

"Yes," Wren told her gently. "We know. We're going."

Odile watched the child draw the spiral, and Wren saw the fence — the woman who had spent fifty years pricing everything in the free cities — decide that some things were not for sale. "The knight first," she said. "He owes you the biggest, and he's the only one of your thieves who can fight."

"He'll be furious."

"Oh, he's always furious. It's how he prays."

* * *

Ser Aldric Vane had been a knight of the Order of the Broken Lance for eleven years, and had been neither a knight nor of any order for six, ever since the day he'd told a superior officer the truth about a massacre at a place called Cinder Ford and been stripped of his spurs for insubordination. He now worked as a hired sword guarding grain caravans, drinking in a tavern called the Drowned Rat, and maintaining a beard that seemed to be attempting a hostile takeover of his face.

Wren found him at his usual table, losing at cards to a boy of perhaps fourteen who was cheating with tremendous skill.

"Aldric."

The big man looked up. His expression went through surprise, suspicion, recognition, and resignation in the space of a breath, arriving finally at the weary fondness of a dog hearing the leash. "*Ledger-man.*"

"You remember me."

"I remember a clerk who saved my life with paperwork." Aldric waved off the card game, tipped the boy a coin anyway — honor among cheats — and cleared a bench with his boot. "Sit. Drink. Explain why a dead man's ghost is buying ale in a city crawling with Grey's inquisitors."

"You've heard I'm hunted."

"Everyone's heard. They're offering silver for information. Gold for the child." He leaned forward. "They're not offering anything for you alive, Wren. You should know that. I heard the number they put on your head and I checked it twice because I thought I'd miscounted the zeros."

"I know." Wren had known since the pigeon died with the warning in its breast: *sold, midnight, the guildmaster's seal.* Corvane hadn't just betrayed them; he'd priced Wren as a problem to be erased rather than a witness to be questioned. There was a cold clarity in that which Wren found almost useful. Grief, it turned out, could be filed like everything else.

"So why are you sitting in a tavern instead of running for the coast?"

"Because running is what they expect, and because I owe people, and because people owe me, and right now those are the same thing." Wren set Mirabel's bundle gently on the table between them. The child emerged from the blankets, blinking, took one look at Aldric's beard, and reached out and patted it with great seriousness, the way one tests whether a thing is load-bearing.

Aldric went very still.

"She doesn't talk?" he said quietly.

"She talks. Just not our words. Not anyone's words anymore, maybe, except hers." Wren hesitated. This was the ledge he'd been circling for days — the truth he'd shown no one but the rats and the dying pigeon. If he gave it to Aldric, he could never take it back. "And she speaks a language I can hear. Because I hear all languages. All of them. Every creature that has one."

The tavern noise went on around them — dice, argument, a fiddle missing half its strings — and in the middle of it Aldric Vane stared at the disgraced clerk who had once saved his neck with a rubber stamp, waiting for the joke.

At Cinder Ford, Aldric had reported the massacre and been ruined for it. He had spent six years believing that telling the truth was a luxury for men with better luck. Now a mild-mannered bookkeeper was telling him a truth that would burn a man at the stake in the capital square, and telling it *to him*, trusting him with it, the way Aldric had once trusted a filing cabinet with his life.

"My brother-in-law was a beast-speaker," Aldric said at last, very low. "Small gift. Birds, mostly. The inquisitors took him when I was still in the order. I signed the transfer order. I didn't know what the transfer was." His hand closed around his mug until the wood creaked. "I've owed him a confession for six years. I suppose I'm paying it to you instead."

"Aldric—"

"I'm in." He cut Wren off with a raised hand. "Don't thank me. Thanking makes it noble, and I've retired from noble. Call it what it is: I'm a thief now too, and I'm stealing back a debt." He looked at Mirabel, who was still gravely testing his beard. "What does she call me?"

"She hasn't named you yet. She will. Fair warning — her names stick."

"Better than spurs did." Aldric drained his mug and stood, and six years fell off him like a dropped cloak; the knight was still in there, spurs or no. "Where are we going?"

"To see a queen."

* * *

The rat-queen of the sewers was not, strictly speaking, a rat.

Her name was Bess Underfoot, and she had been born under the Fishmarket to a mother who mended nets and a father who was mostly absent and entirely fictional. By twelve she had mapped the storm-drains better than the city engineers. By fifteen she commanded a network of beggars, mudlarks, and sewer-rats — the human and rodent varieties alike — that could move a stolen ring across the city faster than a courier and hide a fugitive more thoroughly than a monastery. By twenty she had stopped counting her years altogether, on the grounds that a queen's age is nobody's business.

Wren met her at the bottom of the Cistern Steps, where the torchlight turned the water the color of old honey, and where forty rats sat in a semicircle on the wet stone, watching him with the attentive air of a court awaiting an audience.

"They've been talking about you," Bess said. She was small, quick-eyed, missing two fingers to a trap she refused to discuss, and wore a crown that was, on close inspection, a bent copper colander. "My rats. For years they've come home saying there's a man in the Guild of Impossible Things who leaves crusts on the windowsill and *answers back*. I thought they were exaggerating. Rats exaggerate. It's their one flaw."

"They're very honest brokers."

"They're gossips with tails." She crouched, and the largest rat — a scarred veteran Wren recognized as the one he'd traded bread-ends with for market intelligence over three years — climbed onto her shoulder and regarded Wren with what could only be described as professional respect. "Three days ago they all came home at once, soaked to the bone, saying the crust-man was in the tunnels with a small person who smells like ash and old stone, and that the crust-man was *afraid*, and that the crust-man needed the under-roads. My rats don't bring me rumors, ledger-man. They bring me invoices. So." She spread her eight fingers. "What's the invoice?"

"I need the under-roads. To the surrendered city. For three people, quietly, soon."

"And in exchange?"

"Bess." Wren stepped closer, and the torchlight caught the exhaustion on his face and the resolve underneath it. "In exchange I'm going to tell you what's under the surrendered city, and why Veyran wants it, and what happens to your sewers and your fishmarket and your forty thousand mudlarks if he wakes it wrong. And then you're going to help me anyway, because unlike everyone else in this city, you already believe the ground remembers. Your rats have been telling you for years that something down deep turns over in its sleep. Haven't they."

Bess Underfoot was silent for a long moment. Above them, faintly, came the sounds of the city: carts, bells, the ordinary music of the living. Below them, fainter, came something else — a slow, vast shifting, like a sleeper changing position.

"Yeah," she said finally. "They have." She straightened her colander with dignity. "Right. Here's how this works. I don't take orders and I don't do heroics. I do logistics. You want the under-roads, you get the under-roads — but my people come with rules: nobody touches a soldier unless touched first, nobody dies stupid, and everybody eats. You got food?"

"We have some."

"You'll need more. Welcome to the court of the under-city, ledger-man. Mind the third step, it bites."

* * *

They gathered that night in the back room of Odile's shop, which had never appeared on any tax record and officially did not exist. A single candle. A map of the free cities drawn on the back of a false invoice. Five chairs, of which four were occupied and one held a rat of unusually senior rank.

"So we're agreed," Wren said, standing at the head of the table feeling absurdly like a guildmaster himself, "that none of us are heroes."

"Strongly agreed," said Odile.

"It's practically our founding principle," said Bess.

"I was a hero once," said Aldric. "It didn't take."

"Then we're a business venture," Wren said. "Which suits me. I've spent ten years keeping books for impossible things. Here's tonight's entry." He looked around the candlelight at the fence, the knight, the rat-queen, and the child drawing spirals in spilled tea, and felt something in his chest that was not quite hope and far too warm to be strategy. "One fence, one sword, one crown, one clerk, and one key. Assets: assorted debts, one map, several hundred loyal rodents. Liabilities: an army, an inquisitor, a traitor, and a god-shaped hole under the world." He picked up the pen. "Verdict: solvent."

Mirabel looked up from her spiral. She tugged Wren's sleeve and pointed at each of them in turn — the fence, the knight, the queen — and then pressed her small hand flat over her own chest, and said, in her language of ash and old stone, a soft cluster of syllables that Wren's gift translated without asking his permission:

*Family. Chosen. Witnessed.*

"What did she say?" Aldric asked.

"She said we're official," Wren managed, and wrote it in the margin of the invoice, because a ledger-keeper records what matters.

It was Bess's senior rat who brought the news, arriving damp and urgent through the window gap an hour past midnight, chittering a message that Wren alone at the table could hear and that turned his blood to well-water:

*Iron bells in the night. Soldiers at the Cistern Steps. They are asking the mudlarks about the drains. Someone has told them about the drains.*

Wren set down his pen.

"Pack everything," he said quietly. "We leave by the coffin-route in twenty minutes. And someone wake the queen — the army just found our front door."
