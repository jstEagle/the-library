## 11. Grey's Doubt

Sera Grey had interrogated forty-one people in her career as an inquisitor of the Crown, and she could say without vanity that she had never once enjoyed it. She was good at it — that was different. Enjoyment was for men like her brother Aldric, who had laughed even at funerals, and who had died screaming in a burning barn while something with too many eyes watched from the rafters.

She did not think about the barn. She thought about procedure instead. Procedure was a wall you could lean your whole weight against.

Which was why the report on her desk this morning offended her so deeply.

It wasn't the handwriting, though the handwriting was bad enough — Veyran's survey corps wrote their field reports as though ink were rationed by the word. It wasn't the smug little seal of the Office of Excavation stamped crookedly across the corner. It was the *content*, which was either a clerical catastrophe or a confession, and Grey had spent three sleepless nights deciding which.

The report was dated eleven days before the Ashfen massacre. It described, in the flat bureaucratic language of men who move earth for a living, the successful breaching of "a subsurface structure of pre-kingdom origin" beneath the Ashfen plateau. It listed casualties among the dig crews: none. It noted, almost as an afterthought, that the structure's walls were inscribed with script of unknown provenance, and that the local population — the warden villages — had reacted to the breach "with agitation."

*Agitation.* As if the villagers had been upset about the noise.

Grey set the page down on the scarred oak desk of her quarters in the garrison-house at Marrowgate and looked out the window at the rain. The free cities were grey under it, rooftops steaming, gutters running brown. Somewhere beyond the passes, Veyran's armies were massing, and somewhere beneath her feet, if half of what she'd read in the last week was true, there was a sleeping thing older than every kingdom that had ever tried to own it.

And someone in the Office of Excavation had poked it with a shovel and then written *agitation* in the margin.

"You're making the face again," said Corporal Denn from the doorway.

Denn was twenty-three, freckled, chronically hungry, and the only member of her retinue who spoke to her as though she were a person rather than an instrument of the Crown. This was insubordination. She kept him because he was also the best tracker she had ever worked with, and because his irreverence was, in a way she would deny under oath, restful.

"What face?" she said.

"The one where you look like the paperwork has personally wronged you." He came in, dripping, and put a wrapped bundle on her desk. "Brought you a bun. The baker's wife says you're too thin and she's right."

"I am not paid to be well-fed."

"No one's paid anything since the treasury started minting coins with Veyran's face on them. People bite them to check they're real and get Veyran's nose stuck in their teeth." He nodded at the report. "That the Ashfen thing?"

"It's the Ashfen thing."

"And?"

Grey looked at him. Denn had been with her for four years. He knew what she did to liars. He also knew that she had never once lied to him, which made the next sentence harder than any interrogation she'd ever run.

"And it appears," she said, "that the excavators breached the structure first."

Denn stopped chewing.

"Eleven days before the massacre," she went on, her voice level, procedural, a wall she could lean against. "The warden villages didn't summon anything. They didn't practice forbidden arts. They heard something wake up under their fields, and they went to see, because guarding it was their family trade going back further than records. And then whatever came up out of the breach—" She stopped. She made herself finish. "—killed them. All of them. Except one child, whom our warrant currently describes as an abomination to be recovered alive for study."

Denn swallowed. "The official finding says beast-speakers caused it. That's the whole basis of the purge order. That's why we're hunting the guildhouse."

"Yes."

"That's why your brother—"

"My brother is not relevant to the evidentiary record." Her voice came out colder than she intended, and Denn had the grace to look at the floor. She took a breath. "Aldric is relevant to nothing except my ability to remain objective, which I am doing. Objectively. With buns."

"Right," said Denn gently, and left the bun, and went to check the horses, because he was a better man than the Crown deserved.

Grey sat alone with the report and the rain and the terrible arithmetic of it.

* * *

The problem was that she had built her whole life on a single load-bearing belief: that the law was real. Not perfect — she'd seen judges bought, seen warrants used as weapons, seen the courts of the old king bend like green wood whenever a rich man leaned on them. But underneath all the rot there had been a spine. The law said: *find what is true, and act on it.* Everything else was corruption wearing the law's clothes, and an inquisitor's job was to tell the difference.

Her warrant for the beast-speaker purge bore Veyran's seal. Her orders named the gift itself as the crime. And now the evidence in her hands said the crime had been committed by the man whose seal authorized her to punish it.

She spent the morning doing what she always did when the world tilted: she gathered more evidence. She interviewed the survivors of the survey crew — three men billeted in Marrowgate, drinking away their hazard pay at the Drowned Bell. She did not wear her warrant openly. She wore a widow's grey and asked questions like a woman writing a memorial account for the families of the dead, which was close enough to the truth to be painless.

The first crewman cried into his beer and told her about the singing. "Under the ground. Not through the ears, more... behind them. Like a headache that knows words. Old Tam went down into the breach and came back up white as milk, saying he'd heard his mother, and his mother's been dead twenty years. Then the ground shook, and the captain ordered us out, and by the time we cleared the plateau—" He drank. "You know the rest, m'lady. Everyone knows the rest."

The second crewman lied to her, badly, about the timeline, and she let him, because liars show you the shape of what they're hiding by how they stand around it. He stood around the breach date like a man standing around a corpse.

The third crewman was the foreman's clerk, and he was drunk enough to be honest and frightened enough to be careful, which produced a strange, valuable mixture.

"They told us it was sealed for a reason," he said, staring into his cup. "The wardens. There was this old woman came down to the fence line every day of the dig, just stood there, watching. Never shouted, never cursed us. Just watched. Last day, she said to me — she said, 'When you open the door, ask it what it remembers.' And Tam laughed at her, and then Tam heard his dead mother singing, and none of us laughed after that." He looked up at her with red-rimmed eyes. "The company reports blame the villagers. But the villagers didn't dig, lady. We dug. Whatever got loose, we let it loose. I've written it down a hundred times in my head and burned it a hundred times, because the man who signs my pay says the war needs a reason, and the reason can't be us."

Grey thanked him, paid for his beer, and walked out into the rain feeling the load-bearing belief shift under her like a floor joist going soft.

* * *

She spent the afternoon drafting the report she was obligated to file, and it was here, over cold tea, that her career quietly broke in two.

The report was simple. *Findings of Inquisitor Sera Grey, Third Circle: documentary evidence indicates the subsurface breach at Ashfen preceded the massacre by eleven days; the breach was effected by crews of the Office of Excavation under direct commission of the Regent-Victor; the attribution of the massacre to beast-speaker activity is unsupported by evidence and contradicted by testimony of surviving crew members. Recommend immediate suspension of the purge order pending full inquiry.*

Four sentences. She had written longer reports about smuggled cheese.

She read it six times. Each time, she imagined its journey: from her hand to the regional censor, from the censor to the Office of Internal Loyalty, from there to the desk of the man who had commissioned the excavation, ordered the cover-up, blamed the murdered, and issued her warrant. She imagined what happened next. She had seen what happened to inquisitors whose findings inconvenienced the regime. There was a lake outside the capital with a very still surface, and the Crown maintained it beautifully.

And beneath that fear, quieter and worse, was another thought entirely: *if I file this, the truth dies in a drawer, and the purge continues, and the child dies, and everything Aldric believed in dies with him, politely, with proper documentation.*

Because that was the other thing she had realized in the small hours, staring at the ceiling. Her brother had died because of a beast-speaker — that part was true, that part she had carried for nine years like a stone sewn into her coat. But Aldric had been in that barn investigating a smuggling ring that moved stolen grain during a famine. The beast-speaker had panicked when the soldiers kicked the door in. The famine existed because the old king's court had hoarded the harvest. And the old king's court had fallen to Veyran, who promised order, and delivered excavations, and called the screams of murdered villagers *agitation.*

The stone in her coat had been pointing at the wrong culprit for nine years. Grief makes a poor magistrate.

Denn found her at dusk, sitting with the finished report in front of her and a candle burning down between her fingers.

"Well?" he said. "Did you send it?"

"No."

His face fell. "Sera—"

"Not yet." She turned the paper toward him. "Look at the routing. If I file this through channels, it reaches Veyran before it reaches anyone who could act on it, and then I reach the lake. And the purge goes on. So filing it isn't telling the truth. Filing it is burying the truth in an official drawer with a state funeral."

Denn read it twice. When he looked up, his freckles stood out pale against his skin. "Then what do we do with it?"

"That," said Grey, "is the question I have been failing to answer since dawn."

She rose and went to the window. Below, in the garrison yard, a courier was saddling up — dispatches for the capital, the usual nightly traffic of a nervous occupation. Among tonight's satchels, she knew, rode a copy of her preliminary findings on the Guild of Impossible Things: the child sighted, the staff questioned, the ledger-keeper Wren Okafor whose lies had been very nearly perfect and imperfect in exactly one place. She had reported him honestly. She had reported everything honestly, all her life, and her honesty was currently a weapon pointed at a mud-caked child and a tired man with ink-stained fingers who had flinched, only slightly, when she'd mentioned rats.

An honest weapon in a dishonest hand was still a murder.

"Denn," she said slowly, "when we took this commission, what did we swear?"

He blinked. "To the Crown? Find what is true, and act on it. Protect the realm's people from hidden harms." He paused. "Both halves. You made me memorize both halves. You said most inquisitors forget the second one."

"Did I." Almost, despite everything, she smiled. "I was smarter nine years ago."

"So the oath says find the truth and protect the people." Denn scratched his head. "And the truth is the Crown dug the hole, and the people being harmed are the ones we're hunting. Which means..." He trailed off, looking at her with dawning alarm, the way a man looks at a bridge he's standing on when the ropes start creaking.

"Which means the oath and the warrant have parted ways," Grey said. "And I have to choose which one I follow. Tonight. Because tomorrow the courier rides, and whatever I decide goes with him."

* * *

What she decided, in the end, was neither brave nor clean, which was how most real decisions looked from the inside.

She did not file the report. She did not burn it, either. She folded it into oilcloth, wrapped it in the spare shirt Denn swore she never changed out of, and sewed it into the lining of her travel cloak, where it sat against her shoulder blade like a second spine — the kind of spine, she thought grimly, that the law itself seemed to be missing these days.

Then she sat down and wrote a second document, short and cold and technically flawless: a supplementary finding noting that the initial attribution of the Ashfen event required "further verification against primary sources held in the capital archives," and that until such verification was complete, the inquisitor recommended *restraint* in the handling of the child — alive, unharmed, and unexamined by any office other than her own.

It was a delay. A lawyer's trick, a stalling maneuver dressed in procedure. The first lie of her career, if you counted lies of omission, and she did, and it sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone next to the older one about Aldric.

But it bought time. Time for the child. Time, perhaps, for the truth to find a road to the light that didn't run through the censor's office or the lake.

"There," she said, sealing it. "I've concealed material findings from the Crown, misused procedure to obstruct my own warrant, and committed treason in triplicate."

Denn, holding the candle, looked faintly delighted. "Congratulations?"

"Don't congratulate me. I feel ill." She blew out the seal and stared at the red wax, thinking of the old warden woman at the fence line. *When you open the door, ask it what it remembers.* Perhaps that was all justice ever was — asking what really happened, and having the nerve to write down the answer even when the answer cost you everything. She had opened a door today. She intended, before this was done, to ask her questions of the right people.

"One more thing," she said, pulling on her cloak, feeling the hidden report press warm against her back. "Tomorrow we ride for the surrendered city. Officially, to continue the search."

Denn frowned. "And unofficially?"

"Unofficially, I want to know why the guildmaster of the Guild of Impossible Things sent three separate messages to the capital offering to sell out his own guests — and why the bounty posted on the ledger-keeper last night specifies *dead*, when my warrant says alive." She buckled her sword-belt with hands that were perfectly steady, because procedure was a wall, and she was going to lean on it all the way down. "Somebody in this war wants witnesses silenced rather than studied, Corporal. And I have recently developed a professional interest in people who destroy evidence."

Outside, the rain kept falling on the free cities, patient as an archivist. And sewn against Sera Grey's shoulder blade, four sentences long, the truth waited for a reader worthy of it.
