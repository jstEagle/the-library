## 12. The Usurper's Ear

Veyran Ashgrave, First of His Name by His Own Decree, Lord Protector of the Iron Concord, Warden of the Passes, and — though the ink on that particular title was still drying — Emperor of Everything Worth Having, did not sleep well.

He never had. Even as a boy in his father's drafty keep at Vhal Mora, when sleep should have come easy to a child with a full belly and no enemies yet, Veyran had lain awake cataloguing things. The loose stone in the hearth where the cook hid coins. The way his father's steward flinched at loud noises, which meant there were secrets worth flinching about. The exact number of steps between his bedroom and the armory (forty-one; he'd counted twice).

Other children collected marbles. Veyran collected leverage.

Now he lay in the Palace of Nine Spires — which he had taken, not inherited, which mattered enormously to him — listening to the wind worry at the shutters and thinking about a child.

"A child," he said aloud, to the dark. "It comes down to a *child*."

The dark, as always, declined to argue.

* * *

In the morning he held court in the Amber Hall, because emperors held court, and because sitting on the Concord Throne with its uncomfortable gilded spikes of ornamentation reminded him daily that comfort was for men who had finished. He had not finished.

The petitioners came in their usual procession: a grain factor complaining about tariffs, a minor lordling with a border dispute shaped exactly like greed, a delegation from the Weavers' Guild presenting him with a tapestry of his own coronation in which someone had embroidered his nose slightly too large. He had the weaver flogged — lightly, he was not a monster, and besides, the nose *was* accurate, which made it worse — and moved on.

It was the fourth hour before Master Quillon approached the throne with the particular walk that meant bad news dressed as good news. Quillon was his spymaster, a soft-voiced man with ink-stained fingers and the facial expressiveness of a well-boiled pudding. Veyran trusted him almost completely, which in Veyran's arithmetic was the maximum available trust.

"Your Radiance," Quillon murmured, bowing. "A report from the Ashfen operation."

"Ah." Veyran leaned forward despite himself. The Ashfen. Three years of digging, eleven excavator-machines, a budget that could have bought two navies, and so far: dust, dead wardens, and a hole in the ground that hummed at night in a way that made the dig crews quit in droves. "The seal?"

"Unbroken, Your Radiance. But." Quillon produced a letter with the reverence of a man handling a relic or a snake. "The dig-master's surveyor found records in the warden village before it burned. Bloodlines. The wardens kept genealogies going back nine hundred years. Every line accounted for except one."

"Except one," Veyran repeated.

"One child, unaccounted for. Aged perhaps eight or nine at the time of the... incident." Quillon's pause was masterful. He let the word *incident* do its work; everyone knew what had happened at the Ashfen, and everyone knew better than to say it. "And this morning, a report from our agent embedded in Saltmere. A mute child arrived at the Guild of Impossible Things during the storm last week. Mud-caked. Walking out of the fen road. Speaking no known tongue."

The hall's noise — shuffling feet, murmuring petitioners, the drip of the famous amber fountain — seemed to fall away from Veyran like water off oiled cloth.

"The guildmaster," Veyran said. "Corvane. He reported this himself?"

"He reported *a stray child*, Your Radiance. Our agent recognized the significance. Corvane, as far as we can tell, sees only an orphan who eats too much."

Veyran laughed. It was a real laugh, which startled the nearest courtiers, who had learned to distrust his real laughs even more than his ceremonial ones.

"He sits," Veyran said, delighted, "on the single most valuable creature on this continent, and he files it under *stray*. Oh, that is — Quillon, write that down somewhere. Not for use. For my own enjoyment."

"I have already written it down, Your Radiance."

"You are a treasure. Now." He rose from the throne, which ended court by ancient custom — an emperor standing was an emperor done listening — and swept toward the map room, Quillon trotting at his heel like a loyal, well-fed dog. "Tell me what we know of these wardens. Truly know. Not the songs."

* * *

The map room occupied the palace's north tower, and its centerpiece was Veyran's own obsession rendered in painted plaster: the free cities, the passes, the Ashfen marked in careful gray, and beneath it all — visible only when you knelt and looked at the table's edge-on inlay — the thing itself. The Hollow. A seam of black glass running under the foundations of six cities, drawn from the surveys of the first war's survivors, the ones the old kingdoms had burned along with everything else they feared.

"They were shepherds, essentially," Quillon said, unrolling his notes. "The wardens. Families who lived above the seal and sang to it. The old texts call it *keeping the quiet*. They believed the Hollow was—"

"A weapon," Veyran said.

"Some texts say a weapon, Your Radiance. Others say a wound. One very tedious monastic fragment insists it is a *person*, or was one once, which I confess I cannot make sense of."

"People become weapons easily enough," Veyran said. "Give them enough grievance and a banner." He traced the black seam with one finger, city to city to city. Saltmere. Greyharbor. Vess. All of them sitting fat and complacent on top of the largest reservoir of power the world had ever buried, using it as a *cellar*. "My father told me the first war was fought over grain routes. My father was a fool with excellent posture. The first war was fought over *this*, and the winners were so frightened of what they'd won that they buried it and built cities on the grave to sit on it forever. Like hens."

"With respect, Your Radiance, hens do not build cities."

"These did. Look at them." He tapped Saltmere. "They've had three hundred years of peace and they've spent it perfecting tax law and watered wine. Their councils debate surrender terms already — I have the minutes, Quillon, I have *everything* — and they think their walls will save them. Walls." He straightened. "When the Hollow wakes, walls will be a joke their grandchildren tell."

Quillon made a small sound that in a less disciplined man would have been a sigh.

"And the child?" Veyran asked. "If it is the warden's heir — what can a child do that eleven machines cannot?"

"The machines dig, Your Radiance. The child, if the texts are right, *opens*. The seal answers blood. Or rather—" Quillon consulted his notes, then visibly decided against consulting them further. "Or rather, the seal answered the wardens. They spoke to it. There was a language. The dig crews report the humming changes pitch when anyone speaks near the shaft. Any language. As if it is *listening*."

"It listens," Veyran said softly.

Something turned over in his chest, slow and vast, the feeling he had chased his whole life — the feeling of a key fitting. He had taken a country with ledgers and patience. He had taken the capital in a season, without a battle, because he owned the granaries and the granaries owned the people. Ownership. That was the whole of power, he had learned it young: whoever owns the thing the people need, owns the people.

And here, sleeping under his enemies' feet, was the thing the whole world would need.

"Before winter," he said.

Quillon's pudding-face did not move, but something behind it did. "Your Radiance?"

"We open it before winter. Winter favors the defense — frozen passes, supply lines, all of it, they'll hide behind the weather like cowards behind their mothers. No. We wake the Hollow while the roads are still mine." He was pacing now, the map room's lamplight sliding over the black glass seam. "Move the eleventh machine to the deep shaft. Double the crews. And send for the child."

"The child, Your Radiance?"

"Inquisitor Grey is already in Saltmere hunting beast-speakers, is she not? Efficient woman. Grim. Give her the commission. She brings me the warden's heir alive and unspoiled, and I will give her anything she asks short of my chair." He smiled at the map, at the six fat cities, at the future lying under them like a coin under a floorboard. "A child, Quillon. All those councils, all those walls, all that history — and it turns on whether I get to a child before anyone thinks to ask it questions."

"Shall I have the agents move tonight?"

"Tonight. This hour. And Quillon—" He caught his spymaster at the door, and for a moment the emperor fell away and something older looked out, something that had once been a boy counting steps in the dark. "The child speaks to it. Remember that. Whatever else happens, whatever the machines tear loose, I want the child's mouth close to my ear when the Hollow wakes. If it listens, then it will listen to *me*. Power that answers only to blood is a tragedy waiting for an heir. Power that answers to *me*—" he spread his hands, generous, imperial, "—is history."

"Yes, Your Radiance," said Quillon, and bowed, and went out into the corridor where, in the shadow of a pillar, a pigeon sat on the windowsill regarding him with one round eye before dropping away into the gray morning.

Veyran returned to the map alone.

He knelt, as he liked to, and put his eye level with the inlaid seam of black glass, and for a long moment he thought he heard it — faint, absurd, impossible in a room four hundred miles from the fen — a hum. Low. Patient. Listening.

"Sleep a little longer," he told it, patting the table the way one pats a horse that will soon be yours. "I'm coming."

The hum, if it was there at all, did not answer.

But far away, in a smuggler's tunnel under Saltmere, a dying pigeon had already given up its last breath to carry a warning, and a man with a ledger and a secret was running.
