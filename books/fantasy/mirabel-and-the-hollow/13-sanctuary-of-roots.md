## 13. Sanctuary of Roots

The grove-shrine had been abandoned so long that the forest had begun to argue with it. Moss climbed the standing stones like green creditors. Ferns pushed up between the flagstones of the old processional path, and the roof of the little warden's cell — a single room with a hearth and a window shaped like an eye — had mostly become sky.

Wren stood in the doorway the first morning and listened to what the place remembered.

*Stone-keepers came here,* said the oldest oak, its voice slow as sap. *They brought bread and small griefs. They sang to keep something asleep. Then they stopped coming, and we grew over their songs.*

"Thank you," Wren murmured, and then caught himself, because the child was watching him with those grave dark eyes, and he hadn't meant to speak aloud to anything but her.

She tilted her head. She'd done that a lot in the three days since they'd come down out of the hills — watching him talk to birds, to mice, once, embarrassingly, to a very opinionated hedgehog who had objected to being relocated from the woodpile. She never laughed at him. That was somehow worse than laughing. It was as if she were *filing him away*, the way he filed entries in a ledger: *Wren Okafor, speaks to beasts, condition: incurable.*

Her name — the name she'd finally given him, drawn in spirals in the ash of the hearth and then, patiently, sounded out — was Miru. Or nearly. The true shape of it lived somewhere behind his understanding, in that older tongue she carried like a stone in her chest, and "Miru" was only the shadow it cast in common speech.

He'd taken to calling her Mirabel anyway, because when he'd first tried her name his tongue had tripped and produced something absurd, and she had made a sound he suspected was laughter and refused to correct him. A ledger-man appreciated a debt honestly incurred. He owed her a mispronunciation; he would carry it.

"Right," he said that first morning, clapping his hands together and immediately regretting it, because the sparrows in the eaves scattered in affront. "Lesson one."

Mirabel sat on the fallen lintel of the shrine door, hands folded in her lap like a small magistrate, and waited.

Wren crouched, picked up a stick, and drew in the dirt: a circle. "Sun," he said. "Sun."

Mirabel looked at the circle. Looked at the sun. Looked back at the circle with an expression that suggested she found his pedagogy wanting.

Then she took the stick from his hand, and beside his circle she drew a spiral — tight at the center, opening outward, the mark he'd seen scratched into the guild's oldest ledger, the mark that had started all of this — and she said a word.

It was not *sun*. He knew that much before the meaning arrived, the way her language always arrived: not translated but *remembered*, as if some part of him had known it all along and she was only knocking on the door of it. The word meant *the turning*, or perhaps *the breathing-out*, or perhaps — this was the meaning that settled deepest — *the thing that gives without asking to be thanked*.

"That's," Wren began, and stopped, because his throat had gone tight. "That's bigger than sun."

Mirabel nodded solemnly, as if to say: *everything I have is bigger than what you have.* Then she pointed the stick at his circle again, expectant.

"Sun," he said again, humbler now.

She repeated it — "*Sssun*" — with the careful precision of someone handling a borrowed knife, and then wrote the spiral next to it, pairing them, filing them away. Ledger-keeping. She kept ledgers too, he realized. In her blood, in her bones. A whole accounting of the world, older than any guild.

*Well,* Wren thought, sitting back on his heels in the moss. *We're going to get along.*

* * *

The days found a shape.

Mornings were for words. Wren would draw things in the ash or the dirt — cup, fire, door, rain, *run* (that one she already knew, to his sorrow) — and she would give him back the deep-word for each one, and slowly, entry by entry, they built a bridge between their languages that went both ways.

Afternoons were for chores, which is to say for the comedy of two people trying to survive in a ruin with no money, no allies, and one shared pot. Wren set snares badly. Mirabel fixed them silently and well, having apparently grown up where snares mattered. Wren gathered mushrooms and presented them hopefully; Mirabel sorted them into *food* and *death* piles with the brisk authority of a customs officer, and once quietly moved four of his selections from one pile to the other while maintaining eye contact.

"I've eaten those before," Wren protested.

The hedgehog, from under the woodpile, offered a contemptuous sniff that Wren chose to interpret as agreement with Mirabel.

Evenings were for the fire, and for the stories.

That was how he learned what the Hollow actually was.

He had carried a picture of it since the night he'd pieced together the fragments: a power sealed beneath the free cities after the first war, a weapon buried by the victors, dormant, waiting. Veyran wanted a weapon. Wren had assumed Veyran understood what he was digging for. It was Mirabel, on the fourth evening, drawing in the firelight with a charred stick on the shrine's smooth flagstone, who corrected the ledger.

She drew the seal first — the great ringed spiral, the wardens' mark — and then, beneath it, she did not draw a sword or a flame or any of the shapes a weapon makes. She drew a *wound*. A long, ragged line, and around it, smaller figures: stick-people, hundreds of them, kneeling, hands pressed to the ground.

And she spoke.

The word she used was one he'd learned that morning — *under*, or rather *beneath-and-holding*, the word for the way roots hold a hillside — and the rest came to him in that strange remembered flood, her meaning pouring into the shapes common speech could barely hold:

*It is not sleeping. Sleeping things wake glad. It is hurt. It has been hurt for nine hundred years, and every promise ever sworn over it — every vow the first kings made to bind it gently, every oath the wardens sang to keep it company — it remembers. All of them. Even the ones that were broken. Especially the ones that were broken.*

Wren sat very still.

"A wounded thing," he said slowly. "Under six cities."

Mirabel nodded. Then she added more words, and these came harder, freighted, and he had to close his eyes to receive them:

*Veyran thinks it is a treasure chest. He is digging toward it with iron machines, tearing the roots, breaking the promises still unbroken. But you cannot open a wound and own it. You can only open a wound and be inside it. When he reaches it, everything it remembers will come up through him. Every broken promise in his life. Every lie. He will drown in his own ledger.*

Despite everything — the fear, the exhaustion, the taste of ash that had been in the air since Ashfen — Wren felt a smile tug at his mouth.

"He's a debtor," he said. "And the Hollow keeps accounts."

Mirabel looked at him for a long moment. And then, deliberately, she reached out and tapped the place over his heart, and said one word in her deep language, and the meaning of it settled into him like a coal into snow:

*Same.*

"You think I'm like it?" Wren asked, startled. "A wounded thing that remembers?"

But even as he said it, he knew. He thought of his village burning when he was nine years old, because a beast-speaker had been found there — because *he* had been found there, though the mob never learned which child it was, and his mother had put him on a cart with strangers and told him never, ever to let them hear the animals answer him. He thought of twenty years of swallowing every greeting, every joke, every comfort the sparrows and rats and gulls had offered him in the dark. A thing sealed underground, full of words no one was allowed to hear, remembering everything.

"Yes," he said quietly. "All right. Same."

Mirabel leaned against his shoulder, briefly, the way a bird lands — testing, trusting — and then returned to her side of the fire, dignified, as if the moment had been entered into both ledgers and need not be mentioned again.

* * *

On the sixth day, the rat found them.

Not just any rat — Wren knew this one, or knew its kind: a sewer-rat out of Corvane's city, lean and scarred-eared, one of the queen's runners. It appeared at the edge of the shrine clearing at dusk, sat up on its haunches, and waited with the patience of a creature carrying bad news.

Wren's stomach dropped before the rat even spoke.

"*Ledger-keeper.*" The rat's voice was dry as paper. "*The Queen sends word. The tunnels are watched. They are asking after a man and a child. There is a new price on your head, and it is not a taking-alive price.*"

"How much?" Wren asked, because he was a ledger-man, and numbers were how he kept from panicking.

The rat told him. Wren whistled softly. A sum like that could buy the guildhouse twice over. Could buy the street it stood on.

"They know we're alive, then. Not just fled."

"*They know you went to ground somewhere green,*" the rat said. "*The man Corvane sold you to has hounds that are not hounds. Move soon. The Queen's debt to you is paid with this message. She says — *and here the rat hesitated, embarrassed, in the way of messengers delivering sentiment — *she says keep the small one safe. She liked the story you told about her grandmother.*"

"Tell her—" Wren began, but the rat was already gone, a gray slip into the ferns.

He sat by the cold firepit for a long time after that, listening to the grove breathe. Hounds that are not hounds. Inquisitor Grey's methods, most likely, or worse — Veyran's excavators had things on leashes that the Ashfen had spat out. Sooner or later, the searchers would walk the old roads, and the grove-shrine was on the old roads, because shrines always were. Roads remember pilgrims the way rivers remember rain.

He should wake Mirabel. They should move tonight, tonight, tonight, every instinct he had was screaming it.

Instead he sat, because tomorrow was lesson day, and he had promised her the word for *river*, and she had promised him the word for *home*, and he found — with a kind of quiet horror at himself — that he did not want to leave until the trade was done.

*You're getting attached,* said the practical voice in his head, the one that balanced books. *Attachment is a liability. Write it down.*

*I know,* he answered it. *I'm writing it down. Under losses. Where it belongs.*

* * *

The river lesson happened at dawn, at the actual stream below the shrine, because Mirabel had decided — with the imperiousness of a seven-year-old empress — that some words required field study. She stood at the bank, pointed at the water, and waited.

"River," Wren said. "Water that runs. Water that—"

Mirabel shook her head, impatient, and gave him the deep-word instead, and this one nearly knocked him off his feet.

Because it wasn't *river*. It was *the promise that keeps moving*. The word her people used for running water meant exactly that: water was a promise the world made to itself, to keep going, to keep giving, to never stop even when it was broken over stones — especially then. Broken over stones and still keeping the promise. That was what a river *was*.

"Oh," Wren said.

Mirabel watched him absorb it, and something in her face softened — the first time he'd seen her look simply, ordinarily young, a tired child by a stream instead of the last heir of a slaughtered order.

"Now you," she said, in her careful common speech. It was only her third complete sentence in his language, and she spent it like a coin she'd been saving. "Home. Teach."

Wren opened his mouth. Closed it.

What was the common word? *Home* — a house, a hearth, a roof. He'd had rooms above the guild's counting floor for eleven years. Before that, a village that no longer existed except as a burn scar and a name on no map. The word *home* sat in his mouth like a coin he'd stolen.

So instead he taught her the honest version. He knelt and drew in the mud of the bank: not a house, but a small circle of stick-figures, and among them, deliberately, a sparrow, a rat, a hedgehog, and — after a moment's hesitation — a small girl with spirals in her hair.

"This," he said. "Home is the ones who know your worst secret and stay anyway."

Mirabel studied the drawing. Then she took his hand, turned it palm-up, and traced one slow spiral in the center of it with her finger, and spoke the deep-word for *home* directly into his skin, and it meant: *the place where the wound is allowed to breathe.*

The sparrows, watching from the alder branches, said nothing at all, which from sparrows was a eulogy.

* * *

They left the grove-shrine the next morning, because they had to, and because — Wren admitted it to himself as he shouldered the pack — staying anywhere forever was a luxury neither of their ledgers could afford.

But Mirabel paused at the boundary stone, the last of the old warden-markers, half-swallowed by moss, and pressed her palm flat against it. And through her touch, faintly, the way you feel a heartbeat through a wall, Wren felt what the stone held: centuries of warden-hands, generations of small people saying goodbye to small places, each of them promising to come back.

None of them had. The promises lay in the stone like coins in a closed account.

Mirabel spoke a single word to it — an apology, he realized, an apology on behalf of everyone who had ever broken that promise — and then she turned and walked down the road without looking back, because grief was fuel, and she had miles to burn it on.

Wren followed. Behind them the grove settled back into its long green silence, holding what it held.

They made good distance that day, down out of the hills, along a stream that kept its promise loudly. Toward dusk they crested a ridge, and Mirabel stopped so suddenly that Wren walked into her.

Below, far off across the plain, the free cities glittered in the last light — and beyond them, at the edge of seeing, something else glittered too. A long, wrong gleam. Iron, massing. Camp-fires beyond counting, like a spilled sky.

And rising over it all, thin on the wind, a sound Wren had heard only once before, in stories told by men who'd fought in the first war:

Bells. Not the city bells. The war-bells. The iron bells of Veyran's vanguard, ringing the hour of advance.

Beside him, Mirabel's small hand found his, and gripped it, and she said — in common speech, in his language, in a voice that had no childhood left in it at all:

"Wren. It is waking early."
