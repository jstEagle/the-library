## 14. The Circle Breaks

Sera Grey had been following a dead man for three days.

Not dead yet — that was the trouble. The trail said alive: fresh boot-prints in the frost along the canal road, a stolen loaf's worth of crumbs outside a miller's barn, and once, memorably, a handprint on a gatepost small enough to belong to the child. Alive, then. Frightened. Moving at night and sleeping in hay like something out of a ballad her mother used to sing badly.

She crouched at the edge of the barley field and let the cold work its way through her coat while she thought about how much she disliked ballads.

"You're doing it again," said Vess.

Vess was her junior inquisitor, twenty-two years old, earnest as a hound pup, and constitutionally incapable of silence for longer than a quarter hour. Grey had requested three different partners before him. The fourth request had been denied on the grounds that no one else would ride with her, which Grey considered fair.

"Doing what?" Grey asked.

"Standing in a field looking like you're about to arrest the barley."

"The barley has done nothing wrong."

"So we're agreed." Vess dismounted and squinted at the gatepost. "That's the child's print?"

"It's someone's."

"You've been saying 'it's someone's' since Tarnwick. At some point, Inquisitor, you have to commit to a theory."

Grey committed to nothing. She walked the fence line instead, reading the ground the way other people read letters — quickly, hungrily, with a growing sense that the news was bad. The fugitives were careful. Too careful. Wren Okafor had spent years keeping ledgers for a guild of smugglers and liars; he knew how to leave no trail, which meant every mark he *did* leave was either exhaustion or invitation.

She did not know which possibility she feared more.

* * *

The truth was that Sera Grey had not slept properly since the Ashfen file.

It sat in her saddlebag now, wrapped in oilcloth like something venomous. She had pulled it from the provincial archive in Tarnwick on a whim — or what she told herself was a whim, though whims did not usually keep her awake until the candle drowned. Excavation permits. Haulage contracts. A casualty ledger from the fen settlements, written in the tidy hand of a clerk who had clearly never seen a corpse: *structural collapse, forty-one souls; structural collapse, sixty-three souls; structural collapse, unnumbered.*

Structural collapse. As if the earth had simply gotten tired.

Grey had stood in the ruins of the Ashfen villages herself, two years ago, cataloguing burn patterns for a report nobody read. She remembered thinking the scorch marks were odd — too deep, too even, climbing *out* of the ground rather than down into it. She had filed the observation under *anomaly, unresolved*, because filing things was what she was for. It had not occurred to her then that the anomaly might have a name, a seal, and a signature.

It had occurred to her now. It occurred to her at inconvenient moments: eating, riding, watching Vess hum tunelessly while he watered the horses. The excavators had gone into the fen eleven months before the fires. The excavators had gone in on writs signed by the crown of Veyran. And the beast-speakers she had hunted across half the free cities — the ones who supposedly caused it all — had died in those same fires, in numbers the casualty ledger rounded off like spilled grain.

Her brother had died believing speakers called the fire down. That belief was the load-bearing wall of her whole life, and Grey had spent three weeks tapping it gently, listening to it hollow.

"Stop," Vess said.

Grey looked up. "Stop what?"

"That face. You get it when you're rethinking your entire career. I've seen it twice before. Once in Tarnwick, once when the innkeeper charged us for the good room and gave us the one with the goat."

"There was no goat."

"There was absolutely a goat, Inquisitor."

Grey almost smiled. It was a near thing, these days; her smiles had gone the way of her certainties, occasionally sighted but rarely landing. "We make for the crossroads shrine," she said. "The trail bends east there. If they're heading for the river ferries, we'll cut them off by dusk."

"And if they're not?"

"Then we'll have walked to a shrine for our health, which is more devotion than most inquisitors manage."

* * *

The shrine was older than the warrant in her pocket, which was saying something, because the warrant was old enough to have gone soft at the edges like over-soaked bread. It named Wren Okafor, beast-speaker, wanted alive for questioning regarding crimes against the stability of the realm. *Alive.* Grey had read that word so many times it had stopped meaning anything, the way a word does when you stare at it — *alive, alive, alive* — until it dissolved into mere ink.

The shrine was a ring of standing stones choked with ivy, the kind of place country folk still left bread and buttons for gods whose names they'd forgotten. Grey dismounted at the treeline, which was habit, and put a hand on Vess's shoulder to keep him quiet, which was also habit.

There was a man at the stones.

He was dressed like a drover and armed like an assassin, which in Grey's experience meant he worked for someone who paid well and buried cheap. He was nailing something to the largest stone, and he was doing it with the brisk efficiency of a man on a schedule, and Grey watched him for a long moment before understanding crawled up her spine like cold water.

A poster. He was nailing up posters.

She waited until he rode off south — away from the trail, toward the capital road — and then she crossed the trampled grass and read what he'd left behind.

The paper was good stock. Crown-issue. The seal at the bottom was authentic; Grey had authenticated enough of them to know the difference between a real seal and a forgery, and this one had the particular smug crispness of a document that had passed through the Office of Rewards without anyone questioning it.

The reward notice read:

*WREN OKAFOR. Formerly of the Guild of Impossible Things. Wanted DEAD. Fifty crowns, proof of death required. Bring the head if the body is inconvenient.*

Below it, smaller: *The child is not to be harmed. The child is worth more than the man.*

Grey read it twice. Then a third time, because her mind kept sliding off it, refusing to hold the shape of it, the way a hand refuses a hot iron.

Dead. Not alive. Dead.

The warrant in her pocket said alive. Her orders, signed and sealed, said bring him in breathing. And here was a crown bounty — issued through channels, stamped and priced — offering fifty crowns for his head.

"He's dead already," Vess said quietly, coming up beside her. He'd read it over her shoulder. "They just haven't caught up with him yet."

"No," Grey said. "Someone decided he should be."

"Inquisitor, with respect — this changes the arithmetic. If the crown wants him dead—"

"The crown didn't issue this." She tapped the seal. "This seal did. There's a difference, and you'd know it if you'd ever tried to get a requisition approved. Seals get borrowed. Warrants get forged upward, not downward — nobody forges a document to make their target *harder* to kill." She turned the poster over. Blank. Good paper, official routing marks along the margin. It had come through legitimate hands.

Through Corvane's hands, said a voice in her head that sounded unpleasantly like her own.

Because who else? The guildmaster had sold the fugitives' location once already — Grey knew, because she'd paid for that information herself, in the currency of promises she'd mostly intended to keep. But Corvane had sold her a *location*. This was something else. This was a man covering his tracks. A man who had realized that a living Wren Okafor, sitting in an inquisitor's cell, might talk. Might mention who had fed him to the wolves, and why, and what the guild had traded with Veyran's regime over the years, and on which ledgers those trades appeared.

Dead men keep no ledgers.

"Fifty crowns," Vess said, whistling low. "That's a lot for a clerk."

"It's a lot for a witness," Grey corrected. "Clerks are cheap. Witnesses are expensive. Especially witnesses who can hear what rats say." She heard her own voice go flat and cold, and recognized it as the voice she used on suspects, and understood with distant discomfort that she was using it on herself.

* * *

They followed the trail another day anyway. Grey told herself it was procedure. Procedure was a comfortable word; it fit the mouth nicely and required nothing of the heart.

The trail bent northeast, away from the ferries, toward the hill country where the old grove-shrines thickened like weeds. Clever, she had to admit. Every shrine was a place where locals left offerings and asked no questions; every shrine was a knot of old law that even inquisitors hesitated to kick over. The clerk was hiding his child in the cracks between jurisdictions, and he was doing it with the quiet competence of a man who had spent his whole life being underestimated.

On the second evening they found the campsite. Or rather, they found what was left of it: a hollow beneath a fallen oak, cold ashes raked and scattered, and — this was the part that stayed with Grey — a neat little pile of acorns on a flat stone, arranged in a spiral.

"For the squirrels," Vess said. "He feeds the squirrels. We're hunting a fugitive and he's leaving tips for the local wildlife."

"He's leaving messages," Grey said, but she wasn't sure. She crouched by the spiral and felt something in her chest turn over, slow and painful, like a wagon taking a corner too fast. Because she had grown up on stories about beast-speakers — everyone had — and the stories all agreed on one thing: the gift made you less human. It made you look at people and see meat that talked. It made you love the wrong things.

And here was a man on the run with a price on his head, starving, hunted, sleeping in ditches — and he was spending precious minutes arranging acorns in a pattern so the squirrels would find them. So the squirrels would *know* the food was there. So the squirrels wouldn't worry.

She thought about her brother, then. She thought about him often, but tonight she thought about him specifically: Tomas, seventeen, dead in the fen fires, and how for nine years she had needed his death to mean something. Needed it to be a crime with a name attached. *Beast-speaker* had been the name. It had fit neatly in the warrant-shaped hole in her life, and she had built a career on top of it the way you build a house on a grave — quickly, and without asking the ground's permission.

The ground, it turned out, had opinions.

"Vess," she said. "Ride back to the last village. Buy provisions. Take your time."

He frowned. "You want me out of earshot."

"I want provisions."

"Inquisitor—"

"*Provisions,* Vess."

He went. He was young enough to obey and wise enough, apparently, not to ask twice. Grey listened to his hoofbeats fade, and then she was alone in the clearing with the cold ashes, the spiral of acorns, and the warrant in her pocket, and she took the warrant out and looked at it for a long time.

*Alive for questioning.* Someone had crossed that out somewhere upstream of her. Someone had decided that the questions Wren Okafor could answer were more dangerous than any answers he could give. And the someone had done it quietly, through proper channels, with a real seal, which meant the rot wasn't in some dark alley of the regime. The rot was in the office. The rot had a desk, and a stamp, and probably a very tidy inbox.

Grey had spent her whole life believing that the law was a wall between the innocent and the wolf, and that her job was to be a stone in that wall. Stones didn't get to choose. Stones didn't get to look at the mason and ask what the wall was actually for.

But she was not, she realized, actually a stone. That had always been a story she told herself, and stories could be revised. She was a woman with a horse, a junior partner, a saddlebag full of evidence, and a warrant that had become, sometime in the last three days, a lie she was carrying around like a hot coal.

She struck flint from her kit. She held the warrant by its corner.

It caught reluctantly, the way old documents do, curling and browning at the edges before the flame found its appetite. She held it until the fire reached her fingers, then dropped the last burning scrap onto the cold ashes of Wren Okafor's campfire, where it flared once among the acorns and went out.

The ash spiral was ruined. She found, to her mild surprise, that she felt worse about that than about the warrant.

* * *

Vess came back at dusk with bread, hard cheese, and the expression of a man who has done arithmetic he doesn't like.

"I passed a drover on the road," he said, dismounting. "Southbound. Same one from the shrine, I think — scarred knuckles, army boots under the drover's coat. There were two more riders behind him, maybe a mile back, moving fast." He paused. "They weren't following the fugitive's trail, Inquisitor. They were following ours."

"I know."

"They think we'll lead them to him."

"I know that too."

Vess stood there in the failing light, holding his provisions like an offering, and Grey watched him decide something. She'd seen that look before — on recruits, on suspects, on herself in a mirror, years ago, the night she'd first put on the grey coat. The look of a person realizing that the uniform is a choice, and choices can be unmade.

"What are we doing?" he asked finally. "Officially."

"Officially, we lost the trail at the river. Muddy banks, heavy rain, impossible conditions. We'll write it up honestly." She swung into the saddle. "Unofficially, there's a bounty on a man's head issued by people who were supposed to be asking him questions, and I find I'm no longer willing to be the hand that delivers him. Also, unofficially, I believe the Ashfen fires were set by the excavators, the casualty ledgers were falsified, and the guildmaster Corvane is selling corpses to cover his contracts. Unofficially, I intend to prove all of it."

Vess blinked. "That's treason."

"That's testimony," Grey said. "It only becomes treason if we lose."

Something happened to Vess's face then — a loosening, a settling, like a knot coming untied. He mounted up. "For the record," he said, "I never believed there was a goat."

"There was a goat, Vess."

"There was *not*—"

"Ride," Grey said, and they rode north, away from the trail, letting it go cold behind them — cold, and safe, and theirs alone.

Behind them, far to the northeast, past the hills and the shrines and the darkening groves, thunder rolled. Except it wasn't thunder. Grey had heard artillery before, and excavator-engines, and the great war-bells of the free cities, and this was none of those things. This was deeper. Older. A sound like the earth clearing its throat after a long sleep, deciding whether to speak.

The child was getting close to the Hollow. And whatever happened next, the law would not stop it, because the law was busy burning its own warrants.

Grey turned her collar up against the wind and rode toward the sound.
