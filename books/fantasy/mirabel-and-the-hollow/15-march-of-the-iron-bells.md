## 15. March of the Iron Bells

The bells of Marrowgate had not rung in forty years, and when they began, they rang wrong.

Old Tammas Vetch, who had pulled the retreat-rope at the last war's end and grown old waiting to pull it again, knew within three pulls that something was off. The iron bells were supposed to speak in descending thirds — *fall back, fall back, the walls are lost* — but age and salt air had warped them, and what came out instead was a groaning, arrhythmic clangor that sounded less like an order and more like a city clearing its throat before saying something it dreaded.

"Still counts," Tammas muttered, hauling the rope with both hands while younger men ran past him in the street below. "Bells don't have to be pretty. They just have to be true."

From the western ridge, the vanguard of Veyran's army looked like a spill of ink creeping across a wet page. Ten thousand men, if the scouts could be believed, and behind them the excavator-machines on their great iron treads, dragging siege-works and drilling rigs through the pass like enormous patient beetles. They did not charge. They did not need to. They simply arrived, the way winter arrives, and waited for everyone to do the arithmetic.

In the vanguard rode Captain Ossler Dane, a broad, tired man who had once been a quartermaster and still thought like one. He counted things. It was how he kept his soul from rattling around loose. He counted the free city's watchtowers (nine, two crumbling), its militia (perhaps six hundred pikes, badly fed), its granaries (half-empty — the requisition ledgers he'd intercepted said so plainly). And he counted, as he always did, the cost of the day ahead.

"Captain," said his lieutenant, a young woman named Brek with a scar across her chin she'd earned falling out of a supply wagon, "the council's sent up a white flag. And a herald."

"At dawn?"

"They want to talk tonight."

Ossler grunted. He had expected surrender by week's end; talking tonight meant the council was frightened, and frightened councils talked fast and gave away much. He should have been pleased. Instead he found himself looking past the city's walls to the low green hills beyond, where ordinary people were lighting ordinary lamps, and he felt the particular discomfort of a man who is good at his job and beginning to wonder what the job is for.

"Tell them we'll receive their herald at first light," he said.

"They asked for tonight, sir."

"I know what they asked for." He turned his horse back toward the camp. "Let them spend one more night being a free city. It costs us nothing, and it costs them everything."

Brek looked at him strangely but wrote it down. Ossler Dane counted that look too, and added it to a ledger of his own that no one would ever read.

* * *

The council chamber of Marrowgate smelled of tallow smoke and old arguments. Eleven chairs sat around the long table; nine were filled, because Alderman Pruett had taken ill — or claimed to — and Councillor Maren Ostwick had not come at all, which surprised no one, since Maren had spent three weeks loudly insisting that negotiation was cowardice and then quietly packed two trunks and left for the coast.

Speaker Halvard Renn stood at the head of the table with the Veyran herald's letter in his hands. He was seventy-one years old and had negotiated everything in his life except this.

"They offer terms," he said. "Occupation, not sack. The granaries shared. The garrison disbanded. No reprisals against civilians." A pause. "And the excavators get access to the ground beneath the city. Unrestricted."

Silence. Somewhere below the chamber, faint as a heartbeat, the bells' last echo still trembled in the stones.

"That's not terms," said Councillor Ivet Sallow, a sharp-faced woman who had made her fortune in rope and hemp and trusted nothing that couldn't bear weight. "That's a receipt. We sign that, and we're not a city anymore. We're a hole they dig in."

"And if we refuse?" asked old Councillor Brammel, gently, the way you ask a question you already know the answer to. "We've seen Ashfen. We've seen what happened at Corren's Ford when they didn't bother negotiating. Six hundred pikes against ten thousand. I can do sums, Halvard. Can you?"

Halvard could. That was the misery of it. He had done the sums all evening, and every sum ended the same way, and the only variable left was how many of his people died learning it.

"We buy time," he said at last, and hated the words even as they left him. "One city yields so the others can prepare. If we burn here, Veyran takes the coast cities by spring and there'll be no one left to resist anywhere. If we yield—" his voice caught, "—if we yield, some of us will live to fight later."

"Some of us," Ivet repeated. "Which ones? Have you checked with the ones who won't?"

"No," Halvard said. "I haven't. Because the dead don't attend council meetings, Ivet, and someone has to decide for them anyway. That's the whole cursed job."

It went to vote. Nine hands. Eight rose.

Ivet Sallow kept hers folded on the table, and after the others filed out, she sat alone in the tallow-smelling dark and did her own arithmetic, which involved a boat, a strongbox, and a list of names she intended to keep safe no matter what the council signed away. She was not a brave woman, she told herself. She was a practical one. The distinction felt thinner every year.

At dawn, the white flag went up over Marrowgate, and the bells did not ring again.

* * *

Three days' ride south, in a smugglers' cellar beneath a shuttered dye-house, Wren Okafor heard about the surrender from a rat.

He'd named her Duchess, mostly out of flattery — she was a big grey sewer-rat with a torn ear and the imperious manner of someone who had never once doubted her own importance — and she came to him now along a rafter, whiskers twitching, and poured a stream of images into his mind the way rats did: *iron, many feet, a flag white as milk, the smell of fear coming off a city in waves.*

"You're sure?" Wren asked. "White flag over Marrowgate?"

Duchess confirmed it with the smug certainty of a creature reporting good gossip, then demanded payment. Wren fished a crust of cheese from his pocket and paid. Even now, even with the world ending, debts were debts. It was practically his religion.

Behind him, Pip looked up from the floor, where the child had been drawing spirals in spilled flour for the better part of an hour. The child's face had gone very still.

"What?" Wren asked, though he already knew. His gift had been getting better at hearing meaning under Pip's silence — fragments of the old tongue surfacing like drowned things. The word Pip had just breathed wasn't a word Wren knew, but the feeling under it came through clear as cold water: *buried. awake. soon.*

"Yeah," Wren said quietly. "I know."

Sera Grey looked up from whetting her knife by the brazier. She'd been with them nine days now — nine days of a former inquisitor sleeping with her boots on and apologizing for nothing — and she'd learned to read the room's silences almost as well as Wren read its animals. "Marrowgate?" she said.

"Marrowgate," Wren confirmed. "They surrendered. Terms included letting Veyran's machines dig under the city." He rubbed his eyes. "Under the city, Grey. Do you understand what's down there? What Pip's whole bloodline died keeping shut?"

"The Hollow."

"The Hollow. And Veyran thinks it's a weapon. He's going to put drills into a seal that's held for four hundred years because he believes everything in the world is either owned or waiting to be." Wren laughed, and it came out wrong, brittle as dry kindling. "And we just handed him the door."

Brannic, the disgraced knight, stopped polishing his breastplate. He'd been polishing the same spot for an hour, which was how Brannic prayed. "There'll be resistance," he rumbled. "The other cities. The coast league. Someone will stand."

"Someone stood at Marrowgate," Grey said flatly. "Nine votes to one. You don't beat ten thousand men and a treasury with speeches, knight. I served the machine that's coming. I know its appetite. It doesn't stop for courage. It stops for—" she hesitated, glancing at Pip, "—for something bigger than courage."

The cellar went quiet. In the corner, Quill the fence — a small, quick man currently disguised as a large slow barrel of dye — cleared his throat. "Speaking as a businessman," he said, "there's bad news and worse news, and I've brought both, because I'm thorough."

"Quill," Wren sighed, "please."

"Bad news: Veyran's offering five hundred crowns for your head, dead preferred, which frankly stings. Alive was worth more last month. I feel undervalued." He counted on his fingers. "Worse news: my contacts say the excavators reached the outskirts of Marrowgate yesterday. They're not waiting for the paperwork. They're already drilling survey shafts."

Pip made a sound.

It wasn't speech, exactly. It was the sound a struck bell makes if the bell could grieve — a low, resonant note that seemed to come up through the child's chest and out through the stone floor, and every rat in the cellar fled for the walls, and the brazier flame bent sideways as if ducking. Then it passed, and Pip pressed both small fists against their mouth, eyes huge and wet, and Wren crossed the room and knelt and gathered the child in without thinking, the way you catch something falling.

"It's all right," he lied softly. "It's all right."

But it wasn't, and he knew it, and lying to a child whose language was older than kingdoms felt like a special category of sin.

Later, when Pip slept curled against Brannic's cloak — the knight pretending not to notice, which was his way of loving things — Wren sat with Grey by the dying fire.

"Say it," she said.

"Say what?"

"Whatever you've been chewing on for three days. You get a look. Ledger-face, I call it. Like you're balancing columns and one of them won't come out even."

Wren stared into the coals. "Every road ends the same place, Grey. I keep walking them all and they all end at the same door. The army can't be beaten — you said it yourself. The councils are folding one by one; Marrowgate today, Saltmere next week, and after that there's nothing between Veyran and the whole coast. Negotiation is dead. It was dead before Marrowgate rang those bells; the bells just buried it." His voice dropped. "The only thing under the sky strong enough to stop that army is asleep under Marrowgate. And waking it might kill everyone it saves."

Grey was quiet a long moment. "You've spoken with it. In your dreams, or whatever it is you do. What does it want?"

"To be mourned." The answer came out of him unbidden, true and terrible. "That's all. Four hundred years of broken promises sitting in the dark, and all it wants is for someone to say them out loud. Every oath the first kings swore and broke. Every warden who starved at their post. It wants witness, Grey. And Veyran wants to own it, and the difference between those two things is the difference between a funeral and a robbery." He laughed again, that same broken sound. "And I'm supposed to walk into the middle of it and somehow make a grieving god and a mad king both listen."

"You forgot the third problem," Grey said.

"There's a third problem?"

"You." She nodded at him, matter-of-fact. "To bind it, someone gives it a voice. Your voice, if the wardens' records mean anything — and I've read them, Wren, I read everything, it was my job. You'd be giving up the only part of yourself you never sold. The ledger-keeper who pays every debt in silence." Her mouth twisted. "I arrested people like you for eleven years. I know exactly what that gift costs to hide. And you're going to hand it over like it's nothing."

"It's not nothing." Wren looked at the sleeping child, the rise and fall of one small breath under a stolen cloak. "But I've watched what happens when nobody speaks for the ones who can't. My village burned for it. Pip's village burned for it. Marrowgate just surrendered for it." He straightened. "Somebody has to pay. It may as well be the one who keeps the books."

Grey studied him for a long time. Then she did something she almost never did: she smiled, briefly, crookedly, like a crack in a wall.

"Well," she said. "Then we'd better get you to Marrowgate before Veyran's drills reach something they shouldn't. Brannic! Wake up the barrel. We ride tonight."

From the rafters, unseen, Duchess the rat listened with her head cocked, and when the strange soft-speaking man finally slept, she slipped out through a crack in the wall to carry the news home to her ten thousand subjects beneath the streets of the world — because rats, too, kept ledgers, and the man who fed them had a debt coming, and the deep places belonged to her kind long before they belonged to kings.

Above, the road north lay open and black under a moonless sky. Three days to Marrowgate. Perhaps less, if the drills dug fast.

Perhaps none at all, if the thing below woke hungry.
