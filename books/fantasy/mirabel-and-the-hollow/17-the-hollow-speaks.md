## 17. The Hollow Speaks

The dark below the surrendered city was not empty. Wren had known that from the first stair, but knowing and feeling were different animals entirely, and by the time they reached the bottom of the descent, the feeling had grown teeth.

He could not see the seal chamber so much as sense it — a vastness that pressed back against the small circle of the child's candle-flame the way the sea presses against a cupped hand. The air tasted of old rain and older promises. Somewhere far off, water dripped with the patience of a thing that had been counting the years.

*Here,* the child signed, and then said it aloud in the old tongue, and the word did not echo so much as *arrive*, settling into the stone around them like a key into a lock Wren's body had apparently been shaped to fit.

Grey stood a pace behind them both, her inquisitor's coat still dusted with the ash of the stairwell, her hand nowhere near her sword. That, more than anything, told Wren how deep they were. Sera Grey without her hand on her sword was a woman who had understood something fundamental about where they stood.

"It's listening," she said quietly. Not a question.

"It's always listening," Wren said. "That's rather the whole trouble with it."

The child tugged his sleeve and signed again — the spiral, then the open hands, then the sign Wren had learned to read as *speak* — and Wren's stomach performed a slow, deliberate turn.

"I know," he said. "I know. Give me a moment to be terrified in an orderly fashion."

The child, being six or seven years old and made of considerably sterner material than Wren, simply waited.

He had spoken with animals his whole life. Rats, mostly, in the early years — quick, practical souls who wanted crumbs and gossip in roughly equal measure — and later gulls, cats, one memorably rude mule, and once, in the worst week of his life, a dying horse whose last words had haunted him for a decade. But every one of those conversations had happened inside the world. This — whatever waited beneath them — had been old when the world was learning to walk. Speaking to it felt less like calling across a room and more like knocking on the door of a house that contained the weather.

Still. He had not come all this way, had not burned his ledgers and his life and his precious anonymity, to stand at the final door and clear his throat politely and go home.

Wren Okafor closed his eyes, found the still place behind his breastbone where his gift lived — the small warm room he had kept locked and dark for thirty years — and opened it all the way.

*Hello,* he said, in the way that had no words at all. *My name is Wren. I keep accounts.*

The silence that followed was not silence. It was attention — vast, slow, and terribly, achingly lonely, like being looked at by a lighthouse.

Then the Hollow spoke.

* * *

It did not use sound. It used *memory*. One moment Wren was standing in a dark chamber holding a candle, and the next he was standing in a green country that had not existed for a thousand years, under a sky the color of a robin's egg, and the grass came up to his knees and smelled the way grass is supposed to smell before anyone invents the word *harvest*.

*You keep accounts,* said the Hollow, and its voice was every voice at once — the rat and the gull and the mule and the horse, the child and the king, all folded into one low chord. *Then read me.*

The green country rippled, and Wren saw it all.

He saw the first war — not as the histories told it, with banners and heroes, but as the ground had known it: the weight of marching feet, the rivers running strange colors, the screaming of the earth itself as men dug into its bones for power. He saw the wardens of that age — speakers like him, like the child — who had not defeated the Hollow but had *listened* to it, had heard its grief and rage, and had bound it not with chains but with a promise: *Sleep, and be mourned. Sleep, and be remembered. We will sing you down gently, and one day, when the world has learned kindness, you may wake whole.*

He saw the promise kept, generation after generation. Warden mothers singing to their children in the old tongue. Warden fathers teaching the spiral signs. A long, unbroken thread of small, stubborn tenderness stretched across a thousand years.

And then he saw the Ashfen.

He saw the excavators come, great iron-bellied things chewing into the deep places. He saw the wardens stand before them, unarmed, singing, and he saw what Veyran's men did to singers. He saw the child's village burn in a way that no ledger could ever balance.

Wren came back to himself on his knees in the dark, candle guttering, tears running freely down his face, and found the child's small hand on his shoulder — a gesture so precisely, heartbreakingly adult that it nearly broke him worse than the vision had.

"I'm all right," he lied, in a voice that fooled no one, least of all the ancient power beneath the world.

*Now you know,* said the Hollow. *Now, keeper of accounts. What will you pay?*

The chamber shifted around them. The darkness gathered, and in it Wren began to see shapes — possibilities, offered up like wares at a market stall, each one gleaming.

*You have enemies,* the Hollow said, and there was something almost sly in it, something wounded and clever, the way a beaten dog can still learn to bite. *The man who burns villages. The guildmaster who sold a child for his own comfort. The world that made your gift a crime and burned your village for it. I could unmake them. I could reach up through the roots of the earth and take their names out of the world's mouth. I could give you back your village, your mother, every field they salted — remade, restored, as though the burning never happened.*

And oh, it was beautiful. That was the trap of it, Wren thought dimly. Not the power — anyone could refuse raw power. It was the *mending*. He could smell the bread his mother had baked. He could hear the specific creak of the third stair in his childhood house. He could see the faces, every one of them, the whole village, alive and annoyed and laughing, the way they had been on the last ordinary morning before the soldiers came.

He could have Veyran's empire crumble like wet bread. He could have Corvane on his knees. He could have thirty years of hiding, flinching, paying with silence — *undone*, as though it had never been.

The Hollow waited, patient as water.

Beside him, the child watched him with those old, old eyes, and Wren understood, suddenly and completely, that the child had been offered the same things. In the Ashfen. In the dark of the flight. Probably every night in dreams. And the child — six years old, orphaned, carrying a language like a wound — had walked all this way to offer the Hollow not a wish but a *song*.

Wren got to his feet. His knees complained. He let them.

"No," he said.

The green country flickered. *No?*

"No," Wren said again, and found, to his surprise, that the word came easier the second time, the way the truth always does once you've said it once. "You're offering me the world remade by force, and I know how that ends, because I've read the ledgers. Every tyrant who ever lived thought he was mending something. Veyran thinks he's mending the free cities right now. Corvane thinks he's mending the guild. The men who burned my village thought they were mending the world of people like me." He took a breath. "You can't mend grief by unmaking the people who caused it. You just make new grief and hand it to someone else. Believe me. I keep accounts. The debts always come due somewhere."

*Then what,* said the Hollow, and the slyness was gone now, and what remained was so raw and enormous that the candle-flame bent toward it like a plant toward sun, *do you want?*

And Wren, who had spent his whole life being careful, being quiet, being small, found that he knew the answer the way he knew his own name.

"I want to *witness* you," he said. "That's all. That's everything."

He knelt again — not in submission this time, but the way one kneels at a bedside.

"Everyone who bound you called it sealing. Everyone who dug for you called it taking. Nobody — not in a thousand years — has simply stood here and said: *this happened to you, and it was wrong, and we are sorry.*" His voice cracked, and he let it. "You are not a weapon. You are not a treasure. You are a wounded thing that has been alone in the dark since before my people learned to write, holding a promise that the world forgot how to keep. And I can't give you your green country back. I can't raise the wardens. But I can do the one thing I was actually made for."

He spread his hands.

"Let me speak your grief aloud. All of it. Every broken promise, every burned village, every warden who died singing. Let me say it in the tongues of the living, up in the air and the light, where it can finally be *heard*. Grief that's witnessed can be mourned. And a thing that's mourned—" he glanced at the child, who had gone very still, listening with their whole body, "—a thing that's mourned can rest. Not sealed. Not bound. *Mourned.* There's a difference, and it's the whole difference."

The Hollow was silent for a long time. Long enough that Grey shifted her weight. Long enough that the water-drip counted eleven falls.

*You refuse my gifts,* it said at last, wonderingly, *and offer me a funeral.*

"A memorial," Wren said. "With better attendance."

And then the Hollow did something it had not done, Wren suspected, in a thousand years.

It laughed.

It was not a kind sound, exactly — it was too big, too rusted, too full of everything it had swallowed — but it was a laugh, and the whole chamber rang with it, and dust fell from ceilings that had not seen a ceiling in centuries, and the child began to laugh too, high and bright and utterly fearless, and Grey made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth by professional dignity.

*Small keeper of accounts,* the Hollow said. *You understand nothing, and you understand the one thing that matters. Very well. Witness me.*

The green country rose around them one last time — not as temptation now, but as testimony — and Wren opened his mouth to speak, and felt the Hollow pour a thousand years of grief into him like a river into a cup.

He had just enough time to think, with the last corner of his accountant's mind: *this is going to cost more than I have* — before the first word came.

* * *

He did not remember most of what he said. Later, Grey would tell him that his voice had changed — that it had become many voices, that the rats of the surrendered city had come up out of the sewers in their hundreds to sit in the moonlight and listen, that the gulls had wheeled over the rooftops all night without a single cry, that even the bells, the great iron bells Veyran's army had silenced, had hummed faintly in their towers as though remembering their work.

He said the first war. He said the wardens, all their names, in the old tongue and then in the common speech. He said the Ashfen, and the child's village, and the child's mother, whose name was Issa and who had sung to the roots of things. He said his own village, and his own mother, and the third stair that creaked. He said Corvane's smile and Veyran's hunger and every small, ordinary cruelty that had made the great ones possible.

And the Hollow received it — all of it, every word, the way dry ground receives rain — and somewhere in the long dark of that speaking, Wren felt something in himself begin to quiet. Not break. *Quiet.* Like a room after a storm has passed through it.

The last thing he said, he remembered perfectly, because it was the simplest:

"You are mourned. You are remembered. You may rest."

The green country folded itself away, gently, like a letter being closed.

And far above them, faint through a thousand feet of stone, Wren heard the first bell of dawn begin to ring — and beneath it, a deeper sound, a vast and deliberate shifting, like a mountain turning over in its sleep.

The child grabbed his hand, eyes wide.

*It's moving,* the child signed urgently. *It's waking — the seal is opening — and something else is coming down the stairs.*

Grey already had her sword drawn.

Above them, muffled by stone and distance, came the grinding roar of excavator-machines.
