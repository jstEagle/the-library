## 22. The First Word of Spring

The child had three names now, which was two more than she'd ever owned before.

Her first name was the one the wardens gave her in the Ashfen, the long syllable that meant *last leaf of the burned rowan* — but no one below the mountains could shape it without their tongues tripping, so it lived mostly inside her now, folded up like a moth's wings. Her second name was Wren's fault entirely: he had called her Sparrow for a whole winter because she was small and loud and ate everything, and the name stuck to her like burrs. And her third name was new, given only last month by Maren the baker, who said that any child who could talk a sourdough starter back from the dead deserved a proper hearth-name, and so she was also called Ember, and she liked this one best of all, though she would never say so where Wren could hear it, because he would make *that face*, the smug one, the one that said *I told you so* without using any words at all.

Wren used very few words these days. That was the bargain he had made beneath the earth, and everyone in the free cities knew the story by now — how the beast-speaker had given his voice to the Hollow so that the Hollow could sleep without dreaming of war. What most people did not know, what only Sparrow-Ember-Last-Leaf knew, was that the story was not quite finished. The Hollow had taken his great voice, his thunder-voice, the one that spoke with gulls and rats and horses and the old dark things under stones. But it had left him the small sounds. A door creaking open. Rain on a tin roof. And once — only once, on the worst night of the winter, when she had cried herself sick over a dream of burning — the sound of her own name, carried up through the floorboards from somewhere far below, soft as a hand on her hair.

She had stopped crying immediately. Then she had started again, for different reasons, better ones.

Now it was spring, or near enough. The morning smelled like wet earth and green things deciding to happen, and Sparrow stood on the hill above the half-rebuilt guildhouse with a sapling in a bucket and mud halfway up her arms, and she was, at this precise moment, arguing with a worm.

"You're being ridiculous," she told it, in Common, because worms were terrible at nuance and she'd learned early that if you wanted a worm to do anything you had to be very plain about it. "I'm putting you *back*. That's the whole point. You were in the hole, I need the hole, we're all going to be happier."

The worm expressed, at length, its opinion regarding being dug up by enormous fingers.

"I know," she said, gentler now, switching to the old tongue, because some complaints deserved dignity. *Small brother, I know. The world is full of hands and most of them are careless.* She held him up so he could see the sky, all that shocking blue after the brown dark of the bucket. *But look what the hands built this time.*

Below them, the guildhouse rose in its patched and mismatched glory — half old stone, half new timber, the burn scars still visible along the east wall where Corvane's betrayal had caught fire behind it. They had talked, in the first weeks, of tearing it down altogether. Building something clean. But Sera Grey — who came every restday with bread and official documents she pretended were social calls — had argued for keeping the scars visible. "A court should remember what it survived," she'd said, "or it'll just survive into the same mistake twice." So they'd kept the scars and hung a new sign, and the Guild of Impossible Things had reopened under a charter that Grey herself had drafted, which meant, as Wren put it with one raised eyebrow, that the impossible things were now fully licensed and taxable.

The worm, having seen the sky and found it acceptable, allowed himself to be lowered into the hole.

"Thank you," Sparrow said gravely. "You may have the first turn of the soil. It's tradition." It was not tradition. She had invented it just then. But the worm did not know that, and besides, she was fairly sure traditions had to start somewhere, probably exactly like this, with someone making it up while holding dirt.

She settled the rowan sapling into the earth — a rowan, of course, because her oldest name meant rowan, and Maren had grown it from a cutting of the tree outside the bakery, and trees, unlike people, did not mind having their names shared. She packed the soil down with her palms. She wiped her nose on her sleeve, which made Wren sigh from the bench by the gate, where he sat mending a birdhouse with nails held in his lips, watching her with the particular expression of a man who had given up telling her not to do that sometime around midwinter.

He couldn't tell her anymore. But he could sigh in eleven distinct dialects, and she understood every one.

*Are you ready?* she asked the sapling, in the old tongue, kneeling in the mud. *It's your first spring too.*

The sapling said nothing, because saplings are famously reserved, but its roots shifted in the dark like a sleeper getting comfortable, and that was answer enough.

Far below them — below the hill, below the guildhouse cellars, below the smugglers' tunnels where Wren had once run with her on his back while the world ended politely behind them — something vast turned over in its sleep. Sparrow felt it the way you feel a heartbeat through a floor: not a sound, exactly. A settling. The Hollow had been sleeping for a season now, truly sleeping, not the grinding half-sleep of the sealed years, when it had dreamed its grief into the roots of cities and waited, patient as stone, for someone to come and either free it or break it. Veyran had come to break it. Wren had come to listen to it. And listening, it turned out, had been the thing no one had tried in four hundred years.

*Everyone wants to own the wound,* the Hollow had told Wren, in the chamber beneath the surrendered city, in words that Sparrow had later translated for the councils, standing on a chair so the delegates could see her. *No one asks it how it got there.*

It slept content now. Not healed — Sparrow suspected wounds that old never wholly healed, they just learned to be part of you, the way her memory of the Ashfen was part of her, the smoke-smell that sometimes woke her at night and the way she still counted exits in every room without meaning to. But content. Held. Anchored to a voice that had chosen it freely, which was, according to everything the wardens' songs said, the only kind of anchor that ever held.

And sometimes, on quiet nights, it hummed.

Sparrow stood, brushed off her knees, and looked out over the valley. The road from the passes was busy this morning — carts of seed and timber, a merchant train flying the new banners of the Concord, and, bringing up the rear with the resigned air of a woman who had once burned her warrant and never quite gotten the smell of authority out of her coat, Sera Grey on a dappled mare, escorting a wagonload of orphaned books from the capital's seized archives. Grey had testified for eleven months. She had put Corvane away, and the harbor-master, and two council elders who had thought themselves untouchable, and when the courts wobbled she had simply built new ones, out of stubbornness and paperwork, which Sparrow privately considered the most impossible thing the Guild had ever housed.

Grey raised a hand as she passed the gate. Wren raised a nail in reply. Neither of them smiled, exactly, but the air between them did something warmer than smiling, the way it always did, two people who had stood together in the dark under the world and never needed to discuss it afterward.

Sparrow turned back to her tree.

There was a word she had been saving. All winter she had kept it, the way you keep the last sweet thing in your pocket, taking it out sometimes just to feel its shape. It was the first word of the wardens' song — not the mourning verses, not the sealing verses, but the small verse at the very beginning, the one the old teachers sang over seeds and children and ships leaving harbor. In Common it had no true translation. The closest anyone had managed was *grow anyway*, which Maren liked, and *even here*, which Grey liked, and *yes, despite everything*, which was Wren's, offered in writing on the back of a ledger page, his handwriting small and dry and absolutely certain.

But the word itself was older than all of those. The word itself was the language's first word, the one the child's mother had breathed into her hair the night the excavators came, the night the wardens died, the night a girl walked out of the Ashfen carrying a whole civilization in her mouth. It meant: *the ground remembers, and the ground forgives, and both of these are true at once.*

Sparrow pressed both hands flat against the cool soil around the rowan's roots. She closed her eyes. Below her feet, deep enough that the deepest miners' maps stopped guessing, something sleeping listened without waking, the way a cat listens to rain.

She spoke the word.

It went down through the hill like water finding an old channel. It went into the roots of the guildhouse and the roots of the city and the roots of everything the first war had broken, and somewhere in the dark a weight that had been holding itself very still for four hundred years let out a breath it had been holding since before there were kingdoms, and settled deeper, and dreamed, for the first time in living memory, of nothing at all — just warmth, and dark, and the faint far-off sound of a child laughing above the earth.

The rowan shivered. One new leaf unrolled, bright as a struck match.

"There," Sparrow said, in Common, wiping her hands on her trousers. "That's done."

Wren limped over — the tunnels had left him a limp the healers said would fade, and which had shown no interest whatsoever in fading — and crouched beside her, examining the sapling with the critical eye of a man who had balanced ledgers for a living and trusted nothing that hadn't been counted twice. He touched the new leaf. Then he looked at her, and tilted his head, and made the small sound he saved for questions.

"It's fine," she said. "It's going to be fine. Everything down there is fine."

He raised an eyebrow. Eleven dialects of eyebrow, too.

"*Fine* means fine," she insisted. "I checked. I check every night. You'd know that if you didn't sleep like a murdered man." She grinned at him, gap-toothed, eight years old and fluent in two tongues and three names, and after a moment Wren laughed — silently, the way he did now, shoulders shaking, eyes creased, the laugh itself given away forever and somehow still arriving, because some things don't need a voice, they just need someone to witness them.

He tapped the earth beside the sapling, then pointed at her, then made a shape in the air: *your turn next year, this spot, I'm holding you to it.*

"Obviously," she said. "I'm planting a whole orchard. Rowans and apples and one plum, because Maren says a garden without a plum tree is just showing off."

From the direction of the kitchen came Maren's bellow that breakfast was ready and if the children weren't at the table in five minutes she was feeding it to the gulls, and Sparrow took off down the hill at a dead run, mud flying, Wren following at his lopsided trot, and behind them the rowan stood in the morning light with its single bright leaf turning slowly on its stem like a small green hand, waving.

And far below, deeper than sorrow, deeper than memory, in the warm and ancient dark, something that had once been called a weapon and had always really been a wound heard the running footsteps fade and the breakfast-bell ring and the whole small noisy machinery of the living world go about its day above it — and it slept on, content, its door closed but not locked, its key a word that a child would teach to other children, in springs yet unplanted, for as long as anyone remembered to listen.
