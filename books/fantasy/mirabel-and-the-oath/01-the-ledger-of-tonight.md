## 1. The Ledger of Tonight

The cellar flooded every spring, and every spring the Guildmaster declared it a feature rather than a flaw.

"Water keeps the ink honest," he liked to say, which was nonsense, but the kind of nonsense that had been said in that room for so long it had calcified into policy. So Mirabel Quill balanced the Guild's accounts with her boots propped on a brick, her ledger on a slanted board above the tide line, and the sound of the river muttering through the foundation stones like an old man complaining about his dinner.

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its books the way other institutions kept their secrets: badly, but with excellent penmanship. Mirabel had learned the system in her first month — three ledgers for every transaction. One for what was paid. One for what was owed. And one, bound in grey leather with no title on its spine, for what could not be written down anywhere else.

She was not supposed to touch the third ledger.

She touched it constantly. That was the whole point of being good at your job — you learned where the bodies were buried, and then you learned to read the dates on the graves.

Tonight's work was simple enough: reconcile the autumn quarter against the winter contracts, flag anything older than a decade for review, and initial the margins in green ink because the Guildmaster believed red was vulgar and black invited bad luck. Rain ticked against the high window slit. A lamp burned low beside her. Somewhere overhead, the guildhall creaked through its evening settling, footsteps crossing and recrossing the floor above like thoughts passing through a sleeping head.

She was halfway down a column of candle-wax imports when she noticed the book.

It hadn't been there at supper. She was certain of this the way she was certain of most things — by having counted everything in the room twice. The cellar held one desk, one stool, one shelf of ledgers arranged by year, one iron brazier gone cold since March, and forty-one bricks stacked in the corner where the water came in. She knew these numbers the way other people knew their own heartbeat. And now, on the corner of the desk where the lamp did not quite reach, there was a book she had never catalogued.

It was old. Not Guild-old, which meant fifty years and a coffee stain, but genuinely old — the leather gone soft as chamois, the corners rounded like river stones. No clasp. No title. It lay open, which no book in that room ever did, because Mirabel trained herself to close every volume before leaving it; open books absorbed damp the way the Guild absorbed debts.

Someone had left it open deliberately. Face-up. Facing her chair.

She told herself it was misfiled. She told herself this while wiping her pen, while squaring her current ledger, while doing all the small rituals she used to make the world hold still. Then she picked it up.

The paper was thick and cold, the kind made before papermakers learned to be cheap. The handwriting inside was copperplate, immaculate, the hand of someone who wrote for a living and resented nothing about it. At the top of the page, in faded iron-gall ink:

*1897.*

A ledger, then. She relaxed slightly. Ledgers were her native country; she could read almost anything if it was formatted correctly.

Except this wasn't formatted correctly at all. There were no columns. No debits, no credits, no initials. Just lines of prose, each entry dated, running down the page like a diary kept by someone who expected to be audited by God.

She turned back to find the first entry. The book fell open again under her hands — literally fell, the spine so broken-in at one place that the pages went there on their own, the way a dog returns to its spot by the fire.

Today's date.

Not 1897's date. Today's date — the day, the month, the year as it currently stood, one hundred and twenty-some years after the book claimed to have been written. Her eyes snagged on it and refused to move, the way a boot snags in mud.

And beneath the date, in that flawless copperplate:

*Mirabel Quill, ledger-keeper, third order, shall balance the accounts of the drowned.*

She read it four times. The words didn't rearrange themselves. They didn't glow or bleed or whisper. They just sat there on the page, patient as inventory, and somehow that was worse. Prophecies were supposed to announce themselves. This one had been filed.

Her mouth had gone dry. She noticed this distantly, the way you notice weather in another country. Below the line about her name, the entry continued:

*She shall descend by the stair under the falls, and the court beneath the mountain shall know her by her green ink.*

Green ink. She looked down at her own right hand, at the faint stain along the side of her middle finger where the pen rested, the mark every guildsman carried and complained about and secretly took pride in. Hers was green because she'd been assigned the reconciliation books. Because the Guildmaster had decided, six years ago, that the runaway apprentice who turned up half-frozen at their door with forged references and quick arithmetic would be trusted with the true accounts.

Had he decided? Or had he *known*?

The rain picked up. Water lipped at the base of her brick, retreated, lipped again. She became aware that she was holding the book too tightly, that her thumb had whitened against the page, and she forced herself to set it down flat on the board and look at it like a professional. Like an entry. What was the provenance? Who had access? When was it acquired?

There was no acquisition record. She would have known; she *made* the acquisition records. Which meant the book had entered the Guild's holdings without passing through her hands, and in six years that had happened exactly once before — with a crate of something the Guildmaster carried down himself at night and never spoke of, and which she had been wise enough not to ask about.

So. Someone had placed this book here tonight, open, facing her chair. Someone who wanted her to find it. Or someone who had placed it here long ago and some other power had finally let it surface — and she didn't know which possibility frightened her more.

She turned the pages backward, looking for the beginning of today's entry, for context, for anything. The entries before it were dense, strange, dated in years that hadn't happened yet from 1897's perspective — the book ran forward past its own binding date like a river ignoring its banks. Entries about fires that hadn't burned yet. About a bridge. About names she didn't recognize, followed, always, by small terrible verbs: *shall sign. shall sell. shall forget.*

Then, two pages before hers, a name she did recognize.

*Dominic Delacroix, second order, shall witness what he does not consent to, and be paid for it.*

Mirabel set the book down very carefully, the way you set down something that might be asleep.

Dominic. Dominic who had taught her the roof-routes when she first arrived, laughing at how slow she was, telling her the trick was to never look down until you'd already committed. Dominic who brought her tea on reconciliation nights without being asked, who remembered she hated honey, who signed his name on documents with a flourish he absolutely knew was ridiculous. Dominic whose signature she had seen ten thousand times in the witness column, because witnessing was his job — second order, contracts and courtesies, the charming face the Guild showed to clients.

*Shall witness what he does not consent to, and be paid for it.*

Paid. The word landed wrong, the way a coin lands wrong when it should be a key. In the Guild's grammar, *paid* was never decorative. Paid meant a transaction. Paid meant two parties and a consideration and a line in a ledger somewhere with her green initials waiting at the bottom of it.

She thought: I will look this up. I will go through the third ledger, the grey one, and I will trace every contract Dominic witnessed, and I will find whatever payment this is talking about, and it will turn out to be nothing. A fee. A stipend. The word *paid* meaning only what it usually means.

She thought this, and simultaneously, in the colder back room of her mind where she kept the things she actually believed, she thought: *the book has never been wrong yet.* She had no way to know that. She had owned the book for eleven minutes. But she knew it the way she knew the flood line on the bricks — completely, and without wanting to.

Above her, the footsteps crossed the floor again and stopped. Directly overhead. Someone standing over the trapdoor, listening to whether she was still down here.

Mirabel closed the prophecy book — her hands were steady, she noted with distant pride; her hands were always steady, that was the first thing the Guild had ever praised about her — and slid it off the board into her lap, beneath the reconciliation ledger, where it lay against her knees like a stone pulled warm from a fire.

The trapdoor hinges groaned. Lamplight spilled down the stair, cut by the rail into gold bars.

"Quill?" Dominic's voice, easy and warm, echoing down the brick throat of the stairwell. "You're missing supper. The Guildmaster's asking after the winter contracts."

She looked at the book in her lap. She looked up at the gold bars of light, and at the shape of him leaning over the rail, silhouetted against the hall's brightness — the shape she knew better than any number in any column.

"Tell him they're nearly done," she called back, and her voice came out level, and she hated how easy the lie was, and she loved him, and the book lay heavy as a verdict against her thighs.

"Nearly done," he repeated, amused. "You're always nearly done. Come up before the soup dies."

His footsteps receded. The trapdoor stayed open behind him — he never closed it; he said cellars made her strange, though she'd been strange long before the cellar.

Mirabel waited until the sound of him faded entirely. Then she opened the grey third ledger to its oldest section, dipped her pen, and began working backward through the years, following the trail of one signature she knew better than her own.

Because the prophecy had one more line, the last one on the page, the one she'd read and reread until the letters stopped looking like letters:

*A soul was sold beneath the mountain, and the Guild of Impossible Things brokered the sale.*

She was going to find the entry. She was going to find the price, the parties, the date — and the witness.

And she was going to start with Dominic Delacroix.
