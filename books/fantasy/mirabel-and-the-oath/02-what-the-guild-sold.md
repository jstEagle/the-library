## 2. What the Guild Sold

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its accounts the way a surgeon keeps instruments: clean, ordered, and out of sight until needed. Mirabel had learned the system in her first month — every impossible thing entered twice, once as it was promised and once as it was paid for, because in her experience the gap between those two columns was where people got hurt.

She worked backward from tonight.

That was the trick she'd taught herself, back when she was still an apprentice with ink-stained cuffs and a master who checked her sums twice: when a ledger lies to you, don't read it forward. Read it backward. Lies are built to be believed going forward. Going backward, they have to keep their shape, and most of them can't.

So she started with today's date, written in the margin of a book that predated her grandmother, and she followed it like a thread into the dark.

The entry for tonight was not in the prophecy book. It was in the Guild's own ledger, the great leather-bound one chained to the desk in the flooded cellar-office, and it was written in the Guildmaster's hand — that beautiful, terrible copperplate he used for contracts. *One soul, minor, female, delivered under the mountain. Consideration received: see winter accounts, 1897.*

1897.

Mirabel sat very still. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above her, water moved through the pipes of the guildhouse the way blood moves through a body, and she thought: this building is alive with other people's bargains, and I have been keeping its books for three years without once asking what was in them.

*See winter accounts, 1897.* She turned pages. The paper was old enough to crackle, old enough to smell of dust and tallow and something faintly sweet, like flowers left too long in a closed room. The winter accounts were there, exactly where they should be, kept by a ledger-keeper dead before Mirabel was born. A plague winter. She knew about it vaguely — the Guild had bought grain, bought medicine, bought passage out of the city for families who could pay. Everyone in the trade knew the story. It was told as a triumph.

The consideration column read: *Twelve casks of fever-tincture, forty bushels of rye, passage for nine, and one soul (minor) — received from Odile Vasse, river-house, in exchange for the survival of her household through the plague season. Brokered by the Guild of Impossible Things. Witnessed:*

And then a signature.

Mirabel knew that signature. She had watched it being made a hundred times, on receipts and writs and the little yellow slips apprentices signed when they borrowed tools. A looping D, a flourish like a bird taking off.

Dominic Delacroix.

But that was impossible. The entry was dated 1897. Dominic was twenty-six years old. Dominic had been *born* in —

She checked. Of course she checked; she was a ledger-keeper, checking was her whole religion. The birth records in the front of the ledger, the ones she'd entered herself in her first week. Dominic Delacroix, admitted to the Guild at age six, orphaned, no family, no history. Admitted the same year the plague winter ended.

He would have been five years old in 1897. Five years old, an orphan already or nearly, standing in some smoke-filled office while grown men traded a child's soul across a table — and signing his name to it.

*Witnessed,* the entry said. Not *brokered*. Not *sold*. Witnessed. As if witnessing were nothing. As if a child's scrawled name on a monstrous page were just another formality, another tool signed out, another receipt.

Mirabel put both hands flat on the desk to stop them shaking.

There was more. There was always more; ledgers are honest that way, they cannot help accumulating. Follow-up entries, year after year, in different hands but always the same careful style. *Annual maintenance fee rendered to the court beneath the mountain, per contract 1897-V.* *Soul confirmed held, condition stable.* *Condition stable.* *Condition stable.* Every year, like rent. Like a subscription. The Guild had been paying storage on a stolen child's soul for over a century, and the fees were drawn from the general fund, which meant —

Which meant she had balanced those books. Which meant some part of that money had passed through *her* hands, tidy and anonymous, buried in a line item called *miscellaneous obligations* that she had never once questioned because the Guildmaster's books were always perfect and perfect books do not invite questions.

She thought of the prophecy book, open to today's date, bearing her name. Someone had known. Someone had known for a hundred years, and had let her balance these entries, and had said nothing.

The lamp guttered. In the flooded half of the cellar, where the black water stood mirror-still between the stone piers, her reflection looked back at her looking pale and young and suddenly very far from home.

* * *

She found him in the map room, because he was always in the map room in the evenings, correcting coastlines that didn't need correcting. That was the thing about Dominic — he filled his hands so they wouldn't have to hold anything heavier. He looked up when she came in, and smiled, and the smile was the same smile it had always been, warm and crooked and entirely unguarded, and that was somehow the worst part. He didn't know yet that she knew. For one more moment, they were still who they had been at dinner.

"Dominic," she said, and heard her own voice come out flat as a ruled line. "Contract 1897-V."

The smile stayed on his face but something behind it went out, like a lamp turned down to a coal. He set down his pen. He did not ask how she'd found it, or what she meant, or whether she was sure. That was the second worst part. He wasn't surprised.

"Where did you see it?" he asked quietly.

"The cellar. The main ledger." She held up the notebook she'd brought, though she hadn't written anything in it; her hands had refused. "I've been balancing that account for three years. Three years, Dominic. Miscellaneous obligations. You let me touch it."

"I didn't let you do anything. I'm not the one who decides what you work on."

"No," she agreed. "You're just the one who signed it."

He flinched. Actually flinched, a small motion of the shoulders, quickly mastered, and she hated that she noticed it, hated that some traitor part of her catalogued it the way she catalogued everything — *witness discomfort, note for later review* — instead of simply hating him cleanly, which would have been so much easier.

"You were five years old," she said. "How does a five-year-old witness a soul-sale?"

"The Guildmaster brought me." His voice was level, careful, the voice he used for explaining map projections to new apprentices. "He said my name had to be on it. He never said why. He never says why anything."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have." He stood, and crossed the room, and stopped an arm's length away — close enough to touch, far enough that touching would be a choice. "Mirabel. Ask me anything else. Anything. I'll answer it straight."

"Did you know what you were signing?"

"Yes."

"Did you know what it *was*?"

A pause. Long enough to be its own answer.

"A child's soul," Mirabel said. "Sold to buy rye and medicine. And then maintained, year after year, on fees I reconciled. And nobody — not the Guildmaster, not you, not anyone in this house full of people who call themselves my family — thought I should know what I was keeping books for."

"We protect each other here. That's the whole point of the Guild. That's why you came to us—"

"Don't." Her voice cracked on it and she pressed on anyway, because if she stopped she would never start again. "Don't tell me what the Guild is. I sleep under its roof. I love—" She stopped. Recalibrated. Ruled a line through the sentence. "I trusted you. More than anyone alive. And you sat across from me at dinner last night and passed me the salt and said nothing."

"What would you have had me say?" He spread his hands, and she saw they weren't steady either. "That I signed a piece of paper when I was five because the only man who ever fed me told me to? That I've spent twenty years not asking what happened to the child, because the one time I asked, he looked at me the way he looks at debtors?" He laughed, short and joyless. "You want me to explain? I can't. I was sold too, Mirabel. Just more slowly. Just in installments."

The words landed somewhere beneath her ribs and stayed there.

Because that was the trap of it, the ugly arithmetic of it: she believed him. That was the whole catastrophe. She stood there holding a century of evidence against him and she *believed* him, believed the flinch and the unsteady hands and the bitterness worn smooth by two decades of swallowing it, and she could not make the belief and the betrayal fit inside her at the same time. They were both true. Ledgers taught you that eventually — two columns, both accurate, summing to something unbearable.

"The child," she said. "The Vasse child. Is she alive?"

Dominic went still in a new way. "Why?"

"Answer the question."

"Mirabel—"

"*Is she alive?*"

He looked at her for a long moment, and whatever he saw in her face made him stop choosing his words. "Yes," he said. "Breathing. Obedient. Empty. The mother keeps her by the river. And every year the Guild pays the court beneath the mountain to keep it that way, and every year I sign the remittance, and every year I tell myself that's not the same as signing the first time." He picked up his pen and put it down again. "It's the same. I know it's the same."

Outside, rain had started against the high windows of the map room, soft as fingertips. Somewhere below them, the pipes carried water through the dark, and beneath the water, beneath the falls, beneath the whole weight of the ordinary world, something under the mountain was holding a child's soul in inventory, condition stable, century after century.

Mirabel closed her notebook.

"I'm going to the river-house tomorrow," she said.

Dominic's head came up sharply. "That's not — Mirabel, you can't. The writs alone, the wardens—"

"Then I'll forge one. I keep the blank stock. I know the seals better than the seal-maker does." She said it calmly, almost pleasantly, and watched him understand, watch by watch, exactly how much she had learned in three years of keeping the Guild's books. "You witnessed the sale, Dominic. Consider coming to witness what it bought."

She turned for the door, and his voice caught her at the threshold — quiet, urgent, stripped of all its charm:

"If you go down there, they'll already know your name."

She stopped. Did not turn around.

"I know," she said. "It's in the book."
