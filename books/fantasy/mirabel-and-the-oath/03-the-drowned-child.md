## 3. The Drowned Child

Odile Vasse knew the exact hour her daughter had come back wrong.

It was the fourth of Frostmonth, in the plague winter, and she had sat by the bed for three days doing the arithmetic mothers do — counting breaths, counting the fever's climb, counting the coins she did not have. The physician wanted silver. The apothecary wanted silver. The Guild man who came to the door in his grey coat with his kind, tired eyes wanted something else entirely, and he named it plainly, which Odile would later understand was a courtesy. Most buyers lie.

*The child will live,* he said. *All of her that can be lived without.*

She signed because there was no other paper to sign. She signed because Tomas was already buried under the frozen ground behind the church, because Anouk was the last of five, because grief is a currency too and she was rich in it.

And on the morning of the fourth of Frostmonth, the fever broke like a held note, and Anouk opened her eyes, and they were brown, and they were clear, and they were *empty*, the way a house is empty when the family has gone but the furniture remains.

Odile told herself it was the illness. It is what mothers tell themselves; it is practically a lullaby. She fed her daughter broth and the girl swallowed when told to swallow. She dressed her and the girl lifted her arms when told to lift them. She said *I love you* and Anouk looked at her with those brown, clear, furnished rooms of eyes and said, obediently, *I love you too* — in exactly the tone she'd been taught, at exactly the right moment, the way a clock says the hour.

A clock does not love you. A clock only agrees with you about the time.

Three years now. Anouk ate what was put before her, slept when put to bed, never lied, never cried, never once asked for anything — not a sweet, not a story, not her mother's hand in the dark. Other children woke screaming from nightmares. Anouk did not dream. Odile had watched her sleep whole nights through, waiting for a twitch, a murmur, a single flicker behind the lids, and gotten nothing. Her daughter slept like an unlit lamp.

The river-house smelled of smoke and wet wool, as it always had. Odile stood at the window watching the water go by — the same water that had nearly taken the girl, though the Guild man swore the bargain predated any drowning, that souls are sold long before bodies fail, that the fever was merely the invoice arriving. She had stopped believing in coincidences around the time she started believing in ledgers.

There was a knock at the door. Not the Guild man's knock — she would know that knock in her grave, soft and apologetic, a knock that was sorry before it asked. This one was younger. Quicker. Nervous.

She opened it on a girl of perhaps nineteen, rain-beaded cloak, ink-stained fingers, a satchel slung across her body like a soldier's strap. She had the look of someone who had rehearsed a speech on the walk over and was now discovering the speech did not fit the doorway.

"Madame Vasse," the girl said. "My name is Mirabel Quill. I keep accounts for the Guild of Impossible Things."

Something in Odile's chest went very still and very cold, the way the river went still just before it froze.

"Accounts," she repeated.

"I found an entry," the girl said. "Dated three winters back. I'm not — " She stopped. Started again, and this time the rehearsed speech was gone entirely, and what was underneath was rawer and truer. "I'm not here on the Guild's business. I'm here against it, if that makes sense. I need to see her."

"See who?"

"You know who, madame." And the girl's voice cracked on it, just slightly, just enough. "Please. I've read the line eleven hundred times. I need to see what it *made*."

Odile should have shut the door. Every instinct of three hard winters said shut the door, bar it, put the kettle on and pretend the world outside was still the ordinary world. But the girl stood there with ink on her hands and guilt already written across her face like a first draft, and Odile thought: *she doesn't know yet. She knows the entry. She doesn't know the entry.*

So she stepped aside, and let the accounting come inside.

* * *

Mirabel had prepared herself for a sickroom. That was the image the ledger had built in her head over two sleepless nights: a frail child, pale, maybe scarred by the fever, something she could look at and grieve and then carry back up the hill as evidence. Evidence was manageable. Evidence fit in a satchel next to forged writs and stolen prophecy books.

Anouk Vasse was not sick. Anouk Vasse was sitting at the kitchen table copying letters onto slate, because her mother set her a task every day the way you'd wind a clock, and she was doing it perfectly. Round, careful letters. *A B C D.* Her hand never wavered. Her posture never slumped. She was perhaps eight years old and she wrote like a clerk of forty years' standing.

"Anouk," Odile said gently, "this is Mirabel. She's come to visit."

Anouk looked up. Brown eyes, clear, level, courteous. "Hello, Mirabel," she said. "Welcome to our house."

Every word correct. Every word *placed*. And behind them — nothing. Mirabel had spent her childhood reading faces in the orphanage, learning which matron's mood meant extra bread and which meant a locked cupboard, and she knew within four seconds that there was nothing in this child's face to read. No wariness of strangers, no curiosity, no flicker of *who is this woman in my kitchen*. Just the response, delivered like change from a till.

"Hello, Anouk," Mirabel managed. Her voice came out strange.

"She won't offer you tea," Odile said quietly. "Not because she's rude. Because it won't occur to her. Occurring to things — that's part of what went." She said it flatly, the way you recite a debt you've paid so many times the words have worn smooth. "Sit down, Miss Quill. You came a long way down a hill to look. So look."

Mirabel sat. She watched the child write her letters. She watched her finish the slate, and wait — not restlessly, not patiently, just *wait*, the way furniture waits — until Odile said, "You may wipe it now, love," and Anouk wiped it, and began again. *A B C D.*

"The ledger says the Guild brokered the sale," Mirabel said. Her throat was dry. "It says the buyer was the court beneath the mountain. It says the consideration was — " She couldn't say it. She made herself say it. "Survival through the plague winter. Medicine. Food. The physician's fee."

"All received," Odile said. "Itemized. I could show you the receipts. Your Guild keeps excellent records, Miss Quill. Right up until the record is a child." She turned from the window. "Do you want to know the worst part? He was kind about it. The grey-coat man. He took off his hat. He said he hated this part of the work. He said the alternative was a grave behind the church next to her father, and he was right, and I have hated him for three years for being right."

"There was a witness," Mirabel said. "On the contract. A signature."

"I never learned his name. Young. Polite. He wouldn't meet my eyes." Odile's gaze settled on Mirabel with terrible gentleness. "Does that sound like anyone you know?"

Mirabel's hands were flat on the table, pressing down, as if the table might otherwise tip. She thought of Dominic's signature — she had traced it last night with one finger, over and over, the confident looping D, the way he signed everything, menus, jokes, the margins of her ledgers. *Witnessed and attested, D. Delacroix.* She had loved that signature. She had fallen asleep looking at it more than once, ink-drunk, smiling.

"He didn't explain," she said. "When I confronted him. He didn't deny it either. He just — stood there."

"They're good at standing there," Odile said. "It's in the coat, I think. Comes with the grey."

Anouk finished her second slate. Waited. Odile nodded, and the girl wiped it, and began again, and Mirabel watched the small perfect hand move and felt something in her curdle from guilt into something harder and more useful. Guilt was a feeling. This was going to be a *line item*.

"What does she do all day?" Mirabel asked. "Does she play?"

"She'll play if you show her how. She'll do anything if you show her how. That's the horror of it, Miss Quill — she's *obedient*. The physician called her a miracle. The neighbors call her the best child on the lane. Never tantrums, never lies, never steals jam from the pantry." Odile laughed, one dry note with no floor under it. "Everyone tells me how lucky I am. Sometimes I want to shake them. Sometimes I want to shake *her*, just to see if anything shakes loose. Isn't that a thing to confess to a stranger? Three years, and I have never once seen my daughter want anything. Wanting is the soul, I think. Or wanting is where the soul used to sit, like a dent in a cushion."

Mirabel looked at the child. Anouk, sensing attention — or rather, sensing that attention required acknowledgment — looked back, pleasant and vacant as a painted portrait, and Mirabel thought: *somewhere under this mountain is the rest of her. The wanting. The dreaming. The eight-year-old who cries at nightmares and steals jam.* Filed. Shelved. Kept.

The queen of kept things. The phrase surfaced in her mind unbidden, attached to no memory she owned, and she shivered.

"Madame Vasse," she said. "I'm going to get her back."

Odile went very still. "That's not possible."

"I know it's not possible. I work at the Guild of Impossible Things; impossibility is our entire inventory." Mirabel heard her own voice steadying, hardening, becoming something with a spine in it. "But I found my own name in a book a hundred years old, madame, with tonight's date written beside it, and I have decided that if the universe insists on keeping accounts, then it can bloody well honor its obligations. I'm the ledger-keeper. I know how debts work. And this one," she said, looking at the small round letters, *A B C D*, "is overdue."

For the first time in three years, Odile Vasse wept in front of a stranger — quickly, furiously, wiping her face with her wrist like a woman putting out a fire.

At the door, wrapping her cloak against the rain, Mirabel paused. Behind her, the slate scratched on. *A B C D.* She turned back once, and caught Anouk looking at her — not curious, not wondering, just recording, filing the visitor away among the household objects — and Mirabel smiled at her, and got the smile back, correct and placed, and walked out into the rain with her nails bitten half to the quick.

The stair under the falls opened at moonrise. She had the forged writ in her satchel, the prophecy book against her ribs, and one name burning in her head like a brand: not the queen's, not the child's.

*D. Delacroix. Witnessed and attested.*

Whatever he'd done, whatever he hadn't explained — he was going to stand there while she asked him again. And this time, she was bringing the child's empty eyes with her as evidence.

She just had to get past the wardens first.
