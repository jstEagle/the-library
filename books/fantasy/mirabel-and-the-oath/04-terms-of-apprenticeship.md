## 4. Terms of Apprenticeship

The writ burned green.

Dominic held it over the candle in the Guildmaster's study and watched his own name curl and blacken — *Dominic Delacroix, protected of the Guild of Impossible Things* — the letters going the way of all things the Guild decided to stop keeping. The paper didn't smell like smoke. It smelled like the cellar below the falls, like cold stone and old water, like the place he was about to go down into with no protection at all for the first time in nineteen years.

He had signed a document in that place once. He remembered the weight of the pen.

He remembered everything, which was the problem. Mirabel was about to learn that remembering was not the same as being innocent.

* * *

He had been nine years old when the Guild found him, though *found* was a generous word for what happened at the fish market on the day the fever took the last of the Delacroixes. He'd been sitting on an overturned crate counting herring scales into piles because counting was the only thing left that made sense, when a man in a grey coat crouched down to his level and said:

"You're doing arithmetic."

"I'm counting," Dominic said.

"Counting is arithmetic done honestly." The man had a voice like a ledger closing — soft, final, a little dusty. "Can you do dishonest arithmetic too? Because that's where the money is."

That was Aldous Vane, Guildmaster of the Guild of Impossible Things, and Dominic would spend the rest of his childhood learning that the man had exactly two faces and used them both on purpose: the kind one for the world, and the other one — patient, precise, faintly disappointed — for the people who belonged to him.

Dominic belonged to him within the hour. There was a document. There was always a document. He signed it with a fist around the pen because he didn't know how to hold one yet, and Aldous guided his hand the way you'd guide a child learning to write his own name, which, in fairness, is what he was doing.

*Terms of Apprenticeship.* Room, board, schooling, protection. In exchange: service, secrecy, and the understanding — written in small, beautiful script — that some debts are inherited rather than incurred, and that the Guild would be generous about which ones it chose to collect.

He was nine. He read the word *protection* four times and never made it to the small script.

* * *

Presently, the ash of his writ drifted onto the desk, and Dominic brushed it into his palm like something he might need later as evidence against himself.

Mirabel had been gone six hours. Long enough to forge a writ — he'd taught her the seal himself, God help him, three winters ago, as a game, as a flirtation, *here's how you'd rob us if you ever wanted to* — long enough to reach the stair under the river, if she moved fast and the wardens were lazy. The wardens were always lazy after the tide turned. He'd told her that too.

He kept handing her the tools of his own ruin and calling it love.

Aldous stood at the window with his back turned, which was how he did his most dangerous thinking. Nineteen years of that back. Nineteen years of watching those shoulders decide things.

"You'll bring her up before the new moon," Aldous said. Not a request. Requests were for people who could leave.

"If she'll come," Dominic said.

"She'll come. She's a ledger-keeper. Ledgers want balancing." He turned then, and his face was the second face, the precise one, the one that had never once been disappointed in Dominic without also being right. "And you'll want to hurry, boy. You know what's under the mountain keeps its books better than we do."

"I know what's under the mountain," Dominic said. "I've read the entry."

Something flickered across the Guildmaster's face — quick, quickly buried. Regret, maybe. Or inventory. With Aldous it was hard to tell the difference, and Dominic had stopped trying years ago, because trying was how they got you. You looked for the father in the jailer and found enough of him to stay.

"Go, then," Aldous said gently, and that gentleness was the cruelest trick of all, because it still worked. It still worked, standing there with a burnt writ in his fist and a girl he loved walking into the dark with his signature in her pocket like a knife.

* * *

The winter of the plague, Dominic was eleven.

He remembered it the way you remember a fever — in patches, wrong-angled. The city burning its dead in the tannery yards because the ground was full. The Guild house sealed, salt across every threshold, and inside it forty children who had nowhere else to be, eating thin soup and learning impossible bookkeeping while the bells rang and rang and would not stop ringing.

Aldous fed them. That was the fact at the bottom of everything, the fact Dominic had built his whole life on top of like a floor: when the city ate its children, the Guildmaster opened his stores and did not. Forty children alive who should have been dead. You could forgive a man almost anything with a floor like that under your feet.

You could forgive him almost anything, it turned out. Almost.

Because there were forty-one children that winter, and the forty-first was not at the Guild house. She was in a river-house downstream, in a family named Odile — no, that wasn't right either; Odile was the mother's name, Mirabel had said it like a wound — a family that had come to the Guild in the worst week of the plague with nothing left to sell except the thing nobody sells.

A soul. A child's soul. To the court beneath the mountain, through the Guild's brokerage, for a price that bought grain, medicine, and one more winter for everyone else.

Dominic hadn't known. That was what he'd told himself for ten years. He was eleven; he was mopping the entry hall; the adults handled contracts.

But he'd seen them come in. He remembered that much, and it was the patch of the fever that never healed: a woman with river-mud to her knees holding a little girl by the hand, and the little girl looking around the Guild hall with bright, ordinary curiosity, and Aldous kneeling down to the child's height — he was so good at that, he'd done it for Dominic once — and saying something soft and courteous that Dominic couldn't hear from the doorway, and the mother crying without any sound at all.

And then the paperwork. There was always paperwork.

* * *

Sixteen years old, and the Guildmaster called him into the study and put a contract in front of him and said, "Witness this."

It was a brokerage. He understood brokerages by then; he was the best apprentice the Guild had produced in a generation, and everyone said so, and the saying-so was another chain, finer than the first but the same metal. Witnessing was simple work. You confirmed identities, confirmed consideration, confirmed consent, and signed.

The parties were listed. One party was the Court Beneath the Mountain, represented by its duly appointed herald, who sat across the desk wearing a grey coat and a face like weather coming. The other party was a family name Dominic knew from the ledgers. Downstream. River-house.

The subject of sale was described in the Guild's careful language: *one soul, minor, female, sound and unclaimed.*

Sound and unclaimed. Like a horse. Like a herring.

"Why me?" Dominic asked. His voice came out steady, which he would be grateful for and ashamed of for the rest of his life. "Why am I witnessing?"

"Because you're my best hand," Aldous said, "and because a witness must be someone whose signature means something." A pause, and then, softer, the father's voice, the trapdoor opening: "And because I want you to see what we do here, Dominic. All of it. I won't have you innocent. Innocence is a debt waiting to be collected, and I don't let anyone owe on my watch."

So it was a lesson. So it was a test. So it was both, and he passed, and passing meant signing.

He read the whole contract. Every clause. He was sixteen and brilliant and he read every word, and every word was lawful, and lawful was the whole of what the Guild promised anyone — not good, not kind, just *lawful*, lawful the way a knife is sharp. And at the bottom, above the line marked WITNESS, he wrote his name in his best hand, the hand Aldous had guided when he was nine, and the pen sounded exactly like a pen, which is the terrible thing about pens. They never sound like anything.

The herald took the copy below the mountain. The mother took her price home. The child — Dominic learned later her name was Ede, seven years old, who liked eel soup and could skip a stone five times — went home too, breathing, obedient, empty-eyed, and stayed that way through the winter that killed half the city, because her family lived.

Forty-one children alive who should have been dead. You could build a floor on that. He had. He'd stood on it for ten years and called it ground.

* * *

Now he stood at the head of the stair under the falls with no writ, no protection, and no excuse, and the water roared down beside the steps like applause for something awful.

He'd gone to Mirabel's room before leaving. Her bed unslept-in, her good boots gone, and on her desk the prophecy book open to today's page, because she'd wanted him to find it — or because some part of her had needed one person in the Guild to already know and say so out loud, and he hadn't been there, and now the page lay open like an accusation with his own name buried somewhere in the century-old ink.

He hadn't read the last line. He should have read the last line. Ledger-men read to the bottom; that was the first rule; Aldous taught it the day he taught him to hold a pen.

He started down the stairs.

The dark came up to meet him like it had been keeping his place, and somewhere beneath the mountain a queen who kept everything turned a page, and waited to be paid.
