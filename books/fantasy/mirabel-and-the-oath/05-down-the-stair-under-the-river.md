## 5. Down the Stair Under the River

The writ took Mirabel three hours to forge, and she was ashamed of how easy it was.

That was the thing nobody told you about a life in ledgers: after enough years of copying other people's hands, you stopped having one of your own. She sat in the flooded cellar-office with the Guild's seal-wax warming over her candle, practicing the Guildmaster's signature on scrap paper until it came out of her pen without hesitation — the heavy downstroke, the little hitch where his hand had been broken in '09 and never set right. She had watched him sign things for six years. She could have done it in her sleep. Perhaps, some nights, she had.

*By order of the Guild of Impossible Things,* the forgery read, *the bearer is licensed to conduct an audit of holdings beneath the falls, per clause fourteen of the Founding Accord.*

Clause fourteen was real. She'd checked. It was just that nobody had invoked it in ninety years, because nobody wanted to audit what the Guild kept under the mountain, and the wardens at the stair had orders older than their teeth: *no one goes down.* But orders older than teeth were still younger than clauses, and a forged writ citing a real clause would make them hesitate exactly long enough.

She sealed it, blotted it, and told herself the shaking in her hands was the cold.

* * *

The stair began behind the falls.

Everyone in Millbrook knew the falls — the white rope of water that fell two hundred feet off the mountain's shoulder and made the town rich, or at least made the mills rich. What everyone in Millbrook did *not* know was that there was a door in the rock behind the curtain of water, and that the Guild held the key, and that once every generation someone went down and did not come back up, and the Guild entered it into the books as *attrition, natural.*

Mirabel went at dusk, when the mill workers were walking home with their heads down against the spray. She wore her oilskin and carried a lantern, a ledger, and the writ folded against her heart like a letter from someone who loved her.

The passage behind the falls was narrow and roaring and wet to the waist within ten steps. Then the rock turned, and the sound of the river cut off as if a door had closed on it, and there was the stair.

It was older than the Guild. That was the first thing she understood, standing at the top of it with her lantern raised. The steps were worn hollow in the middle, dished by feet, and not guild boots — the wear was too old, too smooth, the way stone gets when centuries of people have gone somewhere believing it matters. The Guild hadn't built this. The Guild had found it, the way it found everything: something impossible already existing, and put its name on the deed.

The walls were carved. She saw it as the lantern light moved — figures going down the stair ahead of her, carved into the living rock, thousands of them, a procession descending into the dark. Men and women and children, animals, carts, coffins, cradles. All of them going down. None of them carved coming back up.

She counted her heartbeat until it slowed, and then she started down.

* * *

The wardens were waiting at the third landing.

She heard them before she saw them — a wet clicking, like knuckles cracking underwater — and raised her lantern. They stood on the stairs below her, three shapes that might once have been men, in warden coats gone green with damp, their faces the color and texture of things that live under stones. Their eyes caught the lantern light like coins at the bottom of a well.

"Halt," said the tallest, in a voice like silt settling.

Mirabel held out the writ. Her hand, she noted distantly, was steady. Six years of handing documents across desks will do that; your hands learn to be calm even when the rest of you is screaming.

"Guild business," she said. "Audit of holdings. Clause fourteen."

The tall one took the paper. It unfolded it with fingers that had too many joints, and read — she watched its eyes move, left to right, left to right, and thought absurdly, *I should have charged more for the calligraphy.* The silence stretched. Water dripped somewhere in the dark above them, patient as a clock.

"The seal is true," the warden said at last, and it sounded disappointed.

"The signature is true," Mirabel said. "Check the hitch. Left side."

It checked the hitch. Its coin-eyes flicked up at her, and for one terrible moment she saw understanding move through that stony face — not suspicion, exactly. Recognition. The look of a creature that has guarded a door for a hundred years and knows precisely which names are allowed past it, and has just seen one of them.

"You," it said. "You're expected."

"I'm sorry?"

But the wardens were already stepping aside, pressing themselves against the wall with a deference that was worse than any challenge, and the tall one folded her forgery with strange care and handed it back like a relic.

"Go down, keeper," it said. "You are late by one hundred years, but the court keeps excellent accounts. It will have noted the delay."

Mirabel took the writ. Her legs carried her down the next flight before her mind agreed to it.

*Expected.* Someone had written her name in a book in 1897. Someone had signed for a child's soul while Dominic stood witness. And now the doorkeepers of the under-court looked at her like a delivery finally arrived.

She kept walking, because the only way out was through, and because — she admitted it to herself somewhere around the five hundredth step — because the book had said tonight, and tonight was nearly spent, and if prophecy kept appointments then so did she.

* * *

The air changed at the bottom.

She felt it before she saw anything: a warmth, a hush, the pressure of a great interior space, like stepping into a cathedral after years outdoors. Her lantern flame leaned sideways, drawn toward something, and she let it lead.

The stair ended at a threshold of black marble veined with gold — no, not gold, she realized, kneeling to touch it. Ink. The veins in the marble were ink, still wet after a century, still moving, slow as blood through a heart. Words, maybe, if you could read a language that wrote itself.

Beyond the threshold, the under-court opened up around her, and Mirabel Quill, who had spent six years believing she understood what the world contained, stood very still and let it break her understanding.

It was a city. It was a *hoard*. Caverns vaulted high as weather, lit by lamps that burned without oil, and everywhere — everywhere — *things*. Shelves rose out of the dark in ranges like cliffs, miles of shelving, and on the shelves: everything bought and forgotten. Wedding rings still warm. Letters unopened for a hundred years, wax seals sweating. A carousel horse, mid-gallop, dusted with real confetti. Names in jars. First words, bottled. A row of umbrellas, each one labeled with the name of the person who'd lost it and the exact date they'd stopped looking.

And among the shelves, moving on errands of their own, the court's people — if they were people. Pale clerks with ink-stained fingers. Porters carrying crates marked in a script that hurt to read directly. Somewhere, faintly, a bell rang the hour, though which hour it rang she couldn't say.

Everything here was catalogued. She understood that viscerally, professionally, the way you understand a well-run office. This was a ledger the size of a world, and it was *balanced*, and some deep clerkish part of her — the part that had saved her when she ran away from her apprenticeship with nothing but a pen — responded to it with horror and admiration in equal measure.

A pale clerk drifted near, glanced at her, and bowed.

"Keeper Quill," it said. "Your shelf is prepared."

"My *shelf?*"

But it had already glided away, and other clerks were looking now, turning toward her with soft, courteous attention, and she realized the whole court was adjusting itself around her arrival the way water adjusts around a dropped stone. Word of her was moving through the caverns faster than she walked. Everyone knew. Everything knew.

Even the shelves seemed to know. As she passed, following the direction her lantern flame insisted on, she heard it — faintly, from the ranges of kept things, a whispering, a rustling, like pages turning in a windless room. And threaded through it, over and over, in a voice like dry paper:

*Mirabel. Mirabel Quill. Mirabel, Mirabel, come to be filed.*

She gripped her ledger tighter and walked faster.

* * *

The throne room announced itself by smell: candlewax and old paper and, underneath, the green smell of deep water. Two doors of black wood stood open at the end of the final gallery, and between them, on a dais of stacked ledgers — actual ledgers, thousands of them, the spines facing out like brickwork — sat the queen.

Mirabel had expected a monster. What she saw was a woman of indeterminate age dressed in grey silk, sewing. A hoop of fabric lay across her knees, and her needle flashed in and out, unhurried, and she did not look up until Mirabel had crossed half the floor.

Then she looked up, and her eyes were the falls at night: white water in the dark, beautiful and completely indifferent to what fell into them.

"There you are," the queen said, pleasantly. "One hundred years late. Sit down, child. We have terms to discuss, and I do hate discussing terms standing up — it makes everyone reach for weapons."

Mirabel did not sit. "I've come about a soul. A child's. Sold to this court in—" she checked herself, appalled that she knew the number by heart— "in the winter of the plague year. Brokered by the Guild of Impossible Things. Witnessed by Dominic Delacroix."

Something moved across the queen's face that was almost, almost fondness.

"Oh, you *have* been reading," she said. She set aside her sewing and rose, and her shadow rose with her, and her shadow was much taller than she was, and had too many arms, and each arm held a different document. "Yes. The drowned child. Shelf nine thousand and forty, aisle of the unspeakably young. Lovely specimen. Very quiet." She smiled, and her teeth were small and even and perfect as typeset letters. "You want her back."

"I want her returned to her mother."

"Mm. And what will you give me for her?" The queen drifted closer, shadow-arms fanning their documents like a dealer's deck. "You've brought nothing, keeper. No coin. No years. No name — well." A pause, delicate as a needle entering cloth. "You brought your name. It's been on my shelf since 1897. Did you know that? Your page. Your date. Tonight." Her white-water eyes glittered. "I've been keeping it warm for you."

The whispering from the shelves beyond the doors rose, all at once, into something almost like a cheer.

*Mirabel. Mirabel Quill. Come to be filed.*

Mirabel stood in the doorway of the throne room of everything bought and forgotten, holding a forged writ and a stolen truth, and understood with perfect clarity that she had walked exactly as far as the prophecy intended — and that the queen was about to tell her the price.

"Tell me the terms," she said.

The queen's smile widened by one perfect tooth.

"With pleasure," she said. "I do so love an audience that's already paid."
