## 6. The Queen of Kept Things

The stair ended where the river's sound ended.

That was the first thing I understood about the under-court: it was quiet in the way a vault is quiet, not the way a meadow is. The water still moved somewhere below us — I could feel it in the soles of my boots, a low patient thrumming, like something breathing through stone — but its voice had been taken from it, or filed away, or shelved. Everything down here was shelved.

I stood at the mouth of a hall so long I couldn't see the end of it, and every inch of both walls was shelf.

Not wood. The shelves grew out of the rock itself, pale as bone and veined with something that caught my lantern-light and held it a half-second too long, the way a mirror holds your face after you've turned away. And on the shelves — things. I want to say objects, but that word is too kind to what I saw. A child's shoe, laced neatly, standing upright with no foot in it. A wedding ring on a spool of thread. A jar of names — actual names, written on slips of paper so old they'd gone the color of tea, and when I passed close enough one of them stirred against the glass like a moth.

"You'll want to mind the drafts," said a voice behind me. "They unsettle the collection."

I turned too fast and my lantern swung wild. She had not been there. I would swear to my grave she had not been there, and then I would remember that down here, *there* was a negotiable term.

The queen of the under-court was small. That was the second thing I understood, and it took me longer to understand than the silence did. I had imagined — I don't know what I'd imagined. Vastness. Antlers. Something out of the oldest pages of the prophecy book, all teeth and dark water. Instead she was barely taller than me, wrapped in a shawl the color of deep water over slate, her hair silvered and pinned with what looked, absurdly, like ledger pins. Her face was lined the way an old book's spine is lined: from being opened often, and carefully, by hands that knew its value.

She was knitting. That was the detail that nearly broke me. She stood in her hall of stolen everything with two needles clicking softly, working some grey yarn that came off a spool beside her and went back onto another spool ahead of her, unending, and I understood with a cold slide in my stomach that I was looking at the same thread going around forever.

"Mirabel Quill," she said. My name in her mouth sounded like an entry already posted. "Ledger-keeper. Apprentice, formerly. Runaway, formerly." She smiled, and it was a warm smile, a grandmother's smile, and that was the worst thing yet. "You balance accounts beautifully. We've read your work."

"The Guild's ledgers are sealed," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved.

"Sealed things are simply kept things you haven't shelved yet." She set down her needles — they did not stop clicking; I made myself stop listening for them — and gestured down the hall with one small hand. "Come. You've come such a long way down for something. It would be discourteous of me not to show you the house. And you strike me as a girl who appreciates courtesy."

I should have run. I have thought about that many nights since — whether I could have run, whether the stair would even have let me back up. But the truth is simpler and more shameful: I wanted the tour. Some part of me that had spent three years among the Guild's locked cabinets and whispered inventories looked at those endless shelves and felt *at home*, and I hated that part of myself, and I followed it anyway.

* * *

She walked slowly, because she wanted me to look.

"This wing is grief," she said, as if announcing a room's purpose in any great house. "Grief keeps poorly up above. It leaks. It gets mistaken for love, which ruins its value entirely. Down here we keep it dry." On the shelves: letters never sent, tied in bundles by decade. A coat, child-sized, sleeves worn soft at the cuffs. An entire shelf of last words, each one in a little labeled box, and I did not read the labels, because I knew some of the handwriting.

"And this?" I asked, at a section where the shelves held nothing visible at all — just faint impressions, like dust where a book once sat.

"Promises," she said. "The ones people meant when they made them. Those are the rarest stock. Most promises arrive here already broken, and broken promises are common as gravel. But a promise kept until the keeper died — ah." She touched one empty impression with one finger, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping child. "We display those."

"You keep them empty?"

"We keep them *kept*. The promise is still being honored somewhere above, by someone who doesn't know they're doing it. When they stop—" She shrugged, a small economical motion. "Something else goes on the shelf. There is always something else."

The horror of it arrived late, the way cold water arrives — you're in it before you feel it. Every kindness anyone had ever done me, every promise Dominic had ever made me, might be sitting on these shelves right now, dusted and catalogued, and I would never know which ones, and neither would he.

"Does the Guild trade with you often?" I asked. Keep it business. Business was the only ground I had.

"Often is a strong word for a century." She resumed walking, needles clicking somewhere behind us though her hands were empty. "Your Guildmaster's predecessors were more enthusiastic. Souls, mostly, in the lean years. Plague years, flood years. Desperate people sell desperate things, and the Guild learned early that it could stand between the desperate and the deep and take a commission. Very respectable. Very clean. They call it brokering." She glanced back at me, and her eyes were the exact grey of the yarn. "You've read the entries, haven't you? You followed them backward. That's how you found your way to my door."

"I found my way to your door with a writ."

"Mm. Forged, and well forged — I compliment whoever taught you your letterforms. But writs open doors, dear. Ledgers open *reasons*. You came down here because you read what your Guild sold, and to whom, and you couldn't sleep with the entry sitting unbalanced in your chest." She stopped before a shelf and turned to face me fully. "So. Let us do business properly, since you've come all this way to do it."

* * *

The souls were shelved like ledgers.

I don't mean that as a figure of speech. I mean there were spines. Each soul sat on the shelf bound in a cover — leather, cloth, river-reed, whatever the person had been made of — with a label on the spine, and the labels were organized, and the organization was *good*. Alphabetical within acquisition-year. Cross-referenced by debt. I saw a whole run from 1897, the plague winter, thin gray bindings packed tight as famine, and my throat closed, because I knew what year I was looking for and I knew it would be there.

Odile's daughter. The drowned child whose breathing body sat obediently in a river-house above, eating what she was given and wanting nothing, wanting *nothing*, and Odile scrubbing floors in the city to buy back what the winter had taken.

"There," I said. "Third shelf. The small gray one."

The queen didn't move. "You have good eyes for stock."

"I have good eyes for ledgers. That's a ledger. You've bound a child like a ledger."

"I've bound a child like a *child*," the queen said, with mild reproach, as though I'd misfiled something. "A soul is an account of everything a person is. Every debt and credit, every entry, balanced or not. When I hold one, I can read it. Would you like to know what hers says? It says *wanted*. That's rare, in the plague-year stock. Most of them say *tired*. Hers says *wanted* — her mother sang to her every night of the famine, even with nothing in the pot. Do you know what that does to the resale value?" She shook her head, almost fondly. "Neither do I. I don't resell. I only keep."

"Then I'm buying her back."

"Yes." The queen picked up her knitting again — I never saw where the needles came from — and clicked twice, three times, considering. "And here is where I admire you, Mirabel Quill, so listen closely, because admiration from me is worth more than coin. You cannot afford her. No one can afford her. That is not an obstacle; that is the *terms*, and terms are the only honest thing in either of our professions."

"Name them."

"A soul of equal weight," she said. "Due at the next new moon. Not equal in size — children are light, poor things, lighter than they should be. Equal in *weight*. Density of living. A soul that has been loved hard and wanted much and carried debts of its own. I will weigh it myself, on my scale, and my scale has never once been flattered into error." She smiled again, warm as a banked fire. "You have nineteen days. You may bring anyone's. Yours would do beautifully — yours is unusually dense, for a girl your age. All that guilt. Guilt weighs wonderfully."

The hall was very quiet. Somewhere far off, the needles clicked.

"And if I don't pay?"

"Then the account closes as written, and the child stays shelved, and her mother grows old waiting outside a house where nobody home ever asks her to come in." The queen said it without cruelty, which is the thing I cannot make anyone believe afterward. She said it the way I would say *the sum is forty crowns*. "I am not your enemy, ledger-keeper. I am the other party to a contract your own Guild drafted a hundred years ago, with clauses your own Guild signed. I merely enforce. Enforcement gets such a bad reputation among people who sign things without reading them."

I thought of Dominic's signature in the margin. Witnessed, dated, neat as prayer. I thought of him saying *don't go down there* and meaning it with his whole careful heart.

"Nineteen days," I said.

"Nineteen days," she agreed, and pressed a slip of paper into my hand — terms of sale, in a fine antique hand, already signed by her, leaving a blank space where mine would go. "Take the copy. We are civilized parties. And Mirabel—" she caught my sleeve as I turned, lightly, the way you'd catch a page before it tore, "—do read the whole document before you sign anything. Including the parts that aren't legible yet. Especially those."

I walked back up the stair with the terms in my fist and the lantern shaking in the other hand, and it wasn't until I reached the first wardens' landing that I dared look at the final line of the queen's contract, the one that had been blank when she handed it to me.

It wasn't blank anymore.

In ink the color of deep water, in my own handwriting — letter for letter, the slant of my *M*, the way I cross my sevens — it read: *To be paid in full by the soul of Mirabel Quill, due at the new moon.*

The prophecy hadn't been a warning. It had been a draft.
