## 7. New Moon Rising

The Guildmaster found out the way he found out everything: from a ledger.

Dominic was in the counting room when the summons came — a boy in guild grey, breathless, saying only that the Master wanted him above. Not in the cellar-office where the real work happened, but upstairs, in the room with the tall windows and the scale of black iron that weighed nothing anyone could see.

He knew before his boots hit the third stair.

The writ rack stood open. Row after row of sealed protections, each one a life the Guild had underwritten — safe passage, safe dwelling, safe soul — and one hook bare. Writ number forty-one. Mirabel's.

"She took it at dusk," the Guildmaster said. He didn't turn from the window. Below, the city was lighting itself lamp by lamp, ordinary as arithmetic. "Forged the countersignature. Good work, actually. I'd have believed it." A pause. "I did believe it, for six years."

"Master—"

"Do you know what she's carrying down that stair?" The Guildmaster turned then, and his face was the face Dominic had trusted since he was nine years old and starving behind a fish market: patient, kind, absolutely immovable. "A writ of protection signed in her own hand. Which means, under the old law, *she* is the guarantor. Which means if she bargains down there — and she will; she's my best ledger-keeper, she doesn't walk into anything without intending to close it — every contract we've brokered since 1897 becomes contestable. Every one. Do you understand what unravels? The Harrow family's plague-winter terms. The Vane inheritance. Forty souls in that court's keeping, each one anchored to a signature the queen can now call into question."

"She went to bring back a child."

"She went to break a contract." The Guildmaster said it gently, which was worse. "There is no version of this where both things are true."

*There is,* Dominic thought. *You just never looked for it.* But he said nothing, because he had been saying nothing for eleven years, and silence was the first thing the Guild had ever paid him for.

"You'll go down tonight," the Guildmaster continued. "Before the new moon rises fully — the court's power crests at moonset, and after that, negotiations harden like wax. Bring her up. Bring the writ. If she's already struck terms, bring the terms; paper can be amended, drowned, re-signed. Whatever it takes." He crossed the room and put his hand on Dominic's shoulder, and it was heavy the way a father's hand is heavy, the way a lock is heavy. "You were the first child I ever saved, Dominic. Don't make me remember why I stopped saving them."

That was the sentence Dominic carried down out of that room, through the corridors, past the shelves of prophecy books with their century-old dates, all the way to his own narrow cell beneath the eaves.

*Don't make me remember why I stopped saving them.*

As if the saving had ever been free.

* * *

His writ of protection hung on its nail where it had hung for eleven years. Parchment gone soft at the folds, the Guild's seal fat and red as a wound. It said, in language older than either of them:

*Let it be known that Dominic Delacroix, orphan of the river year, stands under the Guild's perpetual care. His name, his labor, his soul's keeping — all held in trust, in perpetuity, against all claims.*

In perpetuity. He'd been proud of that word once. Nine years old, hollow-eyed, plucked off the docks by a man who fed him stew and asked for nothing but everything, and Dominic had read *in perpetuity* and thought it meant *forever safe.*

It meant forever owned. He'd understood that by fifteen. He'd signed as witness anyway, at nineteen, because the Guildmaster asked, and because asking was the closest thing to love the man knew how to give, and because the alternative — the door, the street, the river year again — was not an alternative at all. That was how you sold a child slowly. Not in one bargain, but in a thousand small kindnesses, each one a link, until the chain was indistinguishable from the boy wearing it.

And Mirabel. Mirabel who balanced impossible accounts and hummed when she worked and looked at him across the cellar-office like he was a person rather than a possession. Mirabel who had found her own name in a book dated before her grandmother was born, and instead of running — really running, out of the city, out of the Guild's reach — had gone *down*. Toward the thing. Toward the debt. Because somewhere under all that ink she'd decided a drowned child mattered more than her own safety, and she hadn't even paused to be noble about it. She'd just done the math and followed it into the dark.

He loved her. He had known it for two years and said it zero times, because everything he'd ever been given came with terms, and he would not put terms on her.

But she was descending a stair under the river tonight, alone, holding a piece of paper that made her legally responsible for whatever happened next. And the man who owned him wanted her brought back before she could do the one decent thing anyone in the Guild's history had tried to do.

Dominic took the writ off its nail.

He held it a long time. Eleven years of meals and schooling and warm beds and cold arithmetic. Eleven years of being *kept.* Somewhere below the falls, the new moon was climbing toward moonset, and a girl he loved was standing in front of a queen who collected souls the way the Guild collected signatures.

He lit it from his candle and watched the seal curl and blacken.

It burned faster than he expected. Protections always did, once you stopped believing in them. By the time the flame reached the word *perpetuity*, ash was drifting off his knuckles like grey snow, and he felt — nothing dramatic. No thunderclap. Just a strange lightness, as if a coat he'd worn so long he'd forgotten it had weight had finally come off.

Unprotected. Unowned. Unguilded, if he didn't come back.

He wrote one line on the back of a receipt and left it weighted under the candlestick, where the Guildmaster would find it within the hour:

*I've gone to settle an account you should have settled in 1897. Don't follow. You're not protected down there either — you just never checked.*

Then he took the forged writ Mirabel had left copied in her desk — of course she'd left a copy; she left copies of everything; it was the most Mirabel thing about her — and he went down toward the river, moving fast, before his courage could develop terms of its own.

* * *

The stair began behind the falls.

Everyone in the city knew the waterfall — white rope of it, loud enough to swallow conversation, the kind of place lovers went to say things they didn't want heard. Nobody knew what the noise was *for.* Dominic knew now, walking the wet ledge with spray icing his collar: the falls were a lock. A hundred years of roar to drown the sound of footsteps going down, and coming back up, or not coming back at all.

The stair was cut into living rock, older than the Guild, older than the city's charter. Each step worn concave by feet that had descended carrying something and ascended carrying less. He counted as he went, because counting was the only prayer he'd ever learned. At three hundred steps the light changed — not darker, exactly, but *other*, a glow with no source, the color of lamplight seen through deep water.

At four hundred, he heard it: music, thin and sweet, and underneath it a sound like pages turning. Thousands of pages. The whole mountain, it seemed, was reading.

At five hundred, the stair ended, and the under-court opened before him, and Dominic Delacroix — who had spent his whole life learning what things cost — finally saw the inventory.

Shelves. Shelves rising into dark beyond the reach of sight, and on them, glowing softly, jars and lanterns and small glass bells, each one holding a light that moved the way light moves inside a sleeping person's chest. Souls. Hundreds. Kept, catalogued, shelved by weight.

And between him and the shelves, in a hall of black stone hung with chandeliers of river-glass, he saw her.

Mirabel. Alive. Standing very straight before a tall figure robed in the color of deep water, speaking in the clear, steady voice she used for closing accounts — and beside her, small and dripping and *wrong*, a child with eyes like unlit windows. The drowned girl. Still breathing. Still empty.

The queen turned her head, unhurried, as if she'd heard his heartbeat on the stairs.

"Ah," she said, and her smile was courteous as a contract. "The second signature arrives. Come in, guildsman. We were just discussing payment."

Mirabel looked back at him, and her face did three things at once — relief, terror, and a furious *why did you follow me* — and Dominic stepped off the last stair into the court of kept things, unprotected, unguilded, owned by nothing and no one for the first time in his life.

"I'm not here as the Guild," he said.

"No," the queen agreed pleasantly. "I can see that. You've burned your papers. How *novel.*" Her gaze slid to Mirabel, then to the scale of black iron rising behind her throne, taller than any scale had a right to be. "Which makes this considerably more interesting. You see, children — the moon sets in one hour. And the debt has not yet closed."

Behind her throne, the great scale stirred, though nothing touched it.

And on its far pan, faint as breath on glass, a weight appeared that had not been there before — waiting, patient, already measured.

Already named.
