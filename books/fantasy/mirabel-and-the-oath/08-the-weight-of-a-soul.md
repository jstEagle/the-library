## 8. The Weight of a Soul

He came down the stair with his coat still smoking.

That was the first thing I saw — not his face, not the writ burning in his fist, but the smoke rising off his shoulders like he'd walked through a chimney fire and hadn't bothered to stop. The wardens at the falls had let him pass without a word, because a man carrying nothing is no one's problem, and Dominic Delacroix had just made himself into nothing: no guild standing, no protection, no name in any ledger that mattered.

"You absolute fool," I said, which was the closest I could come to *thank you*.

"Pot," he said, "kettle," and crossed the flooded gallery to me, stepping over a drowned chandelier the way you'd step over a sleeping dog. His boots left wet prints. Somewhere above us, the river went on falling, patient as a creditor.

The queen's hall opened off the gallery like a throat. She was already waiting on her dais of stacked ledgers — actual ledgers, thousands of them, bound in every color debt comes in — and she smiled when she saw him, the way a collector smiles when a second specimen arrives unbidden.

"The witness," she said. "How lovely. Situations resolve so much faster with both parties present."

"We're here for the child," I said. "Odile's daughter. You've held her soul since the plague winter. The Guild sold it to buy grain and medicine for a dying town."

"I know what I bought," the queen said mildly. "I keep excellent records." She lifted one hand, and from the dark behind her dais a shelf slid forward, and on the shelf sat a jar no bigger than a teacup, and inside the jar something glowed the way a candle glows underwater — dimmed, but stubbornly itself. A small light. A child-sized light.

My chest did something complicated and painful.

"Terms were set a century ago," the queen said. "A soul of equal weight, due at the new moon. Tonight is the new moon. So." She gestured, gracious as a hostess pouring tea. "Let us weigh."

* * *

The scale stood at the center of the hall, and it was older than everything else in the room, including the queen, if the look of her was anything to go by. Two pans of black iron hung from a beam of river-wood, and neither pan touched the ground, and neither pan swung. They simply waited.

I set the jar in the left pan. It settled with a sound like a sigh.

"Now," the queen said, "we find what balances a child."

Dominic went first. He emptied his pockets onto the right pan with the brisk efficiency of a man who has audited worse: guild coin, a silver matchcase, his father's watch — the only thing he owned that predated the Guild. The pan didn't move. The watch lay there gleaming and worthless, and something in his face closed slightly, like a door.

"Coin is heavy," the queen observed, "but souls are heavier. Next."

I had come prepared. Of course I had; I'm a ledger-keeper, preparation is my whole personality. From my satchel I took the memory-vial I'd traded for three galleries back — a vial of my seventh summer, the year my grandmother taught me to float in the millpond, purchased at a price I'd rather not itemize even now. I poured it onto the pan. It ran like quicksilver, pooled, shivered.

The scale considered it. The needle — there was a needle, brass, mounted on the beam — trembled once and fell back to zero.

"Memories weigh almost nothing," the queen said, and for the first time she sounded genuinely regretful. "That's the tragedy of them. People give them away so cheaply, and they're worth so little to anyone but the rememberer."

"Years, then," Dominic said quietly. He'd gone pale, but his voice was level. "I'll pledge ten years of my life. Formally. On contract."

The queen's smile sharpened. "Oh, we can do years. We do years all the time." She snapped her fingers, or something equivalent — the sound was more like a lock turning — and a document materialized in the air between us, ink still wet, terms elegant, ten years of Dominic Delacroix's remaining span rendered down to its market weight.

I watched the scale.

The needle moved. It rose — a fraction, a hair, the ghost of hope — and then it settled back to zero again, and the document dissolved into moths, and the moths dissolved into nothing.

"Not enough," the queen said. "Ten years of a man who spent most of his life already belonging to someone else. There isn't much left that's truly yours, guildsman. That's not an insult. It's arithmetic."

Dominic stood very still. I knew that stillness. It was the stillness of a man doing sums he doesn't like.

"Then what," I said, "balances?"

And the queen looked at me — really looked, the way an appraiser looks — and said, gently, kindly, horribly: "You haven't read the last line, have you."

* * *

She produced the prophecy book herself. My book — the Guild's book, the 1897 edition, the one I'd found open on the cellar desk a lifetime ago, which was nine days ago. She must have taken it from me at the door, the way you take a coat. Or perhaps it had always been hers and the Guild had merely borrowed it, the way the Guild borrowed everything.

"Read," she said, and turned to today's page.

I read aloud, because some part of me understood that this was a courtroom now, and courtrooms run on spoken words:

*"And the ledger-keeper shall descend, and the debt shall be weighed, and what the scale accepts shall close the account."*

"That's not the last line," I said. "There's more."

"There is always more," the queen said. "Ink fades where it isn't needed. Look again. Look at what the page says *now*."

The page had changed.

I don't mean the words had been rewritten. I mean they had become legible — lines that had been blank, or blurred, or written in that faint brown ink that might have been water damage, suddenly crisp, suddenly black, suddenly *there*, as though they'd been waiting a hundred and twenty-eight years for exactly this lighting.

*"The debt closes on the one who reads it."*

I read it twice. Three times. The words didn't rearrange themselves into anything kinder.

"No," Dominic said. He said it the way people say *no* to weather, to floods, to things already inside the house. He grabbed the book out of my hands and read it himself, and I watched him arrive where I'd arrived, and I watched him refuse it anyway. "No. Prophecies are drafts. You told me that yourself — your first week at the Guild, you said every prophecy is just a first draft until someone signs it—"

"Then don't sign," I said.

It came out steadier than I felt. Because here was the thing, the terrible clarifying thing, the thing that nine days ago would have kept me up all night and tonight felt almost like relief: I had read the trap closing exactly as written, and some cold clerk-part of me had already begun checking the math. A soul of equal weight. A child's soul, small and unfinished. And mine — mine, which had been hollowing out for years in a cellar office, which belonged to no family, no mother, no town that had traded anything for it. Mine was the only entry in the building that balanced.

"It fits," I said. "That's the worst part. It fits like it was measured."

"Mirabel—"

"Don't." I held up a hand. "Don't do the noble argument. I've read your contracts, Dominic. I know how you argue, and I know how you lose."

The queen watched us with the patience of someone who has attended a great many endings. "For what it's worth," she offered, "the prophecy is not fate. It is a *quotation*. Someone wrote down what happens, because someone always writes down what happens. Whether it can be amended—" and here her eyes did something bright and hungry and interested, "—is a question of authorship. And authorship, little keeper, is a matter of jurisdiction."

"What does that mean?" Dominic demanded.

"It means," the queen said, rising from her dais of ledgers, smoothing her robes of layered receipts, "that the scale will accept whatever closes the account. It has never once cared *what*. Coin. Years. Names. It cares about weight, and weight, in my experience, is a question of *how a thing is written*." She extended one long hand toward the black iron pans. "So. Weigh yourselves. Weigh your love, if you insist. Weigh the truth, if you can lift it. The scale is impartial. The scale is the only honest thing in either of our houses."

"And if nothing weighs enough?" I asked.

"Then the new moon sets," she said, "and the account closes by default, and default, dear girl, has a standard remedy."

She didn't have to say it. The last line hung in the air between us, freshly legible, a hundred and twenty-eight years old and brand new:

*The debt closes on the one who reads it.*

Dominic put the book down on the pan.

Not the jar — the jar stayed where it was, glowing its drowned-glow. He laid the prophecy book itself on the right-hand scale, open to today's page, and the needle jumped, and my heart jumped with it, stupid and hopeful —

— and settled back to zero.

"Of course not," he said, laughing, and it wasn't a good laugh. "It's a *book*. It's paper. It's the cheapest thing I own." He turned to me, and the smoke was still coming off his shoulders, and his eyes were doing the arithmetic he hated. "Mirabel. Listen to me. If the scale takes you, I want you to know — before, while you can still—"

"Don't you dare make a speech," I said. "Speeches are for funerals and I am not attending this one."

But my hand was already moving toward the left pan, toward the jar, toward the small stubborn light of Odile's daughter, and the new moon was already leaning toward the stair, and somewhere above us the river went on falling, patient as a creditor, and I thought: *a first draft. Every prophecy is a first draft.*

Someone just has to pick up the pen.

I looked at the queen. The queen looked at me. And I swear on every ledger I've ever balanced, she *smiled* — not the collector's smile, not the creditor's smile, but something quicker, sharper, almost eager, like a teacher watching a student reach for the wrong answer that might, just possibly, be the right one.

"Well then, keeper," she said. "Amend me."
