## 9. Amendment

The scale did not want her soul.

That was the first thing Mirabel understood, standing on the dais with the queen's silver pan hovering an inch from her sternum like a patient bird. The scale had weighed her — she felt it go through her the way a clerk's thumb goes through a stack of coins, counting, dismissing — and it had found her wanting. Not unworthy. *Insufficient.* A soul was a fixed quantity, and the debt was a child's whole unlived life, and no arithmetic in any court made those equal.

"The prophecy is very tidy," the queen said, circling her, skirts whispering over stone that remembered being riverbed. "Tidy things are so rare down here. I confess I hoped you'd simply lie down on the pan and spare us all the tedium of negotiation."

"I read the contract," Mirabel said. Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "All of it. Every rider, every schedule, every footnote in the margin written by three different clerks across a hundred years."

"And?"

"And none of you ever amended it." She let that sit. "You've kept this court's books for a century and not one of you thought to write in them."

The queen stopped circling.

Above them — far above, past the stair under the falls, past the wardens' gate, up through rock and water and the ordinary gray morning light of the city — Dominic Delacroix was running.

* * *

He had burned his writ at the top of the stair. He'd held the parchment over the warden's brazier and watched the Guild's seal curl into ash, and the moment it caught he felt the protection peel off him like a bandage off a wound — every contract that had shielded him since he was nine years old, every clause that said *this boy belongs to us and therefore to safety*, gone. What remained was just a man in a wet coat descending a stair that ate people, carrying nothing but a lantern and the certain knowledge that the Guildmaster would have him struck from the rolls before dawn.

It should have terrified him. It mostly did. But underneath the terror was something older and simpler: when he was nine, an orphan with lice and a stolen apple, the Guildmaster had looked at him across a desk and said, *Sign here, and you will never be nothing again.* And Dominic had signed, because nothing was the only thing he'd ever owned.

Mirabel had looked at the same ledgers and seen what the signatures bought.

He took the stairs three at a time.

* * *

"Contracts are living documents," Mirabel said. "That's the first thing they teach you. A contract that can't be amended isn't a contract, it's a grave marker." She reached into her coat and drew out the ledger book — her own, the one she balanced every night in the flooded cellar-office, its margins soft with her pencil. "And I am the Guild's ledger-keeper. Which means that for the purposes of every instrument your court has ever accepted from us—" she set the book on the dais, opened it flat— "*I am the Guild.*"

"You are a runaway apprentice," the queen said, but her voice had changed key. Interest, Mirabel thought. Collectors always perk up at a rarity.

"I'm the hand that's touched every page. Ask your scale." She turned to the great balance beside the throne, the one that had rejected coin and memory and years. "Weigh my authority. Go on."

The scale hesitated — she saw the pans tremble, actually tremble — and then the silver pan dipped toward her, once, like a bow.

The queen's smile did not move, but everything behind it did. "Clever girl. And what amendment do you propose? You cannot strike the debt. The child's soul was lawfully purchased. Even I am bound."

"I'm not striking anything." Mirabel uncapped her pen. Her hands were shaking now; she let them. "I'm adding a clause."

She wrote as she spoke, in the Guild's binding style — the old style, the one from the 1897 folio, ink over salt over breath:

*"Schedule Nine, Amendment One: In consideration of the return of the soul herein warehoused (the Child), the debtor offers payment in kind — namely, authorship. All rights, title, and interest in the narrative of this transaction, including its telling, retelling, interpretation, and ending, are hereby conveyed to the undersigned in perpetuity. The Court shall hold the story; the Court shall keep the story; the Court shall own every word of what happened here, forever — and a thing fully told is a thing fully paid."*

She signed. The ink sank into the parchment of the original contract as if the paper had been thirsty for a hundred years.

The queen went very still. "You're selling me the *story*."

"I'm selling you the only version of it. Mine. The one where a mother sold her daughter to buy bread through a plague winter, where a guild brokered it and called it mercy, where a boy signed as witness because he'd been sold first." Mirabel met her eyes. "You hoard kept things. Here is the most kept thing there is. No one else will ever tell it — not the Guild, not Odile, not the child when she wakes. It belongs to your shelves. Forever."

"And your soul?"

"Unencumbered. Read the clause."

Down the last flight of stairs, boots hammering wet stone, Dominic heard the words *unencumbered* and understood exactly what she was doing — and exactly what it would cost if it failed. Because a contract needed surety. A century-old instrument, amended unilaterally, would need collateral or the court could void it all, and then the scale would come back for her, and this time it would not be insufficient. It would be final.

There was one thing left in him worth staking.

He came out onto the dais floor breathing hard, lantern guttering, and every eye in the under-court turned to him — the wardens, the shelved souls humming on their racks like struck glasses, the queen.

"Dominic Delacroix," the queen said pleasantly. "The witness returns. How fitting. Do you come to pay your friend's debt after all?"

"No." He walked past Mirabel — did not look at her, couldn't, or he'd lose the sentence he'd built on nine flights of stairs — and stood before the throne. "I come to stand surety."

"Dom," Mirabel said quietly. "Don't."

"My guild-standing," he said over her. "Full and unconditional. My writs, my protections, my place on the rolls, my name in the Guild's book — all of it, conveyed to the Court of Kept Things as security against this amendment. If the clause fails — if the story doesn't bind, if anyone contests it — the Court may collect me instead. Soul, standing, name, all of it." He drew a breath. "In exchange, the amendment stands, and Mirabel Quill walks out of here owing nothing."

The silence went on long enough that Mirabel could hear the river thinking somewhere overhead.

Then the queen laughed — softly, delightedly, the way a collector laughs at finding two rarities in one drawer. "A guildsman burning his own name to guarantee a runaway's paperwork. Oh, the Guild will *weep*." She extended one pale hand, and a fresh page bloomed in the air before Dominic, blank and waiting. "Sign."

He signed without reading it. Mirabel would have hated that, under other circumstances — she'd taught him better — but she understood. Some clauses you don't need to read, because you wrote them yourself, years ago, in a flooded cellar, without knowing it.

The great scale swung. The silver pan descended, touched the dais, and rose again — empty, satisfied, closed.

On the shelf behind the throne, among a hundred years of warehoused souls, one small light flickered like a candle catching its second wind.

"It's done," the queen said, and for one moment her kindness looked almost real. "Take the child's vessel back up your stair. The soul follows its own road home." Her gaze slid to Mirabel, and sharpened. "But mind the terms, ledger-keeper. Payment is due at collection. Authorship has a body, too — stories must be *carried* out of my court, and someone must carry yours."

"What does that mean?" Mirabel asked.

The queen smiled and did not answer, which was answer enough.

Dominic took her hand. His was cold and steady. "Whatever it costs," he said, "we'll figure it out upstairs. That's what we do. We balance the books."

They climbed together, past the wardens who no longer stopped them, past the falls, toward the gray morning and the river-house where a small empty-eyed girl sat waiting to be filled again.

Neither of them saw the queen open a fresh shelf, and set upon it, gently, a single new ledger — already titled, in ink that dried as they watched:

*Mirabel Quill: the true and complete account.*

The first page was blank.

For now.
