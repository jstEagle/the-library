## 10. Everything Owed, Everything Kept

The river gave the child back at dawn, the way it gives back anything it has no further use for: gently, without apology.

Mirabel stood on the bank with her boots in the shallows and watched Odile wade out to where the girl floated, face up, hair fanned like ink in water. The child coughed once — a small, ordinary, human sound — and then she was crying, and Odile was crying, and the two of them clung to each other in the cold current while Mirabel stood very still and did the only thing she knew how to do.

She counted.

One child, breathing. One mother, weeping. One debt, paid.

It balanced. Somewhere in the ledger of the world, a line closed with a satisfying scratch of pen across paper, and Mirabel felt it close in her own chest too, like a door shutting on a room she'd been standing in for weeks.

"Her name is Sylvie," Odile said later, when they had wrapped the girl in blankets and built a fire on the bank that smoked more than it burned because everything was still wet from the river's long keeping. "She looked at me this morning and said *good morning, Mama*, and I have waited three years to hear her say my name like she meant it."

"She'll be hungry," Mirabel said. "Souls come back hungry."

"I know what to feed her." Odile looked at her then, really looked, and what was in her face was not gratitude exactly. Gratitude was too small a word, and too clean. What was in her face was the look of someone who has seen the bill. "You know what you gave."

"Yes."

"You don't remember what you gave."

"No." Mirabel smiled, and found the smile fit strangely, like a coat borrowed from someone taller. "That's rather the point."

* * *

The queen kept her word, which was the most frightening thing about her.

She had taken the payment at the moment of amendment — not violently, nothing so crude as theft. She had simply extended one pale hand across the scale, touched Mirabel's temple with two fingers, and lifted, the way you lift a bookmark from a book you intend to finish. And everything that was Dominic Delacroix went with it: the way he leaned in doorways as if doors were furniture he distrusted; the sound he made when he laughed, which was less a laugh than a surrender; three years of Tuesday mornings in the flooded cellar-office, passing the same chipped cup back and forth; the night he'd stood in the rain outside her window and told her the truth badly, because he'd never learned to tell truth any other way.

Gone. Shelved. Kept.

Mirabel remembered that there had been a guildsman named Dominic Delacroix. She remembered his signature in the ledger, the elegant slant of it, the way the D looped like a swan's neck. She remembered that he had burned his writ of protection and followed her down into the dark, and that he had staked his standing as surety on her amendment, and that the court beneath the mountain now held his name the way a bank holds collateral.

She remembered all of it the way she remembered figures in a ledger she'd audited but never lived. Accurate. Complete. Utterly bloodless.

"He'll want them back," the queen had said, almost kindly, as Mirabel turned to climb the stair. "Memories are the one thing my collectors can't counterfeit. He'll have to earn every one. It's the cruelest thing I could have done to either of you, and I did it because it was *interesting*." A pause, and then, softer, almost wistful: "Do come again. You're the first client in a century who read the fine print before signing."

* * *

The Guild reckoned with what it brokered the way the Guild reckoned with everything: slowly, in triplicate, and with an eye toward which version would look best in the historical record.

But the historical record had Mirabel Quill in it now, and Mirabel Quill kept the ledgers.

She laid the century of entries out on the great table in the hall above the flooded cellar — every sale, every soul, every signature — and she invited the wardens, the appraisers, the factor of impossible debts, and the Guildmaster himself to read what she had read. She did not shout. She did not accuse. She simply opened the books and stepped back, and let the arithmetic do what arithmetic does when it is finally allowed to speak aloud.

The Guildmaster read for a long time. When he finished, he looked older than he had at breakfast, and Mirabel understood that he had known — had always known — and had spent ninety years telling himself that a ledger balanced was a conscience balanced, and discovering, here at the end, that the two columns had never once reconciled.

"The contracts stand," he said finally. "They were lawful."

"They were *legal*," Mirabel said. "Those aren't the same thing, and you taught me the difference yourself, the day I apprenticed. You said a keeper of impossible things must keep the impossibility honest. Well." She closed the last ledger. "I'm keeping it honest."

The reforms took a season. Sales halted; old bargains were audited; a new office was founded, with Mirabel at its head, called the Office of Things Owed, whose entire function was to find the sold things and buy them home. It was underfunded, understaffed, and widely mocked by the older guildsmen, and Mirabel loved it the way you love a crooked little house that is nonetheless yours.

Dominic resigned the week it opened.

He did it publicly, in the hall, before the whole Guild — surrendered his standing, his rooms, his name in the rolls. When the Guildmaster asked him why, Dominic said only: "Because I signed as witness, and a witness who won't testify is just furniture." Then he walked out past Mirabel without looking at her, because looking at her, he had learned, cost him something he was no longer willing to spend carelessly.

* * *

He came to the river-house on a Tuesday, because Tuesdays had been theirs, though of course she didn't know that, and he had decided that knowing things she didn't was going to be his particular punishment and privilege both.

"Mirabel Quill," he said, standing at the gate like a tradesman calling on a client. "My name is Dominic Delacroix. I'm told I used to know you."

"I know *of* you," she said carefully. "You're in my ledgers."

"I'm in everyone's ledgers. It's one of my more irritating qualities." He held up a small notebook, worn already at the corners. "I've made a list. Every story about us I can reconstruct from letters, from guild records, from the testimony of people who watched us be idiots together. There are forty-one. I intend to tell you one per visit, in order, and you will decide after each one whether it sounds like something worth remembering."

"That's not how memory works," Mirabel said. "You can't just put it back in me."

"No," he agreed. "But the queen keeps the originals, and the queen respects craft. And I figure if I tell them truly enough — if I get every detail right, the chipped cup, the bad umbrella, the time you corrected my handwriting in front of the factor and I sulked for a week — then somewhere down under the mountain, on some shelf, my memories will start to sit up and take notice. Like books that hear their titles being read correctly." He shrugged, and the shrug was exactly the kind of thing she suspected she had once found unbearable and charming in equal measure. "Besides. Even if it never works. Even if you never feel a single thing. I'd rather court you from the first page than not court you at all."

Mirabel looked at him for a long moment. He was a stranger with a familiar signature. He was a line item she had audited. He was, according to forty-one stories she had not yet heard, the best thing she had ever traded away.

"Story one," she said. "Go on, then. But I'm taking notes. If your details don't match the records, I'm charging you a correction fee."

His whole face changed — lit like a lamp behind glass — and Mirabel felt something move far down in the hollowed place where the payment had been taken, faint as water finding a crack in a cellar floor.

Not memory. Not yet. Just the shape of a space where something might, someday, be kept.

"Once," Dominic said, opening his notebook, "there was an apprentice who caught a forgery nobody else could see—"

And high beneath the mountain, in a court of kept things, on a shelf between a drowned bell and a hundred years of Tuesday mornings, a small bright memory stirred at the sound of its own true story being told — and leaned, the way all kept things lean, toward the voice reading it home.
