## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The ink froze first.

Gideon noticed it the way she noticed everything in the scriptorium — as a small failure in an otherwise ordered system. The pot by her left hand had gone stiff overnight, the surface skinned with grey, and when she dipped her pen it came up dragging, leaving a line like a scar instead of a word. She set the pen down, wrapped both hands around the pot, and waited for the warmth to take.

Outside the scriptorium's high window, Saltmere was still white. It was always white. The salt flats ran from the temple wall to the mountains in every direction, a crust a hand-span thick over hardpan, and nothing grew in it, and nothing moved across it except wind and the occasional caravan that had miscalculated its route. The valley held them all the way a bowl holds water: completely, and without interest.

"Your hands are shaking," said Brother Anselm from the next desk. He didn't look up. His own pen scratched on, steady as a drip of water.

"They're cold."

"Cold hands copy crooked lines." He turned a page. "Warm them properly or start again."

She warmed them properly. That was the whole of Gideon Iversen's life, if you wanted to be honest about it, which she tried not to be before midday: warm your hands, copy your lines, keep your ledger clean. She was twenty-six years old and she had been in the temple at Saltmere since she was nine, since the year her father's fishing boat came back without him and his debts came ashore instead. Seventeen years of copying other people's obligations into books so fine and permanent that the temple could hold them over three generations. Somewhere in the archive below her feet was the ledger with her own name in it. Entry 4471. Iversen, G., daughter of Iversen, T., deceased. Principal: eleven marks, four shillings. Interest accrued: she had stopped calculating it years ago, the way you stop looking at a wound.

Eleven marks. Her father had died owing eleven marks, and the temple had taken his daughter at nine marks a decade for the privilege of working it off, and the arithmetic never once moved in her favor. She'd done the sums a hundred times as a girl, hunched over scraps of vellum, hoping she'd made an error. She hadn't. The error was the design.

She dipped her pen and went back to work.

The page in front of her was a debt record for the Halloway family, millers, third generation. *Item: one mill-stone, cracked, valued at two marks. Item: labor of eldest son, contracted age seven, term fifteen years.* The letters came out of her clean and even, because that was the one thing she could make perfect in this valley, and perfection was its own small revenge. If the numbers were going to be cruel, they would at least be legible. Anyone who read her ledgers would see exactly what had been done, in a hand too beautiful to dismiss.

*Item: interest, compounded quarterly.*

Her pen paused over the next line. Not because of the words. Because of the weight against her sternum, under her scribe's smock, where a cord hung with something knotted into it that no ledger had ever recorded.

* * *

She had found it in the flooded cellar eleven days ago, and she had told no one, which was the first dishonest thing she had ever done in seventeen years of institutional life, and the strange part — the part she kept turning over at night like a stone looking for the soft underside — was how easy it had been.

The cellar flooded every spring when the meltwater came down off the mountains and found the old cistern channels beneath the temple. Every spring the archivists drew lots for who went down to salvage what the water had ruined, and every spring Gideon lost, because Brother Anselm believed in fairness the way he believed in cold rooms: theoretically, and applied to everyone equally. So it was Gideon who waded calf-deep through black water by lamplight, lifting sodden ledgers onto the drying racks, salvaging what could be saved and tallying what couldn't.

The ring had been in a strongbox that shouldn't have been there.

That was the thing she kept coming back to. The strongbox itself was wrong — iron, banded, unmarked, sitting on a shelf behind a false panel in the deepest wall, in a section of the cellar that wasn't on any floor plan she'd copied. And inside, dry as the day it was shut, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age: a ring of white metal, or white stone, or something that wasn't either. It was the color of the flats at noon. It fit no finger she owned, being too large, more like something meant to sit on a staff or a standard or the top of a doorpost. And it was warm.

Everything in Saltmere was cold. The water, the walls, the ink, the people. But the ring sat in her palm like a bird's egg held against a lamp, and when she closed her fingers around it, she felt — not sound exactly, not heat exactly — a hum. Low. Patient. Like something breathing very slowly underneath the world.

She should have turned it in. That was procedure; that was her entire life distilled into a single obvious action. Unclaimed property of value goes to the Prior, goes into the inventory, goes into a ledger with her name beside it as finder, and maybe — *maybe* — a remission of six months' interest as reward.

She had stood in the black water for a long time with the ring in her fist, listening to it hum, and then she had dried it on her sleeve, tied it into the cord around her neck, and lied to the archivist about the strongbox being empty.

Seventeen years of clean records. Eleven days of a secret. And the terrible thing, the thing that made her check the knot under her smock three times a day, was that she didn't regret it. Not even a little. The hum against her breastbone was the first interesting thing that had ever happened to her.

"You're drifting," Anselm said.

"I'm composing." She wrote the interest line. "There's a difference."

"There isn't, in this room." But there was something almost gentle in it. Anselm had been the one to teach her her letters, back when she was a furious little salt-crusted child who bit the novices. He was sixty now and bent like a question mark and he copied debts with the serenity of a man who had made peace with the machine he served, or perhaps simply stopped hearing it. Sometimes Gideon looked at him and saw her own future: a spine curved to the shape of the desk, a life measured in folios.

The bell rang terce. Around the scriptorium, forty pens lifted in unison, forty backs straightened, and the day's first division fell. Gideon capped her ink, squared her pages, and joined the procession to the chapel with her hands folded and her secret beating quietly against her chest.

* * *

The stranger arrived during sext, which was itself an offense against order.

Caravans came to Saltmere on market days, scheduled, expected, logged at the gatehouse with their goods and their tolls. This was neither. Gideon saw it from the refectory window: three riders coming up the salt road in a wedge formation, dressed in grey so pale it was nearly the color of the ground they crossed, and for a moment they seemed less to arrive than to condense out of the valley itself. They wore no temple colors. No house colors. On their shoulders, stitched small and neat, was a device she knew only from the margins of royal writs that passed through the scriptorium twice a decade: a crown above a closed eye.

"Inquisitors," said Sister Maren, beside her, and the word dropped through the refectory like a stone through still water. Conversations didn't stop so much as drain away.

"Here?" someone said. "What would they want here? There's nothing here."

That was true, and everyone knew it, and it was supposed to be comforting. Saltmere was famous — insofar as anywhere in the valley was famous — for its emptiness. Nothing grew in the valley. Nothing burned well, nothing rotted quickly, nothing bred plagues, and nothing — this was the fact every child learned with their catechism, the fact the temple taught as theology rather than geography — nothing *magical*. The valley was dead to the gift. Had always been. Pilgrims used to come, centuries ago, before Gideon's time, seeking cure from curses or relief from hauntings, because the salt killed sorcery the way it killed wheat. The blessed and the cursed alike came to the flats and found themselves ordinary. It was the valley's one miracle: that miracles didn't work here.

So why, Gideon thought, watching the three riders dismount at the gatehouse, would the crown send hunters of the gifted to the one place in the world where gifts went to die?

The answer came within the hour, and it came in her own handwriting's worst nightmare: a decree.

They summoned the Prior, and the Prior summoned the senior scribes — Gideon among them, because fair was fair, and fairness meant she got every unpleasant task in rotation — to witness and copy the document for distribution to every household in the temple's jurisdiction. The inquisitor who produced it was a woman called Serath, grey-cloaked, grey-eyed, with a voice like a file drawn slowly across iron. She laid the scroll on the reading table and broke the seal herself, and Gideon read the crown's hand upside down before anyone read it right side up, because reading upside down was a scribe's reflex and a scribe's curse.

*By order of the Regent's court, in the matter of the unlawful practice of the gift: be it known that all persons possessing, concealing, or harboring the talent called magic shall surrender themselves to the crown's inquiry within thirty days of this proclamation. Shelter given to the gifted is treason. Silence regarding the gifted is complicity. Complicity shares the pyre.*

Silence regarding the gifted is complicity.

Gideon's hand, folded correctly at her waist, curled inward until her nails pressed her palm. Under the smock, against her skin, the ring went from warm to hot — a slow bloom of heat like a coal cupped in cloth — and she had to stand through the entire reading, through the Prior's careful, obsequious response, through Serath's flat grey gaze traveling the assembled scribes one face at a time, without letting a single muscle of her expression change.

The ring had never done that before. In eleven days it had hummed, and slept, and hummed. Now it burned like it was trying to speak.

"The valley is clean," the Prior was saying. "It has ever been so. The salt—"

"The salt," agreed Serath, "is why we've come." And there was something in her tone then that Gideon would spend the rest of the night taking apart, the way she took apart a difficult passage of text: patiently, line by line, looking for the meaning underneath. It was not the tone of a hunter arriving at empty country. It was the tone of a fisherman arriving at stocked water.

"We have reason to believe," Serath continued, "that the valley's cleanliness has been... overstated. Persons have been moving through Saltmere. Smugglers. Moving the gifted south, over the flats, out of the crown's reach. Someone is using your emptiness as a road." Her eyes came around the table again, unhurried. "Roads have traffic. Traffic leaves traces. We will want the archives. All of them. Guest registers, gate logs, alms records, tithes. Everything written in this temple for the last ten years."

And because she was who she was — because she was a ledger-keeper down to the marrow, because her whole soul was organized like a filing cabinet — Gideon's mind did the thing it always did under pressure, which was to open drawers.

Guest registers: clean. Gate logs: clean. Alms records: clean. Ten years of Saltmere's paper life passed before her in half a second, and it was spotless, because Saltmere was spotless, because nothing happened here—

Except.

Except that eleven days ago, in a flooded cellar, behind a false panel that appeared on no plan she had ever copied, she had pried open an iron strongbox that no record accounted for, and taken from it an object that hummed, and lied about it in writing.

*Item: one strongbox, contents tallied nil. Scribe: Iversen, G.*

Her lie was in the archive. Her lie was sitting in the exact room these people intended to search.

Serath's gaze reached her, paused — a half-second, no more, the time it takes to read a short line of text — and moved on. Gideon breathed. Outwardly she was a scribe awaiting instructions. Inwardly she was doing arithmetic she had never imagined doing: the distance from the scriptorium to the cellar, the hours of daylight remaining, the likelihood that the inquisitors would begin with the most recent entries.

They would. Of course they would. Any competent searcher starts with the newest paper.

"Copy the decree," Serath said. "Fair hand. Post it at the gate by dusk. And scribes—" she gathered her cloak, and the two silent men behind her gathered theirs, and the three of them looked, for a moment, like the salt itself had grown opinions, "—keep your ledgers honest. We will know if they aren't. Reading lies is the first thing they teach us."

* * *

She went down to the cellar at vespers, when the temple was at prayer and the inquisitors were, according to the brother at the gate, "at supper and questioning the servants."

The water had receded to ankle-depth. Her lamp threw her shadow huge against the shelving, and the dark beyond the lamp was total, the particular total dark of a place underground in a valley where even the air felt drained. She waded to the deep wall, to the false panel, and stood in front of it with her heart going like a bird in a box.

She hadn't resealed it properly. She knew that. Eleven days ago she'd been standing in black water with a humming relic in her fist and terror in her throat, and she'd pushed the panel back into its frame and told herself it would pass inspection, and it wouldn't, it never would, the dust was disturbed, the mortar crumb was fresh —

The panel swung open at her touch.

Empty. The strongbox sat where she'd left it, lid ajar, oilcloth folded on the shelf beside it, and everything exactly as she'd arranged it, which was to say: arranged by a frightened woman in a hurry, and obvious to anyone who looked.

She heard footsteps on the cellar stair.

Not one set. Three. Descending slowly, with the confidence of people who owned whatever they found at the bottom. Lamplight bloomed at the stairhead, and Gideon did the fastest calculation of her life and found there was nowhere in the cellar to be that wasn't visible from the stairs, and so she did the only other thing available to her: she picked up the strongbox, heavy and damning in her arms, and walked toward the light as though she belonged to it.

Brother Anselm came down first, which saved her — Anselm, whose presence explained hers, whose bent silhouette any inquisitor would read as innocent. Behind him came Serath, lamp raised, grey eyes already moving along the shelves. The third figure stayed at the stairhead, a shape against the light.

"Sister Iversen," Anselm said mildly. "The salvage tally. You were going to bring it up."

"Yes, brother." Her voice came out level. Some deep-buried part of her marveled at it. "Forgive me, I was just—" She turned slightly, presenting the strongbox, buying the sentence time. "The flood damage. I was checking the seals."

Serath looked at the strongbox. Looked at her.

"What was in it?" the inquisitor asked.

"Nothing of value," Gideon said. "Water-damaged vestments. Tallied and discarded."

It was a good lie. It was smooth and boring and exactly the kind of answer a tired scribe gives, and she watched it fail anyway, watched Serath's eyes do the thing eyes do when a reader catches a forged line — a flicker, a settling, the small satisfaction of confirmed suspicion. The inquisitor extended one gloved hand.

"Show me."

And Gideon, holding the box out, understood with total clarity that she was finished — that in thirty seconds the glove would lift the lid, find the false bottom she'd never noticed was false, or find the scratch-marks of something recently removed, or simply look at her face for four seconds longer than a liar can bear — and in that same clarity, against her sternum, the ring flared hot enough to sting through the cloth, and hummed, and for one impossible instant she heard it the way you hear a word spoken directly into your ear in an empty room:

*Run.*

Footsteps overhead. Quick ones, on the chapel floor above, crossing toward the cellar stair — and then a crash, a shout, the bell rope snapping taut somewhere, the evening bell ringing wild and unscheduled, and every lamp in the cellar swung upward toward the sound.

In the half-second of lifted attention, Gideon set the strongbox down, stepped back into shadow behind the shelving, and pulled the cord over her head.

When the lamps came back down, the scribe was gone, and somewhere between the cellar wall and the temple's postern gate, running flat-out through the cold with a burning secret against her chest, Gideon Iversen crossed the line between the woman who kept the ledgers and the woman who would spend the rest of her life outside them.

Behind her, faint through the stone, she heard Serath call out — not alarmed, not yet. Almost pleased.

"Seal the gates. Our thief is finally awake."
