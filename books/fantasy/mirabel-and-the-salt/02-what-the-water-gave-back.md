## 2. What the Water Gave Back

The pyre went up on the third morning, in the temple's own courtyard, which was the part that made it obscene.

They could have built it in the market square like they did in the low towns. They could have taken the gifted out past the salt gates and done their work where the gulls wouldn't see. Instead the crown inquisitors chose the flagstones between the scriptorium and the almonry, ten paces from the window where I copied ledgers six hours a day, and they stacked the wood with the same brisk competence men bring to stacking anything — cordwood, coffins, other people's lives.

I watched them through the leaded glass while my pen kept moving. That was the first thing I noticed about myself that week: my pen kept moving.

*Debt of Anselm Corr, tanner's apprentice, four marks eleven shillings, interest accruing at the temple rate of one part in nine.* The words came out of me the way water comes out of a cracked jug, steady and thoughtless, and all the while my eyes were on the courtyard, on the men in ash-grey coats laying faggots in a lattice pattern because someone among them had read the old manuals and knew that a well-built pyre draws from the bottom up.

"Your ink is pooling," said Brother Cassian without looking up from his own desk.

I blotted the line. Four marks eleven shillings became a small grey bloom, and beneath it I wrote the same figure again, clean, as if debts could be rewritten by blotting them. If only. Half my life was spent learning the exact ways in which they couldn't.

The relic sat against my ribs.

I had carried it for two days now, wrapped in a scrap of altar linen inside the false seam of my bodice, and I had not slept properly since. In the flooded cellar below the archive, where the old records go to drown when the temple decides a debt is too old to collect or too true to keep, the water had given it back to me. That was how I thought of it, though thinking it felt like superstition, and superstition in Saltmere is a debt you pay in worse coin than money. The flood had receded; the mud had parted; and there it lay in a shallow basin of standing brine, white as a knucklebone, a ring of some substance that was neither bone nor shell nor salt but agreed with all three, sitting in the water like an eye that had been waiting a long time to be looked into.

I should have logged it. Every find in the archive goes into the *Register of Recovered Effects*, gets weighed, described, assigned to the treasury or the reliquary. My hand had even started the entry. *One ring, material unknown, recovered from—* And then the ring had hummed.

Not rung. Not chimed. Hummed, low, under the edge of hearing, the way a wet finger hums on a bowl's rim, except nothing touched it and no bowl held it and the sound came up through the soles of my feet before it reached my ears. The water around it had shivered into rings. And I had stood in that drowned cellar with my pen still wet and understood, with the flat certainty of a woman reading her own ledger, that if I wrote this ring into any register, it would be gone by nightfall — into the treasury, into the prior's hands, and from the prior's hands into whatever channel the temple uses for things it does not want the valley to know it has.

So I had closed the register. I had wrapped the ring in altar linen, which was itself a theft, small and absurd, and I had lied with my body ever since: walked evenly, sat evenly, let the scribes' stoop settle back over my shoulders like the grey habit of my order. Two days of it. I was already good at it. That frightened me more than the humming did.

*Debt of Anselm Corr,* my pen wrote again, because I had lost my place. *Four marks eleven shillings.*

Below my window, the inquisitors finished the pyre and began, unhurried, to argue about wind direction.

* * *

Her name was Sef. She was fourteen, maybe fifteen, a kitchen novice with flour permanently in the creases of her knuckles and a laugh that got her scolded twice a week. I knew her the way senior novices know the youngest: she fetched my washing water, she stole plums from the almonry stores and always gave me the bruised ones because she said I deserved the sweetest lie available. I had never once thought of her as gifted. In Saltmere nobody thinks of anybody as gifted. That is rather the point of Saltmere.

They came for her at the noon bell, and they came politely, which was somehow worse than chains would have been.

There were three of them in the refectory when I came down for bread: two crown men in ash-grey with the sun-and-shackle badge stitched at the shoulder, and their local adjunct, a soft-voiced temple lawyer named Undermaster Pell who had baptized half the children in the lower town and now stood with his hands folded while strangers read out a decree in his dining hall. The decree was short. I have a scribe's memory for documents, and I can still set every word of it down today without effort, the way you can set down the shape of a wound.

*By order of the Crown, in the matter of the preservation of the Valley Peace: all persons found to carry the unsanctioned gift are to be surrendered to the offices of the Holy Inquiry, for examination, correction, or purging, according to finding. Sheltering, concealing, or failing to report such persons constitutes complicity, and complicity shall be accounted as the crime itself.*

Complicity shall be accounted as the crime itself. I heard those words and felt the ring shift against my ribs, though of course it didn't move; linen doesn't stretch; rings don't breathe. But I felt it, the way you feel a name being called in a language you don't speak.

Sef was brought in from the kitchens with her hands still dusted white. One of the crown men held a thing I had never seen outside the archive's oldest plates: a lodestone needle, long as a forearm, suspended in a glass case, its point swinging slow and hungry. They call it a *divining needle*. It points at the gift the way a compass points north, and it pointed at Sef the moment she crossed the threshold, straining toward her like a dog at a rope's end, and the whole refectory watched the needle and understood what it meant, and Sef watched it too, and I will carry to my grave the exact expression on her face when she realized that everyone in the room had known something about her that she herself had never been told.

"I don't—" she said. "I don't have anything. I burn bread. I'm slow with the mangles. Sister Verity says—"

"The needle does not lie," said the taller inquisitor. He had a pleasant face, which I noted with a kind of detached horror, the way you note a well-made instrument of any kind. "Novice, when did you first make something move without touching it?"

"I never—"

"You are not in trouble," he said gently. "This is not a punishment. It is a mercy. The valley is kept clean so that the many may live in peace, and the few must be corrected so that the peace holds. You understand peace, surely. A kitchen girl understands keeping things clean."

And Sef, who was fifteen and terrified and had been taught since the cradle that the crown speaks with the voice of heaven, nodded. That was the worst of it. She nodded. She let them take her elbow in that courteous grip and walk her toward the courtyard, toward the pyre, and she went the way novices go to prayers, obedient and bewildered, glancing back once at the faces of the people who had fed her for three years.

Nobody met her eyes. I made sure mine were on my bread. Complicity, I thought, shall be accounted as the crime itself, and I understood suddenly that the decree had not been written for the gifted at all. It had been written for us. For the refectory full of people studying their bowls. The pyre was for Sef; the decree was for me.

The ring hummed.

It was very quiet, under the edge of hearing, but I felt it in my teeth, in the bones of my wrists, a single sustained note like a struck bowl that refuses to finish ringing. I pressed my palm flat against my sternum as if I had a cramp. Across the table, Brother Cassian frowned at me. "Indigestion," I said. "The fish."

He returned to his accounts. The hum faded. And I sat in the house of the god whose silence we had all been raised to call holiness, holding a stolen relic against my heart, and understood that I had already chosen a side, days ago, in a flooded cellar, with a pen in my hand. I just hadn't had the courage to know it until now.

* * *

They questioned the staff in the afternoon. Of course they did. Procedure is procedure, and the crown men were thorough the way rot is thorough.

They took us one at a time into the almonry, where the divining needle hung in its glass case on a stand, swinging in tiny idle arcs, pointing at nothing, at everything, at the general faint magnetism of ordinary life. One by one the brothers and sisters and senior novices of Saltmere temple stood before it and were found empty, and were dismissed with a nod, relieved, and I watched each of them walk out wearing the particular shame of the spared.

When my turn came, the tall inquisitor — his name, I learned from the warrant he consulted, was Ser Halvard Vess — looked at me a moment longer than he had looked at the others.

"Gideon Iversen," he read. "Debt-bound. Temple property, indenture outstanding... thirty-one years at current accrual." He glanced up, and his pleasant face did something almost kind. "That is a long sentence, scribe. Do you resent it?"

"It is the terms of my bond," I said. My voice came out level. I was surprised by that. "Resentment is not in the ledger."

"No," he said softly. "It never is. That is why we bring the needle."

He gestured me forward. I stood before the glass case, hands folded, eyes lowered, the picture of a woman who has spent her life being examined by institutions. The needle swung. Idle arc, idle arc. It did not strain toward me. Whatever slept in the ring against my ribs, it slept deeper than the needle's hunger, or the linen and my flesh were enough to muffle it, or — and this thought arrived unbidden and unwelcome — the needle was not looking for dead things, and the ring was not exactly alive either, but something else, something older than the categories the crown's instruments had been built to sort.

Vess watched the needle. Then he watched me.

"You were in the archive cellar last week," he said. "During the flooding. The recovery lists show your mark on the intake records."

"My duty includes the registers," I said. "The cellar flooded; I recorded losses."

"And recoveries?"

"Waterlogged accounts. Three crates of pre-Reformation tithe rolls. Nothing of value." Each sentence was a stone laid flat and true, and I laid them the way I'd been trained to lay them, and I hated how good the training was. "Nothing of value was recovered."

"Nothing," said Vess.

"Nothing of value," I said, and here it was, the moment, the hinge, the crack in my ordered world widening into a doorway — because a scribe knows the difference between a denial and a qualification, and so does an inquisitor, and I had just handed him one, deliberately, the way you hand a guard a coin with your fingers arranged wrong so he'll look at your fingers. Let him chew on the phrase. Let him think the temple's pedantry was evading him rather than that a debt-girl in a grey habit was hiding a king's relic in her bodice. Small lies survive by being interesting in the wrong direction.

Vess considered me. Somewhere beyond the wall, I could hear hammering: the last of the pyre's construction, nails going in, brisk and competent.

"The valley has been clean for two hundred years," he said conversationally. "Do you know why, scribe? Most people think it is geography. Salt kills magic; everyone learns that as children. But salt kills magic everywhere, slowly, the way winter kills grass. Here it died *utterly*. Root and seed. There is a reason, and the reason is written in documents older than your temple, and my office exists because those documents are beginning to surface." He smiled. "Flooded cellars, for instance. Old things giving way. The water table rising after two centuries, bringing up what was sunk to be forgotten. You would be astonished, scribe, what the water gives back, when the binding that holds it begins to fail."

My blood went cold and then hot. He knew — not about the ring, perhaps, not specifically, but about the category of thing the ring belonged to, and he was fishing, pleasantly, patiently, the way a man fishes who has all day and a warrant.

"If such documents surfaced," I said, "they would be surrendered to the inquiry."

"They would," he agreed. "That is the law. And the penalty for failure to surrender?" He didn't wait. "Complicity. Accounted as the crime itself." He tapped the warrant once with one finger. "I say this to everyone, scribe, because most people believe they are not the kind of person who hides things. And then the water gives something back, and they look at it, and they hold it one hour longer than they should, and by the second hour they are no longer innocent, only undiscovered." His pleasant eyes rested on mine. "You are not the kind of person who hides things. Are you?"

"No," I said.

It was the first lie I had ever told to power, and it came out of me smooth and dry as a page, and I heard myself say it and felt — this is the shameful part, the part I set down because a ledger that omits entries is worthless — I felt *strong*. Not guilty. Guilt came later, at night, in waves, with Sef's face on it. In that moment, standing before the crown's needle and the crown's man, lying with a straight spine for the first time in thirty-one years of bonded servitude, I felt the specific cold exhilaration of a debtor who realizes the ledger works both ways: that every record can be altered, that authority is only writing that everyone has agreed to believe, and that I, of all people, knew exactly how such writing was made.

"Dismissed," said Vess.

I bowed. I walked out through the almonry door into the late light, past the courtyard, where the pyre stood finished and waiting, dark against the salt-white evening, and the hammering had stopped, and men were bringing torches.

* * *

I did not sleep. I sat at my desk in the novice dormitory long after the bell of silence, with a stub of candle I was not permitted, and I did sums.

Not ledger sums. The other kind. The kind where you add up what a thing costs.

If I surrendered the ring tomorrow, I bought back my innocence — or the appearance of it, which in Saltmere is the only kind on offer. Vess might even honor the confession; the crown prefers repentance; it makes better doctrine. I would remain a debt-bound scribe until my body wore out, copying figures for a temple that sold a fifteen-year-old girl to a fire in the space of an afternoon, and the valley would stay clean, and silent, and holy, and empty, and I would die as I had lived, a line item.

If I kept the ring, I was already guilty under the decree. Complicity, accounted as the crime itself. Every hour it stayed against my ribs deepened a debt that no temple kept books on, a debt with no interest schedule and no discharge date, owed to a crown that burned children in courtyards and called the smoke mercy.

And underneath both columns, in the margin where honest accountants write the questions they cannot enter in the main book, one more sum:

The needle had not found the ring. Vess believed the valley's emptiness was engineered — a *binding*, he'd called it, a word that means a working, a making, a thing with a maker. The ring hummed when the pyre was built, as if it recognized the occasion. As if it had opinions. Things that are merely objects do not have opinions about fires.

I turned it in the candlelight, unwrapped at last, white as a knucklebone, older than the temple, older probably than the valley's two hundred years of silence. On its inner curve, so fine I needed the candle nearly against my eye to catch it, ran an inscription. Not letters. Figures. Columns of them, tiny as mites, ranked and ruled like —

Like a ledger.

I sat very still. Outside, far off across the flats, a light bloomed where the pyre was, too distant to see flames, only a smudge of orange against the stars, and I made myself watch it because watching seemed like the least I owed. Sef had never moved anything without touching it in her life. Sef burned bread. The needle had found her anyway, because the crown's arithmetic counts a spark and a conflagration as the same figure, and the valley's peace required the column balanced.

The ring hummed once, softly, as the light flared — a note of grief or recognition or rage, I couldn't tell, and then went still.

I wrapped it back in the altar linen. And I blew out the candle, and lay in the dark doing the only thing I knew how to do with an unbearable truth, which was to copy it out, silently, word by word, until I could hold the whole of it in memory:

*I am already complicit. Therefore I am already guilty. Therefore the only question left is what I am guilty of.*

In the morning, the archive register would need an entry reconciled — the cellar inventory, the missing weight, the gap where a ring should be listed and wasn't. Gaps get audited. Gaps get found. Unless, I thought, staring into the dark with my heart going like a hammer in a belfry, unless someone with a scribe's hands and nothing left to lose got to the record first, and wrote the gap shut.

I had until the audit on Thursday.

I did not yet know that I would not spend those two days alone.
