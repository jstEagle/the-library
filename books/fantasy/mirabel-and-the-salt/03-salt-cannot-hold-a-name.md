## 3. Salt Cannot Hold a Name

The temple at Saltmere kept its dead ledgers in a flooded cellar, and that was the first thing Mirabel liked about it. Anything a place tried to drown was worth stealing.

She crouched in the reed-choked drainage channel behind the tithe barn for the better part of an hour, watching the temple's night routine with the patience of someone who had done this eleven times before and buried two of the people who'd taught her how. Saltmere's temple was small as temples went — three bells, forty clerks, one abbot with a taste for candied ginger he recorded in the accounts as *medicinal provisions* — but it sat on the only fresh aquifer in thirty miles of salt flat, and its walls were older than the valley's binding. Old walls remembered things. That was why she was here.

The ring was here too. She was almost sure of it.

*Almost* was a word she'd learned to live on.

The moon did her no favors — fat and white and stupid over the salt flats, throwing enough light to read by. But Saltmere had no dogs; nothing lived long in this valley that needed to smell anything. The binding ate magic like rust ate iron, but it had never been choosy about what else it starved. Even the temple's famous silence had a wrongness to it, Mirabel thought. A hush that wasn't peace. A held breath.

She moved when the third bell finished its last sour note and the night clerk began his rounds of the lamps. Forty steps from the drainage grate to the cellar door. She counted them under her breath out of habit, though her feet knew the way without numbers.

The cellar door was locked, which offended her less than it should have. Locks were honest. Locks said: *someone cared about this once.* She pressed her palm flat against the iron hasp and whispered the smallest working she knew — not a spell, really, more a suggestion, the kind of thing that would gutter out before it reached the end of her arm in any other valley. Here in Saltmere it barely flickered at all. That was the other reason she'd come herself instead of sending one of the Circle's younger runners. In the valley where magic died, nobody guarded against it. The binding was the best lockpick she'd ever owned.

The tumblers sighed open.

Inside, the dark smelled of brine and vellum and something older underneath, mineral and cold, like licking a stone in winter. Water stood shin-deep on the floor — the flooding wasn't seasonal anymore, the whole cellar slowly drowning year by year while the clerks above wrote their tidy entries about *moisture damage, ongoing.* Shelves rose out of the black water in ranks, and every shelf sagged with ledgers wrapped in oilcloth, hundreds of them, thousands, the temple's memory stacked and rotting by inches.

Mirabel waded in. Her boots found the submerged flagstones by feel. Somewhere ahead, in the deepest rank of shelves, the map the Circle had bought from a disgraced archivist said there was a niche walled off from the rest — a cache, sealed maybe two hundred years ago, back when sealing things in temples was still how you hid them from kings.

The ring was supposed to be in the cache.

A salt-white band, no stone, no marking anyone could read — except that the archivist swore his grandfather had seen it glow once, in 1813, the night the old abbot died. And the Circle's oldest member, a woman named Corvane who had spent sixty years collecting fragments of the binding's history the way other people collected debts, had gone white as the flats when Mirabel described it. *Salt-white,* Corvane had said. *Made from the same salt as the valley. If it exists, it was cut from the binding itself. It isn't a key to the king's cage, girl. It might be a piece of the cage.*

Which meant it might be a piece that opened.

Mirabel turned down the fourth aisle, water dragging at her knees now, and put her hand on the oilcloth bundles one after another, reading the spines by touch. *Tithes, 1744–1761. Tithes, 1762–1779. Debts, Assessed.* The temple didn't keep history here. It kept arithmetic. Whole generations of the valley reduced to columns of figures — who owed what, who paid, who died owing. She'd seen the same shelves in six other temples across the free cities, and every time she thought the same thing: give me one book in this room that isn't a bill.

Her fingers stopped.

There. At the aisle's end, where the shelving met the wall, the stones changed color — newer mortar, a seam almost invisible under a century of salt bloom. The cache. She worked her knife into the seam and levered, gently, gently, the way you'd ease a splinter from flesh. A block the size of a prayer book shifted, ground, and came free into her hands.

Behind it: darkness, dry air, and a smell that made the hair on her arms lift. Not dust. Not age. Something like ozone, like the air before lightning, which should have been impossible in a valley where lightning had been bound out of the sky for four hundred years.

She reached in.

Her fingertips found cloth first — linen, dry as paper — and then, wrapped inside it, a circle of something smooth and cold. She drew it out and unwrapped it right there, standing knee-deep in drowned ledgers, because patience had never been her virtue and she wanted to know if she'd crossed two hundred miles of salt for a button.

Salt-white. No stone. No marking.

And warm. Warm as a held hand, in a cellar cold enough to ache.

"Oh," Mirabel breathed, and the sound seemed very loud. "Oh, you're real."

The ring lay in her palm and did nothing at all, which somehow made it worse. Things that were merely objects didn't feel warm. Things that were merely objects didn't make the fine hairs along her forearms stand up like a cat's hackles. She'd handled relics before — frauds mostly, and once a genuine saint's fingerbone that had done nothing but smell bad — and none of them had felt like this, like holding a coal that hadn't decided yet whether to burn you.

She was still staring at it when the lamp came around the corner of the aisle.

Not a lamp. A lantern, hooded, carried low, and behind it a figure in clerk's grey with ink stains to the elbow and a face that went through surprise, calculation, and something that looked horribly like recognition in the space of a single breath.

They stared at each other across ten feet of black water.

Mirabel's mind did what it always did at speed: catalogued exits (one, behind her, blocked by the intruder), weapons (knife, boot; knife, sleeve; the ring, currently useless), and odds (poor). The clerk was young — mid-twenties, maybe — a woman, narrow-built, with the kind of careful, watchful face that belonged to people who had learned early that being noticed was dangerous. Her eyes dropped to the ring in Mirabel's hand, then to the opened cache, then back up, and Mirabel watched her arrive at the obvious conclusion and flinch away from it.

"You're the thief," the clerk said. Her voice was steady. Too steady. "From the archive list. The Vellum Row thefts."

"I'm a great many things," Mirabel said. "Tonight I'm a pilgrim."

"Pilgrims don't pick locks."

"Devout ones do." She let a smile happen, the easy crooked one that had talked her past three checkpoints and one marriage proposal. It didn't land. The clerk's expression didn't move at all, and Mirabel revised her assessment upward: not a frightened functionary. A frightened functionary with discipline. Worse.

"Put it down," the clerk said. "Put it back, and walk out, and I'll say I found the seal broken and nothing missing. I'll say I don't know who did it."

"And why," Mirabel said pleasantly, "would you lie to your own temple?"

Something crossed the clerk's face then — fast, quickly mastered, but Mirabel had spent fifteen years reading faces across smugglers' tables, and she caught it. Not loyalty. Not fear of her. Guilt. Fresh guilt, days old at most, the kind that sat in the eyes like silt in water.

"Because," the clerk said slowly, "if I report it, they'll search the archive. All of it. Every shelf." Her jaw tightened. "And there are things in this cellar that shouldn't be found. By anyone."

Well. That was interesting.

"That's a strange thing for a law-abiding scribe to say," Mirabel murmured.

"I'm not law-abiding. I'm debt-bound. There's a difference, and the difference is that the law doesn't own me, the ledger does." The clerk lifted the lantern a little, and the light caught the ring, and — Mirabel saw it clearly, saw it happen — the light *bent*. Just slightly. Just at the edge, the way heat shimmer bends it over summer stone. The clerk saw it too. Her breath stopped audibly.

For a long moment neither of them moved. The water dripped somewhere. The temple's held-breath silence pressed down on both of them.

"It's warm," the clerk said at last, in a different voice. Smaller. "I found it in the flood debris eight months ago. I should have turned it in. I told myself I would turn it in." She laughed, short and bitter, at herself. "I told myself a lot of things. I'm Gideon, by the way. Gideon Iversen. Scribe, third class, indentured, account number—" She stopped, as if she'd heard herself reciting her own inventory and hated it. "It doesn't matter. You're going to kill me or rob me or both, so my account number doesn't matter."

"I'm not going to kill you," Mirabel said. "Killing clerks is bad for business. Word gets around."

"Rob me, then."

"There's nothing to rob. You're poorer than I am, and I slept in a culvert last week." Mirabel took a slow step forward, testing, the way you test ice. The clerk didn't retreat, which Mirabel noted and disliked; retreat would have been easier to manage. "Here's what's going to happen. I'm taking the ring. You're going to stand very still and remember my face badly. In the morning you'll report a broken seal and a damp cellar, and your conscience will trouble you exactly as much as it already does, which is to say, a great deal, and uselessly."

"No," Gideon said.

Mirabel paused. "No?"

"No." The clerk set the lantern down on a shelf above the waterline, deliberately, freeing both her hands, which was either very brave or very stupid, and Mirabel was beginning to suspect it was both. "You don't understand what that is. Or maybe you do, and that's worse. Eight months I've had it, and I know three things about it. One: it hums. Not aloud — in the teeth, in the bones, like a struck bell that won't finish ringing. Two: it hums loudest near certain people. Three—" she hesitated, and Mirabel watched her decide to spend something costly— "three weeks ago, when the crown inquisitors built their pyre in the temple yard and burned a novice named Tally Fenwick alive for having the gift, the ring screamed. I heard it through three floors of stone. Whatever that thing is, it hates them. It hates what they're doing. And I have spent eight months telling myself that a ring can't hate, because if it can hate, then it can want, and if it can want, then I've been keeping a prisoner in my apron pocket while I copied tithes."

The cellar was very quiet.

Mirabel looked down at the ring in her palm. Warm. Patient. She thought of Corvane's white face, of the binding laid over the whole valley like a lid on a pot, of the king in the east whose name the free cities still argued about — dead? deposed? dreaming? — and she felt the shape of the thing click together, all its edges meeting, the way a lock feels when the last tumbler drops.

"You know what the binding is," she said quietly. "Don't you. You've guessed."

"I know the valley is wrong," Gideon said. "I've known since I was six years old and couldn't tell you why. Nothing grows here that doesn't grow angry. Magic doesn't die at our borders, it dies *in us*, like we're the wound it's trying to close. And everyone walks around calling it peace." Her chin came up. "So yes. I've guessed. Now guess this: if you take that ring out of here tonight, the inquisitors will tear this temple apart looking for it, and when they're done with the temple they'll start on the clerks, and I am the clerk whose hands have touched it. I'm already dead. I just haven't been scheduled yet."

"So come with me."

The words were out before Mirabel had decided to say them, which annoyed her profoundly. Fifteen years of smuggling and she still occasionally forgot the first rule: never make the offer until you've priced the person. But the clerk's face did something complicated — hope and terror fighting openly, like dogs — and Mirabel understood, with a lurch, that she had just offered the one thing Saltmere's ledgers had no column for.

"No," Gideon said again. But softer. And her eyes went to the ring, and stayed there, hungry and afraid in equal measure.

"Then we have a problem," Mirabel said, "because I'm not leaving without it, and you're not letting me leave with it, and I've got a knife and you've got—" she glanced at the shelf— "a lantern and a strong command of accounting."

"I've got the archive," Gideon said. "One shout, and the night clerk comes, and then the watch, and then you find out how well salt holds a hedge-mage. They burn us slower here, you know. The wood's damp."

"They burn you at all?"

"Since the decree. Since the inquisitors came." Gideon's voice stayed level, but her knuckles had gone white around the edge of the shelf. "Forty-one gifted persons surrendered in the valley this season. Tally was the twelfth they burned. She was sixteen. She could make flowers open out of season." She swallowed. "So no, I don't imagine you'll be treated gently. And neither will I, once they learn what I've been hiding. Which means, thief, that you and I are standing in the same flood, and only one of us brought a boat."

Mirabel laughed before she could stop herself — a real laugh, startled out of her, echoing off the drowned shelves. The clerk blinked, visibly recalibrating.

"You're funny," Mirabel said. "Nobody warned me scribes were funny."

"Nobody warns anybody about anything in this valley. That's rather the point of it." Gideon straightened. Ink-stained, soaked to the knee, guilty as sin and twice as tired, she looked suddenly — there was no other word for it — *formidable.* "Here are my terms. You take the ring. I don't stop you; I couldn't, and I'm no longer sure I want to. But you take me with it. Out of the valley. Past the boundary. To wherever people like Tally Fenwick should have been taken instead of onto pyres. You do that, and I'll give you something better than a stolen relic."

"Which is?"

Gideon reached into her apron and drew out a folded sheet of paper, water-spotted but legible even in the lantern light. Mirabel took it, angled it toward the flame, and felt her pulse change.

Ledger pages. Debt records — the temple's own internal assessments, the ones that never left the counting room. Names, amounts, terms, and beside each name in a cramped, precise hand, annotations: *transferred to crown service, 14th of the Reed Moon. Sold to inquisitorial writ, price settled in indulgences.* Page after page of the valley's gifted, tracked not by their gifts but by their debts, sold north like cattle with the paperwork done properly.

"The inquisitors don't hunt the gifted," Gideon said. "They buy them. Every person they've burned was someone whose debt got called in first. This is the schedule, Mirabel-of-no-surname-I'll-ask-for. This is who they take next month, and next quarter, and next year. There's a girl on page four who owes nine marks and can light candles with a look. There's a ferryman on page seven who can find lost things, and they've already sold him twice." She tapped the paper with one ink-black finger. "You want to fight the binding? Fight the bookkeeping. Nobody ever taught you that, did they? Nobody ever taught anyone. Everyone thinks tyranny wears armor. Mostly it wears a green eyeshade and smells of lamp oil."

Mirabel stared at her.

Fifteen years. Fifteen years of running gifted children through culverts and over salt in the dark, of guessing and bribing and losing people to the pyres because she'd always been one step behind the hunters and had assumed the hunters were simply faster than she was. And the whole time — the *whole time* — there had been a schedule. A ledger. Sitting in a counting room, waiting for someone with the wit to steal it.

"You," Mirabel said slowly, "are wasted on tithes."

"I'm wasted on everything," Gideon said. "That's rather the story of my life. Do we have terms or don't we?"

Outside, far above them, the temple bell began to toll — not the hour, not the offices. The alarm pattern. Three, pause, three. Someone had found the drainage grate. Or the unlocked cellar door. Or the noise of two women arguing in a flooded crypt, carrying up through old stone the way voices do when the valley's silence wants to betray you.

Gideon went pale. Mirabel went calm, which was the opposite of what people expected and therefore useful.

"Terms," Mirabel said, snuffing the lantern with two fingers and plunging them both into the dark, where the ring lay warm as a heartbeat in her closed fist. "But not here, and not through the front. Can you hold your breath for ninety seconds?"

"What?"

"The flood has a source. There's a spring channel under the west wall — the binding keeps it from rising, but the channel runs, and it runs *out.* I mapped it from the outside last autumn." She caught Gideon's wrist in the darkness, feeling the clerk's pulse hammering there, quick as a bird's. "Ninety seconds underwater, in the dark, following my hand. After that, salt flats, a horse tied in the reeds, and the longest night of your life. Or stay, and be page five."

Somewhere overhead, boots were crossing the nave. Many boots.

Gideon Iversen took one long, shaking breath — the breath of a woman stepping off the edge of her entire life — and said:

"Ninety seconds. Count them properly. I'll know if you cheat."

* * *

The spring channel was exactly as narrow and exactly as cold as Mirabel had promised, which was to say criminally so. They came up gasping in the reed beds outside the wall, salt crust cracking under their hands, the temple's alarm bells still tolling behind them like a heart with a stutter. The horse — a mean-eyed marsh pony named Bishop, because Mirabel named all her horses after clergy — stood exactly where she'd left him, and bit Gideon affectionately on the shoulder when she climbed up behind Mirabel's saddle.

They were half a mile out onto the white flats, the temple shrinking behind them into a smudge of torchlight and shouting, when Gideon spoke for the first time since the water.

"The ledgers," she said. "I only took the current schedule. There are twenty years of records back in that cellar. Twenty years of names."

"I know," Mirabel said, and put her heels to Bishop's ribs, and the salt flats opened before them, vast and white and moonlit, like a page no one had written on yet. "We'll come back for them."

She didn't say when. Neither of them asked. Ahead, the dark line of the boundary hills waited, and in Mirabel's fist the ring pulsed its slow, patient warmth — once, twice — like something that had been waiting four hundred years to be carried home.
