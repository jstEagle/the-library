## 4. The Debt Comes Due at Midnight

The temple woke me with a bell and a lie.

"Brother Aldous wants you in the counting room," said the novice at my door, a boy called Tam with chapped hands and the flat voice of someone reciting words he didn't understand. "Before matins. He says it's about the cellar inventory."

The cellar inventory. The flooded cellar where I had spent three years cataloguing drowned ledgers, and where, eleven days ago, I had pulled a salt-white ring from the silt of a broken strongbox and put it in my sleeve instead of on the manifest.

I dressed in the dark. My fingers knew the buttons without me. That was the thing about a life like mine — every motion of it was memorized, worn smooth as a ledger's spine, and when the world tilted you felt it in your hands before your head.

The ring was sewn into the hem of my underskirt. It had been there since the night of the pyre, since I'd lied to Inquisitor Vane with his kind gray eyes and told him no, Brother, I've never seen anything like that before, while a novice named Sef burned forty feet away for the crime of lighting candles without flint. The ring had hummed against my thigh through the whole of it, a low sweet note like a wet finger on a glass rim, and I had understood, standing in that crowd of singing smoke, that I was already damned. Lying to an inquisitor wasn't a step off a path. It was a path.

I followed Tam down the cold corridor. Candles guttered in their niches. Salt bloomed white along the mortar joints, the way it did everywhere in Saltmere, creeping up out of the ground like the valley itself was sweating.

Aldous was not alone.

Two men stood in the counting room, and neither wore temple gray. One was crown military — I knew the cut of a redcoat's coat even before I saw the sun-and-salt badge of the inquisition stitched at the collar. The other was worse. He was soft-bodied and precise, with ink-stained cuffs and a clerk's satchel, and he was reading a document with the particular pleasure of a man reading something he has waited all morning to read.

"Gideon," Aldous said. He wouldn't look at me. He was looking at the abacus in front of him, moving beads he wasn't counting. "This is Clerk Ferrow, attached to His Majesty's Commission for the Examination of Persons. He brings papers regarding your account."

My account. My debt. Nine years, four months, and some days I didn't let myself count anymore — the price of my mother's burial, compounded quarterly, owned by the Temple of the Tidewife since I was nineteen years old and too grief-stupid to read the contract's back page.

Ferrow looked up. He had the face of a man who enjoyed other people's arithmetic.

"Gideon Iversen," he said. "Scribe, second class. Outstanding principal of thirty-one crowns, twelve shillings." He smiled. "Or, if you prefer the older measure — nine years of labor."

"I prefer the older measure," I said. My voice came out steady. Nine years of practice at sounding steady in front of men who owned me.

"Then you'll be pleased to know it's been settled."

The room went strange and quiet, the way rooms do just before they hurt you. Aldous moved one bead. Click.

"The temple," Ferrow continued, laying the document flat and turning it toward me with two fingers, "has elected to discharge its own obligations to the crown by transferring certain assets. Your debt is now held directly by the Commission." He tapped a wax seal the color of dried blood. "Effective last midnight. You're crown property now, Mistress Iversen. Congratulations. Your interest rate has been forgiven."

I read the page because reading was the only weapon I had ever been given. Transfer of encumbered persons, remitted to the Commission for the Examination of Persons, per decree of — the words swam and steadied. Per decree. For services rendered and services anticipated.

Services anticipated.

"What services," I said.

"You're a gifted-persons specialist now," Ferrow said pleasantly. "You catalogued the archive cellar, did you not? Including the reliquary holdings?" His eyes came up and rested on mine with the weight of a hand. "The Commission has been very interested in what the Temple of the Tidewife keeps in its cellars. Very interested indeed. And now we have our own expert on staff. Indentured, of course. Room and board provided." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "At the examination house."

The examination house. Everyone in Saltmere knew what happened at the examination house, the same way everyone knows what happens at the bottom of a well. Sef had been taken there first, before the pyre. Sef had come out of it able to name every person who had ever shown her kindness, which was how they'd built their list.

Behind him, Aldous finally looked at me. There was nothing in his face I could use — no apology, no warning, no mercy. Just the mild blankness of a man balancing books. The temple owed the crown for the theft from its own cellar, and the crown had named its price, and the price was me. I was the payment. I had spent nine years being currency, and I'd never once been spent all at once until now.

"When do I report," I asked, because asking was cheaper than screaming.

"Now," said Ferrow. "There's a cart."

* * *

They let me pack a bag. Two shirts, my pen case, my mother's bone comb. The soldier stood in my cell doorway while I folded things, watching my hands the way a man watches a dog he suspects of biting.

The ring sat in my hem and did not hum. It had gone quiet since the pyre night, quiet as a held breath, and I had learned to hate the silence of it almost more than the sound. When it hummed, I knew it wanted something. When it was silent, I only knew that I did.

Tam caught me in the cloister walk. He was carrying candle-boxes he didn't need to carry, and he fell into step beside me for six paces, eyes forward, mouth barely moving.

"They're saying the thief came over the wall," he breathed. "Night before last. They're saying she left salt-prints on the scriptorium roof. Nobody walks on salt, Gideon. Nobody *leaves prints*."

"Then they're saying nonsense," I said.

He glanced at me then, quick and terrified, and I understood that he wasn't warning me. He was asking. Tam had lit candles without flint twice in his life, small warm flickers in the dormitory dark, and he had watched them take Sef, and he was seventeen years old and asking me with his whole rigid body whether the stories were true — whether there was a way out of this valley that didn't end in ash.

"There's a cart waiting," I said aloud, for the soldier's benefit, and squeezed Tam's shoulder once, hard, the closest thing to an answer I could give him with a redcoat ten feet away.

The cart stood in the forecourt. Gray sky, gray salt-haze, the mountains eastward blurred to smudges. Ferrow climbed up beside the driver with his satchel hugged to his chest like a child. The soldier took the bench across from me and set his halberd between us, butt down, blade up, so that the whole ride would be conducted under its shadow.

We went out through the gate. I did not look back at the temple. Looking back is how they catch you — I understood that suddenly, completely, the way you understand a thing you've always known. As long as I kept my face forward, I was still a woman being taken somewhere. The moment I turned around, I'd be a woman leaving.

The road ran white and straight toward the salt flats. And walking beside the road, half a mile out on the crust where nothing grew, was a figure in a patched brown coat, pacing the cart at an easy amble, as if she'd been walking that direction all her life and merely happened to be going the same way.

Mirabel.

She didn't look at me. She didn't look at anyone. She walked with her thumbs hooked in her belt and her face tipped up into the gray light like a woman enjoying weather, and if I hadn't caught her mid-theft in the cellar eleven days ago — if I hadn't spent one furious, electric quarter-hour with a stranger holding a lamp over my strongest box while I held a letter opener over her heart — I'd have taken her for a drover.

She had come back. She hadn't gotten the ring — I'd caught her, we'd fought, she'd fled empty-handed over the wall with my lamp oil burning on her sleeve — and instead of cutting her losses she had walked out onto the salt and was now following the cart carrying the woman who had it, at a distance calculated to be invisible to everyone except the woman who knew to look.

The ring hummed. Once. Soft. Like a word spoken under a breath.

I looked at the soldier. He was watching the horizon. I looked at Ferrow. He was asleep sitting up, mouth open, a man who had already counted everything worth counting today.

I looked at the halberd between us, blade up, and thought: *nine years, four months.* Thought: *Sef's name on a list she couldn't stop giving them.* Thought: *the examination house, room and board, services anticipated.*

Thought: *nothing left to lose weighs nothing at all.*

* * *

It happened at the flats' edge, where the road gave up pretending and became a track, and the track became a suggestion, and the suggestion dissolved into whiteness running to the horizon in every direction. The driver slowed to pick his line between the crust and the soft ground beneath. The cart lurched. The halberd swayed.

I took it with both hands and shoved.

Not at the soldier — I'm not stupid, and he was bigger than me and awake. I shoved it sideways, off-balance, butt swinging into Ferrow's sleeping shoulder hard enough to wake him into a squawk, and in the half-second of confusion — the soldier grabbing for the shaft, Ferrow flailing, the driver hauling reins — I went over the side of the cart into the salt.

I hit crust, rolled, came up running. Behind me: shouting, the crack of the driver's whip, boots hitting salt. Ahead: Mirabel, no longer ambling, no longer pretending, sprinting flat-out across the white with her coat tails snapping, and laughing — actually laughing, a wild bright sound that the valley swallowed instantly, because everything in Saltmere got swallowed.

"This way!" she shouted. "Don't follow my prints!"

"What?"

"Don't follow my *prints*—" and she cut hard left, and I understood, and cut right, and behind us I heard the soldier bellow "Which one! Which tracks!" to a driver who had no idea, because on salt, in haze, in panic, two sets of fleeing footprints are a riddle and every second is a gift.

The crust boomed under my feet — that deep drum-note the flats make when you run them, the ground ringing like a struck bell under miles of salt. My lungs burned. My hem tore on a ridge of crust and I felt the ring shift against my ankle and hum again, louder now, urgent, a tuning fork pressed to my bones.

Something cracked the air past my ear. A crossbow bolt, punching a neat black hole in the white. Then another, wide. Then the shouts fell behind, thinning, the haze taking them, and I realized Mirabel had stopped running and was standing with her arms crossed, watching me come, breathing like a woman who'd climbed a hill.

"They won't cross the flats deep," she said. "Cart horses, crown men. They'll ride the perimeter to the ferry post and wait for word. We have until word travels." She looked me over — mud to the knees, hem torn, chest heaving — and something in her face shifted from appraisal to something almost like respect. "You brought the ring."

"You came back for it."

"I came back for *you*, as it turns out, though I didn't know it yet." She turned and started walking, fast, east, into the blinding white. "Keep up. We want the old causeway markers before dark or the flats will have us."

I stood there one moment longer. Behind me, far off, the cart creaked away north with my bag, my pen case, my mother's comb, my name in a clerk's ledger marked TRANSFERRED. Ahead of me was nothing — white salt, gray sky, a stranger, and a stolen relic sewn into my skirt humming like a heart that wasn't mine.

Nine years I had belonged to the temple. Since midnight I had belonged to the crown. And here, on the dead white waste where magic couldn't live and neither could anything else, I belonged to no one at all.

It should have felt like terror. It did feel like terror. But underneath the terror, sick and bright and shameful, was something else — the feeling of a ledger closing, of a column summing to zero, of a debt finally paid in the only coin I'd ever fully owned.

Nothing left to lose weighs nothing at all. I could run forever.

"Hurry up, scribe," Mirabel called, not turning. "And tell me honestly — when you caught me in that cellar, were you ever actually going to use that letter opener?"

I ran to catch up. "No," I admitted.

"Good," she said. "Because I was never going to use the knife either. Now walk where I walk, exactly where I walk, and whatever hums at you tonight — don't answer it out loud."

The salt stretched ahead of us to the edge of the world, and somewhere out in that whiteness, faint as a memory, the ring began to sing.
