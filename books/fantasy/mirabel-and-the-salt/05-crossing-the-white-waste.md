## 5. Crossing the White Waste

The salt began three miles past the temple walls, and I felt it before I saw it.

It came on like a tide going out of my body. First the warmth in my fingertips guttered — that low banked fire I'd carried since I was six years old, since the night my mother pressed her palm over mine and said, *never let them see it, never let them name you* — and then the deeper things went quiet. The sense of water in the ground. The whisper of iron and where it lay. The small constant hum of the world talking to itself, which most people never hear because they've never had to lose it.

By the time we reached the first white crust, I was just a woman walking.

I hated it immediately.

"Keep moving," I said, because if I stopped talking I would start listening to the silence inside my own skull, and I didn't trust what I'd find there. "The boundary's not a line. It's a slope. Every mile it gets worse."

Gideon looked at me sideways. She had a way of looking sideways that made me feel audited. Ink-stained fingers, ledger-keeper's eyes, a satchel clutched to her chest like the ring inside it might bolt. Three days out of Saltmere and she still walked like someone expecting to be called back.

"Worse how?" she asked.

"You'll see." I didn't say: *I'm already seeing it.* I didn't say: *I have crossed this waste four times, and every crossing I leave a little more of myself on the white, and I don't know if it's waiting for me on the other side or gone for good.* Scribes want exact figures. I wasn't ready to give her one.

The salt flats stretched east like a page no one had written on yet. White to the horizon, cracked into polygons the size of dinner tables, each one edged in a darker mineral stain so the whole waste looked tiled, deliberate, built by something patient. No wind. No birds. The sun sat high and stupid and gave light without warmth, the way a portrait gives you a face without a person.

We walked until the light went long and copper, and then we walked into the dark, because stopping before full dark meant giving the salt an extra night to work on us.

I made camp in the lee of a salt ridge — a wind-carved wall of crusted white taller than a wagon, one of the few features the flat offered — and got a fire going from the pitch-pine I'd hauled out of the hills on my back for exactly this reason. There is nothing on the waste that burns. That's the thing no one tells you about a place where magic dies: it isn't just the gifts that go. It's everything magic ever touched. Nothing grows. Nothing rots. The old caravanserai ruins we'd passed at noon still stood sharp-cornered after two hundred years, because even decay needs a little life in it to do its work.

Gideon sat across the fire with her knees drawn up and her satchel in her lap, and watched me feed the flames with the expression of a woman taking dictation.

"You're different," she said finally.

"I'm starving and filthy. Everyone's different."

"No. I mean—" She gestured, awkward, at all of me. "At the temple you moved like you were hiding something. Now you move like you've lost it."

I should have lied. Lying is half my trade; I smuggle people by smuggling stories. But the silence inside me was loud, and tired people tell the truth.

"The valley kills it," I said. "Magic. You knew that. Everyone in Saltmere knows that — it's the first thing they teach you in the temple school, isn't it? *The valley is blessed plain, the valley is honest earth, the gift cannot live here and that is why we are safe.*"

"That's what it says." Her voice had gone careful. Ledger-careful. "In the catechism."

"And you believed it?"

"I believed it was true," she said. "Whether it was blessed seemed less important."

That was so exactly Gideon that I almost laughed. Almost. My ribs did something complicated instead.

"It's true," I said. "It's just not natural. There's a difference, and the difference has a body count." I poked the fire harder than it needed. "Bindings aren't blessings, scribe. A blessing is a door held open. A binding is a door nailed shut. And when you nail a door shut hard enough, long enough, the wood starts to scream."

She was quiet for a while. The fire cracked. Somewhere far off across the flats, the salt ticked and settled as it cooled, a sound like slow applause from an empty house.

"What kind of binding holds a whole valley?" she asked at last.

"One laid by a king," I said, "with a relic made for the purpose. One strong enough that it doesn't stop at the valley. It bleeds. It thins everything for fifty miles around — that's why the free cities tax magic instead of burning it, why the hedge-craft out here is weak tea, why the gifted who flee Saltmere arrive half-starved in ways food can't fix. The binding is a wound in the world and we've all been living downwind of it." I looked at her. "And the crown knows. The crown has always known. Why do you think the inquisitors burn the gifted in the valley but only brand them everywhere else? You don't destroy the harvest. You just keep the field fallow."

I hadn't meant to say that last part. It slipped out the way truth does when your defenses are guttering along with everything else.

Gideon's hand had gone to the satchel. Through the canvas I could see the shape of it — the ring, salt-white, no bigger than a signet. The thing I'd broken into a temple to steal and come away with a librarian instead.

"You felt it die too," she said quietly. "Just now. Crossing the line."

"I feel it dying every step," I said. "Yes."

"Why keep walking east, then? Toward the capital, toward the binding's heart. It'll be worse there, won't it? For you."

I fed another branch to the fire and took my time answering, because the honest answer was embarrassing.

"Because I've been mortal before," I said. "For three years, once. I survived it. Barely, badly, but I survived it, and I learned the one thing the gift never taught me." I met her eyes. "How to be afraid properly. Fear keeps you alive out here. Magic makes you brave, and brave people die on the salt."

* * *

We traded histories the way traders trade anything — grudgingly, in small lots, each waiting to see what the other would pay.

She went first, which surprised me. Maybe the dark did it. Maybe the fire. Maybe it was that we were two days from anyone who could hear us, and the waste presses secrets out of people like a press presses oil.

"My mother sold me to the temple when I was seven," Gideon said, staring into the flames. "Not sold, exactly. She owed them. Rent-debt, seed-debt, a funeral debt for my father — the temple lends for everything in Saltmere, did you know that? Weddings, births, coffins. There's no way to live there without borrowing, and no way to borrow without becoming theirs." She turned her hands over, showed me the ink stains like bruises. "They took me as payment-in-kind. A child scribe is worth eleven years of ledger labor against a debt of forty marks. I've been copying ever since. Interest compounds. You copy faster than the interest grows for about five years, and then you don't, and then you never will again."

"How much do you owe now?"

"Ninety-one marks." A pause. "Ninety-two as of last quarter-day."

"Forty became ninety-two."

"They're very thorough," she said, and there was something underneath the dryness, something with teeth in it, and I thought: *there she is. There's the woman who ran.*

I paid her back with my own coin, because fair is fair and because I wanted her to understand what she'd tied herself to.

"My mother was a river-witch on the Vell. Real craft, old craft — she could find drowned things, call fish, read a flood three days off. When the purges came up the river, she hid me in a flour barrel and told me to be flour. I was six." The fire popped. "She didn't make it. So I grew up in the reed-towns learning her craft secondhand, from her sisters, from stolen books, from trial and error and the occasional blistering. Hedge-mage. That's the polite word. Smuggler is the honest one. For nine years I've moved the gifted out of the valley — over the waste, through Harrowgate, up to the coast, wherever the Circle has a safehouse and a lie ready. Forty-one people. I keep count the way you keep count of marks."

"Why?"

"Because someone kept count of me," I said. "A woman named Osla pulled me out of a river-town purge when I was nineteen, carried me across this exact salt, and taught me the routes. She used to say: *the valley counts its debts, Mirabel. Someone has to count its people.*" I shrugged, or tried to; it came out stiff. "She died crossing, seven years ago. Cold took her in the open, third night out. I finished her route with her body on the sledge, because you don't leave people on the salt. Not even dead ones. Especially not dead ones."

Gideon absorbed that in silence. Then, in the voice of a woman reading a footnote she couldn't ignore:

"Three years. You said you were mortal once, for three years. What happened?"

Ah. She'd been saving that question. Ledger-keepers always are.

I could have deflected. Instead I heard myself say, "I bound someone."

Her head came up sharply.

"Not the way you're thinking. Not a cage, not a nail. A kindness-binding. My sister." The words tasted like ash and I made myself keep going, because Gideon deserved the truth and because I needed to practice saying it. "Wren. My little sister. Gifted like our mother — stronger, honestly, stronger than me. She was fourteen when the inquisitors reached our stretch of the Vell, and I panicked. I had a route, a boat, a plan — but plans fail, boats sink, and I couldn't protect her forever. So I did the unforgivable sensible thing. I bound her gift. Closed it. Sealed it under layers of craft so deep that no inquisitor's compass would ever twitch in her direction. She'd be safe. Safe and ordinary and alive."

"And?" Gideon asked, though I could see she already knew.

"And it worked," I said. "That's the horror of it. She was never caught, never questioned, never burned. She married a cooper. She has children. She's alive." I stared into the fire until my eyes watered. "And she hasn't truly woken since. Not the way she was. Something in the sealing went wrong — or maybe nothing went wrong, maybe this is just what bindings cost, maybe the gift and the sleeper are the same thing and I put out the lamp to save the house. She looks at me like I'm a stranger who owes her money. Which, I suppose, I am."

The silence stretched. On the salt, silence has weight; it leans on you.

"So when you argue against unbinding the king," Gideon said slowly, working it through the way she'd work through a contract, "it's not doctrine. It's not caution. You've seen what unbinding might mean — or what binding means — from the inside."

"I've seen both sides of the knot," I said. "Bind someone to save them, and you may kill what you loved. Unbind someone to free them, and you may loose the thing that destroys them. The king's binding was meant to hold back his death. What do you suppose happens to a man's death when you've kept it caged for thirty years?" I shook my head. "I don't know. Nobody knows. That's the point. Bindings are doors nailed shut, and behind every one of them is either a prisoner or a flood, and you don't find out which until the nails come out."

Gideon was quiet a long moment. Then she opened the satchel.

The ring sat in her palm, catching firelight the way ice catches it — not reflecting so much as holding, a cold little moon. Even here, even in the dead zone, it looked awake. It was the only thing on the entire waste that looked awake.

"It hums sometimes," she said. "Did you know that? At the pyre it hummed. When the novice — when they—" She stopped, jaw tight. "It hums when the gifted suffer. Or when they're near. I haven't decided which, and I hate that I have to decide."

I leaned forward despite myself. Craftless as I was right now, stripped to skin and fear, I still knew relics when I saw them. I'd spent nine years hearing rumors of this one — the Maker's seal, the key to the king's binding, the thing the Circle had sent me to steal.

"May I?"

She hesitated, then set it in my hand.

Cold. Colder than the salt at midnight, colder than the river in thaw, and under the cold, faint as a pulse through a wall, something turning over. Like a sleeper changing position. Like a door with something leaning against it from the other side.

"It's listening," I said, without meaning to.

"To what?"

"To us. To the route. To the waste." I turned it over. On the band's inner curve, fine as spiderwork, characters I half-recognized from forbidden books — the maker's script, the script of the binding. "This was cut by the same hand that nailed the valley shut. Which means it doesn't just unlock the door." I looked up at her across the fire. "It remembers the hand that locked it."

Gideon went very still. "Then whoever wears it—"

"—doesn't carry a key," I finished. "They carry a memory. The binding's own memory. Every mile we walk east, it'll show her more." I held the ring out. "Which is why it should stay with you, scribe. You're the one it chose to warm to. And I'm the one who's seen what remembering costs."

She took it back, and as her fingers closed over it, the firelight bent — just slightly, just for a heartbeat, the flames leaning toward her like grass toward wind — and Gideon's eyes went wide and fixed on something a thousand miles past me.

"Mirabel," she whispered. "There's a city under the salt."

The fire crackled. The salt ticked its empty applause.

And far to the east, past the dark edge of the waste, something answered — a single pulse through the ring, through her hand, through the ground itself, like a knock on a door from the inside.
