## 6. The Free City of Vellum Row

Harrowgate announced itself by smell before sight: woodsmoke and fish oil and something sweeter underneath, tallow candles burning in a temple that wasn't ours. Then the walls rose out of the salt haze, black stone streaked white at the joints where centuries of wind had worked salt into every seam, and above them a hundred chimneys bleeding smoke into a sky that had no business being that blue.

I stopped walking.

"What's wrong?" Mirabel said.

"Nothing." My throat was tight. "It's just — I've never seen a city."

"You've seen Saltmere."

"Saltmere is a town with delusions." I'd copied its census often enough to know: four thousand souls, one temple, one well brackish enough to make children's teeth go soft, and a wall built less against raiders than against the idea that anyone might want to leave. Harrowgate's wall was taller than Saltmere's whole temple, and it was not the largest thing in view.

The gate stood open. That was the part I couldn't stop looking at. Gates in my experience were for keeping people in; this one yawned wide as a barn door, traffic pouring through it in both directions, and nobody checked anything except cargo. A woman in a tax-collector's sash sat at a table under the archway with a ledger of her own, and I watched her wave through a farmer with a cart of melons after counting exactly none of them.

"They don't inspect?" I whispered.

"They tax," Mirabel said. "This is a free city. Everything's free here except everything. You'll see."

She was right. I saw within an hour.

* * *

Vellum Row was not on any map we bought, because no map we could afford was current enough to include it. We found it the way you find anything worth finding in Harrowgate: by following the smell of ink. It ran off a side street behind the chandlers' quarter, a lane so narrow two people couldn't walk abreast, lined with shops whose signs were hand-lettered and whose windows were full of paper — paper stacked, paper hanging, paper dyed saffron and madder-red and a green I had only ever seen in the temple's one illuminated manuscript, which I had been permitted to touch once, with gloves.

Paper everywhere. Paper like weather.

I stood in the mouth of the lane with my mouth open until Mirabel elbowed me.

"Scribe," she said. "Composure."

"I'm composing," I said. "You're looking at it."

The shop we settled on was called Quill & Kettle, and it was both: a stationer's front half and a tea room back half, run by a broad, gray-braided woman named Odile Vass who looked at my hands before she looked at my face. Scribes' hands tell stories. Mine said: ledger work, decades of it, a callus on the middle finger worn smooth as riverstone.

"Copywork," she said, when I told her what I was. "Fair rates, no questions. Forging—" she paused, weighing me, "—better rates, more questions, but I ask them of everyone, so don't take it personally. Can you do a crown clerk's hand?"

"I can do eleven hands," I said. "Twelve if you count the abbot's, though nobody should count the abbot's, it's a disgrace to the alphabet."

Odile Vass smiled for the first time, and it transformed her face from a closed door into a window. "Start tomorrow. Back room, there's a cot. The girl can sleep in the loft if she doesn't set anything on fire."

"I never set things on fire," Mirabel said, with the tone of someone who absolutely had.

* * *

That first week, I learned what a free city taxed.

Everything. There was a hearth-tax, collected by the chimneyful. A window-tax, assessed by light. A name-tax, which I initially thought was a joke until I found it itemized on the civic rolls I was copying: one copper per year per registered name, which explained why half of Harrowgate went by nicknames and why the man who delivered our ink was legally called "the man who delivers our ink."

And there was a gift-tax.

I found it on the fourth day, buried in a schedule of municipal levies, and I read the entry three times before I understood it. Licensed practitioners of "the art," as the charter delicately put it, paid a quarterly fee scaled to their declared discipline. Unlicensed practice carried fines. Unlicensed practice resulting in "public wonderment" — I copied the phrase with my pen actually shaking — carried a fine plus thirty days' labor repairing whatever the wonderment had been done to.

"It's not persecution," Mirabel said that night, up in the loft where she slept among the paper stock. She'd come down to argue, which was how most of our conversations went now. "It's worse in the valley. At least here you can pay."

"At least in the valley they don't pretend it's commerce."

"The valley pretends it's piety. Which is worse?"

I didn't have an answer, so I asked a different question. "What does it cost? A license?"

"For hedge-craft? More than either of us has." She was quiet a moment. "There are people who help with that. People who remember what magic was for, before it became a line item."

"The Circle."

Her head came around fast. "Who told you about the Circle?"

"You did. Crossing the flats. You talk in your sleep, and also when you're awake, constantly, about a network of smugglers and unlicensed mages who move the gifted out of the valley." I kept copying. I'd learned that Mirabel argued better when you didn't look at her. "I assumed it wasn't a secret, since you told a stranger."

"You weren't a stranger. You were a liability holding a relic."

"And now I'm a colleague holding a relic, sleeping twelve feet below you, who could sell all three of us to the inquisitors for the price of a name-tax refund." I set down my pen. "So maybe tell me the useful parts while I'm still feeling loyal."

She laughed — short, surprised out of her, the first real laugh I'd gotten from her since the salt. "Fine. Tomorrow. There's a chandler on the Row who leaves a blue candle in his window when it's safe to knock. Don't ask me how I know which candle. And Gideon—" she hesitated on the ladder, half in shadow, "—you don't have to come. This part isn't yours. Your job is hands and ink and staying invisible. That's not nothing. That might be everything."

But it was mine. I knew it the way I knew a forged seal from a true one: instantly, and with a sick drop in my stomach. The ring had been humming faintly ever since we crossed out of the dead salt — a thread of warmth against my sternum, patient as interest accruing. Whatever came next, it would come for the ring, and the ring answered to me.

"I'll come," I said.

* * *

The Circle met in the back of a dye-house, which meant that everyone present smelled powerfully of madder root and that anyone watching the door was watching a door workers entered and left at all hours anyway. There were nine of us the first night, sitting on crates among bolts of cloth, lit by a single shuttered lamp.

They were not what I expected, which was embarrassing, because I couldn't have said what I expected. Not wild-eyed heretics. A ferryman with river-gray eyes. Two sisters who finished each other's spells the way other people finished sentences. A boy of perhaps sixteen who kept a salamander — an actual salamander, small and orange and drowsy — in a lantern, licensed as an "exotic pet" at ruinous annual cost, which everyone agreed was the funniest thing any of them had ever done. And presiding, a narrow, precise man called Corvus Latch, who had been a temple auditor before he'd been excommunicated, and who spoke about the usurper's armies in the language of supply lines and ledgers.

He reminded me of me, and I distrusted him immediately.

"We have word from the east," Latch said, when introductions were done. "The harvest-brands. Three hundred marked at Fennmarsh alone since midsummer." He let that sit. Nobody gasped; these were people who had already rearranged their lives around worse news. "The free cities hold because we're scattered and priced. The Circle exists to change the second part. Which brings us to why Mirabel sent word ahead." His eyes found our corner. "You claim to be carrying a key to the king's binding."

Mirabel set the ring on the crate between us.

I felt it leave my pocket like a tooth leaving my jaw. Around the room, the air changed — the lamp flame leaned, the salamander woke and pressed its face to the glass, the ferryman made a sound low in his throat. Even I, who had carried the thing for weeks, felt it differently outside my skin: brighter, hungrier, a coal remembering it used to be fire.

"That's not possible," whispered one of the sisters. "Bindings don't have keys. Bindings have graves."

"This one has a maker's seal," Mirabel said. "Gideon read it. Tell them."

Nine faces turned to me. I am not, by nature, a person who enjoys being looked at; I had spent thirty years cultivating invisibility as a professional skill. But I had read that seal in the visions the ring gave me on the road — had watched an old king press it into molten salt with his own dying hands — and some things, once copied, cannot be uncopied.

"The seal of Aldric the Last," I said. "Pressed at the founding of the binding. The ring is the counter-seal. It was made to open what he closed." I swallowed. "Or so the ring says. It says things. It has a voice. I know how that sounds."

"It sounds like Tuesday," said the boy with the salamander, and several people laughed, and the laugh broke something open in the room, and I understood suddenly why Mirabel had crossed a dead salt waste to reach these people. They were not afraid of each other. I had not been in a room of people unafraid of each other in my entire life.

Something loosened in my chest. Something dangerous. Belonging, I would learn later, always feels like that right before it costs you.

* * *

The likeness went up on the ninth day.

I saw it on my way back from the ink-seller: a fresh bill pasted to the customs post at the Row's mouth, ink still sharp, surrounded by a knot of readers. Crown bills were common enough in Harrowgate — deserters, debt-fleers, smugglers — that most pedestrians walked past without slowing. But this knot wasn't dispersing, and when I drifted close with my parcel held casually against my chest, I saw why.

The face on the bill was mine.

Not exactly. The inquisitors' artist had guessed at details — given me a softer jaw, hair drawn too neatly — but the eyes were right, and the mouth, and there was a caption beneath in a clerk's hand so bad it was almost personal:

GIDEON IVERSEN, SCRIBE OF SALTMERE. WANTED FOR THEFT OF TEMPLE PROPERTY AND FLIGHT FROM CROWN SERVICE. REWARD: 40 CROWNS. SHE IS BELIEVED TO BE IN COMPANY OF THE HEDGE-WITCH KNOWN AS MIRABEL. REPORT ALL SIGHTINGS TO THE INQUISITION HOUSE ON CANDLE STREET.

Forty crowns. I did the arithmetic automatically, the way I did all arithmetic: forty crowns was eight years of my old debt payments. Forty crowns was what the temple had sold me for, roughly, give or take the fees. Somewhere a clerk had valued my entire life at a number, and the number was insultingly round.

A woman beside me squinted at the bill, then at me, then at the bill again.

My heart performed a maneuver I felt in my teeth.

"You're the one who copies for Odile Vass," the woman said. "Quill & Kettle. You did the menus for my sister's wedding. The flourishes were lovely."

"Yes," I said. My voice came out steady, which astonished me. "Terrible likeness, isn't it? The chin's all wrong."

"Mm." The woman looked at the bill a moment longer. Then she took the edge of it between finger and thumb and peeled it off the post, folded it twice, and tucked it into her apron pocket. "Wind's been terrible lately. Bills keep blowing away." She nodded to me and walked off toward the fish market, and I stood there holding my parcel and understanding, with a clarity that hurt, exactly what had just happened.

She had known. Or near enough. And she had chosen.

In Saltmere, that choice didn't exist. In Saltmere there was one story and everyone repeated it or left. Here — here a stranger had committed a small crime on my behalf over a wedding menu, and thought nothing of it, and gone to buy herring.

I wanted to cry. I wanted it so badly and so specifically that I recognized the feeling for what it was: grief, arriving late, for every year I had spent somewhere this was impossible.

Then I turned around and saw the second bill, further down the Row, freshly pasted, and a third beyond it, and a man across the street sketching in a notebook with the particular concentration of someone comparing a live face to a printed one, and the wanting curdled into its opposite.

Belonging was almost within reach. That was precisely what made it bait.

* * *

I burned the day's forgery drafts that evening — properly, in the kettle, per house rules — and went up the ladder to the loft, where Mirabel was mending a hole in her coat by lamplight.

"Bills are up," I said. "Mine. Forty crowns."

She didn't stop sewing. Her needle didn't even waver, but I'd spent weeks learning to read her, and the stillness itself was the flinch. "How bad is the likeness?"

"Bad chin. Good eyes."

"Good eyes are the problem." She bit the thread. "Eyes are how informants earn. Listen to me — you don't walk the Row alone anymore. You don't take deliveries. You work in the back room and I bring you food like a suspiciously well-read ghost. Two weeks, maybe three, until the bills yellow and the reward drops and Candle Street moves on to fresher heretics."

"Two or three weeks of hiding. While the Circle decides whether to march on the capital with a talking ring."

"While the Circle argues about it, yes." She set down the coat. "Latch wants to move fast. Half the others want to bury the thing in a peat bog and forget it. And I—" she stopped.

"You think bindings kill. You've said."

"I've said what I'm willing to say." She looked at me across the little loft, lamplight catching the salt-scars on her knuckles. "But here's what I'll add, since you're the one with the price on your head now. Welcome to it, by the way. It changes you, having a face they want. You start seeing the city in layers — who reads the bills, who walks past, who peels them down. You'll never unsee it."

"I already can't," I admitted. "A woman took mine down today. Put it in her apron. Said the wind's been terrible."

Mirabel smiled slowly. "Harrowgate," she said, in the tone other people said home.

"Harrowgate," I agreed. And then, because the fear had been sitting in me all day and fear makes honest: "Mirabel. If they take me — forty crowns won't buy you, but it'll buy someone to sell you. Promise me you won't trade the ring for me. Whatever they offer. Promise."

She was quiet long enough that the lamp guttered.

"No," she said finally.

"That's not — you're supposed to promise."

"I don't lie to people I—" She cut herself off, jaw working. "I've traded people before. To save other people. I told you I'd say what I'm willing to say, and that's the whole of it: I won't promise, because I don't know who I'll be when the moment comes, and neither do you. What I will promise is that I'll hate whichever choice I make. Does that help?"

It shouldn't have. It was the least comforting sentence ever spoken in a paper loft. But it was true in a way promises aren't, and truth was the currency we'd been trading in since the salt, and I found — sitting there with my face on forty crowns' worth of walls and my name in an inquisitor's ledger and a relic warm against my breastbone — that it helped enormously.

"Fine," I said. "Hate it thoroughly."

Below us, in the shop, the doorbell rang. Odile's voice, easy and commercial: "Closed — oh. Oh, I see. Yes, of course, come in, officer, mind the stock."

Mirabel was on her feet before the second word. Through the floorboards we heard boots — two sets, unhurried, the unhurriedness itself a kind of threat — and Odile's voice again, pitched carefully light:

"Ink? No, officer, we're a stationer's, we deal in ink, that's rather the whole business—"

And then a third voice, dry and courteous and utterly unfamiliar, saying words that dropped through the floorboards like stones through water:

"—a Gideon Iversen, believed employed at this premises. We'll just have a look at your ledger of workers, mistress. All in order, I'm sure. We do so love a clean record."

Mirabel's hand found mine in the dark. Her grip said: window, roofline, now.

Her grip also, I noticed distantly, was shaking.

We moved.
