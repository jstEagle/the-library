## 7. The Circle of Borrowed Fire

The Circle met in the cellar of a chandler's shop on Vellum Row, beneath three floors of tallow and wick, where the air was thick enough to chew and the smell of rendered fat covered the smell of fear. Mirabel had chosen the place herself, four years ago, for exactly that reason. Nobody lingered in a room that stank like this unless they had to.

Twelve of them tonight. That was already too many.

She stood with her back to the wall by the coal chute, arms crossed, watching the faces in the light of the borrowed fire — a flame one of the Vellum Row hedge-witches kept cupped in her palm like a bird she was afraid would fly. It threw shadows up the brick and made everyone look older and guiltier than they were.

Gideon sat near the back with her hands folded on the table like a woman at an audit. Of course she did. The girl had been out of Saltmere nine days and she still sat like the ledger was going to be collected. Her satchel — with the ring inside it — rested against her ankle under the table, and every few minutes her hand drifted down to touch it, quick and involuntary, the way you'd check a wound.

Mirabel didn't like that. She didn't like how often Gideon touched it, or how the ring had started humming when the girl slept, or how Mirabel herself had dreamed of salt three nights running since they'd carried it across the flats. Things that hummed wanted things. In her experience, wanting things was how bindings got made.

"Say it again," said Oskar Vane, who ran the chandler's shop above them and had never once rendered a candle honestly in his life. "Slowly. For the new ones."

Gideon cleared her throat. When she spoke, it was in the flat, precise voice of someone reading from a document, which, Mirabel supposed, she was — the only document she had left.

"The ring is a key. I don't know what it opens yet. But it was made in the palace workshops — the seal on the inner band matches the old king's maker's mark, the same mark on the binding-stones in the valley where I grew up. The valley isn't naturally dead to magic. Someone killed it on purpose. Someone built a cage over the whole valley floor, and the ring is cut from the same craft." She paused. "The same king."

"And the king," said the witch with the fire — Sefa, small and gray-eyed, firelight moving in her pupils like fish — "is bound. Body and soul, to the thing wearing his crown. Everyone in this room has heard the stories. Half of us have smuggled people out from under the armies that thing commands."

"So we use the key," said a broad man named Corvin, a ferryman who'd lost his license for carrying the gifted north. He said it the way you'd say *so we eat*. "We go to the capital. We unbind him. The usurper loses his anchor, his legitimacy, his army's blessing — all of it. One act. One night's work."

"One night's work," Mirabel repeated.

The room turned toward her. She let them. She'd been waiting for this part since before the meeting started, since Gideon had first laid the ring on the table between them and everyone had gone quiet the way people go quiet around money.

"You've never seen a binding come apart," she said. "I have."

"Then tell us," Corvin said. "That's what we're here for."

* * *

She told them about Halden first, because Halden was easy. A binding she'd witnessed at nineteen, on the road south of the flats, when a dying storm-caller had tried to pass his gift into a river rather than let the inquisitors take it. The river hadn't wanted it. The gift had torn loose halfway through and taken most of the man with it, and the river had run strange and hot for a season, and the fish had floated belly-up for thirty miles. Bindings were not locks, she told them. Locks held things still. Bindings *held things down*, and anything held down long enough learned to hate, and hatred was a kind of pressure, and pressure looked for a way out.

"That's a wild working," Sefa said. "Done badly, in panic, by a dying man. The king's binding was made by masters."

"I know," Mirabel said. "I've read the stones in the valley. I've felt the edges of it. It's beautiful work. That's worse."

She told them about the valley itself then — the white waste they'd crossed together, the salt that had been good grazing land once, before the binding. She watched Gideon flinch when she said it, because Gideon had grown up believing the salt was weather, or God, or simply the way the world was. Nine days ago she'd learned it was architecture. Some wounds you could watch close in real time.

"A binding that size doesn't just hold," Mirabel said. "It *feeds*. Everything it holds down goes somewhere. The magic of a whole valley, pressed into the crust for two hundred years — where do you think it went? Into the stone. Into the deep water. Into whatever the old king was trying to cage along with his own death." She looked around the room. "You want to open that cage in the middle of the capital, with the usurper's army camped outside the walls."

"The alternative is leaving it shut," Corvin said.

"The alternative," Mirabel said, "is finding out what else the key opens before we turn it."

And there it was — the argument she'd actually come to make, the one she'd been chewing on since the salt flats, since the night Gideon had fallen asleep mid-sentence and the ring had hummed against Mirabel's palm like something calling home. Because here was the truth she hadn't put on any table yet: the ring wasn't just a key to the king's binding. She was almost sure of it now. It was a master-piece. Cut from the same craft as the valley-binding, tuned to the same hand. Which meant it might open more than one door. Which meant whoever held it might be able to unbind the valley too — or bind something new — or bind themselves, if they were careless, if they were tired, if they were standing in front of a caged god-thing wearing a dead man's crown and it offered them exactly what they'd always wanted.

She knew what bindings offered. That was the whole horror of them. They never took anything by force. They *asked*, sweetly, reasonably, and they asked precisely for the shape of your grief.

"You're stalling," Corvin said. "You've been stalling since Harrowgate. You found the key, you carried it across the waste, and now that it's real you want to put it in a drawer."

"Yes," Mirabel said. "Exactly that."

"Why?"

And because she was tired, and because the tallow-smell was making her head swim, and because twelve faces in borrowed firelight can wear anyone down — she gave them the true answer instead of the tactical one.

"Because I've done it," she said. "I've bound someone. And it worked. Perfectly. And I would undo it with my teeth if I could."

The fire in Sefa's hand guttered. Nobody spoke.

"My sister," Mirabel said. "Lark. She was gifted — hearth-craft, small warm stuff, bread that rose right, fevers that broke. Nothing dangerous. Nothing worth a pyre. But the inquisitors came through our village when she was fourteen, and they had a list, and her name was on it because a neighbor sold it to them for a sack of flour." She heard her own voice go flat and careful, the voice of a woman reading a ledger, and hated it, and kept going. "I couldn't get her out. I was seventeen and I had nothing but a little hedge-craft and a fast horse. So I did the only thing I could think of. I bound her gift. Shut it down so deep even the testers' rods read clean. Clean as the valley. Clean as salt."

"And?" Corvin said quietly.

"She never woke up." Mirabel shrugged, as if it were a small thing, as if her shoulders weren't shaking. "Not dead. Asleep. Fourteen years asleep, in my mother's house, breathing, growing, dreaming — I hope dreaming. I visit when the roads allow. I sit by her bed and I talk to her and sometimes I swear her fingers move, and I don't know if that's her trying to surface or just the body being a body. Every hedge-mage who's examined her says the same thing: the binding is flawless. It will hold until she dies." She looked at her hands. "I built it to save her life. It cost her everything else. That's what bindings do. That's what they *are*. They're grief with a craft to it. And you're asking me to help turn the largest one ever made, in a room full of strangers, on the word of a ring that sings to people in their sleep."

Silence. The fire crackled. Somewhere above them, the chandler's shop bell rang — a customer, ordinary, oblivious, buying candles.

It was Gideon who finally spoke, from the back, in her flat scribe's voice.

"You said the valley-binding feeds. Where does Lark's go?"

Mirabel stared at her. Of all the questions. Of all the cold, precise, terrible questions.

"Into her," Mirabel said. "There's nowhere else for it to go. It's hers. I pressed it down into her and it's been sitting in her like rain under a lid for fourteen years."

"Then when the binding breaks—" Gideon stopped. Started again, slower, the way she did when she was checking arithmetic. "When your binding breaks, she gets all of it back at once. Fourteen years of it. And when the king's binding breaks, whatever it's holding gets two hundred years of it, all at once, in the capital, next to the thing wearing his crown." She looked up. "You're not arguing against freeing him. You're arguing about *timing*."

"I'm arguing—"

"You're arguing that we need to know what we're opening before we open it. Fine. I agree. I'm a scribe; I don't sign documents I haven't read." Gideon's voice didn't rise at all, which somehow made it louder. "But don't dress it up as doubt, Mirabel. You know what's in that cage. You felt it through the ring on the flats, same as I did. The question isn't whether. It's how, and when, and who pays."

*Who pays.* The words went around the room like a coin passed hand to hand, and Mirabel saw each of them bite it — Sefa closing her eyes, Corvin setting his jaw, a young courier in the corner going pale. Idealism was cheap at a distance. Up close it had a price list.

"Fine," Mirabel said at last. "We plan it properly. Reconnaissance first. Names, faces, rotations of the palace guard. We find out what the binding's anchored to — there'll be anchors, there are always anchors, and anchors can be prepared before the key turns. Six weeks. Maybe eight. And nobody moves alone, and nobody speaks of this outside this room, and—" she pointed at the courier, whose name she suddenly couldn't remember, which should have frightened her more than it did, "—you. What's your name?"

"Tally," he said.

"Tally. You've said nothing all night and you've looked at the door four times. Why?"

He blinked. "Habit," he said. "I'm a runner. Runners watch doors."

It was a reasonable answer. That was the thing she'd remember later, lying awake afterward, hating herself: it was a completely reasonable answer, and she believed it, because she wanted to believe it, because a room full of people who'd just agreed to risk their lives deserved to trust each other.

"Fine," she said again. "Same time, six weeks. This room. Come separately. Tell no one."

They snuffed the borrowed fire and went up the stairs one at a time, five minutes apart, into the tallow-dark and the ordinary street.

* * *

Mirabel walked Gideon back toward the lodging-house over the print shop, because the likeness nailed to the customs post now showed a sharp-faced woman with ink-stained fingers, and Mirabel had opinions about walking anywhere alone these days. They didn't talk much. Gideon carried the satchel hugged to her chest like a child, and the ring inside it hummed — Mirabel could feel it through the leather, a thin thread of sound, like a wet finger on a glass rim.

At the corner of Quill Street, Gideon stopped.

"You didn't have to tell them about your sister," she said.

"No," Mirabel agreed. "But they'd have smelled the lie. Hedge-mages smell lies. It's the first thing the craft teaches you — mostly so you can tell your own."

"Is that why you argue so hard? Smelling your own?"

Mirabel laughed despite herself, a short ugly sound. "You're getting bold, scribe."

"I'm getting expensive," Gideon said. "My debt's been sold once already. I'd like to stop being merchandise." She shifted the satchel. "Six weeks. You really believe we can prepare a binding-break in six weeks?"

"I believe we can try. I believe trying carefully beats rushing bravely." Mirabel hesitated. There was one more thing, and she'd decided on the walk over not to say it, and now her mouth betrayed her anyway. "The ring sang to me last night. While you were sleeping. Not words. Just — attention. Like something noticing which pocket its key is in."

Gideon went very still. "It's never sung to me while I was awake."

"That's what worries me. Sleepers answer questions they shouldn't." Mirabel reached out and put her hand flat on the satchel, over the ring, and the humming stopped — instantly, completely, like a servant caught eavesdropping. She snatched her hand back. "Keep it away from your pillow tonight. Keep it away from your *dreams*."

"What does it want, Mirabel?"

"I told you what bindings want. They ask for the shape of your grief." She looked at the girl — the debt-bound scribe with nothing left to lose, who'd crossed a salt desert for a stranger's war, who touched the ring like a wound. "Yours has a very obvious shape. So does mine. Be careful what you agree to in your sleep."

She left Gideon at the lodging-house door and walked back down Vellum Row through the drizzle, counting doorways out of habit, checking reflections in dark windows. The city smelled of wet wool and lamp oil. Somewhere a drunk was singing. It was, she would think afterward, the last ordinary hour for a long time.

She was three streets from the chandler's when she realized she'd forgotten to ask Tally who had vouched for him.

She stopped under an awning, rain ticking on the canvas above her head, and ran the meeting back through her mind the way she'd once run bindings — looking for the flaw, the loose thread, the place where the pressure would get out. Tally, quiet. Tally, watching doors. Tally, who had asked no questions and volunteered nothing and left fifth, not last, so he wouldn't be followed.

Nobody had vouched for him. She'd assumed Oskar had brought him. Oskar hadn't.

Mirabel turned around and walked fast, then ran, back toward the chandler's shop, taking the stairs down into the tallow-stink two at a time — but the cellar was empty, of course it was empty, everyone had scattered properly, and there was nothing there but the scuffed table and the ghost of borrowed fire and the slow drip of the coal chute.

Except — on the table, half-hidden under a bench, a scrap of paper. Small. Folded twice. She picked it up with fingers that had gone cold and unfolded it, and it was nothing, just a chandler's order slip, wicks and tallow, quantities and prices, written in a neat clerk's hand.

Written in a clerk's hand she recognized, because she'd spent nine days watching those fingers move across every page they owned.

Gideon's handwriting. An order slip. Innocuous. Except for the figure in the margin, circled twice: a sum of money, large, and beside it in tiny letters, almost invisible, the words *paid on delivery*.

Mirabel stood in the empty cellar with the slip in her hand and the tallow reek closing in, and thought: *she wouldn't.* And then thought: *the temple sold her debt once already. People sell what's been sold.* And then thought, worst of all, calmest of all: *or someone wrote this in her hand, because they'd studied it, because forgery is a trade like any other, and I taught this city half its forgers myself.*

Paranoia, she'd told the Circle, curdles. She just hadn't said how fast.

Outside, above the stairs, the shop bell rang — a customer, ordinary, oblivious.

Or a knock, arranged in advance.

Mirabel blew out the stub of candle she'd relit, and stood in the dark, listening to footsteps cross the floorboards overhead.
