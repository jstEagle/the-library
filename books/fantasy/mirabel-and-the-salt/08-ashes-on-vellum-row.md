## 8. Ashes on Vellum Row

The night the inquisitors came to Vellum Row, I was forging a death certificate for a man who was still alive.

It was honest work, by Harrowgate standards. Old Corvin Hale had sold his name to a shipping magnate who needed a dead partner more than a living one — debts die with a man, and Corvin's debts had grown teeth. So I sat in the back room of the sign-painter's shop with my ink and my good hand and my bad conscience, and I wrote that Corvin Hale of Vellum Row had perished of lung-rot on the ninth of the Drying Month, witnessed by two clerks whose signatures I could do better than they could.

I was good at it. That was the thing nobody warned you about being good at something dishonest: it stopped feeling dishonest. A ledger is a ledger. The temple taught me that every mark must be true to the page, even when the page lies to the world.

The ring sat warm against my sternum, strung on a cord under my shirt. It had been warm since Harrowgate. Warm like a sleeping bird, like a hand held near a candle. Mirabel said she couldn't feel it at all — "It doesn't trust me," she'd said, half-laughing, half-wounded — but against my skin it hummed its small salt-white hum, and some nights, drifting toward sleep, I almost heard words in it.

That night I heard nothing. I only heard the bells.

Harrowgate had eleven bell towers, and each one rang its own voice — the wool exchange a fat bronze bellow, the tide-chapel a thin silver chatter. I knew them all the way you know your own heartbeat. So when every bell in the city began ringing at once, out of order, overlapping, I knew before my pen finished its stroke what it meant.

Not fire. Not flood. *Inquisition.*

* * *

I found Mirabel in the Circle's meeting-house on Tallow Lane, which was not a meeting-house at all but a dyer's warehouse that smelled of mordant and old urine, and where forty people who should never have been in one room were gathered in one room.

They were arguing. Of course they were arguing. The Circle argued the way water runs downhill — inevitably, and into every crack. I came in through the loading door as a broad-shouldered ferryman named Osk was saying, "We vote again," and a thin woman with burn-scarred hands was saying, "Voting is how they find us," and Mirabel stood on a dye-vat lid in the middle of it all with her arms crossed, looking like a woman watching a cart she'd warned everyone about roll toward a cliff.

She saw me and her whole face changed — not softened, exactly. Sharpened. She read things off me the way I read ledgers.

"You felt it too," she said.

"Bells." I pushed through the crowd to her. "All of them. Mirabel, that's not a drill pattern, that's—"

"A cordon." She said it flatly, already moving, hopping down from the vat. "They ring everything at once so no one can tell direction. Then they seal the gates and work inward. We have maybe an hour before they reach Tallow Lane."

"Then we run now."

"We don't all fit through whatever rat-crack you two came in by," said Osk. He was a big man with a ferryman's shoulders and a child's stubbornness. "There are forty of us. Some of them can't cross open ground without being seen. Some of them—" he gestured at a boy of maybe fourteen, whose fingertips were faintly glowing, like coals under skin, "—can't hide at all."

"They'll scatter," Mirabel said. "Split into fives, take the cellar routes, the ones Sefa mapped. Anyone who reaches the river, take a boat, any boat, cut the moorings if you have to."

"And you?" said the burned woman. There was something in her voice I didn't like — not fear. *Accusation.* "You called this meeting. You told us the ring was worth the risk."

"I told you the ring was real," Mirabel said. "I never told you it was safe. Nothing true is safe."

"That's very pretty," the woman said. "Say it to the pyres."

I grabbed Mirabel's sleeve. "What is she talking about? What risk?"

Mirabel didn't look at me. That was answer enough, and it went through me colder than the bells. She had used the Circle's channels to ask about the ring — I knew that much. I hadn't asked what else asking might cost.

"Later," she said.

"There won't be a later if—"

The front doors came in.

Not opened. *Came in* — both leaves at once, hinges shrieking, driven flat by a battering ram shaped like a question nobody got to ask. Lantern-light poured through the doorway in a hard white sheet, and standing in it, silhouettes: six, eight, twelve men in grey-and-gold, and above them on the threshold a tall figure in white with a stole of black salt-iron links, and I knew that silhouette. Everyone in the valley knew it. Inquisitor-General Vess, who had burned the novice Tam at Saltmere, who had bought my debt, who had been hunting me across three hundred miles of salt without ever once needing to say my name aloud because his writs did it for him.

"By decree of the crown," Vess said, and his voice was mild, almost kind, the voice of a man reading a menu, "all persons practicing or harboring the forbidden arts will submit to examination."

The room detonated.

I wish I could tell it cleanly — who fought and who fled — but truth is a ledger with smudged columns, and what I remember is pieces. Osk hurling a dye-vat lid like a discus and taking an inquisitor off his feet. The glowing boy screaming as hands closed on him, his light flaring bright enough to throw shadows upward. The burned woman going out the loading door with five others, and the door being no escape at all because there were lanterns beyond it too. Mirabel's hand closing around my wrist, her voice right against my ear:

"The roof. Now."

"I'm not leaving them—"

"You're leaving with *me* or you're leaving in irons, and only one of those helps anyone!"

She was right. I hated that she was right. We went up the grain-stacked ladder as salt-iron chains sang below us, and out onto the dyer's roof under a sky gone orange with torchlight, and from up there I saw what the bells had been hiding: Vellum Row was burning at both ends.

Not the buildings. Not yet. They were burning *papers* — great braziers set up at the mouth of the street, and inquisitors feeding them armfuls of confiscated books, contracts, letters, anything written in a mage's hand. Ash rose off the fires in grey feathers and drifted down over the rooftops like the world's worst snow.

"They're burning the records," I said stupidly.

"They're burning the *people*, Gideon. The paper's just how they find them." Mirabel was scanning the rooftops, calculating jumps the way I calculated interest. "Every letter, every ledger, every debt-note — the inquisition reads everything. Names lead to names. That's why they took the Row tonight. Half the Circle's correspondence passes through here."

"And the other reason?"

She didn't answer. Below us, through a broken skylight, I heard Vess's mild voice rise above the chaos, and one word of it reached me clearly enough to stop my heart:

"—the scribe."

* * *

We crossed the roofs of Vellum Row like thieves, which by then is what we were, what we had been since Saltmere. Mirabel went first; I followed; the ring hammered against my chest with every drop and landing, hot as a coal now, and I understood dimly that it wasn't warning me. It was *feeding*. All that fear, all that gift flaring and guttering around us — the thing drank it, or answered it, or mourned it. I didn't know. I was a scribe. I knew ink, not relics.

At the fourth rooftop we found people ahead of us — three figures crouched at the parapet over Tallow Lane, and for one blind second I thought *inquisitors* and Mirabel's hand flashed with light before the nearest figure shouted, "It's us! Stand down, it's Osk!" — and then we were all pressed together behind a chimney, breathing hard, watching the street below fill with grey-and-gold.

Osk had made it out with two others: the burned woman, whose name I finally learned was Sefa — she of the cellar maps — and a girl of about nineteen whom I recognized from Circle meetings and who was shaking so hard her teeth clicked.

"They took Wren," the girl kept saying. "They took Wren, they took Wren—"

"Wren?" Mirabel's head snapped around. "The boy? Where?"

"The loading door. He froze. They had him before—" Her voice broke apart.

Below, the inquisitors were marching their catch down Tallow Lane toward the braziers. I counted seven prisoners, roped neck to neck, and among them a small figure with singed hair who walked with his chin up in a way that broke something in me, because I had seen that exact walk before, in a temple yard in Saltmere, on a novice named Tam, in the last hour of his life.

Wren looked up. I swear to every god I've ever copied prayers to, the boy looked up — straight at our rooftop, straight at the chimney we hid behind — and his eyes found Mirabel, and his cracked lips formed two words I read as easily as my own handwriting:

*Run. Please.*

Or maybe just *run*. Maybe the please was mine.

"He's fourteen," I said. My voice came out wrong, scraped. "Mirabel. He's fourteen years old."

"Yes."

"They'll examine him. They'll put him on the wheel and ask him questions until he tells them about the Circle, about the routes, about—"

"Yes."

"*Yes?*" I rounded on her. Around us the city rang and burned, and ash settled on my shoulders like a shroud being fitted. "You're the great smuggler. You've gotten dozens out. You have cellars and boats and a whole underground railroad of—"

"Gideon." She caught my face in both hands. Her palms were shaking. Hers, shaking. "Look at me. There are sixty inquisitors in this quarter. If I go down there, they take me, and then they have the woman who knows every route, every name, every safe house from here to the coast. Do you understand what I'm saying? They don't want Wren. Wren is bait. They want *me*, and they want *you*, and they will spend that boy's life buying us because that is what inquisitors do — they make you pay for your own people."

"So we let them have him."

"So we *live*," she said, "so there's someone left to come back for him. That's the whole ugly arithmetic of it. I've done this math before. I hate it every time."

And I saw it — saw it plain as a column summing wrong — that she had done this math before, and that somewhere behind her eyes was a name attached to the last time, and that she would rather swallow glass than tell me. Later. Everything was later.

Then the ring went cold.

Not cooled gradually. *Cold* — winter-iron cold, dead cold, the hum cut off like a snuffed wick — and I gasped and clutched my chest, and Mirabel spun toward me, and we both understood at the same moment, in the same horrible instant, what cold meant.

It meant proximity. It meant the binding in the ring recoiling from something. From someone.

From *salt-iron*.

"Down," Mirabel breathed. "Get down—"

Lantern-light bloomed over the parapet behind us.

* * *

They were already on the roof. Four of them, grey-and-gold, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who had planned the exits before they planned the entrance — because of course they had, because Vess read everything, because the Circle had a traitor, because there is always a traitor, I would learn that lesson so many times it wore a groove in me.

Sefa went over the far edge onto the adjoining roof and made it. Osk did not go quietly — he met the first inquisitor like a landslide, and for three glorious seconds the roof belonged to him — and then a chain took his knees and four men took the rest of him, and the girl, the shaking girl, simply raised her hands and said, "Don't hurt me, please, I'll tell you anything, I already told them where you'd be—" and I understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that hers had been the face of the traitor all along, young and terrified and bought.

Mirabel moved.

I have seen her craft since — I have seen her do things with raw magic that made old kings weep — but I have never seen anything in her life as fierce as those ten seconds on the dyer's roof. Light came off her like heat off a forge. She threw the first man into the second. She tore the chain out of the third man's grip and wrapped it around his own throat. She was magnificent, and it was useless, because salt-iron eats magic the way rust eats iron, and they had brought enough of it to fence a pasture, and I watched her gifts gutter — watched her sway, watched the light die in her hands like a lamp running dry — and I understood that she had crossed the salt flats feeling mortal for the first time and had hated it, and now they were making her feel it again here, in the middle of a city, surrounded by everyone she'd tried to save.

"Inquisitor," she said, and her voice was steady, gods help her, steady as a ledger line, "you're arresting the wrong woman. The one you want is standing right next to me holding a stolen relic of the old kingdom."

Every head turned to me.

"No—" Mirabel started, and a chain took her mid-word, and they were fast, they were practiced, they cinched the salt-iron collar on her before she finished the sentence, and her legs folded, and they caught her like men catching a sack of grain, without ceremony, without hatred. That was the worst part. No hatred. Just procedure.

"Mirabel Quill," said the ranking officer, reading from a writ, and I heard my own forged hand in the document's phrasing, my own trade turned against her, "by decree of the crown, you are remanded to examination."

She fought them the whole way to the roof edge — not with magic, that was gone, but with weight and fury and words, words thrown back over her shoulder at me: "*Gideon!* The cisterns — Sefa's maps — the third house past the sign-painter's — *don't follow me, follow the plan—*"

The plan. What plan? There had never been a plan. There had been a woman who always had one, and now there was rope and salt-iron and a street full of ash, and she was gone, hauled down the ladder into the lantern-light, and the last I saw of Mirabel Quill was the back of her head, dark hair coming loose from its braid, held between two men who treated her like cargo.

The remaining inquisitors searched the roof. They found nothing, because I was already nothing — flat against the rooftop behind the chimney, in the shadow of the ash-fall, with my heart going like a stamping horse and the ring lying dead-cold against my skin.

One of them paused near me. Close enough that I could hear him breathe. He looked right at the place where I was, and I looked back at him from inside my stillness, from the scribe's oldest trick — be furniture, be paper, be part of the room — and after a moment that lasted a season, he turned away.

"Clear," he called down.

* * *

I stayed on that roof until the fires burned low and the bells went quiet one by one, and the ash stopped falling, and Vellum Row lay silent under a grey feathered snow of everything it had ever written.

Then I climbed down into the ruin of the night and did the only thing I knew how to do.

I took inventory.

Osk: taken. Sefa: escaped, last seen east. The girl: traitor, name unknown, remember her face. Wren: taken, fourteen years old, walking with his chin up. Mirabel: taken, collared in salt-iron, headed for the inquisition hold beneath the customs house — I knew that much because I'd helped map the quarter, because she'd made me learn every street, because she had believed, right up until the doors came in, that knowledge was armor.

And me: unfound, unchased, free.

Free. I stood in the ash on Vellum Row and laughed until it turned into something else, because that was the joke, wasn't it? The inquisitors had bought my debt once in Saltmere and I'd run from it. Now they'd caught everyone but me, and I was free the way a loose coin is free — worth something only to whoever picks it up.

The ring lay cold in my palm. Dead. Silent. And yet when I closed my fingers around it, I felt — or imagined, or refused to not feel — one faint pulse, deep down, like a heartbeat under snow. Like something waiting to see what I would do.

Everyone I loved was in a cage or scattered. Every lawful road led to the pyre. The law had come to Vellum Row with lanterns and writs and mild, reasonable voices, and it had taken a boy of fourteen and the only person who had ever chosen me, and it had done it all *correctly*, by the book, with paperwork in good order.

So the book was the enemy. Fine. I had spent my whole life serving books. I knew their weaknesses. I knew how they were made, and who signed them, and how a signature could be grown in a bottle of ink and a patient hand.

Justice outside the law, then. Justice the way Mirabel worked magic — wild, illegal, and precise where it counted.

I pulled my hood up, turned my back on the ash, and started walking toward the customs house, doing sums in my head: guards on the night shift, the timing of the prisoner wagons, the shape of an inquisitor's writ and whether my hand could pass for Vess's clerk's.

It could. I'd seen it for three hundred miles.

Somewhere under my shirt, the ring gave one more faint, freezing pulse — and I chose, then and there, to believe it was a yes.
