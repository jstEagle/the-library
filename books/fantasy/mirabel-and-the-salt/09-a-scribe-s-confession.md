## 9. A Scribe's Confession

The writ arrived at the forger's shop on the third morning after the raid, and I recognized the hand before I recognized the seal.

That was the insult of it. Some inquisitor clerk — some tired, underpaid copyist exactly like the one I used to be — had drafted the order transferring Mirabel from the Harrowgate garrison cells to the crown road prison at Vellum Ford, and he'd done it in a fair hand with a lazy terminal flourish on every *r*. I had spent eleven years copying that exact flourish because a dead temple master named Aldous Verrek had invented it and everyone downstream of him copied it without thinking. The empire was a machine made of habits. I knew its gears better than most.

I also knew, holding that writ, that it was wrong.

Not forged-wrong. Just wrong. The transfer date sat three days out, which meant three days of Mirabel in a cell answering questions from men who burned novices on temple steps. The seal was genuine — pressed wax, the crowned eye of the Inquisition, still smelling faintly of tallow. But the routing line beneath it read *by authority of the Provincial Assize*, and there was no provincial assize in Harrowgate. There hadn't been since the free cities bought their charters. Whoever drew this up had never worked east of the valley, and they were improvising, and improvisation by inquisitors meant someone above them wanted Mirabel moved quickly and quietly.

Or wanted her disappeared before anyone could ask what she'd told them.

I set the writ down on Brannic's scarred worktable and looked at my hands. Ink in the cuticles, always, no matter how I scrubbed. A scribe's stain. For eleven years those hands had made faithful copies of other people's debts, other people's sentences, other people's names entered into ledgers that decided whether they ate or starved or burned. I had told myself the oldest lie in the archive: *I only write what is true. The truth is not mine to change.*

The ring sat cold against my sternum where I'd sewn it into my bodice lining. It had gone silent the night of the raid — no hum, no voice, nothing but a small hard weight like a swallowed stone. I had begun to fear that whatever lived in it needed Mirabel near, or needed magic near, and Harrowgate's mage quarter was ash and boarded windows now, and Mirabel was behind three doors and a man with a pike.

"Problem?" Brannic asked from the doorway. He was a broad, gray man who forged shipping manifests and tax stamps and had never once asked me a question about my past, which was why I paid him half my earnings for the corner bench and the good lamp.

"A writ," I said. "Badly routed."

"Client's?"

"The crown's."

He looked at me for a long moment. Brannic had survived forty years in a city where magic was taxed like salt and forgery was taxed worse, and his survival method was a magnificent, weaponized incuriosity. "Then I'm going to the tavern," he said, "and when I come back this table will have nothing on it but honest work." He took his coat off the hook. At the door he paused. "Gideon. The back shutter sticks. Lift, then push."

He left. I stood alone with the writ and the lamp and the sound of Harrowgate's evening bells counting out the hour — six bells, the hour when the garrison changed watch and the cells went an hour with a skeleton staff.

I had kept one clean record my whole life. Not the temple's ledgers — those were true the way a knife is true, sharp and indifferent. I meant my own record. Every entry in the book of Gideon Iversen: debt-bound, obedient, present but never participant. When the novice Tam was dragged to the pyre I had lied to an inquisitor's face, yes, but a small lie, a lie of omission, deniable. When Mirabel broke into the archive I had caught her and could have called the guard and didn't, but not-calling is not the same as acting. I had drifted through my own life like sediment in the flooded cellar, carried by currents, touching nothing, changing nothing.

The writ lay on the table. Three days until Mirabel went to Vellum Ford. Vellum Ford was where transfers stopped being transfers.

I thought about her in the raid — the last thing I'd seen, her face over the crowd as the inquisitors' net came down, and she wasn't looking at her own capture. She was looking at me, at the ring's hiding place against my chest, and her mouth had shaped one word before they hooded her. *Run.* And I hadn't run. I had hidden, which is what scribes do. I had waited three days doing honest work while the woman who called me *debt-girl* with something almost like affection rotted in a cell for a crime that was, if you traced it back far enough, partly mine. She'd come to the temple for the ring. I was the one who'd found it. Everything since — the pyre, the flight across the salt, the Circle scattered, the quarter burning — all of it grew from a seed I'd pulled out of floodwater and kept.

*You are already complicit.* That was the lesson of the pyre, and I had learned it and then done nothing with it, which is the special cowardice of people who think learning is the same as acting.

I lifted the lamp closer and began to read the writ the way I'd been trained to read everything: as a document with a structure, and structures can be rebuilt.

* * *

Forging an inquisitor writ is not primarily a problem of penmanship. Penmanship is the easy part; I could fake Aldous Verrek's hand in my sleep, and did once, in a dream where he stood behind me correcting my spacing. The hard parts are three: the paper, the seal, and the chain of custody.

Paper first. Crown writs were drawn on vellum-stock milled at the king's own works, watermarked with a running stag visible only against light. Harrowgate's chandlers sold confiscated crown stock by the bundle to anyone with coin — the free cities ran on hypocrisy the way mills ran on water. Brannic had a drawer of it. I took four sheets and told myself I'd settle with him later, knowing perfectly well I wouldn't, knowing he'd already priced the theft into my rent.

The seal was harder. Pressed wax can be mimicked but not faked well; the crowned eye has seventeen facets and inquisitors are trained to count them. But I didn't need to fake a seal. I needed a real impression transferred. The original writ had one. There is an old archivist's trick — heat, a smooth stone, patience — for lifting an impression from soft wax and re-setting it onto new. It degrades the facets slightly. It would pass a tired guard at ten feet and a lamplight. It would not survive an expert.

Which meant the rest of the document had to be flawless, because documents are judged as wholes. A guard doesn't count seal facets. A guard reads the name, the authority, the destination, and looks for the thing that will get him whipped: an error his superior would catch.

So I built the writ the way you build a load-bearing wall. Transfer order, prisoner designation *M. Corvane, hedge-craft, remanded.* Authority line — here I improved on the original's sloppiness and cited the Circuit Writ Act, clause nine, which permits transfer on the order of any commissioned officer when the assize is not in session, *the assize not being in session*, and I dated it so. Destination: not Vellum Ford. Vellum Ford would raise questions at the gate, questions requiring signatures. Instead: *to the Salt Cisterns holding, per standing order for gifted prisoners pending transport,* which was real — I'd copied three such orders during my years in the temple archive, because the temple handled the valley's inquisitorial paperwork and I had a memory like a ledger. The cistern holding was a real place, a real procedure, and almost nobody remembered it existed, which is the best quality a lie can have: it is mostly true.

The name of the commissioning officer I took from the raid itself. Inquisitor Halloway Fen — tall, gray-gloved, the one who'd held Mirabel's chin and said *we'll want her talking by Vellum Ford.* His signature appeared on the raid's seizure manifest, a copy of which hung posted outside the provisioner's with the burned quarter's confiscation list. I had stood in front of that posting for an hour that morning, memorizing his hand. He signed fast, left-slanted, with a break in the downstroke of the *H* like a door left open.

I wrote his name and I felt the strangest sensation, writing another person's signature: not guilt, exactly. Guilt implies you did something contrary to your nature. This felt more like putting on a coat that had been hanging in my closet for eleven years, waiting.

Last, the escort authorization — the piece that would put *me* inside the garrison. A transfer requires a custodial clerk to verify the prisoner's identity against the warrant and countersign the handover. Clerks are invisible. Clerks are furniture. I had been furniture for eleven years; I knew exactly how furniture behaved. I wrote myself into the document as *clerk of the circuit, G. Iversen,* and the ink seemed to make no sound at all, though I swear the room got quieter.

One clean record, traded for one criminal act. I blotted the page, held it to the lamp to check the watermark stag, and laughed — actually laughed, alone in a forger's shop at midnight, at the absurdity that the hardest part had been deciding. The doing took two hours.

The back shutter stuck. Lift, then push. I went out into Harrowgate's rain with a crown writ folded against my heart next to a dead king's ring, and I noticed that my hands were not shaking, and I understood that this frightened me more than shaking would have.

* * *

The garrison at Vellum Row had been a wool warehouse before the war, and it still smelled like lanolin and old decisions. I presented myself at the side postern at the seventh bell with my forged writ, my borrowed clerk's tabard — gray, unbleached, deliberately shabby — and the single most important tool in any infiltration ever devised: a clipboard.

"Transfer," I said, in the voice I'd used for eleven years to hand ledgers to masters who outranked me. Bored. Slightly put-upon. The voice of a man — a woman — whose only ambition is to finish and go home.

The sergeant at the postern was young, wet, and miserable. He read the writ top to bottom, which I'd expected, and then read it again from the bottom up, which I hadn't, and my pulse performed a small drumroll while I studied the middle distance with the vacant patience of the truly bureaucratic.

"Cistern holding," he said. "Never heard of it."

"Standing order," I said, and tapped clause nine with one finger, lightly, the way you tap something you've tapped a thousand times. "Gifted prisoners pending transport. You don't hold them in the general cells past seventy-two hours — contamination risk to the other prisoners, and the men won't stand the noise." I let a beat pass. "They sing, sergeant. The gifted ones. All night."

I had invented that on the walk over. It was a terrible detail and a perfect one, because it gave him a reason to want the transfer done that had nothing to do with the writ's authenticity. Fear and laziness, the twin pillars of every empire.

He squinted at the seal. Ten feet, lamplight, tired eyes. The wax gleamed. "Who countersigns?"

"I do. Custodial clerk of the circuit." I produced my own prepared sheet — handover verification, blanks awaiting his mark — and handed him the stylus like a man handing over a burden. People sign faster when the stylus is already in their hand. Another gear of the machine, oiled.

He signed. A guard walked me down through the garrison's guts — past the armory, past a kitchen where someone was burning onions, down stairs that got older and damper until we reached the old warehouse cellar, which the garrison used for storage and, apparently, for prisoners nobody wanted to log properly. Three doors. The third was iron, and through it, silence.

"Ten minutes," the guard said. "Verification only. No conversation beyond confirming identity."

"Obviously," I said, and despised him mildly, and waited for the key.

The cell was dark and smelled of wet straw and blood that wasn't fresh anymore. They'd chained her wrists high — standing restraint, the kind that keeps a prisoner too exhausted to work craft even if she had any left in a salt-city's dead air. Her lip was split. One eye was swollen nearly shut. When the door closed behind me and the guard's boots receded up the stairs, she lifted her head with the slow care of someone cataloguing injuries, and focused the good eye on me.

"Well," Mirabel rasped. "The debt-girl learned to pick pockets."

"I forged a writ."

"I know. I could smell the vellum-stock through the door. Brannic's drawer?" She almost smiled, then thought better of it, jaw tight. "Get these chains off before you brag."

The locks were standard garrison pattern — I'd bought the key blank from the same chandler as the vellum and cut it by eye from a wax rubbing of the corridor lock, a trick Mirabel herself had described across a salt-flat fire, back when we traded histories instead of blood. My hands knew keys. Eleven years of unlocking archive cabinets, of being trusted with the temple's records because I would never, ever misuse them. The lock turned on the second try. The wrist-irons were simpler. When the chain slithered down and her arms came free she made a sound she'd clearly been saving for days and sagged against the wall, working her fingers open one joint at a time.

"Can you walk?"

"I can walk, run, and complain simultaneously. The ring—"

"Safe. Silent, but safe." I hesitated. "It went cold when they took you."

Her good eye sharpened. "Then it's not cold. It's waiting. There's a difference." She pushed off the wall, tested her legs, nodded. "Cisterns?"

"You know them?"

"I know every smuggler's route in this city, including the ones the city forgot it had. The old salt cisterns run under Vellum Row to the river gate. The garrison thinks they're sealed. The garrison is wrong, because sealing things is famously difficult." She was moving now, stiff but functional, and something in her voice had shifted — the wire-taut humor still there, but underneath it, watching me, something new. "Gideon. The writ. Whose name is on it?"

"Halloway Fen."

"And yours?"

"Mine. Clerk of the circuit." I met her eye. "When they trace this — and they will trace it, Fen signs nothing without keeping copies — they'll find my name. My face is already posted. I've just given them a signature to hang beside it."

She was quiet for a moment. In the cell's dark, with her ruined face, she looked suddenly very tired and very clear. "Do you understand what you did up there?"

"I committed treason."

"No." She shook her head slowly. "You stopped being a bystander. There's a door most people go their whole lives without finding, and you walked through it in a borrowed tabard with a clipboard." Her mouth twitched. "It's the least dramatic revolution in history and it's the only kind that works. Come on. Stairs, then down, then we don't stop until the river."

* * *

The cisterns were Harrowgate's bones.

The city had been built on salt trade, and before the charter, before the free cities, some forgotten engineer had carved a lattice of holding tanks and channels into the limestone beneath Vellum Row — vast vaulted chambers where brine had once been stored by the thousand tun. Now they stood empty, or the garrison believed they did, and our footsteps rang in spaces big enough to swallow a church. Mirabel moved through the dark like she'd been born in it, one hand on the wall, counting turns in a low voice — *two left, the broken stair, mind the drop* — and I followed with the lamp turned so low it was barely a coal, and I listened to the echo of us, two criminals in the belly of a city, and felt something I had no ledger-entry for.

Not freedom. Freedom was the salt flats, and that had felt like falling. This was different. This was *aim.*

"They'll burn Brannic's shop when they trace the stock," I said, as we waded a channel knee-deep in cold runoff. "I should have—"

"He'll be gone by dawn. Brannic's survival instinct is a marvel of the age; he probably heard the shutter and started packing." She glanced back at me, the lamp catching the split lip. "You're doing it already."

"Doing what?"

"Rehearsing guilt for crimes you haven't finished committing yet. Save it. Spend it where it buys something." She stopped at a junction where three channels met, black water sliding in from each, and stood listening to a sound I couldn't hear. Then: "There. The river gate. Listen — water moving fast means the grate's rusted through, which means tonight the city's neglect is our accomplice."

We came up under the river gate into a slot of night sky and the smell of open water, and Mirabel hauled herself onto the stone lip and then reached back for my hand, and I took it, and for a moment she just held on, looking at me with her one working eye.

"Fen will have copies," she said. "By tomorrow morning every inquisitor between here and the capital will have your name and your face and a description of the ring. We're done hiding in cities, debt-girl. The Circle's scattered, Harrowgate's burned, and the only road left is the one the ring wants." She touched her own sternum, mirroring where mine hung. "East. To the capital. To the king in the mirror room."

"You believe that's real? The binding? The king?"

"I've seen bindings kill," she said simply. "I've made one kill, in a way. And everything the ring showed you on the flats — you told me yourself, Gideon. The old king, caging his own death. If even half of it is true, then the usurper's armies, the pyres, the harvest-branding — all of it is one wound, and the wound is in the capital, and the only instrument that can open it is sewn into your bodice." She stood, pulling me up beside her onto the riverbank, where the current ran loud and the lights of Harrowgate glittered above us like a city that had never known us at all. "So we walk east. And we don't stop."

Behind us, faint through the cistern grating, a bell began to ring — not the hour bell. The alarm. Someone had found an empty cell, a cut lock, and a signature that opened doors.

Mirabel grinned through her split lip, wild and alive, and pulled me toward the dark of the eastern road.

"Run, clerk," she said. "Your record's dirty now. Let's go make it worth the ink."
