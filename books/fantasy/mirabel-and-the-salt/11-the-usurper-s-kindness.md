## 11. The Usurper's Kindness

The town was called Bracken's Ford, and it smelled like bread.

That was the first wrong thing. After nine days on the road east — sleeping in drainage ditches, eating hardtack Mirabel charmed into something almost edible before the salt's memory wore off her fingers — Gideon had learned to read towns by their smells. Fear smelled like smoke and shuttered windows. Poverty smelled like tallow and old fat. Bracken's Ford smelled like fresh bread, woodsmoke from clean-burning hearths, and under it all, faintly, like the lye they used to scald wounds.

"Something's happening," Mirabel said. She had her hood up, which she hated; hoods made her look like every other traveler on the road, and Mirabel would rather be hanged than anonymous. "Something good."

"That's what worries me."

They came over the last rise as the sun came up behind them, throwing their shadows long across the road ahead, and Gideon stopped walking.

The field outside the town was full of people. Hundreds of them. Refugees — she knew refugees now, knew the particular way they arranged themselves in open ground, in circles with their backs to each other, the way they kept their bundles between their knees. They'd been fleeing east out of the contested provinces for weeks; the roads had been thick with them since Harrowgate. But these weren't scattered. They were *lined up*. Orderly. And moving, in a slow shuffling river, toward a row of long tables where soldiers in crown blue ladled out stew from cauldrons big enough to drown a man in.

Banners hung from the town's gate. Not the old king's sigil — that had been a stag under a yoke of stars, back when there'd been a king worth the name. These were white fields with a single gold circle at the center, plain and clean and readable from a mile off. The usurper's mark. The mark that was on the coins in Gideon's purse, on the writs she'd forged, on the proclamations nailed to every post from the salt flats to here.

"They're feeding them," Mirabel said slowly.

"They're feeding them."

"I don't like it either," Mirabel said, "but I'm not going to pretend I know why. Hungry people are dangerous people. Maybe he's not an idiot."

"He burned a novice alive in Saltmere's square," Gideon said. "He posted my face on every wall in Harrowgate. He's not feeding refugees out of the goodness of his heart."

"No," Mirabel agreed. "But let's find out what it is instead."

---

They joined the line.

It went against every instinct Gideon had. For eleven days she had moved through the world like smoke, avoiding eyes, avoiding roads, avoiding the exact kind of orderly queue that got you counted. But Mirabel was right in the way she was usually right about people: nobody looks twice at someone standing in a bread line. Hunger was the best disguise in the world. So Gideon bent her back, smudged ash on her cheeks, took Mirabel's hand — Mirabel's fingers squeezed hers once, surprised, then held on — and shuffled forward with the rest.

The soldiers were young. That struck her first. Crown blue sat badly on boys who couldn't have been more than nineteen, twenty, their jaws still soft, their hands careful with the ladles. One of them caught Gideon's eye as she reached the table and smiled at her, actually smiled, and pushed her bowl toward her with an extra heel of bread balanced on the rim.

"Eat slow," he said. "There's enough. There's always enough."

*There's always enough.* She carried the words away like a splinter. Armies did not have enough. Armies had requisitions and shortfalls and quartermasters who lied. An army that fed everyone who came to its tables was an army spending something — coin, grain, goodwill — and armies did not spend without expecting return.

The stew was good. That was worse. It was rich, actual meat in it, onions gone soft and sweet, salted properly. Gideon ate it in the shadow of the town wall and watched, and made herself understand what she was seeing.

Past the tables, past the cauldrons, the line continued. At its end stood a pavilion — white canvas, gold trim, the circle stitched large on its front — and beside the pavilion's entrance, a smaller table. A clerk sat there with a ledger open. Beside the clerk stood two men who were not young, who were not soft-jawed, whose blue uniforms were cut differently: fitted, dark-cuffed, with silver thread at the collar. Inquisitors. She knew the cut now the way she knew her own handwriting.

Every refugee passed the clerk's table. Every refugee gave a name. Every refugee held out a hand — and Gideon watched, bowl forgotten, as the clerk pressed something against each palm.

She thought, at first, it was a stamp. Ink. Some mark of passage, a permit, a tally-count for the granaries. It was only when a woman three places ahead of them flinched — a small, bitten-off sound, the sound of someone swallowing pain because crying out might cost her place in the line — that Gideon understood.

Not ink. Heat.

"They're branding them," she whispered.

Mirabel's grip on her arm went iron. "Branding *who*?"

"Everyone." Then, watching closer, correcting herself: "No. Not everyone. Look — most of them get marked and walk on. But some—" 

Some were pulled aside. Gideon watched it happen four times before the pattern resolved. A boy of maybe twelve, a grandmother with a shawl over her head, a man with a limp, a girl about Mirabel's age with restless eyes that kept flicking toward the pavilion like a bird eyeing a snare. Each one touched the clerk's stamp and did not merely flinch. Each one *reacted* — a full-body jolt, a gasp, a stumble backward — as though the brand had found something under the skin and gripped it.

And each one was led gently, so gently, by a soldier's hand on the elbow, into the white pavilion.

"The gifted," Mirabel breathed. Her voice had gone somewhere flat and far away, the voice she used when feeling things too large to feel directly. "The stamp doesn't hurt unless you're carrying. It's a resonance test. Crude, but — gods, it's clever. You can't hide a gift from hot iron laid on a sigil. It *sings*."

"And the ones who sing get taken into the tent."

"And the ones who sing get taken into the tent," Mirabel said, "and come out the back."

Gideon looked. There was a back flap to the pavilion, half-visible from their angle, and beyond it a wagon. Covered, but not well — slats in the sides, and through the slats, when the light hit right, glimpses of faces. Sitting figures. Still figures. More than she could count before the wagon shifted.

Nobody screamed. Nobody fought. That was the thing that turned her stomach coldest. The ones taken aside went quietly, because the soldiers were gentle, because the food was good, because the banner was bright and the clerk smiled and said *thank you, next*, and because after weeks on the road these people wanted, more than freedom, more than safety, to be *handled*. To have someone else decide. The usurper's army had built a machine that harvested the gifted and the gifted walked into it holding their bowls.

"We're leaving," Mirabel said.

"Not yet." Gideon heard her own voice and didn't recognize it. "I need to see the ledger."

"You're insane."

"It's a ledger, Mirabel." Something in her chest had gone very quiet and very clear, the way it went when she faced a page of numbers that didn't add. "Everything is a ledger. Names in, names out. I want to know what column they're writing us into."

---

Getting close to the clerk's table was insultingly easy. The queue did the work for them; they simply didn't leave it. When their turn came, Gideon bowed her head, mumbled a name — *Maren Cotter, out of the Ashvale provinces* — and held out her left hand, palm up, heart hammering with a fear she refused to show.

The clerk was a thin man with ink-stained fingers and the harried air of every temple functionary Gideon had ever known. He barely looked at her. He pressed the stamp to her palm.

It was warm. Warm metal, a raised sigil she felt rather than saw — a circle within a circle — and beneath the warmth, faintly, a hum. Like a tuning fork laid against bone. Gideon held still. The ring, hidden in the lining of her coat against her wrist, pulsed once, softly, like a second heartbeat answering — and Gideon understood with sick clarity that if the stamp could hear anything, it was hearing *that*, and she was one pulse away from the pavilion.

But the stamp was crude, and the ring was old, and whatever the iron sang, the clerk heard nothing. He grunted, released her hand, and made a mark in his ledger.

Gideon craned. Just a glance. Just a scribe's glance, quick and hungry, the way she'd read documents upside-down across desk-tops for fifteen years.

Columns. Of course there were columns. *Name. Province. Trade.* And then a fourth column, headed with no title at all, filled with small neat checkmarks — and beside the checkmarks, in a different hand, quantities. Numbers. Weights, almost. As though the fourth column recorded not whether a person was gifted but *how much*, measured in some unit she didn't recognize, entered by a different clerk inside the pavilion and passed out through the flap.

At the bottom of the page, totals. Running totals, updated with each entry. And below the totals, a single line of text that the clerk had written and rewritten as the day's count grew:

*Provisional quota, eastern circuit: 400 heads. Received to date: 312.*

*Quota.*

Mirabel had gone gray. Gideon realized she must have seen it too, reading upside down over her shoulder, and that neither of them could speak until they were clear of the table. They took their bowls, thanked the boy soldier — Gideon's voice cracked on it, and the boy smiled again, kind as sunrise — and walked, not fast, not slow, out past the cauldrons, past the banners, to the low stone wall at the field's edge where the crowd thinned.

Then Gideon set down her bowl, put her head between her knees, and breathed.

"Quota," Mirabel said above her. "Four hundred heads. Like — like wool. Like a tithe of sheep."

"Not a purge." Gideon lifted her head. Her mouth tasted of iron. "A purge you can resist. A purge makes people run, hide, fight. This—" She gestured back at the field, at the lines, at the mothers holding their children's hands in a queue toward the branding iron. "This is a harvest. He feeds them because starving stock runs. He's kind because kindness is cheaper than chains. Every gift in the eastern provinces, weighed and tallied and carted west in wagons, and the people *thank him*—"

She stopped. Because she had seen it now, all of it, the whole shape of the thing, and the shape was familiar. It was the temple. It was Saltmere. It was a ledger of small debts, a valley that traded its magic for peace, a population that called its cage a blessing because the alternative had a name and the name was fire. The usurper hadn't invented anything. He'd scaled it up. He'd taken the trick the old king had played on one valley and played it on a kingdom — except the old king had caged magic out of grief, and this man was caging it out of appetite.

"What does he want them *for*?" Mirabel asked. "Gifts aren't coin. You can't spend a hedge-mage."

"You can if you've got a binding that eats them." Gideon said it before she could stop herself, and then the two of them looked at each other, and she watched Mirabel arrive at the same arithmetic she had. The binding on the king. The thing wearing his crown. A hunger that size needed feeding. Four hundred heads per circuit, circuit after circuit, wagon after wagon rolling west toward the capital — toward the mirror room, toward the throne, toward whatever waited under the gold circle on the flag.

"Oh," Mirabel said softly. "Oh, that's what the pyres are. That's what the hunts are. He's not cleansing the kingdom. He's *stocking the larder*."

Behind them, in the field, the line moved on. The boy soldier refilled bowls. The clerk wrote names. A little girl at the front of the queue held out her hand to the stamp without fear, because the banner was pretty and the stew was warm and the men in blue were gentle, and when the iron sang against whatever slept in her blood, her mother squeezed her eyes shut and did not make a sound.

Gideon stood up. Her legs shook. She let them.

"We go west," she said. "Tonight. Ahead of the wagons."

Mirabel stared at her. "West is the capital. West is the palace, the inquisitors, the thing itself. We were going to sneak in through the sewers with forged papers, and that plan was already a eulogy."

"I know." Gideon picked up her bowl, looked at the dregs of the usurper's kindness cooling at the bottom, and set it down on the wall for whoever came next. "But there's a wagon full of three hundred and twelve people rolling toward that throne, and every day we take getting there is a day the quota fills. And I've spent my whole life being the clerk who writes the numbers down, Mirabel. I'm done being on this side of the ledger."

For a long moment Mirabel said nothing. Then she pulled her hood back — defiance even now, especially now — and looked east, back the way they'd come, toward the salt, toward the valley, toward everything safe and empty and lying.

"West," she said. "Gods help us. West."

And behind them, faint and cheerful over the field, the clerk called the next name in line.
