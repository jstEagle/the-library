## 12. What Mirabel Bound

The road east out of the border town was a river of the displaced, and we moved in it like two stones carried by a current that did not care where it went. For three days we walked among carts and coffles and families carrying everything they owned on their backs, and for three days Gideon said almost nothing.

I knew what she was carrying. I'd seen it myself, hadn't I? The feeding line, the bread and stew doled out by soldiers with kind faces, and then the branding iron at the end of it, heated cherry-red, pressed to the wrist of anyone whose gift so much as flickered. *Harvest, not purge.* She'd said it first, actually — my quiet scribe, reading the truth off the road faster than I had. That should have made me proud. Instead it made me afraid, because people who learn to see clearly start asking questions, and there was one question I had been not-answering since the salt flats.

She finally asked it on the fourth night, in the ruin of a waystation with a broken roof full of stars.

We had a fire going small, screened behind a fallen wall. Gideon sat cross-legged with the ring in her palm, turning it over the way she turned everything over — carefully, completely, like a page she intended to copy exactly. The salt-white band caught the firelight and gave none of it back.

"You bound someone," she said. "Once."

Not a question. That was the worst part. She'd assembled it herself, out of all the things I hadn't said: the way I flinched from bindings, the way I'd argued against using the ring before the Circle scattered, the scar of knowledge under everything I'd told her about how bindings kill.

I fed a stick into the fire because my hands wanted something to do. "Where did that come from?"

"You talk about unbinding like a surgeon talks about amputation. You know exactly what comes after." She didn't look at me. "And back in Harrowgate, when the Circle asked if you'd ever worked one, you said 'I've seen them done.' You said it too fast. Scribes notice things, Mirabel. It's the whole trade."

I laughed, or something near it. "Of course you noticed. Of course."

"Tell me."

The fire popped. Somewhere down the road a baby was crying, one thin thread of sound in all that dark, and I thought about how many years I'd managed to keep my own thread from unraveling, and how it had never once been because I was strong. Only because no one had pulled.

"Her name was Wren," I said.

Gideon went very still. She was good at stillness; she'd learned it in archives, I supposed, where any movement meant ink spilled or a superior's attention earned.

"My little sister. Two years younger than me, born in a village called Aldery, up in the hill country where nobody checks papers because there's nothing worth taking." I kept my eyes on the fire. "She came into her gift at nine years old. Small thing, at first — she could make cut flowers last, could find lost things, could make our mother's headaches lift. The kind of gift that gets a girl loved right up until somebody reports her."

"The pyres were already burning then?"

"First year of the decree." My voice came out flat, which was how I knew this part was old, worn smooth like a stone I'd been carrying in my pocket so long I'd stopped feeling its edges. "Our village held a hearing. Local priest, local magistrate, a crown observer who'd ridden three days to be bored at us. Wren stood up in front of everyone and swore she wasn't gifted. And the observer smiled at her — he *smiled* — and said, 'Then you won't mind proving it.' They had a relic for testing. A lodestone thing, black glass. Gifted folk can't hold it; it burns them."

"And you—"

"I stole it the night before." The words tasted like ash, which was fitting. "I was fourteen and already half a hedge-witch, enough craft to muffle a relic's hunger. I went into the temple annex and I wrapped that black glass in a binding so tight it couldn't taste anything. Not Wren. Not anyone. A dead stone in a living girl's hand."

"That would have worked," Gideon said slowly. "That would have passed the test."

"It would have. If I'd stopped there." I finally looked at her. "But I was fourteen, and I was terrified, and terror does arithmetic badly. Because a binding wears. Days, weeks, months — and if it wore off during the test, Wren wouldn't just fail, she'd fail *holding proof of tampering*. So I did the only thing that felt safe. I anchored the binding to her. To her gift itself. Made the binding feed on the thing it was hiding, so as long as her gift existed, the binding existed, self-sustaining, invisible, permanent."

The fire cracked. Down the road, the baby had gone quiet.

"She held the stone," Gideon said. "Didn't she."

"She held the stone and it lay cold as river rock in her hand, and the observer yawned, and the village cheered, and I was a hero." I heard my own voice go strange. "And Wren never woke up."

Gideon frowned. "Never woke—"

"Not dead. Not sleeping, either. Awake, walking, talking, laughing. But the part of her that was *her* — the bright part, the part that made flowers last and found lost things — I folded it into the binding, Gideon. I used it as fuel. And a binding that feeds on its own anchor doesn't leave anything over. There was nothing left to wake." I wrapped my arms around my knees. "She lived thirty more years. Married a cooper. Had children. Died last winter, an old woman, contented, they say. And every day of those thirty years she was a house with the hearth swept out. Warm rooms. No fire. My sister is the reason I argue against bindings. Not principle. Guilt."

Silence. The kind that has weight.

When Gideon finally spoke, her voice was careful, the way she handled the temple's oldest ledgers. "You said 'last winter.'"

"Yes."

"And the binding died with her."

"I don't know." I spread my hands. "Bindings tied to a life usually unravel when the life ends. Usually. But mine was self-sustaining. Fed on itself. It's possible—" I stopped, because the next word was too large to say casually.

"Say it," Gideon said.

"It's possible it's still out there. Anchored to nothing. Drifting. Looking for—" I shook my head. "Or it unraveled clean and Wren's gift burned off with her last breath and there's nothing anywhere but an old woman's grave. I don't know. I've been afraid to look for eleven years."

* * *

Gideon didn't answer right away. She took the ring off her palm and set it on the ground between us, deliberately, the way she did everything — a document placed on a table for examination.

"Show me," she said.

"Show you what?"

"What a binding looks like. From outside. You said the valley's binding is visible to your craft — threads, knots, whatever language you use. Show me that first. Something that isn't yours. Then show me—" she paused, and I watched her choose precision over mercy, which I was learning was her form of love, "—show me what you remember of yours."

"Why?"

"Because tomorrow we walk toward the capital, toward the king, toward the biggest binding ever laid on this land. And you're going to have to help me unmake it. I need to know what happens to the person who holds the knife." Her eyes came up to mine. "I need to know if you're asking me to do to a king what you did to your sister."

The words landed like a slap, and they were true, and I loved her a little for saying them.

So I knelt over the ring and let my craft open, slow, the way you open a door in a house where something might be sleeping. The valley's binding first — far away now, westward, a gray lattice laid over the whole salt basin, vast and patient and wrong. I showed her how it sat on the land without gripping it, how it smothered rather than seized. A shroud, not a cage.

"Now mine," I said, and my mouth was dry.

I reached east, past the road, past the dark hills, following a thread only I could feel — guilt makes good thread, it never rots — and there it was.

Still there.

Eleven years, and it was still there. A knot of pale craft hanging in the empty air above a cooper's grave in Aldery, self-fed, self-sustained, anchored to nothing, humming faintly like a struck bell that never quite stops ringing. Waiting. I had known, some part of me had always known, and knowing had changed nothing, because looking at it and touching it are different sins.

"Oh," Gideon said softly. She could see it reflected in my face, in the way my craft recoiled. "It's still alive."

"It's still hungry," I corrected. "Anchored to nothing, it can't feed. So it waits. And things that wait long enough start reaching." I closed my senses off, hard, and sat back on my heels shaking. "There. Now you know what I am. Not a smuggler with a tragic past. A girl who ate her sister's soul to win an argument with a priest."

"No," Gideon said.

I blinked at her.

"You didn't eat her soul. You hid it so well it couldn't come home." Her voice was steady, scribe-steady, and I realized she'd been doing what she always did: transcribing. Taking the wild shape of what I'd told her and setting it down in lines she could check. "Those aren't the same crime. One is murder. The other is—" she searched for the word, "—a filing error. A catastrophic one. Filed wrong for thirty years."

"A filing error," I repeated.

"You're a mage, not a monster. There's a difference and it matters." She picked the ring up again, turned it once. "Here's what I'm deciding, Mirabel, and I want you to hear me decide it. When we reach the king — if we reach him — I will ask you every question I can think of before we touch his binding. What it's anchored to. What feeds it. What happens to the anchor when the knot comes loose. And if the answers are wrong — if freeing him means hollowing him — then we stop, and we find another way, even if there isn't one." She held my gaze. "Because I've seen what your binding did from the inside, now. Wren walked around for thirty years warm and empty, and everyone called it contentment. I spent twenty-six years copying ledgers in Saltmere, warm and empty, and everyone called it peace. I know that house. I lived in it."

My throat closed. "Gideon—"

"So the answer to your question is yes. Unbinding can be violence. I watched it be violence tonight, secondhand, across eleven years and one grave." She turned the ring over one final time and closed her fist around it. "But the binding was the violence, Mirabel. The unbinding is just telling the truth about it. And I'd rather be hurt by the truth than comforted by the lie. I've done comfortable. Comfortable nearly got a novice burned in a temple courtyard while I stood there holding a lie in my mouth."

The fire had burned down to coals. Above the broken roof, the stars were merciless and clear.

"One more thing," I said, because she deserved the whole ledger, not the edited version. "If we free the king, and the king's binding works the way mine did — if his death is the anchor — then unbinding him doesn't save him. It releases whatever the binding was holding back. His death arrives. All of it, at once, deferred all these years." I watched her take that in. "Wren's binding kept her gift caged. The king's keeps his death caged. And death, unlike a gift, doesn't drift afterward looking for an anchor. It finishes its work and goes home satisfied."

Gideon was quiet for a long time. Long enough that I thought she might stand up and walk down the road alone, and I would have deserved it.

Instead she said, "Then we ask him first. He's the one wearing the chains. He gets a vote." And then, quieter, almost to herself: "Wren never got one. That's the real crime, isn't it? Not the binding. That you decided for her."

"That's the real crime," I agreed, and it cost me something to say it, and the something it cost felt like interest finally paid on a very old debt.

She nodded once, decision filed, and lay back against her pack. Within minutes her breathing slowed into sleep — the sleep of someone who has done hard arithmetic and gotten a clean answer.

I stayed up. I looked east, where the capital waited somewhere beyond the hills, and I looked west, where a pale knot hung over my sister's grave, patient as salt.

And between those two points, in the coals of a ruined waystation, I understood at last why the ring had chosen a scribe. Bindings are contracts. Contracts are written. And somewhere in the mirror room of that palace, in ink older than kingdoms, the terms of the oldest contract in the world were waiting for someone who read fine print for a living.

I slept at last, near morning, and dreamed of a bell that never stopped ringing — and woke to Gideon standing over me, the ring blazing white in her fist, her face drained of color.

"Mirabel," she said. "It started pulling. Hard. Whatever's waiting in that palace knows we're coming."
