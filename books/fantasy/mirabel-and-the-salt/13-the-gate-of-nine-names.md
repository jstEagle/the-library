## 13. The Gate of Nine Names

The capital had nine gates, and each one wanted a different lie from us.

Mirabel told me this on the last morning of the road, while we crouched in a drainage ditch outside the walls of Corvane and watched carts queue up like penitents. "Northgate takes merchants. Saltgate takes fishwives. The King's Gate takes clerks." She said it lightly, the way she said most dangerous things, as if danger were a joke she'd already heard the end of.

"Clerks," I repeated.

"You can write. That's rarer than you think. Half the crown's own clerks can barely spell their own names — they hire for the hand, not the head." She dug her elbow into my ribs. "You've spent your whole life copying other people's words, Gideon. Time to make them work for you."

I had been thinking about that all week, actually. Not the infiltration — Mirabel handled the plan, the routes, the bribes we couldn't afford and would attempt anyway. What I kept turning over was smaller and stranger: that everything the temple had done to me, every ledger, every debt column, every hour bent over a desk until my spine learned the shape of servitude, had been training. Not for them. For this.

The temple taught me to write so that I could record what I owed. It never occurred to anyone — least of all me — that a hand trained to copy could learn to invent.

We watched the King's Gate for two days before we went through it.

* * *

The forgery took me six hours, and it was the best work of my life.

Crown clerk writs were not hard to find; Harrowgate's inquisitor offices had burned in the raid, but their paper stock had been hauled out to a salvage yard, and Mirabel knew a boy who knew a man who sold things by the pound. What was hard was the seal. Crown wax carried a die-mark, a pattern pressed deep enough that a forgery showed its seams under lamplight if you knew where to look. I knew where to look. I had spent eleven years looking at seals, cataloguing them in the margins of ledgers because there was nothing else to do with my eyes.

So I didn't forge the seal. I *built* it — layered wax in three pours, tinted with ash and iron filings, pressed between two carved slate dies I cut myself over three nights with a needle borrowed from a tailor who asked no questions. When I held the finished writ up to the fire, the light came through it the way it came through the real ones: evenly, obediently, without opinion.

Mirabel looked at it for a long time.

"What?" I said.

"Nothing." She turned it over. "It's just — when I imagined this part, I imagined us climbing over a wall."

"We're scribes," I said. "Nobody climbs over a wall to steal something everyone leaves on a desk."

She laughed — really laughed, the unguarded version I'd only heard twice since Vellum Row, both times in the dark where she thought I couldn't see her face. "Remind me never to lend you money."

"You couldn't afford the interest."

* * *

The King's Gate at dawn smelled of tar and wet stone and too many bodies. We joined the queue of clerks — Mirabel in a gray traveling coat with her hair bound back and her hands conspicuously empty, me carrying the document case against my chest like a second heart. Inside the case: our forged writs, a genuine letter of assignment I had altered line by line, and the ring.

The ring rode in a false bottom beneath the paperwork, wrapped in wool, cold as a coin left in snow. It had been quiet since Harrowgate. Quiet since the raid. Some nights I took it out and held it and felt nothing come back, and I wondered if whatever lived inside it had died with the Circle's scattered people, or if it was simply waiting, patient as salt, for the place it was made to return to.

Corvane was that place. I understood that now. The visions had shown me the old king's scriptorium, the binding laid down in ink and blood and grief, and every image had been anchored here — these walls, these towers, this gate. The ring wasn't traveling toward the capital. It was traveling home.

"Names," the gate sergeant said, when we reached the front.

"Gideon Vael," I said. "Crown Clerk, Third Register. Assigned to the Palace Archive, records audit." I passed him my writ. "My colleague, Sera Dunmore. Also Third Register."

He looked at the writ. He looked at the seal. And here is what eleven years of copying taught me that no one tells you about power: the men who hold it almost never examine it. Examination implies doubt, and doubt is expensive. The sergeant saw crown wax, crown paper, crown phrasing — the grammar of authority, which I had studied longer than any forger alive — and his eyes did the thing eyes do when they encounter something they've decided not to be responsible for.

"Purpose of the audit?" he asked, because procedure demanded he ask.

"Debt reconciliation," I said. "Every clerk, every requisition, every grain of salt moved through this gate in the last ten years. The Regent wants the books clean before the season turns." I let a flicker of weariness cross my face — the universal expression of the bureaucrat, instantly legible. "It's going to take weeks."

He waved us through with something close to sympathy.

That was the moment I understood we were going to survive the gate, and the understanding landed strangely — not triumphant but hollow, like setting down a weight and finding your body doesn't know how to stand without it. Eleven years of being owned, and I had just walked through the front door of the machine that owned me, wearing its own livery, and no one had looked twice. The temple had never needed chains. Neither did the crown. All either of them ever needed was paper, and people willing to believe it.

* * *

Inside the walls, Corvane was worse than the visions had prepared me for.

Not ruined — that was the thing. The capital was *magnificent*. White towers, banners snapping in the cold wind off the river, markets loud with trade, fountains running even in the frost because the usurper liked the sound of water. The army's supply trains rolled through the streets openly, and children chased the wagons, and women sold hot bread to soldiers whose sleeves bore the brand-hammer insignia I had seen stamped into refugee arms at the border town.

A city fed by a harvest. That was the phrase that stuck in me, walking those bright streets. Everything beautiful here was paid for in something the city never saw itemized.

"The palace archive is in the east range," Mirabel murmured, matching my pace. "Under the mirror wing. The visions —"

"I know where it is." I surprised myself with how steady my voice came out. "The old king built it below ground so the damp wouldn't touch the vellum. There's a clerks' entrance off the Records Court. If our writs are entered in the day-book, we can walk down whenever we're assigned."

"And the binding itself?"

"Below the archive. The visions always ended the same way — stairs going down past the shelves, past the foundations, into rock. He wrote the binding into the bedrock, Mirabel. It's not a spell sitting in a room somewhere. It's the floor the whole palace stands on."

She was quiet for half a block. "Then whoever built this city built it on top of a cage."

"On top of a king," I said. "Same thing, now."

* * *

The Records Court was exactly what I expected, because bureaucracy is the same animal everywhere — I had simply, until now, only ever seen it from inside the cage. Clerks queued at a long oak counter. A registrar with ink-stained cuffs checked writs against a master ledger. Behind him, shelves rose three stories high, stuffed with the empire's memory: requisitions, tax rolls, conscription lists, pyre authorizations.

Pyre authorizations. I saw the label on a shelf-end as we waited, plain as "Grain Stores, Winter Quota." Somewhere behind that label were the names of every gifted person the crown had burned, filed and cross-referenced, probably indexed. Probably *alphabetized*. My hands went cold inside my gloves.

When our turn came, the registrar took my writ, checked the seal against his die-book, and entered us in the day-book. His pen scratched. I watched him write our false names into the true record and felt something turn over in my chest — the specific vertigo of watching your own forgery become official history. From this moment, Gideon Vael existed. She had a signature, an entry date, an assignment. Paper made her real. Paper had made *me* real once, too, in a temple ledger: *Gideon Iversen, indentured, value assessed.* I had spent my life trying to get out from under someone else's entry. Now I understood you could also write yourself in.

"Third Register," the registrar said, handing back the writ. "East range, sub-level one. You'll report to Under-Archivist Corren. He'll assign your sections." A pause. His eyes flicked up at me, and for the first time they were not bored. "You're the one with the hand? The sample sheet?"

"Sample sheet?"

"The requisition came down a month ago. They wanted a copyist tested before assignment to the restricted stacks." He slid a small card across the counter. "Report to Corren first. He does the testing."

Mirabel, beside me, had gone very still in the way she went still instead of running.

"Testing," I said carefully.

"A hand test. Ink control, pressure, fidelity." The registrar was already waving the next clerk forward. "Restricted stack work. They don't let just anyone down there. Next."

We walked out of the queue and across the court, and only when we reached the shadow of the east colonnade did Mirabel speak, low and fast. "They're testing copyists for the restricted stacks. Why? What's down there that needs a *hand*?"

I didn't answer immediately, because I was looking at the card in my palm. Heavy stock. Crown watermark. And at the top, above the blank lines where my results would go, a printed header — routine, administrative, the kind of thing you'd read a hundred times without seeing:

*Palace Archive — Restricted Assignment. Requisition authorized under the seal of the First Binding.*

The old king's seal. The seal from my visions, the one I had watched him press into wax three hundred years ago with a dying man's hand. It was still in use. Still current. Still authorizing requisitions, as though the king who made it had merely stepped out and would be back after lunch.

And beneath the header, in smaller print, the line that stopped my breath:

*Bearer will present, upon testing, any relic of the First Binding recovered by crown agents. Verification required.*

"They know it exists," I whispered. "They've known. They've been *looking* for it."

Mirabel took the card from me. Read it. Read it again. When she looked up, her face had the expression I'd learned to fear on the road — not fear exactly, but the arithmetic behind fear, the rapid silent totaling of everything we'd walked into.

"The ring," she said. "If they've posted a standing requisition for relics of the First Binding — if they're testing every good hand in the archive for work near it — then the binding isn't forgotten, Gideon. It's *maintained*. Someone's been keeping it. Feeding it." Her jaw tightened. "Or afraid of it."

"The usurper," I said. "The thing wearing the crown. It's been waiting for the ring to come back. It built a whole department to wait."

Triumph curdled in my stomach. An hour ago I had walked through the ninth gate untouched, invisible, brilliant — the scribe become the weapon. And the weapon had worked perfectly. That was the hook of it, the ugly symmetry: we had gotten in because the palace expected someone like us. Expected the ring. Had perhaps been expecting it for three hundred years, holding the door open, keeping a desk warm, testing hands.

Somewhere below us, past three stories of shelved empire, past the foundations, down in the rock, the old king hung in his binding. And somewhere above us, in the white towers, the thing that fed on him felt the ring cross the threshold of its city — I was sure of it, sure the way you're sure of weather — and did not send soldiers.

It sent a requisition.

"Corren tests us tomorrow," Mirabel said. "Which means tomorrow we find out whether they want a copyist..." She tucked the card into my breast pocket, over my heart, and patted it once, gently, like arming a fuse. "...or bait."

Above the east range, the palace bells began to ring the hour, and every bell in Corvane answered, and the sound went down through the stones, down through the shelves, down through the foundations, to wherever the king was, and I found myself praying — to nothing, to salt, to anyone — that he could still hear it.
