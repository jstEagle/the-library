## 14. The King in the Mirror Room

The palace recognized us before we recognized it.

That was the first wrong thing. Gideon noticed it — she noticed everything, that was her gift and her curse, a scribe's eye for the detail out of place — but I felt it in my teeth, the way you feel a storm coming over salt water. The clerks' corridor we'd forged our way into was too clean. The guards at the third checkpoint didn't check our papers twice. A servant with a tray of candied plums stepped aside for us without being asked.

"Nobody stops us," I murmured, keeping my eyes forward, my hands loose at my sides where the ring sat hidden under a stolen clerk's cuff. "In a usurper's palace."

"They're expecting someone," Gideon said. "Maybe not us specifically. Maybe anyone who comes down this corridor with the maker's seal."

"That's worse."

"Yes," she said. "It's worse."

We had come through the Gate of Nine Names wearing borrowed faces — hers a clerk's grey, mine a courier's mud-brown — and every door since had opened like a wound opening. The palace of Aldermere had been built to be climbed, a fortress stacked on a fortress, walls within walls, each ring of defense meant to be fought through by an army. We were two women with a forged writ and a dead man's ring, and the palace parted for us like water around a stone.

The ring grew warmer as we went deeper. Not hot. Warm the way a hand is warm. It had been cold since Harrowgate — cold through the raid, cold through the cisterns, cold through three weeks of ditches and hedgerows while we learned the shape of the usurper's roads — and its warming now felt less like guidance than like recognition. *Home,* it seemed to say. *You've brought me home.*

I did not tell Gideon what else it felt like. It felt like being carried.

* * *

The mirror room was at the heart of everything, which surprised me until I thought about it and then it horrified me.

Every binding I had ever seen or made or broken had a geometry to it, and the geometry always told the truth the binder wanted hidden. The old king had laid his binding across the whole valley — across the salt, across the magicless miles where Mirabel Dravenmoor's gifts guttered out like a candle in wind — and such a binding needs an anchor. A keel. Something heavy enough to hold the weight of a silenced land.

We had assumed the anchor would be buried. Sealed. Hidden in some vault beneath the capital, behind wards and names and nine locked gates.

Instead the palace led us up. Up past the throne room, up past the state apartments with their gilded ceilings, up a narrow stair that smelled of beeswax and something older underneath it — something like a tannery, like a riverbed in drought, like the inside of a shell. The stair ended at a single door of black oak with no lock on it.

No lock. Because nothing in this room needed protecting from the world. Only the world needed protecting from it.

Gideon put her hand flat against the door. "It's warm," she said.

"It's been warm for sixty years," I said. "Come on."

She looked at me then — that look she'd been giving me more often lately, the one that said she was cataloguing me too, filing me somewhere between *ally* and *liability* — and I realized she was waiting for me to say something true before we went in. So I gave her the only true thing I had left.

"Whatever's in there, it isn't going to be what the stories say. Stories about bound kings are always about monsters. This is going to be about a man." I swallowed. "And the monster will be the kindest part."

She nodded once, scribe-precise, and pushed the door open.

* * *

Mirrors.

The room was lined with them, floor to vaulted ceiling, hundreds of panes in frames of tarnished silver, and every mirror showed the same thing: an old man sitting in a chair.

Not a throne. A chair — high-backed, plain, the kind a farmer might keep by his fire. He sat in the center of the room with his hands folded in his lap, and he was the most terrible thing I have ever seen, and I have seen bindings eat people alive.

He was dissolving. That is the only word for it. His edges were wrong — not wounded, not wasted, but *unfixed*, as if someone had painted him and the paint hadn't dried. Where his right hand met his sleeve, the boundary wandered: skin became cloth became skin became something pale and grainless that might have been salt. His beard was white at the chin and translucent at the jaw, so that when he turned his head I could see the chair back through him. And threaded all through him, visible only when he moved, were lines — fine silver filaments running out from his chest and spine into the air itself, into the mirrors, into the walls, spreading outward and downward through the whole palace, through the whole country, the way roots spread through soil.

The binding. I knew it the way you know your own handwriting. Sixty years of craft laid down by a dying man trying to hold off the dark, and it had grown into him so thoroughly that there was no longer any telling where the man ended and the working began.

And in the mirrors — this was the part that stopped my breath — in every mirror, the reflection was *better*. Clearer. Whole. In the glass, the old king sat upright and strong, brown-eyed and unbroken, a man in his prime with a crown on his head and no silver threads anywhere. The reflections smiled at us. The man in the chair did not.

"Gideon," I said quietly. "Don't look at the reflections."

"I already am," she whispered. "They're looking back."

* * *

The old man opened his eyes.

They were the color of weathered wood, and they found me first — found the mage in me, maybe, or just found the guilt, which mages carry visibly, like smoke. Then they moved to Gideon and stayed there, and something crossed his ruined face that I will spend the rest of my life trying to describe. Recognition. Relief. Horror. All three at once, the way a drowning man looks at a rope.

"The ring," he said. His voice was dry leaves. "You brought it back."

"We brought it," Gideon said. She stepped forward, and I let her, because this was her part of the story even if it was my craft. "It called us here. It's been calling since Saltmere."

"I know." He closed his eyes again. "I wrote the call myself. Sixty years ago. I wrote it to find a bearer after I was gone." A sound came out of him that might have been a laugh. "I never imagined it would take six decades. I imagined I'd be dust, and some poor fool would dig the thing up and think, *how pretty.*"

"What are you?" I asked. Not kindly. Kindness would have been a lie, and lies are dangerous currency in a room full of mirrors.

His eyes opened again. "Aldous Merren. Third of that name. King of the Nine Cities, Warden of the Salt, Keeper of—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started over, smaller. "A man who was afraid to die. That's the honest title. Everything else is embroidery."

Gideon had gone very still. I watched her do the arithmetic she was born to do — the dates, the decrees, the valley, the binding — and watched the sum arrive in her face like a blow.

"You made the valley," she said. "Saltmere. The magiclessness. You bound the whole valley."

"To cage something," I finished for her, because I'd promised her this answer on the salt flats and never delivered it clean. "Yes?"

"No." The old king's voice cracked on it. "No. Listen to me, because everyone who tells this story tells it wrong, and I have had sixty years to hear it told wrong." He tried to lean forward and the silver threads pulled taut, singing faintly, and he settled back with the patience of the very tired. "I did not bind the valley to cage a creature. I bound the valley to cage *myself.*

* * *

Here is what he told us, in a voice like paper burning slowly, while the mirrors smiled their young smiles and the silver threads hummed their low note:

Aldous Merren the Third had been a great king, and great kings make great enemies, and the greatest enemy he ever made was time. He had built the Nine Cities free. He had broken the guild tyrants, opened the borders, written charters with his own hand. And at sixty years old, with the work half done and his heir a boy of nine, he had lain in his bed one winter night and felt death coming for him the way you feel weather in an old wound — and he had panicked.

He was a mage. That was the secret under all the other secrets; the histories scrubbed it out within a generation. A mage-king, and a strong one, and strength plus terror plus grief equals binding. He began small — wards against illness, charms against age — and each working worked, and each working demanded more, and within five years he was spending his gift faster than his body could bear, pouring himself into workings the way a spendthrift pours coin into a failing venture, because stopping meant admitting the venture had failed, and admitting failure meant dying.

"And then I found it," he said. "Or it found me. There are things that live in the seams of the world, scribe. Old things. Hungry things. Most of them are mindless — storms with appetites. But one of them had learned patience, and it came to me in a dream wearing my father's face, and it offered me a bargain I was too frightened and too clever to refuse and too proud to see through."

"What bargain?" Gideon asked. Her voice was steady. Her knuckles, wrapped around the hidden ring, were white.

"Continuance. It would hold my death at bay — truly hold it, not postpone it — and in exchange it would wear my shape, speak with my voice, sit my throne, finish my work. My work, understand. It swore it would finish what I started. The free cities. The charters. All of it." He laughed again, that dry-leaf sound, and this time it broke apart halfway through. "And it has. Look at the roads. Look at the granaries. The usurper's armies feed refugees because *I* fed refugees, and the thing remembers how, the way a puppet remembers its strings. It has kept every promise it made me. That is the horror of it. It is a faithful servant. It has simply also been eating me for sixty years, one thread at a time, and using my hands to build a kingdom that exists to keep it fed."

"The gifted," I said. "The harvest. The branding."

"Its tithe," he said simply. "Magic is food to it. Always was. The valley binding — that was mine, and I regret it, and I'll say so plainly: I bound the valley because the binding needed a reservoir, a deep still pool of suppressed magic to draw the working's weight away from my body, and the salt flats were empty and nobody important lived there. I told myself nobody important. You can tell I was a king; we say things like that." His gaze went to Gideon, and it was gentle, and gentleness from that ruined face was almost unbearable. "Your temple. Your ledgers. Your whole ordered, quiet, magicless childhood — that was my spillover basin. I'm sorry. I have been sorry for sixty years, and sorry has fixed nothing, which is the second lesson of kingship."

* * *

Gideon sat down on the floor.

Not collapsed — sat, deliberately, cross-legged, the way she used to sit in the archive at Saltmere among the flooded ledgers. I understood it. When the world gets too large, a scribe makes herself small enough to copy it down. She looked up at the old king across ten feet of marble and said, in her flat ledger voice:

"You want us to leave the binding in place."

It wasn't a question, and the king's silence answered it anyway.

"If you unbind me," he said, "I die. Tonight. Within the hour. Sixty years deferred, all arriving at once — no body survives that, no matter whose body. And the thing loses its anchor, yes, its grip on the armies breaks, the free cities rise, everything you came here hoping for comes true." He folded his translucent hands. "But the binding doesn't just hold *me*, child. It holds *it*. While I live, it lives tethered — fed, patient, leashed to my decay. If I die unbound, it goes... wherever hungry things go when the door is open. Into the world. Into whoever it finds first." His wooden eyes moved to me. "Into a hedge-mage standing closest to the working, perhaps. Or into a scribe holding a ring it has coveted for sixty years."

The ring burned against my wrist like a coal.

"So you see," the king said, and his voice was terribly calm, terribly kind, the voice of a man who has rehearsed this speech alone in a room of mirrors for decades, "I am not asking you to save me. I gave up on saving long ago. I am asking you to leave me here. To let the leash hold. To walk out of this room, seal the door, and let me go on being eaten — quietly, usefully, for another sixty years if the thing can be patient — because the alternative is me dying and it hunting."

"Begging," I said. My mouth tasted of salt. "You're begging us not to free you."

"I am begging you," he said, "not to make my death the beginning of something worse. Is that so hard to understand? I have been dying for sixty years. I am very good at it now. I have gotten *comfortable*." And then, softer, the rehearsal finally cracking, the man showing through the king at last: "Please. Please. I have held this door shut with my own body since before your mothers were born. Don't open it. Whatever mercy brought you up those stairs — take it back down with you. Leave me my leash."

The mirrors smiled.

All of them, every pane, the young king in every reflection smiling at us with identical warmth, and I understood suddenly what the mirrors were for — not vanity, not architecture. They were the thing's windows. It had been watching this room forever. It had heard every word.

And in the nearest mirror, the young king's smile widened just slightly, just enough, and his reflected lips moved without sound, and though no voice crossed the glass I read the words as clearly as if they were written in my own hand:

*Choose quickly.*

Gideon's hand found mine in the dark of the room — cold fingers, steady grip, the grip of a woman who had once lied to an inquisitor's face over a pyre and never stopped lying for what was right since. The ring pulsed between our wrists like a second heartbeat.

Above us, through the ceiling, through the floors, through the whole gilded weight of the palace, we both heard it at the same moment: boots. Many boots. Coming up the stair.

The door had no lock. But it was about to have guards.
