## 16. Unbinding

The mirror room had no mirrors.

That was the first thing Mirabel understood when the doors closed behind them — a round chamber at the heart of the palace, walled in polished black stone that reflected nothing, only swallowed the torchlight and gave back a darkness with depth to it, like looking down a well. The floor was the same stone, and in its center was carved a circle of nine names in the old script, each one worn shallow by centuries of feet that had walked across it pretending not to see.

The king lay in the middle of the names.

He was smaller than Mirabel had imagined. She had built him up in her head across all those miles of salt and road — the true king, the caged king, the man whose binding had strangled a whole valley so that he might not die — and what lay on the stone was an old man gone thin as winter honey, his skin stretched over a skull that seemed too large for it, his hands folded on his chest like a man posing for his own effigy. His eyes were open. They were clear. That was the worst part. Whatever the thing wearing his crown had taken from him, it had left him his sight, and it had left him his mind, and it had left him waiting.

"You came," he said. His voice was dry paper. "After all this time. Someone finally came."

"We came," Gideon said beside her.

Mirabel looked at her — at Gideon Iversen, debt-scribe of Saltmere, standing in the dead center of the usurper's palace with a stolen ring on her thumb and ink still under her fingernails from forging her way through three checkpoints — and felt the old fear rise in her throat, the one she knew better than any spell. *I have done this before,* she thought. *I have stood over someone bound and told myself I was saving them.*

She had said as much to Gideon on the road, in the flat gray light outside the border town, after the confession she'd sworn never to make: her sister, the binding laid gentle as a bandage, the sister who slept and slept and never woke whole again. Gideon had listened without flinching, which was worse than flinching, and then she had said, "Then you know exactly what not to do."

It was the only reason Mirabel was still walking toward this room instead of away from it.

"Your majesty," she said now, kneeling at the circle's edge. The stone was cold through her trousers. "We're going to unbind you. And I need you to hear me plainly, because there's no time to be kind about it. When we cut the binding, everything it's been holding back comes through at once. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

The king smiled. It transformed his face, briefly, into someone's father, someone's grandfather, a man who had once been young in a country that still had green in it.

"I have been dying for sixty years," he said. "You are offering to let me finish. It is the first kindness anyone has shown me since my coronation."

* * *

They worked by the light of one candle, because Mirabel did not trust fire in this room, and because Gideon had brought candles — of course she had, packed among her scribe's kit alongside spare nibs and a stoppered flask of iron-gall ink, as if unbinding a king were an exercise in correspondence. Maybe, Mirabel thought, it was.

Gideon knelt opposite her across the king's body, the ring on her right thumb, and she had drawn — gods, she had actually drawn — a diagram in the dust of the floor. Nine columns. The nine names of the circle, each mapped against the layers of the binding as the ring's voice had described them to her in the visions that came to her now almost hourly, whether she wanted them or not.

"It's not one binding," Gideon said, tapping the diagram with a stylus. "It's nine. Nested. He built them like a ledger — each entry referencing the last, each one compounding interest." Her voice had gone flat and precise, the voice Mirabel had heard her use on inquisitors and gate-clerks, the voice that made lies sound like record-keeping. "The outermost layer is the valley. That's the one holding the land. The innermost is—" she hesitated.

"Say it," the king said. "I've had sixty years to learn not to flinch."

"The innermost is holding your death," Gideon said. "Deferred. Not destroyed. It's been accruing the whole time. All of it comes due the moment we cut the last thread."

Mirabel had heard hedge-witches talk about death-work her whole life, usually drunk, usually lying. But she had also seen bindings fail. She had seen what came through the gap when a cage broke badly — in the marsh towns, in the year of the Red Fever, when a desperate mother bound her son's gift and the binding tore and took the boy and half the house with it. Bindings didn't hold death out. They held it *in*, compressed, patient, like water behind a dam made of words.

And here was the difference between her craft and Gideon's, the difference that had taken them the whole journey to understand: Mirabel could feel a binding the way you feel a knot in your own hair, by touch and instinct, and she could pull it apart with her fingers — but instinct lied, instinct flinched, instinct was how her sister had ended up sleeping forty years from her life. Gideon couldn't feel magic at all. What Gideon had was something rarer in Mirabel's experience than any gift: the ability to look at a structure of nine interlocking bindings and *read* it, coldly, completely, the way she read ledgers, without hope or fear getting between her and the numbers.

So they had divided the work the only way it could be divided.

"I read," Gideon had said. "You pull."

Now Mirabel set her palms flat on the stone at the circle's edge and let her gift rise. It came up through her like groundwater finding a crack — wild, warm, smelling faintly of the sea it had crossed to reach her — and the room's darkness *noticed*. The black walls leaned in. Somewhere behind them, deep in the palace, something vast shifted its weight, and every courtier within a mile would have felt it as a shiver, a sudden urge to check on their king.

"Hold," Gideon said, and her voice was steady as a ruled line. "First thread. Outermost. The valley binding — it's anchored to the name *Aldous*, third column. If you pull anything else first, the whole structure collapses inward and takes him before we've freed the land."

"How do you know that?"

"Because the ledger balances," Gideon said simply. "Pull Aldous first and the sum resolves. Pull anything else and it doesn't. Trust the arithmetic, Mirabel. It doesn't care how scared we are."

Mirabel laughed — a short, cracked sound, inappropriate and necessary. Then she found the thread.

It felt like the others always did: a line of will, old and dense as cable, humming with the shape of the man who'd tied it. This one was sixty years stale but still taut, still *held*, and beneath it she could sense the vast slow pressure of what it contained — the valley's stolen magic, generations of it, pooled and pressurized, wanting out.

"Aldous," she said, and pulled.

The thread parted with a sound she felt in her teeth rather than heard, and somewhere far to the west, in a salt-locked valley where nothing green had grown in living memory, Mirabel would have sworn she felt the ground exhale.

"Resolved," Gideon said. Her stylus moved down the column, striking through. "Second thread. *Corvane.* Anchored to the armies — no. Wait." She went pale. "It's anchored to the *crown*. The thing isn't just wearing it. It's *tied to it*. Corvane is the knot that holds the usurper's legitimacy together — every oath sworn on that crown, every soldier's loyalty, it's all routed through this thread. Cut it and—"

"And every army in the kingdom stops believing," Mirabel said. "All at once."

They looked at each other across the king's body.

"That's not a small thing," the king said mildly. "Sixty thousand men, mid-campaign. Oaths breaking is like weather changing. Some of them will do terrible things in the confusion." He turned his head with effort. "Scribe. Your ledger. Does the arithmetic care about that either?"

Gideon was quiet for a long moment. Mirabel watched her — watched her look at the diagram, at the struck-through column, at the eight remaining threads, and Mirabel saw her arrive at the answer the way she arrived at all of them: without flinching.

"No," Gideon said. "It cares that the sum resolves. The rest is politics. Politics can survive a bad season. He can't." She looked down at the king. "Forgive me. For choosing the math over the mess."

"Child," the king said, "you are the first person in sixty years to choose *me* over the mess. Cut the thread."

* * *

The middle threads were the hard ones.

Not because they resisted — because they *remembered*. Each one Mirabel pulled carried something of the old king up through her hands: his coronation day, bright with trumpets; his wife's funeral, gray with rain; the night the sickness first came, and the terror, and the whispering courtier who said *there is a way, majesty, there is a way to defer what cannot be denied* — and the moment of consent, the hinge of the whole catastrophe, when a frightened man had said yes.

Mirabel pulled the fifth thread and got the consent, full-strength, poured directly into her chest, and she understood suddenly why the king had begged them on the road — no, he hadn't begged them, that was later, that was the mirror room — she understood why bindings killed their makers. Not as punishment. As *bookkeeping*. Every deferral kept its receipt.

"Grief," she gasped, when she could speak. "That's what's in these. You weren't caging magic, majesty. You were caging mourning."

"Yes," the king said. He had begun to weep at some point, silently, the tears running into the hollows of his temples. "And it has been very patient."

"Fourth thread resolved," Gideon said, and her voice wavered for the first time, and steadied. "Third. *Halven.* This one's anchored to *you*, Mirabel."

Mirabel's hands stopped above the stone.

"What?"

"Not to your body. To your *craft*. It's — the maker built a defense into the structure. Anyone who works the unbinding gets caught in Halven. It's a snare for exactly what we're doing." Gideon's stylus hovered. "It's designed to transfer the binding into the unbind-er. You cut Halven, and the king's death-deferral becomes *yours*. You'd carry it. All of it. Sixty years of deferred death, accruing, forever."

The room was very quiet. The candle flame stood perfectly straight, as if listening.

"Well," Mirabel said. "There it is."

"There it is," Gideon agreed. Her face was doing something complicated. Mirabel recognized it, because she'd seen it on the road outside the border town, and in the cisterns under Harrowgate, and once, terribly, in the temple cellar by flooded lamplight: Gideon Iversen calculating the cost of a thing and refusing to look away from the total.

"So we don't cut it," Mirabel said. "We route around it. There's always slack in a binding, if the maker was—"

"He wasn't," Gideon said. "I've read it four times. There's no slack. There's no workaround. It's Halven or nothing, and nothing means he dies bound, and the thing keeps the crown, and the valley stays salt." She set down the stylus. "Mirabel. Listen to me. I need to tell you what I've decided, and I need you to not interrupt, because if you interrupt I'll lose my nerve, and I don't have much."

"Don't," Mirabel said.

"I read the structure. You pull. That was the bargain. But Halven doesn't distinguish between the reader and the puller — it catches whoever's *working* at the moment of severance. So it doesn't have to be you." Gideon held up her right hand. The ring sat on her thumb, salt-white, patient. "It's keyed to the bearer anyway. The ring chose me. The visions come to me. The maker's seal answered to me at the gate. By every rule in this structure, I'm the primary worker. You're my instrument."

"I am not going to stand here and watch you—"

"You bound your sister to save her," Gideon said, "and she never forgave you, and you've carried it across a kingdom. I know. I know exactly what it costs to decide for someone else. So I'm not deciding for you. I'm telling you the arithmetic, and the arithmetic says the bearer pays, and I'm the bearer, and I *choose* it. That's the whole difference, Mirabel. That's the entire difference between your sister and me. She wasn't asked. I'm asking myself, and I say yes."

"You don't even know what you'd be carrying—"

"I'm a scribe," Gideon said. "Debt is the only language I've ever spoken fluently. Let me pay this one."

The king made a small sound. When Mirabel looked at him, he had turned his face away, toward the swallowing dark of the wall, an old man granting privacy to a grief that wasn't his.

Mirabel knelt there with her gift roaring in her ears and hated the arithmetic with her whole body, and could not find a single flaw in it.

"Third thread," Gideon said quietly. "On your count."

"On mine," Mirabel said. "*Mine.* You keep your hands off the working until Halven's cut. Promise me. Promise me or I walk out of this room and let the thing keep the crown."

Gideon held out her hand — the ring-hand — palm down over the circle, and said, "Witnessed. Witnessed by the ninth name and the bearer of the ring. My hands stay clear until Halven falls."

The ring flared white.

"Witnessed," the dark agreed, in a voice like sand shifting, and Mirabel did not let herself think about what else had just heard the terms.

* * *

They cut Halven together, in the end — there was no other way; the snare needed two hands to spring, a reader and a puller, and Gideon kept her promise to the letter by reading while Mirabel pulled, and the thread parted, and the snare snapped shut on—

Nothing. On empty air. On the space where a bearer should have been, except that in the instant of severance the ring had flared so bright it left a ghost across Mirabel's vision, and the binding's hunger had lunged for the nearest vessel and found—

The ring itself. The relic. Sixty years of deferred death pouring into a circle of salt-white metal that had been made, Mirabel realized, watching it glow like a coal, for exactly this. A vessel. A ledger. A place to write the debt down.

The maker had planned it all along. The ring was not a key. It was a *purse*.

"It's holding it," Gideon whispered. "All of it. The whole deferral. It's — the ring is going to burn out, Mirabel, it's taking the debt so neither of us—"

"Later," Mirabel said, because six threads remained and the king's breathing had gone thin as thread itself, and whatever the ring could bear, it would have to bear *after*.

They worked the sixth and seventh fast, riding the momentum, Gideon calling anchors and Mirabel pulling, and the room began to change around them — the black walls losing their swallow, catching the candlelight now, throwing back reflections that had never been there before: the three of them, multiplied, a whole gallery of kings and scribes and smugglers watching themselves work. The mirror room filling with mirrors at last, now that its purpose was ending.

The eighth thread was the valley's twin — the binding on the *land* entire, the wide silence the old king had laid over the world so that his private cage would go unnoticed. Mirabel pulled it and felt it part like ice at thaw, and somewhere beyond the palace walls, in fields and forests and free cities, she knew that things were waking up that had been asleep for sixty years. She heard, distantly, a bell in the city below begin to ring with no one ringing it.

"One thread," Gideon said.

The ninth. The innermost. The death itself, coiled at the center, patient as interest.

The king reached up and took Gideon's free hand. "Scribe," he said. "When it comes — don't look away from me. Everyone looks away. I would like one person, at the end, who reads figures for a living, to witness that the account closed clean."

"It closes clean," Gideon said. "I'll certify it."

Mirabel pulled.

The ninth thread did not resist. It *released* — sixty years of deferred death coming home like a tide, and the king's body arched, and his grip on Gideon's hand tightened with a strength that had no business being in it, and his eyes went wide and clear and *grateful*, and then, gently, between one breath and the next, he was finished.

The account closed.

For a heartbeat the room was utterly silent. Then the crown — sitting on its cushion where the courtiers had left it, because the thing that wore it had no head to wear it on in this room, only hands and mouths borrowed from others — the crown *cracked*. A single sound, like ice giving way on a spring river, and a seam of light split it from rim to rim, and through the palace, through the city, through the whole sprawl of the usurper's kingdom, Mirabel felt every oath sworn upon it come loose at once — sixty thousand soldiers feeling, all in the same instant, the peculiar vertigo of remembering that they had never actually believed.

Far away, horns began to sound. Not the palace's horns. Confused ones. Arguing ones.

Gideon knelt with the dead king's hand in hers, and she did not look away until the last breath had fully left, and then she closed his eyes with two fingers, precisely, the way she did everything, and said, in a voice that shook only a little:

"Closed. Certified. Witnessed by the ninth name." She looked up at Mirabel, and her face was wet, and she was smiling. "The ledger balances."

The ring on her thumb had gone dark and cool and lighter than air.

And in the doorway behind them — a doorway that had been locked, that Gideon had forged the writ to open, that no one should have been able to reach past three checkpoints and a garrison — stood a courtier in palace gray, flanked by guards, and his mouth was moving, and the voice that came out of it was not his own.

"*Clever,*" said the thing wearing the crown. "*But a purse can be reopened.*"
