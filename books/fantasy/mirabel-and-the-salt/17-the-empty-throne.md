## 17. The Empty Throne

The king died at dawn, and the palace learned of it the way a body learns of a wound — all at once, from everywhere.

Gideon felt it first as silence. For sixteen days the mirror room had hummed with the thing's attention, a pressure behind her teeth like a tooth about to break. She had stopped noticing it the way you stop noticing a stone in your shoe, until it was gone and every step felt strange. Now the corridors of the royal palace rang with a different sound: bells, but wrong ones, rung by hand instead of rite, clanging out of rhythm because no one alive remembered what the death-knell was supposed to be. The true king had been dead to the world for thirty years. No one had written down how to mourn him.

"The testament," Mirabel said. She was on her feet already, gray-faced, her hands still shaking from the unbinding. There were burns across her palms where the wild craft had turned in her grip like a living thing, and she did not look at them. "We have maybe an hour before someone thinks to seal this wing. Less if the usurper's captains are quick."

"Then we move now."

"Gideon." Mirabel caught her sleeve at the door. Her eyes were red-rimmed and steady. "The king's body is still warm. What we just did — there will be men who call it regicide before the hour is out. Whatever happens next, whatever they say about us—"

"I know what I did," Gideon said. "I wrote it down."

That was the truth of it. While Mirabel had worked the unbinding with her hands full of fire, Gideon had worked it with ink — tracing the binding's structure in the air as the ring's last visions showed it to her, naming each knot of the old king's grief aloud so that Mirabel could cut where Gideon pointed. Scribe-work. Precision. The one skill the temple had given her that turned out to be a weapon after all. And when the binding came apart, when the thing wearing the crown shrieked through forty courtiers' mouths at once and then went out like a snuffed lamp, Gideon had felt something tear loose in her own chest — the ring's voice, the visions, the small bright gift she'd carried since the flooded cellar. It hadn't hurt. That was the terrible part. It had simply left, the way a debt leaves when it is finally paid: relief and emptiness in the same breath.

She didn't have time to grieve a gift she'd only just learned she had. The bells were still ringing.

They went out into the mirror room's antechamber and found the first body.

He was a guard, young, his halberd dropped beside him, kneeling over a courtier in silver-gray who wore the usurper's colors. Both of them were breathing. The guard looked up at them with the blank, washed face of a man waking from a long dream, and Gideon understood: the thing's grip had broken everywhere at once. Every servant, every soldier, every smiling courtier who had spent years speaking with two voices — they were all coming back to themselves in the middle of a palace built on their captivity, and some of them were finding blood on their hands.

"What — what year is it?" the guard whispered.

"Move," Mirabel said gently, stepping past him. "Ask questions later. Trust me on that."

* * *

The great stair was a river running uphill.

Refugees going the wrong direction — that was Gideon's first thought, absurdly, the scribe's instinct to categorize. But these weren't refugees. They were household staff pouring up from the underlevels with candlesticks and kitchen knives and, in one case, an entire ceremonial tapestry wrenched off its rod and carried like a banner. A chambermaid Gideon recognized from the clerks' corridor — a woman who had brought her tea every morning with perfect, hollow courtesy — stood at the landing directing traffic with a voice like a whip-crack. When she saw Gideon's clerk grays, she grabbed her arm.

"You're one of the writing folk. Is it true? Is he dead? The real one?"

"He's dead," Gideon said. "Both of them, in a way."

"Then get out of the way or help." The woman shoved a stack of papers into her hands — requisitions, orders, the usurper's seal stamped in wax gone soft with heat. "Captain Vess is burning the archives. Everything with the crown's hand on it. If you're a scribe who isn't theirs, you'll want to see this."

Gideon looked at the papers. Looked at Mirabel, who was watching the smoke already threading up from the east wing with an expression Gideon had learned to read as *we don't have time for this*. Then she looked at the papers again, and the scribe in her did the arithmetic the soldier in her would have missed.

"They're not destroying evidence," she said slowly. "They're destroying debts."

Mirabel frowned. "What?"

"Look at the seals. Grain levies. Conscription rolls. The indenture contracts — here, this one's marked for the salt valley temples." Her hands were shaking, and not from fear. "The usurper's whole legitimacy is paper, Mirabel. He fed the cities, branded the gifted, marched his armies — and every bit of it was authorized by documents issued in the true king's name while the thing wore him. Burn the archive and there's no proof the authority was false. In fifty years, historians will argue about whether the usurper was ever a usurper at all."

"And the testament?"

"Is in the archive."

They ran.

* * *

The royal archive occupied the oldest part of the palace, a vaulted warren below the east wing where the air tasted of dust and tallow. Gideon had never been inside it — clerks of her grade copied documents; they didn't touch the originals — but she knew its layout the way she knew everything the temple had taught her: by catalog. Wing C, rows one through forty, legal instruments of state. Row thirty-one, testaments and succession. She could have walked it blindfolded, which was fortunate, because the smoke was getting thick enough to do it for her.

The fighting started at the archway.

Not soldiers against rebels — that came later, and worse. This was uglier and more confused: freed servants with kitchen knives against palace guards who were themselves half-freed, some fighting to protect the archive, some trying to burn it, none of them sure anymore whose side they'd been on for the last decade. A man in captain's braid swung a torch at a scullery boy and then stopped mid-swing, staring at his own arm as if it belonged to someone else. Someone was screaming *the king is dead, the king is dead* in a voice that couldn't decide whether it was grieving or celebrating.

A guard lunged at Mirabel and she didn't even slow down — she caught his wrist, turned him into the wall with a economy of motion that spoke of years on the wrong side of the law, and kept moving. But Gideon saw her stagger afterward, saw her press her burned palm flat against the stone. The unbinding had taken more than the ring had taken from Gideon. Mirabel's craft was guttering low, a lamp nearly out of oil, and they both knew what that meant: no wild tricks between here and row thirty-one.

"We're just two people in a hallway," Gideon said. "Act like it."

It worked better than magic would have. They put their heads down, hunched their shoulders, and became exactly what the chaos expected: clerks, insignificant, beneath notice. Twice guards waved them through with the reflexive courtesy drilled into them by the thing's court — *mind the smoke, madams* — and Gideon wanted to laugh and scream at once. Thirty years of tyranny, undone, and politeness still opened doors.

Row thirty-one was burning at both ends.

Someone had already fired the near shelves. Heat rolled off them in waves, and the vellum was catching with a smell Gideon knew too well — she'd smelled it once in Saltmere, when the inquisitors burned a novice's confiscated letters, and she had told herself then that it was only paper. It was never only paper. Every scroll on those shelves was somebody's life reduced to a legal instrument, and the flames were eating them in order, alphabetically, as thorough as any ledger-keeper.

"The far end," Mirabel said. "Thirty-one comes before thirty-nine. If the fire's moving alphabetically—"

"It's moving from the door. Go left."

They went left, coughing, eyes streaming. Gideon pulled scrolls at random and threw them clear — contracts, a marriage settlement, something that looked like a border treaty — unable to read the labels through the smoke, saving what she could and knowing she couldn't save it all. Behind her Mirabel was tearing a tapestry off the wall to beat back the flames, and the fire answered her for one bright second — flinched, drew back — before guttering out of her reach entirely. She swayed. Gideon caught her elbow.

"Save your strength. Find me the shelf marker."

"Third bracket. *Testamenta Regum.*"

There. A cedar box, older than everything around it, its lid stamped with nine interlocking seals — the Gate of Nine Names, the same sigil that had opened the mirror room. Gideon's hands knew what to do even with her gift gone: check the seal integrity, note the accession mark, log the condition. Thirty years of muscle memory. She broke the wax with her thumb and lifted out a single scroll, heavy paper, written in a strong careful hand that faltered only twice.

She read the opening lines by firelight, and her throat closed.

*"To whoever finds this, and has the courage to have looked: I, Aldric Vane, fourth of my name, write this in the last year of my strength, before the binding takes the rest..."*

"It's real," she managed. "Mirabel, it's real, it's his hand, it predates the binding — look at the date—" And then the ceiling beam overhead cracked like a snapped bone, and thinking was over.

* * *

They came out of the east wing into daylight and riot.

The inner courtyard, where Gideon had once stood disguised as a crown clerk with her heart hammering, was unrecognizable. The fountain had been toppled. The usurper's banners — white stag on black — were being torn down by dozens of hands, and where each one fell, someone raised something else: a free city's colors, a guild flag, in one corner a bedsheet dyed salt-white that Gideon recognized with a jolt as the old valley standard, the one the temples had been forbidden to fly for three generations. Somewhere a crowd was chanting a name she didn't know, and then she realized it wasn't a name, it was a word — *charter, charter, charter* — the free cities' old dream, the thing the Circle had argued about around borrowed fires, suddenly shouted by ten thousand mouths in a courtyard that still smelled of smoke.

And at the far gate, marching in formation against all of it, came the usurper's regulars.

Not the branded ones. Not the harvested. These were the professional companies from the border garrisons, the ones who had never worn the thing's second voice, and they moved with the terrible calm of soldiers who had received orders and did not yet know their orders were orphaned. Their captain rode at the front with the usurper's seal on a chain around his neck, holding it up like a talisman, and Gideon watched the crowd's fury meet that seal and hesitate — watched grown men with knives take a half-step back from a piece of wax on a chain, because thirty years of paper does not die in an hour.

"That seal is worth ten thousand soldiers right now," Mirabel breathed. "Until everyone agrees it's worthless, nobody moves first."

"So we make it worthless."

"How, exactly? You can't forge a king's death, Gideon. Well — you could, obviously, you're terrifyingly capable, but not in the next five minutes."

"No." Gideon tightened her grip on the cedar box. "But I can publish one."

She had spent her whole life understanding what documents *did*, not just what they said. A testament in a sealed box was treasure. A testament nailed to a door was law. The difference was not the words — the difference was the reading, public and undeniable, witnessed by enough people that denying it afterward became its own scandal. The temple had taught her that without meaning to. Every debt record she'd ever copied had been read aloud in the temple hall precisely so that no debtor could claim ignorance. She had sat through hundreds of those readings, listening to her own name, learning exactly how power sounded when it was spoken in public.

"There." She pointed to the great doors of the audience hall, tall as trees, facing the courtyard. "Nail it open. Read it from the steps. And we need—" her mind raced through the crowd, cataloging, the old scribe habit turning revolutionary — "a herald's voice. Someone the crowd will go quiet for."

Mirabel was already grinning, wild and exhausted, and for a moment she looked twenty years younger and completely unhinged. "You know what I love about you? Most people would say *this is insane*. You say *we need a herald*."

"I'm a professional."

They found their herald by accident: a temple-trained cantor, of all things, a broad-shouldered woman in servant's gray who had been singing the death-knell alone and out of rhythm since dawn because no one else remembered it. When Mirabel explained — fast, low, urgent — the woman looked at the cedar box, then at the advancing soldiers, then rolled up her sleeves.

"I sang at the old king's coronation festival when I was a girl," she said. "Before the quiet years. I remember how it's done."

The cantor's voice did what armies couldn't. She climbed the audience-hall steps with the scroll held high and began to read the testament in the formal cadence of proclamation, the cadence every soul in that courtyard had heard at every levy announcement and grain decree of their lives — and the crowd *stopped*. The fighting stilled. Even the soldiers at the gate slowed, craning, because the words coming off those steps were the old king's own voice, thirty years deferred, saying plainly and publicly what the usurper had spent a decade burying:

*"...and I name the regency void. What wears my crown wears nothing. Let the free cities hold their charters. Let the salt valley wake. And let no man rule in my name who has not wept at my grave."*

By the third line, people were climbing onto the fountain rubble to hear better. By the fifth, the soldiers at the gate had stopped advancing, their captain's seal suddenly a very small object in a very large silence. By the end, when the cantor read the succession clause — the true king naming no heir, entrusting governance to a charter of the free cities, *witnessed and sealed* — the roar that went up knocked soot from the eaves.

Gideon stood on the steps with ink-stained fingers and a box that had cost her everything, and let herself feel it for exactly four seconds.

Then the captain at the gate drew his sword and pointed it at the steps, and the four seconds ended.

* * *

Later — much later, though neither of them could have said how much later — Gideon would remember the next hour as weather rather than battle: pressure, noise, movement, the crowd surging like a tide and the soldiers breaking not in defeat but in confusion, half of them lowering weapons when they heard the testament read a second and third time from different corners of the courtyard, passed mouth to mouth, the scribe's oldest technology. The captain fought. The captain was dragged from his horse by six former servants and disappeared under a wall of bodies, and Gideon made herself look away, because she had chosen this side and chose meant choosing all of it.

What mattered — what she made Mirabel understand through a bleeding split lip, shouting over the din — was that the box and the scroll had to leave the palace tonight. Public reading was law, but original documents were history, and history had a way of being edited by whoever held the paper. The Circle needed it. The charter councils needed it. And there were people in that crowd — she could feel them, the ones not cheering, the ones watching the steps with flat patient eyes — who served no cause except whoever paid next.

"Vellum Row," Mirabel said, reading her mind. They were pressed together in the shadow of the audience-hall colonnade, both of them wrecked, the cedar box wrapped in the ceremonial tapestry between them. "The underground routes are still intact. We can be in Harrowgate in four days if the roads haven't—"

"Gideon!"

The voice cut through everything. Gideon turned, and her whole body went cold.

Tobias Vess stood at the edge of the colonnade, in clerk's grays gone gray with ash, holding a leather document case she knew on sight — she had forged writs in that case's style, had memorized its stitching. His face was the worst thing she'd seen all day, and she had seen a man trampled. It wasn't angry. It was *pitying*.

"You're alive," he said. "Good. Because I have to tell you what happened at Harrowgate after you left, and you need to sit down."

The cedar box suddenly weighed as much as the palace.

Behind him, through the smoke, the bells had finally found their rhythm — the true death-knell, remembered at last, rolling out over a city that was already changing hands. Gideon did not sit down.
