## 18. The Price of the Ring

The king died at dawn, and the ring began to sing.

I had heard bindings break before — once from the outside, when a Circle elder unwove a curse on a miller's daughter, and the sound had been like a harp string snapping in a quiet room. This was not that. This was every string in the world letting go at once, a chord so vast it wasn't sound anymore but pressure, pushing through my teeth, through the salt crust on my boots, through the stone floor of the mirror room until the mirrors themselves wept condensation.

And through it all, the ring on Gideon's hand sang a small, clear note that only I seemed to hear.

She was kneeling beside the king's body. What was left of him was finally just a body — an old man, thin as winter reeds, his face slack with something that looked almost like gratitude. The thing that had worn his crown for sixty years was gone. I'd felt it go, felt it tear loose from the armies east of the capital like roots ripped out of wet ground. Somewhere out there, ten thousand soldiers were waking up this morning with no idea why they'd marched.

Gideon reached down and closed the old man's eyes with two fingers, gentle, the way you'd close a ledger.

"Mirabel," she said. Her voice was steady. That should have warned me.

I crossed the room too fast and knelt across from her, and that was when I saw her hand.

The ring had been salt-white since the day she pulled it from the flooded cellar in Saltmere. Now it was burning white — not glowing, *burning*, cold fire crawling up her wrist in filaments fine as script, and where the fire touched her skin, her skin was going the same color. Salt-white. Translucent. I could see the ghost of her veins beneath it like ink under vellum.

"Gideon." I grabbed her wrist. The fire didn't burn me; it ignored me entirely, which was worse. "Take it off."

"I tried." Still steady. Still scribe-calm, cataloguing her own death. "It doesn't come off anymore. It hasn't for — since the unbinding started, maybe. Maybe since Vellum Row." She turned her hand over, studying it the way she studied anything: thoroughly, without flinching. "The binding needed a bearer. The king bound his death into the land, and the land held it, and the ring held the land, and now the binding is broken and the debt has to go somewhere." She looked up at me. "It's going into me."

"No." The word came out flat and ugly. "No, that's not how it works. Bindings need a *willing* bearer. That's the whole architecture of them — I've told you, I've *seen* what happens otherwise—"

"You bound your sister to save her," Gideon said quietly. "You've never told me if you asked her first."

The words went into me like a knife finding the gap between ribs. Because I hadn't asked. Because Wren had been sixteen and screaming and the pyres were coming, and I had done the one unforgivable kind thing, and my sister had slept through three years of her own life because of it.

"That was different," I said.

"It was exactly the same." She flexed her whitening fingers. "You're about to do it again. To me. Right now. I can see you building the argument."

I *was*. I could feel it assembling itself behind my teeth — the logic, the desperate arithmetic. *The ring wants a bearer with latent gift. You have the visions, the voice, the thread of it the valley left in you. But I have craft. Real craft. Let me carry it — let me take your place — I know how to hold a binding, I've held one before, I can survive it—*

"Mirabel." Gideon took my face in both hands. Her palms were cold as river stones. "Look at me and lie. Tell me you could carry it and come out the other side whole. Tell me Wren came out whole."

I couldn't. My mouth opened and nothing came out, and Gideon nodded slowly, like I'd confirmed a line item.

"That's what I thought."

* * *

We had maybe minutes. Maybe less. The fire was climbing past her elbow now, and where it climbed, the color drained — her forearm gone the white of the salt flats, the white of the archive paper she'd spent her life copying. I knew what the ring was doing. I'd seen its twin in the Circle's oldest texts, the marginalia nobody read: *the bearer pays in gift, or in self.* A person with no gift paid in flesh. A person with even a seed of magic paid with the seed, and the ring would pull until the seed was gone, and keep pulling until there was nothing left to pull.

Gideon had a seed. Small, buried, the faint hum I'd felt off her the night we caught each other mid-theft in the temple cellar — the reason the ring had chosen her in the first place, the reason it had sung for her and not for me. The visions. The voice. The relic's whisper she'd been hearing since Saltmere, that she'd confessed to me somewhere on the white waste with the fire between us, embarrassed, like telling me she talked to herself.

The ring would take all of it. And then, because a broken binding is greedy and grief-shaped, it might not stop.

Unless someone else offered.

"Here's what happens," I said, and made my voice the teacher's voice, the smuggler's voice, the voice I used on frightened people in cellars at midnight. "You offer it freely, it takes the gift, it lets you go. That's the clean version. The dirty version is it takes the gift and finds the shape of the wound underneath and fills that too. Bindings don't distinguish between magic and memory, Gideon. I've unbound things. I've seen what's left."

"I know." She said it simply. "I watched you tell the Circle. Chapter and verse. You were very good." A ghost of a smile. "You're very good at everything except being loved, Mirabel Dravenmoor, and I'm about to fix that, so listen."

"Don't—" 

"Listen." Her grip tightened on my face. The white had reached her shoulder. Under the translucent skin I could see the fire arranging itself into lines, into columns, into *script* — the binding writing itself into her, and gods help me, it wrote in a hand I recognized. The old king's hand. The same hand that had signed the decree that owned her. Even now, even dying, his work found a scribe.

"The valley," Gideon said. "Sixty years of peace built on a lie. Magicless children growing up half-alive and calling it safety. A temple owning people with paper. Me, copying ledgers for nineteen years because a debt I never incurred said I had to." Her eyes were bright and furious and completely clear. "I have spent my entire life paying other people's prices. My parents' debt. The king's fear. The temple's greed. Every single day of my life, someone else's ledger, someone else's terms, and I signed because I didn't know I was allowed not to sign."

"So don't pay this one either," I whispered. "That's my whole point. Refuse. Walk away. We'll find another way—"

"There is no other way. The binding broke and the debt exists and it will go into the nearest vessel, and the nearest vessel is me, and if I refuse it won't stay refused, it will just take me *unwillingly*, and unwilling is the difference between Wren sleeping and Wren never waking." She said her sister's name back at me like a key turning in a lock. "You taught me that. On the road. You taught me everything that matters, Mirabel, and the last lesson is this one: you cannot save people from their own choices. You can only love them while they make them."

"So let me love you," I said, and my voice cracked straight down the middle, "and let me make the choice, because I am *older* and I am *harder* and I have already lost everyone I have ever—"

"*No.*"

The word rang in the mirror room like the ring's note had rung. In the hundred mirrors around us, a hundred Gideons said it, a hundred Mirabels flinched.

"I refuse," she said. "Formally. As a scribe. Witness me." And she lifted her whitening hand, and the ring blazed, and she spoke in the cadence of contracts — the cadence I'd heard her use on forged writs, on temple clerks, on the gate guards at Harrowgate — "I, Gideon Iversen of Saltmere, freely and without coercion accept the price of the unbinding. Let the record show. Let the record *hold*."

The fire surged up her throat.

I did the only thing left. I threw my craft at it — wild, undisciplined, everything I had, the hedge-magic they burned you for, the smuggler's tricks and the binder's knots, all of it hurled at that climbing flame to smother it, to redirect it, to *take it* —

And it passed through me like wind through a doorway.

Because the contract was spoken. Because willing bearers only. Because she had out-lawyered me in the language of my own art, using the precision they'd beaten into her in a temple scriptorium, and there was nothing in this world or under it I could do about it.

I stopped fighting and held her instead.

* * *

She shook in my arms while the ring worked. I want to write that it was quick, or painless, or beautiful. It was none of those things. It was a taking. The fire crawled down inside her and I felt her gift leave her the way heat leaves a room when the door opens in winter — a slow, inexorable drain, and against my chest I felt the exact moment the voice went silent in her, the relic's whisper she'd carried for months, and she gasped like a diver surfacing, and then kept gasping, because the ring kept pulling, hunting for more, hunting for the wound—

"Say something," I begged her. "Say your name. Say it over and over — give it something to hold onto that isn't magic—"

"Gideon Iversen," she whispered. "Gideon Iversen. Scribe. Of Saltmere. Daughter of Marta and Ede Iversen, who owed nine crowns and eleven coppers, and I never owed anyone anything, I never — Gideon Iversen —"

The pulling slowed. Stopped. The ring dimmed from blazing white to the dull salt-color it had always been, and slid — loose at last, its hunger fed — off her finger and onto the floor between us, where it lay like a dead thing.

Her arm stayed white. From fingertip to shoulder, the skin translucent as sea-glass, the veins showing through like faded ink. It would fade, the texts said, or it wouldn't. The gift was gone. The visions, the voice, the seed the valley had secretly left in her — gone, paid, entered into a ledger no one would ever audit.

But she was breathing. She was warm. She was *there*, blinking up at me from the floor of a dead king's mirror room, and after a long moment she said, hoarse:

"Well. That's the last time anyone owns a piece of me."

And I laughed, or sobbed, or both, and pressed my forehead to hers, and said the thing I had been circling for two thousand miles of salt and road and raid and escape, the thing I'd sworn I'd say plainly or not at all:

"I love you. Not despite this. Not because of it. Just — I love you, Gideon Iversen, and I wanted you to hear it from someone who isn't a ring."

"I know," she said. "I've known since the cisterns. You hold your craft the way you hold everything else — like it might be taken away." Her hand, the living one, came up and touched my jaw. "It won't be. Not by me."

Above us, somewhere in the palace, a bell began to ring. Then another. Then a dozen, ragged, unscheduled — not the measured court bells but the alarm pattern, the *something-has-broken* pattern, and under the ringing came a sound I knew from Harrowgate's riots and Saltmere's festival nights: a crowd, thousands strong, awake and moving.

The king was dead. The thing that wore his crown was ash. And every soldier, every clerk, every courtier who had ever felt the usurper's grip through the binding was waking up this morning free and terrified and looking for someone to blame — or someone to follow.

Gideon sat up. She looked at her white arm, flexed the fingers, and picked the empty ring off the floor. She turned it over once, the way she turned over everything — thoroughly, without flinching — and put it in her pocket.

"The testament," she said. "The king's true testament. He told us where it was sealed before the end. If the city burns before it's read, we traded everything for nothing." She stood, swayed, and I caught her elbow, and she let me, which was its own small miracle. "Come on, Mirabel. One more entry in the ledger."

Down in the streets, the bells kept ringing, and the crowd was getting louder, and somewhere below us a door burst open — boots on stairs, many, fast, coming up toward the mirror room.

I pulled her toward the servant passage, and we ran.
