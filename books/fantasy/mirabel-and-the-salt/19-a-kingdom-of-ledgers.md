## 19. A Kingdom of Ledgers

The Charter Hall had been a counting house once. You could still see it in the bones of the place — the long scarred tables where grain merchants had haggled over bushels, the iron rings in the floor where debtors' carts had been chained while their owners signed away the next seven years of their lives. Now the tables held inkwells instead of scales, and the iron rings held nothing at all, and forty people who had never been allowed to agree on anything were trying to write a country.

Gideon sat at the third table from the window, because the light was best there and because she had learned, in twenty years of copying other people's numbers, that position was power. Whoever sat nearest the master copy set the terms for everyone downstream. She had spent her whole life downstream.

"Read it back," said Vess of Harrowgate, who chaired the session by virtue of being the loudest person in any room she entered.

Gideon drew the draft toward her. Her hands were steady. They were always steady — that was the thing no one warned you about, that the worst weeks of your life would pass through the same fingers as the ordinary ones, and the fingers would not care.

"*Article Nine,*" she read. "*No free city shall levy tax upon the exercise of gift, craft, or craft-adjacent labor. The practice of magic shall be neither licensed nor forbidden, but witnessed, per Article Eleven.*"

"Witnessed how?" someone called from the back — a salt-flat woman with burn scars up one arm, one of the refugees from the border towns. "Who does the witnessing?"

"That's Article Eleven," Vess said. "We're on Nine."

"We were on Nine an hour ago."

"Because you keep objecting to Nine."

"Because Nine is a lie if Eleven has teeth missing."

Gideon kept her eyes on the page and let them argue. This was her second trade now, the one nobody had assigned her: listening to forty frightened, furious, hopeful people shout past each other, and finding the sentence underneath the shouting that they could all actually live inside. It turned out that twenty years of transcribing temple ledgers had taught her something no one intended. Every argument was a ledger. Every grievance had two columns. And somewhere between *what was taken* and *what was owed* there was usually a line small enough to write.

She waited until the burned woman and Vess had run out of breath, then said, quietly, in the voice she'd used on stubborn clerks for two decades:

"What if witnessing isn't a person? What if it's a record?"

Silence. Forty faces turned toward her.

"Every city keeps a registry," she went on. "Not of who *is* gifted — we're not building the inquisitors' lists back with prettier handwriting — but of bindings. Of workings done *to* someone rather than *by* them. If a binding is laid on a person without their consent, it goes in the book. Anyone can read the book. Anyone can petition to have an entry struck." She tapped the page. "You don't need watchers. You need witnesses who come forward on their own, and a place where what they say becomes permanent. Magic doesn't need permission. It needs *memory*."

The burned woman stared at her for a long moment. Then she laughed — a short, cracked sound, not entirely kind.

"A scribe's answer," she said. "Of course. You people think paper saves lives."

"It did mine," Gideon said. "Once. Badly, and late, and at cost. But it did."

Vess slammed her palm flat on the table before anyone could answer that. "Registry of bindings. Struck by petition, readable by all. Mark it provisionally and move to Ten, for the love of every god we've stopped believing in."

Gideon marked it. In the margin, in her own hand, in the small cramped script the temple had drilled into her until it replaced her natural writing, she added a note: *provisional pending Article Eleven review — G.I.*

Then she looked at the note, and crossed out the initials, and wrote *Gideon Iversen* in full, letters tall and plain, the way she had begun signing things three weeks ago when the first charter drafts went out under her name and she realized, with a sensation like a bone setting, that she was allowed.

* * *

Mirabel found her at dusk, when the session broke and the hall emptied into the smell of frying onions and wet stone. The mage came down the aisle between the tables with her hood back and salt dust still in her hair — she never quite washed it out anymore, or maybe she didn't want to; it was the color of the road they'd walked together, the white waste where Mirabel's gifts had guttered and she had felt mortal for the first time and told Gideon about it like it was a gift.

"You look like a woman who won an argument," Mirabel said, dropping onto the bench across from her.

"I look like a woman who postponed one."

"The highest form of winning. Ask any government." Mirabel reached across and pulled the draft toward herself, scanning it with the quick suspicious eye she brought to everything written down. She trusted songs, rumors, the shift of weather, the twitch of a stranger's hands. Paper she regarded as a loaded weapon pointed generally at the poor. "Article Nine. Hm. You got the registry through."

"Provisionally."

"Nothing in a charter is ever more than provisional. That's what makes it a charter and not a gravestone." She set it down. Her fingers lingered on the margin note. "You signed your whole name."

"It seemed strange," Gideon said, "to sign *G.I.* on a document about not letting institutions erase people."

Mirabel looked up at her then, and there was something in her face that Gideon had learned to read across a salt flat and a prison escape and a mirror room full of dying king: the hedge-mage cataloguing damage, checking whether the person in front of her was holding together or merely arranged to look like it.

"And you?" Mirabel asked. "Holding together?"

It was the question everyone asked now, gently, constantly, the way you'd touch a bruise. Three weeks since the unbinding. Three weeks since the king's death arrived like a tide finally allowed to go out, and the thing wearing his crown had shrieked through forty courtiers' mouths at once and then simply — stopped, the way a fire stops when the last of the wood is gone. Three weeks since Gideon had walked out of the palace carrying a dead man's testament and an emptiness behind her ribs where the ring's voice had lived.

The ring itself sat in her pocket right now. It always sat in her pocket. Emptied, cold, plain salt-white stone, worth nothing to anyone. She carried it the way she supposed soldiers carried shrapnel: not from sentiment, exactly. From honesty about what was lodged in her.

"I'm holding together," she said. "And I have one piece of unfinished business that isn't holding anything together at all. It's just sitting there. Being finished business that I haven't finished."

Mirabel raised an eyebrow.

Gideon took out her satchel — the old temple satchel, salt-stained, strap mended twice — and lifted out a ledger.

It was a thin one. Cheap vellum, temple-stamped, its spine cracked from years of being opened to the same pages. She had carried it out of Saltmere without thinking, the way you carry a habit, and she had carried it through everything since, and she had not once opened it since the night she fled. She knew what was in it. She knew it the way you know your own debts.

Her name. Twenty years of entries. *Copy fee, credited against principal. Lodging, debited. Candle allowance, debited. Interest, compounded quarterly, per temple law, per crown sanction, per the ancient and holy arrangement whereby a child orphaned at six owes her keep forever and the sum grows faster than any honest hand can fill it.* Page after page. Column after column. A life measured in a currency designed so that she could never reach zero.

"They gave me a certified copy when I left," Gideon said. "Did I tell you that? After the palace fell, the temple sent a clerk to the free cities' camp with my records, all proper and sealed, because — I assume — they wanted to establish that my debt survived the change of government. That obligations persist across regimes." She smiled, and it felt strange on her face, this new smile that had edges. "He wasn't wrong, technically. Obligations do persist. That's rather the point of what we're writing here."

"Gideon." Mirabel's voice had gone careful. "What are you going to do?"

"There's a brazier by the door," Gideon said. "For warming ink."

"Gideon, that's a temple record. Sealed. Certified. Under the old law, destroying it—"

"Under the old law, I *am* the property the record describes." She stood. Her legs were steady too. Everything about her was steady; it was only ever the world that shook. "Under the new law — the one I spent today writing — a binding laid on a person without consent goes in the registry, and anyone can petition to strike it. Well. I'm petitioning." She weighed the ledger in her hands. It weighed almost nothing. It had always weighed almost nothing. That was the trick of it; that was the whole horror of ledgers; they were feathers that pressed on you like mountains. "I'm striking the entry myself. First petitioner. Someone has to be first, and I've decided it's going to be me, tonight, in front of witnesses, so that tomorrow some clerk in some city can't say the precedent is unclear."

Mirabel stood too. For once she didn't argue. Gideon watched her decide not to — watched the hedge-mage, smuggler of the gifted, breaker of bindings, open her mouth and close it again, and finally just nod, once, slow.

"Front of witnesses," Mirabel said. "Right. Then let's get some."

* * *

They gathered in the Charter Hall after dark, maybe thirty of them — the drafting delegates who hadn't gone to bed, the burned woman whose name was Saret and who had brought her own torch out of what Gideon suspected was professional solidarity, a handful of clerks and cooks and one very old archivist from Harrowgate who had reportedly forged documents for three different rebellions and considered tonight's ceremony a matter of guild interest.

Vess lit the brazier properly. Coals, not flame — Gideon insisted on coals. "If it burns fast, it's theater," she explained. "If it takes time, it's true."

Saret snorted. "A scribe's funeral."

"Yes," Gideon agreed. "Exactly that."

She opened the ledger to the first page. Her own name, written by another hand, in the year she turned six. Below it, the opening balance: *orphanage costs incurred to date*. She had never known the number. She had copied it ten thousand times without ever letting herself total it, because totalling it meant admitting it was real.

Now she read it aloud. All of it. Not every entry — there were thousands, and the room would have grown cold before she finished — but the shape of it. The first page. The worst pages: the year the interest schedule changed, the year she'd begged the provost for a day off to stand at her mother's grave and been charged a *clerical processing fee* for the request. The final page, the certified copy's seal, the clerk's neat notation: *debt survives transfer of sovereignty; balance outstanding*.

Her voice held. She was almost disappointed in it.

When she finished, the room was silent in the particular way rooms are silent when everyone present understands that the ceremony is not really about the person standing at the front.

"By what authority is this debt struck?" asked the old archivist, formally, because formalities mattered — formality was the skeleton the new law would hang on, and everyone in this room knew that laws without skeletons collapsed into mobs.

"By the authority of the petitioner," Gideon said. "Under Article Nine, provisional, pending Eleventh review. Witnessed by—" she looked around, and began naming them, one by one, each name spoken clearly and written in the margin of the ledger itself, so that the record of its own destruction would be complete. Vess. Saret. The archivist, who gave his name as Corvus and added, deadpan, "formerly of several governments, currently of none." Mirabel Dravenmoor — and Gideon paused at that, because she had never heard Mirabel's surname spoken aloud before, and Mirabel flinched slightly, and nodded anyway, and let it be written.

"And witnessed by Gideon Iversen," Gideon said, "petitioner, scribe, formerly property of the Temple of Saltmere, currently — " she thought about it — "currently author."

She fed the ledger to the coals.

It did not burn fast. Cheap vellum is stubborn stuff, water-marked and tallow-treated, and it charred at the edges and resisted, and she had to hold it, page by page, turning it like a cook turning meat, watching twenty years of arithmetic curl and blacken and lift as ash into the dark above the brazier. It took a long time. Long enough for her arms to ache. Long enough for the heat to press against her face like a living thing. Long enough for her to think, somewhere in the middle, of the flooded cellar where she'd found the ring, of the novice they'd dragged to the pyre, of every entry she had ever copied for someone else's ledger, every debt she had recorded that she hadn't owed and couldn't question.

The ashes smelled like a burnt meal. That was the anticlimax of it. Twenty years, and it smelled like someone had ruined dinner.

When the last page crumbled, she wiped her hands on her skirt and found that they were shaking after all. So her body had been keeping score too. Good. At least one part of her had been honest.

Saret stepped forward and put something in Gideon's palm: a pinch of salt, from a small pouch at her belt. Border-country custom, she said — you scatter salt on the ashes of a debt so the wind knows it's finished and nothing grows there again. Not even the memory of owing.

Gideon scattered it. The wind, coming through the high windows of the old counting house, took the ash and the salt both, and kept them.

* * *

Later — much later, the hall empty, the delegates gone to beds they owned outright for the first time in most of their lives — Mirabel found her at the window table, packing her satchel. Gideon worked slowly. There was no hurry anymore, and she was still learning what to do with that.

"You're leaving," Mirabel said. Not a question. She'd seen the satchel.

"Tomorrow. Before the Eleventh Article sessions start, or I'll be arguing registries until midwinter." Gideon folded the last clean quill into its cloth. "There's a courier leaving for the valley in three days. Salt caravans are running again — actual trade, actual roads, the passes open for the first time in a generation. I've been drafting letters for half the camp, everyone sending word home that the war's over. And I keep getting to the end of everyone else's letter and thinking about mine."

"You haven't written yours."

"I've written it eleven times." She smiled ruefully. "In my head. Where all my best work stays."

"What does it say?"

Gideon considered the question honestly, because Mirabel deserved honesty and had paid for it in salt and prison cells and a sister who never woke.

"It says: *To the Provost of the Temple of Saltmere, from Gideon Iversen, freewoman. Your records concerning me are destroyed. Your claim died with the crown that enforced it. I am returning to Saltmere not as your debtor and not as your fugitive, but as the first teacher in a school for children who are about to discover they have gifts, in a valley that is about to discover it can hold magic, and you will find me insufferable about both.*" She paused. "Then it says something less dignified, which is that I want to see the salt flats green."

Mirabel was quiet a moment. Outside, the free city murmured — bells, arguments, someone singing badly in a language Gideon didn't know, all of it the sound of a place deciding to keep existing.

"The binding's dissolution is moving slower than anyone predicted," Mirabel said. "The Circle's scouts say the boundary crust is already cracking near the eastern passes. Water coming back first, then moss, then — who knows. Grass, maybe. Trees, eventually. A generation from now, children will swim in pools that used to be salt pans and never know what it cost." She tilted her head. "You'll teach them?"

"Somebody has to teach them the dangerous lessons."

"I hear the dangerous lessons are taken," Mirabel said. "By a notorious criminal. Formerly of several governments."

"Formerly of none, actually. That's what makes her good at it." Gideon closed the satchel. Under her palm, through the leather, she could feel the ring — cold, small, finished. Tomorrow she would decide what to do with it. Bury it, perhaps, in the first green thing the valley grew. Or give it to the school, to sit in a window and catch light, an emptied thing that had once held a voice, a warning to every child that power always asks for a bearer.

Tonight, though, there was one more task. She drew a fresh sheet toward her, dipped her pen, and wrote, in her own tall plain hand, the first line of the letter she would actually send:

*To the Provost of the Temple of Saltmere —*

—and beneath it, after a moment's thought, one line more, addressed to no institution at all, to the valley itself, to whatever was waking under the white crust as she wrote:

*I am coming home. Get ready to hold me.*

The ink dried. Somewhere east, under the salt, something stirred.
