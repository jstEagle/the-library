## 20. Salt Thaw

I felt the binding die from two hundred miles away.

Not with a sound. With a *loosening* — like a corset cut at the back, like a hand that had been resting on my sternum for thirty years suddenly lifting away. I was standing in a market in Harrowgate, haggling over dried plums I didn't need, when every candle in the square guttered at once and then burned taller than any of them had a right to. A child three stalls down sneezed sparks. The plum-seller's scales tipped without being touched.

The whole city went quiet in the way cities do when something enormous happens somewhere else and everyone pretends it didn't.

I paid for the plums. My hands were shaking so badly I dropped three of them.

"She did it," I said to no one. To the air, which for the first time in my life felt like it was listening. "That stubborn, ledger-keeping fool actually did it."

Because that was the thing about unbindings — I knew this better than anyone alive — they don't just release what was held. They release everything the holding was holding *back*. And Gideon had stood in that mirror room, in front of a king half-dissolved into his own death, and she had chosen to pay whatever price the ring demanded rather than let me pay it. I had argued. I had begged. I had offered my own gift on the altar of her stubbornness, and she had looked at me with those ink-stained scribe's eyes and said, in the flattest voice I have ever heard her use:

"You've already lost someone to a binding, Mirabel. I'm not going to be your second."

And then she'd done it anyway. And the visions — the voice in the ring, the gift she'd carried all her life without knowing its name — had gone out of her like a snuffed lamp. She'd told me afterward, on the road east, very quietly, staring at the fire: "It's strange. It's so quiet. I keep waiting for it to talk."

I'd wanted to tell her then that quiet wasn't emptiness. That some things only grow in silence. But I'd spent too many years being wrong about bindings to trust my own comfort, so I'd just taken her hand, and she'd let me, and we'd sat there until the fire burned down.

Now, standing in Harrowgate's market with magic crawling back into the world like blood into a numb limb, I bought my plums and started walking east.

Home. If you could call it that. If home could be a place you'd spent your whole life smuggling people *out* of.

* * *

The salt flats were different.

I'd crossed them eleven times in my smuggling years, and every crossing had been the same: white to the horizon, hard as fired clay, dead as a debt unpaid. Magic died at the valley's edge like a moth hitting glass. You stepped over the boundary and felt yourself go *thin* — your gifts guttering, your senses narrowing down to just flesh, just Mirabel, mortal and small. I'd hated it. I'd also, if I'm honest, been grateful for it once or twice, in years when being Mirabel-the-hedge-mage was more dangerous than being nobody at all.

Now I crested the last rise above the flats and stopped so hard my pack swung forward and nearly took me off my feet.

Green.

Not much. Not yet. But where the salt crust had cracked along the old caravan road, there were threads of something growing — pale, tentative, the color of new lettuce, pushing up through the white like the world testing whether it was allowed. And where the green showed, the salt had darkened, gone damp, gone *soft*. The flats looked less like a desert and more like a lakebed remembering it had once been a lake.

Magic doesn't announce itself. People expect fireworks. What it actually does is seep — into soil, into water, into the cracks of things — the way spring melt finds every low place in a field. The binding had been a dam across the whole valley, and now the water behind it was coming through, and everything low and long-starved was drinking.

I walked out onto the flats. Halfway across, my gift came back.

It didn't come back the way it left — thinning, guttering, a candle going out. It came back like a tide. One step I was plain Mirabel Dravenmoor, tired woman with bad knees; the next step the salt under my boots was singing. Not loudly. Just a hum, deep and mineral, the sound the land makes when it's been silent so long it's forgotten how. I knelt and put my palm flat on the crust and felt it: the whole valley underneath, waking up cell by cell, vein by vein, an animal coming out of a sleep so deep it had been mistaken for death.

Thirty years ago, someone had laid this binding. An old king, grieving, terrified of dying, who had caged magic itself to cage the thing eating him. And the valley — my valley, Saltmere, the place that raised me and outlawed me in the same breath — had lived inside that cage so long that everyone called the cage *peace*.

I understood why they had. That was the worst part. I'd spent years furious at the temple, at the inquisitors, at the decree that put gifted children on pyres. But the truth was uglier: the valley hadn't built the pyres because it was cruel. It had built them because it was *afraid*, and fear with no outlet curdles into law. In a land where magic couldn't live, the gifted were not people. They were cracks in the wall. Errors in the ledger.

Well. The ledger was open now.

I stood up, wiped my hand on my coat, and kept walking toward the smudge of rooftops on the southern horizon that was Saltmere.

* * *

You always think the town will be smaller when you come back. Mine wasn't. It was exactly as large as I'd left it, which somehow made it worse — all those streets I'd run down as a girl with grass stains on my knees, before anyone knew what I was, before even *I* knew what I was. The same crooked bell tower. The same smell of brine and bread off the tanneries' row. The same temple on its hill, white walls, blue shutters, looking exactly like the place that had owned Gideon Iversen's whole life.

Except.

Except the shutters were open, and the light coming out of them was doing something light shouldn't do — bending, faintly, the way it bends over hot coals or strong workings. Except there were children in the temple yard, a dozen of them, sitting in a circle around a novice — Tamsin, I realized, the girl Gideon had lied to an inquisitor to protect, the one we'd smuggled out and who had come *back*, the little idiot, the brave little idiot — and Tamsin was holding up her hands, and above her palms floated a sphere of water drawn straight out of the temple well, turning slowly, catching the sun, and the children were laughing.

Laughing. At magic. In Saltmere.

I stood at the bottom of the temple hill for a long time, feeling like a ghost at my own funeral, until a voice behind me said, "You're blocking the road, hedge-witch."

I turned. An old woman with a fish cart, squinting at me. Then squinting harder. Then going white.

"Mirabel," she said. "They said you were dead. They said — the decree —"

"The decree's ash," I said. "Like the king. Like the usurper's writs." I dug in my pocket and produced the one document I'd carried out of the capital myself, folded soft as cloth from handling: the free cities' charter, first draft, signed by the council at Harrowgate, bearing the seal of a dead true king and the fresh ink of a living one — because the free cities, in their infinite bureaucratic wisdom, had needed someone to hold the throne until a proper succession could be argued about, and apparently a former smuggler with a documented history of saving gifted lives qualified. I still hadn't decided how I felt about that. "There's a copy for your town council. There are copies for every town council between here and the coast. Nobody's burning anybody in Saltmere ever again."

The fishwife stared at the charter. Stared at me. And then — this is the part I will remember until I die — she reached into her cart, pulled out a fish, and gave it to me.

"For your supper," she said gruffly. "You look half-starved. Your mother fed me through the famine winter, you know. Whole street would've starved without her." She shoved the fish at my chest and turned her cart around. "Don't stand in roads," she said over her shoulder, and was gone.

I stood there holding a fish, crying like a fool, in the middle of the town that had outlawed me.

Belonging earned, Gideon had written in one of her letters on the road. Not inherited. I'd thought it was pretty phrasing at the time. Standing in that street, smelling brine and bread, I finally understood it was the whole architecture of everything.

* * *

I found her in the archive cellar.

Of course I did. Some things don't change; they just change hands. The flooded cellar where she'd found the ring — where a drowned archive had given up a king's binding-work to a debt-girl with quick fingers — was drained now, the water gone wherever the binding's dissolution had sent it, the shelves drying out in the lamplight. Someone had set lamps down there. Someone had been working.

Gideon sat at a table among the shelves with her sleeves rolled up and ink to her elbows, copying. Of course she was copying. The world could end twice over and Gideon Iversen would be found at a desk making sure the ending was properly recorded.

She didn't hear me come down the stairs. Her hair was shorter — she'd cut it somewhere on the road, I never learned exactly when — and there was a scar on her forearm I didn't recognize, thin and white, from the palace revolt. She'd told me about that night in fragments: the corridors full of smoke, courtiers tearing each other's livery off, the two of them running with the king's testament wrapped in oilcloth against Gideon's chest while the thing wearing the crown screamed through a hundred mouths at once. She never talked about the mirror room itself. I never asked. Some rooms you carry closed.

On the table beside her lay the ring.

Emptied. That was the only word for it. It had been salt-white and humming when she'd pulled it from the floodwater; it had sung with a dead king's voice and shown her visions and dragged us both across a kingdom by its pull. Now it was just a band of pale metal, dull as pewter, small enough to lose in a pocket. She wore it sometimes. Mostly it sat on the table while she worked, catching lamplight, keeping her company the way an empty chair keeps company.

"Tamsin says the well water tastes sweet now," she said, without looking up. Which meant she'd heard me on the stairs, which meant her scribe's ears were as sharp as ever, which meant she'd known I was coming since Harrowgate, probably, and had simply decided to finish her page before greeting me. Typical.

"It does," I said. "I drank from it. Also a fishwife gave me a fish, and I cried in the street, and I need you to never tell anyone either of those things."

Her pen paused. Then she laughed — really laughed, head back, the laugh I'd heard maybe six times in a year and a half of shared fires and shared danger and one shared act of world-breaking — and set down her pen, and looked at me.

"It's good to see you," she said simply. No flourish. Gideon didn't do flourishes. "You're late. The school opens in four days."

"The school," I said. "Which you founded. In the temple that owned you."

"In the building," she corrected, "that used to be a temple. The temple dissolved its debts when the binding broke — all of them, everyone's, they had no choice, half their authority was propped up by the crown's writs and the crown's writs are kindling now. The abbot asked me to stay. Asked me to *run* it, actually, the records side." She gestured at the shelves, the drying books, the ledgers of a valley's worth of debts that no longer existed except as history. "Someone has to write down what happened. Someone has to teach the children what their gifts are before they're afraid of them. Fear curdles into law — you taught me that."

"I did not teach you that. I complained about that for years and you told me I was being dramatic."

"And then it turned out to be true, and I updated the record accordingly." She smiled. It faded slowly, the way hers did, like a lamp running low. "Mirabel. Are you staying?"

And there it was. The question under all the questions. Because I had spent my whole life leaving — out of the valley with other people's children, out of towns ahead of inquisitors, out of rooms before anyone could ask me to be permanent. Leaving was the one craft I'd mastered before magic. And here was Gideon, ink-stained and scarred and quieter than she used to be, asking me to stop.

"I saw green on the flats," I said, instead of answering. "Coming in. Real green, growing up through the salt. Give it ten years and this valley will be unrecognizable. Give it twenty and no one will believe it was ever anything else." I came around the table and picked up the ring. It was warm from lying near her hands. "People will forget. That's what people do. They'll forget the pyres and the decree and the lie of peace, and someday some historian will dig up an inquisitor's writ and think it's a forgery, because surely no one actually —"

"They won't forget," Gideon said. "Not here. I won't let them." She tapped the table, and I saw then what she'd been copying all this time: not the old debt ledgers. A new book. A school register, blank pages waiting, and beside it a stack of pages already filled — names, ages, the shape of each child's gift, noted in her precise, patient hand. Dozens of names already. The valley was full of them, waking up like the ground was waking up, children hearing humming in the wells and lights behind their eyes, and every single one of them was going to be written down, and taught, and *kept*.

"That's why I stayed," she said quietly. "I spent my life writing down what people owed. This is the opposite. This is writing down what people *are*." She looked up at me, and her eyes were wet and absolutely steady. "But the dangerous lessons — the bindings, the unbindings, how to know when a gift is eating its bearer — I can't teach those. I don't have the gift anymore, and I don't have the scars. You have both."

The lamplight moved on the water-stained shelves. Somewhere upstairs, faintly, the children laughed again.

"You're asking me to stay," I said. "In Saltmere. Permanently. You understand what you're asking. I haven't slept in the same bed for a full season since I was nineteen."

"I know exactly what I'm asking," Gideon said. "I've drafted it three times. I have notes." And despite everything — despite the mirror room and the price and the quiet where her visions used to be — she grinned, sharp and crooked, the grin of a woman who had once forged an inquisitor's writ with steady hands and a hammering heart. "Term starts in four days, teacher. The register's open. First page is yours if you want it."

I looked at the ring in my palm — empty, quiet, done — and at the register with its waiting pages, and at her.

"Write my name," I said, "and I'll stay."

She dipped her pen. And in a flooded cellar that had once given up a king's secret to a debt-bound girl, in a valley learning to hold magic the way it had once learned to hold nothing, Gideon Iversen wrote *Mirabel Dravenmoor* at the top of a brand-new ledger — and for the first time in either of our lives, a record was made that set no one's debt, bound no one's hands, and cost nothing but ink.

Outside, the salt thawed.
