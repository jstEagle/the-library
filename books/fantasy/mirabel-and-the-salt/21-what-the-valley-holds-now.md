## 21. What the Valley Holds Now

The school had been a granary once, and before that a temple outbuilding, and before that — if the foundation stones were any judge — something older that Saltmere preferred not to name. Gideon liked that about it. Buildings, like people, carried their debts forward, and this one had finally been forgiven enough to hold something new.

She stood in the doorway on the first morning of the thaw-month and watched eleven children file in through doors that no longer locked from the outside.

That detail had taken argument. The temple elders, newly humbled and newly generous with other people's patience, had offered her the granary on the condition that she keep "proper order" — which in temple language meant locks, ledgers, and a register of every child who crossed the threshold, with their gift noted in triplicate. Gideon had spent fifteen years copying such registers. She knew exactly what they were for.

"No ledger," she'd said, standing in the abbot's parlor with salt dust still on her boots from the road. "No names written down anywhere the crown could ever find them. The crown is gone, but crowns come back. That's the first lesson I learned and I'm not unlearning it for anyone's proper order."

Abbot Corren had pressed his lips thin and then, remarkably, laughed. "You sound like your mother."

"My mother sounded like me," Gideon said. "I was just too young to notice."

So there were no locks and no ledger. There was a slate by the door where children scratched whatever mark they liked when they arrived — a bird, a spiral, a smear of chalk that might have been a dog or a storm — and wiped it clean when they left. Attendance was a courtesy, not a record. Gideon had designed it that way on purpose, and every morning when she saw the marks appear and vanish she felt something in her chest loosen a fraction more, like ice going off a river in stages.

Eleven children. In a valley of four thousand souls where magic had not existed for three hundred years, eleven children had already begun to show the gift, and the thaw was barely two months old. Mirabel said the number would grow. Mirabel said a great many things with the flat certainty of someone who had watched magic return to land before, though when Gideon pressed her on where, exactly, she'd seen it, Mirabel developed a sudden fascination with the state of the roof.

"Seats," Gideon called now, and the children scrambled — mostly. A boy named Tam, eight years old and built like a fence post, remained at the window with his palm flat against the glass, watching frost crawl across the pane in patterns he wasn't making on purpose. Or maybe he was. His gift ran cold, the way some children's ran warm, and the frost followed his moods: sharp whorls when he was angry, long soft feathers when he was calm.

"Tam."

"It's doing the thing," Tam said, aggrieved, as though the frost were a sibling who wouldn't stop copying him.

"Then it can do the thing during lessons." Gideon came and stood beside him. Up close the pattern was extraordinary — feathers within feathers, each barbed finer than a scribe's finest hairline. Fifteen years ago she would have wanted to copy it. She still wanted to copy it. Some parts of a person didn't get rewritten; they just got new contexts. "Look how it branches. Every branch decides, and then the next one decides off that. That's what we're doing here. You're deciding what the cold becomes."

Tam looked at her with the deep suspicion of an eight-year-old being handed a metaphor. "I don't decide. It just happens."

"It just happened to me for thirty years too," Gideon said. "Copying ledgers. It just happened. Then one day I decided." She tapped the window once, gently, over the center of the pattern. "Lessons."

He went. The frost stayed on the glass all morning, refusing to melt even as the sun climbed, and Gideon decided to take that as a compliment.

* * *

Mirabel arrived at midday, as she always did, without any discernible approach — one moment the road was empty, the next she was leaning in the doorway with salt wind in her hair and an expression that suggested she'd been listening longer than politeness allowed.

"You're still starting them on shapes," she said.

"I'm starting them on *control*."

"Shapes are control." Mirabel came in and dropped onto the edge of a desk like she hadn't sat in furniture in years, which, Gideon supposed, until recently she hadn't. "You teach them to make the shape small before you teach them to make it stop. Stopping is violence. Smalling is craft." She pulled a stub of chalk from her coat and tossed it to Tam, who caught it against his chest with both hands like a sacred object. "Show me your frost, fence post. Big as you can."

"Mirabel—" Gideon began.

"He's indoors, the floor's stone, and I'm standing right here." Mirabel's voice was light but her eyes weren't. Gideon had learned to read that difference. It was the difference between Mirabel performing recklessness and Mirabel managing risk, and the second one happened far more often than people assumed, because the first one was so much louder.

Tam grinned and spread his fingers. The cold came off him in a wave Gideon felt through her boots. Frost sheeted across the floor, up the desk legs, feathering across the slate by the door, erasing the morning's marks under white lace. The other children oohed. One of them — Sefa, whose gift was a low warm hum that made bread rise and milk keep and had once, terrifyingly, made a beehive follow her home — clapped.

"Good," Mirabel said. "Now take it back. All of it. Slowly."

Tam's grin faltered. He frowned. The frost stayed.

"Slowly," Mirabel repeated, and crouched to his level. "It went out of you like breath. It comes back like breath. You don't yank it. Yanking is how you hurt yourself, and hurting yourself is how you end up scared of your own hands, and being scared of your own hands is how you end up either dead or working for somebody worse than dead." She held out her own hand, palm up. "Breathe in. Pull it home gentle. Like calling a dog you actually like."

Tam breathed. For a long moment nothing. Then the frost shivered, and began to withdraw — not melting, Gideon noticed, but *retracting*, pulling back along its own branches like water running up a hill, folding itself smaller and smaller until it poured back across the floor and up Tam's arm and vanished under his sleeve. The boy swayed. Mirabel steadied his shoulder.

"That," Mirabel said to the whole room, "is the dangerous lesson. Not making it big. Anyone can make it big. Making it *come home*." She straightened, caught Gideon's eye, and something passed between them that needed no words — because they had both seen, in different rooms, in different years, what happened to people who never learned to call it home. Mirabel had seen it in her sister's sleeping face. Gideon had seen it in the mirror room of a palace, in the ruined kindness of a king who had bound his death instead of breathing it in.

Gideon wrote nothing down. But she remembered everything. That was the scribe's gift, the one no unbinding could take: the memory that holds shape.

* * *

In the afternoon the little ones napped on pallets and the older ones practiced, and Gideon sat at her desk by the window and did the work nobody had asked her to do and nobody could stop her from doing: she was writing a curriculum.

Not a ledger. A book. Working title, scrawled and rescrawled: *What the Valley Holds Now*, though Mirabel kept suggesting increasingly ridiculous alternatives (*Salt and Sense*, *Don't Yank It*) and Gideon kept declining them with dignity. It would be a primer. The first ever written for the valley's gifted — plain language, no temple cipher, no crown seal, copyable by anyone with a pen and the nerve. How to feel the gift arrive. How to shape it small. How to call it home. What bindings were and why they were always, always a cage with the lock on the inside. And a last chapter, half-written, that Gideon both dreaded and refused to skip: *When Someone Offers to Take It Away.*

Because that was the scar that didn't show. The valley was free, the usurper's armies had melted like the frost, the free cities' charter was signed and sealed with nine names and a tenth added later in Gideon's own careful hand. But somewhere out there were people who remembered being promised peace in exchange for emptiness, and some of them missed it. Some of the parents who brought their children to the school did so with fear riding their shoulders — not of the children, exactly, but of what the children would cost. Gideon heard it in the doorways. *My mother says we were safer before. My uncle says the pyres were wrong but so is this. Is it true the ring ate your gift? Will it eat hers?*

To that last question Gideon had only one answer, and she gave it plainly, every time, because plainness was the only honest currency left to her.

"The ring didn't eat anything. I gave it. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole lesson."

Whether the parents believed her varied. Whether the children believed her did not. Children, Gideon was learning, believed evidence.

* * *

At day's end she walked the room alone, as she always did, checking shutters, righting chairs, doing the small closing rituals of a woman who had once closed a scriptorium every night of her life and found, to her surprise, that the habits transferred. Some things about being owned you got to keep, once you chose them.

The ring sat in the window.

It had been there since midwinter, when she'd finally stopped carrying it. For months after the unbinding she'd kept it in her pocket, touching it the way you touch a healed break — not because it hurt, but to confirm it didn't. Then one gray afternoon Mirabel had said, "It's finished, Gid. Whatever it took, it took. It's just a ring now," and Gideon had understood that keeping it in her pocket was its own kind of binding — hers, on herself, holding a grief in place past the point of usefulness.

So she'd set it on the sill of the east window, where the morning sun came through the salt-thinned glass, and let it sit.

Emptied, the ring was beautiful in a way it had never been while it spoke. When it had a voice it had been merely strange — salt-white, warm, humming with borrowed purpose. Now it was just itself: a band of pale metal, unmarked, catching light. Sometimes the frost from Tam's window reached along the wall and touched the sill near it, delicately, the way a cat investigates a thing that smells of another country, and withdrew. Sometimes Sefa's hum made it sing faintly against the stone, a note too high to hear properly, more felt than heard.

Gideon picked it up now, out of habit, and turned it in the low gold light.

Nothing. No vision. No voice. No pull toward anything, east or otherwise. Just metal, and light, and the weight of everything it had cost, which was not a property of the ring at all but of the hand holding it.

"You know," Mirabel said from the doorway — she did that, she would always do that, materialize at the exact moment privacy ended — "most people would sell it."

"Most people didn't pay what I paid."

"Most people would say that's exactly why to sell it."

Gideon turned the ring once more and set it back on the sill, precisely in the sun. "Most people aren't scribes. We don't destroy records. Even finished ones." She smiled, and felt the smile reach whatever was underneath it, which these days it usually did. "Besides. Someday some child is going to ask me what it was. I'd rather point at it than describe it."

Mirabel came and stood beside her at the window. Outside, the valley lay green-gold in the late light — actually green now, in patches, the salt crust breaking up in fractal patterns that Tam would have loved, grass coming through in places where nothing had grown in living memory. Down the slope, Saltmere's roofs caught the sun. The temple bell rang the evening hour, and for once it sounded like what it was: a bell, marking time, asking nothing.

"The elders want to expand next year," Gideon said. "Take the old tannery too. They're talking about dormitories. Children from the far farms."

"Far farms." Mirabel snorted. "Half those kids' parents won't send them. You'll be smuggling again before you know it."

"Would you mind?"

Mirabel looked at her. In the window light her face was older than it had been on the salt flats, younger than it had been in Harrowgate's cells, and entirely, complicatedly her own. "I mind doors," she said. "Locked ones. Yours don't have any. So no. I don't mind." She pushed off the sill. "Same time tomorrow?"

"Same time tomorrow. Try not to teach them anything flammable before I've had tea."

"No promises. The girl with the sparks has real talent. Wasted on safety."

"Wasted on *survival*—"

But Mirabel was already gone, down the stairs and out into the evening, laughing, leaving Gideon alone with the ring and the light and the faint smell of chalk and ozone and, beneath it all, the green smell of a valley remembering what it was for.

Gideon stood a moment longer. Eleven marks on a slate, wiped clean. A book half-written. A debt paid in full, in a coin she'd chosen herself.

She blew out the lamp, though the sun hadn't quite set, and left the ring in the window to catch the last of it — and as she turned to go, she thought she saw, just at the edge of seeing, the frost on the far pane bloom one new feather, fine and deliberate, reaching toward the light like a hand learning, at last, what it was allowed to hold.
