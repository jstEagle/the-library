## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The boundary stone at Miller's Rise had cracked again, and I was the one who had to kneel in the frost and pretend I knew why.

"Third time this season," I muttered, running my thumb along the fissure. The stone was older than the guild, older than the road, older — if you believed the stories — than anyone's memory of why we needed it at all. It hummed faintly under my palm, that low dead note all the stones made, like a bell that had been told to stop ringing and had never quite agreed.

That's what null-magic felt like up close. Not silence. A held breath.

I sat back on my heels and looked out over Hollowmere, because looking at the valley was easier than looking at my own future, and the valley at least changed with the light. Morning fog still clung to the low fields like a sheet someone had forgotten to take off the bed. Smoke rose from the chimneys of Thistle Row in thin, obedient columns. Somewhere below, Brother Anselm would be sweeping the guildhouse steps for the fourth time since dawn, because sweeping was the only chore he'd never once complained about, and Merric would be in his study pretending to read a ledger he'd read a hundred times.

And me — Ada Ashgrove, nineteen years old, keeper of cracks in rocks. That was the whole of it. That was the shape of my life, drawn in chalk and then traced over so many times the lines had gone grey.

The guild called us wardens. Wardens of the Vale, keepers of the Quiet Accord, sworn to tend the ninety-one boundary stones that ringed the valley like a string of dull grey beads. What we actually did was walk. We walked the circuit every month, we scraped moss, we patched mortar, we noted which stones sang and which had gone mute, and once a year we held a festival where everyone got drunk and pretended the whole arrangement wasn't the strangest thing in the world.

Because here was the thing nobody in Hollowmere liked to say out loud: magic existed. Everyone knew it existed, the way you know the sea exists even if you've never seen it. There were stories — good ones, the kind Tam's— the kind travelers used to bring before they stopped coming. Cities that floated. Rivers that ran uphill out of sheer spite. People who could pull fire out of the air like thread from a spool.

None of it worked here. In Hollowmere, a spell was just words said with confidence, and a charm was jewelry. The boundary stones drank it all down before it could so much as spark. Ninety-one stones, one valley, and not a drop of wonder anywhere inside the ring.

We were, as far as I could tell, the only people alive who lived in a hole in the world's imagination.

* * *

Merric found me at noon, the way he always did, appearing at my elbow without any sound at all for a man of sixty-odd years and considerable belly.

"You missed breakfast," he said.

"I ate at the stone."

"A hard biscuit is not breakfast, Ada, it's a punishment you've agreed to." He lowered himself onto the grass beside me with the groan of a man who'd been lowering himself onto things for decades and expected no sympathy for it. He looked at the cracked stone, then at me, and his face did the thing it always did when the guild's business came up — went pleasant and closed at the same time, like a door painted to look like a window. "How bad?"

"It'll hold till spring if I re-seat the mortar. But it shouldn't have cracked. None of them should. They're supposed to last forever, aren't they? That's what the archive says. *The Stones Are Eternal and So Is the Accord.* Very reassuring. Terrible poetry."

"The archive says a lot of things." He picked up a pinch of soil and let it run through his fingers. "You know, when I was your age, I asked Old Wenna the same question you're about to ask me."

"I'm not about to ask you anything."

"You're about to ask me why the stones are failing."

I shut my mouth, which he took, correctly, as confirmation.

"And I'll tell you what she told me," Merric went on, "which is that everything built by hands needs tending, and tending is not failure. Tending is the work. The day the stones need nothing from us is the day the guild is finished, and I confess I'd miss the walking."

"That's not an answer, Master Merric. That's a shrug with a beard."

He laughed — he always laughed, that was the maddening thing about him, he met every hard question with warmth until the question gave up and went home. "No," he admitted. "It isn't an answer. Some answers are heavier than a man wants to carry twice." He stood, joints crackling like green wood in a fire, and offered me his hand. "Come down before dark. And Ada — eat something that didn't come out of your saddlebag."

I took his hand. I always took his hand. He'd raised me after my mother died, or at least he'd been there while the guild raised me, teaching me letters and stone-work and how to swear convincingly in three dialects, and loving him was as easy and involuntary as breathing. Understanding him was another matter entirely. He was kind the way deep water is calm — you could float on it happily right up until you thought about what was underneath.

* * *

I want to say something honest about Hollowmere, since I'm the one telling this: it was beautiful, and I hated needing it to be enough.

In autumn it turned copper and gold, the terraced orchards burning color down the hillsides, the mere itself going flat and silver like a coin rubbed smooth. Children were born, and their names went into the guild ledger, and they grew up wanting sensible things — a forge, a farm, a place on the circuit. Nobody left. In living memory, exactly two people had ever crossed the boundary line outward, and one of them had been a madman who claimed the stones whispered to him, and even he had come back within the year, pale and quiet, saying there was nothing out there worth the silence in his head.

Nothing out there. A world full of floating cities and spiteful rivers, and we'd built ourselves a valley with the wonder drained out of it, and called it safety, and called the safety a blessing.

Some nights I climbed onto the guildhouse roof and lay on the warm slates and tried to imagine what magic felt like. Not what it *did* — what it *felt like*. Was it cold? Was it loud? Did it feel like being seen? I'd hold my hand up against the stars and think: somewhere past those ridgelines, a girl my age might be lighting candles with a word, and here I was, lighting them with a match, and being grateful for the match, and hating myself a little for the gratitude.

Restless. That was the word Wenna had used about me before she died, with the fond exasperation of a woman describing weather. *That girl has a door in her heart, Merric, and you keep painting over it.*

She wasn't wrong. There was a door in me, and I'd knocked on it my whole life, and nothing had ever answered.

Until dusk, on the day the stranger came.

* * *

I was on the western circuit, checking the stones along Ashgrove Rise — yes, my family named themselves after a hill, we were that kind of people — when I saw him come over the ridge.

He came out of the last of the light like something the day had forgotten to swallow. Walking was too generous a word for it; he moved the way a dropped puppet moves, each step a separate decision, and I could see even at distance that he was coming apart. Not wounded, exactly. Not bleeding.

Bleeding *light*.

It poured from him in slow ribbons — gold-white, soft as lamp-oil, trailing off his sleeves and hair and dissolving into the air before it touched the ground. Where it fell, the frost on the grass glowed and faded, glowed and faded, like breath on a window. My first thought, absurdly, was that someone had brought a piece of the outside world in with them, some scrap of real magic, and the valley didn't know what to do with it except watch it die.

My second thought was that he was going to fall down the rise and break his neck, and whatever he was, he deserved better than that.

I ran. I don't remember deciding to; my legs simply voted without consulting me. I caught him twenty yards short of the boundary stone, and the moment my hands touched his arms I understood two things at once. First, that he weighed almost nothing, as though most of him had already gone somewhere else. Second, that the light was coming *out through his wounds*, and there were many wounds, and they were old ones, healed wrong, reopened, healed again — a body that had been spent like currency.

"Hey. Hey — I've got you. You're across, you're inside the line, whatever that means, you're—" I didn't know what I was saying. I'd never said *you're safe* to anyone, because no one in Hollowmere had ever needed to hear it.

His eyes found mine. They were brown and ordinary and terrified, and then, as I watched, they began to fill with light from the inside, like windows at evening.

"Found you," he whispered. "Took the long way. Found you anyway." His hand came up — shaking, translucent at the edges, I could see the frost through his fingers — and pressed something against my chest. Folded paper. It was warm. It was *wet*, though there was no blood on it, only a stain spreading like a map of somewhere else. "Have to go to the mountain. Swear it. Swear you'll carry it before the new moon, or it was all—"

"All what?" I said. "All *what*?"

But he was smiling now, the way people smile when a pain finally stops, and the light in him was rising instead of falling, pouring up out of him like smoke that had changed its mind about gravity.

"Tell Merric," he said, "the debt—"

And he came apart in my arms. Not dramatically, not with any horror to it. He simply stopped holding himself together, and broke into motes — hundreds of them, thousands, drifting up through my fingers and past my cheek, each one warm as a candle carried past in the dark. Then the last of them winked out above the boundary stone, and I was kneeling alone in the frost, holding a folded map stained with light that refused to dry, in a valley where magic was supposed to be impossible.

Somewhere behind me, faint and clear, a voice that no longer had a mouth said: *found you.*

I looked down at the map. I looked up at Mount Veyre, black against the last violet band of sky, wearing its snow like a crown it hadn't earned.

Then I did the only sensible thing available to a warden of the Quiet Accord.

I ran home to tell Merric that magic had just died in my arms — and that it had known his name.
