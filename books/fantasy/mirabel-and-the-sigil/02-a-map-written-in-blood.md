## 2. A Map Written in Blood

The stranger died in the guild's east room, which was fitting, because that was where we kept everything we didn't know what to do with.

I'd helped carry him there on a door pried off its hinges, Merric at his shoulders and me at his feet, and I'd felt it even through my boots — that wrongness. The man bled light. Not glowing blood, not some pretty lantern-trick, but actual light leaking out of him through the wounds in his side, pooling on the floorboards like spilled honey and then fading, fading, as if the floor were drinking it. Three drops of it landed on my wrist during the carrying. They were warm. Then they weren't. Then they were gone.

"Null-magic should have stopped that," I said, when we'd laid him down.

Merric didn't answer. He was looking at the man's face with an expression I'd never seen on him before — not the easy, evasive kindness he wore like a good coat, but something older and much more afraid. He looked, for one unguarded moment, like a man reading a letter he'd hoped never to receive.

"I'll fetch water," I said, because someone had to say something normal, and apparently that someone was always going to be me.

When I came back with the basin, Merric had gone, and the stranger's eyes were open.

They were gray eyes, the color of weather about to happen, and they found me immediately, the way dying eyes do — no interest in the room, the rafters, the candle. Just me, standing there with water nobody would drink.

"You're guild," he said. His voice sounded like paper being folded.

"Apprentice," I said. "Ada Ashgrove."

"Ada." He tried the name like he was testing whether it would hold weight. "Good. Ada is a name that carries things." He coughed, and light came up with the cough, little motes of it drifting past his lips and winking out near the ceiling. "Help me sit up."

"You shouldn't—"

"Girl, I have been dying since Greywash. Everything I shouldn't do is already done. Help me *sit*."

I helped him sit. He weighed almost nothing, which frightened me more than the light did. People are supposed to weigh things. He leaned against the wall among the guild's forgotten furniture — a broken loom, three chairs of incompatible vintages, a tax ledger from before I was born — and looked at me with those weather-colored eyes, and said:

"The map. You'll need both hands."

"I don't understand what's happening."

"No," he agreed. "But you will, and sooner than either of us would like. Hands."

He wore a courier's coat, or what had been one — road-gray, many-pocketed, scorched along the left side where something had burned through cloth and nearly through him. From an inner pocket he drew a roll of oilcloth tied with waxed cord, and from another, a knife. He cut the cord himself. My hands were shaking too badly to be trusted with blades, though I didn't tell him that. Dying men can smell fear the way horses smell wolves, and I wanted him to think I was steady.

The oilcloth fell open across my palms.

It was a map. That was the first thing — a map, inked in fine brown lines, of the valley and the mountain above it, Mount Veyre rendered in careful cross-hatching with a spiral drawn at its heart. Rivers, roads, the boundary stones marked as small circles like beads on a string. And over all of it, in a different hand, a darker hand, writing I couldn't read — script that moved. Not legibly, not like letters rearranging themselves, but the way water moves under ice. I looked away and the writing kept moving behind my eyelids.

"It's written in blood," Tam said — I learned his name later, but I knew it then, the way you know a thing you'll grieve. "Mine, mostly. It has to be. Blood remembers what ink forgets."

"What is it?"

"A ledger." His breath caught. "A debt, if you want the uglier word. There's a court beneath that mountain, Ada Ashgrove, and your guildmaster knows its name. Ask him why the valley is safe. Ask him what *safe cost*. And when he won't answer — and he won't — you take this to the mountain yourself. Before the new moon."

"The new moon is nine days away."

"Eight now. Nights are greedy." He smiled, and it was a terrible smile, gallows-lean, the smile of a man who had made peace with something no one should make peace with. "Swear it. Swear you'll carry it. Not read it — carry it. Reading comes later, and reading will hurt, so save it for when you're strong enough to bleed."

"I'm not swearing anything until someone tells me—"

"*Swear.*"

The word came out with light behind it. Every mote still drifting in the east room turned toward him at once, like iron filings finding their magnet, and for a moment the dying courier was the brightest thing in Hollowmere, brighter than the candle, brighter than the lamp of the moon through the window, and I understood that whatever he was, he had not been entirely human for a long time.

So I swore. I put my hand flat on the map — the moving writing shivered under my palm like something waking — and I said the words he gave me: *I will carry it to the mountain before the dark of the moon. I will not open it before the threshold. I will not give it up while I live.*

Light ran up my arm from where my skin touched the blood-script. It didn't burn. It *counted*, somehow — I felt counted, tallied, entered into something's book. The oath settled into me the way a stone settles into a riverbed: permanently, and with the certain knowledge that the water would keep coming.

"Good girl," Tam whispered. He slumped back against the wall. "Now listen, because the rest comes fast. When I go, don't bury anything. Don't mourn out loud. And when the guildmaster asks what I gave you — and he will ask, he'll ask before my body's cold — lie to him. Lie beautifully. Lie the way he taught this whole valley to lie to itself."

"Merric doesn't lie."

"Everyone lies, Ada. Some just do it with better posture." His eyes were dimming; the gray of them was going the gray of ash. "One more thing. You'll hear my voice after. That's not haunting. That's just what's left when a soul's been spent and spent and spent again — it frays, and the frayed bits keep talking. Ignore me when I'm being foolish. I usually am."

"Tam—" I said, though I hadn't known the name a minute ago, and saying it cracked something in my chest I didn't have a name for either.

"Ah. So you do know me." The smile again, fainter. "Then maybe part of you already knew how this ends. Good. That saves time."

And he came apart.

Not dramatically — no scream, no burst. He simply stopped holding himself together, the way a dandelion stops when the wind decides. Light lifted off him in hundreds of small motes, gold-white, unhurried, rising through the east room's dusty air like snow falling upward, and where each mote touched the ceiling it went out with a sound too soft to be a sound. In three breaths there was nothing of him left but the smell of ozone and hot metal, and the shape of him pressed into the wall like a shadow that had outlived its owner, and the map in my hands, heavier than any map had a right to be.

I sat down on the broken loom and held it and did not cry, mostly.

* * *

Merric found me there an hour later. I knew he'd been told — old Wenna had seen the light through the east room window and gone straight to him, because in Hollowmere we run on gossip the way other towns run on grain. He stood in the doorway a long moment, looking at the shadow-shape on the wall, and I watched sixty years settle onto him like snowfall.

"He gave you something," Merric said.

Here was the moment to lie beautifully. I opened my mouth to do it.

"He gave me a map," I said, "and an oath, and about forty questions I intend to get answered."

So much for beautiful. Merric closed his eyes as if I'd struck him, and I felt a small vicious satisfaction in that, and a larger, colder shame right behind it, and I decided to feel both at once because I was done choosing feelings for other people's comfort.

"Show me."

I showed him. I unrolled the oilcloth on the loom frame and watched my guildmaster — kind, evasive, beloved Merric, who had taught me my letters and my knots and how to tell when a boundary stone was sulking — go pale. Not pale like surprise. Pale like a man seeing his own handwriting on a confession he'd buried decades ago. His hand came out toward the map and stopped an inch above it, trembling, not quite touching, the way you'd reach toward a snake you once loved.

"Where did he say to take it?" Merric asked quietly.

"He didn't say. But it's obvious, isn't it?" I tapped the spiral at the mountain's heart. "Here. Before the new moon. Eight days."

"No."

The word dropped into the room like a stone into a well. No splash, no echo — just depth.

"Merric—"

"No, Ada. You will not leave the valley. You will not carry that thing one step toward Veyre. Give it to me and we will speak no more of couriers or oaths or—" his voice did something complicated on the last word, "—or debts."

"You know what it is."

"I know what it costs." He finally looked at me, and his eyes were wet and furious and terrified, all three fighting for the same ground. "Child, I have spent fifty years keeping every soul in this valley alive by keeping every soul in this valley *ignorant*. One girl with a blood-map walking up that mountain unmakes all of it. Do you understand? Everything. The stones, the shield, the—"

"The protection you bought." I said it before I could stop myself, and watched him flinch as if the words had teeth. "That's what Tam called it. A debt. He said you'd know its name."

For a long moment Merric Ashgrove — no relation, though he'd let half the valley believe otherwise for years because it made people trust him — stood very still. Outside, dusk was coming down over Hollowmere, and somewhere a boundary stone hummed its low note, keeping the world's magic out and our wonder in, exactly as it had every night of my nineteen years.

"Go to bed," he said at last, gently, wearily, in the voice he used for ending conversations. "We'll talk in the morning. I promise you answers."

"You promised Tam's ghost you'd say that," said a voice from the corner of the room, dry as old paper, and I jumped so hard I knocked a chair over. "Well. I did warn you I'd keep talking."

Tam's voice. Coming from nowhere. From everywhere. From the frayed edges of a spent soul drifting through the guildhouse like dust.

Merric went white to the lips. And I stood there in the gathering dark with a dead man's laughter in my ears, a living man's lie in the air between us, and a map against my ribs that moved like water under ice — eight days until the new moon, and for the first time in my life, somewhere worth being.

I picked up the chair. I set it down again.

"Morning, then," I said sweetly. "Sleep well, Guildmaster. I know I won't."

And neither, judging by the sound of it, would he.
