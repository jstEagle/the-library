## 3. What Merric Signed

The guild archive smelled of dust, tallow, and forty years of Merric's pipe smoke, which had soaked into the shelves so thoroughly that the books themselves seemed to be quietly smoking. I'd swept it a thousand times. I'd catalogued its contents in my head a hundred more: ledgers of boundary-stone maintenance, treatises on null-geometry nobody under sixty could read, a taxonomic study of valley moths that ran to four volumes because apparently the author had nothing else to live for.

What I had never done was look behind the false shelf.

I found it by accident, which is how all true things are found. Tam's map — folded now into my bodice like a second heartbeat — had bled through onto my fingers again, faint gold smears that wouldn't wash off, and when I braced my hand against the archive's back wall to reach a high shelf, the stone hummed. Not the boundary stones' flat dead hum. Something older. Hungrier.

The wall swung inward six inches.

Behind it: a single iron box, unmarked except for a sigil pressed into the lid that made my teeth ache to look at. I knew that mark. I'd seen it on Tam's map, at the bottom, like a signature.

I should have called for Merric right then. That's what a good guild daughter would have done. But nineteen years of *don't ask about before the Shielding* and *the past is a locked room, Ada, leave it locked* had curdled into something stubborn, and stubbornness says: open the box.

Inside lay a contract.

Not paper. Skin — or something that had been skin once, cured thin as vellum and warm to the touch, though the archive was cold enough to see my breath. The writing moved when I wasn't looking directly at it. I read it anyway. You would have too.

*I, Merric Ashgrove, guildmaster of Hollowmere, do hereby convey unto the Undercourt of Mount Veyre one soul entire, together with all interest accrued thereon, in exchange for the perpetual null-shielding of the Valley of Hollowmere, term of said exchange to be ninety-nine years from the sealing hereof...*

My hands stopped shaking somewhere around the third clause. That frightened me more than the shaking had. Below the terms, in smaller script that writhed like worms in ash, came the part that turned my stomach over:

*And whereas the party of the first part cannot alone satisfy the Court's hunger, he further covenants to deliver, over the term, such souls as shall wander within his keeping, each to be entered upon the Ledger of Veyre...*

The ledger. Tam's map. Every name on it wasn't a treasure map. It was a menu.

And at the bottom, where a seal should be, a drop of blood that still gleamed wet after forty years, and beneath it, in Merric's own careful hand — the hand that had taught me my letters, that had signed my apprentice certificate, that had written *well done* on every report I'd ever filed — the words:

*Forgive me. It bought them time.*

Time. Whose time? There were no names. Just *them*, plural, a whole valley of *thems*, purchased with one soul and paid for with others, trickle-fed to the mountain whenever someone crossed the line and didn't come back, whenever a courier vanished, whenever—

Tam. Bleeding light instead of blood. A courier who had carried something out of the Undercourt and died on our doorstep for it.

I sat down on the floor among forty years of pipe smoke and didn't cry, mostly because I was too busy being furious, and fury is a very efficient preservative.

* * *

Here is what I know of that night, though I wasn't there. Here is what the contract let me see when I pressed my palm flat against it, because cursed documents are generous that way — they want witnesses.

Merric, forty years ago, thirty years old and already guildmaster because everyone older than him had died of the fever winter. Standing at the boundary stones at midnight with a candle and a copy of a rite he'd found in the deepest shelf of this same archive. The valley was dying around him. Magic leaked in through the stones like water through a cracked dam — crops twisting into shapes that screamed if you looked too long, children born with eyes like coin slots, the fever itself no fever at all but something *eating*. He had buried eleven people that winter. He was guildmaster. It was his job to fix things, and he had no tools except the ones the archive offered, and the deepest shelf offered this one.

He spoke the words. The mountain opened an eye.

What looked back at him wore his mother's face — she'd died two years prior, and the Undercourt keeps faces the way other courts keep portraits. It asked him, gently, what he wanted. He said: keep the magic out. Keep my people safe. Whatever it costs.

*Whatever it costs,* the face repeated, delighted, the way you'd repeat a child's charming mistake. *How brave. How very, very brave.*

He signed. The blood-drop sealed. And the moment it did, he felt it — the way you feel a tooth pulled, a hollow with air moving through it where something used to live. He walked home through the newly silent valley, through fields gone suddenly, perfectly ordinary, and he wept the whole way, and by morning he had decided never to tell anyone, because the alternative was telling them what the shield cost, and the shield was holding, and holding things is what guildmasters do.

That was the first lie. There were ninety-eight years of them after.

* * *

I brought the contract to the kitchen, because I wanted witnesses of my own, and because I am not brave enough to confront a man alone in a room with knives. Sela was scrubbing pots. Old Wren sat by the fire pretending to nap, which is how Wren listens best.

Merric came in at dusk smelling of cold stone and lamp oil, saw the contract lying open on the table between us, and stopped in the doorway like a man who has walked into a wall he built himself.

"You went into the archive," he said.

"Someone had to." My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "You've been saying *leave it locked* since I could walk. So I unlocked it."

Sela set down her pot. Wren opened one eye. Nobody left the room. Guild family doesn't work that way.

"It's not what you think," Merric began.

"It's exactly what I think." I turned the contract so he had to look at it. "You sold your soul. Fine. Terrible, but fine — yours is yours. But read the middle part, Merric. Read the part where you agreed to feed it *others*. Couriers. Wanderers. People who trusted the boundary stones meant safety." Tam's ghost-light flickered behind my eyes. "People who knocked on our door bleeding."

"That clause has never—" His voice cracked. "I never invoked it. Forty years, Ada. Do you know what I did instead? I paid the interest myself. Chunks of it. Years off my term. Every time the Court demanded a tithe, I gave it from my own soul rather than—"

"And Tam?" I stood up. The chair scraped. "Was Tam your own soul too? Because he died on our threshold carrying a ledger of names out of that mountain, and every name on it is someone the Undercourt holds, and the contract says the Court holds them *because of you*."

"They were already held," Merric said, and now his voice was low and terrible and old, old in a way I'd never heard it. "Before me. Before the Shielding. The Court harvests whoever crosses it, Ada — that's what the mountain *is*. I didn't create the debt. I only chose who it ate next. Me. For ninety-nine years, it ate me, and the valley lived."

"The valley lived," Wren said softly, from her chair, "and we never knew why we were safe. We thought it was the stones."

"The stones are just geometry," Merric said. "I am the payment plan."

Silence. The fire popped. Sela had both hands pressed over her mouth.

I looked at him — really looked — and underneath the fury I found the thing I'd been trying not to find: love. Nineteen years of it. The man who'd taught me to read null-script, who'd bandaged my hands after my first boundary-walk, who kept honey cakes hidden in the third drawer of his desk because I'd liked them at seven and never stopped liking them at nineteen. That man had sold himself to a monster to keep me alive before I was even born, and had then spent decades quietly feeding that monster extra installments of himself so it wouldn't come looking for dessert elsewhere.

Betrayal braided with love isn't a knot you can cut. You can only hold it, and feel it cut you.

"I'm going," I said.

"No." He crossed the room fast, took my shoulders — his hands were shaking now, finally, where mine had gone calm. "Ada. Listen to me. The new moon is nine days away. If that ledger reaches the Court, they'll call the full debt early. Everything. The shield, the souls, all of it, tonight, and the valley stands naked against the mountain with no guildmaster's soul left to spend. Stay. Let me go to them. Let me pay what's mine to pay."

"That's exactly what the contract wants," I said. "A guildmaster walking willingly into the dark with his debt. You taught me to read, Merric. Read your own signature. They don't want the ledger destroyed. They want *you*."

His grip tightened. For a moment he was thirty again, standing at the stones with a candle, saying *whatever it costs* to a thing wearing his mother's face.

"If you cross that line," he whispered, "you'll be the first Ashgrove in forty years to touch raw magic. You don't know what it does. I don't know what it does. And the Court will feel you coming from a hundred miles away, because you'll be carrying their property."

"Not mine," I said. "Tam's. And Tam asked me to carry it."

"He asked you to carry it to your death."

"He asked me to carry it *before the new moon*." I stepped back, out of his hands, and felt the map shift against my ribs like something breathing. "Nine days. You can lock the archive. You can lock me in my room. But you taught me everything I know about locks, and none of them were ever built to hold me."

Merric closed his eyes. When he opened them, he was guildmaster again, and guildmasters hold things.

"Then take the north road," he said quietly. "It's longer, but the sigil-bearers watch the south pass. And take this—" he pulled a knife from his belt, old, plain, the one he'd worn since before I was born, and pressed it into my hand. "Null-forged. It's the only steel in the valley that remembers how to bite something magical. And Ada—" his voice broke on my name, and he let it, "—if you meet the thing that holds my soul, tell it the interest stops today. Tell it I'm done paying."

Outside, dusk was falling on Hollowmere, and for the last time in my life it fell soft and safe and warded, every boundary stone humming its flat dead song.

By midnight I'd packed a bag, stolen the archive key back from Merric's desk, and stood at the north fence with the null-line glowing faintly ahead of me like the edge of the world.

Magic waited on the other side. So did everything else.

I stepped through.
