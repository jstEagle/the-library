## 4. Crossing the Null Line

I had been dead for three days before I worked out how to open a door, and I want you to appreciate what that means: I spent my first seventy-two hours of eternity drifting through walls like a fool because I kept forgetting I didn't need doors anymore.

Old habits. That's the thing nobody tells you about dying. The body goes, but the habits stay, squatting in whatever's left of you like squatters who've redecorated.

I was following Ada Ashgrove, which was not a hardship, all things considered. She moved through Hollowmere's last night like someone who'd already said her goodbyes and just hadn't told anyone yet. A pack on her back — too heavy, she'd packed it like a woman expecting to be gone a year instead of a woman running for her life. Her guildmaster's cloak over her shoulders, stolen fair and square from a peg by the door. And under her coat, wrapped in oilcloth against her ribs, my map. My ledger. My one contribution to the world now that I'd stopped contributing anything else.

She went to the boundary stones at moonrise, when the valley was at its bluest and quietest, and I have to admit my heart would have been hammering if I'd still had one. Instead I got that ghostly equivalent — a sort of pulling, a tugging at the seams of me, like the whole afterlife leaning in to watch.

The stones stood in their ancient ring at the valley's throat, seven of them, each carved with the null-sigil that had kept magic out of Hollowmere for longer than anyone living could remember. They hummed. Not with sound — with absence. You could feel the nothing coming off them, thick as fog. Inside that ring, no spell could live, no court could reach, no wonder could take root. The guild called it protection.

I called it a cage with very nice wallpaper.

Ada walked up to the nearest stone and put her palm flat against it, the way children do, the way I'd done once upon a life. Then she did something braver. She took out a knife and cut her palm, and pressed the bleeding hand to the sigil, and whispered something I couldn't catch — an apology, maybe, or a promise, or just *goodbye* — and stepped past the stone into the dark beyond the valley's edge.

And the magic came back into her.

Oh, you should have seen it. I've seen a lot of things since I died, most of them unpleasant, but this — this was worth lingering for. The null-line broke over her like cold water over a fevered head. Every thread of power the valley had been strangling out of her for nineteen years came flooding home at once. Light ran up her arms under the skin, silver-blue, wild, untrained, gorgeous. Her hair lifted though there was no wind. She gasped — actually staggered, caught herself on a boulder, and laughed, one sharp startled bark of laughter at the sky like she couldn't help it.

"That's what it feels like," she said, to nobody, to the night, to me. "That's what it *feels like.*"

Exhilaration. Pure, undiluted, nineteen-years-starved exhilaration. She stood there drinking it in, and I drifted close enough to see tears freezing on her cheeks, and I thought: *this is the moment. Right here. Before everything goes wrong. Let her have it.*

Then she took a step, tripped over a root because she was still half-blind with light, set fire to a bush by accident, panicked, put it out by accident, and swore with a fluency that would have made her guildmaster proud.

Right. So. Maybe not pure exhilaration. Maybe exhilaration with a generous helping of "dear gods, what have I done."

She looked back once at Hollowmere — the little scatter of lamplit windows below, the guildhall with its crooked chimney, the whole small warm world she was leaving behind — and then she turned north, toward Mount Veyre, and started walking.

I fell in beside her. Well. Beside-ish. Above, mostly. Ghosts don't walk so much as *drift with intent*, and I'd gotten reasonably good at intent.

"Tam?" she said quietly, without looking up. "You're doing the thing again. The hovering."

"I don't hover. I loiter. There's a difference, and it's dignity."

"You're dead. Dignity ship has sailed." But she smiled, small and tired, and kept walking, and I kept pace, and for a few hours the night was almost kind.

* * *

Here's what I hadn't told her. Here's the thing I'd been carrying around in my incorporeal chest like a stone:

I couldn't rest.

Not wouldn't. *Couldn't.* There's a difference, and it turns out it matters enormously. When Tam Voss died on the boundary stones of Hollowmere with a map pressed out of his reach and into a stranger's hands, he discovered that death is not a door so much as a waiting room, and the waiting room has exactly one exit, and the exit is labeled *ledger destroyed* in letters only the dead can read.

Every hour I stayed un-avenged — un-finished, un-done — I felt myself thinning. Not fading, exactly. Thinning. Like paper held too long near a candle. The Undercourt hadn't killed me outright; they'd let the ledger do it, let the debt I owed burn me down from the inside until there wasn't enough left to hold a body together. What remained was a scrap of me with one job left, and if I failed at the job, there wouldn't even be a scrap.

So yes, I followed Ada Ashgrove north with everything I had left, which admittedly wasn't much, and I told myself the guilt about dragging her along was a problem for a better ghost than me.

Around midnight she made camp in the lee of a fallen oak, and I watched her eat hard bread and dried apples and complain about both, and I thought, absurdly, of my mother. Dead forty years. Probably also a scrap somewhere. Probably also waiting.

"You're brooding," Ada said. "Ghosts can brood? Apparently ghosts can brood."

"I'm strategizing."

"You're staring at me like I owe you money."

"You do, a little. You have my map."

She pulled the oilcloth bundle out and unwrapped it across her knees, and there it was in the firelight: the ledger-map of the Undercourt, every line drawn in my own blood, every name written in the ink they make from promises. A hundred names. A hundred souls caged beneath Mount Veyre, feeding something hungry. Merric Ashgrove's name at the top of the list, oldest entry, deepest debt.

Ada traced the names with one finger, careful, like touching a sleeping animal. "A hundred people," she murmured. "He signed away a hundred people and called it saving the valley."

"He believed he was saving it. That's the trick of it. Nobody sells their soul thinking they're the villain. They sell it thinking they're the hero of a smaller story." I knew whereof I spoke. I'd served the Undercourt for six years before I stole their ledger, and I'd thought of myself as a pragmatist right up until the pragmatism started requiring blood.

"And you?" she asked, looking up at the dark where she somehow always knew I was. "What were you, when you took it?"

"Tired," I said. "Tired and clever, which is a worse combination than tired and stupid. Stupid people die quick. Clever ones die slow, with paperwork."

She huffed — almost a laugh — and wrapped the ledger back up, and tucked it against her heart, and lay down to sleep with her knife in hand the way guild-raised kids do.

I hovered. Loitered. Whatever.

And that's when I felt them.

* * *

It came as a wrongness in the air behind us, south and east, back toward the valley line. A taste like copper and old incense. I'd felt it before — six years of service will teach you the flavor of your masters' hounds — and every ghost-hair I didn't have stood up.

Sigil-bearers. Three of them, maybe four, moving fast through the dark with the court's marks burning on their skin. They'd found where Tam Voss died. They'd found the trail. And they were coming for their ledger the way creditors come for everything.

"Ada." My voice came out sharper than I meant it to. "Ada, wake up."

She was awake before I finished — knife out, rolling to her feet, eyes wide and already scanning the treeline. Guild training. Good. She'd need all of it.

"What?"

"We have company. Behind us. Moving fast." I tried to gauge the distance, which is difficult when you perceive distance primarily as a rumor. "An hour. Maybe less. They travel ugly."

"How many?"

"Three. Possibly four. One of them feels old." Old sigil-bearers were the worst kind. Young ones burned bright and sloppy. Old ones had learned patience, and patience was the court's favorite weapon.

Ada's face did something complicated — fear and resolve wrestling, resolve winning on points. She kicked dirt over the fire, shouldered her pack, and stood there a moment with her head cocked, listening to a night that suddenly had teeth in it.

"They followed the blood," she said. "Your blood. From the stones."

"They follow everything. Debts especially. It's practically a religion with them." I drifted ahead of her, north, willing her to move. "We need distance. Distance and cover, and neither of us is going to get much sleep tonight, which is fine for me and terrible for you."

She started walking. Fast, then faster, breaking into a jog along the old road that wound up toward the mountain's shoulders. The exhilaration of the boundary crossing was gone now, burned off like morning mist, and what was left underneath was colder and more honest. I watched it settle over her — the understanding that this was real, that the court did not send polite letters, that the hundred names in her keeping had a hundred hunters' worth of interest attached.

Halfway up the first ridge she stopped, bent double, breathing hard, and said, without looking at me: "Tam. If they catch us — if it comes to it — you take the ledger and run. Ghosts are fast, right? You drift or whatever. Take it and finish it, even if I—"

"No."

"Tam—"

"I didn't spend my death finding the one person stubborn enough to carry this thing just to have her throw herself away as a diversion in chapter four." I floated down to her eye level, which cost me more effort than I'll admit. "Besides, you can't even see half of what's hunting us. Alone, you're not a diversion. You're an appetizer."

Something between fury and gratitude crossed her face. "Then we'd better not get caught."

"There's the spirit."

We climbed. Behind us, far down the dark slope, I saw it — a flicker of red-gold light through the trees, moving with the smooth unhurried gait of something that knows exactly where its prey is going and is in no rush whatsoever. The mark of the sigil-bearers, burning on skin, lighting their way.

They weren't hurrying.

That frightened me more than anything else had, all night. Hunters who hurry are worried. Hunters who stroll know something you don't.

"Ada," I said softly, as the red glow winked closer behind us and the black mass of Mount Veyre rose against the stars ahead. "Walk faster."
