## 5. The Ferryman's Toll

I have been dead for eleven days now, and I want it on the record that being a ghost is mostly waiting. Waiting while Ada sleeps. Waiting while Ada argues with herself. Waiting while Ada does that thing where she chews her thumbnail and stares at a map like the map owes her money.

The map did owe her money, in a sense. It owed her everything. A ledger of souls, written in my blood, every name the Undercourt holds beneath Mount Veyre — including hers, eventually, if she failed, and mine, permanently, if she quit.

She was not going to quit. I'd known her four days and I already knew that about her the way you know a storm is coming: not because anyone tells you, but because the air changes.

Greywash announced itself before we saw it, the way river towns do — by smell. Tar and fish and woodsmoke, and under all of it the particular green rot of a place that has made its peace with damp. The town clung to the south bank of the Veyre like something that had tried to climb out and given up halfway. Houses leaned on each other for support. Nets hung between them like laundry, or like bunting for a funeral nobody had scheduled yet.

"Charming," Ada said.

"It's honest," I said. "That's more than most towns manage."

She couldn't hear me, of course. Not properly. She heard me the way you hear a song through a wall — the shape of it, the mood, occasionally a whole line arriving unbidden. Mostly she'd taken to talking back anyway, which the locals were going to find alarming, but which I found flattering.

"The ferrywoman," she said, consulting Tam's-map-her-map-now, "runs at dawn."

"It's dusk," I said, uselessly.

"I know," she said, to no one visible. "We wait."

We waited. Ghosts are good at it.

* * *

The ferry house sat at the end of a pier that had been repaired so many times it was less a structure than an argument between generations of carpenters. Inside, a woman sat mending a net with hands that moved like they'd done nothing else for sixty years. Grey hair cut short as a soldier's. Eyes like river water — brown, cold, and moving fast enough over the surface that you never saw what was underneath.

"Maren Ostrey," Ada read from the map's margin, where I'd written notes in a former life. *Ferrywoman. Takes payment in memory. Fair, but do not flinch.*

"Do not flinch," Ada murmured.

"You're going to flinch," I said.

"I'm going to *not* flinch."

Maren looked up. Looked at Ada. Then looked — this was the part that always unsettled the living, when they noticed it — slightly to Ada's left, at approximately me.

"You travel heavy," Maren said.

Ada went still. "You can see him?"

"I can see what rides with people. Been doing this crossing thirty years; you learn the difference between luggage and grief." She set down her net. "Sit. Dawn's two hours off, and we'll need every minute of them, because you're going to tell me why a null-born guild girl is carrying a court-ledger into Veyre, and then you're going to pay me, and then — if I like your answers — I'm going to row you across a river that doesn't like either of us."

* * *

Here is what I remembered about bargaining with ferrymen, from when I was alive and served the other side: they never take coin. Coin is common. Coin is repeatable. What they take is singular, and once it's gone, it's gone in a way that coin never is.

Maren listened to Ada's story with her eyes half-closed, like a cat deciding whether the thing in front of it was food or weather. When Ada finished — Merric, the contract, the hundred souls, the new moon deadline — Maren nodded once.

"The price is a memory," she said. "Not any memory. The river takes what the crossing requires. For a girl carrying stolen death into the Undercourt's own hall—" she paused, tasting it, "—it'll want something that keeps you human. Something warm. That's the toll. The river won't carry passengers who've got nothing left to lose; they go strange, and strange passengers tip the boat."

"And if I refuse?" Ada asked.

"Then you swim, and the Veyre keeps what it catches." Maren said it without cruelty, the way you'd report the tide times. "You'll get one chance to choose. When I ask, the memories will rise up like fish to a lamp, and you'll pick one, and I'll take it, and you won't miss it. That's the mercy of it. You won't even know what you paid."

"That's not mercy," Ada said quietly. "That's theft with good manners."

"Yes," Maren agreed. "Most survival is."

And there it was — the question I'd been circling since the boundary stones, since I pressed a blood-soaked map into a stranger's hands and made her swear. *What does it cost?* Not the dramatic cost, the dying-for-a-cause cost. The small ones. The installments. Every mile toward Veyre took a piece out of her, and I was the one holding the map, and I could not decide whether that made me her guide or just another collector.

"Ask me," Ada said.

Maren reached across the table and took both her hands. Her thumbs found the pulse points, and her eyes went somewhere else entirely.

* * *

I watched the memories come up in Ada like fish to a lamp. I know because I could see them — ghosts see things, it's nearly all we do. They rose around her head in the dark of the ferry house, faint and silver: a boy falling out of an apple tree and laughing on the way down. The taste of honey bread. Merric's voice saying *you did well, Ashgrove*, the only time he ever had. Rain on the guildhouse roof. A hundred small warmths, each one a brick in the wall of who she was.

And among them, brighter than the rest, a woman's face. Dark hair going grey at the temples. Laugh lines. Hands floury to the wrist. Singing badly and knowing it.

Ada's mother. Dead nine years. The one memory she guarded the way the valley guarded itself — absolutely, and at any cost.

"Don't," I said. "Ada. Don't pick that one. Pick anything else. Pick the apple tree, pick the rain, pick—"

Her hand turned palm-up in Maren's grip, and she chose.

It didn't hurt. That was the horror of it. There was no tearing, no scream. Maren simply breathed in, slow, like drawing thread through cloth, and the woman's face dimmed and guttered and went out, and Ada blinked twice and said:

"When do we leave?"

* * *

Dawn came up grey and gold over the Veyre, and the river lived up to its reputation. It ran against the wind. It ran, in places, against the current. Mist stood on it in shapes that were almost figures and were almost not, and none of them crossed the boat's wake.

Ada sat in the stern with her arms around her knees. I sat — perched, hovered, whatever you call what I do — beside her.

"How do you feel?" I asked, though I knew she'd catch maybe half of it.

"Lighter," she said, and frowned, because she could feel the wrongness of the word without finding it. "Like a room after furniture's been moved. I keep reaching for something and it isn't there, and I don't know what the something is." She looked at me — at the space near me — sharply. "Did I pay? What did I pay?"

"You don't want to know."

"That's exactly why I want to know."

I told her. All of it. The face, the singing, the flour on the hands. Because she deserved the truth even if the truth was cruel, and because some stubborn, ghostly part of me believed that a debt unpaid-in-memory is still a debt, and someone ought to keep her books honestly since the Undercourt kept theirs so well.

She didn't cry. She went very still, the way she'd gone still reading the contract in the archive, and then she said, "Good."

"*Good?*"

"Now I know what they take. Now I know the shape of the price." Her jaw set. "Let the Undercourt send whatever it likes. I've already started paying. I intend to make it count."

I have heard battle oaths from warriors and vows from priests, and none of them landed like that flat, quiet sentence from a nineteen-year-old girl in a leaking ferry.

Maren rowed and pretended not to listen, which meant she heard every word.

* * *

We made the far bank by mid-morning, where the road began its long climb toward the mountain's shadow. Maren held the boat against the current while Ada stepped onto mud and stone, and for a moment the old woman's eyes flicked to me again.

"She'll do," Maren said. To me, plainly, unmistakably. "Tell your ghost I said so."

Ada spun. "You *can* hear him."

"I can hear what the river carries." Maren was already pushing off. "One more thing, guild-girl, free of charge. The men who burned through Greywash yesterday asking after you — sigil-bearers, three of them, wearing marks like brands — they went to the records hall first. Names, ledgers, manifests. Everything written down about everyone who ever passed through." She poled away from the bank, and her voice carried flat across the water. "They're not hunting you, child. They're erasing the roads behind you. By tonight there'll be no record anywhere that Greywash exists, and no witness in it who remembers your face — except me, and I've already sold the useful parts."

Smoke was rising from the town behind us. Thin, at first. Then not thin.

Ada watched it. I watched her watch it, and thought about how the Undercourt worked — how it never simply killed, it *unmade*. Souls, names, towns, mothers' faces. Everything reduced to candlelight and ash and a gap in the world shaped like a person.

"They burned it because of us," she said.

"They burned it because of what you're carrying," I corrected. "There's a difference. Small one. But it matters."

"Does it?"

"No," I admitted. "But it's all I've got."

She shouldered her pack, and turned her back on the smoke, and faced the mountain — Mount Veyre, black against the morning, wearing its crown of cloud like a judge wearing a hood.

Behind us, Greywash burned its way out of history. Ahead of us, the road climbed into country where the wards began, and where the sigil on the arm of every hunter sent for us would burn bright as a beacon.

And on the back of Ada's right hand, where she couldn't see it yet, the first faint line of a mark was rising — not hers, not yet, but patient, the way ink is patient, the way debts are patient.

The mountain knew we were coming.

It had sent the bill ahead.
