## 6. Sigil and Shadow

### Ada

The mountain road was a liar.

It looked like a road — a pale scar of switchbacks stitched up Veyre's southern flank, wide enough for carts once, back when anyone still had business with the mountain. Now it was just gravel and old frost-cracks and the occasional shrine to a god whose name the weather had eaten off the stone. Tam walked beside me, or rather floated beside me with his boots an inch above the gravel out of what I could only assume was spite, and he kept saying things like *the pass narrows ahead* and *they'll expect us at the narrows* in the tone of a man describing breakfast.

"You could lead with that," I said. "The *they'll expect us* part."

"I did lead with it. You were humming."

"I was not humming."

"You were. Something mournful. Very on-brand."

I adjusted the strap of my pack and didn't answer, because the truth was I *had* been humming, some half-remembered guild song Merric used to sing when he thought no one was listening, and thinking about Merric made my chest do a thing I didn't have time for. Three days past the null line. Three days of magic soaking into everything like rain into dry cloth — the grass glowing faintly green at dusk, birds that sang in harmonies no bird should know, and under it all, threaded through every living thing, the slow cold pulse of the Undercourt's attention. We were being watched. I could feel it the way you feel a draft from a door left open somewhere in a big house.

Tam felt it too. He'd gone quiet about an hour ago, which for Tam was its own kind of alarm bell.

"The narrows," he said again, and this time there was nothing wry in it. "Ada. The narrows."

I looked up. The road pinched between two walls of black stone, close enough that I could have touched both sides with my elbows out. A perfect place for an ambush, if you were the sort of person who thought in ambushes. Which, apparently, everyone but me was.

"Could we go around?" I asked, already knowing.

"Three days around. New moon is in five."

"Of course it is."

I went into the narrows anyway, because I am either brave or stupid and the difference has never mattered much in practice. The light changed first — went thin and gray, then wrong, shadows leaning against the direction of the sun. Then the air itself changed, thickening like the moment before a storm, and Tam said, very softly, "*Down*—"

The first sigil-bearer came off the wall like something poured rather than thrown. Gray cloak, gray face, and across his cheek a mark that moved — a curling line of silver script that wrote and unwrote itself, alive, hungry. He hit the road where I'd been standing half a heartbeat earlier and the gravel fused to glass.

I rolled, came up with the courier's knife — Tam's knife, technically, though ownership seemed beside the point now — and did the thing Merric had drilled into every guild kid since before I could walk: *don't fight the magic, fight the man carrying it.* Null-magic training. The one inheritance Hollowmere ever gave me that worked outside its borders.

The second bearer dropped from above. I heard Tam shout a warning in a voice that cracked the air, and then the world went white and loud, and after that it was mostly instinct and terror and the specific, horrible intimacy of close fighting — his breath, my breath, the knife finding the gap beneath his arm because he'd overreached, because they always overreach when they think they've already won.

He looked surprised. That's what I remember most. Not pain — surprise. Like he'd genuinely never considered that a nineteen-year-old girl with a stolen knife might be the end of him.

Then he fell, and the moving mark on his face began to crawl.

"Ada." Tam's voice, urgent, from somewhere above me. "Ada, get away from him."

But the mark was already leaving him — sliding off his skin like water off glass, streaming through the air toward me, toward the open wound on my forearm I hadn't even noticed getting. It touched the cut and *wrote itself in*, silver script sinking beneath my skin, and the pain was —

Well. Imagine your arm remembering every fire it never survived.

I screamed. I'm not going to pretend I did anything dignified. I sat down hard in the gravel and held my arm and screamed while the sigil curled along my veins like ivy finding a wall, and Tam knelt beside me — actually knelt, ghost knees on ghost ground, which I hadn't known he could do — and put his hand near my shoulder without touching it.

"Breathe," he said. "It's done. Breathe."

"It's *in* me—"

"Yes."

"*What is it?*"

"A key," Tam said quietly. "And a leash. They're usually the same thing down here."

* * *

### Tam

She took the mark the way she took everything — loudly, furiously, and then immediately asking practical questions, which is how I knew she'd survive this. Grief comes later with people like Ada. First the work.

We made camp in the lee of a fallen waystone, and she sat with her sleeve pushed up, watching the sigil move under her skin. It had settled along her forearm now, a line of silver script coiling from wrist to elbow, writing and unwriting itself in a language I knew far better than I'd ever wanted to.

"It's slowing down," she said.

"It's settling. Learning you." I kept my voice level. "By morning it'll be quiet. And any ward the court set between here and Veyre will read you as one of theirs."

"That's useful."

"It's also a brand, Ada. Every hour it's in you, it writes a little deeper. Weeks, you'd be fine. Months—" I stopped.

"Months, what?"

"Months, you start hearing them. The court. Their thoughts, their hungers. By the end you don't remember wanting anything they didn't want first." I looked at the fire instead of her face. "I've seen it happen."

The silence stretched. When I finally made myself look up, she wasn't looking at her arm anymore. She was looking at me.

"You said 'I've seen it,'" she repeated. "Not 'I've read about it.' Not 'the stories say.'"

"Ada—"

"You've seen it." Her voice had gone very careful, the way it went when she was angry and deciding whether to spend it. "You said you were a courier. You said the court killed you for carrying the ledger."

"They did kill me for carrying the ledger." The firelight went through me, as it went through everything, and I hated, suddenly, that she couldn't see my hands shake. "That part was true. But couriers don't get assigned ledgers, Ada. Ledgers get assigned *couriers*. I served the Undercourt for eleven years. I carried their writs, their summons, their debts. I delivered souls to the candle-halls and told myself it was just a job, the way everyone down there tells themselves it's just a job." I made myself keep going. "The sigil-bearers who are hunting us? I trained two of them. The wards you're going to walk through? I helped design three of them. And the reason I knew exactly what that mark would do to you is that I wore one for eleven years, and the day I finally understood what it was turning me into was the day I stole the ledger and ran."

She didn't say anything. The wind moved through the waystone's broken crown and made a sound almost like breathing.

"Say something," I said. "Throw something. Ghosts can be hit, it turns out, if you throw it hard enough and mean it."

"Why tell me now?"

"Because you just killed a man today, and you're going to carry that, and I would rather you carry it knowing you're not the only one on this road with blood on the inside where nobody can see it." I paused. "Also because you deserve to know what's writing itself into your arm. I wouldn't trust me either, right now. But you should have the information."

Ada looked at me for a long moment. Nineteen years old, sitting in the ruins of a dead god's waystation, marked by the enemy, hunted by my former colleagues, and she was weighing my soul like a merchant weighing coin.

"You could have let me find out in Veyre," she said finally. "When it was too late to matter."

"Yes."

"You didn't."

"No."

"Why?"

"Because—" and here it was, the thing I'd been circling since the river town, since the boundary stones, since the moment a dying man pressed a map into a stranger's hands because she was the first person in twenty years who looked like she might actually *do* something. "Because guilt shared is guilt halved. That's not a proverb. That's just arithmetic I learned too late, and I'm trying to learn it properly this time, before there's no one left to share it with."

* * *

### Ada

I threw a rock at him. Not hard, and not meaning it — well, meaning maybe forty percent of it — and it passed through his shoulder and clattered into the dark, and he laughed, and something in my chest unclenched for the first time since the narrows.

"Guilt shared is guilt halved," I said. "That's terrible arithmetic. Guilt shared is guilt doubled, usually. That's why people keep it."

"And yet."

"And yet." I pulled my sleeve back down over the sigil. Under the fabric it pulsed faintly, warm as a sleeping animal. "So. Eleven years. What changed?"

"What?"

"What made you run? People don't steal from the Undercourt over a bad Tuesday. Something happened."

Tam was quiet long enough that I thought he wouldn't answer. The fire burned low. Somewhere above us, an owl asked its one question and got no reply.

"There was a woman," he said at last. "In Greywash, actually — small world. She ferried people across the Wash, back before the tolls got ugly. She had a son the court claimed a debt on. Sixteen years old, owed for his father's gambling. Standard contract, standard terms." His voice had gone flat, recitation-flat, the voice of someone who has rehearsed a thing until the feeling drains out of it. "I delivered the summons. I watched them take him. And I remember standing on the ferry dock afterward thinking: I know the ledger. I know every name in it. I could burn the whole book, and I've known it for years, and I've delivered a hundred boys like him, and what exactly is the difference between me and the ones holding the candles?"

"The difference is you stopped."

"After eleven years. After a hundred boys." He shook his head. "Don't canonize me, Ada. I'm not the hero of this story. I'm the coward who finally did the math."

"You're the ghost who's walking back into the court that owns him to fix it," I said. "That's not cowardice. That's just late."

He looked at me then — really looked, the way the living forget to do — and whatever he saw in my face, it must have been enough, because he nodded once, slow, like a door closing gently.

"Get some sleep," he said. "Tomorrow we climb. And tomorrow night we reach the first of the outer wards, and you'll need to walk through it wearing that mark like you belong to them, and it will feel like drowning, and you cannot flinch. Flinching reads as fear. Fear reads as prey."

"Wonderful. Anything else?"

"Yes." He hesitated, and for a moment he looked less like a ghost and more like a man. "Thank you. For not making me say all of it twice."

I lay back against the waystone and closed my eyes, and the sigil pulsed warm against my arm, patient as a seed. And in the last drift before sleep, I heard Tam murmur something to himself — not to me, not quite to the fire. To the mountain, maybe. Or to whoever was listening on the other side of it.

"We're coming," he said. "All hundred and one of us."

I didn't ask him what he meant. I was too afraid I already knew.
