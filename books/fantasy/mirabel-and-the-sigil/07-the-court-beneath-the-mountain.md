## 7. The Court Beneath the Mountain

I had died once already, so you'd think the under-halls of Veyre would hold no fresh terrors for me. You'd be wrong. Dying is quick. The under-halls are patient.

We came down through a crack in the mountain's hip — Ada's word for it, not mine; she has a way of describing geography like it's an animal she's mildly annoyed with. The passage was old before the guild was old, older than the null-line, older than the lie that magic and stone don't mix. Our borrowed sigil-light walked ahead of us on the walls, waking up carvings that I remembered from the other side of my life: processions of figures, all of them faceless, all of them carrying candles.

"They're not carrying candles," I said quietly.

Ada looked at the wall, then at me, and I watched her get there the slow, honest way she gets everywhere. "They *are* the candles."

"That's the general idea, yes."

She didn't say anything else for a long while. That's one of the things about her that frightens people who don't know her — she doesn't fill silence with comfort. She lets it sit there until it tells her something true.

The stairs went down in spirals, each landing colder than the last, until cold stopped being temperature and became a kind of attention. Something down here knew we'd come. Something down here had been expecting us since before Tam Voss stole a ledger and ran, which is to say: since always. Courts like this don't chase. They wait. Chasing implies you might escape.

At the bottom of the seventh spiral, the walls opened out and we stood at the edge of the Hall of Kept Light.

I wish I could tell you it was beautiful. It was beautiful. That's the trap of it.

Ten thousand candles, maybe more, burning in tiers that rose into a dark too high to see. But they weren't wax. Each flame sat in a little cage of black glass, and each flame moved — leaned, flickered, turned — the way a face turns toward a familiar voice. And when our footsteps echoed, a hundred small flames flinched at once, like birds startled off a wire.

Ada made a sound I've only heard once before, from my own throat, a lifetime ago. Not a gasp. A recognition.

"Tam," she said. "Which one is yours?"

"I don't know," I said, which was true, and also the most useless sentence I have ever spoken in two lives.

* * *

Here is what I never told her on the road, because the road was for other confessions and there are only so many a person can carry uphill: I worked in this hall for eleven years. I was a lamplighter. That was the polite title. What I actually did was trim the cages, feed the flames their ration of memory — grief burns longest, joy burns brightest, and love, if you can get it fresh, will keep a soul lit for a century — and carry the ones that guttered down to the Refectory, where the court dined.

I told myself I was a servant, not a thief of anything. Servants don't choose what's served.

The night I ran, I took the ledger because I'd finally read it cover to cover, the way you read a menu when you're hungry enough to be honest. A hundred names from Hollowmere and the valleys around it. Souls sold by desperate people to desperate buyers, and one great lump sum at the top of the page, signed in a guildmaster's careful hand: *Merric Ashgrove, in consideration of the valley's keeping.*

He'd signed for a hundred strangers' worth of protection and thought he was only signing for himself. Or he hadn't thought at all. With Merric, those were usually the same thing.

Ada found his flame before I could stop her.

It was on the fourth tier, in a cage newer than the others, burning a steady gold that none of the older souls managed anymore. She climbed the tier-iron with her bare hands — the metal bites, I wanted to tell her, it remembers being a serpent — and pressed her palm flat against the glass, and the flame inside leaned into her hand like a cat.

"Oh," she said. Very softly. The softest I ever heard her. "Oh, you're still *you*."

And the flame brightened. That's the cruelty of the place. The dead can't speak, but they can answer.

"He was called early," I said. "The debt came due the moment you crossed the null-line with the ledger. The contract reads it as delivery." I paused. "I'm sorry. I did the arithmetic wrong. I thought we had until the new moon."

"You thought," she said, without turning around, "that the Undercourt plays fair."

"I thought they played *slowly*. There's a difference. It matters less than I hoped."

She stayed up there a long time, palm to glass, while Merric's flame burned toward her the way a fire burns toward a draft. Nineteen years old, and she was saying goodbye to the man who raised her through four inches of stolen glass, and she did it without a single wasted motion, the way she does everything. When she finally climbed down, her hands were bleeding from the tier-iron and she didn't mention it, and I didn't either, because mentioning it would have been a kindness and neither of us could afford one just then.

* * *

The court came to us in the Refectory antechamber, because courts like this never come to you where you're strong.

It wore a shape it thought we'd find reasonable: a tall figure in grey, face smooth as an unlit candle, voice like warm air over coals. It introduced itself as the Seneschal of the Deep Court, Steward of Kept Flames, and it offered us chairs, which was the most frightening thing it did. Furniture means negotiation. Negotiation means it thinks it can win.

"The ledger," it said, holding out a hand like folded paper. "And the courier. In exchange, the Ashgrove flame walks free. Full pardon. The valley's shield continues uninterrupted. Everyone lives exactly as they have lived. Isn't that what you came for?"

And here is the thing I need you to understand about Ada Ashgrove, the thing I understood in that moment better than I understood anything in either of my lifetimes: she *wanted* it. You could see the wanting move through her like weather. Merric, whole. Hollowmere safe. The whole terrible arithmetic solved with one signature — hers, this time, but the same hand, the same careful letters, the same bargain Merric had struck forty years ago thinking he was only spending himself.

She looked at the Seneschal. She looked at me.

And I did the bravest cowardly thing available to me. I said, "Take the deal."

Her head snapped around. "Tam—"

"Take it." My voice came out steadier than I felt, which is the ghost's one advantage over the living: no pulse to betray you. "You didn't come down here for me. You came for him. I'm already dead, Ada. Dead twice over, if we're counting, and I am, professionally. One more doesn't change the total."

"It changes *my* total," she said.

"There isn't a version of this," I said, "where you trade me and sleep well again. I know you. I've haunted you for three hundred miles. You'd spend the rest of your life doing sums in the dark, and the sums would always come out wrong, and you'd hate yourself for a debt you didn't even owe. Don't make me the price of your peace. I didn't steal that ledger so you could sign a new one."

The Seneschal watched us with its candle-smooth face, and I swear the room got warmer. It liked this. Dissension seasons the meat.

"Touching," it said. "But the offer stands only while the ledger stands unburned. Decide quickly. Candles are patient. Courts are not."

Ada closed her eyes. I watched her do the thing she does — let the silence sit until it tells her something true. And whatever it told her, it took a long time, long enough that the Seneschal's fingers began to drum, long enough that I started composing the apology I'd say to her flame someday, in whatever hall they kept mine.

Then she opened her eyes, and they were the flattest, kindest grey I have ever seen, and she said:

"No."

The word dropped into the antechamber like a stone into a well, and every flame in the hall above us — ten thousand of them, maybe more — flickered at once.

"No?" said the Seneschal.

"No," Ada said. "Because I asked the candles a question while you were monologuing, and they answered. All hundred of the valley's names are up there. All hundred are still burning. Do you know what that means? It means the shield hasn't been fed in forty years. It means Merric's soul has been sitting in a cage this whole time, *already paid*, already spent, and the valley kept its protection anyway — because the protection was never powered by them. It was powered by the *contract*. By paper. By fear." She stepped forward, bleeding hands and all, and the sigil on her arm glowed through her sleeve like a coal under cloth. "You don't want the ledger back because it's dangerous. You want it back because it proves you've been lying to everyone — including yourselves — since before my guildmaster was born."

For the first time in eleven years of service and one lifetime of haunting, I saw something flicker across the Seneschal's face. Not anger. Something worse. *Recalculation.*

"Cute," it said. "The thief lectures the court. And yet the terms remain: return what is ours, or the Ashgrove flame gutters with the rest when we call the debt properly. We have ways of making a hundred years pass in an afternoon, girl."

"Then we'd best be quick," Ada said, and pulled the ledger from her coat, and held it over the nearest candle-cage — Merric's cage, his gold light trembling against the vellum — and looked at me across the room with a question in her eyes that wasn't really a question at all.

It was a countdown.

"Ada," I said carefully, "what exactly are you—"

"*Run,*" she said, and struck the first spark.
