## 8. The Bargain Refused

The Undercourt's throne room was a cathedral built by something that had heard about cathedrals secondhand.

That was my first thought, standing on the obsidian floor with Tam's ghost cold at my shoulder and the ledger burning a hole through the satchel against my ribs. The hall was vast, yes. The pillars soared, yes. But every proportion was wrong in a way that made my teeth ache — arches that curved too far, shadows that pooled at angles light couldn't explain, a ceiling that seemed to breathe when I wasn't looking directly at it. Whoever had designed this place had studied beauty the way a poacher studies a deer. From the outside. With intent.

And at the far end, where an altar should have been, stood the candles.

A hundred of them. No — I counted, because counting was easier than feeling — a hundred and six, each one flickering inside a cage of black iron no bigger than a birdcage, each flame burning a different color. Gold. Green. A blue so pale it was almost white. They hung from chains in tiered rows like a chandelier designed by a grief-stricken god, and the light they cast didn't illuminate so much as *remember* illumination, a glow that felt like looking at photographs of a summer that ended badly.

"That's them," Tam said quietly. "That's everyone."

I found Merric's flame on the fourth row. I don't know how I knew which one it was. Maybe the ledger told me — it had grown warm against my side since we'd entered the under-halls, warm the way a dog goes warm when its master comes home. The flame was amber, steady, patient. It looked like him. It looked like every evening of my childhood, Merric mending boundary stones with his sleeves rolled up, humming off-key, telling me the valley was safe because he'd made sure of it.

Safe. Right.

"Ada." The voice came from everywhere and nowhere, the way voices do in dreams, except this was no dream and I was painfully, catastrophically awake. "You've brought me such a gift."

She descended from the breathing ceiling the way smoke descends when a fire dies — unhurried, inevitable, gathering herself into a shape as she came. By the time her feet touched the obsidian she looked almost like a woman: tall, gray-robed, beautiful in the way that a frozen lake is beautiful right before it takes a child through the ice. Her crown was made of sigils, living ones, crawling over each other like insects trying to escape a jar.

"I am Veyre," she said, though I hadn't asked. "And you are the courier's thief, and the guildmaster's girl, and you are holding sixty years of my property in a satchel."

"It's not your property."

"Everything beneath this mountain is mine." She smiled. It did not reach anywhere near her eyes, which were the color of candle-smoke. "But let us not quarrel over definitions. You want something. I want something. Let us do what courts do best."

She gestured, and one of the cages drifted down from the tiers, descending through the air slow as a soap bubble. It stopped at the level of my heart. Inside, the amber flame leaned toward me, the way a cat leans toward a familiar hand.

Merric.

My throat closed. Sixty years of evasions, of half-answers, of *don't ask about that, Ada* — and here he was, small enough to hold, bright enough to break.

"The trade is simple," Veyre said. "The ledger, and the ghost who stole it. In exchange, the guildmaster walks free. Whole. Restored to his flesh, his debt dissolved, his valley protected for another lifetime." She tilted her head. "You love him. I can taste it from here. Love makes such excellent mortar for bargains."

"And the other hundred and five?"

"Remain exactly where they are."

Something in my chest went very quiet and very cold, the way Hollowmere went quiet before a hard frost killed everything green.

"Explain that," I said. "Slowly. I want to hear you say it."

Veyre's smile widened by a single tooth. "You've seen the shield, girl. The null-magic that keeps my court out of your little valley. Did you imagine null-magic grew on trees? Nothing is free. Not protection. Not peace. Not *nothing*." She spread her hands toward the hanging cages, a priestess blessing her congregation. "Every soul in this room burns to feed it. Every year, a little more of them renders down. Your guildmaster bought you all a prison of safety, and these hundred and five are the fuel it runs on. They have been burning since before you were born. Some of them are nearly wicks now."

Tam made a sound beside me. Not a word. Just a sound, small and wounded, the sound of a man seeing his own grave for the second time.

"If I take your trade," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect, "the shield stays lit."

"For another sixty years. Perhaps seventy. And then you will need new souls, and we will negotiate again, and your guild will go on selling what it does not own." She stepped closer. This close, I could see that her robe wasn't fabric at all but layered pages, thousands of them, contracts written fine as hair. "Or you give me back what is mine, and everyone in this room stays exactly as they are, and your valley sleeps safely another night. Everyone lives, Ada Ashgrove. Isn't that what heroes want? Everyone lives?"

Everyone lives. Except the hundred and five. Except Tam, handed back into whatever servitude he'd escaped. Except Merric, freed only so the machine could keep eating.

I thought of the boundary stones. I thought of how, as a girl, I'd pressed my palm against one and felt nothing at all — no magic, no wonder, just cool granite and my own pulse. I'd spent nineteen years inside a wall built out of people, calling it home.

"No," I said.

Veyre's smile stopped moving.

"I'm sorry — I must have misheard—"

"You didn't." I pulled the ledger from the satchel and held it up between us, blood-marked map, soul-ledger, sixty years of arithmetic done in other people's lives. "You heard me perfectly. I'm not trading a hundred and five people for one. I'm not trading *anyone*. That's the whole point of the thing you'll never understand, being made of contracts. People aren't currency. They're not fuel. They're not *collateral*." My hand was shaking. I let it shake. "So no. Refused. Declined. Take it however your court records things."

For a long moment the throne room was silent except for the breathing ceiling and the small, terrible crackle of a hundred and six souls doing their work.

Then Veyre laughed, and the laugh was worse than anything else she'd done, because it sounded genuinely delighted.

"Oh," she breathed. "*Oh.* Do you know how long it's been since someone refused me in my own hall?" Her form rippled, the woman-shape sliding like oil. "You understand what happens now. The moment you declared refusal, you became a thief in the eyes of the court. A thief of souls. And we have *procedures* for thieves."

She raised one hand, and the nearest pillar uncoiled a length of shadow that moved like a striking snake.

Tam moved faster.

I never saw him cross the space between us — ghosts don't walk so much as *arrive* — but suddenly he was between me and the shadow, arms spread, and the darkness broke against him like a wave against a piling. He turned his head, and even translucent, even worn thin by decades of running, his grin was the same grin of the dying man who'd pressed a map into my hands and made me swear.

"Ledger's hers now," he said. "Which means the debt of carrying it is hers. But the debt of *stealing* it—" his voice cracked, and he steadied it, "—that one's mine. Always was. Court! Hey. *Hey.* Look here. One stolen soul, returned voluntarily. That counts for something in your books, doesn't it? Remission. Reduction. Call it what you like — but you can't touch her while I'm settling accounts."

"Tam, no—"

"Ada." He looked at me, and his eyes were doing something ghosts' eyes shouldn't be able to do, which is cry. "I spent thirty years serving them. Thirty years carrying their marks, collecting their debts, feeding people into cages like those. Stealing the ledger was the first decent thing I ever did, and I botched it, and a hundred and five people kept burning because I botched it." He turned back to Veyre. "Let me finish it. Open the cages myself. Every soul I helped put in one — I'll spend whatever rest I had left buying them out. That's the trade. *My* rest for *their* freedom. Put it in your books."

Veyre regarded him for a long, long moment. Somewhere above us, the ceiling exhaled.

"A soul's chance at peace," she mused, "is worth perhaps... three cages. Four, if the regret is fresh."

"Then I'm overpaying," Tam said, "and I always was a bad negotiator. Ada — the ledger. Burn page one. Burn them all. Don't stop for me, don't wait for me, don't you dare *grieve* for me until you're above ground. Go."

The shadow struck. He caught it. Held it. And in the space he bought me — three seconds, maybe four, an eternity priced at exactly one ghost's salvation — I ran for the cages.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about breaking soul-cages: the locks are made of law, not iron. You can't pick them. You can't smash them. But a ledger, it turns out, is the master key to its own inventory — and mine was already burning in my hands, and where the flames touched the iron, the iron *remembered it was only iron*.

The first cage fell open like a birdcage in a storm. The gold flame inside rose, stretched, and became — for one impossible heartbeat — a face. A young man. Someone's son. He looked at me with eyes full of sixty years of dark, and mouthed something I couldn't hear, and then streamed upward through the breathing ceiling and was gone.

The second cage. The third. Behind me, Tam was laughing and straining and holding, a ghost wrestling a mountain, buying me seconds at rates no soul should pay. The court was screaming now — real screaming, alarms made of law, wards flaring red along every wall. Veyre's voice cut through it all, no longer amused:

"*Seize the thief. Seize them both. Let the valley's shield gutter — let them watch Hollowmere go dark —*"

My hands were scorched. The sigil on my arm — the dead bearer's mark, the brand I'd taken on the mountain road — was burning hot enough to blister, as if the court were writing new terms on my skin. Thief of souls, it said, in letters I couldn't read but could feel. Thief of souls, forever.

Cage after cage after cage. Faces rising, streaming free, gone. Forty. Fifty. Seventy. The chandelier-light dimmed row by row, and somewhere far above us, I knew, the null-magic over Hollowmere was flickering like a lamp running dry — and I kept opening cages anyway, because there was no version of this where I got to be innocent.

The last cage but two. The last cage but one.

Merric's.

The amber flame leaned toward my scorched hands, and I swear on every stone in Hollowmere it hummed — the off-key hum, the mending-stones hum, *safe, Ada, I made sure of it* — and I stood there with my fingers on the lock and understood that I could open it. Right now. Free him first. Carry him out ahead of the others, get him above ground while the wards were still collapsing—

And behind me, Tam shouted — not words, just my name, just *Ada*, urgent as a hand on the collar—

Because the ceiling had stopped breathing.

It was coming down.

* * *

I opened Merric's cage last.

Not because I hesitated — okay, I hesitated, one heartbeat, maybe two, and I'll carry that heartbeat to my grave — but because freeing him meant the shield died *now*, fully, finally, and everything the hundred and five had burned for would end in the same instant I chose it. There was no version where I saved him and saved the valley. There never had been. Merric had known that for sixty years, and he'd let me grow up inside the lie because the truth was a bill he couldn't pay.

I paid it.

The lock remembered it was only iron. The cage fell open. The amber flame rose, hovered before my face for one long moment — close enough that I felt warmth like a hand on my cheek, like every kindness he'd ever done me compressed into a single touch — and then it, too, streamed upward, through the cracking ceiling, toward a sky I desperately hoped still existed.

The last cage held a flame the color of dawn-gray, small and guttering, nearly a wick. Tam's own. His payment, held in escrow, waiting for him to finish settling accounts.

He sagged against the dissolving shadow, translucent as breath on glass, and looked at it. At his own name in the last locked cage. At me, holding a ledger that was mostly ash and entirely fire.

"Don't," he said.

"Tam—"

"That's my payment, Ada. That's what I bought. If you burn the last page before I—" he coughed, a horrible sound for someone with no lungs, "—before I finish, the deal breaks, and everything I just spent goes for nothing. Let me have it. Let me have *one thing* I negotiated well."

The floor was splitting. Veyre was rising out of it, no longer pretending to be a woman, a shape made of contracts and fury, and every ward in the under-halls was converging on the two of us, and above us a hundred and five souls were streaking for the surface like reversed rain.

I tucked the burning ledger against my chest, took one last look at the small gray flame in its cage, and ran.

Above us, the mountain began, very softly, to sing — the sound of a shield dying, the sound of Hollowmere's long protection unraveling thread by thread, the sound of everything I loved about home ending so that everyone in it could finally be free.

Behind me, Tam stood alone in the collapsing dark, guarding the door I'd come through, keeping his bargain.

I had a warden to kill, a stair to climb, and a hundred and five souls to shepherd home.

The first of the Undercourt's shadows hit the stairwell just as the last of the light left the cages — and I ran straight into it.
