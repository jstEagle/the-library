## 9. When the Candles Go Out

The ledger burned the way nothing in Hollowmere had ever been allowed to burn — greedily, hungrily, as if it had been waiting fifty years for permission.

I ran with it clutched against my chest, and it ran with me, flames licking up my sleeves without scorching, because of course they didn't. Undercourt fire doesn't destroy what it loves. It only changes the terms.

Behind me, a hundred souls poured up the stairwell like a river deciding, after centuries of being a lake, to remember which way was down. Or up. They were light — that was the horror and the beauty of them, candleflame souls in jars for so long that freedom looked exactly like burning — and they lit the collapsing under-halls better than any torch, throwing our shadows huge and frantic against walls that were coming apart in sheets of black stone.

The mountain did not appreciate being robbed.

"Left!" Tam's voice came from everywhere and nowhere, the way voices do when they've stopped bothering with lungs. "Ada, *left* — the right gallery goes down into the wax rooms—"

"I know what's in the wax rooms," I gasped, and cut left.

I did not know what was in the wax rooms. I knew enough never to want to find out.

* * *

Let me back up, because running narratives deserve at least the courtesy of a beginning.

It had gone like this: Tam broke the cages. Not gently — there was no gentle way to unmake a prison that had been built out of someone's patience — but thoroughly, the way he seemed to do everything once he'd finally decided to do it. The Undercourt had offered him rest. His rest. Fifty years of service, betrayal, guilt, and haunting, all of it forgiven and filed away, one small soul laid neatly on a shelf forever. All he had to do was stand aside and let me hand over the ledger.

He'd looked at me across that hall of caged candlelight, and I'd watched him choose.

"Sorry," he'd said to the court, conversationally, right before he put his hand through the first cage. "Changed my filing system."

Then everything had come apart, and we'd run.

That was four stairwells ago. The warden had found us two stairwells after that.

* * *

You should understand something about the Undercourt's warden: it was not a monster. Monsters are honest. It was an *administrator*.

It caught us on the Long Gallery — a corridor of white stone where every soul the court had ever held had left a mark, little scorched signatures on the walls, a guestbook written in grief. It unfolded itself out of the shadows between two signatures, tall and gray and patient, wearing robes that moved like settling dust, and its face was the worst part: a face like a clerk who has seen every possible plea and found them all correctly formatted.

"You are in possession of court property," it said. Its voice was dry paper. "The ledger. The souls. The debt."

"The debt is mine to burn," I said. I still had the ledger against my chest, and it was still burning, and I was starting to suspect the fire and I were having a conversation neither of us would admit to. "That's how debts work. Someone holds them. Someone ends them."

"The court holds all debts." The warden tilted its head, and somewhere above us, a ceiling groaned. "Including yours. You carry a sigil you killed for. You carry stolen flame. You carry—" it paused, consulting some inner ledger of its own, "—one hundred and seven escaped assets, one defected ghost, and a contract marked for destruction. Your account is considerably overdrawn, Ashgrove."

"My guildmaster's name is Merric Vane," I said, "and his soul is not an asset."

"No," the warden agreed, with terrible mildness. "It is collateral. As are you, now. Shall we discuss terms?"

What it offered, in that moment, in that corridor, while the mountain shook around us — I won't pretend I didn't feel the pull of it. That's the thing nobody tells you about bargains with the deep places: they're always *reasonable*. The warden didn't threaten. It itemized. It showed me, in cold columns of light, exactly what I could keep if I surrendered the ledger — my own soul, Tam's rest, even a portion of the hundred, returned to their families, less administrative fees. Less the valley's shield, which would need feeding regardless. Less, and less, and less, each line item a little door closing, until what remained of the bargain was a small dark room with me in it, alive, intact, and owned.

Tam drifted between us like smoke making a decision.

"Don't read the fine print," he said. "There isn't any. There never was. That's the trick — they make you think refusing is the reckless option."

"It usually is," said the warden.

"Yeah," said Tam. "Isn't it great?"

I ran. The warden let me take three steps before the gallery ended.

* * *

Here is what I learned about fighting an administrator: you cannot beat it at its own game. Every blow I struck with stolen sigil-fire, it absorbed into a new clause. Every escape route I found, it had already annotated. It herded us upward — always upward, toward the surface, toward the boundary of its jurisdiction, and I understood too late that it wasn't trying to stop us. It was trying to make us stop *ourselves*, at the last threshold, where the cost of going on would be visible from a long way off.

Because here was the other thing, the thing the warden had itemized in very small print indeed: the valley's shield was fed by the caged souls. A hundred lives, banked like coals, keeping Hollowmere safe from the magic that would otherwise pour in and remake it — or unmake it. Free the souls, and the shield guttered. Burn the contract, and the shield died outright. And beyond the shield, beyond the null-line my guild had tended for three generations, waited everything the court beneath Mount Veyre had ever wanted: a valley full of people who had forgotten magic existed, ripe for the remembering.

The warden didn't have to catch me. It just had to show me the price and wait for my love to do its work.

We burst out onto the Bridge of Names — a span of black glass over a chasm where the court kept its oldest holdings, and by then the whole under-mountain was screaming, halls folding into halls, the architecture of half a century of quiet theft eating itself. The hundred souls streamed past us across the bridge, and their light fell through the glass floor, and far below I saw them: shelves. Rows and rows of shelves, mostly empty now, and on the deepest row, apart from the others, one jar still glowing with a steady, stubborn, familiar warmth.

Merric.

My guildmaster. My keeper of boundary stones and bad tea and fifty years of kind evasions. His soul sat in its jar like a man waiting patiently at a station, and when the light of the escaping hundred touched it, it leaned toward them — toward home, toward the valley he'd sold himself to protect — and I swear on every stone in Hollowmere that jar glowed brighter.

"There," Tam said softly. "Last item on the manifest."

The warden stood at the far end of the bridge. Of course it did. It had probably measured the bridge.

"One soul," it said. "Yours to claim, if you claim it now. The ledger burns; the contract stands until the name is consumed. Bring me the ledger, Ashgrove, and take your guildmaster home. The valley keeps its shield. The hundred return to custody. Everyone is made whole." A pause, dry as dust. "Except those who were never whole to begin with. They are beyond accounting."

And there it was. The whole story, balanced on a bridge, in one sentence: save the one you love, and feed everyone else to the fire forever. Or end it — all of it — and let the valley learn what it felt like to be ordinary, and mortal, and *reachable*.

Tam hovered at my shoulder. He didn't say anything. He'd already paid his part; the cages stood open behind us, and whatever rest he might have earned was burning away with the ledger, spending itself to keep a hundred souls moving upward. Ghosts don't get second deaths, I think. But they can spend themselves down to almost nothing. He was almost nothing now, and he was smiling.

"Burn it," he said. "All of it. Even my page. *Especially* my page."

I opened the ledger.

The fire took it gladly. Page after page went up — names I'd never known, debts I'd never signed, fifty years of quiet arithmetic performed on human lives — and with every page the bridge shuddered and the warden took a step forward, patient as interest accruing, and I read the names as they burned because someone ought to. Someone owed them that much. A ferrywoman's daughter. A tinker from Greywash. A boy who'd carried maps.

And then the last page, and the name at the top of it, in ink that gleamed like a fresh signature:

*Merric Vane.*

I held it over the ledger-flame, and my hands shook, and I want to be honest with you, whoever finds this account: I hesitated. One breath. Maybe two. Long enough to see it all — the valley green and safe behind its invisible wall, Merric old and mortal and *there*, teaching some new apprentice to tend the stones, and me beside him, and none of it costing anyone anything except a hundred strangers whose faces I'd never learned.

Long enough to understand that this was exactly the room the warden had built for me. Small, dark, warm, and owned.

"No," I said. To the warden. To myself. To the girl who'd spent nineteen years longing for a life larger than the valley and had somehow, horribly, been offered it in the shape of a smaller one.

I fed Merric's name to the fire.

* * *

The debt unmade itself the way a knot unmakes when you find the one true end and pull: all at once, everywhere, with a sound like a held breath released over fifty years.

The warden came apart mid-stride. Not dramatically — administratively. Its clauses unraveled, its itemized light scattered into the chasm, and the last thing it did, I swear, was look mildly inconvenienced, as though we had filed our paperwork incorrectly and it would simply have to note the discrepancy and move on. Then it was dust, and the dust was falling, and the bridge was gone beneath my feet.

I don't remember crossing the rest of the way. I remember the jar — I smashed it against the last standing pillar, and Merric's soul rose out of it like a man standing up after a long sit, stretching, blinking at the light of a hundred freed companions — and I remember Tam, thinner than smoke now, translucent as early morning.

"Well," he said. "That's the ledger done. And the shield, I'd wager. Feel it?"

I did. Even down there, even buried under a mile of collapsing mountain, I felt it go — the null-line, the valley's long-held silence, fifty years of protection exhaling its last borrowed breath. Somewhere above us, the boundary stones were going dark one by one, and the world outside was leaning in, curious and hungry and *alive*.

"Go on," I told Tam. My voice cracked on it. "You've earned —"

"Rest?" He grinned, and it was his courier's grin, the one from the first night, bloodied and bright. "Maybe. After. I want to see the valley one more time first. See what it looks like without its walls." He flickered, steadied. "Walk fast, Ashgrove. The mountain's coming down, and I hear the surface is about to get very interesting."

Above us, through a fissure splitting the ceiling wide, I saw stars — real ones, close ones, closer than stars had any right to be — and understood that interesting had already begun.

I put my head down and climbed toward the light, with a hundred souls streaming past me like a river running home, and behind us the Undercourt's candles went out for good.
