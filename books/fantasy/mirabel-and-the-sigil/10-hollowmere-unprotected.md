## 10. Hollowmere, Unprotected

The valley looked smaller from the ridge.

I had spent nineteen years thinking of Hollowmere as the whole world — the crooked rooftops, the millpond, the boundary stones marching up the slopes like patient old dogs waiting to be told they'd done a good job. From up here, with the wind pulling at my coat and a hundred souls riding somewhere behind my ribs like embers I couldn't quite put down, it looked like what it was: a bowl. A pretty one. A kept one.

And now, unkept.

I felt it before I saw it. The null-magic that had lived in my bones since birth — that flat, muffled quiet every Hollowmere child learned to mistake for silence — was gone. In its place the world hummed. Not loudly. Just *there*, like a note held under water, like the moment before rain. Magic had come back into the valley the way light comes back into a room when someone finally opens shutters that have been painted over for decades. Everything was the same and nothing was.

The boundary stones were just stones now. I crouched beside the first one on the path down and touched its mossy crown. Cold granite. No thrum, no ward, no guild-sigil glowing faintly beneath the lichen. It had been a fence around a garden for sixty years, and now it was a rock, and the garden belonged to the weather.

"Go on," I told it. "Retire."

It did not answer, which I chose to take as agreement.

* * *

Hollowmere knew something had changed before anyone could say what. That's the thing about living inside a shield your whole life — you don't notice the pressure until it lifts, and then everyone walks around for three days feeling like their ears have popped.

The baker's bread rose wrong — too well, too golden, crust crackling in shapes that looked almost deliberate. Old Marta's rheumatism eased so suddenly she wept into her porridge and frightened her grandson. The millpond froze in spirals instead of sheets. Children were skipping stones across it and the stones were skipping *back*, which delighted the children and caused two grown men to move to their cousin's house in Lowmarsh until things settled.

And the guildhall — our proud, dwindling, threadbare guildhall with its eleven remaining members and its roof that leaked directly onto the map table — stood open-doored in the morning light, and nobody quite knew what to do inside it.

I came through those doors on the fourth day, road-dusted, sigil-burned, carrying a ledger's ashes in a pouch at my belt because it seemed wrong to leave them under a mountain. Eleven faces turned toward me. Some of them had thought I was dead. Some had hoped. Guilds are small; both reactions are honest.

"You're back," said Wren, who was seventeen and still believed the guild mattered.

"I'm back."

"The wards—"

"Gone." I set my pack on the map table, right where Merric used to spread his careful, evasive charts. "All of them. Permanently. We're not wardens anymore. We're just people who live near a mountain."

Nobody cheered. Nobody cried, either, though Marta's grandson would later swear he saw old Fenwick's eyes shine, and Fenwick denied it with the vigor of a man who had definitely been crying. They looked at me the way you look at someone who has come back from a place you only know from nightmares, and I understood that in Hollowmere's case, the nightmare had been the valley itself all along, and none of us had ever had the vocabulary for it.

Merric wasn't there.

* * *

I found him at the boundary stone where Tam had died.

He was sitting on the ground beside it like an old man resting on a walk, which is exactly what he was now. Sixty years of borrowed time had been called in, and the debt collector had apparently taken pity, or perhaps the Undercourt's collapse had simply let everyone go at once — Merric aged not in hours but in the space between one breath and the next, his hair going white as ash, his hands suddenly the hands of a man who had earned every one of his seventy-odd years honestly, which was more than could be said for anything else about him.

He heard me coming. He didn't turn around.

"They tell me the shield ran on them," he said. "The hundred souls. Fenwick worked it out from the way the wards died — all at once, like a lamp running out of oil, not like a wall being broken."

"It did."

"And I signed for it. I sat at a table under the mountain with a thing wearing a courtier's face, and I read the terms, and I signed anyway, because the alternative was watching the court eat this valley one farmstead at a time, and I told myself a hundred strangers was a price I could carry." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry as the ledger's ashes. "I never even asked their names. You'd think a man selling a hundred souls would at least ask their names."

"Tam Vess," I said. "Courier, out of Greywash. Twenty-six when they took him. He liked river songs and hated being cold, and he spent thirty years as candlelight trying to work up the courage to steal himself back." I sat down on the stone beside him. "There were ninety-nine others. I don't know their names yet. But the ledger burned whole, and the court's claim broke with it, and somewhere in the world right now there are a hundred families who are going to stop dreaming about candles and never know why. I intend to find out the names anyway. It'll take years. I've decided I have years."

Merric nodded slowly. His new-old hands were shaking.

"I'm sorry," he said. "Not the apology. The other kind — the kind where an old man says a true thing and means it and knows it doesn't fix anything. I loved this valley, Ada. I loved the guild. I loved—" He stopped. Started again. "I loved you like a daughter, and I let you grow up inside a lie I built, and then I forbade you to leave it, and you went anyway, and thank every god whose name I no longer have the standing to invoke, you went anyway."

"I know why you did it," I said. "That's the worst part. If you'd done it out of greed or cowardice I could hate you cleanly. But you did it out of love, badly measured, and love badly measured is how half the tragedies in the world get written."

"Will you stay? The guild will need—"

"The guild is finished, Merric. Let it be finished kindly. Teach them to read the old contracts so they recognize one if it's ever put in front of them. Teach them that protection bought with other people is just theft with extra steps. Then let them be farmers and bakers and children who skip stones that skip back." I looked out over the valley, humming with returned magic, wild and unguarded and alive. "They don't need wardens. They need neighbors."

"And you?"

I took a breath. It came easier than I expected.

"I forgive you," I said. "That's not the same as staying. I forgave you somewhere on the mountain road, actually, around the point where I realized I'd inherited your sin — I was willing to trade a hundred souls for one man I loved, and I had to learn in the worst room under the world that love doesn't get to do arithmetic like that. So we're even, you and I. Both of us loved badly. Both of us got corrected." I stood. My knees cracked. Nineteen is too young for that, but mountains age you. "I'm leaving at dawn."

"Where?"

"Everywhere the ledger's ashes point. There are names to recover. Families to find. And past them—" I shrugged, and felt the sigil on my forearm shift, fainter than yesterday, its lines gone from black to the grey of an old scar. "A world that's been locked out of this valley for sixty years. I'd like to meet it."

Merric looked up at me for a long moment. Then he smiled — small, ruined, real.

"Your mother would have gone with you," he said. "She always said the valley was a cradle, and cradles are for leaving."

I hadn't remembered her voice in years. Not since Greywash, since the ferrywoman's toll, since I paid for passage with my mother's face and walked off the boat unable to picture her. I'd grieved that memory like a limb. But standing there, hearing Merric say her words in his stranger's voice, I found I didn't need the face. The words were enough. She'd wanted me out of the cradle. Somewhere, somehow, she'd known.

"Thank you," I said, and meant it, and left him sitting by the dead stone in the morning light, mortal at last, guarding nothing.

* * *

Dawn came up gold and unwarded.

I packed light: the ash-pouch, a knife, bread that had risen suspiciously well, and Tam's ghost — or what remained of him, which these days was less a presence than a warmth, a flicker at the edge of my sight like a candle seen through frosted glass. Since the cages broke he'd been fading by degrees, and he'd made me promise not to make it a funeral.

"You'll be insufferable about it," he'd said. "'Oh, poor Tam, rest eternal, such a tragedy.' I was a courier, Ada. Dying at the end of a delivery is just good logistics."

So we didn't mourn. We walked.

At the last boundary stone — the first one, the one I'd told to retire — I stopped and pulled back my sleeve. The sigil-bearer's mark, the brand I'd taken off a dying man's hand on the mountain road, had faded to a whisper. Another week, maybe two, and it would be gone entirely, and with it the last trace of the Undercourt anywhere in the world. I traced it once with my thumb. It was warm. Almost companionable. A scar that remembered being a door.

"Last chance to haunt me properly," I said to the air beside me.

The air flickered. For a heartbeat I saw him — river-dark hair, that grin he'd worn while stealing the most dangerous document in creation, the look of a man who had finally, *finally* delivered. He didn't speak. He didn't need to. He pressed something against my palm that had no weight at all, and then the warmth went out of the morning like a hand letting go, gently, at the end of a long road.

I opened my fingers. Nothing. And everything. Somewhere behind my ribs, a hundred embers settled into rest, and one of them, the smallest, winked out last and brightest, like a courier signing off on a job well run.

"Well," I said, to the valley, to the stone, to nobody. "That's that."

I shouldered my pack. Behind me, Hollowmere woke without its shield — smoke from chimneys rising straight and free, magic threading through the fields like water finding old channels, a whole valley learning to live unguarded, which is the hardest and most ordinary thing there is. Ahead of me, the road ran down out of the cradle and into a world I had never been allowed to touch, full of courts and contracts and names I meant to dig out of the dark one by one, starting with ninety-nine strangers and ending, someday, somewhere, with whatever came next.

I had no map.

But I'd carried one written in blood clear across a mountain, and I'd learned the only lesson worth the carrying: the way forward doesn't need to be marked. It just needs to be walked.

Ada Ashgrove, thief of souls, ex-warden of a valley that no longer needed keeping, stepped over the dead boundary stone and out of Hollowmere —

and the world, humming, opened like a door.
