## 1. The Date That Wasn't Supposed to Exist

The trouble with a library that only one person ever visited was that the dust had opinions.

Ansel Underhill blew a cloud of it off the top shelf of the gatehouse library and sneezed with such violence that a moth, dozing in the rafters, woke up indignant and looped twice around her head before settling on her shoulder like a small gray apology.

"Yes, yes," she told it. "I'm here. I'm always here."

Restocking day came once a season, when she carried the winter's worth of candle stubs, ink, lamp oil, and—her favorite part—a crate of new books up the winding stair from the village below. Not that Hollowmere produced many books. The valley had two weavers, one reluctant schoolteacher, and a poet who had published exactly nothing in forty years and defended his record fiercely. Most of what Ansel shelved were replacements: cookbooks gone soft at the corners, almanacs whose pages had been thinned by years of thumbing, the occasional novel ordered through the pass-traders and read so often its spine had given up all structural ambition.

She liked restocking days for the same reason she liked all her days: they were the same. There was a comfort in repetition that outsiders never understood. Wake before dawn. Walk the gate's perimeter while the mist still clung to the stones like a second skin. Check the ward-lamps—seven of them, one for each stone of the arch, each burning a flame that gave no heat and cast no shadow. Log the readings. Feed the cat, who was not technically hers but had appointed himself to her household eleven years ago and had never once acknowledged the arrangement aloud.

Then the library. Then supper. Then sleep.

Forty-one years of it, if you counted from the night old Gatekeeper Maren had found an infant wrapped in a wool blanket on the threshold stone, with no note but a single line stitched into the hem: *She stays until she chooses otherwise.*

Ansel had chosen otherwise zero times.

"Right," she said to the moth, and got to work.

* * *

The books went back in order of stubbornness, which was how Ansel organized everything: the ones that fought decay hardest went on the high dry shelves, the fragile ones near the hearth where the air stayed kind. The almanacs first—they were the most abused, dog-eared within a week of arrival every year because half the valley consulted them about planting and the other half argued with them about weather. Then the cookbooks. Then, at the very top, the shelf nobody touched: the old books, the inherited ones, the leather-and-iron spines that smelled of cold cellars even in summer.

That was where the prophecy book lived.

It had come with the gatehouse, as far as anyone knew. Maren had kept it there; Maren's predecessor had too, going back through the ledgers of keepers like beads on a string. It was bound in green leather gone black at the edges, and its title had worn away entirely, which Ansel had always privately felt was the book being modest. She dusted it once a season, same as today, same as every year, tilting it toward the window light out of habit—

—and stopped.

There was writing on the flyleaf.

Not the usual writing. The book bore, on its first page, a single inscription in faded brown ink, a century old at least, in a fine slanted hand: *For the keeper who comes after. Read slowly. Some doors only open once.*

Below it, in the same hand—or so Ansel had believed, all these years—ran a date.

She knew that date the way she knew her own heartbeat, because she checked this book every season and the date had never changed. It sat beneath the inscription like a signature: a day, a month, a year. A hundred years past. Ancient history. The day, presumably, when whoever wrote the thing had finished writing it.

Except that today, tilted in the autumn light, the date did not say what it had always said.

Today it said tomorrow.

Ansel stood very still. The moth on her shoulder fidgeted, sensing, perhaps, that something in the room had gone wrong at a level below sound.

She took the book down with both hands—the way you'd handle a bird with a broken wing—and set it on the reading table under the good lamp. She put on her spectacles, though her eyes were fine; spectacles made looking deliberate, and deliberate looking was how you caught mistakes. She leaned close.

The ink was brown. Old brown, the brown of oak galls and a hundred years of patience. But the numerals themselves were crisp. Too crisp. They sat on the page like fresh footprints in old snow.

"Coincidence," she said aloud, because saying things aloud made them smaller. "Somebody re-inked it. Somebody bored. Somebody—" she gestured vaguely at the ceiling, at the valley, at the entire concept of other people—"somebody with too much time."

Nobody in Hollowmere had too much time. That was rather the point of Hollowmere.

She fetched the ledger of keepers down from its chain—it was chained because Maren had chained it, and Ansel had never seen reason to break a dead woman's habits—and flipped to the page of dates. Every season, forty-one years of them, in her own careful hand: the day she'd dusted the prophecy book, and beside it, transcribed, the date on its flyleaf. Unchanged. Unchanging. A little column of identical numbers marching down the page like soldiers who had been given one order and intended to follow it into eternity.

Tomorrow's date appeared nowhere in the column.

So either the book had changed overnight—and books did not change, books were the one thing in the world that held still—or she had misread it for forty-one years, which was worse, or—

The ink moved.

There was no better word for it, though Ansel would spend the walk home trying to find one. The numerals did not smear or blur. They *shifted*, the way a snake shifts, muscle passing beneath skin: the strokes of the numbers gathered themselves, hesitated, and settled into a slightly different shape. Still tomorrow's date. But written differently now. As if the book were practicing.

As if it wanted to be legible to her specifically.

Ansel sat down. Her knees made the decision without consulting her, which she supposed was fair, since her mind had resigned from all decision-making effective immediately.

"All right," she said to the book, in the tone she used on goats and other creatures testing boundaries. "Let's be clear about the rules. Books don't do that."

The book, being a book, declined to respond. But the lamp flame bent sideways, though there was no draft, and in the corner of her vision the shadow of the shelf seemed very slightly longer than the shelf itself.

She looked at the inscription again. *For the keeper who comes after.* She had read those words hundreds of times and always understood them to mean Maren, then herself, then some future keeper not yet born—a courteous relay of dusting duties stretching into forever. But tonight, with the ink still settling, the words arranged themselves differently in her mouth. *The keeper who comes after.* After what? After whom? It sounded less like an instruction now and more like a title. Less like a note left behind and more like a name waiting to be claimed.

Her name was already claimed, thank you. Ansel Underhill, gatekeeper of Hollowmere, keeper of seven unshadowed flames, owner (in the loosest sense) of one judgmental cat. She had a kettle with a whistle tuned to E-flat. She had a mending basket she fully intended to get to. She had a life built out of days that repeated, and she had built it that way on purpose, stone by patient stone, and no century-old book with ideas above its station was going to—

The ink shifted again.

This time she watched it happen properly, chin in hand, refusing to flinch. The numerals gathered, trembled—and then, beneath the date, a single new word began to surface, rising through the paper like a face coming up through dark water.

She slammed the book shut.

The sound echoed off the stone walls with a finality she found deeply satisfying. The moth departed for safer literary territory. Outside the narrow window, the mist was rising off the valley floor the way it always did at dusk, curling against the gate-stones and sliding away, repelled, obedient, contained. Seven ward-lamps burned along the arch, steady as ever. Everything out there was exactly as it had been this morning.

Everything in here had a word it wanted to give her.

Ansel pressed both palms flat on the closed cover. The leather was warm. Not lamp-warm, not hand-warm. Warm like something breathing under it, slow and patient, in no hurry at all.

"I'll open you tomorrow," she told it. "On your own schedule, apparently. Since you're so fond of dates."

And because she was her mother's daughter in every way except the ways that mattered—because Maren had taught her that fear was just information arriving early—she did not lock the book away or bury it or drop it down the well, though all three options presented themselves with considerable charm. She set it back on its shelf, spine out, in its exact old place between the water-stained hymnal and the atlas of a world nobody in Hollowmere had ever needed mapped.

Then she banked the fire, trimmed the lamps, and stood a moment in the doorway with her hand on the latch, looking back at the dark shelf where the green-black book sat, keeping its new secret the way the valley kept everything else.

The last thing she saw, before she closed the door, was the faintest seep of brown light along the edge of its pages. Like ink. Like dawn. Like a door, somewhere deep in the paper, quietly beginning to open.

At the bottom of the stairs, the cat was waiting for her, tail curled, unimpressed.

"Don't," said Ansel. "I know."

But knowing, that night, lying awake in the gatehouse with the E-flat kettle silent on its hook and tomorrow's date sitting in her chest like a swallowed coal—knowing did not help at all. Because for the first time in forty-one years, Ansel Underhill lay in her bed in the valley that kept magic out, and listened to the silence beyond the walls, and heard it listening back.

And far off, at the top of the pass where no road should have been, a lantern flickered once—twice—like someone walking down toward Hollowmere who had a very long way to travel and all night to do it.
