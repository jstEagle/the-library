## 2. What the Valley Keeps Out

Ansel Underhill had a system for everything, and the system said: when your world tilts, put it back level with chores.

So at dawn she swept the gatehouse steps, though no traveler had come in eleven days. She fed the hens and scolded the rooster, who deserved it. She restocked the lamp oil, mended a shutter hinge, and alphabetized the tincture shelf, which had been drifting toward chaos under her predecessor's lazy hand for thirty years. By midmorning the valley was tidy, the gatehouse was tidy, and Ansel Underhill was almost—*almost*—tidy of mind.

The book ruined it.

She'd left *The Vane Prophecies* on the reading desk overnight, which she never did, because books left out gathered damp. But she hadn't been able to make herself shelve it. It sat there now in the slanted light like a cat that had decided to live with you whether you agreed or not.

"Coincidence," she told it firmly. "Ink fades. Ink smears. Old paper does strange things."

The book did not argue. That was somehow worse.

She made tea she didn't drink. She stood at the window and looked down the long green throat of Hollowmere—the terraced farms, the millpond winking, the crooked bell tower of Little Ashford where everyone knew her name and her business and exactly how many sugar lumps she took. A good valley. A safe valley. Her valley, insofar as anyone's valley belonged to them.

And beyond the ridgeline, past the standing stones that marked the boundary, the rest of the world, which she had seen precisely four times in twenty-six years, on sanctioned supply trips to Millbrook, each one logged, each one chaperoned by the ward's rules: touch nothing enchanted, speak to no one twice, return before dusk.

Keeper's daughter. Keeper's granddaughter. Keeper, now, since Mother died. The gate did not open from this side without a keeper's hand, and the keeper did not leave without leaving the gate unkept, and so the whole arrangement turned on Ansel like a wheel turns on its axle: necessary, fixed, unexamined.

Until last night, when a book older than the axle had written tomorrow's date in its own margins.

"I'm going to test the ward," she announced to the hens, because announcing things made them real, and real things could be handled. "That's all. A routine check. Keepers check wards."

The hens were unimpressed. Hens are difficult to impress.

* * *

The boundary stones stood in a ring on the northern ridge, nine of them, gray as old teeth, each carved with the same mark: a circle bisected by a line. The Gate Mark. She'd traced it a thousand times with a finger, the way children trace fence posts.

The proper test was ceremonial. Salt, silver, a spoken verse from the maintenance manual. Ansel brought the salt and the verse and then, standing before the ninth stone with the wind combing the heather, she did something no manual recommended.

She cut her thumb on her belt knife and pressed it to the stone.

It was, she would think later, the most keeper-ish reckless thing she had ever done. The blood sank in—not beading, not running, but *sinking*, the way rain sinks into thirsty ground—and the stone woke.

Light ran the ring. All nine stones lit at once, a slow amber pulse traveling clockwise around the ridge like a thought traveling a mind, and beneath her feet Ansel felt the ward turn over. Not the familiar hum she'd known all her life, the low drowsy drone of a barrier doing its work. Something else. Something with attention in it.

"Oh," she said, very quietly, the way one speaks in a sickroom.

Because she could feel what the ward was doing, now that it was awake enough to be felt. It wasn't pushing outward. It wasn't holding magic at bay along some invisible wall. It was *contracting*—drawing itself inward, tighter and tighter, wrapping around something at the center of the valley like a fist closing around a jewel.

Around her.

Her blood hadn't tripped an alarm. It had been *recognized*. The ward knew her the way a lock knows its key, and it was sealing itself around her, snug as a coat, and every instinct she owned stood up at once and screamed.

She snatched her hand back. The light dimmed but didn't die. The stones kept their slow pulse, patient as a heartbeat.

"That's not—" Her voice cracked; she tried again. "That's not what wards do."

Wards repelled. Wards kept out. Grandmother had taught her the doctrine before she'd taught her letters: *the valley is a cup turned upside down over a candle; nothing wild may enter.* Ansel had built her whole life inside that sentence.

But a cup turned upside down doesn't need to know whose candle it covers.

This ward knew hers.

She walked the ring once, twice, pressing her palm flat to each stone without blood this time, and felt the same thing at every one: recognition, contraction, a seal settling into place around the center of the valley like a hand closing gently, gently, around something precious and breakable.

Not a wall. A *cradle*.

"No," she said aloud, to the stones, to the wind, to the century-old book waiting back in the gatehouse with tomorrow inked in its margins. "No, that's—"

"You've noticed, then."

The voice came from behind her, from outside the ring, from the direction of the pass where no road ran and no traveler should be, and Ansel spun with her knife already drawn, because a keeper learns to draw first and apologize to livestock later.

A woman stood just beyond the ninth stone. Lean, sun-browned, dressed in travel leathers gone soft with use, a great leather map case slung across her back and a surveyor's tripod folded under one arm like a musket. Dusty boots. Sharp, amused eyes the color of strong tea. She looked, Ansel thought absurdly, like a fox that had learned to walk upright and found it profitable.

"You're outside the ward," Ansel said. Her heart was hammering. "Nothing gets in."

"Mm." The woman tilted her head. "Interesting theory. Mind if I test it?" And she stepped across the line of stones.

Ansel braced for the recoil, the flash, the gentle violence that had turned back peddlers and pilgrims and one memorably stubborn bear. Nothing happened. The woman crossed the boundary the way you'd step over a threshold into a friend's kitchen, dusting her boots politely on the heather, and the ward didn't so much as flicker.

Except—it did. Ansel felt it, deep in her sternum where the stones' pulse had lodged: a ripple of *recognition*, passing over the stranger and finding nothing to hold. Like a hand closing around a jewel and finding, beside it, only a pebble. The ward ignored Wren-whatever-her-name-was completely.

It only cared about Ansel.

"You're not surprised," the stranger said, watching her face. "I've crossed forty-one sealed boundaries in my career, and every warden on the other side looks surprised. You look like someone who's just done arithmetic she didn't want to finish."

"Who are you?" Ansel kept the knife up. It felt theatrical. The woman clearly knew it too; her eyes crinkled.

"Wren Halloway. Cartographer, formerly of the Royal Survey, currently of nowhere in particular." She sketched a bow that managed to be both genuine and mocking. "I map things that have been deliberately unmapped. Hollowmere has been off every chart for a hundred years, which is cartography's equivalent of a shout in the night. So I came to find out why."

"The valley isn't secret," Ansel said. "We trade in Millbrook."

"Millbrook," Wren repeated, tasting the word. "Which appears on maps as a market town with two roads out. One east. One south. No road north into any valley at all." She unslung the map case with a practiced shrug and drew out a rolled chart, weighting the corners with stones. "People remember trading with you the way you remember a dream about buying bread. Pleasant. Unverifiable. Gone by noon."

Ansel looked at the map. Hollowmere was indeed absent—a blank fold of mountains where her whole life was lived, rendered as anonymous shading, as if the surveyor had grown bored or frightened or bribed.

"My grandmother maintained that," she heard herself say. "The forgetting. It's part of the ward."

"It's part of the *seal*," Wren said. "Different animal entirely. A ward keeps things out. A seal keeps something *in*—and persuades the rest of the world not to look too hard at the box." She crouched, studying the ninth stone's carving, and her teasing sharpness dropped away into something more careful. "I've spent six years chasing a rumor. A lost heir. Written about in a book called *The Vane Prophecies*, by an author nobody can find any record of ever having existed. The prophecy names a keeper born to a hidden valley, blood-bound to the crown, raised as a lock instead of a person."

The wind moved through the heather. Somewhere below, a cowbell clanked, ordinary as bread.

"I don't have royal blood," Ansel said. "I have hen-blood and tincture-blood and a grandmother who smelled of camphor."

"Prophecies are rarely considerate about pedigree." Wren rose, brushed off her knees, and looked at her directly, and there was nothing foxy in her face now—only a kind of tired, professional pity. "What did the stone do when you bled on it?"

"How do you—"

"You've got a bandaged thumb and a knife in your hand and you're standing closer to the stones than any warden stands unless she's testing something desperate. I map things, Miss Underhill. People are mostly maps too."

Ansel's mouth was dry. "It recognized me. The ward—it closed around me. Around the valley's center. Like—" She hated the word even as she said it. "Like a cradle."

Wren nodded slowly, as if Ansel had confirmed a figure she'd already penciled in. "Then here's my professional assessment, free of charge, and I'm sorry for it: you're not the gatekeeper of Hollowmere. You're the *contents*."

The word went through her like cold water through cloth.

Everything rearranged. Twenty-six years of doctrine flipped over like a rug beaten clean: *the valley keeps magic out; the valley protects the world; the keeper guards the gate.* And underneath, all along, the true sentence, plain as day once you stopped flinching from it: *the valley keeps Ansel in.* Every rule, every ritual, every chaperoned trip to Millbrook and forgotten-by-noon friendship—not protection. Containment. She had tended her own cage with such love, such diligence, oiling its hinges daily, and called the oiling a sacred duty.

She sat down. Not gracefully—her knees simply resigned—and found herself sitting in the heather with the amber pulse of the stones washing over her, laughing in a small unhinged way that she couldn't seem to stop.

"A lock," she said. "You said raised as a lock instead of a person. That's very accurate. I'd like to file a complaint with the author."

"You may get the chance," Wren said. "If half of what the prophecies claim is true, Edith Vane has been dead a hundred years, and dead authors are famously difficult to serve papers to." She held out a hand—weathered, ink-stained at the fingertips, a cartographer's hand. "Come on, then. Up. Sitting in a ward that's actively sealing around you seems unwise, and I say that as someone whose entire profession is knowing where not to stand."

Ansel took the hand. It was warm and rough and real, and being pulled upright by a stranger who shouldn't have been able to enter her valley at all was somehow the strangest thing that had happened yet, which was saying something.

"Why?" she asked. "Why come? Six years chasing a rumor. Why?"

Wren shrugged, retrieving her stones and re-rolling her map with quick, economical motions. "Because somebody erased a valley from the world and I wanted to know what was worth erasing." She glanced sideways, tea-colored eyes glinting. "Also because the prophecy's final verses mention a date. Tomorrow's, as it happens. And a choice. And I've read enough endings to know that whoever's named in them usually prefers a bit of warning."

Ansel went still. "Tomorrow's date."

"Mm. The same one, I'd wager, that turned up somewhere it shouldn't have recently. In a certain old book." Wren shouldered her map case and started down the ridge toward the gatehouse, walking backward a few steps so she could grin at Ansel properly. "You've got the look of a woman who found tomorrow written in yesterday's ink. So. Are you going to invite me in for tea, heir of Hollowmere, or shall we stand on the ridge while the valley finishes deciding what you are?"

Below them, the amber pulse of the stones beat on, steady as a second heartbeat, and Ansel—who had spent her whole life believing she knew exactly what the valley kept out—followed the stranger down, carrying the far more terrible knowledge of what it kept in.

And behind them, faint but unmistakable, the ninth stone began, very softly, to hum a word.

She couldn't hear it yet.

But she recognized, with a keeper's ear, the sound of a name being spoken.
