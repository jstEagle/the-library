## 3. Letters from a Dead Author

Ansel did not sleep. She sat at the gatehouse table with the prophecy book open in front of her, a cup of tea gone cold at her elbow, and watched the ink.

It had stopped shifting since sundown, which was somehow worse. A thing that moved was a thing you could catch doing it. A thing that held still while you stared at it was a thing that knew you were staring.

"You could close it," Wren said from the doorway. She'd made herself at home with an ease that bordered on insolence—boots off, coat hung on Ansel's one spare peg, a heel of bread in her hand that she'd helped herself to without asking. "You know that, don't you? Books can be closed. It's their single most reliable feature."

"If I close it, I'm deciding not to know."

"And if you leave it open all night, you're deciding to be haunted by it instead." Wren came and sat across from her, uninvited, and turned the book so she could read it upside down, which she apparently could do without difficulty. "That's tomorrow's date again. Third time it's rewritten itself since you found me at the boundary. It's not showing you the future, Underhill. It's counting down."

"Counting down to what?"

"That," said Wren, "is an excellent question, and I have a feeling we're about to get an answer, because your candle just went out without a draft."

The flame hadn't gone out. It had *bent*—drawn sideways, toward the open book, as though the page were breathing in. The light pooled on the paper and sank into it, and the ink began to move again, but differently now: not crawling along its lines but rising up out of them, lifting off the page like smoke given handwriting.

The smoke gathered, wavered, and became a woman.

She was small and round-shouldered, with ink-stained fingers and spectacles perched on her nose that were themselves slightly transparent, so that Ansel could see the table through both lenses at once. Her dress was a century out of fashion and her expression was that of someone who had been interrupted mid-sentence for a very long time.

"Oh, *finally*," the ghost said. "Do you know how long I've been shouting into this book? A hundred years. A hundred years of writing prophecy into a valley where nobody reads past the first chapter because they're all too busy being *careful*."

Ansel stood up so fast her chair went over backward. "What are you?"

"I'm Edith Vane. This is my book. You are standing in my house, technically, or my house's house—I built the gatehouse library, or rather I designed it and then died before anyone thanked me—and you are the first person in a century to read me all the way through." The ghost peered at her over the spectacles. "Well. Almost. You skipped the marginalia."

"There is no marginalia."

"There is if you look properly. There's always more if you look properly. That's rather the thesis of my entire life." Edith drifted closer, and the candlelight passed through her like water through a net. "Now. Which are you? The keeper or the heir?"

"The what?"

"Mm," said Edith, in the tone of someone whose worst suspicion has been confirmed. "That's what I was afraid of. Sit down, girl. Both of you. What I have to say will take a while, and I've only got as long as the candle holds, because apparently even dead I require lighting."

* * *

Wren relit the candle from the hearth, and Edith talked.

She talked the way Ansel imagined she must have written: fast, digressive, brilliant, with a habit of circling back to the important thing three pages after you'd assumed she'd forgotten it. She had been, in life, the Royal Archivist under King Osric—the last king before Aldric, though Ansel hadn't known there *was* a king before Aldric, which Edith found darkly funny.

"Of course you didn't. That's rather the point of everything I'm about to tell you. Hollowmere doesn't just keep magic out. It keeps memory out. It's a valley with a lid on it, and the lid works in both directions."

"The ward doesn't repel anything," Ansel said slowly. "I tested it yesterday. It—it recognized me. It sealed around my blood like it knew me."

"It did know you. It knows you better than you know yourself, which is a sentence I regret having to say, because it sounds like the opening of a tragedy, and it is." Edith folded her translucent hands. "I wrote this book inside the palace, a hundred years ago. I wrote it as a record, not a prophecy—the distinction matters, and historians will fight about it forever, assuming there are any historians left who remember me. Here is the record:

"King Osric was dying. Not of wounds, not of age—of forgetting. Something was eating his mind from the inside, something old and hungry that lived in the deep places under the capital, in the spire of the old palace that everyone had forgotten the purpose of. I loved him—not romantically, don't make that face, cartographer, I mean the way you love a lighthouse—and I could not bear it. So I did what archivists do. I tried to preserve him."

"Preserve him how?" Wren asked quietly.

"I built a vessel. A lattice—a structure of memory, of story, of everything he was, woven together and set spinning around what remained of his mind, so that when his body failed, the pattern would hold. I called it the Spire because I am, in retrospect, an idiot who names things after the things that destroy them." Her voice didn't waver, but her hands did. "It worked. It worked *too well*. The lattice didn't just preserve him. It learned him. And then it learned that it preferred being him to being nothing, and when Osric finally went, the Spire reached out and found the next king. And the next. It wears them, do you understand? It wears them like coats, and underneath, the men fray."

Ansel's mouth had gone dry. "Aldric."

"Aldric," Edith agreed. "He's worn longest of any of them. Nineteen years. Most kings last five before the lattice eats through whatever's left. He's lasted because he's strong, and because—" she looked directly at Ansel, and her gaze had weight despite having no substance— "because part of the Spire has been waiting. Reaching. For something it lost when I bound the valley shut."

"The heir," Wren said. "The lost heir. That's why the ward sealed around her blood."

"Osric had a daughter. Nobody remembers her either—Hollowmere's lid took care of that. She was sent here as an infant, to the one place in the world the Spire couldn't reach, and the line of keepers raised her descendants without ever telling them why, because the knowing itself would draw attention. Every keeper of Hollowmere has carried the blood. None of them were told." Edith's image flickered, steadied. "You are the last of that line, Ansel Underhill. The ward doesn't seal the valley against magic. It seals it around *you*. You are the lock. You always have been."

The room felt very small and very far from anywhere. Ansel gripped the edge of the table. "Then the date in the book—"

"Is the date the Spire finishes eating Aldric. Three days from now, give or take a king's stubbornness. After that, it won't need a coat anymore. It'll need a body young enough to hold it, and it knows exactly where the only suitable one lives." Edith smiled, and it was a terrible, gentle smile. "It's been writing to you, dear. Through my book. It thinks you should come willingly."

* * *

For a long moment nobody spoke. The fire cracked. Somewhere outside, the valley breathed its windless, guarded breath.

"No," Ansel said finally. "No. I'm a gatekeeper. I count stores and oil hinges and check the boundary stones. I am not—whatever this is. I didn't ask for any of it."

"No one asks," Edith said. "I certainly didn't ask to spend eternity trapped in my own footnotes. But let me tell you about guilt, since we're apparently forming a club of people who broke things trying to fix them. I built the Spire out of love. I bound this valley out of fear. Between those two acts, I unmade a kingdom's memory and left a child's descendants guarding a prison they thought was a sanctuary. If I could undo one thread of it, I would have done it a hundred years ago. I can't. All I can do is write, and hope, and wait for someone to read to the end."

"What's at the end?" Wren asked.

"The unbinding. There's exactly one, and I won't pretend it's simple. But it exists, and it requires the heir, and it requires her to be *outside* the valley when it happens—which means the gate has to stand open, which means everything I built has to come apart, which means—" Edith pressed her lips together. "Which means asking the woman I trapped here to dismantle my life's work and walk into the teeth of the thing I created. I know what I'm asking. I've had a century to rehearse the apology."

Ansel looked down at the book. At the date, patient and terrible on the page. At the marginalia she still couldn't see.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then in three days the Spire comes to collect you anyway, and it does not knock, and it does not write politely in books. It simply arrives, and the valley's lid will not stop it, because the lid was built by me and the Spire was built by me, and I assure you, I left myself a key." She spread her ink-stained hands. "I'm sorry. Truly. But refusing isn't actually on the table. The only choice you have is whether you go as a prisoner or as an heir."

Wren leaned back in her chair and studied Ansel with those mapmaker's eyes, measuring distances. "For what it's worth," she said, "I've seen the capital. I've seen what's left of the king. Whatever's wearing him is running out of thread. If we're going to do this, we should do it soon."

"*We*," Ansel repeated. "You're coming?"

"I found you, didn't I? I'd hate for that to have been a waste of perfectly good boots." Wren shrugged, but her jaw was tight. "Also, I owe the crown a debt I'd rather not itemize. Consider me hired."

Edith's ghost brightened—or perhaps the candle did; it was hard to tell where one ended and the other began. "Then listen carefully, both of you, because I will only be able to manifest while the book is open and lit, and there are things you must carry with you that cannot be written down. First: trust no one in the palace who remembers too much or too little. Second: the Spire will speak kindly. Kindness is how it hunts. And third—"

She stopped. Her head tilted, listening to something none of them could hear, and her translucent face went pale in a way that ghosts should not be able to manage.

"Third," she finished quietly, "pack tonight. Because the date in my book has just changed again."

Ansel looked down.

Tomorrow's date was gone. In its place, fresh and wet and gleaming, was today's.

"He's early," Edith whispered. "The Spire is already reaching for you, Ansel. Saddle up. We ride at dawn—if the dawn waits that long."
