## 4. Leaving Hollowmere

Ansel had packed the gatehouse three times before dawn.

The first time she packed like a woman going to market: bread, cheese, a change of linen, her good boots. The second time she unpacked everything and repacked it like a woman fleeing a fire, which felt dishonest, because no one was chasing her yet. The third time she sat on the floor of her little room with a single candle and asked herself what a person brought when she might never come home.

She brought the prophecy book. She brought her mother's thimble, though she couldn't sew worth a damn. She brought the iron key to the gate, because it seemed wrong to leave Hollowmere without carrying its lock.

"You'll want the oilskin," Wren called from downstairs. "It rains sideways past the ridge. I've been rained on sideways in six provinces and I can tell you it builds character, but you have enough character."

Ansel came down with her pack and found Wren Halloway standing in the gatehouse doorway in the grey first light, looking out at the valley the way other people looked at cathedrals. The cartographer was a compact, weathered person of indeterminate age, with ink-stained fingers and a coat that had been patched so many times the patches had begun patching each other. A rolled map hung at their hip like a sword.

"It's smaller than I imagined," Wren said. "The valley."

"Everyone says that."

"No—smaller isn't worse." Wren tapped their temple. "I've mapped places so vast they swallowed the people walking through them. This is the kind of place that holds you. I can see why you never left."

"I wasn't allowed to leave."

"Mm," said Wren, who had read the prophecy twice and Ansel's whole life once, by inference, and knew better than to argue before breakfast.

They ate standing up. Edith Vane's ghost hovered near the hearth where her book had lived for a century, translucent as breath on glass, watching Ansel eat bread with an expression of such fierce maternal disapproval that Ansel nearly laughed.

"You're not eating enough for the road," Edith said.

"I'm eating plenty."

"You're eating like a girl who expects to be back by supper."

Ansel stopped chewing. That was exactly how she'd packed the first time.

Edith's form flickered—the ghost could only hold shape near her own book, and Ansel had taken to carrying it in a satchel against her ribs, which meant Edith drifted after her like a very opinionated lantern. "I wrote the leaving chapter of my own life badly," the ghost said quietly. "Don't copy me. Eat the cheese too."

* * *

The gate itself was nothing much to look at: two stone posts, an iron arch gone green with moss, the road running through it toward the pass. Ansel had walked this stretch ten thousand times. She had swept these posts. She had carved her initials into the northern one at age nine and lied about it for a decade.

But today she would walk *through*, and the ward would let her—or rather, the ward would do whatever it did when its keeper's blood crossed its own threshold—and Ansel found her feet would not move.

Wren waited a respectful distance back. Edith hovered at her shoulder, close enough that the cold coming off her made Ansel's ear ache.

"When I built the boundary," Edith said, "I anchored it to a drop of royal blood. It was meant to be symbolic. Kings love symbolism; it's cheaper than justice." Her voice went thin. "I didn't know then whose blood it would recognize. Or that it would recognize blood at all, only... presence. Only *her*."

"The heir," Ansel said. "Me. A baby I've never met."

"A baby who grew into a keeper," Edith said. "Which is more than I managed. Go on, then. The world's been waiting a hundred years and it's terribly impatient."

Ansel put one foot past the posts.

The world *sang*.

There was no gentler word for it. One stride she stood in the familiar hush of Hollowmere—the valley's famous silence, the flat quiet she'd mistaken all her life for peace—and the next stride she stepped into a roar of color and pressure and light that had been there all along, everywhere, pressing against the ward like the sea against a window.

Magic. Not as story, not as rumor, but as *weather*. It poured into her senses through doors she hadn't known were boarded shut. She could feel the ley-lines under the road like veins of hot metal. She could feel the trees thinking slow green thoughts. She could feel the sun as a physical weight, generous and enormous, and somewhere far to the east—a smudge, a wound, a hunger—the Spire, turning over in its sleep beneath a crown.

Her knees hit the road. She was dimly aware of Wren catching her pack, of Edith crying out, of her own hands pressed flat against dirt that hummed like a struck bell.

"Breathe," Wren said, crouched beside her. "In through the nose. It's a flood, not a drowning. Well—it's both. But the flood part wins if you breathe."

"It's—" Ansel's voice cracked. "It's *everywhere*. How does anyone think? How does anyone *walk*?"

"Badly, at first. Then you learn to listen the way you listen to rain—one sound among many, none of them yours to carry." Wren's hand was steady between her shoulder blades. "You've been deaf since birth, Underhill. Today the bandages came off. Of course the light hurts."

Ansel breathed. In through the nose, out through the mouth, while the world roared its colors at her and slowly, slowly resolved into something she could survive: not a flood anymore but a landscape, layered and alive, terrifying and so beautiful that tears ran down her face without asking permission.

Behind her, through the gate, Hollowmere lay silent and flat and safe as a held breath.

She grieved for it right there on the road, openly, the way you grieve for a parent who is still alive—you grieve for the version of things that can't come back. The small life. The routine. The girl who believed the silence was protection instead of a cage humming to itself.

Then she got up, because the road went east, and grief is a poor traveling companion if you let it drive.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a standing stone that Wren insisted on greeting personally, shaking its hand, or the lichen-covered approximation of a hand.

"Old friend?" Ansel asked.

"Survey marker. My old master set it forty years ago and swore it was older than the kingdom." Wren built a small fire with the economy of someone who had done it in rain, wind, and once, memorably, during a border skirmish. "Ley-stones mark where the lines cross. Which brings us to tonight's lesson, if your head's stopped ringing."

"It's humming. Politely."

"That's progress." Wren unrolled a map across their knees—not paper, but vellum gone soft as cloth, covered in Wren's own dense, beautiful hand. Roads, rivers, mountains. And threaded through all of it, in ink that shimmered faintly when the firelight caught it, lines. Great slow curves crossing the land like the migration paths of invisible birds.

"Ley-lines," Wren said. "The roads magic travels when it thinks nobody's watching. Every hedge-witch knows a trickle of them. Cartographers of my particular disgrace know the whole map." They tapped a line that ran from the eastern mountains down through the heart of the country. "This one runs under Hollowmere. It's why your valley could be warded at all—you can't dam a river without a river. And it flows—" their finger traced east, past forests and fens, to a city drawn in careful detail— "*there*. To the capital. To the palace. To him."

"To the Spire."

"To the thing drinking through the king." Wren's jaw tightened. "A century ago, Edith anchored her working to this line. Everything the Spire takes, it takes along this current. And everything we need to unbind will have to travel back along it. Us, included."

"So we follow the river to its mouth."

"We follow the river to its mouth," Wren agreed, "and try very hard not to be drunk by it on the way."

They practiced until the fire burned low. Wren taught her to feel a ley-line without touching the map—to find the faint warmth in the ground, the way birdsong bent around it, the way her new senses could trace its course like a finger under a tablecloth. Ansel was clumsy at first, then less clumsy, then—somewhere past midnight, cold, exhausted, grinning like a fool—she found the line herself, unaided, and felt it thrumming under the meadow like a plucked string still singing.

"There," she whispered. "I can feel it. It goes all the way to him, doesn't it. I could walk it blindfolded."

"Don't," Wren said sharply. Then, softer: "That's the trap, Underhill. The line doesn't just go to the palace. Things on the line can feel *you* walking it. You're not a mouse sneaking along the wall. You're a lit candle moving through a dark house."

Ansel pulled her senses back from the line as if from a hot stove. In her satchel, muffled but unmistakable, Edith's book grew cold.

"She's listening," Ansel said.

"She's always listening. It's her worst habit." Wren banked the fire. "Sleep. Tomorrow we reach the crossroads at Fenmarsh, and after that the country stops being empty."

Ansel lay back on her bedroll and stared up at stars that now, with her new sight, were not merely stars but *lamps*—hung along lines of light that arced over the world in patterns no atlas had ever dared draw. Beautiful. Terrifying. Open.

For twenty-five years she had guarded a door, believing she kept the world safe from what was inside it.

Now she knew she was what the door was for. And the world outside was vast, and singing, and one thread of its song led straight to a king wearing something that used to be his own mind.

She slept, eventually. But in the last moment before sleep took her, she felt it—far off east, along the bright thread of the ley-line, something turning its attention toward the west.

Toward the open gate.

Toward *her*.

Something in the dark, patient as a spider, began—very gently—to pull the thread.
