## 5. The City That Forgot Its King

The capital of Veyland announced itself three miles before we reached it, the way a wealthy uncle announces himself at a funeral—loudly, expensively, and with no particular interest in who he's stepping on.

Wren had seen it from a cartographer's distance a hundred times: the copper roofs going green down the hillside like moss on a sleeping giant, the river split by ten bridges, the palace spire—*the* spire, though nobody called it that anymore—rising out of the middle like a needle someone had forgotten to pull from the world's thumb. But she'd never come at it on foot, dusty and road-worn, with a companion who kept stopping to stare at ordinary things as if they were miracles.

"Ansel," she said for the fourth time that morning. "You can gawk at the aqueduct *after* we're inside the walls. Right now you look like exactly what you are."

"Which is what?"

"A farm girl seeing her first city."

Ansel's chin came up. "I am not a farm girl."

"You're a gatekeeper who has personally known fewer than forty people in her entire life. In this town, that makes you a farm girl." Wren softened it with a grin. "It's not an insult. Farm girls survive cities. Ladies faint in them."

They passed through the Southgate in a river of carts and chickens and one extremely indignant goose, and Wren felt the old familiar knot tighten in her chest—the one that meant she was back in a place where she owed money to people who collected debts with knives.

But something else was wrong too, and it took her until they were three streets into the Tanner's Quarter to name it.

Nobody looked up.

Not at Ansel, which was the miracle—because Wren had spent two hundred miles watching strangers do double-takes at that face, that unmistakable jaw, those grey eyes that belonged on a coin—but also not at anything. The streets were full. The shops were open. The fountains ran and the bells rang noon and the whole city hummed along like a well-oiled machine, and yet Wren could not find a single person who seemed to be looking *at* anything. They walked where they walked. They bought what they bought. A busker played a fiddle beautifully on the corner of Cordwainer Street and received not one coin, because no one heard him.

"Hollowmere is quiet," Ansel said quietly beside her. "But it's quiet like held breath. This is quiet like—" She searched for it. "Like a room after everyone's left."

"Yeah." Wren steered them left, away from the processional avenue. "I noticed."

A baker leaned in his doorway, arms crossed, watching his own loaves cool with an expression of mild bafflement, as though bread were a hobby he'd taken up recently and couldn't remember why.

Wren stopped him. "Good morning. Fine weather for the season."

"Mm." He smiled vaguely.

"Big crowds expected soon? With the king's festival coming?"

"What festival?"

"The—the Ascension Day festival. For the king."

The baker's brow furrowed pleasantly, like a man trying to recall a dream. "We have a king?"

And there it was. Not hostility. Not fear. Just a soft, smiling blankness, worn so comfortably the man didn't even know it was a wound.

Wren thanked him, bought a loaf she didn't want, and walked on until they turned down the alley behind the Drowned Rat, where the noise of the city fell away and she could breathe.

"They don't remember him," Ansel said. It wasn't a question. She'd read the same thing Wren had, in the set of that baker's shoulders. "All of them?"

"The Spire eats slowly," Wren said. "Starts with the edges. First you forget a face, then you forget why the face mattered, then you forget there was ever a reason to look up." She knocked a specific rhythm on a specific door—a rhythm she'd invented herself, eleven years ago, and had prayed every day since that the man behind it still answered to it.

The door opened on a chain. One eye appeared. The eye traveled from Wren's boots to her hat and back.

"No," said the eye.

"Wren Halloway."

"I know who you are. That's the problem." The door began to close.

Wren wedged her boot in it. "Doss. I need a room, a rumor, and a route into the palace underlevels. In that order."

"You need a grave, is what you need. Do you know who's asking after you? Do you know how many people came through here last month carrying your description?" The eye flicked past her—to Ansel—and stopped. The chain rattled. The door opened all the way.

Doss Merrow was sixty, built like a rain barrel, and had once been the finest lockman in Veyland before a job gone wrong cost him two fingers and his nerve. He stared at Ansel now with the naked, unguarded awe of a man seeing a ghost in daylight.

"Well," he said faintly. "Well, well."

"Doss."

"That's—" He pointed with his maimed hand. "That's the old queen's face. That's the portrait in the Long Gallery, before they took them all down. I carried half those frames out myself, back when." He blinked hard, shook himself, and glared at Wren with sudden ferocity. "You absolute idiot. You brought *her* here. To *this* city. You might as well have hung a bell round her neck."

"We're careful," Wren said.

"You're *dead*, you're just both too stupid to lie down." But he hauled them inside anyway, because Doss had always been a coward with a generous heart, which in Wren's experience was the most useful combination a person could have.

* * *

His rooms above the Drowned Rat smelled of tallow and old paper, and every wall was covered in maps—not Wren's elegant commissioned work, but Doss's own, scrawled, obsessive, annotated in six colors of ink. Maps of the palace underlevels. Maps of the sewers. Maps, Wren noted with a chill, of the palace *spire itself*, drawn over and over, each attempt less accurate than the last, as though the building refused to hold its shape in memory.

"It won't stay put on paper," Doss said, catching her look. He poured three cups of wine without asking. "I've tried forty times. Every drawing comes out different. My hand knows what my head doesn't." He drank. "You feel it too, don't you. Being here. Like walking through soup."

"I feel watched," Wren said.

"Everyone feels watched. Nobody remembers by whom." He set down his cup. "Now. Room, I can do. Rumor, I can do. But hear the rumor first, because it changes everything."

He leaned forward.

"Three nights ago, a courier came from the palace to the Lord Protector's house. Ordinary enough. Except the courier was met at the gate by name, and the name was 'Halloway.' Not yours—your old employer's. Corvane. And the message the courier carried, per my cousin who waters the Protector's plants, contained one line." Doss's voice dropped. "*The gatekeeper's daughter is on the road.*"

The room went very still. On the table between them, the wine trembled faintly in Ansel's cup—from her hand, Wren realized, not from any tremor in the floorboards.

"That's impossible," Ansel said. "No one knew about me. Edith's book named me *yesterday*. Yesterday, Wren. We rode straight here."

"Someone knew before the book did," Wren said slowly. Her mind was doing the cold arithmetic it did best. "Or—" and here was the thought she liked least, the thought that fit the facts like a key fits a lock, "—or someone made sure the book would find you. Prophecies don't fall open to the right page by accident, Ansel. Someone wanted you found. Wanted you *here*."

"Corvane," Doss said. "Lord Protector Corvane. Ten years regent for a king nobody can picture. Do you know what happens to a regency when the king wakes up? It ends. Do you know what happens to Corvane?" He drew a finger across his throat, gently, almost apologetically.

"He needs the king asleep forever," Wren said. "And he needs whatever's keeping it asleep fed." She looked at Ansel—at the grey eyes, the jaw off the vanished portraits, the heir nobody was supposed to know existed, walking willingly into the one city where she was the most valuable thing alive. "Oh, Ansel. We haven't come to rescue the kingdom. We've been *invited*."

"That's absurd," Ansel said. But her voice wobbled, because she had grown up reading wards, and wards taught you one lesson above all others: things that look like doors are sometimes traps, and things that look like traps are sometimes doors, and the only way to tell is whose hands are on the hinges.

"Absurd," Doss agreed cheerfully. "Also true. Now drink your wine, because I'm only telling you the rest once."

"The rest?"

"How to get into the palace." He tapped the wall of maps, on the spire that wouldn't hold its shape. "Because if Corvane's expecting you, girl, then the front door's a mouth. And mouths," he said, "are for swallowing."

Outside, the bells rang the quarter hour, sweet and hollow, and somewhere above them—Wren would have sworn it, though she could never afterward say how she knew—something in the tall white spire turned its attention downward, patient as a spider, and waited for them to climb.
