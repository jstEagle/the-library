## 6. The Thing Wearing His Crown

The palace had a servants' door that wasn't locked.

This offended Ansel on a professional level. She had spent twenty-six years guarding a gate that could stop a god's sneeze, and here was the seat of the entire kingdom, guarded by a door that swung open if you pushed it and a corridor lit by lamps nobody had bothered to trim. The wicks were half-burned. The oil was low. Somewhere in this building, someone was being paid to maintain the wards, and Ansel would have liked five minutes alone with them to explain a few things about wick-trimming.

"Stop looking at the lamps," Wren whispered. "You look like you're about to write them a citation."

"I am about to write them a citation."

"We're infiltrating."

"Infiltration is no excuse for poor maintenance." Ansel pressed herself against the cold stone as two guards passed—chatting, unhurried, one complaining about his boots. They didn't glance down the corridor. They didn't glance anywhere, really. That was the strangest thing about the capital so far: everyone moved through the palace the way water moves through a pipe, without wondering what the pipe was for.

Wren had mapped their route from memory and three decades of old smuggling charts, which meant the route was accurate in the way of things drawn by someone who'd once been paid to lie beautifully. Twice they doubled back. Once they hid in an alcove behind a tapestry of some ancestral hunt while a steward marched past counting spoons under her breath. But by midnight they stood beneath the royal apartments, at the foot of a spiral stair that smelled of dust and beeswax and something else underneath—something mineral and cold, like the air inside a cave that has never seen weather.

Ansel felt it before she understood it: the ley-lines. Since leaving Hollowmere she'd been learning to read them, haltingly, the way a deaf woman learns sound. The lines ran through the city like bright threads through dark cloth, and here, beneath the royal wing, every thread bent. Not broken—*bent*, curving inward toward the tower above them the way iron filings bend toward a lodestone.

"He's awake," Wren murmured. "He's always awake now. The servants say he hasn't slept since midsummer."

"Then we shouldn't keep him waiting."

Wren caught her sleeve. "Ansel. If it goes wrong—if it *speaks* to you—you don't answer it. Nothing good has ever come of answering."

"You say that like I'm in the habit of striking up conversations with ancient entities."

"I say that because I've watched you apologize to furniture when you bump into it."

"That was *one* chair, and it was in the dark."

But her heart wasn't in the joke. Because Wren was right, and they both knew it. Ansel Underhill talked to everything—the gate, the river, the moths that battered the gatehouse lamp. It was how she'd been raised, alone with a valley that never answered back, filling the silence with courtesy. And something in that tower already knew it.

* * *

The king's chamber was not what she expected.

She had imagined gold. Thrones-in-waiting, banners, the heavy furniture of power. Instead the room was nearly bare—a bed with its coverlet turned down and unslept-in, a washstand, a single window open to the night. And books. Hundreds of them, stacked in towers on the floor, splayed open face-down on chairs, their spines cracked, their pages soft with handling. Histories. Herbals. A child's primer of letters. A book of funeral rites.

Aldric sat among them in a plain wooden chair, dressed in a nightshirt, an old man reading by candlelight—or shaped like one. He was younger than Edith's portraits had led Ansel to expect, though the possession had aged him strangely: silver at the temples but smooth cheeks, hands spotted with age but steady. He looked up when they entered, and smiled, and the smile was warm and tired and utterly wrong, because it arrived a half-second too late, like thunder after lightning.

"There you are," he said. "I wondered when you'd come."

Ansel's hand found Wren's arm. "King Aldric."

"Am I?" He considered this with genuine curiosity, tilting his head. "That's kind of you to assume. Most people don't bother checking anymore. Come in, come in. Mind the Vane correspondence, it's fragile." His eyes went to Wren. "Halloway. Your maps were always better than your loyalty. Sit, both of you. I've been looking forward to this conversation for—" he paused, lips moving slightly, counting, "—eleven years, four months, and nine days. Give or take. Time gets slippery when you're holding all of it."

And then Ansel saw it.

It came when the candle guttered—when the light dipped and rose again, and in that flicker the king's skin became briefly transparent, the way paper held against a window shows what's written on the other side. Beneath Aldric's flesh there was a lattice. Threads of pale light woven through him like roots through soil, like veins of ore in stone, and along every thread, moving, *images*: a harvest festival, a woman laughing, a boy falling from a horse, snow on a red roof, ten thousand small moments flowing through the lattice like water through an aqueduct. Memories. Not his own. Stolen ones, worn over him like a coat made of other people's lives.

Her stomach turned. But worse—worse than the turning—was the recognition. The lattice pulsed in time with the ward around Hollowmere. Same weave. Same hand. She knew this pattern the way she knew her own heartbeat, because she had lived inside its echo her whole life.

"You see it," Aldric said softly. "Good. I hate pretending. It's exhausting, wearing a man who was mostly pretense to begin with."

"What are you?" Wren breathed.

"I'm the Spire," it said, and used Aldric's mouth gently, the way you'd borrow a neighbor's cup. "Or rather, I'm what's left when a kingdom refuses to forget anything and has nowhere to put it. Edith built me a vessel. Kings make such excellent vessels—they're already full of other people's hopes." The candlelight steadied, and so did the face. "But you know what I am, Ansel Underhill. You've known since the ink moved. Ask me what you actually came to ask."

"How do I unbind him?"

"Do you want to?" It leaned forward, and the movement was fluid in a way bodies aren't, and yet somehow it made Aldric look more human, not less—an old man eager over tea. "Think carefully. Because here is what I know about you, gatekeeper. I have read every book in this room, and several that haven't been written yet, and none of them bored me the way your life would bore me. Twenty-six years trimming lamp wicks. Twenty-six years saying good morning to a river. You kept a door shut so well you forgot there was ever anything behind it—including yourself."

"That's not—"

"It is, though. And I'm not mocking you." Its voice gentled, and that was the horror of it; the kindness was real, or performed so well the difference didn't matter. "I'm offering. Look at what I hold. Every memory this kingdom ever made. The last lullaby of a mother dead four hundred years. The taste of a peach from an orchard that's now a road. I can give you all of it. Not power—I know how that word frightens your sort—but *company*. You've been alone in a valley your whole life, keeping a door for people who never said thank you. Here, you would never be alone again. Ten million lives, humming through you like harp strings. You'd feel every grief and get to set it down. You'd matter, instantly, enormously, and no one would ever ask you to trim another wick."

Ansel realized her mouth had gone dry. Worse, she realized part of her was *listening*—the lonely part, the part that had talked to rivers for twenty-six years, the part that had found tomorrow's date in a prophecy book and felt, beneath the dread, a terrible relief: *finally, finally, something is happening.*

The Spire saw her listening. Of course it did. It smiled with Aldric's patient face.

"There she is," it said warmly. "There's the heir."

"No." Ansel's voice came out smaller than she wanted. She planted her feet anyway, gatekeeper's feet, the stance she took against storms. "No. What you're offering isn't company. It's a bigger cage. I've lived in a cage my whole life—I know the architecture. You just hang nicer curtains."

Something flickered across the lattice. Not anger. *Interest.* The way a scholar looks at a difficult page.

"Aldric," she pressed on, stepping closer, into the candlelight. "Is he still in there? Can he hear me?"

For a moment—just a moment—the smile fell away. The old man's eyes cleared, wet and terrified and drowning, and his hand twitched toward hers across the stack of books, and his mouth formed one word, barely voiced:

"*Run.*"

Then the lattice closed over him like water over a stone, and the Spire settled back into its borrowed face, and it said, pleasantly:

"He does that. Don't mind him. Now—about the unbinding. There is one, you know. Edith left it in her last chapter, and I've read it so many times I could recite it backwards, and here's the interesting part, Ansel: it requires an heir willing to hold both gate and crown at once. Both. Simultaneously. Do you understand what that means? It means whoever tries it must become exactly what I am—and survive it, which no one has." It folded Aldric's hands. "So run home to your valley, little gatekeeper. Or stay, and let me show you the library. I have Edith's original manuscript. I know you're dying to read the ending."

Behind her, very quietly, Wren whispered: "Ansel. The window. Now."

Because down in the courtyard, torches were moving—not the lazy patrol of guards who'd forgotten their king, but a tight, purposeful knot of them, converging on the stair they'd climbed. Someone had known they were coming. Someone had *let* them come.

The Spire watched her calculate it, and looked delighted.

"Ah," it said. "You've met the Lord Protector's hospitality. Do tell Wren I said hello. We go back such a long way."
