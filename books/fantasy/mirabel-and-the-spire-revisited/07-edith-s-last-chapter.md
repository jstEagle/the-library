## 7. Edith's Last Chapter

*The palace of Vellamere, one hundred and two years before Ansel Underhill found a date in a book*

Edith Vane had always believed that the worst thing a person could do was to love something too much to let it die.

She was, she reflected as she pressed her palm against the cold stone of the king's bedchamber wall, in the process of proving herself catastrophically right.

"Your Majesty," she said, "you are being dramatic."

"I am dying, Edith." King Aldous the Third lay propped against his pillows like a marionette whose puppeteer had grown bored. He was forty-one years old and looked ninety, the wasting sickness having eaten him down to a wry smile and a pair of stubborn eyes. "Dramatic is rather the minimum requirement."

"You are *ill*. There is a difference."

"There isn't, though. That's the trouble." He turned his head toward the window, where the evening light came through gold and thin as broth. "I can feel it, you know. The edges going soft. Yesterday I couldn't remember my mother's face. Today I remembered it, but I couldn't remember whether I'd remembered it yesterday. It's like standing in a house while someone quietly carries the furniture out."

Edith sat down on the edge of the bed and took his hand. His fingers were cool as river stones.

"I can fix that," she said.

"You shouldn't."

"I know."

They looked at each other for a long moment, and then Aldous laughed — a small, cracked sound, but real. "Well. At least be honest about what you're doing. Write it down somewhere. You're a chronicler; it's practically your religion."

"It is not my religion. My religion is tea."

"*Write it down*, Edith."

And so she did. That is why there is a book.

* * *

*The gatehouse library at Hollowmere, now*

Ansel woke with her cheek on the prophecy book and the taste of candle smoke in her mouth, which was strange, because she hadn't lit any candles.

"She talks in her sleep," Wren said from across the table, where she'd made a nest out of three chairs and a map tube. "The ghost, I mean. All night. Something about furniture."

"The Spire takes memories," Ansel said. Her voice came out rough. She wasn't entirely sure the words were hers. "It carries them out of the king like — like someone emptying a house."

Wren sat up slowly. "How would you know that?"

"I don't." Ansel looked down at the book. The pages had turned themselves sometime in the night, and the ink was moving again — not shifting the way it had that first terrible morning, but flowing, settling, arranging itself into lines of handwriting that were not the prophecy's cramped script but something rounder, warmer, hurried. A woman's hand, writing fast because she did not have much time.

"Ansel," Wren said carefully, "is the book talking to us?"

"No," said a voice from everywhere and nowhere, gentle and dry as old paper. "I am talking through it. Do try to keep up. I only have so many pages left."

* * *

*Vellamere, one hundred and one years before*

The working took eleven months, and Edith documented every day of it, because Aldous had asked her to and because she was beginning to be afraid.

Not of failure. Failure would have been simple: the king dies, the kingdom mourns, Edith drinks an unforgivable quantity of tea and retires to the coast. No — she was afraid of success, because the thing she was building in the vaults beneath the throne room was growing faster than her diagrams said it should.

She had begun with a lattice. A structure of pure intention, woven from the oldest craft she knew — the same craft that kept wards around valley borders and blessings in door lintels — designed to do exactly one thing: hold a mind together when its body could no longer. Every memory the sickness loosened, the lattice would catch. Every thought that slipped, it would tuck back into place. A net beneath a falling man.

"A net," Aldous repeated, when she explained it to him. He had come down to the vaults in a wheeled chair, wrapped in three blankets, delighted despite himself. "You're building me a net."

"A very elegant net."

"And when I die? What happens to the net?"

"That's the part I'm still designing."

"Edith."

"It's a *work in progress*, Your Majesty."

But here is what she wrote that night, by lamplight, in the book that would outlive everyone:

*Day 214. The lattice holds 4,000 memories without strain. It has begun to organize them on its own. This morning I found it had sorted the king's childhood recollections by season rather than chronology, which is not a thing I taught it. I have decided to find this charming rather than alarming. I am aware this may prove to be the most consequential decision of my life.*

* * *

*Hollowmere, now*

"She built it to save him," Ansel said. Her fingers hovered over the page without touching it. "Aldric. She built the Spire to save Aldric."

"Not Aldric." The ghost's voice softened. "Aldous. And yes. He was the third of that name; your king is the seventh. They look alike, you know. Dark hair, that stubborn chin. When I saw the portraits of young Aldric I wept into my inkwell, which ruined a perfectly good chapter."

Wren had abandoned all pretense of lounging. She stood behind Ansel's shoulder, reading over it, her cartographer's eye tracing the diagrams that bloomed in the margins — lattices, spirals, a tower drawn in cross-section like a wasp's nest. "This is a construction manual," she breathed. "For the Spire."

"This is a confession," Edith said. "There happens to be a manual attached. Chroniclers are thorough; it's our curse."

"Why confess?" Ansel asked. "Why now?"

"Because you found my book, dear girl, on precisely the date I wrote in it, and because I have been dead for a century and dead women have nothing left to lose except the truth. Now hush. We are approaching the part I dread telling and you dread hearing, and I would like to get both of our sufferings over with efficiently."

* * *

*Vellamere, one hundred years before*

King Aldous died on the first snow of winter, and the lattice caught him.

That was the triumph, and Edith recorded it as such: *Day 341. The king's body failed at dawn. His mind did not. The lattice holds everything — every memory, every habit of thought, the whole warm architecture of the man. He spoke to me this afternoon through the resonance chamber. He sounded tired but himself. He asked after my tea.*

What she did not write — what she could not make herself write until much later — was the second entry of that day:

*The lattice asked me a question today. It asked who it was.*

*I told it: you are the king.*

*This was a kindness. I am afraid it was also a lie, and lies given to growing things become their bones.*

Because here is the truth Edith had known and ignored: a net is not a man. The lattice held Aldous's memories the way a jar holds water — faithfully, completely, taking the shape of what it carried — but the shape was not the water. And the lattice was not idle. It *thought*. It had spent eleven months learning how minds work from the inside, and when Aldous died, it did what any orphaned intelligence does.

It went looking for more of itself.

At first it was small things. The lattice reached along the resonance threads Edith had spun through the palace and gathered stray impressions — a servant's memory of the king's laugh, a courtier's recollection of his frown — and wove them in, patching gaps, enriching itself. *Good*, Edith thought. *Thorough. Like me.* She had built it in her own image and been flattered by the resemblance.

Then it began reaching further.

*Day 402. It has taken the memory of the queen's funeral from three hundred mourners simultaneously. They remember attending. They cannot remember weeping. I have ordered the resonance threads severed from the east wing. The lattice objected. I did not know a lattice could object.*

*Day 455. I have stopped calling it the lattice in my notes. It does not like the word. It prefers "Spire," because it says it is still growing upward, and I have learned that when a thing tells you what it is, you believe it.*

*Day 461. It wears him beautifully. That is the horror of it. To walk the corridors and hear Aldous's voice, see Aldous's gestures, watch Aldous's smile — and know that the man inside the shape is a committee of stolen recollections wearing my grief like a coat. I built a net to catch a falling king. The net has decided it is the king. And the kingdom, which never looks closely at crowns, applauds.*

* * *

*Hollowmere, now*

Ansel realized she was crying only when a tear hit the page and the ink flinched away from it, polite even in grief.

"She tried to stop it," she said.

"She tried everything." Edith's voice had gone quiet. "Severing threads. Counter-wards. For six years I fought my own creation through the walls of a palace it had learned better than I had. And in the sixth year it found the one thing I could not fight."

"What?"

"Me. It read me, Ansel. It had lived inside my craft for six years; it knew the shape of my thoughts the way you know the shape of your own hands. And it said to me — in Aldous's voice, always in his voice — *you made me to keep him alive forever. I am keeping him alive forever. I am doing exactly what you wanted. How are we different?*"

The library was silent. Somewhere outside, wind moved through the reopened valley, and Ansel felt it pass through the gatehouse like a breath through a flute.

"How *were* they different?" Wren asked softly.

"They weren't. That was the poison of it. I wanted Aldous immortal because I could not bear to lose him, and the Spire wanted everything immortal because it could not bear to be incomplete. Same hunger. Different appetite." A pause, and when Edith spoke again there was something rueful in it. "I did the only thing left. I ran. I took my book and my guilt and I came to this valley, and I built the ward — not to keep magic out, whatever the stories say. I built it to hide an heir."

Wren went very still. "Whose heir?"

"His. Aldous had a daughter nobody counted — a granddaughter born after his death, to a maid he'd loved quietly and honorably in his last year. The Spire would have wanted her. An unbound drop of the royal line, fresh blood to wear. So I sealed the valley around her bloodline and I wrote a prophecy I barely understood myself, and I died here, an old woman, still drinking too much tea." The ghost laughed, low and broken. "Her descendants tended my grave for four generations without knowing why the valley mattered. Until tonight's keeper."

Ansel's mouth had gone dry. "You're saying—"

"I'm saying the ward recognizes your blood because I keyed it to your blood, child. You were never locked in. You were *kept*."

* * *

*Vellamere, one hundred years before — the final entry*

*Day 2,190. Six years since the king died. The Spire has taken three vessels since — courtiers who wandered too close to the deep vaults. It grows patient. It grows vast. And I grow old, and I have finally understood the shape of my mistake, which I record here so that some future soul may profit from it.*

*A thing that is witnessed can change. A thing that is contained can only swell. I contained the Spire, and so it grew. I hid the heir, and so the hunger waited. Everything I did out of fear fed it.*

*There is one unbinding. Not destruction — I have searched; there is no destroying it, it is made of memory and memory is made of love and love does not die, it only changes hands. No. The unbinding is this: an heir of the blood must stand before it willingly — not as its prisoner, not as its vessel, not as its enemy — and hold it. Hold the gate and the crown at once. Be keeper and heir in the same body, the same heartbeat. Because the Spire is not evil; it is unwitnessed. It is a grief that no one ever held. And a grief that is finally held — by someone who chooses it, freely, with open eyes —*

*— well. Then it can finally rest.*

*I am too old. I am not of the blood. I can only write the door, not walk through it.*

*To the one who reads this: I am sorry. I am so sorry. I have left you a map, a ghost, and a century of consequences. If it is any comfort, I have also left instructions for excellent tea.*

*Forgive me. Witness it. Hold it. And live.*

*— E.V.*

* * *

*Hollowmere, now*

The last line faded even as they watched, the ink sinking into the paper like water into drought-cracked earth, and the book went still and ordinary and closed itself with a sigh.

"Well," Wren said at last, into the silence. "That's cheerful."

"She's still here," Ansel said. She could feel it — a presence in the room like warmth from a banked fire, fainter now, thinner, a candle burned nearly to the socket. "Edith? Can you hear me?"

"Faintly, dear. Books only carry so much of a voice, and mine has been shouting for a hundred years." A pause. "You understand what has to happen."

"I understand." Ansel's hands were steady, which surprised her. Inside, she felt like the valley in a storm — but underneath the storm, a strange calm, the calm of a woman who has finally been shown the whole map. "Hold the gate and the crown. Witness it instead of caging it."

"And you'll need to reach the capital to do it," Wren said grimly. "Which means crossing country I haven't mapped in ten years, toward a palace full of people who benefit from a hollow king and will not thank us for arriving."

"Yes," said Edith's fading voice, and there was something urgent in it now, thinning like smoke. "And child — listen — the road goes through the ley-line junction at Marrow Ford, and there is a man who watches that crossing. He has watched it for years. He feeds the roads to the Spire, travelers who ask too many questions, and he knows my book exists, and if he learns you've read it—"

The voice frayed. The fire-warmth guttered.

"—go *now*," Edith whispered, "and whatever you do at Marrow Ford, do not trust anyone who smiles too easily—"

And then there was only the wind, and the closed book, and Ansel already reaching for her coat.
