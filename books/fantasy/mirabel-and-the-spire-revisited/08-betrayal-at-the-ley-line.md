## 8. Betrayal at the Ley-Line

Wren Halloway had been betrayed exactly twice in her life, and both times she'd seen it coming. The first was her old business partner, Tam, who had sold her maps to the Lord Protector's men and then had the decency to look ashamed about it. The second was herself, on the night she'd signed her name to a commission she should have burned instead.

So when the third betrayal arrived—warm bread, a friendly fire, and the smell of roasting partridge on the banks of the Silvermere ley-line—she didn't recognize it at all.

"Smells like home," Ansel said, crouched by the fire, turning a spit with the careful concentration of someone who had spent twenty years perfecting one skill and was now applying it to dinner. "Well. It smells like gatehouse stew, which is not the same thing, but it's close enough that my chest hurts a little."

"That's either homesickness or heartburn," Wren said. "Hard to tell with you."

They had made good time since the capital. Three days east along the old survey roads, following the ley-lines as they braided and split beneath the earth like roots of some enormous patient tree. Ansel had gotten frighteningly good at reading them. She'd point at an unremarkable stretch of meadow and say, *there's a knot under there, something old*, and when they dug—or when they simply walked around it, which was usually wiser—there it would be: a standing stone, a buried arch, a well that hummed if you put your ear to it.

Tonight the lines ran quiet. That should have been the first warning. Ley-lines were never quiet this close to Silvermere; the confluence there was one of the loudest in the kingdom, a place where the world's magic pooled like rainwater in a cellar. But the evening was still, the birds were singing, and Wren was tired enough to mistake peace for safety.

The rider came up the road at dusk. A single figure on a gray horse, traveling cloak, no livery, leading a pack mule loaded with what looked like surveying equipment.

"Fellow cartographer," Wren said, standing, shading her eyes. "Or a very committed bandit."

The figure dismounted at the edge of their camp, pushed back a hood, and smiled a smile Wren knew down to its fillings.

"Master Halloway," said Corvus Dane, Lord Protector of the Realm, dressed as modestly as a man could dress while still somehow radiating authority from every seam. "You're a difficult woman to find. I've missed you terribly."

Every muscle in Wren's body performed the same maneuver simultaneously: it locked. Behind her, she heard Ansel set down the spit.

"My lord," Wren managed. Her voice came out steady, which she counted as her single professional triumph of the evening. "What an unexpected honor."

"Yes, everyone says that." Dane warmed his hands at their fire as though he owned it, which, in a sense, he did—he owned most things within a hundred miles, including, Wren now understood with sickening clarity, the road they were sitting beside. "May I? I find camp food tastes better than anything my kitchens produce. Probably the fear."

He took a partridge leg. He actually took a partridge leg. And he ate it while looking at Ansel with the fond, appraising expression of a man examining a horse he intended to buy.

"You know," he said conversationally, "I've been feeding it for eleven years. Carefully. Thoughtfully. A beggar here, a debtor there, always the ones nobody would miss—the Spire has preferences, you see. It likes minds with a little texture. Hollow people taste like nothing." He licked a thumb. "But it's never been satisfied. Like a man eating broth when what he wants is bread. And then—" he gestured at Ansel with the bone, delighted— "*you* walked out of a valley that hasn't opened in a hundred years, wearing Edith Vane's face and carrying her book, and suddenly the whole meal plan changes."

Ansel rose slowly. Wren watched her do it and thought, absurdly, *she stands like a gatekeeper even now. Feet planted. Weight low.*

"I don't understand," Ansel said.

"No," said Dane kindly. "You wouldn't. You were raised behind a ward, poor thing. Let me educate you. The king you're so desperate to save—I admire the mission, truly, it's very noble—is not imprisoned by the Spire. He is *sustained* by it. Remove the thing wearing him and Aldric dies in the space of a breath. Eleven years ago his mind began to fail—grief, mostly, the queen's death—and I went looking for a solution, because a dead king means regents, and regents mean civil war, and I have seen civil war." His face did something almost human. Almost grief. "Edith Vane left notes. Incomplete ones. I finished them. I built the lattice myself, and I have kept it fed ever since, and the realm has known ten years of peace. So before you start calling me monster, girl, ask yourself what you'd have done."

"You could have looked for another way," Ansel said quietly.

"I did. For years. There wasn't one." He tossed the bone into the fire. "And then your cartographer friend found something interesting in the archives, didn't she. Go on, Wren. Tell her what you found. Tell her why you really came to Hollowmere."

And here it was. Here was the moment Wren had been dreading since the day she'd knocked on a gatehouse door with a lie folded up in her satchel like a letter she couldn't bear to post.

She had told Ansel the truth, eventually. Most of it. She'd told her about the commission, about being paid to find the lost heir, about how she'd torn up the contract and stayed anyway. What she had *not* told her—what she had burned rather than tell her—was the second page. The clause. *Should the heir be located, deliver her to the Lord Protector's agents for assessment.* She'd told herself the clause was dead paper, that tearing the contract killed it.

Corvus Dane held out his hand, palm up, across their little fire.

"The original, Master Halloway. You kept a copy. You always keep copies—it's why I hired you."

Wren's hand moved toward her coat before her brain could stop it. That was the humiliation of it: her body remembered being his employee better than her heart remembered leaving.

"Wren." Ansel's voice. Not loud. Just *there*, the way the valley's silence used to be there. "Is that true?"

"There's a clause," Wren said. Her mouth had gone dry as old vellum. "On the second page. I tore it up. I burned it. Ansel, I swear to you, the day I understood what you were—not what you were worth, what you *were*—I chose you over him. I've been choosing you every day since."

"Have you?" Dane asked pleasantly. "Then you won't mind telling me why you led her along the Silvermere road. The only confluence in the kingdom deep enough to open a vessel-channel. You mapped these lines yourself, Halloway. You knew exactly where you were walking her."

The bottom dropped out of Wren's stomach.

She hadn't. Had she? She'd chosen the Silvermere route because it was fast, because the inns were good, because—

Because the map had been in her hand, and her hand had been his hand once, trained by him, and maybe some part of her that she'd never examined had still been working the old commission all along, walking the lost heir toward the assessment like a compass needle that hadn't noticed its north had changed.

"I didn't," she whispered. And then, honestly, which was worse: "I don't know."

"Oh, I believe you," Dane said, and that was the cruelest thing—he sounded like he meant it. "That's the tragedy of conscience, Master Halloway. It leaks. Now."

The campfire went out. Not guttered—*went out*, snuffed like a candle pinched between wet fingers, and the dark that replaced it was the wrong kind of dark, the kind with structure in it, the kind that leaned.

Men came out of the treeline where no men had been. Six of them, maybe eight; Wren stopped counting when she felt the cold circle of a blade-guard against her spine. They wore no colors. They didn't need to. Everything in the realm wore his colors eventually.

Ansel didn't run. Wren watched her not run—watched her stand in the dark with her chin up and her hands half-raised, feeling for ley-lines that weren't singing anymore, because Dane's people had spent three days burying iron along this stretch of road, and Wren realized she'd known that too, somewhere, in the trained part of her, and had said nothing.

"You can't take her by force," Wren said loudly, to cover the shaking. "The unbinding needs consent. Your own notes say so—you wrote it in the margin of folio nineteen, I *catalogued* it—"

"And there you are," Dane said warmly, "confirming why I hired you. Yes. Consent. Which is why we won't force anything." He stepped closer to Ansel, and his voice gentled into something genuinely terrible, because it was sincere. "Girl. Gatekeeper. Listen to me. The king you met in that palace—the man who spoke kindly to you—that *was* Aldric, what's left of him, and he is nine-tenths gone. If you walk away tonight, he dies within the year, slowly, from the inside, and everything he was dissolves into that lattice forever. But if you come with me willingly—if you let the Spire complete itself through you, keeper and heir together—then Aldric's memories live on. All of them. Held safe in something that will never die. Isn't that what you wanted? To keep things?"

Wren saw it land. Saw the exact spot in Ansel's face where the offer found purchase—the same spot, Wren realized, where the Spire's own voice had found purchase in the throne room. *It offers her a place at its side,* Edith had written, *and the horror is that the offer is honest.*

"You're lying," Ansel said. But her voice wavered on the word, and Dane heard it, and smiled.

"Am I? Ask your cartographer. She's read the notes. She knows more about the lattice than anyone alive except me." He turned to Wren, and held out his hand again, palm up, patient as stone. "Well, Master Halloway. One last chance to be useful. Tell her I'm lying. Convince her. You're very convincing—it's in your file."

The fire pit smoked. The soldiers waited. Somewhere far off, muffled under yards of buried iron, the ley-line hummed like a struck bell that couldn't quite finish ringing.

And Wren Halloway, disgraced royal cartographer, betrayer twice-over and possibly three, looked at the girl she had led here and understood that whatever she said next would decide whether she was a traitor or merely a fool.

"Ansel," she said. "Folio nineteen. Read it yourself. Don't take my word. Don't take *his*. Never take mine." Her voice cracked clean down the middle. "But if you remember one thing I've ever told you—remember that I stayed. Whatever else is true, I *stayed*."

Dane sighed, theatrical, almost fond. "Bind her hands. Gently. And someone fetch the lady's book—we'll want her reading material for the road."

As they closed in, Ansel's eyes found Wren's through the dark, and Wren saw in them not hatred, which she could have borne, but a question:

*Did you stay? Or did you just lead me here slowly?*

And Wren had no answer that wasn't both.

* * *

They marched them through the night toward Silvermere, where the ground hummed under the iron like a throat clearing before it spoke, and where, at the bottom of the deepest well of magic in the kingdom, something ancient and patient and hungry had already begun, gently, to make room.
