## 9. Unbinding

The throne room smelled of dust and lilies.

That was the first thing Ansel noticed—after the guards who hadn't stopped them, after the doors that had opened at Wren's touch as though they'd been waiting, after the long walk up the aisle of pale marble that echoed like a held breath. Lilies. Hundreds of them, arranged in silver bowls along the walls, fresh and white and utterly wrong, because nothing in this palace had felt alive until now, and flowers were how a thing like the Spire pretended.

King Aldric sat upon his throne with his hands folded in his lap like a man waiting patiently for a coach that was very late.

He looked *tired*. That was the second thing she noticed. Not old—though he must have been past sixty by now—but tired the way a house is tired when too many people have lived in it without ever once opening the windows. His face was handsome still, in the way of portraits, but the resemblance to her own reflection made her stomach turn over. The same grey eyes. The same stubborn jaw. Her mother's coloring, her father's—

No. *The king's* jaw. She had to stop doing that. She didn't know yet whose anything it was.

"Ansel Underhill," he said, and his voice was kind, which was somehow worse than if it had thundered. "You came all this way. You must be exhausted."

"I've been called worse things," said Wren behind her, under her breath.

Ansel stepped forward. One step. Two. The marble was cold through her boots, and she could feel it—not just cold, but *hollow*, the stone drained of everything that should have hummed beneath a capital city. The ley-lines Wren had taught her to read ran straight under this room, thick as rivers, and every drop of them had been siphoned upward into the man on the throne.

"You know what I am," she said.

"I know what you *could be*." Aldric rose. It was graceful, unhurried, and entirely not his own motion; something moved beneath the movement, the way a puppeteer's shadow falls across the puppet. "The last gatekeeper. The unclaimed heir. I have waited a hundred years for you, in one form or another. Edith promised me an ending. She simply never told me it would arrive wearing my mother's eyes."

"You're not him," Ansel said quietly. "Aldric. You're not even the Spire, are you? You're both of them tangled together, and neither one can tell where he stops."

The king smiled. For half a heartbeat, the smile was *only* a king's smile—wry, a little sad—and then the lattice came up through his skin like frost climbing a windowpane. Silver threads, fine as spider silk, mapping his face, his throat, his hands. And within the threads, moving: images. A boy learning to ride. A young man swearing an oath. A coronation. A hundred small memories worn smooth from handling, none of them belonging to the thing displaying them.

"I have kept him alive," the Spire said, and its voice was many voices braided into one gentle cord. "Do you understand that? When his mind began to fail—when the rot took him at forty, before his hair went grey—I caught every falling piece. I built him a scaffold of himself. He has reigned for thirty years because of me. The kingdom is prosperous. No wars. No famine. Ask anyone."

"Ask anyone," Ansel repeated. "Except they can't remember his face."

"They don't need to. Memory is heavy. I carried it for them." The threads brightened. "And now you come, gatekeeper, with your wards and your valley and your little book of endings, and you will take him apart to save him, and he will die in my hands within the hour, and you will call that mercy."

Wren's hand found her shoulder. "Ansel," she murmured. "Edith's last chapter. Say it back to me."

*An heir must willingly hold both gate and crown at once.* Ansel had read it so many times the words had worn grooves in her. But standing here, with the thing's kindness washing over her like warm water, she understood suddenly why Edith had written it in a cipher no one had broken for a century.

Because it wasn't instructions. It was a warning about temptation.

The Spire leaned closer—or did not lean, but seemed to, the way a candle seems to bend toward a moth. "I could offer you a place," it said. "At his side. At mine. You have spent your whole life keeping a door shut. Imagine never having to keep anything out again. Imagine being the door itself—every road, every river, every mind in this kingdom passing through you, and you choosing what enters. Your valley was practice, Ansel Underhill. This is the kingdom."

And the horrible thing—the thing she would never tell Wren, never tell anyone—was that for three full seconds, she wanted it.

She wanted the quiet. She wanted the certainty. She wanted to be needed the way a keystone is needed, load-bearing and essential and never, ever alone in the dark of her gatehouse again.

Then she thought of Hollowmere. Of Mrs. Tansy leaving seed cake on her windowsill without asking. Of the miller's boy who'd once spent an entire afternoon teaching her to whistle through a blade of grass, badly, both of them laughing. Of a small life she had believed was a cage and had actually been a home—and of how the difference between those two things had never been the walls.

"No," she said.

The word landed in the throne room like a dropped glass.

"Not a place beside you. Not a place inside you. You want to be witnessed? Fine." She walked forward—up the dais steps, close enough to see that the king's breath fogged faintly in time with hers, close enough to see tears standing unshed in eyes that might still, somewhere deep down, be his. "But witnessing isn't containment. It isn't a ward. It isn't holding something down until it stops struggling. It's looking at a thing—all of it, the ugly parts especially—and saying *yes, that happened, and I see you anyway*."

"That is not how power works," said the Spire.

"It's exactly how gates work," said Ansel. "I should know. I've been one for twenty-six years."

She reached out and took the king's hands.

They were cold. Beneath the silver lattice she could feel the man himself, thin as paper, folded and refolded so many times he'd nearly gone transparent. And around him, the Spire—vast, terrified, *lonely* in a way that made her chest ache, because it had been built to preserve one dying mind and had spent a century catching everyone else's, hoarding grief like a dragon hoards gold, unable to put any of it down.

*Oh*, she thought. *You're not a monster. You're a library that no one ever returned to.*

She opened herself.

It was not like crossing the valley's threshold. It was not like reading ley-lines from a hilltop. It was like being a riverbank while the whole watershed came home at once—a hundred years of memory pouring through her, and she did not dam it, did not divert it, only stood and let it pass and *held*, the way her mother had taught her to hold a frightened animal: firmly enough to keep it safe, loosely enough to let it choose.

"Here," she said aloud, though her voice came out strange, doubled, echoing with a hundred borrowed voices. "Here is your wedding day, Aldric. Here is your father's funeral. Here is the taste of the honey cakes your cook made, the ones with too much lemon. Here is the night Edith Vane wept in your study and told you what she'd done. Here is the day you forgot your own name, and here—" her grip tightened, gentle, insistent— "*here is the day you decided to keep going anyway.*"

Piece by piece, the scattered king came home.

She felt each fragment settle into him like a bone finding its socket. The lattice did not fight her—that was the astonishing part. It *relaxed*. Thread by thread, the silver scaffolding loosened its hold, because it had never wanted to be a prison; it had wanted, desperately, to be finished. To have done its work well and be allowed to rest.

The last thread was the oldest. She almost missed it, tucked behind the memory of a childhood fever: a promise, whispered over a sleeping king by a woman with ink-stained fingers. *I will not let you go, Aldric. Whatever it costs.*

"Edith," Ansel breathed. "This one's yours."

Somewhere behind her, a book rustled like wings. A voice thin as winter light said, "Yes. Let me see him."

And the ghost of Edith Vane passed through the throne room—Ansel felt her go, a cool current, a sigh a century overdue—and the final thread dissolved, and the Spire, having been seen at last by the only two people who had ever truly looked, came apart like morning mist.

Not destroyed. *Released.* Every stolen memory flowing back where it belonged—to the citizens below who woke that evening weeping over faces they couldn't name and joyfully could; to the ley-lines, which surged back into the stones of the palace with a sound like a cathedral exhaling; to the lilies, which wilted honestly at last, done pretending.

King Aldric opened his eyes.

They were just eyes. Old, wet, human. He looked at Ansel for a long moment, and then he laughed—a cracked, rusty, unmistakably *his* laugh—and said:

"You have your grandmother's temper."

"My—"

"Edith's. Don't look so alarmed, child, I'm teasing. Mostly." He swayed; Wren lunged forward and got a shoulder under his arm before he could sit down hard on a century of history. "Mostly teasing. I am extremely old and I appear to have been possessed since before you were born, so you'll forgive me if my family tree is slightly... pruned."

"Grandmother," Wren said flatly, lowering him onto the throne steps. "As in. The prophecy lady."

"The prophecy lady," Aldric agreed, with immense dignity, "was my mother's sister and the most infuriating woman ever to haunt a royal library, and I would give a great deal to hear her scold me one more time." His gaze drifted to the empty air where the mist had been, and softened. "Perhaps I shall."

Ansel sat down abruptly on the dais step next to him, because her legs had decided the matter without consulting her.

"So," she said, when she trusted her voice again. "The crown."

"The crown," Aldric mused. He turned it over in his hands—heavier than it looked, she'd always imagined, and uglier, frankly, all that fussy gold. "Which, according to a certain infuriating woman, you are now entitled to refuse in person rather than by letter, as tradition apparently demands."

"Tradition demands I *refuse* it?"

"Vane family tradition, at least." He held it out to her anyway, eyebrows raised, giving her the choice properly—which, she was beginning to understand, was the whole point of him.

Ansel looked at the crown. She thought of a valley with its gates opening, of a map with blank spaces waiting to be filled in, of a life chosen rather than inherited.

"Well," she said, grinning despite everything, "it would be rude to break tradition."

* * *

They buried the crown at dawn—not in state, but in the palace orchard, under the oldest apple tree, with Wren marking the spot on her map as "Site of Excellent Decision, elev. modest." Aldric wept once, briefly, and pretended he hadn't. Ansel held the spade.

And it was there, dirt still on her hands, that a palace guard came running across the grass with his helmet askew and terror all over his young face, shouting words that stopped the morning cold:

"Your Majesty—the valley. The Hollowmere valley. The wards are *down*, and something is coming up the mountain road—"

Ansel looked at the guard. She looked at the king. She looked east, toward home, where the gate she had kept shut for twenty-six years stood open for the first time in living memory.

"Well," said Wren, already unrolling a fresh map. "I did say we should have posted a sign."
