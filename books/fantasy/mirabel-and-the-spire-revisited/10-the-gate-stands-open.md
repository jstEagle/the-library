## 10. The Gate Stands Open

The morning after the world changed, Ansel woke in a bed that was not hers, in a palace that smelled of beeswax and old stone, and lay still for a long while, listening.

Magic had a sound. She hadn't known that before she left Hollowmere. It sounded like distant water, like wind through wheat, like a choir humming one note just below hearing. For twenty-six years she had lived on the silent side of a wall, and now the wall was down and the whole world sang to her, and she found—to her considerable surprise—that she did not hate it.

She got up, dressed in borrowed clothes that were too fine by half (the seamstress had insisted; Ansel had drawn the line at embroidery), and went looking for breakfast.

She found King Aldric in the kitchens.

Not the throne room, not the council chamber, not any of the dozen places a restored king was presumably meant to be. The kitchens, sleeves rolled to the elbow, arguing amiably with a cook about the correct amount of butter in porridge while a cluster of servants watched him with expressions caught somewhere between awe and disbelief—as though they expected him to dissolve like sugar in rain.

"The heir of Hollowmere," he said when he saw her, "eats standing up. I remember that much." His face did something complicated—grief and wonder tangled together—and then he smiled, and it was his own smile, worn and warm, not the Spire's polished imitation. "Sit. There's bread."

"I don't—" Ansel began, and then thought better of it, because the bread did smell extraordinary. She sat.

They ate in something like silence for a while. Around them, the kitchen slowly remembered how to be a kitchen instead of an audience chamber, and the servants drifted back to their work, though more than one kept glancing over as if checking that the king remained solid.

"I owe you an apology," Aldric said at last. "Several, actually. A century's worth, if we're counting Edith's share too."

"You didn't build the thing."

"No. But I let it drive. That's its own kind of building." He turned his cup slowly on the table. "There's a matter I've been circling since yesterday, and I'm told by three separate advisors that kings should not circle matters. So." He looked up at her, and there it was—the weight she'd felt in the throne room, except now it was offered rather than imposed. "The succession is yours, Ansel Underhill. By blood, by right, and frankly by deed. Half my court watched you unmake a century-old horror with nothing but stubbornness and a dead woman's book. They'd follow you tomorrow."

Ansel set down her bread.

She had known this was coming. She'd felt it gathering all through the celebrations, all through the toasts she'd drunk water at because someone had to keep their wits about them. And yet knowing a thing and hearing it spoken aloud were different animals entirely, and the second one had teeth.

"Your Majesty," she said carefully, "I spent my whole life guarding a gate because I believed the world outside would end if I stopped. I was wrong about almost everything—the valley, the prophecy, myself. But I was right about one thing: I'm good at keeping things. Not ruling them." She shook her head before he could speak. "You're already whole again. The court remembers your face—I made sure of that personally. What they need isn't a new king. It's the old one, back where he belongs."

"And if I falter again?"

"Then I'll come back and yell at you," Ansel said. "I'm very good at yelling. Ask Wren."

Aldric laughed—a real laugh, startled out of him—and for a moment he looked young, or perhaps just human, which amounted to the same thing. "Warden of Hollowmere, then," he said. "If the crown doesn't fit, perhaps the gate will. Though I'm told you intend to leave it open."

"Permanently."

"That's either very brave or very foolish."

"Edith would say both," Ansel said. "I'm choosing to take that as a compliment."

* * *

Hollowmere looked different from the south road.

That was the first thing. Ansel had walked out of that valley exactly once, terrified, clutching a ghost's book like a shield, and every step away from home had felt like tearing something. Now she crested the ridge with Wren beside her and the mule behind them, and the valley opened below them green and gold in the late afternoon light, and it looked—

Smaller. Kinder. Like a place rather than a prison. Like somewhere you could leave and come back to, which was, she supposed, the entire point.

"Well," said Wren Halloway, disgraced royal cartographer, professional pessimist, and the only person alive who had ever out-stubborned her, "it hasn't burned down. I'll admit I had money on fire."

"You bet against my house?"

"I hedge my bets. It's how I survived the court." Wren shaded her eyes against the sun, surveying the valley with the particular hunger Ansel had learned to recognize: the look of a mapmaker seeing unmapped country. "You know what strikes me? Nobody's ever charted this place properly. A hundred years of isolation, and the royal surveys just draw a blank with 'here be wards' written across it. Now that the wards are down—" She glanced sideways at Ansel, casual in the way that meant anything but. "I could fix that. If the warden approved."

Ansel looked at her.

Wren had lost her commission for her, technically—for following a rumor into a forbidden valley, for staying, for standing in a throne room between Ansel and the Lord Protector's men without so much as a sword. She had a career to rebuild in the capital, a name to clear, a king newly capable of clearing it. And here she stood on a windswept ridge asking permission to stay in a village of two hundred people who thought cartography was a type of soup.

"Warden approves," Ansel said, and was rewarded with the rarest of sights: Wren Halloway smiling without irony.

"Good. Because I've already started." She patted the satchel at her hip. "Three days ago. Don't make that face—you were busy being feted by royalty, someone had to do useful work."

They came down into the valley as the sun went low, and Hollowmere came out to meet them.

That was the second difference. Before, the villagers had kept a careful distance from the gatehouse—not from Ansel herself, but from what she represented, the way people keep distance from a church or a cliff edge. Now they crowded the road. Old Marta from the mill pressed a loaf into Ansel's hands. The Tulliver boys demanded to know if it was true she'd fought a monster, and whether it had been big, and whether she'd screamed, and were visibly disappointed to learn she'd mostly held a book and talked to it. Tam the smith, who had not spoken a full sentence to Ansel in six years, gripped her shoulder and said, "Welcome home, lass," in a voice that cracked on the last word.

And threaded through all of it, subtle as thread itself, magic moved among them. A hedge witch's charm that actually worked now. A child's imaginary friend that cast a shadow. The well water tasting faintly of starlight, according to Marta, who refused to elaborate.

Some of the villagers were frightened. Ansel wouldn't pretend otherwise. There were closed doors and long silences and at least one family talking seriously about leaving. But others were curious, and curiosity, Ansel had learned, was sturdier than fear—it just needed feeding.

"We'll need rules," she told the gathered crowd that evening, standing on the gatehouse steps where she'd stood a thousand times before, except now the great doors behind her stood wide open to the evening air. "Real ones, decided together, not a hundred years of secrets. Magic comes in, but it answers to the valley. Anyone who wants to learn does it proper, with teaching, not trial and error. And nobody—" she looked around at their faces, frightened and hopeful alike, "—nobody gets shut out. Not from the valley, and not from deciding what it becomes. That's the whole lesson, if you ask me. Walls don't protect anyone. They just decide who suffers alone."

It wasn't a speech for the ages. Half of them probably didn't understand it yet. But Tam nodded slowly, and Marta squeezed her hand, and the Tulliver boys asked if the rules included being allowed to have a dragon, and Ansel said absolutely not, and knew with perfect certainty that within a year there would be a dragon.

* * *

Later, when the crowd had gone home and the lamps were lit, Ansel climbed to the top of the gatehouse wall, where a ghost was waiting.

Edith Vane sat on the parapet with her legs dangling over the drop, looking for all the world like a tired scholar taking a break from grading papers. A century of existence bound to a book had not improved her posture.

"You're fading," Ansel said, sitting beside her.

"I'm finishing," Edith corrected. "There's a difference. A book ends. That's what makes it a book." She turned her head, and her translucent face wore an expression Ansel had come to know well: the guilt of an author reading reviews of her own catastrophe. "I built walls because I was afraid. You took them down because you understood. If I'd been braver—" 

"If you'd been braver, I'd never have existed. Or I'd have grown up in a palace, learning to want a crown." Ansel shrugged. "I used to think about that. All the lives I might have had. But this is the one I got, and today I noticed something strange: I chose it. All of it. The gate, the valley, even the leaving and coming back. Nobody handed me a destiny. I picked up my own life off the floor and put it where I wanted it." She smiled. "You gave me that, in your terrible roundabout way. So thank you. And also, your handwriting is atrocious."

"It was fashionable," Edith said with dignity, and then, quieter: "Will you write it down? What happened. Properly. Someone should, and I find I no longer trust my own ending."

"I'll write it," Ansel promised. "And I'll start it with the truest thing I know: that belonging isn't something you inherit. It's something you choose, and then choose again every day after."

Edith was quiet a moment. Then she laughed—a small, surprised sound, like the first note of the song the world had been humming to Ansel all week.

"A keeper and an heir," she said. "Both at once. I wrote that in a prophecy a hundred years ago and never once believed it." She stood, and her edges had gone soft as candle smoke. "Look after the valley, Warden. And look after the mapmaker. She pretends she doesn't need looking after, which is how I know she does."

And she was gone—not with drama, not with light, just gently, the way a chapter ends when the story finally knows where it's going.

* * *

Spring came early to Hollowmere that year, or possibly the valley simply decided spring was allowed whenever it pleased now.

Ansel stood in the gateway on a bright morning, watching Wren work. The cartographer had driven stakes in a great arc across the meadow below the gate, pacing distances, muttering measurements, occasionally shouting questions at the ley-lines themselves—which, now that magic flowed freely, sometimes answered. Her new map of the valley was going to be the finest thing she'd ever drawn. She'd told Ansel as much, then denied saying it, then admitted it again over dinner.

"Oi, Underhill!" Wren called, squinting up at her. "Your ward-line's drifting again. Third time this week. Either your boundary's breathing or my instruments are haunted, and I refuse to accept the second option."

"It's breathing," Ansel called back. "The valley's settling into being open. Give it a season."

"Give it a season, she says. I'll give it a season." Wren made a note, then paused, frowning at something past Ansel's shoulder. "Speaking of seasons. How do you feel about visitors?"

"Depends on the visitor."

"Mm. Then you may want to form an opinion quickly." Wren pointed down the south road, where, small in the distance but unmistakably approaching, rode a party of perhaps a dozen figures. At their head flew a banner Ansel recognized from the capital: the crowned tower of House Aldric.

And riding beside the banner-bearer, tall and unmistakable even at half a mile, a figure in traveling clothes with no crown at all—who raised one hand in greeting the moment he saw the open gate.

Ansel sighed, deeply and from the heart.

"He's coming to inspect, isn't he," she said. "The king is coming to inspect my valley."

"Worse," said Wren, grinning. "He's coming to visit. There's a difference, and I suspect you're about to explain it to him at length."

Ansel squared her shoulders, touched the old gatepost—one keeper's habit she intended to keep forever—and went down to open the door wide enough for whoever chose to walk through it.
