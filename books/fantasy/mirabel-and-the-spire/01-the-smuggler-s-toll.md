## 1. The Smuggler's Toll

The rain on the Kestrel Pass came down the way it always did in Thornfell's border country — sideways, with intent, like it had a grudge against travelers specifically and me most of all.

I'd been crossing this pass for ten years, back and forth, back and forth, carrying things the Church of the Silence didn't want carried: fever-bark from the southern slopes, willow tincture, poppy resin, once — memorably — a crate of prayer candles that a village elder had begged me for and then buried under her floorboards like contraband, which, technically, they were. Everything was contraband now. Medicine, candles, songs in the old key, the names of dead gods. The Church called it *the Quieting*. The smugglers called it Tuesday.

I call myself Jonah Kavanagh when I'm feeling honest, which is rare, and at high altitude, which is rarer. Up here there's no one to lie to but the rain, and the rain already knows.

The mule — I'd named him Bishop, out of spite, because the Church insisted animals had no souls and therefore couldn't be offended — picked his way along the switchback with more sense than I had ever shown in my life. My pack sat heavy across his back: forty pounds of medicine bound for Hollowmere, a village that officially did not exist anymore, having been struck from the tithe rolls three winters ago after its priest was taken. Unofficially, about sixty people still lived there, coughing through a lungfever that the Church's answer to — prayer, silence, and if that failed, a quick burial — was not going to fix.

"Almost there," I told Bishop, because talking to mules is cheaper than talking to myself, though not by much.

He flicked an ear at me. Even the mule was skeptical.

* * *

You have to understand what ten years does to a man who used to be somebody.

I don't mean somebody important. I mean somebody *known*. In Thornfell, before, everyone had known what I was — storm-touched, they said, born under a sky that wouldn't leave me alone. There were four of us in my grandmother's line going back six generations, and the Church had tolerated us the way you tolerate a leaky roof: watchfully, waiting for the flood. Then the storm came that killed the royal line, and tolerance ended, and so did Thornfell as anything but a name the Church was trying very hard to forget.

They exiled me for a storm I never called. That's the short version. The long version is ten years long and mostly consists of walking.

So I walked. And somewhere around year three I learned that exile has a trade attached to it, if you're willing to carry things over passes in weather that would make a lesser man write his will. The wardens of the Silence patrolled the roads with their grey cloaks and their grey faces and their doctrine that the world should be quiet — no gods, no prayers answered, no storms called, no miracles, nothing that *spoke*. They let the sick die quietly. It was, they said, holy.

I disagreed, loudly, in the privacy of my own skull, while smuggling fever-bark past them at two in the morning.

That night on the pass, the wardens were waiting at the toll — a shelf of flat stone where the trail narrowed between two walls of granite, wide enough for one mule and one liar. Three of them, lanterns hooded, rain streaming off their oilskins. The tall one raised a hand and I reined Bishop to a stop, because running is for people whose cargo is worth less than their life, and mine wasn't.

"Papers," the tall warden said.

I handed them over. Papers are easy; I'm very good at being someone else on paper. These days I was Aldous Fen, a licensed herb-man out of the lowland markets, dull as dishwater, no history, no name worth a bounty. The Church had a price on Jonah Kavanagh, but Jonah Kavanagh had drowned, officially, in the White River, eight years ago, and I attended the funeral in spirit every year.

The tall warden read by lantern-light, rain drumming on the paper. His two companions stood with the particular patience of men who have stopped a hundred travelers and found nothing ninety-nine times, and who resent the hundredth anyway.

"Fen," he said. "Cargo?"

"Fever-bark and willow tincture. Licensed quantities." I pitched my voice bored. Boredom is armor. "Unless the Church has decided fevers are doctrinal now."

Something flickered behind his eyes. Wardens don't like jokes; humor implies a world worth laughing in, and that's dangerously close to a world worth praying in. He folded my papers and handed them back slowly, making me wait, making the rain wait too, apparently.

"Open the pack."

Bishop shifted his weight. I kept my hands loose. This was the part where being dull saved me — I unbuckled the pack with the weary competence of a man who'd done it a dozen times, laid out the bark bundles and the stoppered jars, and watched the tall warden poke through them with two fingers like they might bite him.

Medicine wasn't strictly contraband. That was the beautiful, rotten genius of the Silence: they didn't ban healing, they just made sure nothing *worked* that they hadn't blessed, and they didn't bless anything that worked without them. Fever-bark grew wild on the southern slopes and refused, botanically, to require a warden's permission. So it moved through the mountains in smugglers' packs, and children in struck-off villages lived or died depending on whether someone like me felt like walking in the rain.

The warden repacked nothing. He just stepped back and gestured vaguely, the universal sign of *get off my mountain*, and I buckled the pack, clicked my tongue at Bishop, and walked past three men of God into the dark and the downpour.

I was maybe fifty yards past the toll when the world spoke.

* * *

It came from below the trail — from the valley, where Hollowmere crouched in its fold of hills like something hiding. At first I thought it was wind. Wind makes sounds in these passes; I know all of them, the moan through the saddle rocks, the whistle off the scree, the low hollow breath in the pines. This was none of those.

This was a voice.

A woman's voice, thin with distance and thinner with exhaustion, saying words I hadn't heard spoken aloud since I was nineteen years old:

*"Old One under the hill, old One in the stone — hear me. My daughter burns with fever. Hear me."*

Prayer. Not the Silence's kind — not the bowed head and the empty room and the careful nothing that the Church taught, prayer as a discipline, prayer as holding your breath forever. This was the other kind. The old kind. Addressed by name, with the little rhymed cadence my grandmother used to murmur over the kettle, the kind that assumed someone was listening.

And then — and I have turned this moment over so many times since that I've worn it smooth, and it still doesn't sit right in my hand — the valley answered.

Not with thunder. Not with anything dramatic. With warmth. I felt it the way you feel a hearth from the next room: a pressure of heat against the cold, rising up out of the ground beneath Hollowmere, soft and gold and utterly impossible. The rain above the village — I could see it from the switchback, a whole curtain of silver falling through the dark — slowed. Thickened. Fell gentler, like a hand unclenching.

And the voice said, in a register underneath hearing, underneath thought, older than either:

*She is heard.*

Bishop stopped walking. Mules are sensible about some things and superstitious about everything else, and he planted all four hooves and stared down into the valley with his ears pinned, and I stood next to him in the rain and felt ten years crack down the middle like river ice.

Because I knew that voice. Not the woman praying — the *answer*. I knew what it felt like when something enormous and half-asleep turns its attention toward a small mortal problem and decides, against all odds, to care. I'd spent my whole childhood being looked at like that by the sky. Storms answered me before I knew asking was a skill. And then one storm answered wrong, or I called it wrong, or the truth was worse than both, and the royal house burned down around its own lightning rods, and the Church took the only storm-touched man left standing and put him outside the border and told the world the quiet had come at last.

Ten years of nothing. Ten years of prayers falling upward into a ceiling of grey silence, ten years of the Church nodding sagely — *see? No one listens. No one was ever there.* Ten years of me almost believing it, because believing it hurt less than hoping.

And now a fever-mad woman in a struck-off village had prayed the old way, and something under the hill had rolled over and *answered her*.

"You're waking up," I said to the valley. My voice came out strange. "After ten years, you pick *now*."

The warmth pulsed once, faintly, like something acknowledging it had been addressed — or like something too far gone to do more than twitch. That was the detail that turned wonder into worry, standing there getting soaked: the answer had been small. Tired. A god answering a child's fever should have been a sunrise. This was a candle guttering, spending its last wax on one small warmth.

The old gods weren't waking. One of them was dying, and doing its dying loudly enough that the whole country could hear.

Which meant the Church would hear it too.

* * *

I made the last mile to Hollowmere in record time, mostly because Bishop caught my mood, and mules move fastest when they've decided their rider has lost his mind and someone sensible ought to get them home.

The village hadn't improved. Sixty-odd souls in houses built of slate and stubbornness, smoke leaking from roofs, a shrine at the crossroads with its old god-stone defaced — chiseled smooth by some warden's chisel a decade ago, and now, I noticed, with a fresh sprig of rowan tucked into the crack at its base. Someone had started leaving offerings again. Someone had started *hoping* again. Hope, in Thornfell, was the most dangerous cargo on any road, and here it was growing wild.

The mother who'd prayed met me at the first house, a narrow woman with rain-wet hair and eyes that had the look I remembered from before — the look of someone who has asked and been answered and doesn't yet know whether to be grateful or terrified. Her daughter lay by the fire, small and flushed and breathing in that shallow, whistling way that lungfever has, and I knelt and got the fever-bark steeping and did the things herb-men do, and the mother hovered, and finally she said:

"You heard it. On the pass. You heard Him answer."

"I heard something," I said, which was true and insufficient.

"The Old One under the hill." She said it the way you say a name you've been punished for saying. "My gran prayed to Him. My mother was too scared. I thought — I thought the stories were just stories. But He heard me. He *heard*." Her face did something complicated — joy and grief fighting for the same ground. "Why would He wait ten years and then come back just to die?"

I didn't have an answer for that. I had fever-bark and a license that said I was Aldous Fen, and neither of those was an answer.

"He's not the only one," said a voice from the doorway — the village elder, ancient and bent, wrapped in a blanket despite the heat of the fire. "There's talk in the high valleys. A light on Mount Serel where no light's been since the Quiet. Wells that taste of snowmelt in midsummer. And in the Spire country—" She stopped herself, the way people do when a word is too big for the room. The Spire. Everyone in this country stopped themselves at the Spire eventually. "Things are stirring," she finished, inadequately. "The Church knows. There are more wardens on the roads than I've seen since the exile purges. They're calling it contagion. They're burning villages that pray."

"Burning—"

"Hollowmere's struck off the rolls. Officially nobody lives here. Officially, whatever happens here isn't happening." She smiled without any humor in it at all. "We're very convenient, officially."

I looked at the girl by the fire, drinking fever-bark with the grim determination of the young, and at the rowan sprig in the defaced god-stone, and at the rain still falling soft over the valley where something vast and tired had spent its last strength on one small warmth, and I did the arithmetic I'd spent ten years refusing to do.

The Church had exiled me to end a story. Storm-touched Jonah Kavanagh, gone, forgotten, the border closed, the silence total. And now the story was starting again without me — gods waking, prayers answered, wardens mobilizing, villages burning — and I was standing in the middle of it holding somebody else's medicine, wearing somebody else's name.

"You should go," the elder said, reading me exactly. "Before the wardens circle back. And Jonah—" I went very still. She smiled at whatever crossed my face. "Fifty years I've lived in these hills. I remember faces better than papers. Go south, if you're wise. The Church is heading for the high country, and whatever's waking up there, it's waking up *loud*."

"I'm not wise," I said. "It's why I'm still alive."

Outside, the rain had stopped entirely — the first dry night on the pass in a month, as if the sky itself were holding its breath. To the north, past the black teeth of the ridge line, I could see a faint glow on the shoulders of the high peaks that wasn't dawn and wasn't fire and wasn't anything the Church's silent heaven had any business producing.

Something was burning up there. Or being born. With gods, the two are hard to tell apart, and I had a decade-old scar that proved it.

Bishop nudged my shoulder, wanting direction. South, said the elder's advice. South meant lowlands, licenses, a dull safe life as a dull safe man.

North meant the Spire country, and whatever was waking there, and the Church's wardens moving toward it like antibodies toward a wound.

I stood in the mud of a village that officially didn't exist, listening to a child breathe easier, and told myself I was just deciding which pass had better grazing.

"North," I said to Bishop, and hated how much my voice wanted it.

He sighed the sigh of the long-suffering, and we took the high trail, and behind us the god under Hollowmere's hill dimmed a little more, spending itself like a candle in a draft — and far to the north, on a white road I couldn't see yet, something else had already begun: a chapel, a priestess, and a whisper with a name in it.

But that, as I would learn soon enough and too late, was not my story yet.

It was about to be.
