## 2. What the Silence Keeps

The Silence did not answer prayers. That was the whole point of it, and I had known the whole point of it since I was six years old, when Mother Anselm first put a broom in my hands and told me that sweeping a chapel floor was itself a kind of prayer — that the god we served had gone quiet on purpose, and our work was to keep the room tidy until It chose to speak again, if It ever did.

I believed this the way you believe in winter. Completely, and without much joy.

Ten years I had swept this chapel at Kestrel Ford — no, wait, that chapel comes later; forgive me, memory is a smuggler too, and it moves things around when you aren't looking. Ten years I had swept the chapel at Hollowmere, a village so far up the White Road that the road itself seemed to lose interest halfway there. Stone walls, slate roof, twelve pews, one bell with a crack in it that made every ringing sound like an apology. The Silence's sigil above the door: a closed mouth carved in grey granite, weathered now to something almost gentle, almost smiling. The mason had probably meant severity. Time had made it look like the mouth was about to say something kind.

It never did. That was the doctrine. The god had spoken once — long ago, catastrophically, depending on which account you trusted — and then withdrawn Its voice from the world as penance or punishment or simple exhaustion, and the Church of the Silence existed to keep that withdrawal holy. We did not pray for answers. We prayed *into* the silence, and the silence held us, and that holding was supposed to be enough.

Here is what nobody tells you about serving a silent god: after a while, you start talking to the silence like it's a person who has stepped out of the room and might come back any minute. You leave the good bread out. You keep Its chair warm.

I lit the morning candles the way I always did — seven of them, left to right, because Anselm had drilled the order into me until my hands could do it blind, which was fortunate, because most mornings they were. My fingers were stiff with cold. Hollowmere sits high enough that autumn arrives like a creditor, early and unannounced, and by the time the rest of the country is thinking about harvest festivals we are already burning peat and apologizing to our knuckles.

"Good morning," I said to the empty chapel, because I always said it, because the silence deserved courtesy even if it never returned it. "It's the seventeenth of Reaping-Moon. Cold enough to crack stones. Sister Pell's cough is worse. I've asked you about that eleven times now, which I know is against doctrine, so consider this prayer number twelve, delivered quietly, where no one can count it."

Nothing answered. Nothing ever answered. And yet I felt, as I felt every morning, the small absurd lift of hope before the certainty settled back down over it like snow.

I want to be honest here, since honesty is apparently going to matter later: I loved the Silence the way you love a house you grew up in. Not because it was warm — it wasn't — but because it was *shaped* to me. Every rule fit some part of me exactly. Do not speak the god's name aloud. Do not ask for things. Keep the lamp trimmed, the floor swept, the bell cracked and rung anyway. There is a real comfort, if you are the sort of person who worries constantly, in a faith whose central miracle is that nothing happens.

And I was that sort of person. I worried the way other people breathed. I worried about Sister Pell's lungs and the winter stores and whether the warden who'd ridden through last month had counted our candles, because wardens counted candles now, and candle-counts had become a language, and the Church of the Silence had started reading its own faithful like suspicious letters.

That was the other thing nobody told you: the Silence had grown teeth. Somewhere in the ten years since the storm that killed the royal line — the storm, the *storm*, the word every adult in Thornfell still lowered their voice around the way you lower it around a grave — somewhere in those years, keeping the silence holy had stopped being a devotion and become a border patrol. Wardens rode the mountain roads asking questions no priestess was permitted to answer. Old shrines in the high passes were being pulled down stone by stone, not because they were loud but because they were *old*, and old things remembered voices.

I swept. I trimmed the lamp. I rang the cracked bell at noon, and its apology rolled down the valley where maybe three shepherds heard it and none of them looked up.

The whisper came at dusk, and it came, insultingly, while I was doing the accounts.

Candle-tallow, two pounds. Peat, delivered short again — Tam Osgar's cart axle had broken on the switchback and he'd sworn on his mother's grave he'd make it up, and I'd believed him, because Tam's mother's grave was the only oath anyone in Hollowmere took seriously. A new hinge for the sacristy door, because Brother Colm had sworn the old one groaned in a way that "disturbed the silence," which I felt was theologically confused but practically true.

I was writing *hinge, iron, 4 coppers* in my careful round hand when the air in the chapel changed.

I don't know how else to describe it. Not temperature — though the cold did deepen, the way it does when a cloud crosses the sun. Something else. A pressure, like the moment before your ears pop on a high pass. The seven candles all leaned at once, flames bending toward the altar as if drawn by a draft that had no source, and the shadows swung around the walls like a compass needle finding north.

And then, inside my skull, in a voice that was not a voice — more like remembering a sound than hearing one — someone said:

*Wren.*

I dropped the ink pot. It shattered. Ink went everywhere, across the accounts, across my skirts, across four hundred years of flagstones that had absorbed worse, presumably, though I couldn't have named what at that moment. I stood there with my heart slamming and my hands out like a woman expecting to catch something, and I said, out loud, in the empty chapel, in a voice that came out much smaller than I intended:

"That's not funny."

Because it wasn't funny, and also because it couldn't be happening, and also — this is the part I'm ashamed of, the part I turned over for years afterward — because some traitorous cellar-deep part of me had been waiting for exactly this since I was six years old with a broom, and terror and longing feel identical in the body. Same hammering heart. Same hot face. Same inability to move.

*Wren,* it said again, and this time it was not a name spoken but a name *known* — the way you know your own hand in the dark without looking. The pressure in the air gathered itself toward the altar, toward the closed-mouth sigil above it, and the granite mouth —

I swear this on Tam Osgar's mother's grave, which is the only oath I trust —

— the granite mouth opened.

Not much. A seam. A hairline crack of darkness where there had been solid stone for four centuries, and out of that crack came light, but not candle-light, not sunlight, nothing with a color I had a word for. It was the light of the moment just before you wake from a dream you'll lose in three seconds. It pooled on the altar like water finding its level.

And in the middle of the pool sat a spark.

Small. The size of a coal at the end of its burning, when the fire has given up on the log and is just keeping itself company. It should have been dying — everything about it said dying, guttering, spent — and yet looking at it was like looking directly into the fact that the world exists, which is not an experience the human eye is built for. I felt my knees inform me they were considering resignation. I gripped the altar rail.

"You're not supposed to be here," I whispered. Doctrine, reflex, whatever. "You're not supposed to be anywhere. You went quiet. That was the whole — that's the entire religion."

The spark pulsed. And the not-voice came again, and this time it was tired. That's what undid me, what took the awe and snapped it sideways into something closer to grief. It was *tired*, the way Anselm had been tired at the end, the way a person is tired who has been carrying something heavy for so long they've forgotten the weight isn't normal.

*I did go quiet,* it said. *Quiet is not the same as absent.*

"What does that mean?" I asked the granite mouth, the pool of impossible light, the empty pews. "What does that — you can't just — there are *rules*. I keep the rules. The rules are the only reason I'm any use to anyone."

*Take it.*

"No."

*Wren.* My name again, and this time it carried something underneath it, something I would spend chapters' worth of nights trying to describe and never quite manage: it carried the specific tenderness of someone handing you the last of their food. *Take it, or it goes out here, alone, on a stone table, and everything it remembers goes with it.*

"What is it?"

*A life. Mine. What's left of mine.*

"I'm a priestess of the Silence," I said, and my voice cracked in the middle of the word *priestess*, which rather undermined the authority of the statement. "My job is to keep things quiet. My job is to sweep the floor and trim the lamp and ring a bell that sounds sorry for itself, and NOT —" I was nearly shouting now, which in a chapel of the Silence is roughly equivalent to arson — "to hold a *god* in my hands like a stray cat!"

The spark pulsed again. And I would give a great deal to be able to tell you I resisted longer. That I threw up wards, or ran for Anselm's memory, or did anything other than what I actually did, which was stand there arguing theology with a dying ember for the length of perhaps nine breaths before my feet — my feet, traitors, mutineers — carried me around the altar rail.

Up close, the light was warm. That was the detail that broke me. All that terror, all that doctrine, and the thing itself was warm as a hand.

"It will hurt," I said. Not a question. I knew it the way you know rain is coming, in the ache of old breaks.

*Yes.*

"And no one will ever explain why."

*No one has ever explained why,* it agreed, with what I could have sworn, in a being made of exhausted light, was dry humor. *Welcome to faith.*

I cupped my hands.

I want to say I reached for it. The truth is it reached for me — rose off the stone like a spark rising off a log, drifted the last terrible inch, and settled into my palms with a weight far greater than its size, a weight like a sleeping child, like a debt, like a name.

And then it went *in*.

Not through the skin. Through everything. I felt it pass through the accounts I hadn't finished, through the broom-swept floors of twenty years, through the six-year-old girl with the broom, through every silent morning I had ever greeted with "good morning" and meant it. It settled behind my sternum, in the place where dread lives, and evicted the dread, and moved in, and lit a lamp.

The chapel went ordinary. Candles upright, shadows sane, granite mouth seamlessly shut as if it had never opened, as if stone could lie, which apparently it can, because I was standing there scorched and shaking with ink drying on my skirts and a god's dying life beating inside my chest like a second, smaller heart.

I did the only reasonable thing.

I sat down on the altar steps, put my head between my knees, and laughed — the high, unhinged laughter of a woman whose entire religion has just contradicted itself in her hands. Because that's what had happened, I understood dimly, through the roaring in my ears. The Silence had kept something after all. All these years of nothing-happening, of tidiness and doctrine and cracked bells — it hadn't been absence. It had been *keeping*. A vault. And tonight the vault had opened, and handed its contents to the sweeper.

"Why me?" I asked the empty air, when I could breathe again. "There are senior priests. There's the seminary at Vail. There are people who've studied forty years and never once talked to their ink pots."

The warmth behind my sternum pulsed, slow, patient, tired.

*Because you kept talking to Me,* it said, *when everyone knew I wasn't listening.*

Then it said nothing more. But I felt it settle deeper, the way a bird settles on a nest, and I understood — with the particular clarity that arrives right alongside catastrophe — that it was not going to leave, and that I was not going to be permitted to be a minor priestess in a minor chapel ever again.

Outside, full dark had come down over Hollowmere. And down the valley, faint but unmistakable, came a sound I had been trained my whole life to dread the way farmers dread locusts:

Horses. Many horses. Riding hard, uphill, in the dark.

Wardens didn't ride at night. Wardens didn't ride at night unless they were hunting something.

I put out the candles — all seven, left to right, my hands steady in the way of hands that have stopped consulting their owner — and pressed my palm flat against my sternum, against the small burning weight that had chosen me, of all the swept floors in all the chapels in the world, to die inside of.

"Hold still," I whispered to it, "and whatever happens —"

The warmth pulsed once, and I would spend a very long time learning what that pulse meant, but even then, even that first night, I understood it perfectly:

*Run.*
