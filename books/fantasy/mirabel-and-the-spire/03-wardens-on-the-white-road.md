## 3. Wardens on the White Road

The trouble with oaths, I've found, is that they only hold until the weather turns.

I'd sworn a great many things in ten years. Never to set foot past the Thornfell border. Never to answer a knock at night. And above all, never — *never* — to go back for anyone, because the last time I'd gone back for someone I'd come out of it with a dead country on my shoulders and a price on my head that could buy a small cathedral.

So naturally, when I heard the bells of Hallowmere ringing wrong — not the slow toll for funerals but the fast, panicked clanging that means *run* — I was already three days past the border with a mule loaded down with feverfew and willowbark, telling myself the smoke on the horizon was just a hearth fire.

It was not a hearth fire.

* * *

The White Road runs along the spine of the mountains like an old scar, and Hallowmere sits in the fold of it, a village of slate roofs and sheep and maybe two hundred souls who had never done anything worse than pray to the wrong gods quietly. I'd passed through two days earlier. Sold the widow Brann medicine for her grandson's lungs. Ate a bowl of barley soup that tasted mostly of salt and gratitude. The innkeeper's daughter had laughed at something I said, and I'd thought, idly, that this was what peace looked like from the outside.

Now the smoke stood up over it in black columns, and the wind carried the smell I knew too well — burning thatch, burning wool, and underneath it, that sweetish reek of consecrated oil that the Church of the Silence uses when it wants a fire to be official.

I stopped on the ridge with the mule shifting nervously behind me and did the arithmetic every smuggler does. Two hundred souls, none of them mine. One cargo of medicine, definitely mine. Zero reasons to descend into a Church purge.

"Keep walking," I told myself. "You're good at this. Ten years of practice."

The mule, whose name is Turnip and whose judgment I trust more than my own, refused to move forward or backward. She just stood there with her ears pricked toward the smoke, as if waiting for me to stop lying to myself.

Below, through the haze, I could see them: wardens in their grey-and-white livery, moving through the streets in pairs, methodical as weevils. Not soldiers — the Church doesn't waste soldiers on villages. Wardens are clerks with swords, men who file reports on the souls they burn, and there were perhaps thirty of them going door to door with a list. Looking for one person, by the shape of it. One person worth thirty wardens and a village's worth of oil.

A spark-bearer. The word arrived in my head unbidden, and I realized I'd been hearing it for weeks now, in the way you hear a word everywhere once it's been said to you once. In taverns, in whispers, in the careful silence of people who'd noticed the old shrines being cleaned. The old gods waking. Prayers answered in the high country. And the Church of the Silence — which had spent a century teaching us that silence was holy, that the gods were dead and *good riddance* — hunting whoever carried the proof otherwise.

I should have walked away. Every instinct I'd honed since exile said walk away, and I want to be honest here: I tried. I actually turned Turnip around and got maybe fifty yards down the trail before the wind shifted and brought me a sound I'll be hearing for the rest of my life.

Not screaming. Screaming I can handle; screaming is just fear with the lid off. This was quieter. It was a child's voice, high and thin through the smoke, saying the same thing over and over:

"*Please. Please. Please.*"

A prayer. An actual prayer, addressed to whatever was left to hear it.

And somewhere under my ribs, in the place where I keep the storm I didn't call, something answered before I could stop it. Not thunder. Just a shift, a pressure change, the air going taut the way it does before lightning. Ten years of keeping my head down and my hands in my pockets, and my own blood still moved toward trouble like water toward a drain.

"Oh, hell," I said, to no god in particular, since apparently they were busy. "Turnip, wait here."

She didn't, of course. But she followed slowly, which amounted to permission.

* * *

I came into Hallowmere from the goat paths behind the shrine, because if you're going to break a ten-year oath you may as well do it competently. The back lanes were empty — everyone herded into the square or fled into the hills — and the smoke hung low and greasy, stinging the eyes. The shrine itself was a little stone thing no bigger than a woodshed, dedicated to some mountain god whose name even the oldest villagers had forgotten, and it was already burning. Which struck me as excessive, since the god in question had been silent for a century and posed no threat whatsoever except to the Church's monopoly on silence.

That's when I saw her.

She was half in the shrine doorway, half out, and she wasn't running. That was the first strange thing. The second was that she was carrying something cupped against her chest with both hands, hunched over it like a woman sheltering a candleflame in wind, except there was no flame. There was nothing visible at all. But the air around her hands bent, the way air bends over hot coals, and the smoke near her curled *away* instead of in.

Half-frozen, I'd have called her later — though it wasn't cold that day. It was the look of her. Grey-lipped, shaking, eyes wide and fixed on nothing, the particular stillness of someone whose body has decided to keep moving without consulting the rest of her. Shock, or prayer, or both. She wore the undyed wool of a novitiate, Church-cut, and that confused me more than anything else, because what was a priestess of the Silence doing holding a piece of a god like contraband?

Three wardens had her cornered between the burning shrine and the well. They weren't rushing. Wardens never rush; it's bad for the paperwork. One held the list. One held a sword. The third held a censer, and from it came that sweet oil-smell, and he was saying something in the flat reciting voice they use — the Rite of Unmaking, probably, the words that make a fire holy so nobody has to call it murder.

I did the math again. Three wardens. One of me. The odds were terrible, and I'd like to report that I weighed them like a rational man.

What actually happened is that the child's voice said "*please*" one more time — somewhere, anywhere, I never found out where — and my feet made the decision my oath had forbidden, and I was moving.

Here's what ten years of smuggling teaches you about fighting: don't. Smugglers win fights the way rivers win arguments, by being somewhere else when the blow lands. I came off the wall behind the well warden — the one with the sword, because habits — and put him into the water with a shoulder to the kidneys. He went down with a splash and a great deal of indignation. The censer-bearer turned, and I took the censer across his wrist and then his knees, and he sat down hard among the coals and started swearing in liturgical Latin, which I confess I enjoyed more than I should have.

The third one, the list-keeper, was faster than his colleagues. He had a knife at the priestess's throat before I'd finished standing up, and he was looking at me with the calm of a man reading an inventory.

"Jonah Kavanagh," he said.

My name. My true name, out loud, in a burning village, from a stranger's mouth. The Church keeps its lists, and mine has been on them for ten years, and hearing it spoken felt like a hand closing around the back of my neck.

"The exile," he went on, conversational, while the knife pressed a bead of red against her skin. "Storm-caller. Regicide. You're worth more than she is, actually. Lay down and I won't—"

I've been asked since whether I regret what happened next, and the honest answer is that I don't remember deciding it. There was a pressure in the air, the same tautness as before, and the sky over the ridge — clear sky, blue as a saint's robe — grumbled. Once. Low. Like something enormous rolling over in its sleep.

The warden heard it. So did I. And whatever he saw in my face made him decide that the spark-bearer, valuable as she was, was not worth standing next to a storm-caller while the sky took an interest.

He ran. Cowardice, or wisdom; in the Church those are often the same thing.

* * *

Afterward there was the part I'm less proud of, which is that I froze.

She was swaying in the doorway of the burning shrine, still cupping her invisible cargo, still grey-lipped, and the roof beam chose that moment to give way with a crack like a snapped bone. Embers sheeted down. Any competent rescuer would have grabbed her and gone.

Instead I stood there looking at her hands, at the bent air, at the place where the smoke wouldn't go, and I thought: *that's real. The old gods are real, and this half-dead girl is holding one, and I have just spent my name and my safety and ten years of careful nothing on a stranger because a child prayed.*

The beam fell. Instinct — that same cursed instinct, older than any oath — took over where sense had failed. I crossed the space, wrapped an arm around her, and hauled us both out through the side of the shrine where the wall had half-collapsed, into the goat-lane, into the smoke, up the path while the roof came down behind us in a gout of orange.

She weighed almost nothing. That frightened me more than the wardens had. She was shivering so hard her teeth clicked, and she kept murmuring — not to me, not to anyone — fragments of something. A name, maybe. Her own? The god's? I couldn't tell, and I didn't ask, because asking questions is how you die in the mountains, and also because part of me very much did not want to know what she'd say.

We climbed. Behind us Hallowmere burned, and the bell kept its panicked rhythm until someone finally silenced it, and I did not look back, because looking back is another thing I swore off ten years ago and clearly I'm no good at oaths.

Turnip met us at the treeline with the insufferable calm of an animal who predicted all of this. I dumped the priestess over the saddle — gently, roughly, I honestly couldn't tell you — and led us up into the pines as the light went grey and the first snow of the season began to fall, fat and indifferent, ash and flake together drifting down on the smoke.

* * *

By full dark we were in a smuggler's cave I knew from the old days, a hollow behind a waterfall frozen to lace, and I'd gotten a fire going and forced maybe half a cup of broth into her. She sat wrapped in my spare cloak, staring into the flames, hands still cupped in her lap around the nothing-that-was-something. The shivering had eased. The grey had gone out of her lips. Whatever she carried, it was keeping her alive, or she was keeping it alive, and I suspected the distinction mattered enormously and I wanted no part of finding out.

"You're Jonah Kavanagh," she said. First words. Voice like gravel dragged through honey.

"Am I."

"They said your name. In the square." She looked at me then, and her eyes were the unsettling kind, pale grey, the color of the road we'd left. "They said you called the storm that killed the royal line."

The fire popped. Somewhere above us, the waterfall muttered its one note.

"That," I said carefully, "is what they say."

"And did you?"

And there it was. The question I'd been dodging for ten years, asked by a half-frozen priestess with a god in her hands, in a cave that smelled of wet stone and mule. I fed the fire a stick and bought myself a moment.

"I broke a ten-year rule today," I said. "Going back for people. It's a bad habit of mine. It got me exiled the first time." I nodded at her cupped hands. "Whatever that is, it's why thirty wardens burned two hundred people's homes today. Which means tomorrow there will be sixty. And they know my name, and now they know yours, and I would very much like to know what I've gotten myself into before I decide how much of it to regret."

She looked at the fire for a long time. When she spoke again, her hands tightened around the empty air, protective, certain.

"It answered me," she said. "My whole life, the Silence never answered. And then it did." She lifted her eyes to mine, and there was terror in them, and awe, and something that looked horribly like love. "It's dying, Jonah Kavanagh. And it asked *me* to carry it home."

Outside, the snow thickened, and far off down the valley — too far to be natural, too steady to be weather — I heard the low groan of the sky again, like something remembering it had a voice.

So did she. We looked at each other across the fire, two people freshly ruined, and I understood with perfect clarity that my oath had not merely broken today.

It had shattered — and the sound it made was exactly like thunder.
