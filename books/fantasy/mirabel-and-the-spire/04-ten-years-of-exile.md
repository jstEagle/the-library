## 4. Ten Years of Exile

The smuggler's cave smelled like wet wool and old lies, which was fitting, because I'd brought both.

It sat behind a curtain of ivy on the north face of the Kestrel Pass, one of a dozen holes in the mountains that the trade roads had worn into themselves over the centuries — places where contraband went to sleep between owners and where men like me went to stop being seen. There were names scratched into the stone near the entrance, some of them mine, or versions of mine: *J. Kav*, *the Stormcrow*, *that bastard who owes Vessa forty crowns*. I'd been all of those at one time or another. It's a strange thing, exile. You don't lose your name so much as collect replacements for it, the way a man with no coat collects other people's.

Wren slept by the fire for most of the day, curled around her satchel like it might get up and walk off without her. The spark inside her glowed faintly through the canvas whenever she dreamed, a warm gold pulse against the grey of the cave, and twice I caught myself staring at it the way you stare at a hearth in winter — forgetting, briefly, that what you're looking at is the reason half the Church's hounds in three counties are out in this weather.

She'd stopped shivering around noon. That was something. When I'd pulled her out of the smoke at Aldenmere she'd been more ash than girl, lips blue, hands blistered from dragging burning thatch off a stranger's child, and she hadn't said a word the whole first mile. Just walked. People who've watched their whole world burn do that sometimes. They walk like the walking is the only prayer left to them.

I fed the fire small. Dry pine, no green wood, nothing that smoked. Ten years of practice. You learn a lot of useless-adjacent skills in exile — how to read smoke, how to sleep sitting up, how to eat slowly enough that hunger stops arguing with you. None of it makes you wise. It just makes you quiet.

Around dusk she woke, and the first thing she did was check the satchel. The second thing she did was look at me with an expression I recognized, because I wore it myself for about a decade: the look of someone deciding whether the person across the fire is a rescuer or just a slower kind of danger.

"You carried me," she said. Her voice was rough as gravel road.

"Part of the way."

"Why?"

Good question. I'd been asking it myself since the White Road. "You were heavy," I said, "and leaving you seemed rude."

She didn't laugh. Priestesses of the Silence, I was learning, treated humor the way wardens treated heresy — noted, catalogued, and scheduled for later punishment. She pulled her knees up and studied me over them, and the spark pulsed once under her collarbone like a second heartbeat that hadn't decided whose side it was on.

"They say you called the storm," she said.

And there it was. Ten years, four hundred miles, and a mountain range between us, and still the first real conversation anyone wanted to have with Jonah Kavanagh was the same one. I should have been used to it. I wasn't. You never get used to being a story people already know the ending of.

"Do they," I said.

"In Thornfell they do. In the lowlands too. The storm that killed the royal line — the Night of Nine Bells. They say a weather-caller named Kavanagh raised it out of spite." She tilted her head. "They say you hated the crown."

"I didn't hate the crown."

"Then why—"

"Because I was there," I said, "and being somewhere is all it takes."

* * *

Here is what I told her, in the end, because she asked plainly and because — God help me, or whatever passed for Him in those hills — I was tired of carrying it alone.

I was twenty-two. I'd come down from the high pastures to Thornfell City for the Festival of Anchors, the one week a year when the Spire was opened to pilgrims and the whole capital smelled like beeswax and fried dough and horse. I was nobody. A shepherd's son with a weather-gift too small to matter — I could call a rain-shower to save a hay crop, nudge a hailstorm off a flock, that sort of thing. Small magic. Village magic. The kind the Church tolerated the way you tolerate a mouse in the wall: annoying, but beneath the cost of a trap.

I remember the bells. Nine of them, ringing midnight, and then not ringing, because the ninth bell never finished its swing. I remember thinking the sky looked wrong — not dark, but *thin*, like cloth worn through. And I remember the wind coming down off the mountains with a sound I have never managed to describe to anyone without lying, because every honest word for it is a scream.

The storm took the palace first. Took the royal family in their beds, took the council chamber, took the cathedral roof and dropped it into the square. Took nine hundred people in the space of an hour, and when dawn came, the country's heart was a hole and everyone in it was looking for someone to blame.

They found me because my gift was known. Because I'd argued with a tithe-collector that spring. Because a warden remembered seeing me look up at the sky before the bells, and a man looking at the sky must be talking to it, and a man talking to the sky must be commanding it. The logic of fear doesn't need to be good. It just needs to be fast.

"The Church tried you," Wren said quietly. "There was a trial."

"There was a show," I corrected. "Trials have evidence. This had a confession — theirs, written for me, which I refused to sign — and a sentence, which I couldn't refuse at all. Exile. Struck from the rolls. Name struck from the border-stones. If I ever set foot in Thornfell again, the wardens had leave to kill me on sight." I turned my hands over, palms up, the way they'd made me hold them in the square while the archdeacon pronounced his judgment. "Ten years ago next month."

"And you didn't call it." Not a question. A statement, flat and certain, delivered with the same calm she probably used to tell dying people they were dying.

"No," I said. "I didn't call it."

"Jonah." She leaned forward, and the firelight moved in her eyes, and for one absurd moment I thought she was going to take my hand. "I'm a priestess. I've spent six years learning what gods can and can't do, what prayers weigh, what storms cost. A storm like that — a storm that kills a royal line in their own beds, behind wards laid by the Silence itself — that isn't village magic. That isn't any mortal's magic. No weather-caller alive could have raised it, and if one had tried, he'd have burned himself out doing it. There wouldn't be enough left of him to exile."

"So?"

"So either you're lying to me," she said, "or someone else wanted the royals dead, and needed a shepherd's son to hang it on." She held my gaze. "And you strike me as a lot of things, Jonah Kavanagh, but I've watched you lie about the price of passage and I know your face when you do it. Your face right now isn't lying."

I looked at the fire. The fire looked back, in the way fires do, offering nothing.

"It doesn't matter," I said.

"It matters enormously—"

"It doesn't," I said, harder than I meant to, "because knowing it changes nothing. The verdict stands. The name stays struck. The Church owns the story, and the Church has ten years of sermons built on it, and if I stand in a square tomorrow shouting the truth until my voice gives out, do you know what happens?" I spread my hands. "Nothing. Or worse than nothing. Because a scapegoat who protests is just a scapegoat with ambition, and ambition confirms the crime. That's how it works. That's how it's always worked."

Wren was quiet for a long moment. The spark pulsed under her skin, slow, thoughtful, like something listening through her.

"That's not loneliness talking," she said finally. "That's scar tissue."

"You'd know."

"Yes," she said simply. "I would."

* * *

We ate. Dried mutton, hard bread, water from the seep at the cave's back wall that tasted of iron and stone. Luxury, by my standards. While we ate she kept looking at me in that way she had — direct, unembarrassed, like a person reading a difficult text aloud to herself — and eventually I cracked first, because I always crack first, it's my single greatest flaw after cowardice and poor financial planning.

"What?"

"You slept sitting up," she said. "With your back to the wall and your knife in your hand. All day."

"Habit."

"Ten years of habit."

"Give or take."

She wrapped her arms around her knees. "What was it like? The first year."

Nobody had ever asked me that. In ten years — not Vessa, not the other runners, not the sympathetic innkeepers who gave me the corner table away from the windows — no one had asked what it was actually *like*. They'd asked what happened, sure. Everyone loves the story of the fall. But the fall takes a night; the landing takes years, and nobody wants to hear about the years.

"Cold," I said. "That's the honest answer. Everyone imagines exile as drama — the ride out, the gates closing, the last look back. It's none of that. It's cold. It's realizing you don't own anything, including the ground you sleep on. It's learning that 'where are you from' is a question with teeth, and building a whole personality out of shrugging at it." I picked a splinter of bark out of the fire and watched it curl. "The first year I kept walking toward the border. Every spring. Like a bird hitting a window. I'd get within sight of the passes and turn around, and I told myself it was strategy, that I was waiting for the heat to die down, but it wasn't strategy. It was homesickness with nowhere to put itself."

"And now?"

"Now I know better than to look."

"Liar," she said, softly, without malice. "You came back for me."

"I came back because you were there."

"Same thing."

"It really isn't."

But even as I said it, I heard how thin it sounded, and worse, I felt how thin it was. Because here was the truth underneath, the one I'd been stepping around since the White Road: I had sworn, loudly and repeatedly and mostly to myself, that I would never go back for anyone. Never be the fool who threw his neck onto the block for another person's sake. And then I'd seen a girl standing in burning snow holding something the whole machinery of the Church wanted dead, and my feet had started moving before my principles finished their argument. Ten years of careful, practiced solitude, undone in about eleven seconds.

Exile, it turns out, is easy to keep. It's the ending of it that costs.

* * *

She slept again near midnight, properly this time, stretched out with her boots on and her hand resting on the satchel. I sat up and kept watch, because habits, and because the mountains had gone quiet in a way I didn't trust. Not silent — quiet. The difference matters. Silence is empty. Quiet is *listening*.

And around the hour when honest people are asleep and dishonest ones are at their most honest, I heard it.

A voice. Faint, from the direction of the satchel, or from Wren, or from somewhere behind both — a voice like wind through a keyhole, saying words I couldn't make out. Wren stirred and murmured in her sleep, and the murmuring answered the voice, back and forth, a conversation conducted entirely below the level of waking.

The spark flared gold through the canvas. Once. Twice. Then settled.

I sat very still for a long time, knife loose in my hand, watching a sleeping priestess talk to a god in her dreams, and I thought: *the old stories are true, then. They're waking up. After everything, after the Silence scrubbed the mountains clean, they're waking up — and the first thing one of them did was crawl into a girl and start running.*

The voice came again, clearer this time, threading up through Wren's sleep like smoke through floorboards. And though I couldn't parse the words, I caught the shape of them, the cadence — and the shape was a name.

Not Wren's.

Mine.

The fire popped. Wren sighed and rolled over, and the spark dimmed to embers, and the voice was gone as if it had never been.

I didn't sleep the rest of the night. Somewhere east, over the passes, in a country that had struck my name from its stones, something old had just said my name back to me — and I had the distinct, crawling feeling that whatever door it was trying to open, I was standing on the wrong side of it.
