## 5. The Spark Speaks

The god talks in its sleep.

I want to be clear about that from the start, because everything else that happened in the smuggler's cave — Jonah's silences, the cold, the way he watched me like I might detonate — all of it was easier to bear than the talking. When a god sleeps inside your chest and dreams out loud, you learn things about yourself you'd rather not know. For instance: I flinch at my own name now. It comes out of my own mouth sometimes, in a voice that isn't mine, and there is no preparing for that.

"Wren," it said, on the second night, while Jonah slept with his back to us and his knife loose in his hand even so. "Wren, Wren, the door remembers."

"Shh," I whispered, which is an absurd thing to say to a god, like hushing a landslide.

It didn't shh. Gods don't, apparently. That was one of the first lessons of carrying the spark: everything I'd been taught about the divine in the chapel at Kestrel Ford was wrong, or rather, it had been written by people who had never actually heard one. The Silence taught that the old gods were gone, that their silence was proof of death, that our duty was to keep the candles lit anyway because ritual outlives belief. We were sextons of a graveyard. Nobody told me the grave could wake up.

Nobody told me it would be *warm*.

* * *

Let me back up, because Jonah asked me to tell him what it felt like and I lied to him — said it was like holding a coal, said it hurt but I could manage. Both true. Both insufficient. He deserved better than that, but I've learned that men who carry ten years of guilt like a stone in the belly don't have much room left for other people's burdens, and besides, I didn't understand it myself yet.

Here is what I understood by the third morning:

The spark was dying. I knew this the way you know a fever has turned — not through any single symptom but through the whole shape of the thing. When it first pressed itself into my hands in that chapel where nothing had answered for years, it had been bright enough to hurt. Now it flickered. And when it dreamed, the dreams were not random. They were *directional*. A god does not dream of doors unless it wants to go through one.

I sat cross-legged near the cave mouth, watching rain come down the mountain in grey sheets, and I listened.

"The Spire," the spark murmured, using my voice, my throat, a sound like wind through a keyhole. "Half here. Half elsewhere. The anchor. The anchor, the anchor." A pause, and then, with something that in a person I would have called grief: "Thornfell leaks. Thornfell thins. Where the border fails, the country forgets its own edges."

I thought about that. I thought about the villages we'd passed on the white road, the ones where the maps stopped agreeing with the ground — a bridge that appeared on no chart, a hill that had been a valley when Jonah was a boy, if his muttered complaints were anything to go by. I'd assumed smugglers' gossip. Smugglers exaggerate; it's practically their trade.

But a border isn't a wall. I knew that much from scripture, the parts the Church hadn't burned. A border is a promise a country makes to itself: *here I end, and there something else begins.* Break the promise and the country doesn't die. It just stops being sure it's anywhere.

"And the door?" I asked quietly. "What door?"

The spark went still inside me, the way a bird goes still when a hawk crosses the sky. Then, very softly, in a tone I can only describe as ashamed:

"The door is me."

* * *

Jonah woke at midday, or what passes for midday under that much cloud, and built a fire small enough to hide in a cupped hand. He was good at smallness, Jonah. Small fires, small movements, small kindnesses delivered sideways so you couldn't catch him at them. He handed me tea that tasted like boiled pine and regret, and he didn't ask how I'd slept, because he already knew. You can't sleep next to a dreaming god. He'd learned that fast.

"It talked again," I said.

"I heard." He stared into the fire. "It called me 'the storm-bearer.' In your voice. Do you know how unpleasant that is?"

"You have no idea."

"Try me."

So I told him. Not everything — I kept the door-is-me part folded away like a letter I wasn't ready to open — but the shape of it. The Spire. The anchor. The border failing, Thornfell thinning at its edges like cloth worn through. He listened the way he did everything, carefully and as though listening might cost him something.

"The Spire," he repeated. "That's—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "That's where they held me before the exile. The trial. What there was of a trial." A short laugh with no humor in it anywhere. "They stood me in front of the Spire and read out the names of the dead. Took most of a day. There were a lot of names."

I didn't say anything. I've learned that when a man opens a door like that, the worst thing you can do is walk through it for him.

"They'll expect us to go there," he said instead. "If the Church has any sense — and Hale has sense, God rot him, he has plenty — they'll know the spark will run for the Spire eventually. It's a straight line. We're walking down the barrel of it."

"Then we walk faster."

He looked at me then, really looked, and there was something in his face I couldn't read — worry wearing the mask of irritation, maybe. "Wren. What exactly happens when you get there? What does it want?"

And that was the question, wasn't it. That was the question I'd been circling for three days like a moth around a lamp, because every time I came close to the answer the spark would stir and murmur *the door is me* and I would find something else urgently necessary to think about.

"I don't know yet," I said.

He nodded slowly, the nod of a man filing a lie in a drawer he keeps for other people's lies, and I felt it land somewhere under my ribs, right next to the god.

* * *

That night the dream got worse.

I should explain how it works, when the spark dreams aloud. It borrows. My voice, mostly, but also my hands — I'd wake with my fingers moving, tracing shapes on the cave floor, spirals and thresholds, the geometry of gates. Once I caught myself standing. Just standing, facing the cave wall, arms slightly out, like a woman pressing her palms against a window from the inside. Jonah saw that one too. Neither of us mentioned it. There are mercies between people who are fleeing together, and silence is the largest of them.

But that night it didn't borrow my body. That night it borrowed my memory.

I was back in the chapel at Kestrel Ford — not the ruined one where I'd healed the child, the original, the one where I'd served since I was nine years old. Candles lit. Silence thick as wool. And the spark stood beside me in the dream, wearing — this is the part I can hardly write — wearing a shape. Not a body, exactly. More like the idea of a body, the way you remember someone after they're gone: an outline of presence, tall and narrow, features smoothed off like river stones.

It showed me the Spire.

Not from outside. From inside, from the top, from the room where the border had once been anchored — and I understood suddenly that borders aren't lines drawn on the ground at all. They're *held*. Someone holds them, the way you hold a rope, the way you hold a note of music. The Spire was the fist around the rope, and the fist had been open for ten years, and the rope was slipping, and below it —

Below it was Thornfell. All of it. Seen from above, seen whole, and *thinning*. Villages going soft at the edges, roads forgetting where they went, rivers running backward into themselves. Not destroyed. *Unwritten.* A country being gently, patiently erased, the way the Silence erased gods — not with fire but with forgetting.

"That's happening now," I said in the dream. "That's happening while we sit in a cave drinking pine-needle tea."

"Yes," said the god, in a voice like the last candle in a dark church.

"And closing the border stops it."

"Yes."

"What does closing it cost?"

The god was quiet for a long time. In dreams, quiet stretches. I had time to notice things — that the chapel candles were burning blue at their tips, that outside the windows there was no weather at all, that the god would not look at me. It kept its stone-smooth face turned toward the altar, toward the place where the Silence had lived, empty for a decade.

"You know what it costs," it said finally. "You have known since the night I pressed it into your hands. You are a priestess. You know what sparks are for."

Sparks are for giving back. That was the oldest teaching, older than the Silence, older than the Church that strangled it: a god's power given into mortal keeping must return through mortal hands, and the hands do not survive the returning. The vessel pours itself out with the wine. Every initiate learns this. We learn it as history, as theology, as a thing that happened to other people in other centuries.

We do not learn it as a schedule.

"No," I said, and my dream-voice cracked. "No, there's another way. There's always—"

"There is not always another way," the god said, gently, terribly. "There is only ever the way, and whether we walk it alone."

And then — this is why I loved it, this is why loving it was going to kill me — it did the cruelest kind thing imaginable. It let go of the dream. It released me back into my body, into the cold and the dark and the sound of Jonah breathing two arm-lengths away, and it left me alone with the arithmetic.

A god's life against a country's. Except it wasn't even that, was it. It was my life against a country's, because the spark couldn't pour itself out without me, and the country was dissolving *now*, village by village, name by name, while I lay in a cave doing sums.

I did the sums. That was the worst part. I lay there in the dark and I did them honestly, the way I'd been taught to approach scripture — without flinching, without bargaining — and the sums came out the same way they always come out. One life. Thousands of lives. A whole country's worth of remembering against one girl who had never been more than a keeper of candles.

The math was easy. That's how I knew I was in trouble. When the math is easy and your chest still hurts, the chest knows something the math doesn't.

* * *

The hounds came at dawn.

Not Brother Hale himself — I'd know that particular flavor of dread anywhere, the pressure behind the eyes, the smell of ink and cold iron — but his dogs, the ones the Church bred for tracking the faithful. They don't track scent. They track *prayer*. Anywhere within miles that someone has spoken to a god, the hounds turn toward it like compass needles, and I had spent three nights sleeping next to a god who talked in its sleep.

I woke to Jonah's hand over my mouth and his eyes very close to mine, black and calm, saying nothing, and beyond him at the cave mouth: light. Torchlight, wrong for dawn, bobbing up the switchback trail below us. Voices. The flat chanted cadence of wardens at their work — *Silence, Silence, the country sleeps* — and underneath it, faintly, the belling of the hounds. A sound like bells underwater.

"They've got our trail," Jonah breathed. "How—"

"I know how." My face was hot with shame. "It's me. It's been me every night. It dreams and the dreams carry, and the hounds hear it."

He took that in. I watched him decide not to be angry, watched the anger arrive and get put away unused, and I understood that this was a skill he'd practiced for ten years on nobody but himself.

"Can you stop it?" he asked.

"I don't know. It's not — it doesn't take requests, Jonah. It's a *god*. Dying, but still." I pressed my hand flat against my sternum, where the spark sat flickering like a trapped moth. "Please," I whispered to it, not caring how it looked, "please, be quiet, please, just until we're clear—"

The spark stirred. And spoke. Aloud, in my voice, at full volume, in the silence after the wardens' chant faded:

"*The door. The door. Bring her to the door.*"

Every torch on the mountainside swung toward us at once.

Jonah moved faster than I'd seen him move — snuffed the fire with his palm, kicked dirt over the ash, hauled me up by the wrist toward the cave's back wall where, I realized with a lurch, the darkness had a draft in it. Air moving. A way through.

"Back of the cave," he said, shoving a bundle at me — the medicine satchel, the tea tin, everything we owned. "There's a chimney shaft, old diggers' work. It's tight and it goes up and you're going to hate it."

"Where does it come out?"

"Nowhere good. Somewhere higher." He glanced back at the torchlight, closer now, orange fingers reaching into the cave mouth, and for one moment his composure slipped and I saw underneath it: a man who had sworn never to run back into Thornfell for anyone, running back into Thornfell for me, twice now, and counting the cost each time. "Wren. Whatever it's asking of you — whatever the door is — don't decide tonight. Promise me. Decisions made while running are how people end up damned."

Something in my chest twisted, because I had already decided, three nights ago, lying awake doing honest sums. But I said, "I promise," because it was mostly true. I hadn't decided *out loud*.

The hounds bayed, close enough now that I could feel the sound in my teeth, and the spark flared against my ribs like a heart remembering joy, and I climbed into the dark after Jonah, toward nowhere good, carrying a god that was calling me home.
