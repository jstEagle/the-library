## 6. A Bargain in Salt

The smuggler-queen's court was a salt cellar with delusions of grandeur.

That was my first thought, anyway, standing in the entrance of the Brinehall while a woman the size of a cathedral door looked me over like a horse she intended to buy cheap. The hall itself had been carved out of an old sea-cave back when this coast still had a sea in it — before the Church drained the shallows and called the empty basin "reclaimed," as if you could reclaim something that had never belonged to you. Now the cave sat forty feet above the tide line, its floor crusted white with salt that crept up the walls in frozen waves, glittering under a hundred tallow lamps like the inside of some enormous, blasphemous geode.

"Jonah Kavanagh." The queen said my name the way you'd spit out a fishbone.

I hadn't given it to her. That was the thing about smugglers — they knew things the way priests knew scripture, except they'd actually read it. "You have me at a disadvantage," I said. "You know my name, and I only know yours is Maren Ostrey, which everyone knows, so really I'm the one being generous here."

Maren Ostrey had been running contraband through the Salt Reach for longer than I'd been alive, or close enough to make no difference. They said she'd started as a mule carrying fever-medicine over the passes when the Church declared medicine a luxury of the faithless. They said she'd hanged two wardens from her own rigging and sent their boots back to the bishop with a polite note. They said a lot of things about Maren Ostrey, and the remarkable part was that most of them were true.

She sat on a chair that had probably been a throne in some drowned barony, salt-crusted oak and a cushion that might once have been velvet. Behind her, along the curved walls of the Brinehall, her court went about its business: clerks counting crates, a boy sharpening knives with the patience of someone who enjoyed it, three women playing cards over a barrel that I sincerely hoped had held pickled herring in a previous life. Nobody looked at us. In the Brinehall, not looking at things was a professional skill.

"You brought me a priestess," Maren said.

"I brought you a sick woman who happens to be a priestess." Wren was asleep in the corner on a pallet one of Maren's people had grudgingly provided, curled around herself like a comma, the spark hidden under three layers of wool and a story about pilgrimage. She'd slept maybe four hours in three days. The god inside her didn't seem to require rest, but it did seem to require warmth, and lately it had been taking hers.

"A priestess of the Silence," Maren continued, "who is, according to every warden between here and the mountains, carrying something the Church wants very badly. And you want passage through my territory to the Spire road." She leaned back. The throne creaked. Somewhere behind her left ear, I noticed, she'd tucked a knife, which seemed redundant given the collection visible at her belt, but I suppose redundancy is what keeps queens alive. "Tell me why I shouldn't sell you both to Brother Hale and retire to somewhere warm."

"Because Hale doesn't pay in anything you can spend."

That got a flicker of interest. "Go on."

"The Church pays in absolution, Maren. You can't buy rope with absolution. You can't pay your people. Half your routes are already choked because wardens don't take bribes anymore — they take confessions. You know this. It's why you're sitting in a cave instead of a harbor house." I shrugged, which hurt, because everything hurt; we'd come down off the White Road at a pace that would've embarrassed a landslide. "I'm offering coin. Real coin. Or the closest thing to it."

"And what's that?"

"My name."

The card game stopped. Not dramatically — no one gasped, no one stood up. But the dealing paused mid-shuffle, and the knife-boy lifted his head, and even the clerks' quills went quiet against the paper. In the Brinehall, silence was a currency too, and I'd just spent a fortune of it.

Maren studied me for a long moment. "The Church has a price on Jonah Kavanagh," she said. "Has had one for ten years. Two thousand crowns, last I heard, and Hale raised it in the spring. A name like that isn't coin. It's a mine."

"It's a mine that's about to collapse," I said. "Think it through. The Silence is shaking apart — you've seen it. Prayers getting answered in the high villages, worshippers burning, wardens deserting because they prayed for their mother's fever to break and it broke. The whole edifice is cracking. When it goes, what happens to a bounty?"

"Gone," said one of the card women, without looking up. "Bounties die with churches."

"Hush, Petra." But Maren's eyes narrowed, and I could see the abacus turning behind them. "Or the bounty doubles. Desperate institutions get generous."

"Desperate institutions also stop paying old debts to focus on new enemies. Ask anyone who ever lent money to a dying king." I took a step closer, salt crunching under my boots. "Here's the trade, laid out plain. You give us passage — carts, papers, safe houses, the whole machinery — all the way to the Spire road. In exchange, I sign over my name. Not the alias. The true one. Kavanagh of Thornfell, storm-called, king-killer, whatever the ballads are saying this month. You hold it. You can sell it back to me someday if you like, or sell it to whoever's buying names when the Church falls, or frame it over your hearth. But while you hold it, I can't be ransomed, tracked by it, or threatened with it. And neither can anyone who buys information about me from your network, because you'll control the supply."

It was a good pitch. I'd been composing it in my head for two days, mostly during the hours when Wren was asleep and I was watching the treeline and pretending I wasn't afraid. Good pitches have a flaw, though: they have to survive contact with the person you're pitching to, and Maren Ostrey had survived thirty years of kings, bishops, and weather by never letting anyone else finish the arithmetic.

"Why," she said, "does a man who spent ten years building aliases want to hand me the one name he can't replace? Men don't give up their last coin unless they're buying something worth more than the coin. What's worth more?"

I glanced at the corner. Wren had shifted in her sleep, one hand emerging from the blankets, fingers curled loosely like she was holding something invisible. Which, in fairness, she was.

"Don't," Maren said softly. "Don't answer that with a look. Looks are free, and I don't deal in free."

"She'll die without passage," I said. "Not might. Will. The thing she's carrying is killing her by inches, and the only place it can be put down is the Spire, and the only road to the Spire runs through your territory, and the Church has closed every other road with more enthusiasm than competence. So either you help us, or you watch a woman die slowly in your own hall, and then you explain to your people why the Brinehall is where miracles go to suffocate. Because that's what she's carrying, Maren. A miracle. Maybe the last one this country gets."

Silence again. The real kind this time, the kind with weight.

Then Maren laughed — a short, hard sound, like a door slamming. "A miracle. In my hall. You have some nerve, exile."

"I've been told nerve is my best feature. It's certainly my only consistent one."

* * *

They fed us in a side chamber that smelled of lamp oil and dried cod, because apparently even smuggler-queens ran on cod. Wren woke up halfway through the bowl of something stew-like and ate with the focused desperation of someone who'd forgotten food existed. I watched her eat and felt the strange, unearned tenderness that had been creeping up on me since the chapel at Kestrel Ford, and I pushed it down where I kept the other things I couldn't afford.

"You're pale," she said.

"I'm always pale. It's the climate of my personality."

"No." She set down the spoon. Her eyes had that quality they got when the spark was awake in her — a faint luminosity, like light behind frosted glass. "You're frightened. What did you promise her?"

I told her. All of it. She listened the way she listened to prayers, completely, which was worse than being argued with.

"You gave away your name," she said when I finished. "Your actual name. The one the Church —"

"The one the Church wants dead, yes. But dead things can't be used, Wren. That was the point. Ten years ago I buried Jonah Kavanagh in a snowdrift and walked out the other side as a medicine-runner with no history. It worked. It kept me alive. And now I've dug him up and handed him to a woman who sells secrets by the pound, because—" I stopped, because the honest ending of that sentence was standing in front of me holding a spoon.

"Because of me," Wren said quietly.

"Because of logistics," I said. "You're a logistical problem."

Something crossed her face — not quite a smile, more the place where a smile had recently been. "Jonah." She said my name carefully, like testing ice. "Do you understand what you did in there? Everyone in this country knows that name. Children know it. There are songs. The Church made sure of that — they needed a monster, so they built one out loud. And you just... handed it over. Gave the monster away."

"That was rather the idea."

"No." She shook her head. "The idea was strategy. What you did was surrender. Your name was the last thing they didn't have. Everything else — your family, your home, your country, ten years of your life — they already took. And you gave them the rest, or the next best thing to it, to keep me moving for another week." Her voice didn't rise. That was the terrible thing about Wren; she never rose, she just descended, calmly, into truths you'd been avoiding. "Exile was supposed to protect you. Wasn't it? That's what you told me in the cave. You stayed away so they'd have nothing left to take."

"They took it anyway," I said. "That's the joke. Exile doesn't protect anything. It just spreads the loss out over time so you can pretend it isn't happening."

Wren reached across the table. Her hand was warm — too warm, always too warm now, the spark burning through her like a candle through its last inch of wax — and she rested it on mine, and I let it stay there, which was its own kind of surrender.

"When this is over," she said, "we'll get it back. Your name. Whatever it costs, we'll—"

"Wren." I turned my hand over and held hers, briefly, before I could think better of it. "When this is over, there may not be an over. Let's not spend money we haven't stolen yet."

* * *

Maren summoned me at dusk to sign the bargain, and the signing was nothing like I expected, because nothing in the Brinehall was ever what it appeared. No contract. No ink. Instead they led me down a stair cut into the living rock, past storerooms full of contraband I pretended not to identify, to a small round chamber where the salt had grown into columns like the pipes of an organ, and in the center of the floor was a shallow basin of pure white crystal, worn smooth by decades of use.

"Salt remembers," Maren said, coming down the stairs behind me with a lantern. "That's the oldest law of the trade, older than any of your churches. Blood washes out, wax melts, paper burns. Salt holds. Every bargain struck in this room in the last sixty years is still in that basin. Break one, and the salt knows. And everyone who deals with me knows the salt knows." She set the lantern down. "So. The terms, spoken plainly, witnessed by the salt. You give me the name Jonah Kavanagh — the whole of it, the man and the myth — freely, for passage to the Spire road for yourself and the priestess, by cart and safe-house, until you stand at the foot of the tower. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

"Say it into the basin."

I knelt. The crystal was cold against my lips, cold as the snowdrift where I'd buried myself a decade ago, and for a moment I was twenty-two years old again, kneeling in the wreck of Thornfell, listening to the wardens read out the charges for crimes I hadn't committed while the ash came down like grey snow. *Jonah Kavanagh, called storm-wright. Jonah Kavanagh, king-killer.* They'd taken everything else with those words. Now I was handing over the words themselves.

"Jonah Kavanagh," I said into the salt. "Of Thornfell. Storm-wright. Son of Aldous and Merrit. Brother to Liss. The whole of it, freely given, for the road."

The salt in the basin shivered — just a tremor, a settling, the way sand shifts after a footstep. I told myself it was the vibration of my voice in the stone. Mostly I believed it.

Maren crouched beside the basin and spoke her half: "Passage given, passage kept. The name is held, not sold, not spent, not whispered, until the bearer stands at the Spire or the bearer is dead. Witnessed." Then she straightened up with a grunt that punctured the ceremony nicely, and looked at me with something that in a lesser woman might have been regret. "You know what you just did, exile?"

"Became slightly harder to ransom and considerably easier to forget?"

"You became a story with no owner." She picked up the lantern. "Stories without owners get rewritten. That's how your Church wrote yours in the first place — the real Kavanagh died in the snow, and what was left walking around was theirs. Now there's no real Kavanagh at all. Just whatever people decide to say." She started up the stairs, then paused. "For what it's worth — and it isn't worth much, coming from me — I read the old reports. Before your time, when I was still running mules. The storm that killed the royal line came out of a clear sky. No storm-wright alive could have called it. I always wondered why nobody asked the obvious question."

My heart did something complicated. "Which question?"

"Why the Church was so certain, so fast, when everyone else was still digging bodies out." She shrugged. "But certainty is their whole business, isn't it. Come on. Cart leaves at moonrise, and the driver is Petra, who cheats at cards and drives like the apocalypse is personal. Try to sleep."

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat in the side chamber with the cod smell and the lamp oil and thought about names.

Here's what nobody tells you about exile: it isn't a punishment. Punishment ends. Exile is a wound you keep open on purpose, because the alternative — closing it, deciding you're someone new, someone unburdened — feels like betraying the person you were before. For ten years I'd carried Jonah Kavanagh around like a stone in a pack, telling myself the weight was penance, that someday I'd set it down at the right altar and be forgiven. Tonight I'd set it down in a salt cellar, sold for cart-hire, and the world hadn't ended. The stone was gone and my shoulders ached where it used to sit, and I couldn't tell if I was lighter or hollowed out.

Wren found me there near midnight, wrapped in her blanket, hair loose, looking younger than the weight she carried should have allowed.

"You heard," I said.

"Maren tells her people everything. It's how she rules." She sat down across from me. Between us, on the table, the lamp flame bent toward her, just slightly, like a plant toward sun. Even the fire wanted to be near her. "How does it feel?"

"Like losing a tooth you'd gotten used to. My tongue keeps going to the gap."

She almost smiled. Then the smile faded, and she looked at her hands, and when she spoke again her voice had changed — lower, careful, the voice she used when the god was leaning close and she wasn't sure whose words would come out. "Jonah, there's something I need to tell you. About the storm. About Thornfell. Something I saw, or was shown, and I've been trying to find the way to say it, and I keep failing, because every way I say it breaks something, and I don't know how to fix what it breaks."

The lamp flame bent further. Outside, far off, a dog began to bark, and then stopped, as if reconsidering.

"What did you see?" I asked.

And Wren opened her mouth —

— and closed it, because the door banged open and Petra filled it, card-cheat, apocalypse-driver, knife at her hip and urgency all over her face.

"Riders on the coast road," she said. "Church livery. Six of them, moving fast, and they're not patrolling — they're coming here. Wardens don't ride at night unless they're led by black cloth." She looked at me. "Inquisitor's colors, exile. Someone sold word of you after all, and it wasn't the salt."

Behind her, from the depths of the Brinehall, I heard Maren Ostrey begin, very calmly, to give orders.
