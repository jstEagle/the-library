## 8. Hunters of the Silence

The river at Marrow's Crossing was the color of old pewter and about as welcoming. It ran wide and shallow over a bed of slate, chattering to itself like a man who'd been paid to talk, and on its far bank stood the ferry house — or what remained of one. The roof had gone to moss and crows. The ferry itself was a flat-bottomed scow tied to a post, looking exactly as trustworthy as everything else in this country, which is to say not at all.

"We could swim," Wren said.

"You can't swim."

"I could learn very quickly."

"It's snowmelt," I said. "You'd learn it in the time it takes to die of it." I said it lightly, because that's what I do with things that frighten me — I dress them up in a joke and send them out into company. But the truth underneath was simple: she was carrying a god's dying spark under her ribs, and I had promised myself, somewhere around chapter four of this disaster, that I would get her across this river alive.

I'd also promised myself, ten years ago, that I'd never come back for anyone. You'll notice how my promises are holding up. I'm considering giving them up entirely, like a vice.

Wren crouched at the water's edge and put her fingers in it, which she did with everything — streams, stones, the scarred wood of chapel doors. As if the world were a text she was still learning to read. "It's cold," she reported, unnecessarily.

"Thank you. I'll alert the almanacs."

She smiled. That was new, too — the smiling. Three days out from Kestrel Ford, where she'd healed a dying child and turned my whole understanding of what I was protecting inside out like a pocket, she had started smiling at small things. Cold rivers. Crows. My terrible jokes, which were not actually jokes but a defense mechanism with better posture.

Then the smile went away all at once, the way a candle goes out when someone opens a door.

"What?" I said.

"Someone's praying," she whispered.

* * *

Here's what you need to understand about prayers in Thornfell, now that the old gods have started waking up: they're loud. Not to everyone. To Wren they were apparently deafening — she'd spent the last three days flinching at hedgerows because some farmer behind them was asking a nameless something for rain, or luck, or his mother's arthritis. To me they were nothing. I'd been exiled from that whole conversation a decade ago, and the gods hadn't written since.

But even I heard this one.

It came down the valley ahead of the wardens, the way thunder comes before weather — except backwards, if you follow me, because the prayer came first and the storm was already here. A woman's voice, raw and cracked, carried on wind that shouldn't have been able to carry anything so specific:

*Old one, old one, under the stone — they've come for the priestess, hide her, hide her—*

And then, unmistakably, something answered. Not words. A pressure. The air over the far hills went strange and heavy, like the sky drawing breath.

"They're close," Wren said. She was on her feet now, and the spark under her skin had begun to glow faintly through her collarbones, gold light leaking out of her like she was a cracked lantern. That happened when she was frightened. It was going to be the death of us both, that light. "Jonah, they're *close*, I can feel the Silence moving — it's like cold water, it's coming up the valley—"

"How many?"

"I don't — dozens? More. And something else. Something that doesn't feel like men."

I knew what didn't feel like men. Everyone in Thornfell knew, though we weren't supposed to say it above a whisper, and preferably not that. The Church of the Silence didn't send mere wardens after spark-bearers. It sent inquisitors. And inquisitors came with hounds that had been prayed empty — animals with the prayer sucked out of them until nothing looked back from their eyes but obedience.

"Downstream," I said. "There's a ford below the ferry house. Slate bottom, knee-deep if we're lucky, chest-deep if we're honest. We cross fast and we're in the pines before they crest the hill."

"And if we're not lucky?"

"Then we're swimmers after all."

We ran. I want that recorded accurately, for posterity: we did not walk briskly, we did not make a strategic withdrawal, we ran like two people being chased by the concept of God's disapproval, which, in fairness, we were.

* * *

The ford was worse than I'd estimated, which is the ford's way. The water came up past our waists and shoved at us like it had opinions about our crossing, and the slate underfoot was slick as oiled glass. Wren went down twice. The second time I caught her by the arm and hauled her up, and she was shaking — not from the cold, or not only from the cold.

"It's him," she gasped. Water streaming off her chin. "The thing that doesn't feel like men. He's reading the valley, Jonah, he's — every prayer people make, he hears them, and he's asking them where we are—"

"Can he hear yours?"

"No. Mine's—" she pressed a hand to her sternum, where the spark sat. "Mine's hidden. But everyone else's isn't. There's a shepherd half a mile back who just thought about us without meaning to, and now the hounds know—"

The hounds announced themselves then, because subtlety has never been the Church's strong suit. They came over the ridge in a black spill, six of them, moving wrong — too smooth, legs working in unison like something operating them from inside rather than the reverse. Behind them came the wardens, gray-robed, and behind the wardens—

Behind the wardens came a man on a white mule, which should have been comic and was instead the most frightening thing I'd seen in ten years.

He rode slowly, unhurried, hands folded on the pommel of his saddle, and even at three hundred yards I could see that he was ordinary-looking. That was the horror of it. You expect monsters to look like monsters. Brother Hale looked like a schoolmaster. Like a man who would help you fix a wagon wheel and then ask, gently, while you worked, whether you had ever entertained doubts.

"He sees us," Wren breathed.

Of course he saw us. We were standing waist-deep in a river in broad daylight, glowing faintly gold. We were possibly the easiest thing he'd ever seen.

"Hale," I said.

"You know him?"

"I know *of* him. Everyone does. He's the one they send when they want a village emptied politely." I got my shoulder under Wren's arm and pushed her toward the far bank, toward the pines, toward anywhere that wasn't here. "Move. Don't run on the slate, slide — fall forward if you fall at all—"

"Jonah." Her hand closed on my sleeve. Her eyes were enormous and gold-flecked and terrified, and beneath the terror was something else, something that made my stomach drop before she even said it. "They'll take me. And you — they don't take you, Jonah. They don't take storm-callers. They burn them on the spot."

"Cheerful little religion you used to work for."

"Jonah—"

The lead hound hit the water.

I have seen a lot of bad things. I once watched a man get eaten by his own oxen during the famine years, and I've been shot, stabbed, and briefly employed by the smuggler-queen Sera Voss, which combines most categories of harm. None of it prepared me for watching those hounds cross the river. They didn't swim. They walked along the bottom, submerged, and the water around them went still and gray and dead, fish floating up belly-white in their wake. Where they moved, the river forgot how to be a river.

Wren made a sound I'll hear for the rest of my life.

And I did the thing I swore I would never do again.

* * *

Let me tell you about the storm that killed Thornfell's royal line, since I'll be calling its sibling in a moment and you deserve the family history.

Ten years ago I was a border-warden's son with a gift nobody wanted and everybody feared — the old blood, the storm-calling, the thing the Church called blasphemy with legs. On the night the palace burned, there was a storm over Thornfell City that no one called and no one could explain, lightning out of a clear sky, and it killed the royal line to a soul. And I was found at the edge of the city with my hands full of weather I couldn't remember making.

That's the official story, anyway. My version is more pathetic: I felt the storm building for hours and ran *toward* it like an idiot, trying to understand it, trying maybe to stop it, and arrived just in time to be blamed for it. Whether I called it and forgot, whether it used me, whether it simply passed through the place where I stood — I've turned it over ten thousand times and never gotten an answer. What I got instead was exile, a price on my head, and a decade of learning to keep my hands in my pockets whenever the sky got moody.

Because here's the thing about storm-calling: it's not a skill. It's a door, and once you've opened it, the weather knows where you live. Every storm since has leaned toward me like a dog hearing its name. For ten years I kept that door shut with both hands.

At Marrow's Crossing, with six dead-eyed hounds walking up a riverbed toward the girl I'd decided to save, I opened it.

I didn't decide to. I want to be clear about that. There was no moment of grim resolve, no noble sacrifice of principle. There was a hound's head breaking the surface twenty feet away, and Wren's hand in mine, and the oldest instinct I own — the one that broke my promise never to go back for anyone, the one that dragged me into a burning village in chapter three — and then the sky simply *answered*, the way it always should have, the way it had been waiting to.

The cloud came over the ridge like spilled ink.

Not gradually. Not building. One heartbeat the afternoon was gray and ordinary; the next, the light died, and the temperature dropped ten degrees, and the pressure changed so hard my ears popped, and the storm was *there*, sitting on the valley, and it was mine. I could feel every drop of it. I could feel the hailstones queuing up in the upper dark like soldiers awaiting orders, and the lightning coiling in the cloud-guts, patient, eager, *pleased* —

*There you are,* said the storm, in the wordless way storms speak. *There you are, there you are.*

"Jonah," Wren said, and her voice had gone strange. "Jonah, your eyes—"

"Get behind me."

"Your eyes are *silver*—"

"*Get behind me.*"

The first hound lunged out of the water onto the slate bank, and I gave the storm permission.

What happened next took perhaps forty seconds and will take longer than that to describe, because that's the unfair arithmetic of violence. The hail came down sideways, fist-sized, screaming, and it fell everywhere except in a circle around Wren and me — the storm parted around us like water around a stone, courteous, devoted, horrifying. Lightning hit the river. The whole crossing flashed white, and when my vision cleared, three hounds lay steaming on the bank, and the other three had stopped, and were standing in the shallows with their empty eyes fixed on me, and for the first time I saw something in them that might have been hesitation.

Or recognition. God help me. Or recognition.

The wardens broke. Gray robes scattering back up the hill, shields abandoned, one of them on his knees with his hands over his head, mouthing something — a prayer, probably, to a Church whose entire doctrine was that prayers go nowhere. I hope, for his sake, that this once, one was answered.

Brother Hale did not break.

He sat his white mule at the top of the ridge with hail hammering the ground around him and none of it touching him either — I noticed that, I noticed it with a lurch, that the storm avoided him the same way it shielded us, and filed it away in the drawer of things I don't want to think about. And he looked down at me across the ruined crossing, this ordinary schoolmaster face, and he *smiled*. Small. Almost kind.

Like a man confirming a suspicion. Like a man checking a name off a list he'd written ten years ago.

Then he turned the mule, unhurried, and rode away, and the hounds flowed after him like ink running back into the pen, and the storm — my storm, my beautiful terrible answer of a storm — rolled grumbling after them for a mile before I could persuade it to stop.

Persuade it. That's what it was. Like talking down a large, adoring animal that has just killed something for you and wants to know if you're pleased.

* * *

We made the pines. Of course we made the pines; nothing in this story is allowed to kill us yet, the plot has plans. We collapsed against a granite outcrop under the trees, soaked, hail-bruised, alive, and for a long moment neither of us said anything, because the storm was still overhead — gentler now, just rain, just weather, but present, attentive, leaning down toward me like a listener.

"Make it stop," Wren said quietly.

"It's stopping."

"It's *waiting*. Can't you feel it? It's waiting for you to need it again." She turned and looked at me, and whatever she saw in my face made her reach out and take my hand. My right hand. The one I'd raised. "Jonah. What did it feel like?"

And here is where I should have lied. Lying is one of my principal skills; I've had ten years of practice lying to wardens, to smugglers, to mirrors. I should have said *it felt like nothing, it felt like a tool, I hate it.*

"It felt like coming home," I said. "That's what it felt like. Ten years I told myself I was rid of it, and then it answered like I'd called it yesterday, like it had been standing outside my whole life with its hat in its hands. And it *loved* it, Wren. The killing part. It loved that best."

"That's not—"

"The storm that killed the royals," I said, and my voice came out flat and strange, a stranger's voice, "was shaped like this one. Exactly like this one. Same hail, same lightning, same — courtesy. The wardens who found me said they'd never seen weather act like it had a master. And now I've made it again. Same shape. Same *handwriting*." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Ten years I told myself I didn't do it. That it used me, or passed through me, or — anything. Anything but *me*. And I just stood in a river and wrote my signature across the sky, and Brother Hale watched me do it, and he *smiled*, Wren. He smiled like a man who's finally been proven right."

Wren's grip tightened on my hand. She was quiet for a moment, and in the quiet the rain ticked against the pine needles, and the storm breathed overhead, patient as a debt.

"Jonah," she said at last, very carefully, the way you step onto ice you suspect is thin. "The night of the storm. The night the royals died. Tell me exactly what you remember."

"Why?"

"Because I need to know what you *don't*."

I looked at her. Rain ran off her hood, off her chin, and under her wet collarbones the spark pulsed slow and gold, and her face was doing something complicated — grief and fury and guilt all fighting for the same ground — and she was looking at me the way you look at someone when you know something about them that they don't know themselves.

"Wren," I said slowly. "What did you see?"

Her mouth opened.

And above us, through the pines, from the direction of the ridge, a bell began to ring — a thin, cold, silver-sounding bell, the kind the Church hangs on its inquisitors' altars, the kind that means a hunt has become a crusade. Hale's bell. Calling something down the valley toward us that made the storm itself shift uneasily, my loyal monster pressing closer to my side like a dog that smells a bigger dog coming.

Wren closed her mouth.

"Not yet," she whispered. Not to me. To herself, or to the spark, or to whatever gods were left listening. "*Not yet.*"

The bell rang on.
