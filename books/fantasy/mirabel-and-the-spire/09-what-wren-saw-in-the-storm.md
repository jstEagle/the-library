## 9. What Wren Saw in the Storm

The storm Jonah called had a shape.

I want to be clear about that, because later — when I finally told him, when the truth came out of me like a splinter that had been in too deep to pull — he asked me how I'd known. And I said: it had a shape. The same shape as the one from ten years ago. I'd never seen the old one; I was a child in a chapel in the mountains when Thornfell's royal line died. But the god inside me had seen it. Had felt it. Had been there, at the edge of it, screaming into a silence that wouldn't listen.

So when the storm broke over the river crossing and Brother Hale's wardens scattered like startled crows, and Jonah grabbed my wrist and hauled me into the middle of it — into the eye, of all places, because of course he did, because the man has an instinct for the exact wrong place to stand — the god looked up through my eyes and said, quietly, the way you'd say *that coat belonged to your grandmother*:

*I know this weather.*

And then it showed me everything.

* * *

You should understand something about carrying a god. It is not like carrying a passenger. It is not even like carrying a child. It is like carrying a lamp made of someone else's memories, and every so often the wind turns the flame, and the light falls on things you were not prepared to see.

I had been prepared for fragments. That's what the spark had given me so far — pieces. A door. A border. A tower with its roots in two countries at once. I'd learned to hold the pieces loosely, the way you hold a bird: firmly enough that it doesn't escape, gently enough that it doesn't die.

This was not a fragment.

We stood in the eye of the storm — a circle of dead calm maybe thirty feet across, rain falling in walls around us, lightning walking the clouds like something pacing a cage — and the god opened its hand inside my chest, and the past poured in.

I saw the palace at Thornfell first. Not as I'd imagined it from chapel stories — all gold and solemnity — but as it actually was: drafty, overstaffed, smelling faintly of candlewax and wet dog, because the king kept hunting hounds indoors against all advice. I saw the royal line alive. That was the part that undid me before anything else could. I had grown up on their deaths; they were the founding wound of my faith, the reason the Silence ruled, the great grief we were taught to keep. I had never once thought of them as people who were *alive first*. There was a princess, maybe nine years old, racing a kitchen boy down a corridor in slippers. There was the king laughing so hard at some joke I couldn't hear that he had to put his cup down. Ordinary joy. The kind you don't notice until you're shown its absence.

Then the light shifted, and I saw the Church.

Not wardens. Not the grey-robed brothers who swept ash off chapel steps. I saw the inner order — the ones whose existence I had been taught was a rumor spread by heretics. They moved through the palace like water finding cracks. And they carried something. I couldn't name it at first; it looked like a vessel, a reliquary, but wrong — a container built to hold not a holy thing but an *absence*, a jar for silence itself, and when they opened it in the heart of the palace, the sound didn't go out of the room.

It went out of the *world*. Locally. Precisely. Like a surgeon cutting.

The gods of Thornfell lived in the borders — that much I knew, that much every priestess knew, though we'd been taught to call it superstition. What I hadn't known was what happened when you cut a god's country away from its god while the god still held it. The border didn't just fall. It *tore*. And a torn border in a world where storms are prayers and prayers have weight —

The sky answered.

That was the storm. Not Jonah's. Not anyone's, or everyone's. The sky itself, grieving and furious, coming down on the place where the royal line died, because the royal line were the last blood-anointed keepers of the old covenant, and killing them inside the tear was like striking a bell made of lightning.

The Church opened their silence-jar wider and let the storm take the blame.

I saw them do it. I saw a man — young then, dark-haired, with a face I would come to know far too well in the weeks after — I saw Brother Hale, ten years younger, standing in the wreckage with rain streaming off him, directing the wardens, arranging the evidence, pointing toward a boy on the palace steps who hadn't called anything, who had been *trying to stop it*, who had his hands raised against the storm like a man trying to hold up a ceiling.

Jonah Kavanagh was sixteen years old, and he was screaming at the sky to stop.

The god showed me that twice. It wanted me to be sure. Gods are careful about truths like this; they know what truths do to people.

*He did not call it,* the spark said, and its voice in my head was the voice of something very old watching something very familiar happen again. *They always find a boy. A boy with the gift and no family worth protecting him. The Silence cannot kill the gift, so it kills the reputation instead. It has done this before. It will do it again.*

I stood in the eye of a storm Jonah had made, and I understood that I was looking at the second half of a sentence the Church had written ten years ago.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about fury: it is cold first.

The heat comes later. What comes first is a kind of terrible clarity, a settling, like sediment dropping out of water. I stood in that circle of calm with rain roaring around me and I thought, with perfect composure: *they took ten years of that man's life.* Then, with slightly less composure: *and they sent me to die carrying a spark they probably manufactured the conditions for too.* Then the sediment finished dropping and I found at the bottom of the glass a single clear fact, and the fact was this:

Jonah does not know.

He has spent ten years believing he killed them. He has built his whole exile — his flinching from his own hands, his rule about never going back for anyone, the way he holds his arms like a man afraid of what they've done — on a lie. Not his lie. *Their* lie. He is carrying someone else's crime the way I am carrying someone else's god, except no one ever chose this for him, and no one ever told him he could put it down.

And I knew. I knew, standing three feet away from him, watching him watch the storm he'd called with an expression I now recognized as horror at his own reflection — and I said nothing.

Why? You can ask. Everyone would ask. Here is the answer, and I'm not proud of it, but I won't dress it up either: because the moment I understood the truth, I also understood what it would do to him, and I was afraid. Not of him. Of the *timing*. We were in the eye of a storm in Church territory with an inquisitor regrouping on the far bank and a border to cross and a Spire to open, and I thought — I *reasoned*, coldly, the way the Church reasons — that a man who has just learned he wasted ten years grieving a crime he didn't commit might not be steady enough to get us through the next three days. That he might break, or freeze, or turn around and walk into Hale's hands out of sheer ruined wanting to confess to *someone*.

So I swallowed it. I took the truth the god had handed me and I folded it small and I put it somewhere deep, in the same place I'd been putting my fear about the Spire, about the door, about what giving the spark back might mean for me. My chest was becoming quite a crowded place. Truths, fears, a dying god. There was barely room left for Wren.

*You should tell him,* the spark said. It sounded tired. Dying things often sound tired, but this was a different tiredness — the tiredness of a parent watching a child make a mistake it recognizes.

*Not yet,* I said.

*That is what the Church said about the royals,* it said. *There was never a right time. There is only the time you have.*

*I'll tell him,* I said. *After the crossing. After we're safe.*

And the god went quiet in the way that meant it had decided arguing with me wasn't worth its remaining strength, which is its own kind of rebuke, if you know how to hear it.

* * *

The storm lasted eleven minutes. I counted, because counting is what I do when I am holding something I must not drop.

When it ended — not faded, *ended*, all at once, like a hand unclenching — the river had risen and the wardens were gone, and Jonah turned to me with rain running off his chin and that look on his face. I knew the look now. I'd seen it in the cave, telling me about the storm that damned him. It was the look of a man checking whether the person beside him had finally seen the monster and was calculating the distance to the door.

"You should run," he said. "While it's quiet. While they're regrouping. I'm — " He gestured at the whole soaked sky, at the wreckage of the crossing, at himself. "You saw what I am."

What I saw, in fact, was a twenty-six-year-old man standing in the ruins of a lie he'd been fed at sixteen, apologizing for weather. But I couldn't say that. So I said: "I saw a storm that saved our lives."

"It's the same storm." His voice cracked on it, just slightly. "The shape. It's the same shape as — " He stopped himself, the way he always stopped himself, as if finishing the sentence would summon it.

"I know," I said.

And oh, the way he looked at me when I said that. Like I'd spoken in a language he'd only heard once, ten years ago, in the worst night of his life. "How do you know?"

"The god," I said. "It remembers. Gods remember weather the way priests remember psalms." Which was true. Every word of it true. Just not the whole truth, and I have since learned that a partial truth offered smoothly is more dangerous than a lie shouted from a rooftop, because lies get caught and partial truths get *believed*.

He stared at me a moment longer. Then he nodded, slowly, and something in his shoulders eased by a fraction — the fraction of a man deciding that if the god remembered the storm and hadn't fled, perhaps the storm wasn't the worst thing about him after all.

"Come on," he said. "The ford's flooded downstream. There's a footbridge the smugglers use, half a mile north. We can make it before Hale stops panicking about his drowned horses."

I followed him along the bank. The god walked with us, quiet, and I felt it watching me the way you watch someone carrying a full cup across a crowded room.

* * *

The footbridge was exactly as trustworthy as smuggler bridges usually are, which is to say it held, but complained the entire way, and I spent the crossing thinking about the nature of complaints. Bridges complain and are forgiven. Men complain and are exiled. Priests are not permitted to complain at all, which is why I was doing mine internally, at volume.

On the far side, in the trees, Jonah stopped and looked back at the river. Hale's banners were moving along the opposite bank, small in the grey distance, reorganizing. He'd lost us the crossing. He would not lose us twice; I understood that about him already, the way you understand a dog that has caught a scent.

"He'll follow us to the Spire," Jonah said.

"Yes."

"And the Spire needs you alive and willing, which means he won't risk arrows." Jonah rubbed a hand over his face. "Which means he'll try to take you the way he takes everything. By making you think surrender is mercy."

Something cold walked down my spine, because that was almost exactly what Hale had begun doing with his letters — the ones he'd started leaving pinned to trees along our route, addressed to *the Priestess of the Spark*, each one more reasonable than the last, offering terms, offering protection, offering, in the most recent one, a single line that had been sitting in my mind like a fishhook:

*Give us the spark and the storm-caller walks free. This is the only mercy the Silence permits, and it expires.*

I hadn't shown Jonah the letters. Another folded thing in the crowded drawer of my chest. I was beginning to suspect that drawer was going to fail under load, and that everything in it was going to come out at once, at the worst possible moment, the way drawers do.

"Wren." Jonah was watching me. "You went somewhere just now."

"I'm tired," I said, which was true, and which had become my most-used lie, which is a bad sign in a priestess, since lying is supposed to be outside our portfolio entirely. The Silence had taken even that from me, apparently — the ability to be honest without cost.

He accepted it, because he was tired too, and because men who believe themselves monsters assume any secret you keep from them is a sensible precaution rather than a wound. That's the cruelest part of what the Church did to him. They didn't just take ten years. They taught him to expect secrets, to expect betrayal, to read every hesitation as the beginning of a knife. So when I hesitated — and I hesitated, I couldn't help it, the truth was pressing against the back of my teeth like a fever — he saw it, and I watched him file it away behind his eyes in the place where he kept the storm, and I knew exactly what story he was telling himself about it.

He thought I was going to trade him.

I saw it land. I saw the small, resigned settling of it, the way a man accepts rain. *Of course she will,* that look said. *Everyone does, eventually. Why would she be different? She's known me three weeks. She's seen what I am.*

And I stood there in the dripping trees with the whole truth burning a hole through the drawer where I'd hidden it, knowing that one sentence — *they lied to you, Jonah, you were sixteen, you tried to stop it* — would have undone that look entirely, would have rebuilt him from the foundation up — and I said:

"We should keep moving."

Because I was afraid. Because I reasoned. Because I told myself there would be a better time, a safer time, a time after the next danger instead of inside it.

The god said nothing at all, and its silence was louder than Hale's bells.

* * *

We walked north through the afternoon, climbing, the valley of the crossing falling away behind us. Somewhere below, wardens were dragging drowned horses out of the shallows and writing reports in which the storm would be attributed to the storm-caller, because attribution is the Church's oldest sacrament. Somewhere ahead was the ascent to Thornfell, and the Spire, and a door that needed a spark, and a god that needed a home, and a man who needed a truth I was rationing out like medicine in a famine.

That night, in the shelter of a rock overhang, Jonah slept badly, the way he always did, twitching through whatever the storm-shape dreamed. I sat awake and held the spark in my hands and let it show me the princess in the corridor one more time, running in her slippers, laughing, alive.

"They killed children," I whispered to it. "Their own anointed keepers. And they blamed a boy."

*Yes,* the god said.

"And he thinks it was him."

*Yes.*

"Ten years."

*Yes.* A pause. The flame guttered, steadied. *Priestess. I have watched mortals carry false guilt for centuries. It is heavier than true guilt. True guilt can be paid for. False guilt only grows.* Another pause, longer. *And I have watched the ones who knew the truth stay silent to protect the carrier. It never protects him. It only means that when the truth arrives — and it always arrives — it arrives as a betrayal from the last person he trusted.*

"I'll tell him," I said again.

*You said that at the crossing.*

"After the Spire. When it's done. When nothing he learns can shake loose what we've built."

*Nothing you build on a hidden truth stays built,* the god said. *Ask the Church. Their whole cathedral stands on one, and look how long the mortar is lasting.*

I didn't answer. I sat in the dark with a dying god in my lap and a living man asleep six feet away, both of them trusting me, and I told myself the story I'd been telling myself since the river: that I was protecting him. That timing mattered. That there would be a moment, soon, on the mountain, when the truth could be set down gently instead of dropped.

Below us, far off in the black valley, a bell began to ring — one of Hale's camp bells, urgent, repeated. Signals. Orders moving. Wardens breaking camp in the night, taking a path that bent east, then north.

Toward the mountains. Toward the pass we'd need tomorrow. Toward the Spire.

And threaded through the bell-sound, faint but unmistakable, carried on the wind like a message that had learned to travel: the god lifted its head inside me and said,

*Hale knows another way in. And priestess — he is not bringing soldiers.*

*He is bringing the other jars.*
