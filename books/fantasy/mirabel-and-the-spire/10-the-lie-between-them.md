## 10. The Lie Between Them

There is a particular kind of silence that a woman makes when she is deciding whether to betray you. I'd heard it before — from smugglers, from lovers, once from my own brother — and it sounds like nothing at all. It sounds like a fire settling. Like someone breathing. It sounds, in fact, like the absence of anything worth noticing, which is exactly why you have to notice it.

I noticed it on the third night out of Kestrel Ford, while Wren sat across our small fire turning a strip of dried mutton over and over without eating any of it.

We were holed up in a shepherd's bothy above the Greywater valley, the kind of stone hut that had been abandoned so long the moss had opinions about the furniture. Rain came down outside in the patient, businesslike way rain does in the mountains, as if it had a contract to fulfill and no feelings about it either way. Inside: one fire, two blankets, half a wheel of cheese going hard at the edges, and a priestess of the Silence who could not stop looking at me sideways.

"You're staring," I said, without looking up from the knife I was sharpening. Sharpening a knife when you're nervous is a time-honored tradition. It gives your hands something honest to do while your gut does the lying.

"I'm not."

"You've looked at me eleven times since the cheese. I counted."

"That's absurd."

"It's also true. Twelve." I set the whetstone down. "Wren. Whatever it is, say it or don't. But stop feeding me that look like I'm a debt you're trying to remember how much you owe on."

She opened her mouth. Closed it. The mutton got turned again, thirteenth inspection, fourteenth glance. And then she said, in a voice so carefully casual it rang like a cracked bell:

"Jonah. When we reach the Spire — what happens to you?"

I should have answered honestly. That was my first mistake, and I want it noted that I recognized it as a mistake even as I made it, which is somehow worse than not knowing better.

"What happens to any of us," I said. "The god closes the border. Thornfell stops dissolving into whatever it's dissolving into. You go back to — well, there's no chapel left to tend, but you'll find something. Priests are adaptable. And I walk back down the mountain the way I came, and we never see each other again, and in ten years you'll be an old woman telling scandalized novices about the smuggler with the bad attitude."

The spark under her skin flickered. I'd learned to see it by then — a gold light behind her collarbone, visible only when the god inside her felt something strongly enough to leak. Right now it pulsed like a heartbeat running too fast.

"And if the Church catches us before then?" she asked. "If Brother Hale—"

"Hale's three days behind us and losing ground. The river took two of his wardens' horses and his dignity along with them."

"If he catches *you*," she said, and her voice did the thing again — that careful, level, rehearsed thing. "They have a price on you. A living price. Hale said so, back at the ford. He said they want Jonah Kavanagh alive."

"He said a lot of things. Men who burn villages for a living will say anything."

"But it's true, isn't it? They want you alive." She was leaning forward now, firelight catching the hollows under her eyes. "Why, Jonah? Why would the Church of the Silence — a church that burns people for *praying* — want a storm-caller delivered breathing?"

And there it was. The question I'd spent ten years not asking myself, wrapped up neat and handed back to me by a twenty-three-year-old girl who tended dead chapels.

"They want to make an example," I said. "Storm-caller kills the royal line, storm-caller dies publicly, story ends, everyone goes home reassured. It's theater. The Church loves theater. All that silence, and underneath it they're just —"

"Jonah."

"— just men in robes with a very good publicity operation —"

"*Jonah.*" She said my name the way you'd grab someone's sleeve at the edge of a cliff. "That isn't why."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the rain, a rock loosened on the slope above us and clattered down fifty feet of scree, and we both flinched, and for one stupid moment I was grateful to the mountain for interrupting, because I knew — I *knew* — that whatever she'd been about to say next was going to change the shape of my life, and I wasn't ready. I have never been ready. Readiness is for people whose lives haven't already ended once.

But the rock settled, and the moment didn't pass, it just sat there between us like a live coal.

"How would you know what their reason is?" I asked quietly.

Wren looked at the fire. Wren looked at her hands. Wren looked at the mutton, which by now had achieved a state of rotation best described as devotional. And then she lifted her chin and said, "Because of what the god shows me. In dreams. Fragments. It remembers things about Thornfell, about the night of the storm, and—"

"And?"

"And it's not clear yet. It's dying, Jonah. Its memories come through like light through dirty glass. I can't tell you something I don't understand myself."

Every word of that was plausible. Every word of it was probably even true. But I'd spent ten years among liars and smugglers and one truly gifted customs official in Vellmark, and I know what truth looks like when it's being rationed. She wasn't lying to me. She was deciding how much of the truth I could survive, and she hadn't finished the arithmetic.

"Wren," I said. "Look at me."

She looked at me. Her eyes were doing that thing where they're brave and terrified in equal measure, which I was beginning to suspect was simply her face's default setting.

"If there's something I need to know," I said, "tell me now. Not at the Spire. Not when it's convenient. Now, while I can still do something with it besides bleed."

"I don't — I can't be sure yet —"

"You're sure enough to ask what happens to me afterward. You're sure enough to bring up Hale wanting me alive. You're sure enough to have started this conversation three times tonight and swallowed it twice." I kept my voice level. It cost more than I let show. "So here's what I think. I think the god showed you something about me. About the storm. And I think you're sitting on it because you're not certain I'll still help you get to the Spire once you tell me."

Her breath caught. The spark flared bright enough to throw shadows up the bothy wall — my shadow, hers, and a third shape that moved wrong, that leaned when she leaned and reached when she reached, and I decided firmly not to think about that.

"That's not fair," she whispered.

"No," I agreed. "It isn't. Most true things aren't."

* * *

She didn't tell me that night. She rolled into her blanket with her back to me and the spark dimming under her skin like a banked coal, and I lay awake listening to the rain and conducting a full audit of my life, which is an activity I recommend to no one.

Here is what ten years of exile teaches you about yourself: it teaches you that trust is a currency, and you left most of yours in a country that no longer exists. What I had left of it, I had put — against every instinct, against the explicit terms of my own survival — into a priestess I'd pulled out of a burning village because my hands moved before my brain could file its usual objection.

And now my brain wanted its objection heard.

Because here was the arithmetic I couldn't escape. Wren needed to reach the Spire. The Church wanted Wren stopped and me alive. I was, functionally, the only thing standing between her and a cell — but I was also, potentially, the thing that could buy her way *out* of one. A priestess carrying stolen divinity doesn't get mercy. A priestess who delivers the man who murdered the royal line? That's a different conversation. That's a conversation with chairs offered and tea poured.

I knew how Hale would frame it. I'd heard him at the river crossing, that silk-over-iron voice: *Kavanagh is the Church's errand, not its enemy. Bring him home, daughter, and all sins are negotiable.*

All sins are negotiable. Including hers. Including the child she'd healed at Kestrel Ford, maybe, if the trade was good enough.

I turned it over and over until the fire burned down to orange teeth. And the worst part — the part I keep coming back to, the part that shames me even now — is that some cold practical corner of my mind started doing *its* arithmetic in response. If she means to sell me, do I leave first? Do I tie her hands? Do I —

No. I shut that door. But the fact that it existed to shut, that I had to shut it at all, told me everything about what ten years had made of me. Jonah Kavanagh, who once went back into a collapsing mine shaft for a stranger's dog. Jonah Kavanagh, who now lay in the dark calculating whether to abandon a sleeping girl in the rain because she might, might, might trade him to save her god.

Exile doesn't break you all at once. It files you down. Ten years of filing, and apparently what was left was this: a man who counts how many times someone glances at him across a cheese.

* * *

Morning came gray and dripping. Wren was up before me, which was unusual, packing with the brisk efficiency of someone who has made a decision, which was more unusual still.

"We move today?" I asked.

"We move today." She wouldn't quite meet my eyes. "The god is restless. It wants the Spire. It's been pulling north all night like a dog on a rope."

"Mm." I kicked dirt over the fire pit. "And which direction does *Wren* want to pull?"

That landed. I saw it land — a small flinch, quickly mastered. "North," she said. "Same direction. It always has been."

"Has it." I slung my pack. "Funny. Because last night it sounded like there was a fork in the road. One path leads to the Spire with me. One path leads somewhere else, somewhere you were thinking about pretty hard while you rotated that mutton forty times."

"Thirty-eight," she said automatically, and then, furiously, as if angry at herself for the deflection: "Jonah, I would never —"

"Never what?" I turned on her, and I heard my own voice come out harder than I intended, ten years of filed-down edges showing all at once. "Say it. Finish the sentence you keep starting. You'd never what, Wren?"

The rain drummed on the bothy roof. The spark guttered low, like it was holding its breath too.

"I would never trade you," she said. "Not to Hale. Not to anyone. Not for the Spire, not for the border, not for —" Her voice cracked clean through. "You pulled me out of a fire. Nobody has ever — my whole life I have served a god who asks everything and answers almost nothing, and you, you *smuggler*, you walked into smoke for me without even knowing my name, and you think I'd sell you?" She was crying now, angrily, wiping her face with the heel of her hand like the tears were an administrative error. "I'm not telling you something because it will hurt you, not because I'm trading you. There's a difference. There is a *difference*."

"Then tell me anyway."

"I *can't*. Not yet. Not until I understand it, because if I say it wrong —" She pressed both hands flat against her sternum, over the spark, and the light there flared white-gold and desperate. "If I say it wrong, you'll do something reckless, and I need you alive and I need you with me, and those are the only two things I am sure of in the entire world right now. Can you accept that? Can you accept *not knowing*, for a few more days, on my word that it isn't betrayal?"

And there it was. The whole question, laid out on the wet flagstones between us.

On my word. From a woman I'd known nine days. Who served a god I couldn't see, carrying secrets I couldn't verify, asking me to extend credit on faith — which, I note for the record, is precisely the transaction the Church of the Silence had outlawed, and possibly the reason the old gods were dying in the first place. Nobody lends trust anymore. Everyone pays cash up front or walks away.

Ten years ago, I'd have walked away. Ten years ago I'd have been down the scree slope before she finished the sentence, and I'd have told myself it was prudence, and the filing would have gone one layer deeper.

Instead I heard myself say: "Three days."

Wren blinked. "What?"

"You get three days. To understand whatever it is. After that, you tell me — right, wrong, incomplete, in fragments, whatever you've got. Because here's my word in return, Wren: I don't need the truth to be comfortable. I've lived ten years inside a lie that wasn't even mine. One more uncomfortable truth won't kill me." I picked up my staff. "What'll kill me is being managed. Being *handled*. Being the cargo everyone's protecting from information. So. Three days."

She wiped her face a final time. Underneath the tears she looked, for the first time since the ford, less frightened — not unafraid, but aligned, like something crooked had been set back toward straight.

"Three days," she agreed. Then, quieter, almost to herself, with her hand still pressed over the spark: "It's about the royals, Jonah. That much I'll give you now, so you can sleep. Whatever the god is trying to show me — it's about who really killed them."

The rain kept falling. The mountain stood around us, old and indifferent, wearing its new green like a widow who'd finally stopped dressing in black and couldn't explain why.

I started walking north. Behind me, I heard Wren fall into step, and above us — faint, at the very edge of hearing, in a register that wasn't sound so much as pressure — the spark hummed once, like a struck bell.

Three days. I'd given my word.

I just wished I believed I'd survive receiving what she owed me.
