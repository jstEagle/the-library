## 11. The Ascent to Thornfell

**JONAH**

The border of Thornfell wasn't a line. It was a feeling — like walking into a room where someone had just been crying.

I'd crossed it a hundred times as a smuggler, back when it was only a line on other men's maps, and I'd never felt anything. Now the air changed at the old boundary stone, half-swallowed by heather, and Wren stopped walking like she'd hit glass.

"It remembers us," she said.

"Countries don't remember."

"This one's starting to." She touched the stone with two fingers, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you weren't sure was friendly. "Can't you feel it? Like a name being said very quietly, over and over, under the wind."

I could, actually. That was the problem. For ten years I'd built my life on the assumption that Thornfell was dead — that whatever had died in the storm had stayed dead, and my exile was just geography. Now the dead country was breathing under my boots, and every mile north made it louder.

We climbed. The White Road gave out after the second pass and became a smuggler's track, then a goat path, then a suggestion. Wren walked it like she'd been born to mountains, which annoyed me in a way I didn't examine too closely. I walked it like a man whose knees had opinions.

The first village appeared at dusk on the third day: Harrow Fell, twelve houses, a chapel with its roof caved in, and smoke rising from three chimneys.

"People," Wren breathed.

"People who are about to have a very bad evening," I said, because I'd already seen the second smoke — not hearth-smoke but pyre-smoke, greasy and black, coming from behind the chapel.

She was moving before I finished the sentence. I followed, because following her had apparently become a thing I did now, and because leaving her alone in a country where the Church burned people for praying was not a thing I could do with my conscience, such as it remained.

Behind the chapel, in a yard of blackened grass, they were burning a prayer.

Not a person — not yet. A bundle of paper, pinned under stones, pages fluttering orange as the fire ate them. An old man knelt beside the fire with his hands bound, and a warden in grey stood over him reading from a ledger, reciting the charges like a grocer listing prices.

"—answered prayers, count of nine. Unlicensed devotion, count of one. Speaking the old names aloud, count of—"

"Fourteen," said the old man, through a split lip. "You missed fourteen. I said them fourteen times."

I'll give the warden this: he didn't flinch. He raised his cudgel with the patience of a man doing arithmetic.

Wren stepped out of the shadows and said, in a voice I felt in my teeth rather than heard:

*Stop.*

The fire went out.

Not guttered. Not stamped. *Out*, all at once, like a held breath released, and in the sudden dark every page of the burning prayer lay unburnt on the grass, white as snowfall.

The warden turned. The old man turned. And then, God help me, so did half the village — faces appearing at windows, at doorways, drawn by whatever that voice had been, which was not entirely Wren's.

"You're her," the warden said. Not afraid. Worse — reverent. "The spark-bearer. Brother Hale has described—"

"He's described me wrong," Wren said, in her own voice this time, ordinary and young and shaking slightly. "There's nothing to describe. Go home."

And here is what I learned about a country remembering itself: the warden looked at her, and then he looked at the unburnt pages, and then he picked up his cudgel and his ledger and *left*. Walked off down the mountain road without another word, the way a man leaves a room where something larger than him has just stood up.

The old man gathered his prayers into his arms like a rescued child.

"Fourteen times," he told Wren, beaming through the blood. "And on the fourteenth, the well water came sweet again. My wife drank it before she passed. Sweet water, after six years of bitter. That's how I knew the gods were waking. That's how I knew to keep saying the names."

Wren knelt beside him. I kept watch on the road and pretended not to listen, which is a skill I've refined to an art.

"What names do you say?" she asked.

"All of them. But mostly hers." He nodded toward the dead chapel. "The Lady of the Threshold. She was the door-keeper, before. My grandmother said the Spire was her house."

I felt that one go through me like cold water. The Spire. Even here, twelve houses and a ruined roof away from everything, the Spire.

"We're going there," Wren said quietly. "To her house."

The old man looked at her for a long moment, and then at me, standing in the dark with my hood up like the criminal I officially was.

"Then take the high track past Sorrel Water," he said. "And when you pass the shrine at the ford, leave an offering. She likes bread. Or salt. Or, if you've got it—" he smiled, gap-toothed, "—a story. She always liked those best."

**WREN**

The god dreamed while we climbed, and I had learned to let it dream through me, the way you let a fever talk.

It came in fragments, usually at dusk or dawn, when the light couldn't decide. I would stop walking and Jonah would stop walking, and I would say things I didn't know I knew.

*A threshold of white stone. A bell that rings downward instead of up. Count the steps — no, don't count them, counting is how you get lost. The door is where the country is thinnest. The door is where I am dying.*

Jonah wrote it all down in a little grease-pencil notebook, in handwriting far too neat for a smuggler. He never asked me if the fragments frightened me. I think he already knew the answer, and thought asking would be cruel, and I loved him a little for the silence, though I hadn't said so, and didn't plan to. Some things you carry better unwrapped.

On the fifth day we came to Sorrel Water, and I understood why the old man had warned us.

The village was still burning.

Not the houses — the *people*. Three pyres in the square, and wardens everywhere, grey coats and iron lanterns, dragging families out into the snow-mud while a tall figure in black watched from the chapel steps with his hands folded. I didn't need anyone to tell me it was Hale. You can feel an inquisitor the way you feel a draft under a door.

"They answered prayers here," Jonah said flatly. Beside me, tight-jawed. "Look. They've strung the offerings up as evidence."

They had. Dried flowers, bread crusts, little carved tokens — the small change of devotion, hung from the chapel railings with tags, catalogued. A whole village's tenderness laid out like stolen goods.

"We go around," Jonah said.

"Yes."

"We can't help them."

"I know."

"We could die trying, and they'd still burn tomorrow."

"*I know*, Jonah." My voice cracked on his name, and the spark stirred under my ribs, waking, interested, hungry to be used — and that was the horror of it, that some part of me wanted to walk down there and burn the wardens' lanterns out and call it mercy. "That's why we're going around. Because if I go down there, I won't stop at mercy."

He looked at me then — really looked, the way he rarely let himself — and nodded once, and we cut east into the trees.

But the god wouldn't go around. As we climbed above the village, higher and colder, it rose in me like floodwater and spoke through my mouth, and this time it wasn't fragments. This time it was a sentence, whole and terrible:

*I taught them to pray. Every one of them. And now I cannot even hold their hands while they burn.*

I sat down in the snow. I couldn't help it. Grief for a god is a strange thing — it has nowhere to put itself, no ritual, no grave to visit. It just sits in your chest and breathes.

Jonah crouched next to me. He didn't touch me. He took off his pack, dug out the heel of a loaf, and set it on a flat stone facing the valley.

"For the Lady of the Threshold," he said. "The old man said she liked stories. Bread will have to do. I'm bad at stories."

"That's not true. You told me about the storm."

"The storm isn't a story. It's a confession with weather in it."

Despite everything — the pyres below, the god drowning in my chest — I laughed. Just once, sharp, surprised out of me. He almost smiled. Almost. With Jonah, the almost was the whole gift.

We left the bread on the stone and climbed on, and below us Sorrel Water burned until dawn.

* * *

**JONAH**

Here's what nobody tells you about a country remembering itself: it's beautiful first.

Every ruin we passed had flowers in it. Not weeds — flowers, specific ones, growing in patterns, in rows, in shapes that used to mean things. Bellflowers ringing out of a collapsed granary. White blossoms opening along a broken wall in a line as straight as any mason ever laid. The land was decorating its own corpse, and it was heartbreaking, and I hated how much I loved it.

"Thornfell was a garden country," Wren said, walking ahead of me on the ridge. "Before the Silence. Everyone knows that part. What they don't know is that the gardens were prayers. You planted in the god's name and the god grew it with you. The whole border was one long hedge of it."

"And now?"

"Now it's remembering what it planted." She turned, and the wind took her hair sideways, and for a second she looked less like a priestess than like something the mountain had grown itself. "Jonah. Do you feel how it's watching us?"

I did. I'd been feeling it for two days. The sensation of being observed by terrain — of every ridge having attention.

"It's not hostile," she said, answering the question I hadn't asked. "It's *hopeful*. That's worse, somehow."

"It's much worse."

By the seventh day we could see it: the Spire, a grey needle against the northern sky, impossibly far and impossibly present, the way large things are. Half of it looked solid. The other half looked like a memory of a tower, edges softening into sky, and my eyes kept sliding off the seam between the two.

Wren stared at it and shivered — not from cold.

"It's dimmer," she whispered. "When the spark first woke me, I could feel the Spire like a heartbeat. Now it's..." She pressed a hand to her sternum. "Fainter. Fainter every day. Whatever's holding it half-in-the-world is running out."

"Then we walk faster."

"Jonah." She didn't turn from the view. "If we fail — if the door doesn't open — what happens to Thornfell?"

"Nothing. It's already happened. Thornfell dissolved ten years ago. It's been a nameless ruin ever since; the maps just haven't admitted it."

"But the villages. The people. The old man with his fourteen names."

"They're not in Thornfell," I said, and heard how cruel it sounded, and said it anyway, because she deserved the truth more than comfort. "They're in what's left of it. And what's left is thinning. Another generation, maybe two, and there won't be enough country under their feet to stand on. The Church calls it cleansing. The mapmakers call it reassignment. The truth is the country is *dying*, Wren, and has been since the storm, and everyone's been too polite or too frightened to say it out loud."

She was quiet a long time.

"Then we're not saving it," she said finally. "We're sitting with it while it dies."

"If we're lucky."

"And if we're not lucky?"

"If we're not lucky, the Church gets the spark, cages it with the others, and finishes the job."

That night we sheltered in a shepherd's bothy with a fire small enough to hide and a view of the Spire through the door crack, and I took first watch, and somewhere past midnight Wren started talking in her sleep.

Not dreaming. *Translating.* The god spoke through her even now, low and urgent, and I sat with my knees drawn up and listened to a dying deity dictate its last wishes through a sleeping girl, and I wrote down every word.

*A door needs two sides. I am one side. Find me the other. The storm-child knows the other side. Ask the storm-child. Tell him — tell him the storm was never his sin. Tell him—*

She rolled over and the thread snapped, and she slept on, ordinary, exhausted, twenty years old.

*Tell him the storm was never his sin.*

I sat with that until the fire burned down to red memory, and I decided — carefully, deliberately, the way you decide not to open a letter — that sleep-talk was not evidence. That gods dreamed in riddles. That ten years of guilt did not dissolve on the testimony of a whisper.

I decided it, and I didn't believe it, and I wrote the words down anyway, in my too-neat handwriting, and closed the notebook like a coffin lid.

Above us the Spire stood against the stars, half real, half remembered, and around its base — I saw it then, for the first time, this close — fires. Many fires. Ringing the tower like a crown of lit candles.

Grey coats. Iron lanterns. The Church had arrived first.

"Wren," I said softly, and she woke instantly, the way soldiers wake, and looked where I pointed, and went very still.

"How many?" she asked.

"More than we can fight. Fewer than can climb fast." I was already repacking the bag. "We go tonight. We go straight up the pilgrim stair, through the middle of them, and we pray your Lady of the Threshold still likes stories."

Because here was the thing I hadn't told her, the thing the old man's words had lodged in me like a splinter: the shrine at the ford was two hours behind us, and I'd gone back for it alone, on the fourth night, while she slept. I'd left a story at the shrine of the Lady of the Threshold. My story. The storm, the royals, the exile — all of it, spoken aloud to a piece of standing stone in the dark, the first time in ten years I'd said the whole truth to anything that might be listening.

Whether anything had listened — whether something had *accepted* — I supposed we were about to find out.

I shouldered the pack. Below us, the Church's fires ringed the dying god's house, and above us the sky was clear, and clear skies, in my experience, were the surest sign that weather was coming.

"Wake up, Thornfell," I muttered to the dark. "Your exile's coming home."
