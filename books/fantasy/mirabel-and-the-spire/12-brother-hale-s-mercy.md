## 12. Brother Hale's Mercy

I want it on the record — whoever keeps records now that my god is dying and the Church of the Silence burns its own congregations — that I did not wander off. Jonah went to scout the ford. He said, "Stay here," in the voice he uses when he means *stay here or I will come back and be insufferable about it*, and I stayed. I sat against a fallen birch with my hood up and my hands around the spark like a girl warming them at a stove, and I was *obedient*, which for me is roughly as common as snow in high summer.

The wardens took me anyway.

They came out of the trees the way weather comes — no warning you could have used, just a sudden wrongness in the air and then six men in grey with iron rods and faces like closed doors. One of them put a hand over my mouth before I'd finished deciding to scream. Another wrapped something cold around the spark through my shirt, some kind of null-iron cage, and the god inside me went quiet as a snuffed candle.

That was the worst part. Not the hands. Not the hood dropped over my head. The *quiet*. Ten days of carrying a dying god, ten days of whispers and warmth and fragments of a door, and then nothing, and I understood in one sick lurch how a widow feels at the funeral — present at the center of an absence.

They marched me downhill for hours. When they finally pulled the hood off, we were standing in the shell of a wayhouse, one of the old border stations Thornfell had kept before the borders stopped mattering to anyone but ghosts. Rain came through the burned roof. A fire hissed under what remained of the mantel, giving off more smoke than light.

And sitting before it, reading — actually *reading*, a small book held delicately in long fingers, as though the world were a library and he had all the time ever printed — was Brother Hale.

I had never seen him. I knew him anyway. Everyone who carried anything the Church wanted knew Brother Hale the way sailors know a storm's name: not the man, the reputation. The warden who had never lost a quarry. The priest who had burned eleven villages in the eastern valleys and wept at every one, they said, genuinely wept, and lit the next fire with the same hands.

He looked up from his book with mild, intelligent eyes and said, "You're shorter than I expected."

"You're exactly as tall as everyone says," I said. "It's disappointing. I hoped the stories were exaggerating."

Something flickered across his face — not anger. Interest. He set the book down, squared it carefully with the edge of a stone, and gestured to a stool near the fire.

"Sit, priestess. You're wet. So am I. Let us be wet together, briefly, like civilized people."

I sat. Not because he frightened me — though he did, the way deep water frightens, quietly and completely — but because my legs had been marching since dawn and the fire was warm and I have never been a martyr to discomfort. Martyrdom, I've always felt, should at least come with a chair.

* * *

"You are Wren of the Silence," Hale said. "Third daughter of a tanner in Lowmarsh. Entered the cloister at nine because your mother wanted one child fed by the Church and you were the one who could sit still longest." He said it without cruelty, the way a man reads a shipping manifest. "You were a mediocre student. Your meditations were described by your preceptor as 'sincere but noisy.' You talked to the god during silent prayer. Constantly. It drove two older sisters to request transfer."

"That's slander," I said. "It drove three."

The ghost of a smile touched his mouth and vanished. "And yet the god chose you. Out of every sister in every cloister in the mountains, the last spark of the old faith fell into the lap of the novice who couldn't stop chattering during meditation." He leaned forward. Firelight moved in his eyes. "Do you know why?"

"Because I'm special?" I offered. "Because destiny has a sense of humor? Because—"

"Because you were already talking to it," Hale said. "Before it woke. For years, into a silence that never answered, you spoke anyway. The god didn't choose a vessel. It chose a *voice*. Someone who would carry it out loud." He folded his hands. "Which brings me to my offer, and I want you to understand, Wren of Lowmarsh, that I am offering this once, and I do not make offers twice."

Outside, the rain thickened. Somewhere up the slope, toward the ford, a nightbird called and got no answer.

"The spark dies," Hale said simply. "You know this better than anyone. It has been fading since the day it entered you. Every miracle costs it; every day costs it. In a month, perhaps two, it gutters out regardless of what any of us do, and you will be an empty girl standing in a doorway with nothing behind her." He let that settle. He was good at letting things settle; you could feel him weighing each silence like a coin. "The Church cannot permit the old gods to wake. Not out of hatred — you may believe that or not, it changes nothing — but because what wakes in the mountains now is not only the gentle ones. There are things under Thornfell that prayed-to became hungry, and the Silence was built to keep them sleeping. Every answered prayer is a crack in a dam. You are carrying a cupful of the flood and calling it holy water."

"And your offer," I said, because he was circling and I wanted him to land.

"The spark," Hale said. "Given freely, to me, tonight. In exchange: the smuggler walks free. No warrant, no hunt, no record. Jonah Kavanagh leaves these mountains alive and unmolested, with his name cleared of the storm — I can do that, priestess, I have the authority, and unlike most of my brothers I keep my word down to the letter." His voice softened by a degree, which was somehow worse than if it had hardened. "He has spent ten years paying for a crime he did not commit. You can end that tonight. You can give him back his name, his country, his future. All it costs is a god that is dying anyway, and a few weeks of your life that will feel, I promise you, like years."

The fire popped. I looked at my hands.

Here is the thing nobody tells you about being offered a clean, generous, monstrous bargain: the horror isn't in the monster's terms. The horror is in how long you take to refuse.

Because I thought about it. God forgive me — forgive whichever of us is still listening — I thought about it. I thought about Jonah's face when he talked about the storm, that careful blankness he wore over a wound ten years old. I thought about handing him back his life like returning a coat he'd lent me. I thought about the spark, small and guttering in my chest, and how tired it was, and how it had told me once, half-dreaming, *it shouldn't have been me, little bird, I'm too heavy for you* — and wouldn't giving it to Hale be a mercy? To the god, even? A quick death in iron instead of a slow one in a girl?

And underneath all of it, quiet as sediment, another thought I didn't want to look at directly: if the spark left me, I would stop being hunted. I could go home. There was a chapel in Lowmarsh where my mother still lit candles, and a river, and a life that had been interrupted at nineteen and might be resumed like a book someone else had been minding.

Hale watched me think. That was the most frightening thing about him — he didn't press. He had made his offer and he simply *waited*, patient as erosion, letting the fire and the rain and my own clever treacherous heart do his arguing for him.

"You're wondering if I'm lying about Jonah," he said at last. "Check. Ask your god — oh." A thin smile. "Forgive me. It's asleep in the cage. Well. Then ask me anything, and weigh my answers. I have burned villages, priestess, and I will answer for them before whatever judges us both. But I have never broken a bargain, and I will not break this one. The Church of the Silence keeps the Silence. We do not keep lies. Lies make noise."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then we proceed to the unpleasant portion of the evening, which I have genuinely tried to avoid, and which I will not describe in advance, because anticipation does half my work for me." He picked his book back up, as though the audience were concluded. "Take whatever time you need. The rain is on our side; Kavanagh won't come looking until dawn, and by then you'll have decided."

* * *

I don't know how long I sat there. An hour. Three. Long enough for the fire to burn down to a red eye and be fed twice by a warden who never once looked at me. Long enough for the rain to stop and start again. I turned the bargain over so many times it wore smooth as a river stone, and somewhere in the turning, without noticing the moment it happened, I stopped weighing it and started knowing.

It wasn't a grand moment. I want to say there was lightning, a voice, a sign. There wasn't. There was just me, cold and damp on a stool in a burned wayhouse, realizing that I had been doing arithmetic with the wrong numbers.

Because here is what Hale's offer assumed, and what I had been assuming too: that the spark was a thing I carried, separate from me, spendable. That Jonah's life and the god's life were coins on opposite sides of a scale, and mercy meant choosing the heavier grief.

But the spark wasn't a coin. It was a *voice*. It had whispered to me in the dark of the chapel at Kestrel Ford while I healed a child neither of us could save alone. It had shown me the Spire in dreams, grieving over a door like an old woman grieving over a house. It had called me *little bird* in a tone I have only otherwise heard from my mother, once, the year the fever took my sisters. It was dying, yes. Everything I love has a habit of doing that. But you do not hand your dying mother to the people who poisoned her because her death is coming anyway and they've offered to pay your brother's debts for it.

And Jonah — Jonah, who broke his own oldest rule to pull me out of the smoke, who tells the story of his own damnation like a man reciting someone else's sins, who watches me sleep by the fire with an expression he thinks I don't see, the look of a man checking whether something good is still real —

I could buy him his name. And he would spend the rest of his life knowing it had been bought with the death of the last god and the breaking of the girl who carried her, and Jonah Kavanagh, I was fairly sure by then, was constitutionally incapable of spending a gift like that. It would hollow him. Hale wasn't offering me Jonah's life. He was offering me a different kind of exile for Jonah, one with clean papers, and calling it mercy.

"Brother Hale," I said.

He looked up from his book. He had, I noticed, been watching me the whole time over the top of it. Of course he had.

"No," I said.

"Elaborate," he said pleasantly. "Refusals interest me far more than acceptances. Acceptance tells me about a person's fear. Refusal tells me about their architecture."

"Then here's my architecture." My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "You're right that the spark is dying. You're right that I'm tired, and scared, and that part of me wanted to say yes so badly I could taste it — you built your whole offer on that taste, and it was good work, and I hope it keeps you warm at night. But you've made one mistake."

"Only one? Flattering."

"You think love is a debt that can be paid on someone's behalf." I stood up. My legs had gone stiff and I nearly swayed and I decided, in that moment, that I would fall down before I'd show it. "Jonah doesn't need his name back badly enough to buy it at that price, and I don't love him — *care* about him — cheaply enough to sell a god to cover the cost. If you want the spark, Brother Hale, you'll have to take it. And it will cost you more than you've budgeted. It always does. That's the thing about living currency — it spends itself fighting."

Hale regarded me for a long moment. Then he closed his book — gently, reverently, the way he probably handled everything except people — and sighed.

"I did hope," he said, "that you'd be sensible. Sensible women live longer." He rose, and the wardens shifted around the room like furniture rearranging itself into a threat. "You should also know, before we proceed, that your refusal changes nothing about Kavanagh's fate. I offered you his life as a courtesy. I withdraw the courtesy. He is already condemned — the storm at the river crossing saw to that. Six witnesses felt storm-shape in the air, priestess. The same shape as ten years ago. He has signed his own sentence in weather."

Cold went through me that had nothing to do with the wet. "You *knew*. You let me sit here all this time and you knew you'd already condemned him — the offer was never real."

"It was entirely real," Hale said, with genuine, terrible sincerity. "That is what makes it mercy, Wren. Mercy is only mercy when the alternative is already in motion." He nodded to the wardens. "Take her to the Spire. She'll open the door whether she chooses to or not; the god will decide that for her when it wakes, and gods are sentimental about their vessels. And send riders to the ford. Quietly. I want Kavanagh taken alive if it can be managed."

"If it can't?" one of them asked.

Hale settled back into his chair and opened his book again, finding his page with one finger.

"Then the mountain keeps what it catches," he said, "and I'll write his name in the report myself. I'm told my handwriting is very clear."

They put the hood back over my head. As they marched me out into the rain, up the trail toward the black silhouette of the Spire against the clouds, I did the only thing left to me.

I reached down into the iron-caged dark where my god slept, past the null-cold and the silence, and I pressed everything I had against the bars — the wayhouse, the fire, the offer, the refusal, and above all one sentence, repeated like a heartbeat, like a prayer shouted into a silence that had never once answered until it did:

*They're going to kill him. They're going to kill Jonah. Wake up.*

Far below us, back toward the ford, thunder rolled — long, low, and, I would have sworn on my own grave, *answering*.
