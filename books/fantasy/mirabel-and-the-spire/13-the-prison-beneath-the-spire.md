## 13. The Prison Beneath the Spire

There are two ways to surrender to the Church of the Silence. The first is to be dragged in by your collar, bleeding, while a warden reads you the charges you already know by heart. I'd done that one ten years ago. It's overrated.

The second is to walk up to their gate with your hands empty and your name on your lips, and watch the wardens' faces do something complicated. That was the one on offer this morning, and I took it.

"Jonah Kavanagh," I said to the gate-sergeant at the Spire's outer wall. "I believe you have a bounty."

He looked at me the way men look at a dog that has walked into a butcher shop and lain down on the block. Somewhere behind his eyes, arithmetic happened — bounty, promotion, the awkward question of why the notorious storm-caller had delivered himself like a parcel.

"You're joking," he said.

"I've been told my humor is dry."

He called for irons. I gave him my wrists without being asked, which unsettled him more than any resistance would have. There is a particular kind of fear that lives in soldiers who are handed exactly what they were promised, because it means someone else is running the story, and they are not in it.

They marched me down through the Spire's outer works — past barracks, past a chapel where the Silence's grey banners hung so still it seemed the air itself had been instructed not to move — and then down. Always down. Stairs cut into the mountain's root, older than the Church, older than Thornfell if the carvings meant anything. The walls changed as we descended: dressed stone, then rough-hewn, then stone that wasn't stone at all but something veined and faintly warm, like the inside of a living thing.

I'd spent ten years telling myself I never wanted to see the Spire again. I hadn't counted on coming back as merchandise.

The deal was simple, which is the only kind of deal worth making with the Church, since they'll complicate anything anyway. Myself, freely given, name confirmed, in exchange for Wren walking out of the valley unmolested. No pursuit for three days. Brother Hale had agreed to it in writing, which from Hale was either a genuine concession or the most elegant insult ever drafted. I chose to believe the former, mostly because the alternative kept me up at night, and I needed sleep more than I needed cynicism.

Wren had fought me on it, of course. She'd stood in the mouth of the smuggler's tunnel with the spark cupped against her chest like a coal she was afraid of dropping, and she'd said, "You swore you'd never go back for anyone."

"I did," I said. "I'm not going back for anyone. I'm going back for you. Apparently there's a difference, and apparently I'm the last person in Thornfell to learn it."

She'd cried, a little, angrily, the way people cry when they're furious at themselves for having something to lose. Then she'd pressed her forehead to mine and said something in the old tongue — a blessing, or a curse, or possibly just her god clearing its throat — and gone east into the fog with the smuggler-queen's guide, and I had turned around and walked toward the thing I'd fled half my life.

So here I was. Irons on my wrists, rain in my collar, and a warden ahead of me whose torch threw shadows that moved wrong on the veined walls.

* * *

The cells began at the bottom of the stairs, and they were wrong in a way I didn't have words for at first.

Not dungeons. Dungeons smell of rot and despair and other people's bad decisions. These smelled of — incense? No. Older than incense. Rain on hot stone. Pine sap. Woodsmoke and river water and the green smell of a meadow just after cutting. A hundred smells that had no business being underground, layered over each other until the air itself felt crowded.

And light. Not torchlight. Light that came from nowhere, pooling in corners, moving when nothing moved.

The first cell we passed held an old man sitting cross-legged on bare stone, and he was glowing. Not metaphorically. His skin gave off a soft gold radiance, and where his shadow should have fallen, instead there was a circle of tiny growing things — moss, mushrooms, a single stubborn flower pushing up through granite that has no business hosting a flower.

"He answered," the old man said as we passed, smiling at nothing. "Someone prayed for rain in the dry season, forty years ago, and I answered, and they put me down here and I'm still answering. Every day it rains somewhere. Every day I answer." He laughed, gently, madly. "They can't make me stop. They can only keep me where no one can pray."

I stopped walking. The warden behind me shoved me forward, but I dug my heels in, because I had understood something all at once and completely, the way you understand a fall after your foot has already left the ledge.

"They're not prisoners," I said. "They're gods."

"They're heretics," the sergeant said, in the flat tone of a man reciting doctrine to keep from thinking about it.

"That man is answering prayers right now. Through four feet of rock."

"The Silence keeps what must be kept," he said, and there it was — the catechism, worn smooth as a river stone from use, deployed like a shield. But his torch hand shook, and I saw him glance at the glowing cell the way you glance at a door you've been told is solid and have heard breathing through.

We went deeper.

Cell after cell. A woman made entirely of river-light, lying in a dry channel that remembered being water, murmuring the names of fish that no longer existed anywhere but in her. A thing that might once have been a bear, or a mountain, folded impossibly into a space too small for it, breathing slow tectonic breaths. A child — a child who looked six years old and whose eyes held weather systems — humming to herself in a cell whose walls were frosted with eternal frost, and every breath she exhaled became snowflakes that melted before they touched the floor, because even caged, even buried, she could not stop making small beautiful things.

Somewhere above us, the Church of the Silence preached that the old gods were dead. That they had died with the royal line, drowned in the storm, gone quiet as a closed mouth. And here beneath the Spire, in the dark under the country's spine, they lay in rows like harvested wheat, still alive, still answering, still *answering*, because that is the one thing a god cannot not do.

Prayer goes up. Answer comes down. The Church had simply built a ceiling.

I thought of Wren, and the spark against her chest, and understood with terrible clarity what would have happened to her if I hadn't pulled her out of that burning village. Not death. Death would have been a mercy with a shape. She'd have ended up here, in the warm dark, glowing forever, answering prayers no one was allowed to pray.

Something in my chest, which had spent ten years being carefully, professionally numb, began to heat.

* * *

They put me in a cell too, eventually. Mine was ordinary — dressed stone, iron door, a slot for the bread they presumably expected me to refuse on principle. I ate the bread. Principles are for people with better prospects.

But my cell had a neighbor.

Through the wall, faint as a voice through deep water, came singing. Not words. A sound like wind deciding to be music. And with each note, the air in my cell grew sweeter, cleaner, as though the stone itself were remembering sky.

"Hello?" I said.

The singing paused. Then, amused, ancient, tired: *"The storm-caller. They actually caught you."*

"You know me?"

*"Everyone below knows you, Jonah Kavanagh. You're the excuse. Ten years we've listened to them preach your name as the reason the silence is necessary. You are the sermon, little storm. The whole architecture of the cage rests on the story of your guilt."*

I sat down on the cold floor with my back to that wall, because standing suddenly seemed dishonest.

"I didn't call the storm," I said. To the wall. To the dark. To whatever was listening. "I want that recorded somewhere other than my own skull. I have said it to myself every night for ten years and it hasn't taken root anywhere."

A long quiet. Then, softer: *"We know."*

The word landed in me like a stone dropped down a well — falling and falling, and I waited for it to hit bottom, and it never quite did.

"What do you mean, you know?"

*"Gods hear what is true, even caged. Especially caged. We have had ten years and nothing to do but listen." A pause, and something almost like pity moved through the wall, a warmth in the stone. "You did not kill the royal line, child. But you will want to know who did, and that knowledge is not ours to give. It belongs to a man with a soft voice and clean hands, and he walks above us right now."*

Hale. Of course. Everything circled back to Hale eventually, like water finding the sea.

"Why are you telling me this?" I asked. "What do you get?"

Another pause. When the voice came again, it had lost its amusement entirely.

*"Because you are the first person in ten years to walk into this place on purpose. Because the spark-bearer is out there with the last free piece of a god, heading for a door we can feel from down here, a door that is starting to open. And because—" the voice cracked, actually cracked, like ice on a spring river, "—because I have been answering prayers into a ceiling for three hundred years, storm-caller, and I am so very tired, and if someone is finally going to break this place, I would like it to be someone who owes us nothing and chooses us anyway. That is the only prayer I have left. Do not answer it out of duty. Duty is how they built all this."*

I pressed my palm flat against the wall. It was warm. All this time I'd thought the warmth was the mountain's. It wasn't. It was them. Hundreds of them, thousands maybe, stacked in the dark under the country, keeping the borders anchored by existing, keeping the world stitched together by being imprisoned inside it — and the Church calling it silence, calling it peace, calling it the will of a god who had never spoken at all.

The rage arrived quietly, which surprised me. I'd expected it to come like my storms, all noise and wreckage. Instead it came like groundwater, rising slow and certain, filling every empty place the exile had carved out of me, until there was no room left for anything else.

Ten years I'd believed I deserved the dark. Ten years I'd carried a crime like a coat that fit too well, because taking it off meant admitting someone else had put it on me, and that meant the exile had been theft, and that meant the loneliness had been for nothing.

It had been for nothing.

I laughed. It came out wrong, wet at the edges. The god on the other side of the wall sang to me until I stopped shaking, and neither of us mentioned it after.

* * *

Near dawn — if dawn means anything a hundred fathoms underground; I measured it by the frost-child's humming changing key — boots came down the stairs. Two wardens, and behind them a figure in grey whose footsteps made no sound at all, which is its own kind of announcement.

Brother Hale stopped at my door. Up close, he looked exactly as he had at the river crossing: mild, tidy, a man you'd trust with your luggage and never with your soul. He smiled through the bars.

"Jonah Kavanagh. Freely surrendered. The Church thanks you for your cooperation." He let that hang, savoring it. "Your companion has been granted passage, as agreed. Though I confess curiosity: a man spends ten years running from us, and then hands himself over for a girl he's known a month. Sentiment? Or something more practical?"

"Call it an investment," I said. "You'll want to check on it in a few days."

His smile thinned by a degree. He knew about the spark. He'd always known about the spark. What he didn't know — couldn't know, yet — was that the spark was currently walking toward the Spire with a smuggler's guide and a priestess's stubbornness, and that everything he'd built down here was about to meet the one thing it had no cell for.

"I have questions for you," Hale said. "About the storm. About Thornfell. About what really happened, ten years ago." He tilted his head, birdlike. "Unless you'd prefer to keep believing your version. Guilt is comfortable, in my experience. Like a cell. Most men never ask what's holding the walls up."

Behind me, through the warm stone, the caged god went very, very still. Listening.

I stood up. My legs had decided before the rest of me did.

"Ask," I said.

Hale unlocked the door, and the sound of it — iron turning in iron, the lock that had held me and everyone like me for ten years — opened onto whatever came next.
