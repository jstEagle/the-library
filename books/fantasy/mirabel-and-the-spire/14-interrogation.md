## 14. Interrogation

The cell beneath the Spire was not built for men. I understood that within the first hour, when the walls began to hum.

Not metaphorically. Actually humming — a low, patient note that lived in the stone the way a fly lives in honey. The Church had carved these cells into the Spire's roots centuries ago, and whatever they'd used to do it still worked, because the walls weren't just holding me. They were listening.

I'd been in worse rooms. There was the winter I spent in a smuggler's hold with a leaking cask of pickled eels and a man named Doss who talked in his sleep about his mother. There was the confessional at Thornfell's high chapel, which I'd hidden in once as a boy and learned that priests sigh more than anyone alive. But this room wanted something from me, and it hadn't yet decided how to ask.

So I did what any sensible prisoner does. I counted stones, tested the door, and talked to myself in a voice too low for the walls to catch anything useful.

"Jonah Kavanagh," I said to the dark, "you have an absolute gift for this."

The gift being: surrendering himself to buy a priestess's freedom, then sitting in a humming hole waiting to find out whether the trade would hold. Wren had gone free. That was the bargain, struck in the mud above ground while Brother Hale watched me with the expression of a man appraising a horse he intended to work to death. Her freedom for my surrender. She'd fought it. Of course she'd fought it — she had a god's spark in her chest and a spine like a whipcord, and she'd called me a fool in three different registers before Hale's wardens closed around me.

But she'd gone. That was the part I kept turning over. She could have run. Instead she'd stood there on the ridge with her fists clenched and her eyes wet, and mouthed one word at me through the rain: *live*.

I was working on it.

* * *

They came for me on the second day, or what I judged was the second day, because the cell had no windows and my sense of time had begun to drift like a boat off its mooring. The door opened without a sound, which was somehow worse than a shriek of hinges, and Brother Hale stepped through carrying a single lantern and a small folding table under his arm.

A folding table. He set it up between us like we were meeting to discuss grain prices.

"Master Kavanagh." He sat. He gestured to the floor across from him, courteous as a host offering a cushion. "Please."

"I'll stand," I said. "It's the only advantage I've got."

Hale smiled. He was not what I'd expected from ten years of nightmares. I'd imagined the Church's chief inquisitor as some vast slab of a man, all iron and incense. Instead he was slight, gray-templed, with ink-stained fingers and the mild face of a schoolmaster who genuinely loved his students and grieved every one he had to fail. The lantern light caught a thin scar along his jaw, old and white.

"You're thinner than your file suggests," he said.

"You keep a file on me?"

"The Church keeps files on everything." He produced a leather folio from inside his coat and laid it on the table with the care of a man handling scripture. "Yours is quite thick. Ten years of reports. Sightings. Rumors. A storm-shape recorded over the White Road last month, matching the signature of the Thornfell event to a degree our weather-readers found... distressing."

"That wasn't me," I said. "That was weather doing what weather does."

"Of course." He opened the folio. "Shall we begin with the storm?"

I laughed. It came out wrong — too sharp, bouncing off the humming walls. "Brother Hale. You've got me in a box under your tower. You've traded away the only leverage I had. Whatever you want from me, you can take it. Why are we doing the part where you're polite?"

Hale looked up at me, and for a moment the schoolmaster fell away and something older looked out — tired, I thought. Genuinely, humanly tired.

"Because," he said, "for the first time in ten years, I am going to tell someone the truth, and I find I'd rather do it to a man who's standing than a man who's broken. Sit down, Jonah. This will go easier on your knees."

I sat. Not because he asked. Because my knees had heard him.

* * *

He began, as all Church lessons begin, with doctrine.

"The Silence teaches that the old gods died of their own weight," he recited, hands folded on the table. "That they grew so fat on worship they collapsed under themselves, and the world was better for it. That prayer is a debt unpaid. That borders must be anchored or nations dissolve. You know the catechism."

"I was raised on it."

"And abandoned it."

"I was exiled by it. Abandonment seemed implied."

Something flickered across his face — not anger. Recognition, maybe. He turned the folio around and slid it toward me. "Do you know why the Church fears the gods waking? Truly?"

"Because answered prayers make bad congregants."

"Because answered prayers make *auditors*." His voice stayed gentle. "When a god answers a prayer, the answer carries evidence. A healed child carries evidence of the healing. A held border carries evidence of the holding. And evidence, Master Kavanagh, has a way of pointing backward."

I stared at him. The walls hummed. Somewhere far above, water dripped through the Spire's bones.

"What are you telling me?" I said.

Hale took a breath, and I watched a man decide to burn his own life down, because that is exactly what his next words were.

"The royal line of Thornfell was not killed by a storm."

The cell went very quiet. Even the humming seemed to lean in.

"They were killed in the night before it," Hale continued, each word placed like a surgeon setting bone. "Poisoned. All nine of them, in the space of an hour, by agents of the Church of the Silence acting under sealed orders from the Concordance itself. The storm came after. The storm was ours — called by our own weather-priests, amplified, aimed. It destroyed the palace, the records, the witnesses. It gave the country a wound so large no one would look closely enough to see the knife."

The lantern flame didn't waver. My hands, I noticed distantly, were shaking in my lap where I hoped the dark hid them.

"Why," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's. "Why would—"

"Because the royal line was negotiating to restore the old gods' shrines." Hale said it flatly, without drama, which made it infinitely worse. "Queen Isolde's grandfather had suppressed them; her father had tolerated them; she meant to reopen them. Formal charters. Legal worship. The Concordance had spent two hundred years building a country with no room in it for gods, and the crown of Thornfell was about to hand the keys back. So the Concordance killed the crown. And then it needed a reason the country would believe."

The humming filled the silence. I let it fill everything, because the alternative was letting what he'd said fill it.

"A storm-child," Hale said softly. "A boy born under lightning, marked in the parish rolls, capable — barely, untrained, unproven — of calling weather. A scapegoat doesn't need to be guilty, Jonah. It needs to be *available*. You were seventeen. You had no family to speak for you. Your gift was real but small, small enough that no one could prove you *hadn't* done it, and fear does the rest. We tried you in absentia. We burned your name from the rolls. We exiled you. And the country slept easier believing a boy had murdered its kings than believing its church had."

I don't remember standing. I remember the table skidding, the lantern rocking, Hale not flinching, and my fist stopping an inch from his face because some animal part of me understood that if I hit him I would never stop hitting him, and there was a woman somewhere out there who had mouthed the word *live* at me through the rain.

I put my hand against the wall instead. The stone was warm. It hummed against my palm like something breathing.

"Ten years," I said.

"Yes."

"Ten years I—" The words wouldn't come in order. They came in shards. "Every winter. Every time the sky went green before hail. Every time someone flinched when thunder rolled, I thought — that's mine. That's the shape I left in the world. I built my whole life around a crime I didn't commit. I taught myself to be small so I couldn't hurt anyone. I let them hunt me. I let them *price* me." I turned to look at him. "You let me carry it."

"We needed you to carry it," Hale said. "That was the entire design. A guilt carried sincerely is worth a hundred forced confessions. You believed it, Jonah. Do you understand what a gift that was to us? No inquisitor ever had to break you. You broke yourself, faithfully, for a decade. The Church could not have asked for a better penitent."

I slid down the wall until I was sitting. It seemed like the correct architectural decision. The stone hummed against my back, warm as a living thing, and I thought — absurdly, hysterically — *the cells are full of answered prayers, and even the walls here are kinder than the men.*

* * *

Time passed. I don't know how much. Hale waited it out with the patience of his profession, refilling the lantern once, and when I finally spoke again my voice had settled into something flat and cold that I didn't recognize as mine.

"Why tell me now?"

"Because it's over." He folded his hands. "The gods are waking. The spark-bearer is loose with a dying god in her chest. The Spire is stirring. Within a season — perhaps within weeks — the Silence will either hold Thornfell or lose it entirely, and either way, the lie stops mattering. A dead secret is just a story. I am an old man who has spent his life keeping a story straight, and I find, at the end, that I would like one person outside the Concordance to know it was a story at all."

"That's not mercy. That's bookkeeping."

"Yes," Hale agreed mildly. "Most mercy is."

"And Wren? The girl with the spark? What happens to her in your honest accounting?"

Hale was quiet for a long moment. When he answered, the schoolmaster was gone entirely, and what remained was just a tired man in a lantern-lit cellar.

"She walks into the Spire and gives the spark back, and the border closes, and Thornfell keeps its name, and she dies. Or she refuses, and the god dies anyway, and the country dissolves, and she lives in whatever comes after. Those are the doors. The Church prefers the first. I confess—" and here his voice did something I hadn't heard it do, something almost human, almost sorry — "I no longer know which I prefer."

"You could open a third door."

"There is no third door. There hasn't been since the night we killed the royals. Everything since has been the price of that hour." He began packing his folio, unhurried. "Except, perhaps, one thing. One variable the Concordance never planned for."

"Which is?"

He looked at me across the little folding table, and there was something in his face I refused, absolutely refused, to read as respect.

"A scapegoat who finds out he's innocent," Hale said, "stops behaving like one. Guilt made you predictable, Jonah. I've just taken that from the Church, and given it to you, and I am not certain I've done the right thing. But I know what you'll do with it. Men like you always do the same thing. You'll stop protecting yourself and start protecting her harder." He rose, folded the table under his arm, and lifted the lantern. At the door he paused, half-turned, gray hair silvered in the light.

"For what it's worth — and I know precisely what it's worth, which is nothing — Queen Isolde signed the charter reopening the shrines four days before we poisoned her. It survived the fire. It's in the Concordance vaults. The last lawful act of the last queen of Thornfell was to welcome your gods home." A pause. "They hanged the herald who read it aloud. I was twenty-three. I held the ladder."

The door closed without a sound.

* * *

I sat in the humming dark for a long time afterward, and I will be honest, because a man who's just been handed back his innocence ought to be able to afford honesty: I did not feel free. I felt robbed.

Robbed of the guilt. That's the sentence I kept circling, the one that sounds mad until you sit inside it. Ten years, I had owned that storm. It was mine — my sin, my weight, my reason to be small and careful and alone. Every mile of exile had meaning because I'd earned it. And now a mild-faced man with a folding table had reached into my chest and taken the one thing I'd built my whole life around, and left me holding nothing but rage and arithmetic. Ten years. Ten winters. Ten thousand miles walked at the edge of maps. Every kindness I'd refused, every door I hadn't knocked on, every person I'd let walk past because a man who kills kings shouldn't get close to anyone — all of it spent paying a debt that was never mine.

Grief, I learned that night, is not only for the dead. You can grieve a decade. You can stand at its grave and weep for the man you might have been if the Church hadn't needed a boy to blame.

And underneath the grief, coiling and waking like something in a cellar wall, the other thing. The hot thing. Because Hale had told me the truth for his own reasons, but truths don't stay owned. The Church had killed a queen mid-signature. The Church had hanged a herald. The Church had caged gods in the cells around me — I could feel them, faint and furious, like banked fires behind the stone — and called it silence, and called it peace, and called it holy.

Wren was out there somewhere, walking toward a door that would erase her, believing sacrifice was the only liturgy left. She thought she was giving herself to a god's dying wish.

She didn't know yet that the wish itself was built on a lie — that the whole desperate arithmetic of borders and sparks and necessary deaths traced back to one poisoned hour and a storm the Church had aimed like a weapon. That the god she loved was dying inside a crime scene.

I pressed my palm flat against the warm humming stone, and for the first time in my life I did it deliberately, the way you'd knock on a neighbor's door.

"Hey," I whispered, to whatever caged and listening things the Silence had locked in the dark. "I don't suppose any of you can get a message to a priestess with a spark in her chest. Tell her to wait. Tell her the door she's about to walk through was built on a lie."

The stone pulsed once under my hand — slow, deep, unmistakable.

Somewhere above me, in the dark heart of the Spire, something ancient rolled over in its sleep and began, very quietly, to listen.
