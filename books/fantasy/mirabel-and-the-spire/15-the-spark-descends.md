## 15. The Spark Descends

The Spire did not look like a door from the outside. It looked like a wound that had healed badly — a tower of black stone rising out of the mountain's shoulder, its top third simply *gone*, sheared off as if the sky had taken a bite and found it wanting. Ivy had given up on it centuries ago. Even the lichen grew in careful patterns, like children avoiding a mad relative's house.

I had served the Silence for eleven years, and in all that time I had never once been told what the Silence was keeping quiet about.

Now I knew. It was this. It was always this.

"You're doing the thing," Jonah said behind me. "The thing where you stand very still and pretend you're not afraid."

"I'm a priestess," I said. "Stillness is liturgy."

"Uh-huh." He came up beside me, close enough that I could feel the warmth of him through my ruined cloak, which was less romantic than it sounds, because everything I owned smelled of woodsmoke and river water and one memorable incident involving a goat. "Wren."

"Don't say it like a eulogy."

"Then don't walk up to it like one."

I looked at him. Ten days ago he'd been a smuggler with medicine in his pack and a price on his name, and now he was — what? A storm-caller. A liar's scapegoat. The man who had pulled me out of a burning village because some old instinct in him refused to die quietly, no matter how hard exile tried. The man whose face, when Brother Hale told us both the truth about the storm, had come apart so completely that I had wanted to look away and hadn't.

"The spark has to go back," I said. "You heard it. The border is failing. Thornfell is already half-dissolved — you saw the village where the church bell rang backward until someone remembered it forward. If the door doesn't close—"

"—the country finishes coming apart. Yes. I was there for the sermon." His jaw worked. "And the part where giving it back means giving back *you*."

"That part is implied in the theology."

"It's not theology, Wren, it's arithmetic, and I've never trusted arithmetic that ends in a person."

I almost smiled. That was the trouble with Jonah Kavanagh. He made you want to smile at exactly the moments when smiling would be surrender.

* * *

We made camp in the lee of the Spire's foundation wall, because the wind up there came off the border itself and it blew through cloth and skin and, I suspected, memory. I built a fire small enough to hide and large enough to matter, and while Jonah slept — he slept like a man who expected to be woken by violence, in snatches, one hand always near his knife — I sat with the spark in my cupped hands and listened to it breathe.

That is what it did. Breathe. A dying god, folded down small enough to fit between a girl's palms, breathing like a sleeper in the next room.

*Little wren,* it said. It had called me that since the beginning, since the chapel where the Silence had stopped answering and something older had whispered my name instead. *You are afraid.*

"I'm not."

*Lying is a form of prayer too,* it said, with the dry warmth it sometimes managed, *but it is not one of mine.*

I fed a twig to the fire. "I've given you eleven years," I said. "First to the Silence, when it never answered, and then to you, when it turned out the Silence was just you, holding your breath. I know how to give things. It's the one skill they taught me properly."

*Ah.* The spark dimmed, brightened. Grief has a color; I had learned it. *You think devotion means being spent.*

"I think devotion means meaning it," I said. "When you offer something, you have to mean it, or the offering is empty. So I'm going to sit here all night and mean it. And in the morning I'm going to walk into your tower and put you back where you belong, and the border will hold, and Thornfell will keep whatever shape it still remembers, and I'll be—" I paused. "Gone. Which is fine. Which is the whole point of a priestess. You don't keep the candle. You *are* the candle."

The silence that followed was long enough that I checked whether the god had finally died early, out of sheer embarrassment.

*Who taught you that?*

"The Church of the Silence."

*The men who burned the royal line and blamed a boy for a storm.* The spark flared, and for a moment it wasn't warm at all. *The men who caged gods in cells beneath their own altars and called the cages chapels. They taught you to be a candle. Of course they did. Candles are consumed. Candles ask nothing. Candles are very convenient.*

"They were wrong about many things," I admitted. "But they weren't wrong about me. I don't have anything else to be."

*Little wren.* And then the god did something it had never done before. It laughed. Quietly, tiredly, the way an old man laughs at a joke he's too weak to explain. *You have spent eleven years believing you are fuel.*

"What else would I be?"

*A priestess,* it said. *A bridge is not eaten by the river it crosses. A door is not destroyed by the house it opens. Wren. Come inside tomorrow. Walk as far as you can bear to walk. But hear me first, and hear me clearly: I never asked for a sacrifice. Not yours. Not anyone's. The ones who told you gods run on blood were the same ones who needed you to believe it.*

I sat very still, the way I do when I'm pretending not to be afraid.

Because if the god didn't want my death — if the whole architecture of my ending, the beautiful clean arithmetic of it, the one thing I had ever been certain I could give — if that was wrong, then I didn't know what I was walking toward. And worse, far worse, in a place I didn't want to look: I didn't know who I was if I wasn't an offering.

Devotion, it turns out, has a limit. I found mine sitting by a small fire under a broken tower, terrified not of dying, but of being told I might live.

* * *

Morning came gray and thin. Jonah was awake before me, which meant he'd probably never really slept. He had built the fire up and heated water and was holding out a tin cup of something that claimed to be tea the way a mule claims to be a horse.

"Drink," he said. "If you're going to erase yourself, do it hydrated."

"Gallows humor. From you. Remarkable."

"I learned it from a priestess who makes jokes about candles." He sat across from me, elbows on knees, and studied my face with that terrible directness of his, the one that made wardens confess and smugglers pay their debts. "You're going to tell me I can't stop you."

"Yes."

"And you're going to tell me it has to be you."

"Yes."

"And I'm going to say something honest, and you're going to hate it." He looked at the fire. When he spoke again his voice had dropped into the register he used maybe twice a year, the one with no armor over it. "Ten years I carried a crime that was never mine, because carrying it was easier than finding out who actually did. I know what it costs to believe a lie because the truth would unmake you. Don't build your altar on one, Wren. Whatever you're about to do in there — make sure it's true."

I thought of the god's voice in the dark. *A door is not destroyed by the house it opens.*

"It's true," I said, and it even was — mostly. The spark had to go home. The border had to close. Those parts were bedrock. What trembled underneath was only the question of what going home would cost, and I found, to my enormous irritation, that I couldn't make myself say *everything* anymore. The certainty wouldn't come. I kept hearing that tired laugh.

Jonah watched me not-say-it. He was good at reading what people withheld; ten years of smuggling will do that. But he let it go, which was its own kind of mercy, and stood, and shouldered his pack.

"I'm walking you to the door," he said. "Not in. To the door. That's the deal, and I'll arm-wrestle you for it if I have to, and I cheat."

"You would."

"I would. It's how I survived the decade you were learning hymns."

* * *

The Spire's entrance was a doorway at the base of the tower, tall as a chapel arch, carved with a script neither of us could read — though when I touched it, the spark in my chest read it aloud, softly, like a grandmother remembering a lullaby. *Here the country kept its word. Here the word was kept.*

Inside, the air changed.

It's hard to describe what it's like to step into a building that is only partly in the world. My boots struck stone and the sound came back late, delayed, as though the echo had to travel farther than the walls allowed. Light fell through windows that showed, not the gray morning we'd left, but a green country under a gold sun — Thornfell as it had been, or as it insisted on remembering itself. Through one window I saw orchards. Through another, a river I knew from maps that no longer existed. Through a third, nothing at all: white, patient, unfinished, the places the country had already forgotten.

Jonah stopped at the threshold like a man at the edge of cold water.

"To the door," he said. "That was the deal."

"I remember."

"Wren." He caught my hand. His palm was rough and warm and shaking slightly, and he pretended it wasn't, and I pretended not to notice, and that mutual fiction felt more intimate than anything the Church had ever warned me about. "Whatever's in there — if there's a way out that isn't erasure, take it. Promise me you'll look for it. Not for me. For you. Because I've met you now, the actual you, the one under the vestments, and the world is worse off if it doesn't get to keep her."

I looked up at him. He had a smudge of soot on his jaw that he'd carried for two days because there was no mirror and no reason to care, and I thought, absurdly, *I want to be the person who tells him about the smudge.* I want small mornings. I want to argue about tea. I want the ordinary years that martyrs never get, and I am apparently so greedy that even facing my god's deathbed I'm haggling for more.

"I promise I'll look," I said.

He nodded once, released my hand, and stepped back into the morning. Through the doorway I watched him stand guard in the gray light, arms crossed, watching the road we'd come by, watching for wardens, watching for Hale, watching for anything that might take this choice away from me. Guarding my exit even though he believed there wasn't one.

Then I turned, and walked into the Spire alone.

* * *

The stair went up in spirals, and the spirals went up in ways stairs shouldn't. Sometimes I climbed and arrived below where I'd started. Once I passed a window showing a harvest festival, and the people in it turned and looked at me, and bowed, and went back to dancing, and I understood that they were memories wearing bodies the way actors wear costumes, and that the country behind these windows was running out of costume.

The spark grew heavier with every step. Not painful — *denser*. As though each turn of the stair added a year to it, or took one away. By the time I reached the chamber at the top — the anchor room, round as a drum, open to a sky that was half blue and half that patient white — I was carrying it against my chest with both arms, the way you carry a sleeping child you're afraid to wake.

In the center of the room stood the border-stone: a pillar of the same black rock as the tower, waist-high, its surface crawling faintly with light like embers under ash. Most of the embers were out. The country beyond the white windows flickered as I watched.

*I'm here,* I said.

*So you are.* The voice came from everywhere and nowhere, from the stone and the spark and the walls themselves, and it sounded smaller than it had by the fire. Dying sounds like that. Not dramatic. Just quieter, and quieter, and quieter. *You came to spend yourself.*

"I came to bring you home."

*Those are different sentences, little wren, however carefully you build them to rhyme.* The light in the stone gathered, focused, and for a moment I felt it looking at me — truly looking, the way no one had ever looked at me, not as a vessel or a servant or a candle, but as Wren, the specific and unrepeatable Wren, the girl who talked back to gods and hid her fear behind liturgy. *Ask me what I want.*

"You want your spark back. You want the border closed. You want Thornfell to survive."

*I want those things the way a drowning man wants the shore. Now ask me what I want.*

My throat closed. "What do you want?"

The silence stretched. Somewhere below us, faintly, I heard a sound I refused to identify as shouting — Jonah's voice, or wardens', or both, the world arriving on schedule to hurry the sacred along.

*Eleven hundred years ago,* the god said, *when they raised this stone, the rite required a death. One life, freely given, to anchor the border. And a woman gave hers — gladly, proudly, the way you intended to climb these stairs. And it worked. And every priest since has taught that the working requires the same price forever. But little wren — the woman who died that day was my first priestess, and I loved her, and I have had eleven hundred years to examine that moment from every angle, and I will tell you what I found in the examining.*

The embers in the stone pulsed once, slow, like a heart deciding.

*I found that the border held because she loved her country. Not because she died. Death was only the shape her love happened to take, and the men who came after — the ones who liked tidy rites and convenient candles — mistook the shape for the substance. Every sacrifice since has been a copy of a copy of a copy, and none of them were needed, and I was too weak, for too long, to say so.*

I sat down. My legs simply declined further service, and I sat on the cold stone floor of a dying god's heart and stared at it.

"No," I said. "No, you're telling me this *now*? After I've spent the whole climb meaning it? After I said goodbye to—" My voice cracked, and I hated it, and I pressed on anyway, furious and laughing and crying all at once, which I believe is the traditional posture of a devotee meeting her god's honesty for the first time. "Do you know what I did last night? I inventoried my life. I decided the tea was bad enough that I wouldn't miss it. I *rationaled my own existence* and filed it under acceptable losses, and you're telling me the ledger was wrong?"

*The ledger was wrong,* it said gently. *It was written by the Church of the Silence, and everything they wrote was wrong. They killed kings and called it fate. They caged their brothers' gods and called it doctrine. They taught a frightened girl in a silent chapel that her highest purpose was to be used up — because people who believe they exist to be spent do not ask why they are being spent, or on whom.*

The white beyond the windows crept closer. A window that had shown orchards an hour ago now showed nothing at all.

"But the border," I whispered. "It's failing. Someone has to—"

*Someone has to choose,* the god agreed. *But a choice is not a coin, Wren. It does not have to be paid in flesh. There may be a way to close the border that costs grief instead of blood. There may be no way at all. I am very old and very nearly finished, and my sight is going out like yours does at dusk — shapes first, then colors, then only the sense of light. But I know this much, and I stake my last ember on it: I never wanted your death. I accepted it, the way the drowning accept the water, because I could see no other shore. Do not mistake my exhaustion for desire.*

I knelt by the border-stone and laid my hands on it, and the spark in my chest leaned toward it like a child toward an open door.

"Then show me," I said. "Show me the other way. Or if there isn't one — if the only door out of this is the one through me — then tell me plainly, god, no kindness, no cushioning, because I have given my whole life to you and I will not spend my death on a euphemism."

The stone warmed beneath my hands. All around us the last windows of remembered Thornfell flickered — orchards, rivers, dancers — a country holding its breath.

And the god began, at last, to tell me the truth about doors.

Which is when the shouting below became unmistakable, and Jonah's voice rose up the stairwell, raw and furious, calling my name — and with it came the sound I had dreaded since Kestrel Ford: wardens' boots on stone, and beneath them, calm as a funeral bell, Brother Hale saying, "After her. The girl first. Kill the storm-caller if he argues."
