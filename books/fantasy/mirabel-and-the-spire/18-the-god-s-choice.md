## 18. The God's Choice

The anchor room at the top of the Spire was not a room. I want to be clear about that, because everything else that happened depended on it not being a room.

It was a threshold wearing the costume of a room. There were walls — pale stone, veined with something that might have been silver or might have been the memory of silver — and there was a floor under my feet that felt like standing on a held breath. But the far wall had a door in it, and the door had no other side. When I looked at it straight on, my eyes reported a doorway opening onto green hills and a river the color of hammered tin, and then they reported nothing at all, because Thornfell-as-it-remembered-itself kept sliding out of focus like a word you've said too many times.

*That's the border,* the god said, in the voice I had carried against my ribs for weeks now. It sounded tired. Gods aren't supposed to sound tired. *That's what's left of me holding it.*

"You're holding a country together with your fingernails," I said. "Metaphorically. You don't have fingernails."

*I have you,* it said, which was worse, and truer.

Jonah stood near the doorway-that-was-only-a-doorway, arms crossed, watching the hills appear and vanish with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like. Behind us, somewhere down in the bones of the Spire, Brother Hale and his wardens were climbing. We had perhaps an hour. Perhaps less. Jonah had called a storm at the base of the tower — a real one this time, one that answered him like a hound answering its own name — and it was still raging outside the windows, rain lashing glass that hadn't been glass for two hundred years.

"Tell me again," Jonah said, without turning around. "Slowly. And don't dress it up."

The god sighed through me. I felt it in my teeth.

*The border between Thornfell and everywhere else was anchored here,* it said. *When the royal line died, the anchor broke. I caught what I could. I have been catching what I could for ten years, and I am nearly empty, and when I am empty the border fails entirely, and Thornfell does not fall — Thornfell dissolves. Every village, every grave, every name. Unwritten.*

"And the fix," Jonah said.

*The spark returns to me. All of it. The priestess who carries it—*

"I know what happens to the priestess," Jonah said flatly. "I've done the arithmetic too."

I raised my hand. "If we're going to discuss my death, I'd like to be included. It's polite."

Jonah looked at me then. He has a face built for scowling at weather, and he aimed all of it at me. "You're not dying."

"That's very sweet, but it's not really our decision, is it? It's his." I put my hand flat against my sternum, where the spark lived. It was warm, the way a hearthstone is warm long after the fire's gone — warmth that was mostly memory, running on habit. "Well? You heard him. Don't dress it up. Is there another way?"

The god was quiet for so long that I counted four of Jonah's slow breaths before it answered.

*No,* it said. *There is no other way. There is only a choice.*

"A choice between what?" Jonah asked. "You've told her she dies either way if the border falls. Everyone dies if the border falls."

*Not her.* The god's voice went soft, and I felt it settle into me like someone sitting down heavily in a chair they've been standing beside for hours. *Wren. If the spark comes back to me whole, I can hold Thornfell for another age. Mountains, rivers, names. Everything restored. She is spent in the spending — erased, unwritten with the rest of what I cannot save. If the spark does not come back — if I let go — I die. Truly die. Not sleeping, not waiting. Gone. And Thornfell goes with me, gently, over years instead of centuries. And Wren walks out of this tower alive.*

The rain filled the silence. Down below, faint as a headache, I could hear boots on stone.

"Well," I said. My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "That's not much of a choice, is it? That's just arithmetic with extra steps. One country against one priestess. Any abbot could do that sum."

*It is not a sum,* the god said. *Sums are how the Church thinks. This is why I am asking, and not taking. I could take it, you know. I have never told you otherwise. The spark would return whether you walked forward or dug your heels in. I am asking because you knelt in a chapel that no longer answered, and prayed anyway, and your prayer was the first thing I had felt in ten years that did not hurt.*

Something in my chest cracked along an old seam.

"I was twelve," I said. "I prayed for my mother's cough to get better. That was my first prayer you answered."

*Your second. Your first was 'please let there be bread tomorrow.' I answered that one too. Your mother hid the loaf and told you the baker owed her a favor.* The god laughed, or made the shape of laughing, a small warmth moving through me like a swallow of wine. *I have been inside your prayers since you were small enough to believe the Silence was listening. Do you understand what I am telling you, Wren? I do not want your life. I have never wanted your life. I wanted someone to talk to.*

Jonah had gone very still by the door. He does stillness well; it's a smuggler's skill, and a survivor's, and I think underneath both it's just a man who learned early that being noticed was dangerous. But right then he wasn't hiding. He was watching me the way you watch a candle flame in a draft — like the whole dark room depends on it.

"The Church is coming," he said quietly. "Whatever you two are deciding, decide it before Hale gets here, because he'll decide it for you, and his answer involves a pyre."

*He is right,* the god said. *And there is a third thing I must tell you, and I have been a coward about it, which is a strange thing for a god to admit, but here we are.*

"Gods can be cowards?"

*We invented it.* The warmth in my chest dimmed, gathered itself. *When I die, the door closes behind you. But a dying god can spend what remains however it wishes. I can spend it restoring Thornfell — a country reborn, empty of everyone who remembers it. I can spend it sealing the border fast, a clean death, quick. Or—*

*Or I can spend it holding this door open. For as long as it takes two people to walk through it. And nothing else.*

The meaning of that landed on me slowly, the way cold water lands — first the shock, then the spreading ache. "That's it? That's the whole inheritance? Two refugees and an open door?"

*Two people,* the god corrected. *And every prayer anyone ever prays after this will land somewhere new. Somewhere unnamed. Something small will grow there. I will not see it. But I have watched prayers for three thousand years, Wren, and I know how they behave. They find cracks. They grow in them. You were a crack in me, once, and look what grew.*

"Don't," I said, and my voice finally broke, because I had been holding it together since Kestrel Ford, since the child with the fever, since the storm, since the cells beneath the Spire, and a god quoting your own life back at you is more than any spine is built for. "Don't make it beautiful. Don't make dying beautiful, that's the Church's trick, they've been making burning beautiful for two hundred years—"

*I know,* it said gently. *I watched them learn it from watching me. That is rather the point.*

Boots on stone, closer now. A voice below, giving orders — Hale's voice, dry as ledger paper, unhurried. He always sounded unhurried. It was the most frightening thing about him.

Jonah crossed the room to me. Up close he smelled of rain and woodsmoke and the particular iron tang of a man who has been calling storms all day. "Wren." He said my name carefully, like it was something he'd found and didn't want to startle. "Ask it plainly. Ask it if it wants to live."

I almost laughed. "Gods don't—"

"Ask it anyway. Everything else in this country got a vote except the things that mattered. Let it have one."

So I closed my eyes, and I put my hand back over the spark, and I asked. *Do you want to live?*

The silence went on so long I thought the question had fallen through the floor of the world.

*Yes,* the god said at last, and the honesty of it was like a blade laid flat against my skin — no edge, but all the weight. *I want to live the way rivers want the sea. It is not a thought. It is what I am made of. Three thousand years, Wren, and I have loved every hour of it — the bread-prayers and the grief-prayers and the terrible wedding vows and the old woman in the western valleys who thanked me every morning for her hips, which were dreadful, and her garden, which was magnificent. I do not want to stop. But wanting is not the shape of the choice. The shape of the choice is: what is a god for?*

"And what is a god for?" Jonah asked, because of course he could hear it too now; the spark had stopped bothering with privacy weeks ago.

*A god is for keeping the door open,* the god said simply. *That is all any of us ever were. Borders, thresholds, the space between one hard thing and the next. The Church thinks we are power. We were never power. We were passage.*

Below us, the boots arrived at the last landing. I heard the scrape of a warden's halberd against stone, and Hale's voice saying something mild and awful like *gently, please, gently*, the way he'd said it to the villagers at the ford.

Jonah took my hand. His was cold and callused and completely steady. "Through the door," he said. "Both of us. Now. Whatever it decides, we don't let it decide alone in here with him."

We turned toward the doorway — and the god spoke one last time, and its voice had changed. All the tiredness was gone. What remained was very old and very calm, the way mountains are calm.

*Wren. Last thing. When you pray, after this — and you will pray, you are incapable of not praying, I have checked — no one will answer. Not for a long time. Do not mistake that for absence. Silence is only a door that hasn't grown its hinge yet.*

"I love you," I said. It seemed important to say it out loud, to the air, to the warm place under my ribs. "I know you're not supposed to say that to gods. I don't care."

*I know,* it said, and the warmth in my chest flared once, bright as a struck match, bright as a chapel candle, bright as a girl kneeling in the dark asking for bread. *That is why this is easy.*

Then it let go.

I don't know how else to describe it. Ten thousand mornings of borrowed warmth, gone all at once, and what rushed into the space it left wasn't emptiness — it was *room*. Room like a deep breath. Room like a field after harvest. And in the same instant the Spire shuddered, and the door in the far wall stopped flickering, and the green hills and the tin-colored river snapped into focus, permanent, held — held by nothing now but a dead god's final instruction, a spent coin pressed flat into the lintel, buying exactly as much time as two people needed and not one second more.

The wall behind us exploded inward in a bloom of white light and Hale's wardens poured through it, and Jonah shoved me toward the door, and I ran — out of the country that had made me, out of the god that had unmade itself for me, out of the Silence and into whatever came after —

and behind me, in a voice like the last coal going gray, something said:

*Go gently. Both of you. Go gently.*

* * *

The stairs were gone. That was the first thing I understood, halfway down a staircase that was busy forgetting itself — steps softening under my feet like sugar in rain, the banister turning to smoke that remembered being oak. Jonah had my wrist and I had the rail and neither of them was entirely real anymore, and above us the Spire groaned like a ship changing its mind about the sea.

"It's letting go," Jonah shouted over the roar. "The whole tower — it's spending everything—"

"It's holding the door!" I shouted back. "It promised!"

Above us, light. Below us, light. The Spire was coming apart from both ends at once, stone becoming morning, and through the windows — through windows that were now suggestions of windows — I could see the storm Jonah had called bending down over the mountain like a father over a cradle, and beyond the storm, for one impossible moment, I saw Thornfell entire. Whole. Restored in the act of ending: every village lit, every river running, every grave with its name still legible, a country remembering itself perfectly in the instant before it was allowed to forget.

It was the most beautiful thing I will ever see, and I saw it for four seconds, and I am going to spend the rest of my life describing it badly to people who weren't there.

"Wren!" Jonah's arm around my waist. The last stair. The last door — a plain door, smuggler's pine, the kind you'd find in any chapel vestry in the world, because of course that's what a god would choose, of course it would be *that* —

We went through.

Behind us, the Spire fell with a sound like a name being spoken for the last time, and Brother Hale's shout was swallowed somewhere inside it, and the light went out of the windows, and the mountain stood ordinary and bare against the ordinary sky.

Jonah set me down in wet grass. I was crying. I noted this with a kind of distant editorial interest, the way you note weather. My chest was very quiet. My chest had been full of a god for weeks, and now it was full of nothing but heartbeat, and the nothing was so large I could have lain down in it and lived there.

"He chose," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's. "He could have had everything back. A whole country. An age of it. And he chose a door held open long enough for two people to walk through."

Jonah crouched in front of me. Rain — real rain, ordinary rain, the honest kind — ran off his jaw. He looked older than he had an hour ago and lighter too, the way men look after funerals for people they loved badly and well.

"What do we do now?" he asked. "Priestess of a god who doesn't exist. That's — there's no manual for that."

"No," I agreed. "There isn't."

Somewhere behind us, muffled by distance and rock and the ordinary falling of ordinary rain, I thought I heard voices — survivors, maybe, wardens crawling out of rubble, the world insisting on continuing. And somewhere ahead of us, down the slope, past the tree line, there was a road I didn't know, leading to a country that wasn't on any map, in a world where no one would ever answer my prayers again.

Except.

Except that as we started walking — Jonah's hand finding mine without either of us deciding it, the way hands do now — I felt, in the great quiet room where the spark used to live, something small shift. Not a voice. Not warmth. More like the memory of a hinge, or the promise of one.

A door that hadn't grown its hinge yet.

I squeezed Jonah's hand and kept walking, and did not tell him yet. Some things you carry until you're sure.
