## 19. The Falling of the Spire

The god died the way a candle dies — not all at once, but in a long, reluctant guttering that made you want to cup your hands around it and breathe warm air at it like an idiot.

I was holding Wren when it happened. She'd gone boneless at the border's anchor, the spark finally spent, and for one black moment I thought the god had taken her with it after all. Then she coughed, and swore — a small, filthy oath in chapel Latin that no priestess should have known, and which I resolved to ask her about later, when there was a later — and I understood that we had won something. I just didn't yet know how much it would cost to carry it out.

"Jonah." Her voice came out scraped raw. "Is the door still open?"

I looked up. The Spire's great hall stretched away from us into a country that wasn't on any map — green hills under a gold afternoon, Thornfell as it remembered being, hanging inside the tower like a painting someone had nailed over a wound. And behind us, through the archway we'd come in by, the real world: rain, rock, the smell of wet ash. Between the two, the door stood open. It was already narrowing. Not closing — *narrowing*, the way a dying man's vision narrows, everything pulling toward a single bright point.

"It's open," I said. "It won't be for long."

And then Brother Hale came up the stairs.

* * *

You'd think a man who'd spent ten years running from the Church would feel something triumphant at the sight of his chief tormentor staggering into a collapsing miracle, sword drawn, robes smoking from the storm I'd called. What I actually felt was tired. Bone-tired, soul-tired, the specific exhaustion of a man who has just learned that the worst thing he ever did was nothing at all — that the storm which killed the royal line, the storm that named him kinslayer and exile, had been Hale's own Church's work, and he'd carried the guilt of it like a second spine for a decade.

Hale looked terrible. Good, some petty corner of me noted. His left arm hung wrong where a falling beam had caught him, and his face was streaked with soot and something that might have been tears or might have been sweat, and in this place I honestly couldn't tell you which would have been worse.

"You can't leave," he said. It wasn't a threat. That was the horrible thing about it — it came out of him like a prayer, like the last article of a creed he'd built his whole life on and could not now put down. "The border must hold. The country must be *named*. If the door closes without—"

"The door closes either way, Brother." Wren's voice, from my arms, thin but steady. "He chose. He's spending himself to hold it open for us. You're standing in a god's last act, and your part in it is to get out of the way."

Hale's eyes found me over her head. There was a madness in them, but it was the orderly kind, the kind that files itself and keeps records, and I recognized it because I'd seen it in every warden who ever burned a village: the certainty that the world is a ledger and someone must keep the accounts.

"Kavanagh," he said. "You of all people. You know what happens when borders fail. You *were* the failure."

"I was the scapegoat," I said. "There's a difference. You wrote it."

The Spire shuddered. Somewhere far above us, stone the size of a chapel began its long argument with gravity. Dust sifted down through the gold light of the remembered afternoon, and where the dust landed on the remembered grass, the grass flickered — hills stuttering like a candle flame, a shepherd on a distant slope dissolving into outline and reassembling a half-second late. The country inside the Spire was forgetting itself even as we stood in it. I watched a river lose its banks, then its water, then its idea of being a river, and I felt something in my chest crack along a seam I hadn't known was there.

Because here's the thing nobody tells you about exile: after enough years, the place you were thrown out of stops being a place and becomes a story you tell about yourself. "I'm the man who was exiled from Thornfell." Take away Thornfell, and what does that sentence even mean? Hale had spent ten years trying to silence a country. He was about to succeed, and it was going to take me down with it, because I had built my whole ruined identity on its foundations.

"Come with us," Wren said to Hale.

I nearly dropped her.

"He'll kill us the moment we're through," I said.

"No," Hale said quietly, and raised his sword one-handed, and I saw that he meant to die here, in the doorway, holding the line that no longer existed. "No, priestess. The Silence does not retreat. It endures. Someone must endure."

The Spire groaned. Above us, the ceiling of the remembered sky developed a crack, and through the crack I could see — I swear on whatever gods are left, dead or otherwise — ordinary grey rainclouds. Our world, leaking in.

"Hale," I said. "Listen to me. Just once, listen. Your Church caged gods in cells beneath this tower. I saw them. Answered prayers in iron boxes. Whatever you think you were preserving, you were *imprisoning*, and the prison is coming down, and the only question left is whether you die as a jailer or walk out as a man."

For a heartbeat — one heartbeat, no more — something moved behind his eyes. Something young, maybe; some novitiate who'd entered the Silence for reasons that predated the ledgers. Then the Spire gave another lurch, a beam the length of a ship's mast tore loose overhead, and Hale did the last thing I ever expected him to do.

He stepped aside.

Not out. Aside — clearing the doorway, sword lowering, face turned up toward the falling dark like a man watching the verdict come down. "Go," he said. "The Silence will find you. Wherever names are kept, we will find you."

"Brother," Wren said, and there was grief in it, actual grief, for this man who'd hunted her across half a mountain range, "there won't be anywhere for the Silence to be."

Then the world fell on him.

* * *

I want to tell it cleanly, but it doesn't come out clean. Here is what I remember:

I ran. With Wren in my arms — she weighed almost nothing, which frightened me more than the collapse did — I ran for the narrowing door while the remembered country came apart around us. The gold afternoon went out like a lamp. The hills folded. I heard, impossibly, faintly, the sound of bells from a village that had been dead for ten years, ringing for a service no one would ever attend, and then the bells were gone too, unmade mid-peal, and the sound they left behind was the sound of a name being erased from a page.

Behind me, Hale stood in the falling dark with his sword raised and his arm broken and his creed intact, and the country he had tried to silence took him back. Not cruelly. That's the part I can't shake. A beam came down, and then another, and then the whole weight of the Spire's upper reaches, stone that had been quarried from Thornfell's mountains by Thornfell's hands, and it covered him the way an avalanche covers a man — totally, indifferently, without malice. The country didn't hate him. The country didn't know he was there. He had spent his life insisting that silence was holy, and in the end the silence simply agreed with him, at volume.

I heard him say something as it came down. I've turned it over since, and I think — I will probably always think — it was a prayer. Not to the Silence. To whatever was leaving. An apology, maybe, or a request to be remembered, which was the one thing the dying god could no longer grant anyone.

Then the door was in front of me, a slit of grey rain-light barely wide enough for a cat, and the pull of the real world came through it cold and wet and honest, and I turned sideways with Wren against my chest and pushed through—

—and the Spire fell behind us with a sound I will hear for the rest of my life, which is the sound of a country saying its own name one last time and then stopping.

* * *

We came out onto the mountainside in the rain, and the mountain was wrong.

That took me a moment to understand, because the mountain itself was fine — granite, scree, the stunted pines I'd been dodging for two days. What was wrong was everything the mountain had been *in*. Maps live in more places than paper. Names live in trails and boundary stones and the way shepherds point when they give directions, and all of that was draining out of the world even as I knelt there in the wet heather with Wren coughing in my arms. I felt it go. I don't know how else to describe it — a pressure behind the eyes, a word on the tip of the tongue that slides away when you reach for it. I tried to say "Thornfell" and got as far as the *Th* before the shape of it dissolved, and what remained in my mouth was just weather. Just geography. A range of mountains where a country used to be.

Wren was crying. Silently, the way she did everything that mattered, tears cutting channels through the ash on her face.

"It's gone," she said. "He held the door and it's still gone. All of it. Everything he was."

"Yeah," I said, uselessly, because I had nothing better, and set her down gently against a boulder, and sat beside her, and let the rain do what rain does.

Here's what survival feels like, in case you're wondering, in case you ever win: it feels like loss wearing a borrowed coat. You expect triumph to have a taste. It doesn't. It tastes like rain and blood and the inside of your own mouth, and the person next to you is alive, and the thing you fought for is dead anyway, and you sit in the wet heather and realize that winning and losing were never opposites — they were just two ways of walking away from the same burning building.

"We're alive," Wren said, after a while, in the tone of someone checking arithmetic she distrusts.

"Barely. Both categories."

She laughed. It came out cracked and half-drowned and it was the best sound I'd heard in ten years, and I don't mean that romantically, or I do, but mostly I mean that laughter was proof the god hadn't taken everything. It had left this. One woman, breathing, laughing badly, in the rain, on a mountain that no longer belonged to any country at all.

"Where do we even go?" she asked. "There's no Thornfell to be exiled *from* anymore. You're free, Jonah. Do you understand? The sentence is void. The crime never happened. There's no—" She stopped. I watched her hit the same wall I'd hit a minute earlier. "There's no name for any of it."

I looked back once, at the heap of rubble where the Spire had stood, already going soft-edged in the rain. Somewhere under it was a man who had believed in silence so hard he'd become it. Somewhere under it, in cells that no longer existed, had been gods in cages, and their cages were rubble now too, and I chose — deliberately, the way you choose to close a door on a sickroom — not to wonder what had happened to them. Some questions you survive by not asking.

"Downhill," I said. "Downhill is always somewhere. Smuggler's rule."

"And after downhill?"

"After downhill," I said, "we find out who we are when we're not exiles. Should take years. Might take the rest of our lives." I got my shoulder under her arm and hauled us both upright, and the mountain — nameless now, ours now, or nobody's — held steady under our feet. "Fair warning: I have no idea who that is. Ten years ago I was a man with a country and a crime. Now I've got neither."

Wren leaned into me, light as a bird, smelling of ash and rain and chapel incense that had outlived its god.

"You've got a name," she said. "It's just not written down anywhere anymore. That's not the same as gone."

We started down. Behind us the rain filled in our footprints, patient as a ledger-keeper, erasing the only evidence that anyone had ever walked away from the fall of Thornfell alive.

By the time we reached the treeline, I couldn't have told you what we'd escaped from. Only that we had, and that we were together, and that the silence where a country used to be was the loudest thing I'd ever heard.

Somewhere below us, past the snowline, a smuggler's road waited, and at the end of it a queen who owned my name. I'd sold it to buy passage. I was going to want it back — or learn to live without it. Either way, the road went downhill first, and downhill, thank the dead gods, is always somewhere.
