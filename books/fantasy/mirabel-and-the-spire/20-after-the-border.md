## 20. After the Border

The first thing I did on the far side of the border was throw up.

I want it noted, for the record of a church that no longer exists to keep records, that I had just walked out of a collapsing tower between worlds, carried over the shoulder of a man who smelled like rain and blood and burnt wool, and my body's grand response to survival was to be violently sick into a ditch full of somebody else's daffodils.

Jonah held my hair back without comment. That's the kind of man he is — the kind who has seen enough terrible things that a priestess vomiting into spring flowers doesn't even make the list.

"There," he said, when I was done. "Now you're officially alive."

"I don't feel officially alive."

"Give it a minute. It grows on you. Like moss."

We were in a country I didn't know. That was the whole point of the door — the god had spent its last spark holding it open, and we'd fallen through into green hills and low stone walls and a sky so ordinary it felt almost insulting. Somewhere behind us, Thornfell's name was fading out of the world like ink left in the rain. Somewhere behind us, Brother Hale was buried under the country he tried to silence. Somewhere behind us, everything I had ever been.

And here — here there were daffodils, and a ditch, and a farmer's dog barking at us from a fence line with the cheerful aggression of an animal that had never once heard a prayer answered.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about surviving something impossible: the world does not rearrange itself around your survival. The sun comes up. Birds do their small bird business. A woman in a market asks if you want apples, and when you stare at her too long she asks again, louder, as though grief is a language barrier.

We walked for two days before we found a town with a name. Jonah bartered work for bread — he's good with his hands in the way exiles are good with their hands, all-purpose and uncomplaining — and I sat in the loft of a stranger's barn and tried to figure out how to be a person.

Because here's the thing. I had been a priestess since I was six years old. I had memorized the liturgies of the Silence, learned to hold stillness in my body like water holds the shape of its cup. And then a dying god had pressed its spark into my hands and said, essentially, *hold this*, and I had held it through fire and snow and rivers and one extremely memorable storm, and now —

Now the cup was empty. Now the spark was spent. Now the god whose voice I had carried against my ribs for a season of running was gone, gone the way a candle is gone after it gutters: not destroyed, exactly, because nothing is ever destroyed, but *departed*, returned to whatever dark it came from, leaving me standing in a barn loft with wax on my fingers and no idea what my hands were for.

I cried about it. Forgive me; I know it's not dignified. But dignity is a Church virtue and the Church is ash, so I lay down in the hay and cried like a child, great ugly sobs that startled the farmer's cat, and I cried for the god, and I cried for Thornfell, which wasn't even a place anymore but a memory wearing a place's clothes, and I cried for myself, who had been a vessel and was now just a girl with chapped hands and no title that meant anything anywhere.

The door creaked below me. Jonah climbed up with two bowls of stew and set them down without asking whether I wanted one. He sat against the wall across from me, long legs folded up, and waited.

"You're staring," I said finally.

"I'm waiting."

"For what?"

"For you to tell me to go away." He turned the spoon over in his fingers. "You haven't yet. So I'm staying."

"That's not how invitations work."

"It's how mine work," he said. "Eat your stew."

I ate my stew. It was bad stew, mostly barley and onion, and it was the best thing I had ever tasted, because my body had apparently decided that survival was negotiable until further notice and hot food tipped the scales. When I finished, Jonah took the bowl, and I expected him to leave, and instead he stayed sitting there in the lamplight with his back against the wall like a man keeping watch over nothing in particular.

"Why are you still here?" I asked him.

He considered the question honestly, which is more than most people would have done. "You haven't told me to go."

"Jonah."

"Wren."

"You could be anywhere. You're free. The Silence is falling apart — we've seen it, every town we've passed, wardens tearing off their badges or burning their own chapels. There's no bounty on you anymore. There's no *anything* on you anymore. You could walk in any direction and be a man with no name and no price and no priestess dragging him through hell." I hugged my knees. "So why are you in this barn?"

He was quiet for a while. Outside, the wind moved through the fields, and somewhere a nightjar called, and none of it required anything of me, and that was the strangest part — a world that simply went on.

"When I was exiled," he said at last, "the worst part wasn't the walking. Everyone thinks it's the walking. Ten years of roads, cold camps, wardens' eyes on you. No. The worst part was that nobody asked me to stay. Not once. Not a single soul in ten years looked at me and said, *stay*. Because why would they? I was the storm-caller. I was the curse. Asking me to stay would have been like inviting lightning to sleep by your hearth." He rubbed his thumb along the scar on his palm — the one from the Spire's threshold, the one I'd watched him get. "So I got very good at leaving before anyone could decide I should. It's a skill. Like juggling. Useless, but impressive."

"And now?"

"Now someone hasn't told me to go," he said, "and I find I've forgotten how to start."

I should have said something wise then. I was, after all, trained in wisdom — six years old in a chapel, learning the liturgies, learning that silence is where truth lives. But all I managed was:

"Don't go, then."

And he nodded, once, like a man accepting terms of surrender, and lay back against the wall, and slept.

* * *

Grief, I discovered, is not a single thing. It's a village. Some days the grief for my god came to visit, and some days the grief for Thornfell, and some days the grief for the girl I had been before any of it — the one who believed that devotion was a straight road and sacrifice was its destination, who had walked into the Spire fully prepared to be erased and had been saved instead, which is somehow harder.

The god never wanted a sacrifice. It told me that at the end, with its last breath of light. *I never wanted this. I wanted a witness.* And I have thought about that every day since. What does it mean, to be the witness of a dead god? Whose prayers do I carry now? Where do they go?

We drifted south through the spring. Jonah worked; I sat in chapels.

That sounds mad, I know. Every chapel we found belonged to the Silence, and the Silence was dying everywhere — its wardens deserting, its inquisitors scattered, its great grey doctrine crumbling like a wall with no mortar. But I kept going into them. I couldn't help it. I'd sit on a bench in a cold nave and wait, the way I used to wait, and nothing answered, and I'd sit anyway. Habit. Hope. Stubbornness. Take your pick.

One evening in a chapel outside a town called Marrow's Ford — the names here are all bones and rivers, it's a grim little country and I love it — Jonah came and sat beside me. He didn't pray. He'd rather chew glass than pray, I think, and fair enough, given what prayer cost him. He just sat, shoulder warm against mine, while I stared at the empty alcove where a god should have been.

"You keep looking for it," he said.

"Not for it. It's gone. I know it's gone." I traced a crack in the bench with my finger. "I'm looking for what's left. There's always something left. Ash is proof of fire."

"Ash is also just ash," he said, but gently, the way he says cruel things.

"I was its hands for a season. I healed a child with its light. I felt it dream. You don't just... put that down." I looked at him. "What did you put down? After the exile. After ten years. Did you ever stop being the storm?"

He was quiet long enough that I thought he wouldn't answer. Then: "No. You don't stop being what you are. You just stop apologizing for it." He flexed his hand, and I swear the air pressure changed, faintly, like the room taking a breath. "The storm didn't damn me, Wren. The lie did. The storm was always mine. I'm only now learning to say that out loud."

"So what am I, then?" I asked, and hated how small my voice came out. "If I'm not a priestess of the Silence, and the Silence is dead, and the god I actually served is dead — what's left? A girl who talks to empty rooms?"

"A girl who talks to empty rooms," Jonah said, "who pulled me out of a burning village when I'd sworn never to pull anyone out of anything. Who argued with a god and won. Who chose a man over her own salvation in under a breath." He shrugged, awkward, a big man trying to be small enough for the sentence. "You ask what's left. From where I'm sitting, quite a lot is left."

I kissed him then. In the empty chapel, in front of the empty alcove, with the dust motes drifting down through the last of the light like the world's smallest, quietest snowfall. He froze for exactly one heartbeat — the heartbeat of a man who has spent ten years believing himself uninvited anywhere — and then his hand came up to my jaw, careful, like I was something newly answered.

Later, lying in the chapel's back room on a pallet that smelled of cedar and old incense, I asked him the question I'd been carrying since the Spire fell.

"What happens to us now? There's no mission. No spark. No hunters — well, fewer. We're just two people who happened to each other during an apocalypse."

"We could walk," he said into the dark. "You always struck me as a walker."

"That's not a plan, Jonah."

"No," he agreed. "It's better than a plan. Plans are things you make when you don't trust the road. I trust the road now. Ten years of roads taught me one useful thing: they go somewhere. Always. Even when it looks like they don't."

I fell asleep to the sound of his breathing and the absence of a god, and it was, strangely, the most peaceful sleep I'd had since the spark first woke in my hands.

* * *

But peace, in my experience, is a rumor. Three mornings later, in the market square of Marrow's Ford, a woman grabbed my sleeve.

She was a farmwife, broad and weathered, with flour on her forearms and desperation in her face, and she said, "You're her. The miracle-worker. They talk about you on the roads — the priestess who heals."

"I'm not—" I started, because I'm honest to a fault, and also because the word *priestess* caught in my throat like a fishbone.

"My daughter," the woman said, and her grip tightened. "Fever four days. The herb-woman's given up. Please. I saw what you did at Kestrel Ford — my cousin's sister-in-law was there, she *saw*—"

I looked at Jonah. He was watching me with an expression I couldn't read — or could, and didn't want to. Because the spark was gone. Whatever I had carried, I had carried it into the Spire and given it back, or given it away, or watched it die holding the door open for us, and there was nothing left in me but a girl with chapped hands and a head full of liturgies for gods who weren't listening.

"I can't," I said. "Whatever I had — it's spent. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry."

The woman's face did something terrible. Not anger — worse. Understanding. She let go of my sleeve like she was setting down something heavy, thanked me anyway, because grief makes people polite, and walked back into the crowd toward her dying daughter.

I stood in the market square with apples in my basket and my heart in pieces, and Jonah put his hand on my shoulder, and I said, "Take me to her."

"Wren—"

"Not to heal her. I can't heal her. But I held a dying god for a season, Jonah. I know how to sit with death. Nobody taught the Silence that — the Silence only knew how to *wait*. I know how to stay." My voice cracked and I let it. "Take me to her anyway."

He took me to her.

The girl was eleven, burning, delirious, in a house that smelled of tallow and fear. I sat by her bed all night. I did the only liturgy I had left — not the Silence's, not the old god's, but one I made up out of the raw material of everything I'd lost. I told her about the Spire. About a god who chose to die so that two strangers could live. About borders and doors and the difference between an ending and a departure. I talked to her the way you talk to someone crossing a dark field, so they know the voice on the other side is friendly.

Near dawn, her fever broke.

The herb-woman said it was the willow bark, finally working. The mother said it was a miracle. I said nothing, because I genuinely don't know, and I have decided I'm allowed not to know. Maybe the fever broke on its own. Maybe something small and new and unnamed heard a girl talking in a sickroom and leaned closer. Maybe ash really is proof of fire, and fire leaves embers, and embers leave seeds.

But that night, packing to move on — because the mother's gratitude was becoming dangerous, people were starting to *look* at me, and I have seen what happens to women whom crowds decide are holy — Jonah appeared in the doorway with our bags already shouldered and a strange look on his face.

"The smuggler-queen," he said. "Vessa. She keeps roads through these hills. I paid her my true name for our passage south, back before all this. Names are currency with her kind." He rolled his shoulders like a man preparing to lift something heavy. "The Silence is collapsing. Her price on my name is void. Which means I can buy it back — if she'll sell."

"Why would you want it back?" I asked. "Your name is what damned you."

"Exactly," he said, and smiled, and it was the first smile I'd seen on him that didn't cost anything. "It's mine. It damned me, so it's mine. I'm going to go take it back." He held out his hand. "Come with me. If you want. Not because you need to. Because I'm asking."

Outside, far off in the hills, a chapel bell rang — one of the Silence's own, rung by no warden, for no service. Just ringing. Just a country remembering how to make noise.

I took his hand.

"Yes," I said. "Let's go get your name."
