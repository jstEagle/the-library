## 21. The Price of a Name

The Saltwife's court smelled the same as it had three weeks ago: tar, tallow, and the particular staleness of a hundred people who had all decided that bathing was a rumor spread by the Church. I stood at the mouth of the smuggler's tunnel with my hands empty and my name in my throat like a stone I'd been carrying so long I'd forgotten which pocket I'd put it in.

Wren waited outside. She'd wanted to come in — she'd argued about it on the road down from the pass, in that quiet way of hers where losing an argument sounds exactly like winning one — but I'd told her this was a debt I had to pay alone.

"You always say that," she'd said.

"I'm consistent."

"You're a coward with good posture."

Which was fair. But some doors you have to walk through by yourself or they don't count, and this one had been waiting ten years for me to walk through it.

* * *

The Saltwife held court in the belly of a beached ship called the *Generous Widow*, which had not been generous to anyone since before I was born and was now wedged into a sea cave like a whale that had given up on dignity. Lanterns hung from the ribs. Smugglers sat on crates playing cards with dice that were weighted in ways that would have made a bishop weep, if bishops wept, which the Church of the Silence maintained they did not, because feeling things was the first step toward praying to something.

I knew the drill. You don't approach the throne of a smuggler-queen empty-handed unless you're coming to sell yourself, and I had already sold the best part of myself here once. My name. Jonah Kavanagh, written into her ledger in exchange for passage through the White Road when the Church's wardens had been close enough to smell the incense on them. The name that carried a bounty. The name that made every warden between here and the coast look twice at any tall man with storm-grey eyes and a limp he'd earned falling off a mountain that hated him.

The Saltwife sat on the quarterdeck under a canopy of patched sailcloth, eating something out of a bowl with a spoon she held like a dagger. She was seventy if she was a day, small as a child and hard as a ship's keel, with rings on every finger and a parrot on her shoulder that had once, memorably, recited a Church litany back at an inquisitor until the man cried.

"Jonah," she said, without looking up. "Or do you prefer 'the man who sold his name to me for a boat ride'? I've been calling you that behind your back. It's catchier."

"I've come to buy it back."

"Mm." She took another spoonful. "And what are you paying?"

"That's rather the question, isn't it."

She looked up then. Her eyes were the pale grey of old ice, and they went through me the way they went through everyone, cataloguing damage, assessing resale value. Whatever she saw made her set the bowl down.

"You look different," she said. "Thinner. Not in the body. In the — " She waved the spoon vaguely at my chest. "In the cargo."

"A lot's happened."

"It usually does, around you. Sit down." There was no chair. I sat on a crate anyway, because arguing with the Saltwife about furniture is like arguing with the tide about schedules. "You want your name back. Fine. Here's the thing about names, Jonah Kavanagh, and I'll tell you for free because it's worth more than anything you could pay me: a name is only worth what someone will pay to take it away from you. And yours — "

She snapped her fingers at a boy lounging by the mast. "Ledger."

The boy brought a book the size of a paving stone. The Saltwife licked her thumb, found the page, and turned it toward me.

There was my name. Jonah Kavanagh. And across it, in her own hand, in ink so fresh it still shone:

**VOID.**

"Bounty's dead," she said. "Church pulled it. All of them, actually — every bounty, every writ, every price on every head from here to the coast. Pulled them overnight, like a tablecloth out from under the dishes. My contacts in three dioceses say the Silence is collapsing. Chapels going dark. Wardens deserting. One of my girls swears she saw an inquisitor sitting in a ditch crying, though inquisitors cry so rarely that I suspect she was lying for the pleasure of the image."

I stared at the word. VOID. Two syllables. Four letters. It should have felt like a door opening.

It felt like a trapdoor.

"When?" I said.

"Nine days ago. Right about, if you're asking, when a tower fell off the edge of the world and took a country's name with it." She smiled, and it wasn't a kind smile; the Saltwife didn't keep those in stock. "You wouldn't know anything about that."

"No," I said. "Nothing."

"Liar," said the parrot.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about exile: it becomes load-bearing.

For ten years, everything I was had been built on top of the fact that I couldn't go home. Every choice I made — the smuggling routes, the false names, the rule about never going back for anyone, a rule I broke inside a week of meeting Wren — every single one of them was a beam resting on that foundation. *They took Thornfell from you. They took your name. You are the man who cannot return.*

Remove the foundation and the whole structure just stands there, swaying, wondering what it's for.

I walked out of the *Generous Widow* with my name back in my ledger-entry way — the Saltwife charged me nothing, which frightened me more than any price would have, because the Saltwife giving something for free meant she thought she'd already been paid, and I spent half a mile trying to work out what I'd handed over without noticing — and Wren was sitting on a rock outside the tunnel mouth, mending a tear in her sleeve with thread she'd stolen from me.

"Well?" she said.

"I'm free."

She looked up. "Say it again."

"The bounty's void. The Church pulled every writ. Hale's network is — whatever happens to a spiderweb when you burn the spider. I can walk into any town on the coast and give my true name to the first guard who asks, and he'll shrug." I heard my own voice doing something strange with the words, flattening them out, like a man reading a list of someone else's possessions. "Ten years, and it's over. Just — over. No fight. No reckoning. Nobody even apologizes."

Wren put down the mending. She studied me the way she studied chapel ruins, with that particular tenderness she reserved for broken things that were pretending to be structurally sound.

"You wanted them to suffer," she said. Not accusing. Just seeing.

"I wanted it to *cost* them something. Ten years of my life, and they just — file it. Void. Next entry." I sat down beside her on the rock. Below us the valley opened out, all autumn gold and woodsmoke, ordinary as bread. "When they exiled me, it was the loudest thing that ever happened to me. Bells. Proclamations. My face on every church door in the country. And now it un-happens in the night, quietly, while I'm sleeping in a ditch, and nobody tells me."

"They're busy collapsing," Wren said. "A dying thing doesn't apologize to the people it hurt. It doesn't even notice them. I watched my god die, Jonah. Believe me. Dying takes all the room there is."

I knew she was right. I also knew that being right wasn't the same as being comforted, and that she knew that too, and that this — two people sitting on a rock knowing things about each other that couldn't be fixed, only kept company — was apparently what my life was now.

"I don't know how to be this," I said finally. "Not exiled. I've been no-one's-son and everyone's-criminal for so long I built a personality out of it. Who am I when the Church can't be bothered to hate me?"

"Jonah Kavanagh," she said. "You bought it back. Use it."

* * *

We went down into the valley because Wren wanted to see whether the stories were true, and because I wanted to test the freedom the way you test ice — standing on it, shifting your weight, listening for the crack.

The stories were true.

The village we came to at dusk was called Marlow's Ford, though the sign had been repainted so many times the name had gone soft, and it had a chapel. Of course it had a chapel; every village in what had been Thornfell had a chapel, and for ten years those chapels had been the emptiest buildings in the world, swept daily, candleless, their altars stripped bare by men in grey who believed that silence was a form of hygiene.

This chapel had candles. Dozens of them, guttering in the draft of the open door, and people kneeling in front of them — farmers, a smith with his apron still on, an old woman whose lips moved around words she clearly hadn't dared say aloud in a decade. And above the altar, where the Church had once nailed a placard reading THE SILENCE SPEAKS IN ITS OWN TIME, someone had hung a sprig of rowan berries, red as wounds, red as the spark Wren had carried.

Nobody was burning. Nobody was watching. A warden's tabard hung off a fencepost like a scarecrow's discarded skin.

Wren stood in the doorway a long time. I watched her watch them, and I saw her hands curl slightly at her sides, the ghost of holding something that wasn't there anymore.

"They're praying to nothing," she said softly. "The god's dead. I felt it go. There's nothing left to answer them."

But the old woman at the front lifted her head just then, and her face — I'll carry that face for a long time. It was the face of someone who has knocked on a door for ten years and heard, at last, not an answer, but footsteps. Something moving on the other side. Not the god she'd prayed to. Something else. Something new, or something very old wearing new clothes, or maybe just the sound of a country remembering its own name the way I was learning mine.

"Something answered her," Wren whispered. "Jonah. Something answered her, and it isn't Him."

* * *

That night we slept in the chapel's vestry, because the villagers offered and because Wren had developed, somewhere on the road, a weakness for roofs. I lay awake on a pallet that smelled of dust and old linen, listening to the candles hiss, doing the arithmetic of my life.

Ten years of exile: void. One name: reclaimed. One god: dead. One country: dissolved, its borders gone, its royal line a footnote in a story the Church would never admit it wrote. One priestess asleep in the next room, breathing slow, alive because a dying god had decided she was worth more than a border.

By any honest accounting, I had lost almost everything I'd started with and gained almost nothing I'd planned for. And yet lying there in the dark I felt lighter than I had since I was nineteen years old and the sky over Thornfell tore itself apart above my head.

Too light. That was the trouble. Freedom that arrives too quietly to trust — that's the phrase I'd use, if anyone asked, though nobody asked, because nobody had asked me anything in ten years except "where are you going" and "what are you carrying."

Because here's what kept me awake, staring at the rafters: the Church didn't collapse on its own. Institutions that size don't fall like that, all at once, everywhere, overnight. Something pushed. Something came up underneath them the way a storm comes up under a calm sea, and I had stood in the eye of enough weather lately to know the feel of it. The god had died holding a door open. Doors don't stay open on their own. Things come through.

And in the morning, when I went out to fetch water from the well, the old woman from the chapel was waiting for me by the fence, and she said, in the tone of someone passing along news about the weather:

"There's a light in the Spire again. Down in the valley where the tower used to stand. Been burning three nights now. And this morning—" she hesitated, and her eyes went past me, to Wren, who had come out into the grey dawn light with her sleeves rolled and her dead god's absence hanging around her like a coat. "This morning there are footprints leading away from it. Fresh ones. Heading north."

Toward us.

The old woman smiled, and it was the kindest, most frightening thing I'd seen all year.

"Someone's coming to thank you," she said.
