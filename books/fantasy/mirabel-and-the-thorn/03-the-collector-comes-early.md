## 3. The Collector Comes Early

Mirabel knew the man was coming before the man did.

She knew it the way she knew the fire wanted wood, the way she knew the bread on the table was thinking about going stale. Things told her things. The world was full of small voices that most people had stopped listening to, but Mirabel had never learned to stop, because stopping was not something her kind of knowing allowed. The wind came down off the Thornfell with a taste in it like cold iron and old coins, and it said: *soon*. The hearth-flames leaned all one direction, toward the door, as if bowing. And under the floorboards, in the roots of the mountain itself, something turned over in its sleep and began counting.

She did not know what it counted. She only knew that when it finished, it would finish with her name.

The tall man — Astrid, though his true name was a folded thing he kept behind his ribs where she could not read it — was mending a net by the fire. He did not feel the mountain counting. His ears were the ordinary kind. But he saw her go still, needle-thread hanging from her fingers where she had been winding it into shapes, and he set down his net.

"What is it?" he asked. Then, because she could not answer, he asked the way he had learned to ask, which was with his eyes moving to the paper and the charcoal stub he kept for her on the shelf.

Mirabel took them and drew quickly, pressing hard. She drew the wind first, because the wind had spoken loudest: a long gray tongue coming down between two peaks. Then she drew the taste of it — iron, coins — as best she could, which was a circle with teeth marks. Then she drew the thing underneath the house, the turning-over thing, and gave it many legs, because everything that counts has many legs. Spiders count their steps. Clocks count their hours. The mountain was counting its debt.

Astrid looked at the drawing for a long time. The firelight moved on his face and made his shadows argue with each other.

"That's not a wolf," he said quietly. "That's not anything I've mapped."

He was wrong, and she loved him a little for being wrong, the way you love a bird that cannot see the snare. It *was* something he had mapped. It was in the oldest margins of his oldest maps, in the writing that glowed when she spoke. The gray-faced ones who came to collect. Her mother's voice lived in her memory like a stone at the bottom of a well — *they do not knock twice* — and around that stone, water: the smell of the kitchen, the fever, the ledger-man with his soft hands saying *a soul of her weight will clear it, and she'll have time besides, seven years of borrowed breath, more than some get.* Her mother had wept into the flour. Mirabel remembered the flour more than the weeping. White dust on dark hands. That was how remembering worked for her: not stories, but objects, each one real, each one heavy, carried forever.

The wind said *soon*, and the mountain said *soon*, and the fire bowed low.

Then the knock came. Once.

* * *

Astrid moved fast after that, faster than she had thought his long careful body could move. He blew out every candle but one. He pulled back the corner of the rug by the pantry — and there was a door in the floor, a door she had not known was there, though she had lived in this house for the whole of her borrowed autumn. Under the door, stairs went down into breathing dark.

"The tunnels," he said. "Old roads. Smugglers cut them before your grandmother's grandmother was a rumor." His voice was steady but his hands were not, and Mirabel watched his hands the way she watched everything, because hands tell truths faces are too proud to tell. "Whatever comes to that door, whatever it says, you stay below. You don't come up. You don't make a sound. Can you do that?"

She nodded. Then, because lying still felt like a kind of silence too, she shook her head instead, and pointed at him, and pointed at herself, and laced her fingers together. *Together.* It was not a word she could say. It was a word she could draw with her whole body.

Something crossed his face then, quick as a fish under ice. He crouched so they were eye to eye, and his eyes were the color of the sky over the pass in winter, which is to say the color of a thing deciding whether to snow.

"No," he said gently. "Not together. Not this time. This time you're the treasure and I'm the map, and maps don't get to be brave."

She did not know what he meant. But she went down the stairs anyway, because his hands had stopped shaking once he touched her shoulder, and that seemed important. He closed the door over her head. Darkness came down like a lid.

* * *

In the dark, Mirabel listened.

The smugglers' tunnel was a throat of earth and old timber, and it spoke to her in its own way — groaning when the wind pressed on the mountain's shoulders, dripping its slow syllables somewhere to her left. She sat with her knees drawn up and her arms around them and she made herself small the way her mother had taught her to make herself small during the fever-year, when strangers in gray came through the village asking questions about debts.

Gray. They had worn gray even then. She had thought, in her smallness, that they were weather given bodies.

Above her, through the floorboards, through the door, through the skin of the house, she heard the visitor enter.

It did not walk like people walk. People walk in a rhythm, left-right-left-right, a little song the body sings without knowing. This thing walked in *counts*. One step. A pause exactly as long as the step. Another step. As if it were measuring the house with its feet, or as if walking were a tax it paid grudgingly, coin by coin.

"Mapmaker." The voice was not loud. It was the voice of a ledger being opened, of paper sliding against paper. "You have an item in your keeping."

"She's a child," Astrid said. His voice came down through the boards flattened by the wood, but Mirabel could hear the shape of it, the way you can hear the shape of a river under ice. "She's sick and mute and half-frozen. She wandered out of the storm."

"She is a *balance outstanding*." A pause, measured precisely. "Sold in good faith. Signed in blood and flour, which the court accepts as binding. Seven years of borrowed breath were granted. The grant ends at solstice. You are harboring what does not belong to the surface world."

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"You lie," said the Collector, and there was no anger in it at all, which was the worst part. Anger would have been weather. This was arithmetic. "Lying is permitted. It changes nothing. Weights do not care what you say about them. I am here early because early is efficient. The court dislikes loose entries. Loose entries become lost entries, and lost entries become *audits*, and audits are expensive for everyone." Another pause, another measured footfall, closer now, directly above her. Dust sifted from the ceiling between the floorboards and landed in her hair like gray snow. "I will leave a token. When solstice comes, I will return. If the entry is still loose, I will take it, and I will also take the harborer's weight, as handling fees. Do you understand handling fees, mapmaker?"

Silence from above. Then Astrid's voice, very quiet: "I understand you."

"Good. Understanding is the beginning of compliance."

Mirabel pressed both hands over her mouth. Not because she might cry out — she had no cry in her, she had never had a cry since the day of the signing — but because her whole body wanted to run *up* the stairs, toward the sound of him, the way a plant wants the sun, stupidly, helplessly, root and all. She held herself down. She held herself down the way the mountain holds down its fires.

The footsteps counted their way to the door. The door opened. Cold poured in; she tasted it even down here, iron and coins and something older, something like the inside of a locked book.

Then the footsteps counted away, down the path, into the wind, and the wind swallowed them without comment, because wind eats everything eventually.

* * *

She waited until the house above stopped holding its breath. She waited longer than that, because waiting was free and mistakes were not. Only when Astrid's boots crossed the floor overhead — his particular rhythm, left-right-left-right, the human song — did she climb the stairs and push at the door.

He was standing at the front door of the house, very still, looking at the doorframe.

Mirabel came to stand beside him and followed his gaze.

There, driven deep into the wood beside the latch, stood a thorn. It was black as the space between stars, longer than her hand, and it had gone into the frame as if the frame were butter. No hammer had struck it. There was no wound in the air where a hand would have been. It simply *was*, the way a scar is, the way a debt is. And it was growing — slowly, the way mountains grow, but growing — a fine hairline crack running up from it through the grain of the doorframe, like a vein, like a signature.

Astrid reached out and touched it. He snatched his fingers back at once. When Mirabel looked at his fingertips, they were gray — not cold-gray, not frost-gray, but the flat colorless gray of ash, of paper burned past saving. The color faded slowly, like a tide going out.

"It's a promise," he said. His voice was rough, scraped. "Or a threat. Same thing, probably, written in their alphabet." He looked down at her, and his face did the arguing thing again, shadows pulling different directions. "Solstice. That's — " he counted under his breath, lips moving — "nineteen days. It said nineteen days. It wasn't supposed to come at all yet. Vessa said collection happens at solstice, not before. Why come early? Why leave a — "

He stopped. Mirabel watched him arrive at the answer the way you watch someone walk into a room where bad news is already sitting.

"Because it wanted me to see it," he said. "Because fear makes people run, and running leaves tracks. Or it wanted me to feel the clock." He laughed, one short ugly note with no music in it. "Nineteen days. I can't fight this. You can't outrun a debt, girl. Debts live under the ground you'd run across. They're *in* the ground."

Mirabel tugged his sleeve. When he looked down, she held up her hands and drew in the air, big slow shapes, the language of her body: *the tunnels. the deep roads. the mountain's own doors.* She had felt them beneath the house all along, the old ways threading down through the rock like veins through a wrist. Everything that goes down must have doors. Doors open. Doors are just promises in reverse.

Astrid stared at her hands moving. Then at the thorn. Then at her again, and something changed in his face — the fish-under-ice look again, but this time the fish came up, and it was carrying something in its mouth.

"There's a man," he said slowly, "on the ferry at Ravenmere. An exile. Corvane. Half-blind Vessa says he came *up* from below, years ago, and lived." He put his hand flat against the doorframe, carefully, beside the thorn but not touching it, the way you'd put your hand near a sleeping animal whose temper you didn't trust. "If anyone knows the price of stealing back a soul — "

Outside, the wind came down off the Thornfell again, and this time it did not say *soon*.

This time it said *nineteen*, over and over, like a bell with a crack in it, and far beneath the floorboards, beneath the tunnels, beneath the roots of everything, the many-legged counter turned over in its sleep and began, patiently, to count down.
