## 4. The Exile of the Deep Roads

The river at Hollowmere ran the color of old pewter, and it smelled of iron and rot and something older beneath both — a smell Astrid had only ever encountered in the deepest caves he'd mapped, where the air itself seemed to have been standing still for centuries.

He came down from the high road in the gray hour before dawn, Mirabel's small hand wrapped in his. She had not slept. Neither had he. The black thorn driven into his doorframe three nights past had begun, impossibly, to *grow* — fine filaments creeping along the wood like veins of ink through wet paper — and when he'd pulled it free with tongs and thrown it into the hearth, it had screamed. Not loudly. That was the worst of it. A thin, patient sound, like a kettle far away in another house, and it had gone on until dawn.

So they walked. Through frost-stiffened bracken, past the shrine where travelers left copper coins for a god no one could name anymore, down to the water where the ferryman kept his lantern lit at all hours, because he did not trust the dark, or perhaps because the dark did not trust him.

Corvane was already on the water when they arrived, poling a flat barge through the mist with slow, deliberate strokes, as though the river were a thing that might be offended by haste. He was taller than Astrid remembered — ferry work stretched a man, Vessa had said — with a face like a cliff face, all planes and weathering, and eyes the color of deep water over deep stone. When they were younger men, before the fever, before everything, Astrid had seen him once at a market fair and thought: *there is a man counting something behind his face.* He understood now what the counting was. Corvane had spent two hundred years, by some accounts, keeping ledgers for a court that lived beneath the world, and ledgers never leave a man. They live in the hands. In the way he held the pole exactly perpendicular, exactly measured, as if even the river owed him an accounting.

Astrid raised the lantern. "Passage."

Corvane's pole stopped. The barge drifted, turning slowly, until its blunt nose pointed at the bank.

"Surveyor Dravenmoor." His voice was gravel poured slow. "You don't fish."

"I don't."

"You don't travel the Mere. You told the toll-keeper at Stonebridge, four years ago, that you'd sooner swim than pay a ferryman's price." A pause. "I remember prices. It's what I'm for."

"I need the deep roads." Astrid stepped to the water's edge, and Mirabel pressed closer against his leg, her breath fogging in the cold. "Under the Thornfell. All the way down."

For a long moment the only sound was the river worrying at the pilings, and somewhere in the reeds a bird making a noise like a hinge that wanted oil.

Then Corvane laughed. It was a terrible sound, mostly because there was so little of it — one dry exhalation, quickly strangled, as though laughter too appeared somewhere in his accounts.

"The deep roads," he said. "To the Undercourt. With a *child*." His gaze moved to Mirabel, and something crossed his face — not softness, nothing so useful as softness, but recognition. The look of a man seeing a debt walk around in shoes. "She's marked. I can see it from here. Gray at the edges, like paper going damp." He looked back at Astrid. "How much time?"

"Winter solstice. Less now."

"And you mean to go down and take her back." It was not a question, but Astrid answered it anyway, because some things needed saying aloud to become real.

"Yes."

"Steal her," Corvane said, "from the court that owns her. From the Collectors who have never once, in the whole history of their office, lost a soul entrusted to them." He poled the barge in against the bank, wood grinding softly on shingle. "Get on, then. We'll talk where the reeds can't listen. Reeds repeat things. Everything out here does."

* * *

The ferryman's hut stood on a spit of gravel where the river bent, low-roofed, chimney smoking a thread of greenish smoke that smelled faintly of candlewax and grave-dirt. Inside it was spare as a monk's cell: one cot, one table, one chair, walls hung with nets that had not been nets for a very long time. Astrid looked closer and went cold. They were ledgers. Strips of vellum and bark-paper and something that might have been skin, stitched into net-shapes, every mesh knotted around a name. Hundreds of names. Thousands. Some of the knots still moved, very slightly, like sleeping throats.

Mirabel saw them too. She made a small sound and turned her face into Astrid's coat, and he put a hand on her hair and did not let himself shake.

"You keep their contracts," he said quietly.

"I keep *records*. There's a difference, and the difference is the only thing I own." Corvane set a kettle on the hook over the fire, and the ritual of it steadied his voice. "Two hundred years I served the Undercourt as steward of passages. Every road down, every gate, every toll. I wrote the maps you're about to ask me for." He turned. "And when I refused to enter one final entry — a child's name, a mother's signature, a debt inherited — they unmade my office and cast me up through the mountain's throat like a pit of a fruit. So." He sat, folding his long frame onto the stool. "Ask."

Astrid set his satchel on the table and drew out the map.

He had carried it for eleven years and shown it to no one. It was his masterwork and his shame both: a survey of the Thornfell's northern approaches made during the last expedition he'd taken with his brother Tomas, before the fever winter, before the empty house, before the silence that had filled his life ever since. But it was more than a survey. In the western margin, in Tomas's careless hand, was a sketch of the pass at Greywater with a joke written beneath it in their private childhood cipher; and along the southern edge, in his father's hand, corrections to the river courses that no living surveyor knew; and in the corner, faded almost to nothing, his mother's mark — a small thorn-and-star she'd drawn on every map in the house, a blessing, she'd said, so that whoever carried it would find their way home.

Three dead hands on one sheet of paper. Astrid laid it flat and smoothed it once, and found his own fingers trembling, and hated them for it.

"This," he said. "For passage knowledge. All of it. Down to the court itself."

Corvane did not touch the map. He looked at it the way other men looked at relics, or wounds.

"You know what you're offering."

"I know what it is."

"It is not parchment, surveyor. It is the last place those people exist. Burn a man's portrait and he dies a second time." Corvane's eyes lifted. "And you'd trade them for a road."

"For a *child*," Astrid said, and his voice came out lower and rougher than he intended. "She has no one. Her mother sold her to save the rest of her family — I've read enough in Vessa's books to guess the shape of it — and now the debt's come due, and everyone who might have fought for her is dust or cowardice. Except me." He flattened his palm on the map, over his mother's fading star. "I have spent eleven years keeping everyone at arm's length because holding on hurt too much. Well. Here is my arm's length. Take the map."

Silence. The kettle began, faintly, to sing.

Corvane reached out at last — slowly, the way one reaches toward a bird that may startle — and turned the map toward himself. His finger traced the southern edge, the dead father's rivers, and stopped at the corner star. Something worked in his jaw.

"A mother's mark," he said. "We had those, below. Before the courts grew teeth. A sign that the road remembers you're loved, even when you forget it yourself." He withdrew his hand as if the paper had grown warm. "You'll want it back after. Everyone wants it back after. And I will give it back, because I am done keeping things that belong to grief." He looked up, and for one moment the ledger-keeper fell away and Astrid saw the exile underneath: old, and tired, and alone in a way that made Astrid's eleven years look like an afternoon. "But first you will hear the price. All of it. Because you are about to do the stupidest, bravest thing a man can do, and I will not have you walking into that mountain thinking courage is currency. It isn't. Down there, courage is just the smell of meat."

"Tell me."

"The Undercourt does not deal in coin. Coin is a symbol, and symbols are for the sunlit world. Below, everything is weighed in kind." Corvane rose and went to the wall of knotted names, and touched none of them. "A soul sold in desperation can be redeemed — the old law allows it, the law older than the court itself, which is why they hate it and honor it in the same breath. But redemption requires balance. You cannot buy back a soul with gold or blood or years. You must pay with a soul of equal weight. Freely given. Fully surrendered." He turned. "Do you understand what a soul weighs, surveyor? It is not your breath. It is not your pulse. It is every love you have ever carried, every memory bound to love, every grief that proves the love was real. That is the weight. That is what the scales measure."

Astrid's mouth had gone dry. "And if the soul offered is heavier than the one redeemed?"

"Then the court keeps the difference." Corvane smiled, and it was the worst expression Astrid had ever seen on a human face, because it was gentle. "They are *very* fond of heavy souls. A man who has loved much and lost much — a man who has spent eleven years doing nothing but grieving, feeding the weight nightly like a caged animal—" He shook his head slowly. "You are not walking into that mountain to make a trade, Astrid Dravenmoor. You are walking in carrying a feast."

Outside, the light had changed. The pewter river had gone bright and hard, and the mist was burning off the reeds, and somewhere upstream a bell was ringing — not the chapel bell, which was cracked and dull, but something thinner, sweeter, wrong. Three notes. Then three again. Then three.

Mirabel stiffened against Astrid's side. She grabbed his sleeve and pulled, and when he looked down at her, her eyes were enormous and fixed on the eastern hills, where the morning fog was parting around something tall and gray that should not have been visible at that distance, and yet was.

Corvane blew out the candle that wasn't lit. The hut went dim anyway.

"They've moved the collection forward," he said quietly. "Or they've sent a second Collector to be certain. Either way, surveyor — the roads I was going to teach you over a week of careful talk?" He was already pulling a rolled bundle of vellum from beneath the floorboards, already knotting his ferryman's coat. "You'll learn them tonight. We leave within the hour, ahead of it, or we don't leave at all."

The bell rang three notes again. Closer.

Mirabel's hand found Astrid's and gripped it, small and fierce, and Astrid folded the map — his father's rivers, his brother's joke, his mother's star — into his coat, over his heart, and followed the exile down to the water.
