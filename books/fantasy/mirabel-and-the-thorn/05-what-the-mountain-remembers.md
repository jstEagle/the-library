## 5. What the Mountain Remembers

Mirabel thought of remembering as a kind of weather.

It came over her the way fog came off the low fields in autumn — not all at once, but in patches, thickening at the edges of things until the whole world was soft and wrong and full of shapes that had no business being there. The first patch came on the third morning of the climb, while Astrid was shaking frost from his boots and she was watching a spider rebuild its web across a juniper branch, thread by patient thread.

The spider reminded her of someone.

She did not know who. That was the terrible arithmetic of it: the memory arrived without its name attached, like a letter with the seal broken and the signature torn away. A woman's hands, quick and clever, spinning something — thread, or wool, or light — and a voice humming under her breath, a tune that Mirabel's mouth began to hum before her mind could stop it.

Astrid looked up from his map. "What's that song?"

She couldn't answer. She never could. Words came to her only in the old tongue, the one that made ink glow, and even those words were heavy as river stones; she spent them sparingly. So she only looked at him, and kept humming, and watched something move behind his eyes — that flicker he got when she surprised him, quick as a fish under ice.

"You don't know what you're humming," he said. Not a question. He had learned to read her silences the way she was learning to read his maps: slowly, and then all at once. "Do you."

She shook her head. Then, because it felt like a lie to leave it there, she pressed one hand flat against her own chest, where the humming lived.

Astrid was quiet for a long moment. The wind moved through the pines above them, carrying the smell of snow that hadn't fallen yet. "We'll find out," he said finally, and folded the map away, and shouldered his pack, and started up the trail again. But his voice had gone careful, the way it went careful whenever the mountain was mentioned, and Mirabel understood — in the wordless way she understood most things — that he was afraid for her, and that he thought she didn't notice.

She noticed everything. That was the trouble with being made mostly of silence: there was room in her for the whole world.

* * *

The memories came more often as they climbed.

They came in smells first — woodsmoke that smelled like *home* though she had no home she could name; bread rising; a particular kind of rain on a particular kind of roof, slate, not shingle, drumming a rhythm she knew down in her bones. They came in colors: a blue door. She woke in the night in the hollow beneath the leaning rock where they'd sheltered, certain of the blue door, certain of the brass handle worn bright by a thousand hands, and she lay staring at the stars through the crack above her and grieved for a door.

That was the strangest part, she decided. You could grieve for a door. Grief didn't need a person attached. It would fasten itself to anything — a tune, a handle, the smell of wet wool — the way burrs fastened to a hem.

On the fifth day they crossed a stream running fast with snowmelt, and Astrid carried her across on his back because the stepping stones were iced. His shoulders were bony and warm and smelled of pine resin and old paper, and halfway across, with the water roaring below them, another memory broke open inside her like an egg.

*A man's back. This same jolt of being carried. But the man had been thinner, poorer, his coat patched at the elbows, and he had been walking not across a stream but through a market crowd, and he had been saying, over his shoulder, in a voice rough with trying to be cheerful:* Almost there, little bird. Almost there.

Her mother had called her little bird.

The knowledge landed whole, complete, undeniable — and with it came the rest, tumbling after like stones down a scree slope. *Little bird.* A kitchen with a blue door. A debt. Her mother's face at night, lit by candle-end, doing sums on the back of a bill of sale, the numbers getting worse every month. And then a visitor in fine gray clothes whose face Mirabel could not hold onto no matter how hard she gripped it, a face like water, like smoke, like a hole cut in the air — and her mother kneeling, and her mother crying without any sound at all, which was how Mirabel had learned that grief could be silent too, that silence wasn't emptiness, sometimes silence was the loudest thing in a room—

"Mirabel." Astrid set her down on the far bank. "You've gone white as the snowline. What happened?"

She opened her mouth. Nothing came. The old tongue offered her words — *mother, sold, mine, gone* — but each one felt like pulling a tooth, and besides, words were the wrong shape for this. Words were narrow. What lived in her now was wide as a valley.

So she tugged at his sleeve instead, and pointed at his pack, where the maps were rolled.

He frowned. "You want to draw?"

She nodded so hard her hood fell back.

* * *

They stopped early that day, in the lee of a granite shoulder where the wind couldn't reach. Astrid unrolled the least important of his maps — a survey of the lower foothills, done years ago, its edges soft with handling — and smoothed it flat on a slab of stone, weighting the corners with pebbles. From his kit he took out the stub of charcoal pencil he used for field corrections and held it out to her, handle first, the way you'd offer a knife to a stranger: carefully, point turned away.

"Margins are yours," he said. "I've always believed maps should hold more than roads anyway. Every good map I own has somebody's whole life scribbled in the edges. Battles fought over a well. Weddings marked with a cross. My—" He stopped. Something crossed his face, quick and closed, like a shutter drawn. "People mark what matters. Go on."

Mirabel took the pencil.

She had never drawn anything before, or if she had, the memory of it was still sealed somewhere in the fog. But her hand seemed to know things her mind did not, and the first stroke went down sure and dark: a rectangle. A door. She shaded it blue — or tried to; charcoal only gave her black, so she pressed hard, and the door came out the deepest black on the page, bluer than black, a door at midnight.

Then the house around it. Low walls, slate roof — she drew the roof lines and heard again the rain-drumming, *that* rhythm, hers. A chimney. Smoke curling up in a spiral that she drew twice around before she realized she was drawing it the way her mother had hummed, round and round, the same tune.

Astrid had gone very still beside her. He was watching her hands, not the paper, and there was an expression on his face she had no name for yet — she filed it away with all the others she was collecting from him, in the growing gallery behind her ribs.

She drew the kitchen next, small in the margin: a table, a candle, a woman bent over figures. She drew the woman small because that was how memory held her — far away, candle-lit, always just about to turn around. She drew the woman's hands, because those she remembered perfectly: quick, clever, spinning. She drew the bill of sale on the table, and the numbers going down and down like steps into a cellar, and then, because the truth of it rose in her throat like sickness, she drew the gray-faced visitor. Or she tried to. Her pencil made a shape and her hand refused it, dragging the charcoal into a smear, a smudge, a hole in the paper's skin — and she understood suddenly why she could not draw the face, and the understanding was so cold that she dropped the pencil.

Astrid picked it up. He studied the smeared place for a long time.

"That's the Collector," he said quietly. Not a question either. He was good at not-questions. "You saw him. Before."

She nodded once. Her teeth wanted to chatter and she wouldn't let them.

"And this woman." His finger hovered over the small figure at the table, not quite touching. "Your mother."

The word went through her like an arrow finding the gap in armor. *Mother.* She had carried the shape of the word since the doorstep, since Vessa's blind eyes had widened and her voice had dropped to a whisper — *a sold soul* — but carrying a word and knowing it were different things, and now she knew it, and knowing it hurt exactly as much as Astrid had warned her knowing things would.

She nodded again. And then, because the hurt needed somewhere to go, she leaned sideways into him, forehead against his arm, the way a bird leans into the wind so it won't be blown backward.

He froze. For three heartbeats he was stone, a man who had forgotten what touching was for. Then, slowly, carefully, as if handling something older and more fragile than any document in Vessa's archive, he put his arm around her shoulders.

"There was a debt," he said. It wasn't a question, but she answered it anyway, nodding against his sleeve. "And she paid it with you." His voice did something complicated on the last words, cracked down the middle and mended badly. "Gods. She must have—"

He didn't finish. Mirabel was glad. Some sentences deserved to die unfinished.

They sat like that while the light went long and gold across the foothills below, and the shadows of the Thornfell peaks reached eastward toward them like fingers. After a while Astrid said, "Draw me more. Everything you remember. Every scrap. I want your story in these margins where I can keep it safe." A pause. "Maps are how I keep things safe. It's the only way I know how."

So she drew.

She filled the margins of the foothill survey and then, when that was full, he gave her the valley map, and then the ridge survey, turning each one over so she could use the backs. She drew the blue door eleven times, because eleven times was how many mornings she could remember her mother lifting her to touch the brass handle, a morning-blessing, *knock knock, who's home? Only love, only us.* She drew the kitchen garden, the bean rows, the crooked fence. She drew a hen with an expression of great judgment. She drew herself — small, stick-armed, hair wild — standing in a doorway, and beside the doorway figure she wrote nothing, because she could not write, but Astrid looked at it and said softly, "That's you," and she nodded, and something settled in her chest, some piece sliding home.

By full dark she had covered six maps, and Astrid had built a fire, and he sat feeding it twigs and reading her drawings the way other men read scripture — closely, hungrily, murmuring observations. "Slate roof, so the lowlands, near the quarries maybe. Bean rows mean a south-facing plot. Your mother spun — wool, or flax?" He glanced up. "There's a spindle in your picture of the corner shelf. Flax, then. Linen country. I can find linen country, Mirabel. I've mapped half the world's back doors." His eyes had a light in them she hadn't seen before, fierce and banked like a coal. "When this is done — after solstice — we'll find that blue door. Whatever's left of it. We'll find where you come from."

*Where you come from.* She turned the phrase over. It assumed a past. It assumed a future. It assumed, most impossibly of all, an *after* — that there would be two of them, together, on the far side of the mountain, with time enough to go looking for doors.

Found family, she thought, though she had no word for it. It was like a fire built in a place where no fire had ever been. Everything about it was improbable. Everything about it was warm.

She crawled into her bedroll and slept better than she had since the doorstep, with six maps full of her mother lying coiled in Astrid's pack like sleeping birds.

* * *

The dream came near dawn, and it was not a memory. She knew the difference now; memories had warmth in them even when they hurt, and this had none.

She stood on a road of black glass beneath a sky of roots, and the gray-faced thing was there, except here, in the deep country of sleep, its face was not blank after all. Here it had a face, and the face was smiling, and it held up one long finger the way a schoolmaster holds up a correction.

*Early,* it said, in the old tongue, in a voice like frost forming. *The court grows impatient. The thorn is planted. The door is marked.*

Behind it, vast and slow, the mountain breathed.

Mirabel woke with the taste of iron in her mouth and found Astrid already awake, already standing, looking north along the trail with his hand shading his eyes. Far ahead, where the pass narrowed between two shoulders of rock, something dark was nailed to a waystone post — a splinter of black wood, longer than her forearm, driven deep, still fresh enough that the stone around it wept sap-dark tears.

Astrid stared at it for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was very quiet.

"We're expected."
