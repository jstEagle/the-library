## 6. The Tollgate of Names

The mountain did not look like a place where names mattered. It looked like weather given bones — a shoulder of black stone shouldering the clouds, its lower slopes stitched with pine and old snow, its summit lost in a gray that was neither cloud nor rock but something between. Astrid had drawn it a hundred times from a hundred angles. He had never once thought to draw what lived inside it.

Now he could not stop thinking about it.

They climbed through the last of the forest as the light went long and copper, Mirabel riding his shoulders with her small hands laced under his chin and her breath warm against the back of his neck. She had not spoken — she could not, not in any tongue his ears could hold — but all day she had been tapping out rhythms against his collarbone, little patterns of pressure that he was beginning, uneasily, to understand. Two taps meant *look*. Three meant *stop*. A slow drag of her finger along his jaw meant something he had no word for yet, though it made his chest ache in a way he had decided not to examine.

Corvane walked ahead, ferryman's coat swapped for a traveler's oilskin, his lantern hooded so that only a coin of amber light showed at his feet. The exile moved like a man returning to a house he had sworn never to enter again: quick, then hesitant; certain, then listening for footsteps behind him.

"The road ends," Corvane said, without turning. "What comes after it isn't a road. Try to remember that when you see it."

"I've mapped smuggler roads for twenty years," Astrid said. "There's always another road."

"Not this time." Corvane stopped at the lip of a granite shelf where the pines gave up all at once, as if a hand had swept them aside. Beyond it the mountain rose sheer, and set into that sheer face — so obvious now that Astrid felt foolish for not seeing it from below — stood a gate.

It was not built. That was his first thought, and his second, and his third. It had *grown*, the way a scar grows, or a tooth through a child's gum. Two pillars of living root, black and glossy as lacquered wood, rose forty feet into the dusk and arched together at the top, braiding into a crown of thorns. Between them hung a door of pale wood, doorless in the way that mist is doorless — a surface that shimmered like water standing on end. And carved into the lintel, in letters that seemed to shift when looked at directly, ran a single line of script.

Astrid knew it. His stomach dropped before his mind caught up, because he knew it from the margins of his oldest maps, from notes in a dead surveyor's hand, from Vessa's trembling finger tracing the same curves on her desk three days ago.

*Undercourt speech.*

"What does it say?" he asked, though part of him already knew.

Corvane's jaw worked. "It says, *Name yourselves, or be named.*"

* * *

They made camp in the lee of the shelf, though none of them slept. Astrid laid out his maps by lantern light and found himself doing what he always did when afraid: measuring. The gate's height against the cliff. The distance from the treeline. The angle of approach. As if ink and arithmetic could make a thing less terrible.

Mirabel sat cross-legged beside him, watching his pen move. After a while she reached over, took the pen from his fingers with the gentle imperiousness she applied to everything, and drew in the margin — a small figure, stick-limbed, holding hands with a taller one. Above them she scratched a rough triangle with a jagged crown: the gate. Then she crossed out the tall figure.

Astrid stared at it. "I'm still going through."

She looked up at him. Her eyes were dark and enormous and older than seven years old, older than seventy, and in them was an expression he recognized from mirrors in his worst years: the look of someone who has already cost other people too much.

"No," he said quietly, and took her hand, and folded her small fingers closed around the pen. "You don't get to decide what I can afford. Nobody's ever asked me that question, and I've been asking it of myself since I was younger than you. Let me answer it once for someone else."

She held his gaze a long moment. Then she leaned her head against his arm, and stayed there, and did not draw anything more.

Across the fire, Corvane watched them with an expression Astrid couldn't read — pity, maybe, or its colder cousin, recognition.

"You should know what you're walking toward," the exile said. "Not the court. Not yet. The gate first. The gate is honest, which is more than I can say for anything past it." He turned his hooded lantern so its coin of light fell across his own face, hollowed and gray. "Every threshold into the deep demands payment. Down there, coin is worthless. Blood is cheap — they have buckets of it. What they trade in is *true things*. A name is the truest thing a person carries."

"My name is Astrid Dravenmoor."

"Is it?" Corvane smiled without humor. "That's two names, man. A given name and a family name. The given one is yours alone — the court can't take what only belongs to you; it has no weight to anyone else. But Dravenmoor..." He said it slowly, tasting it. "That's your father's name, and his father's, and every soul who ever carried it. Graves in a churchyard wearing it. Children not yet born who would have worn it. That's weight. That's *wealth*."

Astrid's mouth had gone dry. "And if I refuse?"

"Then the gate names you itself." Corvane's voice dropped. "I've seen it done, once, to a smuggler who thought he could bluff his way through. The gate spoke his whole self back at him — every secret, every shame, every name he'd ever called himself in the dark — and then it kept speaking, and it didn't stop, and by the time we got him away from it there wasn't enough of him left to know which way was down. The gate doesn't kill you. It just tells the truth until nothing is left but the truth."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above, wind moved through bare branches with a sound like whispering, like a crowd murmuring behind a door.

"And if I give it?" Astrid asked. "The family name?"

Corvane was quiet for a long moment. When he answered, his voice was almost gentle, which was somehow worse.

"You'll feel it leave. And you'll find out what you're actually made of, because everything your name was holding up will come down at once. Get some sleep. Tomorrow you'll want steady legs."

But Astrid did not sleep. He lay with Mirabel curled against his side, breathing in the small rhythm of her, and tried to imagine being a man without a family name, and discovered he could not. It was like trying to imagine the sky without blue in it. *Dravenmoor* was the smell of his father's pipe smoke. It was his mother saying it sternly when he'd tracked mud across her clean floor. It was carved, he suddenly remembered with a jolt, on the headstones up at Greywater Rise — three of them now, fever-taken, moss already softening the letters. If he gave the name away, what happened to the stones? What happened to the boy he had been, kneeling in the frozen churchyard dirt, promising them he'd survive?

He fell asleep near dawn with the question unanswered, and dreamed of doors.

* * *

They approached the gate at midmorning, under a sky the color of unpolished pewter. Up close, the roots were worse than they had looked from below — they *breathed*, slowly, the black bark swelling and easing like the flank of a sleeping animal, and the pale door between them rippled at their approach, sensing them the way water senses a dropped stone.

Mirabel pressed close to Astrid's leg. He could feel her heartbeat through the fabric of his coat, quick as a bird's.

"Last chance to turn around," Corvane said. There was no mockery in it. "No one would blame you."

"Yes, they would," Astrid said. "I would." He crouched, brought his face level with Mirabel's, and tapped twice against his own chest — *look at me* — then pointed at the gate, then at her, then laid his palm flat over his heart. *I go in. You come with me. This is why.*

She understood. She understood everything, this child; it was the world that kept failing to understand her. She took his big hand in both of her small ones and squeezed once, hard, and stepped forward with him.

The moment they crossed beneath the arch, the gate woke.

Light moved through the roots like sap, rising from the ground, climbing, gathering in the braided crown until the thorns glowed a deep arterial red. The pale door shivered. And a voice came from everywhere — from the bark, from the stone, from inside Astrid's own skull, vast and sexless and patient as erosion.

***NAME YOURSELVES.***

Corvane stepped forward first. "Corvane," he said, and his voice came out stripped of everything but the word itself. "Once steward of the third ledger. Once called other things. I give what remains: Corvane."

The light pulsed. Something invisible left him — Astrid saw it happen, saw the man's shoulders drop half an inch, saw ten years settle onto his face between one breath and the next. Whatever the gate had taken, it had taken something real.

Then the voice turned to Mirabel, and the whole gate *leaned*, the way a crowd leans toward a stage.

***LITTLE LEDGER. YOUR NAME WAS SOLD. WE HOLD IT.***

Mirabel shook her head, once, sharp. She pointed at herself, then at Astrid, then drew a line in the air between them — and the gate was silent a moment, considering, and Astrid understood that some bargain older than either of them had just been invoked. She was not giving her name. She was *attached* to his. Whatever happened next would happen to both of them or neither.

***THEN THE ONE WHO CLAIMS HER MUST PAY HIS OWN.***

Astrid stepped forward. His legs felt like someone else's legs. "Astrid," he said. "Given name. That's yours, if you want it—"

***THE GIVEN NAME IS DUST TO US. WEIGH WHAT CARRIES WEIGHT.***

And here is the thing Astrid would remember afterward, in whatever life waited on the far side of this: he expected to hesitate. He expected the animal in him to rear up and fight. Instead there was a strange, ringing clarity, the kind he'd only felt twice before — once leaping a crevasse with the ice crumbling behind him, once signing the papers that sold his family's house. The moment after there is no going back is, horribly, the calmest moment there is.

"Astrid Dravenmoor," he said. "Take the second part. Take all of it. Every Dravenmoor who ever carried it, every one who ever will. It's yours. Freely given."

The gate exhaled.

What followed was not pain, exactly — pain has edges, and this had none. It began as a warmth behind his breastbone, a loosening, like a knot slipping. Then it became a pulling, deep and steady, and Astrid felt the name come loose the way a tooth comes loose: rooted in flesh, rocked, torn free with a small wet sound only he could hear. Memories poured out of the socket it left behind — his father teaching him to hold a compass, *steady, boy, the needle finds north but you find the way*; his mother's voice calling the name across a evening yard; the three headstones, the frozen ground, the boy kneeling alone. They drained away like light draining from a lamp, and he grabbed at them and his hands closed on nothing, and the last thing to go was the sound of his own surname spoken aloud in his mother's voice —

and then it was gone, and he was on his knees on cold stone, and he could not remember why he was crying.

Because he was crying. He knew that much. Tears were running down his face and he had no idea what for, and that absence — that smooth, polished hole where something enormous used to be — was worse than any grief he had ever known, because grief at least remembers what it mourns.

Small arms went around his neck. Mirabel, shaking, pressing her face against him, making sounds that were almost words.

"Astrid," Corvane said hoarsely. Just that. Testing whether the word still answered to a man.

"Astrid," Astrid repeated. It fit strangely, like a coat missing a button. "Just... Astrid. Is that right? Was there ever more?"

"There was more." The exile hauled him upright, and his gray face was grim as the mountain itself. "And here's what you need to hear before we go through that door. The gate didn't take your name, cartographer. Gates don't keep anything — they're just mouths. Everything that passes these lips goes down to the court." He nodded at the shimmering door, which had begun, silently, to open onto darkness and the smell of candlesmoke and cold earth.

"They're already collecting," Corvane said. "Everything you pay on the road down, they hold in trust. Which means somewhere beneath us, in a ledger older than kingdoms, there's a fresh entry with your family's name written in it — and a debt marked *unpaid*."

The door swung wide.

Beyond it, stairs descended into the dark, and from far below, faint as a heartbeat, came the sound of many voices reciting names.
