## 7. The Court Beneath

The stair went down longer than any mountain had a right to be deep.

Astrid counted the first thousand steps out of habit — the same instinct that had him measuring every pass and switchback of the Thornfell for twenty years — and then stopped counting, because the numbers began to feel like an insult. The steps were not cut. They were *grown*, each one the knuckled root of some tree that must have stood before the mountains stood, and they curved inward as they descended, spiraling toward a heart he could not see but could feel: a slow, vast pulse in the stone, like the mountain breathing in its sleep.

Candlelight rose to meet them from below. Not torchlight — candlelight, thousands upon thousands of small flames floating in the dark like seeds on a windless pond, and each flame burned without wax, without wick, without smoke.

"Souls," Corvane said quietly, not turning around. "Or the light they give off once the court has finished with the rest of them. Try not to look at any one flame too long."

"Why?"

"Because some of them will look back."

Astrid looked anyway. He was a mapmaker; looking was the whole of his trade. And one flame, drifting near his shoulder, did seem to pause — did seem to lean toward him the way a moth leans toward glass — and in its light he saw, for half a heartbeat, a kitchen. A woman's hands kneading bread. A boy's laughter from another room. His own hands, younger, holding a cup of tea he never got to drink.

He tore his eyes away and gripped the root-stair until his knuckles ached.

Mirabel walked between them with her hand fisted in the back of Astrid's coat, the way she had since the Tollgate of Names took his family name and left a hollow where it used to sit. She could not speak — her voice belonged to the court now, part of the price her mother had paid — but she made a small sound when the flames drifted close, a sound like a kettle about to sing, and Astrid found he understood her better than he had ever understood anyone who talked.

*We are almost there,* she signed against his sleeve, fingers quick as moths. *I remember this dark. I dreamed it every night since I was sold.*

*I know,* he signed back. They had built their language on the ascent, in the long cold camps: a shorthand of taps and shapes borrowed from smugglers' signals and Mirabel's own invention. *I know. But we go in together, and we come out together.*

She pressed her forehead briefly against his arm. Then the stair ended, and the Undercourt opened beneath them like a held breath finally released.

* * *

It was a cathedral. It was a forest. It was both, and neither, and Mirabel's chest ached with the seeing of it.

Roots thicker than houses arched overhead into a vaulted dark, and down those roots ran channels of golden sap that caught the drifting soul-flames and carried them, glowing, into the depths. Between the roots stood shelves — miles of shelves, climbing into darkness — and on the shelves stood candles. No. Not candles.

*People.*

They were shaped like candles because the ledger required them to be shaped like candles: tall, thin, still, their skin the pale gray of tallow, their heads bowed under little crowns of fire. Each one bore a line of script written down the length of their body in ink that moved like something alive, and Mirabel understood, with a child's terrible literalness, that the script *was* them. Their names. Their debts. The weight of what they had been sold for. When a debt was paid, the script faded. When a soul was spent entirely, the flame above its head guttered out, and the body was carried away by gray-faced Collectors to wherever spent things go.

Somewhere in these shelves was a candle with her name on it.

She had known this place before, in the way you know a dream after waking — the shape of it, the smell of hot wax and cold earth — but knowing and standing in it were different animals entirely. Her knees wanted to fold. Her hands wanted to cover her face.

Then Astrid's hand settled on her shoulder, heavy and warm and human, and her knees remembered their work.

He crouched beside her, and his eyes — gray as the Collectors' faces but nothing like them, nothing like them at all — searched hers. *Scared?*

She nodded.

*Good,* he signed. *Brave isn't the absence of scared. Brave is scared with somewhere to be.* He said it lightly, the way he said everything, but she had learned to read the tremor underneath his signs, the way his thumb pressed too hard against his own palm. He was terrified too. He simply refused to let the terror lead.

Corvane moved ahead of them through the aisles like a man walking through a graveyard he had helped dig. The exile kept his hood up and his eyes down, and where his shadow fell across the candle-people, they stirred — just slightly, the way wheat stirs — recognizing, perhaps, what he had once been.

"The ledgers," Corvane murmured. "The living ones are here in the outer courts. Contracts older than memory are deeper in, past the Rootthrone. If the girl's paper survives, it will be here." He stopped before a shelf and drew a breath that shuddered at its edges. "I sold my own sister's debt to this place, three hundred years ago. I have walked these aisles in dreams ever since. I would thank you not to make me walk them longer than we must."

Mirabel touched his sleeve — gently, because grief made people fragile even when they were old and gray and exiled — and pointed down the row.

There. Third shelf, eye height. A candle smaller than the others, its wax-thin shoulders barely grown, its crown of flame burning low and blue.

And down its front, in moving ink, a single line:

*MIRABEL. SOLD FOR ONE WINTER'S BREAD AND A MOTHER'S LIFE. REDEMPTION PERMITTED.*

Her heart slammed. Redemption permitted. There was a way. There was a—

But below that line, in smaller script, the ink went on, and Astrid read it aloud in a voice that came out cracked:

*"—by a soul of greater weight, freely given, freely named, freely surrendered at the hour of the solstice. No coin. No blood. No substitution by force or fraud. The court does not want your gold. The court wants what gold cannot buy."*

The blue flame above the small candle flickered, and for a moment Mirabel saw her mother's kitchen again — saw the bread, the debt, the desperate arithmetic of love — and understood exactly what the court wanted, and exactly what it cost, and turned to look at Astrid with her whole soul in her eyes.

He was already reading the clause again. Already reading it a third time. And she watched, helpless and mute, as awe curdled slowly into horror across his face, the way milk curdles in a storm.

* * *

*A soul of greater weight, freely given.*

Astrid stood in the candlelit aisle and felt the words rearrange everything he had carried down the mountain: the maps, the tunnels, the tollgate tooth-ache where his family name used to live, all of it suddenly revealed as mere approach — the long road to a door that opened only onto a price.

He thought of Vessa's pale face in the archive. *The child is a sold soul.* He thought of the black thorn in his doorframe. He thought of Mirabel's drawings blooming in his map margins, day after day on the ascent: a mother's hands, a loaf of bread, a stick figure with gray eyes holding a smaller figure's hand against the snow.

His memories of everyone he ever loved. That was the weight of a soul. That was what the clause meant, though it dressed itself in legal silk. The court did not deal in abstractions. It dealt in *contents*. And the contents of a man's soul were the people in it.

If he paid, he would walk out of this mountain with Mirabel's hand in his and no idea why his chest ached when he looked at her. No idea why the smell of bread could undo him. No name, no past, no graves to visit, no griefs — and no loves. A man emptied and reissued, like a map with the margins scraped clean.

And if he did not pay, the blue flame above the small candle would gutter out at solstice, and the gray-faced things would carry her away to wherever spent things go, and the last warm thing in his life would be a memory he no longer had.

"There is another way," Corvane said behind him, very softly. "There is always another way. That is what agents say, and I am — whatever I am now. Run. Take her north over the ice fields. The court's writ thins in the far countries. You might buy her years."

"Years," Astrid repeated.

"A collector came early once. One can come early again."

Astrid looked at the small candle. At the moving ink. At Mirabel, whose eyes had not left him, whose small hand had found the hem of his coat again and closed around it with the grip of someone who has already been sold once and knows precisely what being unclaimed means.

The pulse of the mountain beat through the floor, slow and patient. Somewhere above them, the sun crawled toward solstice.

"I need to think," Astrid said, and his voice sounded strange to him, scraped thin, like the Tollgate had taken more than his name. "Is there anywhere in this place a man can stand and not be watched by the dead?"

"No," Corvane said. "That is rather the point of it."

But he led them anyway, deeper past the shelves, to a root-arch where the sap-light pooled gold and the candle-people's whispers faded to almost nothing, and there Astrid sat down on the living wood with Mirabel's contract unrolled across his knees, and began — as he had done his whole life, with passes and rivers and roads no one else dared — to map the way through.

Mirabel knelt beside him. She took the charcoal stub from his pocket, and on the blank margin of the contract itself, in quick sure strokes, she drew a picture: the stick figure with gray eyes, and beside it a second figure, small, and between them a line, and above the line a word she had learned from watching his lips on the ascent, the only word of his language she could shape without a voice.

*Us.*

Astrid stared at it for a long time.

Then, from the dark beyond the root-arch, soft as wax dripping, a voice that was not Corvane's said: "You have read the clause, cartographer. Now let us discuss whether you mean it."
