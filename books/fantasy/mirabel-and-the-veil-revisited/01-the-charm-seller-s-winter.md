## 1. The Charm Seller's Winter

The frost came to Corvane the week before the Feast of Lanterns, and with it came Elodie Underhill's busiest season.

She had learned this in her first winter in the city, when she was nineteen and still foolish enough to believe that luck could be sold honestly. Seven years later she knew better — knew that what she sold was not luck but comfort, not charm but ceremony, and that the difference between the two was the difference between a life and a noose. But the customers did not know it, or did not care to, and so every December her little table appeared in the Shambles market between the tallow-chandler's stall and the woman who sold roasted chestnuts, and every December the people of Corvane came shuffling through the cold to buy small folded papers and knotted cords and river stones painted with symbols that meant nothing at all.

That was the trick of it. That was the whole art, seven years of it: nothing at all.

"Here you are, mistress." Elodie pressed a gray pebble into the fishwife's chapped hand. The stone had been painted that morning with a spiral in cheap red ochre, the kind of mark any child might scrawl on a fence post. "For safe passage over ice. Carry it in your left pocket, and mind you don't tell anyone where you got it."

"And it works?" The fishwife squinted at it, turning it over. Her breath plumed white between them.

"It works as well as anything works," Elodie said, which was the closest to honesty she ever allowed herself in public, and the fishwife laughed, satisfied, and paid her two coppers, and went away believing herself protected against the treachery of frozen streets.

Elodie watched her go and felt the familiar ache settle behind her ribs, the one that never quite left her anymore. Because here was the secret under all the other secrets, the one she kept even from herself on most days: the stone *would* work. Not because of the ochre spiral. Because Elodie had held it that morning in both hands, in the dark of her rented room above the cooper's shop, and had let a thread of warmth pass from her fingers into it — barely a thread, less than a candleflame's worth, so faint that even she could almost pretend she hadn't done it — and the fishwife would cross the ice this winter without falling, and would never know why, and would come back next year for another stone.

Magic outlawed was not magic gone. It was only magic made lonely.

* * *

Corvane in winter was a city of smoke and bells. Smoke rose from ten thousand chimneys and hung in the gray air like a curtain drawn between the city and whatever sky might be above it; bells rang from the King's Spire every hour, and from the smaller chapels at odder hours, and once each day — at noon, precisely — from the tower of the Office of Inquiry, whose bell was iron-black and whose tone made dogs howl and sensible people lower their voices.

Everyone in Corvane knew what the black bell meant. It meant the witch-finders were in session.

Elodie had arrived in the city in spring, seven years ago, with blistered feet and a stolen loaf and nothing else, and she had spent her first week learning the geography of fear the way other newcomers learned the location of the fountains and the guildhalls. She learned which taverns kept their mouths shut and which ones sold gossip to the Inquiry by the pint. She learned that the witch-finders wore gray coats and carried long iron rods tipped with cold-forged silver, and that the rods were said to grow warm near unlicensed power, though she had stood close enough to touch one once, at a checkpoint on the Marsh Bridge, and had felt nothing but her own heart trying to break her ribs. She learned the word they used — *practitioner*, spoken flatly, like a diagnosis — and she learned what happened in the square behind the Office of Inquiry on the first Friday of every month, and after that she made very sure she was always somewhere else on Friday mornings.

Her gift was small. That was what she told herself, in the early years, huddled under blankets in rooms she could barely afford. A hedge-witch's handful of tricks: warmth coaxed into cold things, small mending, the quieting of fevers, luck turned gentle in a pocket. Mistress Ivenna had called it *hearthcraft*, with something like contempt, back when Elodie had been her apprentice and had thought the old woman's scorn was just an old woman's way. "You'll never be more than a kitchen-charmer, girl," Ivenna had said. "Your power is the kind that keeps bread from burning." And Elodie had believed her, right up until the night everything burned anyway, and she had run, and had kept running for seven years, and had taught herself in those seven years that a small gift was not a safe gift. Small gifts got people hanged too. The Inquiry did not grade its gallows.

So she hid it. She hid it the way a lame man hides a limp, constantly, unconsciously, with a thousand small corrections performed every hour of every day. When the fever took the cooper's daughter last winter, Elodie sat with the child through three terrible nights and did *nothing* — held her hand, wiped her brow, changed the cloths, and let the fever burn however it pleased, because laying hands on a sick child and cooling its blood with a thought was the kind of thing neighbors remembered, and remembered wrong, and whispered about. The girl lived anyway. Elodie told herself the medicine-woman's tonics had done it, and mostly believed it, and did not sleep properly for a week.

When the bread in her oven threatened to catch, she pulled it out early like any ordinary woman. When her joints ached with cold, she rubbed them with goose fat like any ordinary woman. When her luck ran bad — and it ran bad for everyone, that was the whole point of luck — she endured it like any ordinary woman, and bought a painted stone from some other seller at another market, and smiled at the joke of it, alone, in the dark, where nobody could see her smile.

Careful mediocrity. That was the phrase she'd coined for it, years ago, and it had become a kind of prayer. Be forgettable. Be dull. Be the charm-seller whose charms are obviously nothing, whose voice is pleasant but not memorable, whose face is the kind faces slide off of. She had practiced dullness until it fit her like a well-worn glove, and if sometimes, late at night, she caught her reflection in a window and saw a stranger looking back — a thin, careful woman of twenty-six with ash-brown hair and eyes that gave away nothing — she reminded herself that the stranger was the price, and the price was fair.

Most days she believed it.

* * *

The Feast of Lanterns fell on the year's longest night, and the week before it, the Shambles filled up like a cup. Elodie's little table did brisk trade: knots for safe childbirth, stones for honest dealings, folded paper stars that young men bought to give to young women and pretended were jokes. She kept her prices low and her patter bland and her eyes moving, always moving, the way a deer's eyes move in a clearing.

It was the chestnut-seller who warned her, on the fourth day of the feast-week.

"Marek's boy came through an hour ago," the old woman murmured, not looking up from her brazier. She spoke around the motion of turning chestnuts, lips barely moving, a skill Elodie recognized and admired. "Gray coats. Six of them, working up from Tanner's Row. Rods out."

Elodie's hands did not stop folding the paper star she was holding. This was part of the discipline too: fear happened in the body, but the body could be trained. "Market day," she said lightly. "They do love their market days."

"They're not collecting tolls, girl." The chestnut-seller's name was Bess; she had sold chestnuts in the Shambles for thirty years and had survived thirty years of witch-finders by knowing exactly when to look bored. "Tanner's Row is where the dyer lives. The one whose wife had the fits last month."

Elodie remembered the dyer's wife. Everyone on Tanner's Row remembered the dyer's wife, who had screamed things in her sleep in a language nobody knew, and whose husband had wept in the street and sworn she'd always been delicate. Elodie also remembered, with a coldness spreading through her middle, that she had sold the dyer's wife a knot in October. A simple knot, three loops, for restful sleep. Painted cord, no craft in it at all — she had been *careful* — but the dyer's wife had slept soundly ever after, and sleeping soundly after screaming nights was exactly the sort of improvement that got noticed, and noticed wrongly, in a city where the black bell rang at noon.

"I think I'll take my lunch early," Elodie said.

Bess snorted. "Take your table down while you're at it. They don't like sellers of curiosities. You know that."

She did know that. Curiosity-sellers occupied an uncomfortable middle ground in the Inquiry's taxonomy of sin — too respectable to arrest on sight, too suspicious to leave alone. Every raid swept them up for questioning, and questioning was its own punishment: the cold room, the questions asked slowly, the rods passed over your hands while a man with a ledger watched your face for the flicker that meant guilt. Elodie had been questioned twice in seven years. Both times she had been released by evening, having proven herself exactly as fraudulent as she appeared. Both times she had gone home and been quietly, thoroughly sick, and had lain awake listening to the bells, wondering how many more times her luck could hold.

But today there was the dyer's wife, and a knot with her fingerprints — figuratively, only figuratively, she had never touched the woman — and six gray coats working up from Tanner's Row.

She packed the table. It took four minutes; she had drilled it. The painted stones went into the basket beneath the false bottom, the cords into the flour sack, the paper stars into her coat lining, and the plain goods — the candles, the dried lavender, the honest honey — stayed out on top, because a woman fleeing her stall looked guilty and a woman carrying home unsold candles merely looked thrifty. She nodded to Bess, who did not nod back, because Bess understood that even a nod was a thread that could be followed, and walked north through the Shambles at the pace of a woman with errands.

She was halfway to the cooper's shop when the bells began.

Not the noon bell — it was barely mid-morning. These were the chapel bells, ringing fast and arrhythmic, the alarm-pattern that every child in Corvane learned alongside their letters. Down the length of Cordwainer Street, doors began to shut. Shutters clacked. Somewhere a mother called a child indoors with a sharpness that cut through the ringing.

Elodie stepped into the shadow of a doorway and made herself small, and watched the gray coats come.

Six of them, as Bess had said, walking in loose formation up the center of the street with the unhurried confidence of men who had never once needed to hurry. Their coats were the gray of old ash, their boots were polished, and their rods were out — long staves of dark iron capped with dull silver, held level, sweeping gently left and right as they walked, the way a man sweeps a reaping-hook through standing grain. At their head walked a woman Elodie didn't know, tall and narrow-faced, with a ledger chained to her belt and eyes the color of dishwater that moved across the street's faces the way her men's rods moved across its doorways.

The rods were warm. Elodie couldn't see it, couldn't feel it from forty paces, but she knew it the way she knew her own pulse, because she had spent seven years learning everything there was to know about the instruments of her destruction. Cold-forged silver warmed near unlicensed power. And somewhere behind her, back down the street, in the direction of the Shambles, in the direction of her abandoned stall and her basket with its false bottom and its basketful of stones she had warmed that morning with a thread of herself —

One of the rods dipped. Just slightly. Just enough.

The woman with the ledger stopped. Said something Elodie couldn't hear. One of the gray coats peeled away from the formation and began walking toward the mouth of the Shambles, rod level, unhurried, and Elodie understood with perfect clarity that he was walking toward her stall, toward her basket, toward the false bottom that had fooled customs men and thieves and one very persistent tax assessor, and that painted stones warmed by hedge-craft held their warmth for days, and that she had made them only that morning.

She walked. Not ran — ran, and she'd be stopped inside a hundred paces, and running women got questions, and questions got rods, and rods got fires. She walked, fast, taking the first turning, then the second, then a third that doubled back through the tanners' yards where the stink covered everything including fear, and behind her the alarm bells kept ringing and somewhere a woman was being taken from a house on Tanner's Row, and ahead of her lay the cooper's shop and the stairs and the room where she would sit with her back against the door and count her breaths until her hands stopped shaking.

She made it. Of course she made it. She always made it. That was the whole architecture of her life: the flight, the shaking, the counting, the morning after, the table set up again in a different market under a slightly different name. Seven years of it. She was good at it now, and being good at it was the loneliest thing she knew.

* * *

The room above the cooper's shop was cold, and she did not warm it.

That was the discipline within the discipline: never use the gift at home. Never warm the water, never mend the cracked window-glass, never coax the fire. Home was where habits showed. So she lit the fire with flint and steel like any ordinary woman, and sat close to it like any ordinary woman, and shivered like any ordinary woman while the flames took their slow time, and ate bread and hard cheese, and listened to the city outside.

The bells had stopped. In their place came the other sounds, the ones that followed: cart wheels, many carts, rolling toward the square behind the Office of Inquiry. Voices in the street below, hushed, relishing. Old Marta the cooper's wife calling up the stairs, "You hear? They've taken the dyer's wife! Fits, they say. Speaking backwards in her sleep!" — and Elodie opening her door a crack and saying, "How dreadful," in a voice as bland as porridge, and closing it again, and sitting back down by the fire with her supper going cold in her lap.

The dyer's wife. Whose fits had stopped in October. Whose sleep had gone sweet and dreamless after buying a three-looped knot from a charm-seller in the Shambles.

They wouldn't find anything. That was the horror of it, the thing Elodie had to make herself look at directly, the way you look at a wound to see how deep it goes: they wouldn't find anything, because there was nothing to find, because Elodie had been *careful*. The knot was painted cord. The stones were stones. Whatever the dyer's wife had, it wasn't Elodie's doing — couldn't be — and yet the Inquiry did not need proof, not really, not when suspicion would serve. They would question the charm-sellers of the Shambles. There were four of them. Elodie was the one whose stall had been nearest, the one who'd packed up early, the one Bess had spoken to, the one half the market had seen hurrying away with a basket when the bells began.

By tomorrow, someone would remember that. By tomorrow, the woman with the dishwater eyes would have a name: Elodie Underhill, charm-seller, no family local, no guild, no history in the city beyond seven years of perfect, suspicious blankness.

Seven years of careful mediocrity, and it had taken one frightened woman's peaceful sleep to undo it.

Elodie sat by the fire until the light died, and then sat longer, in the dark, because lighting a candle felt like a decision and she wasn't ready to decide anything. Through the wall she could hear Marta and the cooper talking, their voices rising and falling, comfortable, married, ordinary. Down in the street a drunk sang something about the Feast of Lanterns. The city went on being a city, indifferent, enormous, full of ten thousand lives none of which were hers.

*I could leave tonight,* she thought. It was the old thought, the faithful thought, the one that visited every time the gray coats came near. Pack the basket, slip out the south gate before dawn, be twenty miles gone by noon, start again in some other town under some other name. She was good at leaving. It was the one inheritance her seven years had given her that she could rely on.

But she was tired. Gods, she was tired. Tired in a way that sleep didn't touch — tired of the false bottoms and the bland voice and the flint and steel and the fevers she let burn, tired of being a stranger in her own reflection, tired of a life that was not a life but a hiding place shaped like one. She was twenty-six years old and she had been running since she was nineteen, and somewhere in those seven years the running had stopped being something she did and become something she was, and she could no longer remember the shape of the girl she'd been before, the one who had thought hedge-magic was a heritage instead of a death sentence, the one who had loved an old woman who betrayed her, the one whose hands used to glow.

The fire burned down to embers. The embers glowed, orange and gold, and Elodie stared into them, and the ache behind her ribs spread outward until her whole chest was full of it.

And then — because she was alone, and because no one could see, and because she wanted, just once, just for a moment, to stop pretending — she reached out and touched the dying fire with her gift.

Just a thread. Barely a breath of warmth, poured into the embers the way you'd breathe on coals, and the fire leapt up bright and clean and golden, throwing light across the little room, and for three heartbeats — three heartbeats, no more — Elodie Underhill sat in a pool of warmth and light that she had made, that was *hers*, that no law and no ledger and no gray-coated man with a silver-tipped rod could unmake, and she remembered who she was.

Then she closed her hand, and the fire sank back to embers, and the dark came in again like a tide.

She was still sitting there, in the dark, listening to the embers tick as they cooled, when someone knocked on the door below.

Three knocks. Slow. Heavy. The knock of a man leaning his weight against a doorframe.

Marta's footsteps crossed the ground floor; the bar scraped; the door opened a hand's width on its chain. Cold air poured up the stairwell, and with it came a smell that did not belong to Corvane in winter — green things, river water, something wild and far away — and a voice, slurred with pain, saying words that made Marta cry out and made Elodie rise slowly to her feet with her heart hammering like a fist:

"—need the charm-seller. Tell her—" A wet, ragged breath. "Tell her *Mirabel*."
