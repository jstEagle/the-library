## 2. A Stranger at the Door

The knock came at the hour when Corvane's bells had long since gone to sleep and even the watchmen had stopped pretending to be awake.

Elodie heard it first as a dream — a soft, arrhythmic tapping that wove itself into her sleep like a thread pulled loose from a hem. Then she was awake, and it was real, and her heart was already hammering before she understood why. Seven years in this city had taught her body things her mind refused to learn: that certain sounds meant certain deaths. A knock after midnight was one of them.

She lay still beneath her blanket and listened. The tap came again — weaker now, dragging, as though the hand behind it could barely lift itself. Not the knock of the witch-finders, then. They did not knock weakly. They knocked like men who owned the door and everything behind it, three blows with an iron-shod staff, and by the third blow they were already coming through.

This was something else. Something hurt.

*Don't answer it.* The voice in her head was old and worn smooth, the voice of Mistress Ivenna's kitchen, of seven years of careful mediocrity, of every small survival she had stitched together in the crooked house on Tanner's Lane. *You are a charm-seller who sells nothing but brass and superstition. You are nobody. Nobodies do not open doors at midnight.*

But her feet were already on the cold floorboards, and her hand was already on the latch, because there was a second sound beneath the knocking now — a wet, labored breathing, like bellows full of water — and whatever else Elodie Underhill had become in seven years of hiding, she had never once managed to become the kind of person who let someone die on her step.

She opened the door a hand's width. Cold air poured in, smelling of river mud and coal smoke and the particular metallic tang that had hung over Corvane all week, ever since the witch-finders' wagons had come rattling through the Saltgate.

He fell against the door before she could stop him.

He was tall — or he had been, once; he seemed to be folding inward, collapsing into himself like a tent with its poles cut — and he was drenched in blood. Not his own, or not only his own. It had dried black along his sleeves and fresh and bright across his chest, where three wounds wept through the torn wool of his coat, neat and deliberate, the kind of wounds made by someone who knew exactly where to put a knife. His face was gray as tallow. His eyes, when they found hers, were startlingly clear.

"You're the one," he said.

"I'm not anything," Elodie whispered, which was the truest sentence she owned. "Go away. Find a physicker. There's one on Cordwainer Street, past the shrine, the door with the blue—"

"There's no time." He said it without drama, without pleading, the way a man says *it's raining*. He said it the way people say things when they have already done all their arguing with themselves and lost. "They're burning the bridges behind me. Listen to me. I need you to take it."

"Take what? I don't know you. I don't want—"

His hand shot out and caught her wrist, and his grip was astonishing, furnace-hot, the grip of a drowning man who has decided you are driftwood. In his other hand he held something folded — a square of some material that was neither parchment nor cloth, heavy and strange, wrapped in oilcloth gone soft with age.

"Misabel—" he began, and coughed, and the cough brought blood up into his mouth, and he spat it onto her doorstep with the indifference of a man past caring about messes. He tried again. "*Mirabel.*"

The word went through Elodie like a needle finding an old scar.

She had not heard that word spoken aloud in seven years. She had barely allowed herself to think it. It belonged to the part of her life she had buried so deep she sometimes convinced herself it had never happened — the hedge-witch's cottage, the lessons in the dark, the word Ivenna had used only twice, in whispers, with the shutters barred, as if even the name might be overheard by something listening from the other side of the world.

Mirabel. The realm behind the Veil. The place that did not exist, officially, except in heresy trials and old wives' warnings and the kind of songs that got their singers questioned.

"How do you know that word?" she breathed.

But he wasn't answering. He was pressing the folded thing into her hands, folding her fingers around it with both of his bloody ones, and his clarity was going out like a lamp in wind. "Bearer," he murmured. "It chose. I only carried. It chose—" And then his legs simply stopped being legs, and Elodie, ninety pounds of her, took the whole weight of a dying man against her chest and went down with him onto her own threshold, the package trapped between them, his blood soaking instantly through her nightgown, warm as bathwater.

"No," she said stupidly. "No, no — get up, get up, you can't die here, you cannot die *here*—"

Because here was the stairwell of the crooked house on Tanner's Lane, six steps up from a shared courtyard where four families hung their washing, and Mrs. Pell across the way rose before dawn to salt her fish, and if anyone found a dead man bleeding out on Elodie Underhill's step, the questions would begin, and the questions would not stop at *who is this man*. The questions would go where questions about Elodie always wanted to go. To the charms that were too often right. To the way candles behaved around her when she forgot herself. To the rumor, two winters old now, that she'd been seen talking to a cat as though it answered.

To the thing under the floorboard beneath her bed, wrapped in waxed linen, that she told herself every morning was nothing, nothing, nothing.

The man shuddered once, hugely, like a horse refusing a fence. His eyes found hers again and held them, and in them Elodie saw something that frightened her more than the blood did: not fear. Relief. The bone-deep relief of a courier who has finally, *finally*, gotten the parcel out of his hands.

"It knows you," he whispered. Blood threaded from the corner of his mouth. "I saw it... wake up. When I passed the shrine. It doesn't do that. In thirty years it's never..." His voice frayed to a thread. "Burn it, if you're wise. Bury it, if you're braver than me. But don't let the gray coats take it. Whatever else happens. Don't let them take it."

Then the light in his eyes guttered, steadied, guttered again — and went out between one breath and the next, the way candle flames go, all at once and completely, leaving Elodie kneeling in her doorway holding a dead stranger and a mystery while the cold rain began to fall softly on them both.

* * *

She did not scream. That surprised her later. She sat in the doorway for a long time — she would never know how long, an hour, perhaps more, until the rain had soaked her through and her teeth were chattering too hard to stop — with the man's head in her lap and her mind moving in tight, useless circles like a fly against glass.

*Wake Mrs. Pell. Wake the watch. No. The watch asks questions. The physicker on Cordwainer Street — he'll ask questions. Leave him. Run. Run now, take nothing, run the way you ran from Ivenna's, run the way you've always run—*

But running had a cost, and she had paid it once already, and she was tired, bone-tired, in a way that seven years had not cured but only compounded, the way debts compound. She had a stall in the Lamplight Market. She had a regulars' row — old Marta who bought the fertility knots, the cooper's boy who swore her luck-brass kept his father sober, half the fishwives of Salt Row who called her *the quiet one* and trusted her precisely because she gave them nothing real. She had a kettle. She had a chair by the window where the light came through in the afternoon. She had built a life out of small dull safe things, stone by stone, for seven years, and every stone of it was built on the single foundation of *no one looks twice at Elodie Underhill.*

A dead man on her step would bring every eye in Corvane down on her like a dropped roof.

So she did the thing she was worst at, the thing hiding had starved in her: she decided.

It took three attempts to drag him inside. He was heavier dead than alive, the way bodies are, and she was not strong, and the stairs were narrow, and twice she had to stop and press her forehead against the wall and breathe through the panic until it loosened enough to let her move. She got him up the six steps, through the door, across the little room with its market shelves and its banked fire, and into the back chamber where she stored her stock-in-trade. The floor there was flagstone. Flagstones, she reasoned with a ghastly calm, could be lifted. She had lifted one before, seven years ago, the night she'd buried the box.

She lit every candle she owned. Not for light — for courage. Fire had always been the one honest thing in her life, the element that answered her a little too readily, and she needed its company tonight.

While she worked — rolling him onto the sacking she used for market stalls, scrubbing the threshold with lye water and then with sand, hauling the bloody sacking toward the hearth — the folded thing sat on her table where she had put it, deliberately, at arm's length, the way one puts down a snake.

Oilcloth, tied with cord gone brittle. Inside, she could feel layers. Something dense. Something that, when she brushed it with her fingertips in passing, felt — warm. Warmer than the room. Warmer than it should have been, sitting alone on cold oak.

She did not unwrap it. She was proud of that, later, and ashamed of it too, because the truth was she didn't unwrap it for the same reason she hadn't thrown it in the river: she was afraid of it, and she was afraid *of being afraid of it*, and both fears together pinned her like a specimen to a board.

Near dawn, the fire had taken the sacking and most of the blood-soaked bedding, and the smell was terrible, and the man lay on her floor wrapped in a second layer of sacking, and Elodie stood over him with her sleeves rolled and her hands raw from lye and realized she was going to have to bury him in her own cellar, like a murderess in a ballad, and that the strangest part was how calm she felt. Calm the way the sea is calm over a wreck.

"I'm sorry," she told him. It seemed important to say it aloud. "I don't even know your name."

He didn't answer, obviously. But his coat pocket, stiff with dried blood, gaped slightly open, and in it she could see the corner of a letter — paper, real paper, covered in a fine cramped hand — and a locket on a broken chain.

She should not have looked. She looked.

The letter was unfinished, dated nine days past, addressed to *S.V.* and signed only with a single initial, *E.V.*, and the visible line read: *If I do not reach the bearer in time, tell her the map is not a treasure, it is a wound, and wounds left untended—*

The rest was soaked dark with blood.

The locket held a miniature portrait, painted small and lovingly: a girl of perhaps fifteen, dark-haired, sharp-chinned, furious-eyed even in paint, as if the artist had caught her mid-argument and she had won it.

Elodie closed the locket and put it back in the dead man's pocket with hands that were not quite steady, because the portrait's eyes had been green — a very particular green, the green of new beech leaves — and she had seen eyes exactly that color once before, long ago, in a face across a cottage table, in the days before everything.

Coincidence, she told herself. The world was full of green eyes.

Her own reflection in the window said nothing, but it looked unconvinced.

* * *

By the time the sky went from ink to slate to the bruised pearl of a Corvane dawn, the job was done, and done better than she'd believed herself capable of. The flagstone in the back room sat flush and innocent over its terrible cargo, dusted with spilled flour from her market sacks. The blood was scrubbed or burned. The man's boots and coat — evidence, weight, trouble — she broke apart and fed to the fire over several hours, feeding it slowly so the flames never flared high enough to show above the chimney pot. The letter and the locket she could not burn. She told herself it was because burning them might destroy something someone needed — a daughter, maybe, a *S.V.* who deserved to know how her father died — and that was true, but underneath the truth sat another one, quieter: she could not burn them for the same reason she could not throw away the folded thing. They had *chosen* her, somehow, all of it, the way the falling beam chooses where to land.

She washed until her skin was pink and stinging. She dressed in her market clothes, the drab brown dress, the plain scarf. She opened her shutters on the gray morning and watched Tanner's Lane come alive — Mrs. Pell salting fish, the cooper's boy hauling staves, the milk cart grinding past — and everything was exactly as it had always been, and everything was different, because Elodie stood at her window holding a secret the size of a corpse, and below her the city went about its business, unaware, and would remain unaware right up until the moment it wasn't.

The folded thing still sat on the table. All night it had sat there, and here is what she had not let herself notice until the daylight made denial impossible:

In the dark, it had been faintly, steadily warm. Now, in the gray morning light, it was warmer still. And the oilcloth wrapping — she was almost sure, though she had not touched it in hours — had shifted. Loosened. As if something inside had been turning over in its sleep.

And beneath her breastbone, in the place where she kept the thing she had buried seven years ago — the small outlawed warmth she starved daily with mediocrity and fear — something stirred. Not painfully. Worse than painfully: *gladly*. Like a dog hearing its name after years of silence.

"No," Elodie said aloud, to the package, to the warmth, to the dead man under her floor, to the whole rotten arrangement of fate. "Whatever you are. Whoever you think I am. No. I'm a charm-seller. I sell brass and superstition. I'm nobody."

The package sat on the table in the strengthening light.

And very slowly, with a sound like a sigh, like paper breathing, it began — without any hand near it — to unfold.
