## 4. Witch-Finders at the Market

The witch-finders came back on a Thursday, which Elodie would remember afterward as the cruelest detail of all — because Thursdays were market days, and she had always loved market days.

She was at her stall before dawn, as she had been every morning for six years, arranging her wares with the practiced carelessness of a woman who wanted to seem slightly duller than she was. Dried lavender for sleep. Iron nails bundled in red thread — iron was safe, iron was *church-approved*, the priests said it kept spirits out rather than calling them in. Little knotted cords sold as "luck" that were, in truth, nothing but wool and a whispered blessing so faint even Elodie sometimes wondered whether she'd imagined putting anything into them at all.

That was the trick of it. That had always been the trick of it. Be small. Be forgettable. Sell just enough charm to keep customers coming and not one whisper more. Seven years of careful mediocrity, worn smooth as a river stone, and some mornings she almost believed she *was* the stone — that there was nothing underneath but more stone, all the way down.

Then the map sat folded in its hiding place beneath her floorboards, humming to itself like a kettle about to boil, and she could no longer pretend the stone was all there was.

*Stop thinking about it,* she told herself, and arranged another bundle of lavender with hands that did not quite tremble.

The morning was cold enough to see breath. Corvane's market square filled slowly around her: fishwives shouting over the price of herring, a tinker with his cart of dented pots, the baker's boy hauling trays of rolls whose smell made Elodie's stomach clench with a hunger she couldn't afford to indulge until sales picked up after noon. Old Marta from the chandlery waved at her across the square. The cobbler's apprentice, Piet, lingered at her stall longer than he needed to buy a luck-knot, the way he did most weeks, and Elodie gave him her best smile — warm, but not too warm; friendly, but not promising — and watched him go with something that might have been fondness if she'd let it become anything.

This was the life she'd built. Stall and stairwell, neighbors and routines, a name in the city rolls — *Elodie Marsh*, charm-seller, licensed, taxed, unremarkable. Six years to build it. She had told herself, in the dark hours after the stranger died in her stairwell, that she would not have to leave it. That bodies stayed buried, maps stayed hidden, and cities forgot faster than they remembered.

She should have known better. Cities never forgot. They only waited.

* * *

They came at midmorning, when the square was at its fullest, which Elodie understood was deliberate. Witch-finders liked audiences. Fear worked better in crowds.

There were four of them this time, not two. They entered from the cathedral side in their gray-and-white livery, moving through the market like a blade through dough, and the noise of the square changed around them — not silence, exactly, but a new texture to the noise, a hush woven through the haggling, the way water goes still before it freezes.

Elodie knew the tall one. He'd led the raid on her street three nights ago, the one that had nearly ended everything. His name, according to the gossip that traveled faster than any official notice, was Under-Deacon Ferren, and he had a face like a well-used knife handle and eyes that never seemed to blink quite when other people's did. He carried the instruments of his trade in a long case slung across his back, and everyone in the square pretended very hard not to look at it.

He stopped three stalls down and began asking questions.

Elodie went back to her lavender. Her hands were steady. She made them be steady, the way she made everything in her life be what it needed to be, and she listened with the whole surface of her skin while appearing to listen with none of it.

"—seen him before, you say? When?"

"Twice," said the voice of the tinker, Joss, who was honest to the point of stupidity. "Bought a pot off me a fortnight past. Said he was traveling. Foreign sort of accent."

"What kind of foreign?"

"I couldn't say, m'lord. Northern, maybe? Or over the sea. He paid in silver, good weight."

"And where did he lodge?"

A shrug Elodie could hear. "No idea. Traveling folk don't always lodge proper."

Ferren moved on. Elodie counted his footsteps by the creak of his boot leather, stall to stall, question to question, and with each answer the shape of the net drew tighter around the square, around her street, around her stairwell where a dead man's blood had soaked into boards she'd scrubbed until her knuckles bled and still hadn't gotten entirely clean.

*Had anyone seen where he went that night? Had anyone heard anything? A cry, a fall, a door?*

Old Marta, bless her and damn her, had heard something. Elodie heard her say so.

"Funny noise, night before last. Like someone falling down stairs. I thought it was the Vances' boy again, drunk, but his mother says he wasn't out." A pause. "That was on Lantern Street, wasn't it? Where the charm-woman lives?"

The word *charm-woman* landed in Elodie's chest like a dropped stone.

*Not me. Not my name. Just the woman who sells charms, one of four on this street alone—*

But Ferren had already turned, and she felt his attention come down the square toward her stall like weather changing, like pressure before a storm breaks, and there was nowhere in Corvane to hide from being noticed except inside the crowd of the unnoticed, and Marta had just pointed her out of it.

"Charm-seller," Ferren said, arriving at her stall. Up close he smelled of lamp oil and something medicinal, camphor maybe, or whatever they used to preserve the things in his case. "You're the one on Lantern Street. The stairwell."

"I have a stall on Lantern Street," Elodie said. Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of it. "I live above it. Everyone on the street knows me."

"Everyone on the street is precisely who I've come to talk to." He didn't smile. She suspected he'd forgotten how, or found it unnecessary, the way a hammer finds smiling unnecessary. "You heard a man die on your stairs two nights ago."

The square had gone quiet around them now, properly quiet, forty faces pretending to work while listening with every fiber they owned. Elodie felt their attention on her back like sunlight through a magnifying lens.

"No," she said.

"You didn't hear a man die on your stairs."

"There was no man on my stairs." Which was true, now. There was no man anywhere anymore, and that was the whole problem. "If someone fell, I slept through it. I sleep heavy. Always have. You can ask—" *Ask whom? Ask what?* "—anyone."

"I intend to." Ferren's gaze moved over her stall with terrible patience. Lavender. Nails. Knots. His eyes snagged on the luck-cords, and Elodie's heart performed a slow, cold revolution. "You sell charms."

"I sell *wool*," Elodie said. "Knotted wool. It's a country custom. Farmers hang them on their gates. There's no law against knots."

"There are laws against *effect*," Ferren said softly. "Knots that hold wind. Cords that stop blood. Charms that turn aside ill-luck. The Synod has ruled on such things for two hundred years, mistress, and the ruling is that they are either fraud or sorcery, and sorcery is death." He picked up one of the cords between two fingers, turned it, set it down. "Which are you? Fraud or sorcery?"

"Fraud," Elodie said, and hated how quickly it came, how easily she wore it, seven years of practice fitting her like a second skin. "Fraud, under-deacon. Wool and hope. If I could actually work charms, do you think I'd be selling them for two coppers apiece in a winter market?"

Something flickered behind his unblinking eyes. Not belief. Interest. The interest of a man cataloguing.

"That," he said, "is exactly what a sorceress would say."

He bought a luck-knot. He paid with a copper and made her give change, counting it out slowly while the whole square watched, and then he thanked her with a little bow that was somehow worse than a threat, and moved on to the chandlery, and Elodie stood behind her stall with her heart slamming against her ribs and the taste of copper in her mouth, and understood, with the flat clarity that comes after fear instead of during it:

It was over. Whatever happened next, however many questions she answered correctly, it was over. He'd marked her. Witch-finders marked the way wolves marked, and they did not unmark.

* * *

She lasted until the square began to empty at dusk. She took down her stall, packed her unsold lavender, counted her coppers twice because her hands wouldn't behave, and walked home through streets gone blue with early dark, taking the long way, checking reflections in shop glass the way she hadn't since her first year in Corvane. No gray livery following. Yet.

Her rooms above the stall smelled of dried herbs and lamp oil and, faintly, underneath everything, of the map.

She lit no lamp. She stood in the dark listening to the building settle, and then she knelt and pried up the third floorboard from the wall — the loose one, the one that had taken her a month to loosen convincingly so it would look like rot rather than concealment — and drew out the oilcloth bundle.

The map was warm. It was always warm now, ever since the night it had unfolded itself in her hands and poured seven years of buried magic back into her blood like a dam breaking. She hadn't touched it since. She'd wrapped it, buried it, told herself that whatever it was, it could stay down there in the dark where outlawed things belonged.

But she could feel it through the oilcloth, faint as a heartbeat through a coat, and tonight — tonight she unwrapped it, because she needed to know what she was running with.

In the light of the single candle she finally allowed herself, the map lay on her table and did nothing. It looked like a map. Inked coastlines, mountain ranges drawn in fine crosshatch, rivers branching like veins. And threaded through all of it, visible only when the candle flame swayed, a second layer of markings that no cartographer had ever put on paper: lines of silver-gray that moved. Slowly. Like frost growing across a window. One line ran along the eastern edge of the chart, following the mountains, and where it passed, the ink beneath it darkened, and Elodie's skin prickled with the old familiar wrongness she'd spent her whole adult life smothering.

The Veil. Tam Vess — the mad cartographer in the stocks, the heretic whose pamphlets she'd once read for a laugh — had written about a Veil. A boundary. A wall between the world of men and the world that men had agreed, long ago, to pretend wasn't there.

And the map was a picture of it. Living. Breathing. Failing, if the thinning patches along its length meant what she feared they meant.

"Don't," she said aloud to it, to the room, to herself. "Don't ask me. Find someone else. I'm nobody. I'm a fraud selling wool, I told him so myself, I *told him*—"

The silver line pulsed, once, gently, like a hand squeezing hers.

Elodie sat down hard on her stool and put her face in her hands.

* * *

She packed by candlelight, and the packing was the worst part, worse than the lying, worse than Ferren's unblinking eyes. Because packing meant choosing, and choosing meant admitting, and admitting meant grieving, and she had done this before — gods, she had done this before, at seventeen, with a bundle of clothes and a stolen amulet and Mistress Ivenna's voice ringing in her ears — and she had sworn, walking out of that village with frostbite in her fingers and her whole life burning behind her, that she would never again own more than she could abandon in ten minutes.

She'd broken the oath. That was the thing. Somewhere in six years of Thursdays and regular customers and Piet's shy lingering at her stall and Marta bringing her honey cakes at Winterturn, she had broken it. She'd let herself have a *life*. Heavy things. Rooted things. A copper kettle that poured true. A quilt she'd traded three months of charm-sales for. A chair that fit her back.

She looked at the chair and had to sit down on the bed and breathe for a while.

Ten minutes. Everything she could carry in ten minutes. Clothes, rolled tight. The money-box — thirty-one crowns and a handful of silver, six years of scraping, and it would buy her perhaps a month of running. Her mother's ring, which she'd never worn and never thrown away. The knife. The map.

She held the quilt for a long moment, feeling its weight, its warmth, the specific dumb animal comfort of a thing made by human hands for no purpose but kindness. Then she folded it over the chair-back neatly, the way you'd fold a flag over a coffin, and left it.

Downstairs, through the floor, she heard the Vance boy come home drunk and singing, heard his mother scold him with love underneath the anger, heard the ordinary music of a street going to sleep, and thought: *this is what it costs. Not the danger. This. The leaving.*

She'd been so tired of running. That was the joke of it, the bitter punchline seven years in the making. She had built an entire self out of staying — out of the same stall, the same room, the same neighbors, the same small honest fraud of a life — and she had almost convinced herself that staying was who she was, that Elodie Marsh was real and permanent and *safe*, and the girl who fled her mistress in the snow was someone else, someone dead.

But the girl who fled had only been waiting. She'd felt her all week, standing behind Elodie's eyes, patient as the map, holding the bundle she'd carried at seventeen.

Elodie snuffed the candle. In the dark, the map's silver lines glowed faintly, patiently, and she thought she could feel it waiting too.

"One condition," she whispered to it, because talking to maps was now apparently her life. "We run north. Away from the border. Away from wherever you want to go. I find someone who can read you, someone who can tell me how to make you stop, and then I put you down. I swear it. I didn't choose you and I don't want you, and if there's a way to give you back, I'll find it."

The map said nothing, in the way of maps. But the silver line along the eastern mountains brightened, briefly, like a road catching the last of the sun, and Elodie chose — firmly, deliberately — not to interpret that as an answer.

She shouldered her bundle, left the rent in the dish by the door with a note saying she'd gone to her sister's in the country (she had no sister; the lie cost her nothing anymore; lies were the one inheritance she'd kept from her old life), and slipped out into the frozen dark of Lantern Street.

Behind her, the quilt sat folded on the chair like a grave marker for the woman who'd lived here until Thursday.

Ahead of her, three streets over and closing fast, lantern-light bobbed in a pattern she recognized with sick familiarity: a patrol, moving with purpose, toward the market district. Toward her street. Ferren had said he intended to ask everyone, and witch-finders asked at night, when doors opened to fear faster than to daylight.

Elodie pulled her hood up and turned the other way, toward the river, toward the gates, toward the one place in Corvane where a disgraced heretic cartographer sat in the stocks spitting curses at passersby — the only soul in this city who might know what the warm, patient, glowing thing against her spine actually was.

Running again. Ten minutes' warning, same as always.

At least, she thought grimly, setting her feet against the ice-slick cobbles, this time she was leaving before they knocked.
