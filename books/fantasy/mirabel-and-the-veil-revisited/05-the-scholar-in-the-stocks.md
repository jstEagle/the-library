## 5. The Scholar in the Stocks

Tam Vess had learned, over four months of imprisonment, that the worst part was not the cold. It was not the damp that crept into his joints like a debt collector, nor the bread that could have doubled as roof-slate, nor even the rats — though the rats were bold enough to have opinions about him and one had recently begun nesting in his spare boot.

The worst part was being right with no one left to tell.

He sat on the stone shelf that served as bed and chair and everything else in cell nine of Corvane's Chancery Prison, and he drew maps in his head because they would not let him draw them anywhere else. He drew the coastline of Mirabel as he believed it truly lay — not where the Crown's sanctioned charts insisted it must be, behind a wall of fog at the world's edge, but folded close against the human kingdoms like a second skin. He drew the Veil itself: not a line but a membrane, thinning here, thickening there, breathing. He had drawn it a hundred times for the Royal Cartographic Office, and a hundred times Master Aldery had looked at it with that particular expression of a man watching a dog walk on its hind legs — impressive, unnatural, and to be discouraged.

"You understand," Aldery had said, the day before the arrest, "that a map is an argument, Vess. And yours argues for the existence of a country we have no treaty with, no trade with, and no desire to acknowledge."

"It exists," Tam had said. "I've proven it exists. The old charts prove it. The Veilwarden fragments prove it—"

"The Veilwardens are *heresy*, Vess. The Church burned their charts. The Crown burned their descendants. There is a reason for both fires." Aldery had sighed then, and it had almost been kind. "You're the best hand I've ever trained. That's what makes this so tiresome."

*Tiresome.* Four months in cell nine, awaiting trial for heresy — a charge that carried the pyre if the witch-finders got jurisdiction, mere disgrace if the Chancery kept him — and his life's work reduced by his own mentor to a word meaning *inconvenient*.

The lamp in the corridor guttered. Footsteps passed, unhurried: the night warder doing his rounds. Tam counted them out of habit. Forty-one paces down, forty-one back. The man stopped at the grate sometimes and stared in without speaking, the way men stare at animals they can't decide whether to fear or eat.

Tonight the footsteps paused longer than usual.

"Vess," the warder said. His voice was strange — flattened, careful. "You've a visitor."

"At this hour?" Tam rose, stiff-jawed. Visitors did not come at this hour. Visitors came in daylight, escorted, with clerks to record every word. A visitor at midnight was either a bribe or a noose. "Who?"

The warder didn't answer. Instead there came a sound Tam took a moment to identify because he had never heard it inside the Chancery before: the soft, metallic *click* of a lockpick finding its home in a lock that was not the cell door's.

The corridor went dark. The warder made a noise like a man falling asleep very suddenly and very heavily against a wall.

Then his cell door swung open, and a woman stood in it holding a stolen ring of keys and a lantern shuttered to a knife-thin slit of light, and she looked — there was no other way to describe it — absolutely furious about the whole enterprise.

"Tam Vess?" she said.

"Depends who's asking," Tam said, "and whether asking is going to get me killed."

"The witch-finders are coming for you tomorrow. Trial's been moved up. Pyre's been approved." She said it the way one reads a laundry list. "So your options are me, or ash. Choose quickly; I've never done this before and I'd like to be gone before I learn how badly I'm doing it."

Tam looked at her. Small, sharp-faced, dressed like a market woman in a hooded cloak that fit her badly, hands steady around the keys despite whatever she meant by *I've never done this before*. Her eyes had a quality he associated with trapped things: bright, watchful, calculating exits.

"And you are?"

"Nobody. A charm-seller. Your rescue is a business arrangement." She thrust a bundle of dark cloth at his chest. "That's a guard's coat. Put it on. We're walking out the front."

"The *front*?"

"Do you have a better idea?"

"I have several ideas. Most of them involve not walking past forty guards."

"Most of mine involve not being burned alive, so we'll call it even." She was already moving, checking the corridor with quick glances. "Come on. Every moment you stand there philosophizing, I age."

It occurred to Tam, later and often, that he should have asked more questions. Who sent her. What she wanted. Why a charm-seller — a phrase that, spoken aloud in Corvane, was roughly equivalent to confessing to petty sorcery — would risk the pyre herself to free a disgraced cartographer she had plainly never met. But the coat smelled of onions and the woman was already three steps down the corridor, and the alternative was ash, so Tam Vess, who had spent his whole life believing that careful men survived and reckless ones became cautionary tales, walked out of his cell after her like a fool following a will-o'-the-wisp into a marsh.

Which, he would reflect, was more or less exactly what happened.

* * *

Elodie had planned the escape eleven times. She had planned it in the bathhouse, planning it while scrubbing other women's laundry, planning it while lying awake listening to the map breathe in its hiding place beneath her floorboards — because the map did breathe now, she was nearly sure of it, a faint paper rustle in the silence like something sleeping and dreaming, and it had gotten worse since the stranger died, and she needed it read *before* it did whatever unreadable living documents eventually did.

Eleven plans. All of them had involved bribing someone, because bribery was quiet and reliable and Elodie had spent seven years learning that quiet and reliable kept you alive. But the bribable clerk had been reassigned, the bribable warder had developed scruples along with a gambling debt too large for Elodie's purse, and the plan had collapsed into its twelfth and final iteration, which consisted of: steal keys, hit warder with lantern, hope.

She hated hoping. Hoping was what people did instead of preparing.

"Left here," she whispered, counting doors. Cell nine to the stair, stair to the records room, records room to the yard, yard to the gate where a laundry cart departed at the change of watch and would, tonight, contain two extra bundles of dirty linen, one of which coughed.

Behind her, the scholar was talking. Of course he was. He hadn't stopped talking since the cell.

"—the gate guard changes at the third bell, which works in our favor only if the third bell has already rung, has it? I lost track of bells somewhere around the fourth month. Also, whoever you are, you should know that hitting a Chancery warder carries a heavier sentence than harboring a heretic, legally speaking, so if we're caught you may wish to claim I forced you—"

"If we're caught," Elodie said through her teeth, "we'll both be claiming a great many things, none of them true. Now stop talking."

"I don't stop talking under stress. It's a documented trait. My mother wrote to the Office about it twice."

"*Stop.*"

Miraculously, he did — for perhaps thirty seconds, which she later learned was a personal record. They reached the records room, all shadow and shelving and the ghost-smell of ink, and Elodie pressed herself against the wall beside the window and listened. Rain, fine and persistent. The yard below, empty except for the cart, the mule, and one bored gate-guard sheltering under the archway.

"Window," she said. "Rope. You first."

"That rope is four feet short of the ground."

"I know."

"Ah," said Tam Vess, in the tone of a man revising his opinion of the evening from *bad* to *educational*. "You intend for me to fall."

"I intend for you to fall *quietly*. Bend your knees. Roll."

"I am a cartographer. My entire professional value resides in my hands."

"Then tuck them in and roll on your shoulder. Go."

He went. To his credit — and Elodie noted the credit grudgingly, filing it away next to her mounting suspicion that she had rescued either a genius or an idiot and would not know which for some time — he fell well, folding at the last moment and rolling into the mud beside the cart with only one undignified squelch and a muttered oath that included several anatomical impossibilities.

Elodie followed, lighter, quicker, landing soft as she'd landed soft a thousand times running from things. She hauled him up by the collar before he could speak and shoved him toward the cart.

"In. Under the canvas. Don't breathe loud."

They burrowed among the linens, sour and gray and reeking of lye, and pulled the canvas down, and waited. Above them the rain ticked. Beyond the canvas slats, the gate-guard stamped and yawned. Somewhere in the prison behind them, someone had finally discovered the warder sleeping against a wall with a lump on his head, and voices were rising.

"Hold," Elodie breathed.

"Holding," Tam whispered. Then, because he was who he was: "Statistically, carts are searched at the gate in roughly one incident out of—"

The bell above the gate rang the change of watch. The guard waved the cart through with the profound indifference of a man whose shift was ending. The wheels rattled over cobblestones, turned left, turned right, and Corvane's prison walls slid away behind them into the rain.

Under the canvas, in the dark, in the stink, Tam Vess began to laugh. It was a rusty sound, unpracticed, as if the machinery of it had seized from disuse, and it shook the linens.

"We're out," he said. "You got us out. Through the *front*. With laundry." He laughed again, helplessly. "I want you to know that when I write my memoirs — assuming I live to write memoirs, which currently feels ambitious — this will occupy an entire chapter."

"Don't write memoirs," Elodie said. "Memoirs get people found."

* * *

The safehouse was a cellar beneath a shuttered dyer's shop in Tanner's Row, rented a week ago under a false name and chosen because it had two exits, no windows, and neighbors too busy feuding with each other to notice anyone else. Elodie lit one candle, shielded it with a bowl, and finally turned to look properly at what she had bought with a night of terror and a stolen ring of keys.

In the candlelight, Tam Vess looked less like a prisoner and more like a drawing of one: ink-stained fingers, hollow cheeks, hair in need of both cutting and forgiveness, and eyes that went immediately — she watched them go, tracked them like compass needles — to the satchel at her hip.

To the shape inside the satchel.

"You have it," he said quietly. All the laughter had gone out of him. "You actually have one."

Elodie's hand closed over the satchel flap. "Have *what*."

"A Veilwarden chart." He said the word the way a priest says the name of a forbidden god — reverent and afraid at once. "I've seen fragments. Two, in the restricted archive, before Aldery decided I'd seen too much and locked the cabinet. Rubbings of rubbings, half illegible. The Crown destroyed every complete one after the war. If that's what I think it is, it's the only—" He stopped himself. Physically, visibly, biting down on the rest like a man refusing a second drink. "Why am I here? Really. Nobody breaks a heretic out of the Chancery out of charity. Charity doesn't pick locks."

Elodie had rehearsed this part too. *I found it. I need it read. Pay him, point him at the problem, keep your name out of it.* She had rehearsed it eleven times along with the escape.

What she said, because exhaustion strips rehearsal away, was: "A man died in my stairwell four nights ago. He put this in my hands and said 'Mirabel' and died. Since then it's been — moving. Unfolding itself. Getting warm. And tonight, before I came for you, I caught it *pulling*, like a fish on a line, toward—" she gestured vaguely southward, toward the border, toward everything she refused to name, "—and I don't know what it wants, and the witch-finders are already asking my neighbors questions about a dead man, and I have seven years of keeping my head down ruined in one week, and you are apparently the only person in this city who might know what I'm carrying." She ran out of breath and, embarrassingly, near the edge of something hotter. "So. Can you read it or not?"

Silence. The candle fluttered. Tam Vess looked at her for a long moment, and she watched him do the arithmetic — dead stranger, moving map, hunted bearer — and arrive at a sum that frightened him. She could see it land. She took a small, bitter satisfaction in it. Let him be frightened. Everyone in this story was frightened; it was the one thing they'd all have in common.

"Yes," he said finally. "I can read it. Or I can try. But understand what you're asking, charm-seller. These charts weren't maps of places. They were maps of the *boundary* — the Veil between here and Mirabel. Living instruments, keyed to a bloodline. The Veilwardens didn't carry them; by every account I've ever found, the charts *carried themselves*, choosing bearers of the warden blood. Which means either that dead man handed you the most dangerous object in the human kingdoms by chance—" his eyes narrowed, "—or he handed it to you *on purpose*, because it told him to, because of what you are."

"I'm a charm-seller," Elodie said flatly. "I sell knots and copper coins and the suggestion of good luck to gullible fishermen. That's all I am. That's all I've been for seven years, and it's worked, and I'd like it to go on working."

"Charm-sellers don't break scholars out of prisons."

"Desperate ones do."

"Desperate *witches* do," Tam said, softly, and Elodie felt the word go through her like a splinter finding its depth.

She should have lied. She was good at lying; she'd had practice. But she was tired down to the marrow, and the map was warm against her hip like a sleeping animal, and this ridiculous, talkative, imprisoned man was the first person in seven years who might actually *understand* — and so she heard herself say, in a voice she barely recognized:

"My mistress taught me hedge-craft when I was fourteen. I buried it when I ran. I have buried it every single day since, and it has stayed buried, until four days ago, when that map touched my hands and it *stopped staying buried*." Her hands had fists in them without her permission. "So don't tell me what I am. I know what I am. What I want to know is what I'm *carrying*, and whether it can be put down."

Something shifted in Tam's face. The combative gleam banked itself; underneath it was an older, wearier light. He lowered himself onto the cellar's one stool, wincing, and held out his hands.

"Show me," he said. "And then we'll both find out how much trouble we're in."

Elodie hesitated. Every instinct honed across seven years of careful mediocrity screamed at her — *don't show anyone, don't tell anyone, hide it deeper, run farther* — and every one of those instincts had kept her alive and small and alone, and looking at Tam Vess's ink-stained, waiting hands, she understood with sudden clarity that those instincts had also kept her *ignorant*, and that ignorance was a luxury the map had already taken from her.

She untied the satchel. She drew out the folded square of leather and parchment, heavier than it should be, warmer than the room.

It unfolded itself before she could touch the folds.

Tam Vess made a sound she would remember for years — a small, involuntary noise, the sound of a man seeing a legend stand up and breathe. In the candlelight the ink was moving, crawling, lines redrawing themselves in slow peristalsis like veins under skin, and where the map showed the borderlands between the human kingdoms and the blank space where Mirabel officially did not exist, the blank space was not blank. It was full. Coastlines, forests, roads, a city rendered in silver that seemed to hold its own faint weather — and threaded through all of it, a single luminous seam, the Veil itself, running like a scar of light.

And along that seam, in three places, the ink pulsed. Darkly. Rhythmically.

Like wounds.

"Oh," Tam said. He had gone very pale. "Oh, that's not good. Charm-seller — Elodie, whatever your name is — that is *not good*. Those aren't landmarks. Those are tears. The Veil is tearing, and this chart isn't showing it to us—" he looked up at her, and all the pedantry, all the irony, had burned away and left something raw, "—it's *asking* us to do something about it."

Outside, far off across the sleeping city, a church bell began to toll the hour, and beneath its measured voice Elodie heard — or imagined she heard, or would spend years deciding whether she had heard — the map answer, faint and patient, like a knock at a door.

Somewhere in Corvane, she knew, the witch-finders were already awake.
