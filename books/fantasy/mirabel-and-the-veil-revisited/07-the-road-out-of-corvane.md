## 7. The Road Out of Corvane

They left before the fourth bell, when the city was at its grayest and most forgiving.

Elodie had learned long ago that Corvane told two kinds of lies: the ones it spoke aloud, in its market cries and its bells and its criers proclaiming the Crown's mercy, and the ones it wore on its face — the soft fog off the river that made even the gallows look gentle, the lamplighters' flames guttering gold against wet stone as though the city were warm when it was anything but. Dawn was the hour when both kinds of lying were thickest, and so dawn was when she moved.

She had done this before. That was the bitter joke of it. Seven years ago she had slipped out of a village with nothing but a satchel and a burn scar she still didn't like to touch, and now here she was again, packing by candlelight with her hands steady only because she refused to let them be otherwise.

The bundle was small. It always was. A change of clothes, a knife, dried plums, the copper kettle she'd sold charms out of for six years and could not bring herself to leave behind. And wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom, where it could not see her and she could not see it, the map.

Even through three layers of cloth she could feel it. Not warmth, exactly. Attention. Like a cat pretending to sleep.

"You're staring at your bag," Tam Vess said from the doorway.

"I'm inventorying."

"You're staring." The scholar leaned against the frame of her narrow stairwell door with his arms crossed, looking — Elodie thought not for the first time — like a heron that had been dressed by someone who hated it. His coat was too fine for flight and too ruined for anything else; he'd slept in it two nights running, and ink stained his fingers to the second knuckle. "In my experience, people inventory when they believe they have a future. You're deciding what to abandon."

"Six years of my life," Elodie said. "That's what I'm abandoning. The stall. Mrs. Quill's rent. The name Underhill I built out of nothing because no one ever asks after a charm-seller named Underhill."

"No one will ask after you at all if we're caught standing here." Tam glanced down the stairwell, toward the street, where the fog lay thick as wool. "The witch-finders questioned the baker yesterday. The one whose window faces your stairs. He described a man bleeding to death on your step in detail I would call literary if it weren't going to get us hanged."

"He described it wrong. The man fell *after* he knocked. There's a difference between dying on someone's step and being let in to die."

"The witch-finders are not known for their appreciation of nuance."

Elodie pulled the drawstring tight and slung the bag across her body, map-side against her spine, where her heartbeat might convince it — and herself — that everything was ordinary. "Where's Sera?"

"Watching the gate road. She said if she's to travel with us until you're dead or the map is hers, whichever comes first, she'd at least choose the route." Tam's mouth did something that was almost a smile and then thought better of it. "Charming woman. I've missed conversation with people who threaten me honestly. At court everyone did it behind fans."

* * *

The streets near the river were empty in the way that made Elodie's skin prickle rather than soothe. Corvane never truly slept — there were always bakers, always drunks, always the night-soil men making their rounds — but the fog swallowed sound and shape alike, and they walked through a city reduced to suggestion: a bridge, a bell tower, a wall of shuttered houses leaning together like conspirators.

Sera Vane waited beneath the arch of Tanner's Bridge, hood up, arms folded, a short sword at her hip that she wore the way other women wore jewelry — as an announcement. She was her father's daughter in the face and in nothing else; where the stranger in Elodie's stairwell had died gentle-eyed and apologetic, Sera looked at Elodie the way a drawn bow looks at a target.

"The east gate's watched," she said without greeting. "Two of them, in Crown gray, checking carts. They've got one of those dogs."

"One of those—" Tam began.

"Tracker hounds. Bred on ash from burned pyres." Sera said it flatly, reciting something she'd learned recently and hated learning. "My father wrote about them. Said a good one can smell a spell three days old." Her jaw tightened around the word *spell*, around the accusation underneath it. "So maybe explain to me again why wherever we're going, we can't go there without the thing that killed him."

"The map didn't kill your father," Elodie said, for perhaps the twentieth time.

"Something did. Something wanted him dead and you holding that paper, and I don't believe in coincidence. My father taught me better." For just a moment the rage in her face cracked along an older fault line, and underneath it Elodie saw the thing Sera actually carried: not anger but grief, wearing anger like armor wears a body. Then the crack sealed. "West gate, then. The pilgrim road. It adds two days."

"Two days is nothing," Tam said. "Being hanged is quite a lot."

They went west through streets that grew poorer and quieter, past the tanneries and the rope-walks, past the last row of houses where the city simply gave up and became fields. Elodie kept her eyes down and her gift folded small inside her, smaller than it had ever been, a coal banked under ash. But the map pressed against her back like a hand between her shoulder blades, and every few steps she felt that attention sharpen, as if it knew — as if it had always known — which direction the horizon lay.

The west gate was a squat arch in a wall the kings had stopped maintaining a century ago, and beyond it the pilgrim road ran pale through morning mist toward hills that were more rumor than geography. No guards. Sera read the emptiness the way Tam read maps, and Elodie saw her relax by exactly one degree.

Then the fog ahead of them stood up.

It happened fast, the way violence always does — too fast for the mind, slow enough for the body to understand everything far too late. Four figures resolved out of the mist on the road itself, not approaching but already there, arranged in the loose crescent that meant *we have done this many times*. Crown gray coats. The white knot of the Office of Inquiry at each shoulder. And in the center, a tall man with a face like a closed ledger, holding a leashed shape at his side that was dog-adjacent and nothing any kennel had produced.

"Witch-finder," Tam breathed, and the word came out with all the reverence of a curse.

"Underhill." The tall man's voice carried easily through the fog, unhurried, almost kind. "Elodie Underhill, charm-seller, of Candlemaker's Row. We've been hoping to make your acquaintance." He smiled the way winter smiles. "You've had a difficult week. A dead man on your stairs. A break-in at the gaol — the scholar Vess, missing from his cell since Tuesday. Strange weather for coincidences, isn't it? We don't care for coincidences in Corvane."

Elodie's mind went white and then very clear, the way it had in the raid two years ago, the way it always did when hiding stopped being possible and something older than hiding took over. She felt the coal in her chest stir. She crushed it down. Not here. Not with four blades and a beast bred on ashes.

"We're just travelers," she said, and heard how badly it sounded.

"Yes," the witch-finder agreed pleasantly. "Travelers. With a scholar who was imprisoned for claiming the fae realm is real, traveling with a charm-seller the hound has been circling since the bridge, and—" his eyes moved to Sera, cataloguing her sword, her stance, her father's jaw, "—a young woman we've been looking for since her father's body was reported. Mistress Vane. Your mother will be relieved."

Something in Sera shifted, a blade leaving a sheath in the way she stood.

"Give us the bag, mistress Underhill," the witch-finder said, "and this can be a matter of questioning rather than a matter of fire. I am reasonable. The Crown is reasonable. Magic is the only unreasonable thing left in the world, and we mean to reason with it thoroughly."

The hound's head turned. Its nostrils flared toward Elodie's spine, toward the oilcloth, toward the thing that had chosen her, and it began — softly, horribly — to sing. Not a bark. A thin, rising keen, like a kettle, like a widow, like something that knew exactly what it smelled.

And the map woke up.

Elodie felt it happen rather than chose it: heat blooming against her back, and beneath her feet the road itself seemed to lean, the fog pulling inward like a breath drawn through teeth. Light moved under the ground. She had one heartbeat to think *no, not here, not me* —

— and then the world came apart from the left.

It came apart quietly, which was somehow worse. One moment the leftmost witch-finder was raising his crossbow; the next he was simply elsewhere, hurled ten feet into the ditch with a sound like a door slamming, his weapon spinning away into the mist. A man stepped out of the fog where no man had been — big, bearded, moving with the terrible economy of someone who had done violence so often it had worn smooth, like a river stone. He carried a soldier's bill-hook, Crown-issue, and he used it the way a farmer uses a scythe: without flourish, without hatred, without stopping.

The second witch-finder died reaching for his whistle. The third managed a scream, and the tall one — the reasonable one — backed away two steps, hound shrieking at the end of its leash, and did the arithmetic soldiers do, and ran. The hound tore free and fled after him, its song fading into the fog like something ashamed.

It was over in less time than it takes to tell. The stranger stood among the bodies, breathing hard but evenly, and looked at the three of them — at Tam frozen mid-cringe, at Sera with her sword half-drawn, at Elodie with light still crawling faintly under her skin and terror written plainly where she'd failed to hide it.

He looked longest at Elodie. At whatever it was she was failing to hide.

"Well," Tam said faintly. "That was — I want to be very clear that I had no part in that."

"Nobody asked you anything yet," the man said. His voice was low and roughened, a voice used to orders and to giving none anymore. He wiped the bill-hook on the dead man's coat with the detached efficiency of habit, and something about the gesture — the practiced, unprotesting way he cleaned Crown blood off Crown steel — snagged Elodie's attention and held it.

"You're wearing the king's coat," Sera said. Her sword was fully out now, angled not quite at him. "Under the gray. I can see the red at your collar. So either you're the bravest witch-finder I've ever seen or you're a deserter who just murdered four of his own officers."

"Three officers and a handler," the man corrected. He said it without pride and without shame, the way a man reports the weather. "And I'm neither brave nor yours to classify." He shouldered the bill-hook and turned, walking backward a few steps so he faced them while he spoke, eyes never leaving the fog behind them. "Whistles carry. There'll be a patrol post within earshot, and riders within the quarter hour. If you're going somewhere, go. If you're staying, stay, but do it quietly."

"Wait." Elodie's voice came out steadier than she felt. "Who are you?"

For the first time he looked at her directly, and she saw that his eyes were the color of wet slate and very tired, tired in the marrow, the tiredness of a man carrying something heavy that he intended to keep carrying. Whatever he saw in her face — the light still fading under her skin, the fear, the question — he answered it with a shrug that declined everything.

"Brannoc Hale," he said. "I ask no questions and I offer no explanations. That's the whole arrangement. Take it or walk back to those gentlemen."

"That's not an arrangement, that's a hostage situation with extra steps," Tam muttered.

"It's the only kind I offer."

"And why?" Sera demanded. "Why save us? People don't kill witch-finders for strangers. What do you want?"

Hale looked at her, and then at the road, and then — briefly, unwillingly — at the place where Elodie's bag hung, where the map lay quiet again, feigning sleep. When he spoke, his voice had dropped into something lower, something that wasn't for all of them.

"I was at Kettleford," he said. "Two years ago. You'll have heard of it. The burning." He adjusted his grip on the bill-hook, knuckles whitening and relaxing, whitening and relaxing. "I've been walking away from that day ever since, and every road I take, I find I'm still walking toward things like it. So." He turned fully away, toward the open country, toward the pale line of the pilgrim road and the hills beyond. "I'm going north. There's a shepherd's track past the mill that skips the patrol post entirely. Anyone walking the same direction can walk behind me. Anyone asking questions can walk somewhere else."

He walked. He did not look back to see whether they followed.

* * *

They followed, of course. Elodie told herself it was because Hale knew the track and the track mattered, and that was true, but it was not the whole truth and she suspected he knew it wasn't. The whole truth was simpler and stranger: in six years of selling luck to people who needed it, Elodie Underhill had never once seen anyone step out of the fog for a stranger. Luck was a thing you bought. What Hale had done was not for sale, and she did not know yet what it cost, but she recognized the currency. She'd been paying in it her whole life.

The shepherd's track climbed away from the pilgrim road through gorse and bracken, narrow and stony, invisible from below. By the time the sun burned the fog off the valley, Corvane was a smudge of smoke behind them, and Elodie made herself stop looking at it. Six years. Mrs. Quill would find the rent paid through the season and the kettle gone and would shake her head and say *that girl was always too careful to be honest*, and that would be the whole of her eulogy in that city. She found, to her surprise, that grief and relief arrived together, twins, indistinguishable.

They walked single file: Hale first, setting a pace that was punishing and unapologetic; then Tam, who complained about his boots, the terrain, the century, and the fundamental cruelty of elevation; then Sera, who kept glancing back the way a hawk checks a treeline; then Elodie, with the map against her spine, growing warmer as the miles passed, warmer as the land rose, as though it too could feel how much farther the horizon had become.

At midday they stopped at a stream, and the fragile parliament of their little company convened without anyone declaring it.

"So," Tam said, crouched at the water, drinking from cupped hands. "Deserter. Murderer, technically, though I'll note the victims were extremely poor choices of victim. Travels alone, knows the patrol posts, knows the tracks, kills four trained men in eleven seconds." He sat back on his heels and looked at Hale with the frank, appraising stare he probably thought was scholarly. "You knew what we were. Back there. Before the hound sang. You looked at Elodie and you weren't surprised by the light under her skin."

"Tam," Elodie said warningly.

"No, I want it noted that our rescuer is a man collecting secrets like debt notes, and I'd like to know whose ledger he's working from."

Hale drank from the stream in long, unhurried pulls, then wiped his beard. "I ask no questions," he said. "I also answer none. You keep confusing the arrangement."

"It's not an arrangement, it's a hostage situation—"

"—with extra steps, yes, you said." Almost, almost, something moved at the corner of Hale's mouth. It might have been amusement. It might have been a scar. "Look at the road behind us, scholar. Tell me what you see."

Tam looked. So did Elodie. Far below, where the pilgrim road crossed the plain, a smudge of dust was moving — riders, five or six, keeping pace with the hills, patient as tide.

"They're not searching," Sera said quietly. "They're herding. Keeping parallel. Waiting for us to come down."

"They know where we're going," Elodie said, and the realization settled into her coldly, heavier than the map. "Or they know where the map wants to go. It's the same thing, isn't it?" She touched the oilcloth through the canvas, and beneath her palm the map pulsed once, gently, unmistakably — acknowledgment, agreement, appetite. Toward the border. Toward Mirabel. Toward whatever waited at the end of its ink. "It doesn't matter which road we pick. It matters whether we get there first."

Hale watched her hand on the bag for a long moment. Whatever he was thinking, he filed it away unshared. "North," he said, standing. "There's a ford past the hills where the patrols don't cross until full dark. We move now, we cross clean, and tomorrow nobody follows us anywhere." He picked up his bill-hook. "After that, we'll see what questions I still don't ask."

They walked. The hills took them in, folding gorse and stone and silence around the four of them, and Elodie fell into the rhythm of putting one foot in front of the other, which was, she reflected, the oldest skill she owned. Fleeing. She had hoped seven years would retire it. Instead it had only added companions.

But once, near dusk, when the others were arguing about the ford and the light was going amber and long across the moor, Brannoc Hale dropped back beside her — just for a dozen steps, no more — and said, quietly, without looking at her:

"The hound sang at you. Not the bag. *You.*" A pause, filled with wind and footsteps. "Whatever you're carrying, girl — it's not luggage. It knows you." Another pause, longer. "I've seen that before. Once." And then he lengthened his stride and returned to the front, and offered nothing else, and asked nothing at all.

Behind them, far down in the blue evening, the riders' dust still paralleled the hills, patient as tide.

And against her spine, warm as a sleeping animal dreaming of home, the map pulled north.
