## 8. The Daughter of the Dead Man

Sera Vane had learned to track the way other people learned to pray: alone, in the dark, with a desperation that passed for faith.

Her father had taught her the first lessons before she was old enough to understand why they mattered. *Watch the ground, little wren. Watch the edges of things. A man who walks soft still leaves a shadow on the dew.* He'd made a game of it, when she was six — sending her ahead down forest paths to find where he'd hidden a honey-cake or a copper bit, laughing his low rolling laugh when she emerged triumphant from the bracken with her prize held high. She hadn't known then that he was teaching her to hunt him. That one day the game would be the only thing left of him, and she would play it across half a kingdom with no prize at the end but a grave.

She found the first trace of them three days out of Corvane, at a crossroads shrine where travelers still left offerings despite the witch-finders' frowns. A scrap of wax from a candle stub. A boot print pressed deep at the edge of the mud — a woman's boot, small, worn unevenly on the outer heel, the print of someone who walked carefully because she had spent years learning not to be seen.

Sera crouched over it for a long time, her breath fogging in the gray morning air, and felt the rage come up in her like floodwater finding a crack in a wall.

*Her.* The charm-seller. The woman her father had died reaching for.

She had gone back through Corvane's records after they burned him — or whatever it was they did with bodies no one claimed, and Sera refused to imagine it, though she imagined it every night anyway. Aldous Vane, listed as a vagrant, cause of death unrecorded. No belongings returned. No note. But Sera had grown up reading her father's silences the way scholars read books, and she knew what his silences meant. She knew he had been carrying something in the last months of his life that frightened him badly enough to make him drink, badly enough to make him check the street twice from every window, badly enough that when he finally came to her — pale, shaking, pressing a purse of coin into her hands with words he couldn't finish — she had let him walk away rather than ask.

*I'll explain everything, wren. I promise. When this is done—*

The promise had died in a stairwell in Corvane, bled out onto someone else's stones.

And now that someone else was walking north with a folded piece of her father's life tucked into her coat, and Sera Vane intended to take it back.

* * *

She caught up with them on the fourth day, at dusk, in the ruins of an old waystation where the pilgrim road bent around a hill of standing stones. She had watched the company for two hours from the treeline, cataloguing them with her father's cold patience.

The charm-seller first — Elodie Underhill, the innkeeper's wife in Corvane had called her, though there was nothing of the innkeeper about the way she moved. Small, dark-haired, watchful. She kept her hands busy even at rest, mending a strap or turning something over in her fingers, and twice Sera saw her glance at the treeline without any obvious reason to. Not a soldier, then. But not a fool either.

The scholar second — Tam Vess, and Sera knew that name, everyone who could read knew that name, the royal cartographer whose heresy trial had been the talk of Corvane's coffeehouses for a season. Gaunt, ink-stained, complaining steadily under his breath as he tried to coax a fire from damp wood. His hands shook. He would be no fight at all.

The third one worried her. Big, quiet, moving like a man who had once been taught to move by people who taught killing for a living. He sat apart from the fire with his back to a stone, a sword across his knees, and he never quite looked at anything directly — which meant he was looking at everything. Deserted soldiery, if Sera guessed right, and she had grown up guessing right about men like that. Her father had collected them the way other men collected debts.

Three of them. One of whom might be dangerous.

Sera checked her knives. Two at her belt, one in her boot, one up her sleeve where her father had shown her how to wear it so a customs man's pat would find nothing. Then she did what her father had taught her to do before any fight: she waited for the wind, and she thought about what she wanted.

Not their deaths. She wanted the map. She wanted the truth. And she wanted — though she would have cut out her own tongue before admitting it — to look into the charm-seller's eyes and see whether there was anything there worth hating, or whether hatred was all she was going to have left.

* * *

She went in low, out of the wind, the way the deer went through the hazel scrub, and she was ten feet from the fire before the big man's head came up.

He didn't shout. That was the worst part, afterward — that he didn't shout, didn't scramble, just rose off the ground with a smooth unhurried motion and put himself between her and the others as though he'd been waiting for her all evening. His eyes were calm and terribly tired.

"Evening," he said.

Sera threw the sleeve-knife at his throat.

It was a good throw — her father would have said it was the best he'd ever seen from her — and it should have ended the conversation. But the big man turned his shoulder into it, almost lazily, and the blade skipped off the leather of his coat and buried itself in the stone behind him with a sound like a snapped tooth. Then the world became very fast and very simple.

She got the belt knife out and inside his guard, opened a line of red along his forearm, felt the fierce animal joy of contact — and then his hand closed on her wrist and the joy was gone, crushed out of her arm like water from a rag. He didn't strike her. He twisted, stepped, and suddenly the ground was against her back and his knee was pinning her sword-arm and his face was close above hers, and he was saying, quietly, as though continuing a conversation:

"I said evening. Not good evening. I want that noted."

"Brannoc." The charm-seller's voice, sharp with alarm. "Don't hurt her — Brannoc, wait—"

"She cut me," Brannoc said, without looking away from Sera. "I'm noting that too."

"Let her up."

"She'll go for you next. Look at her eyes."

Sera looked at the charm-seller, then, upside down and pinned, and the charm-seller looked back — and Sera saw it happen, the moment of recognition crossing the woman's face like a cloud crossing a field. She saw Elodie Underhill remember a bleeding stranger in a stairwell. She saw her understand, all at once, who Sera must be.

"You're his daughter," Elodie said softly. "Aldous's daughter."

Something in Sera tore loose then, some knot she had been tying tighter every day since Corvane, and she stopped being a tracker and a fighter and became only a girl with her father's blood cooling somewhere in an unmarked pit. She surged against Brannoc's knee, snarling, and got the boot knife free, and would have done something unforgivable with it if Tam Vess hadn't lunged forward and caught her wrist — gently, absurdly gently, a scholar's grip, ink-stained fingers saying *no, no, child, no* — and the fight went out of her all at once, like a snuffed candle.

She lay in the cold grass and wept, furiously, silently, hating every tear.

* * *

They gave her tea, which was an insult, and a place by the fire, which was worse, because it was warm and she didn't want to be warm, she wanted to be a blade. Brannoc bound his forearm without complaint and sat where he could see both her hands. Tam hovered at the edge of the firelight like a man unsure whether he was jailer or chaplain.

"The map," Sera said at last, because her voice had to start somewhere. "My father's map. You have it."

"I have it," Elodie said.

"It killed him."

"No." Elodie's hands went still in her lap. "No, I don't think it did. I think—" She hesitated, and Sera watched her choose honesty over comfort, watched her decide not to soften it. "I think men were hunting him for it. Witch-finders, maybe. Or worse. He was wounded when he reached my door. Whatever killed him started long before my stairwell."

"And you took it." Sera's voice came out flat and dead. "A dying man gives you the only thing he died protecting, and you keep it. You run north with it. You don't send word. You don't look for family. Nothing."

"I didn't know he had family."

"Did you look?"

The silence answered for Elodie, and Sera drank it like wine, bitter as it was. She stood, slowly, letting them see her empty hands, and Brannoc tensed but did not move.

"My father carried that thing for eleven months," she said. "Eleven months of drinking and flinching and checking locks, and I let him carry it alone because I thought it was debt. Gambling debts, Crown debts, some woman's husband — I invented a dozen reasons because none of them required me to ask him the real question. And now he's dead, and the answer is a folded piece of paper that makes a hedge-witch run north with a heretic and a deserter, and nobody — *nobody* — will tell me what it is."

Tam Vess cleared his throat. "That is," he began, in the tone of a man stepping onto thin ice because the far shore looked important, "a question with a very long answer, and parts of it are, ah, formally heretical, and parts of it are likely to get us all burned—"

"Tam," Elodie said.

"—but the short version," he finished bravely, "is that it isn't paper. Or rather, it is, but it's also alive, and it has decided it likes her." He pointed at Elodie with his teacup. "Which is a sentence I have now said aloud, gods help me."

Sera stared at him. Then at Elodie. The charm-seller's face had gone carefully blank in the way of people who have practiced blankness for years, and beneath it Sera glimpsed something that surprised her: fear. Real fear, old fear, the fear of a person who has spent a long time hiding and has just been told the hiding is over.

"You're lying," Sera said, but weakly, because Tam Vess had the particular dignity of a man who had already lost everything worth lying to protect.

"I never lie about maps," Tam said. "It's the only integrity I have left."

* * *

They argued until the moon rose. Sera demanded the map; Elodie refused, not with defiance but with a strange, helpless sincerity — *it comes back to me, I've tried to be rid of it* — which was somehow more infuriating than greed would have been. Brannoc said perhaps forty words total, all of them load-bearing. Tam talked too much and knew too much and flinched every time hooves might have sounded on the road.

And underneath the argument, Sera felt the shape of her grief changing. It had been a simple thing until tonight: her father dead, the world at fault, vengeance owed to whoever held the account. But accounts, she was beginning to suspect, were more complicated than knives. The woman across the fire had hidden a corpse rather than leave it for the witch-finders. Had lied to armed men to buy a dead stranger peace. Was now running from everything she'd built because of a dying man's last choice — a choice, Sera realized with a slow cold shock, that her father had made *for* her, had pressed into her hands exactly as he had once pressed coins into Sera's.

*Why her?* The question rose hot in Sera's throat. Why hand it to a stranger instead of his own daughter? Unless he hadn't had time. Unless he'd been choosing the nearest hands, not the best ones. Unless—

Unless he had known what carrying it cost, and had loved her too much to give it to her.

The thought ambushed her so completely that she had to turn her face toward the dark and breathe until her eyes stopped burning.

"I'm coming with you," she said finally, to the fire, because she couldn't say it to their faces yet.

Elodie looked up. "You don't have to—"

"Yes, I do." Sera sat back down, spine straight, hands flat on her knees, every inch of her a drawn blade pretending to be a seated woman. "You're going to get that thing wherever it wants to go, and the men who killed my father are going to follow it, and when they catch up — and they will — I intend to be there. For now, we travel together. Don't mistake it for trust. The moment you give me a reason, I take the map and I'm gone."

Brannoc, from his post against the stone, regarded her with those tired calm eyes and said, "Everyone here's got a reason to leave. Makes it fair."

Tam sighed the sigh of a man watching his odds of a quiet death recede. "Well. Four, then. Four is a proper company. Three is an anecdote."

Sera lay down with her back to all of them, her father's knives within reach, and stared at the stars through the broken roof of the waystation. Grief was still there — it would always be there, she understood that now, it was not a river she would cross but a country she would live in. But tonight, for the first time, it had a direction. It pointed north, along the road, toward whatever her father had died trying to reach.

Sleep came slowly. Just before it took her, she heard Elodie move nearby, and a whisper, barely more than breath:

"For what it's worth — I'm sorry. About your father. He deserved better than my stairwell."

Sera didn't answer. But in the morning, when they broke camp, she fell into step beside the charm-seller without being asked, and neither of them mentioned it, and the road went on northward through the gray hills — toward the border country, where the trees, though none of them knew it yet, had already begun to grow in spirals.
