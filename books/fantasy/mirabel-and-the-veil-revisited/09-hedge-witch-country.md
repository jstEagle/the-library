## 9. Hedge-Witch Country

The road remembered her feet before she remembered the road.

That was the thing about old paths — they wore themselves into you, grooves in the sole and the soul both, so that seven years of city cobbles could not quite smooth them away. Elodie knew it the moment the company crested the low hill above Alderbrook and saw the valley open below: the river running silver through the willows, the thatched roofs huddled like sheep against the cold, the crooked chimney of a cottage on the far rise where smoke should have been rising and was not.

She stopped walking.

"Here?" Tam Vess asked, coming up beside her with his satchel clutched to his chest like a shield. He had not stopped clutching it since the stocks. "This is where you learned?"

"This is where I was made," Elodie said, and heard how bitter it sounded, and did not correct it.

Behind them, Brannoc said nothing at all, which was his way. He stood a little apart from the road with his hand resting easy near his knife, scanning the valley the way he scanned everything — as if the world were a field of ambushes and he had simply not yet found the one with his name on it. Sera sat on a milestone, arms crossed, radiating the particular patience of someone who intended to follow them whether they walked or crawled.

The map rode against Elodie's ribs inside her coat, warm as a sleeping animal. It had grown warmer every mile for two days now, and she had told herself it was the season, the exercise, anything but what it actually was: the map pulling her home.

"It's called Alderbrook," she said, because Tam was waiting and because saying it aloud might make it smaller. "Population of maybe two hundred when I left. Everyone knows everyone's business, which means everyone knows who to point at when the witch-finders come asking." She pulled her hood lower. "We go through quietly. We buy bread if we can, we don't buy it if we can't, and we are gone before anyone looks twice at us."

"You grew up here," Sera said flatly, "and your plan is to be invisible in it."

"I lived here," Elodie corrected. "I didn't grow up. Growing up is what happens when you're allowed to stay."

She started down the hill before anyone could answer, and the others fell into step behind her, and the road carried her down into the past like a slow boat into deep water.

* * *

Alderbrook had not changed, which was somehow worse than if it had.

Change would have given her somewhere new to look. Instead there was the same well with its moss-slicked stones, the same leaning fence around Widow Marsh's garden, the same three geese — or the great-grandgeese of the same three geese — patrolling the lane with imperial disdain. The bakery still stood on the corner by the bridge, and the smell of it reached her halfway across the square: dark rye and caraway, the smell of cold mornings and warm hands, the smell of being fifteen and hungry and certain the whole world was waiting for her.

Her stomach clenched around the memory like a fist.

"There's a market day," Tam murmured, nodding toward the square where stalls were going up under a gray sky. "Good. Crowds are cover."

"Crowds are witnesses," Elodie said. But she let him steer her toward them anyway, because bread was bread, and because some stubborn part of her wanted to walk these lanes one more time with her head up, even disguised, even afraid.

The market was small and half-asleep, the way winter markets were. A man sold apples gone soft at the crown. A woman had laid out skeins of wool dyed the colors of rust and onion skin. And at the far end, tucked between a cooper's cart and a pile of turnips, an old woman sat behind a folding table hung with charms.

Elodie stopped so suddenly that Sera walked into her back.

The charms were nothing. That was the first thing she saw, with a professional's eye: knotted red thread, hagstones on leather cords, little bundles of dried herbs tied with twine. Hedge trinkets. The kind of thing she herself had sold in Corvane for six years, hollow as eggshells, harmless as buttons. The old woman selling them had a face like a walnut and eyes that missed nothing, and she was telling a farmwife that the knot-charm would keep foxes from the henhouse, and the farmwife was paying three coppers for it, and none of it was real.

Except.

Except that the knots were tied in threes, and the third knot was hidden inside the twist, and Elodie knew that pattern. She had tied ten thousand of those patterns. She had learned it at a workbench by a north-facing window while frost ferned the glass, from a woman with ink-stained fingers and a voice like gravel wrapped in velvet, who had said: *Anyone can tie a knot, girl. The trick is knowing which knot is a promise and which is a lie.*

"Mistress Ivenna taught me that," Elodie said aloud, without meaning to.

Tam looked at her sharply. "Who?"

"My teacher." Her mouth had gone dry. "The charm-seller over there — that's Alderbrook style. That's *her* style. Someone here is still tying her knots."

"Your witch mistress," Sera said, with the delicacy of a boot heel. "The one who betrayed you."

"I never said betrayed."

"You didn't have to. You say her name like it's a splinter."

Elodie turned away from the stall, from the walnut-faced woman, from the knots that were promises or lies depending on which side of the string you stood. "Buy the bread," she said. "I need air."

She did not wait for them to follow. She needed, just for a moment, to not be looked at.

* * *

She ended up, of course, at the cottage.

Not Ivenna's cottage — that stood up on the far rise, and she was not ready, and told herself firmly she might never be ready, and then found her boots carrying her along the mill path anyway because her body had apparently decided the matter without consulting her. No, first she went to the place she had slept: the lean-to behind the tannery where Ivenna had lodged her apprentice, one thin mattress and one thin blanket and a window that whistled all winter. The tannery was closed, its vats green with standing rainwater, its yard full of nettles. The lean-to had fallen down entirely.

Seven years. Seven years, and the town hadn't bothered to keep her corner of it standing.

She stood in the nettles and felt something crack quietly inside her chest, not painfully — more like ice shifting on a pond, the sound of something giving way that had seemed solid.

"You hated her."

Sera's voice came from the fence line. Elodie hadn't heard her approach; the girl moved quietly when she wanted to, which was most of the time. She leaned on the rotten rail with her arms folded, watching Elodie with those gray, unblinking eyes that were so much her father's.

"I don't hate her," Elodie said.

"You're standing in a nettle patch glaring at a collapsed shed. Where I'm from, that's hatred."

"Where I'm from," Elodie said, "that's grief." She brushed the nettles with the back of her hand, careful, an old habit — hedge-witches knew nettles the way soldiers knew their blades, the sting and the cure living in the same stem. "Ivenna took me in when I was fourteen. My mother was dead, my father had gone off to a war nobody won, and I was burning things. Little fires. Candles lighting themselves when I cried, milk souring when I was angry. The village noticed. Villages always notice. Another year and they'd have sent for a witch-finder, and I'd have died in a Corvane cell before my seventeenth birthday." She paused. "Ivenna got to me first. Bought my indenture for almost nothing. Told everyone she needed a servant girl."

"And instead she taught you."

"She taught me control. She taught me that magic wasn't a fire to put out but a craft to learn, that fear was just power wearing a mask it hadn't been fitted for yet." Elodie's voice went strange in her own ears. "For four years she was the only person alive who knew what I was, and she never once flinched. Do you understand what that's worth? To be known — wholly known — and not flinched at?"

Sera was quiet for a moment. When she spoke again, her voice had lost some of its edge. "So what happened?"

"The witch-finders happened. They came to Alderbrook in the autumn of my fourth year — not for me, for a family upriver, the Hedleys, who'd been curing cattle with words no one had written down. Ivenna knew they were coming. She had friends in low places, informants, whatever you want to call them. She had time to get the Hedleys out. She had time to get *me* out." Elodie's hands had curled into fists without her permission. "Instead she wrote me a letter, gave me thirty silver crowns, and told me to run east and never send word. And then she stayed. She stayed and she covered our tracks, and when the finders asked her if she knew of any practitioners in the area, she —"

Elodie stopped. The memory rose like floodwater, the way it always did, complete and merciless: the taproom of the Bridge Inn, herself disguised in a boy's cap among the rafters where she had no business being, listening to Ivenna's gravel-velvet voice say, clear and steady and carrying, *"Witchcraft? In Alderbrook? Mistress, I've kept this village's records for twenty years, and I'll thank you to check them before you insult my neighbors."*

Lying. Flawlessly. Sacrificing herself to protect the village, the Hedleys, everyone —

— except the one girl whose whole life she held in her ink-stained hands.

"She denied me," Elodie finished. "Not the others. Me. She named the Hedleys' escape route to throw the finders off the scent, and she let them believe I'd fled alone, untaught, a stray spark with no master. Which meant they hunted a loose end instead of a lineage. Which meant I lived." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "Do you see the shape of it? She saved my life by erasing hers. By making sure that if they ever caught me, I couldn't give them her. Four years of being the only person who truly knew me, and in the end I was a debt she paid off and crossed out of her ledger."

"Or," Sera said slowly, "she saved your life the only way that worked, and let you hate her for it so the hate would keep you moving, keep you hiding, keep you alive." She shrugged, uncomfortable with her own generosity. "My father lied to me for years too. About what he carried, about why we moved, about who was following us. I spent two years wanting to dig him up and kill him again. Now I mostly want to ask him why."

"Why do you think I came back?" Elodie said quietly.

And then, because the map chose that exact moment to pulse against her ribs — a slow, insistent heat, like a second heartbeat waking up — she added, "Come on. There's one more place."

* * *

The path up the far rise was overgrown but not forgotten. Her feet found it the way water finds an old channel: between the roots, around the fallen stone, past the hazel copse that had once been a hedge and had given up pretending. The brambles caught at her coat. The wind smelled of wet leaf-rot and woodsmoke from the village below, and underneath both, faintly, unmistakably, of dried vervain and beeswax and something sharper — a herb she couldn't name, green and bitter, that made the fine hairs on her arms stand up.

The cottage stood where it had always stood, squat and stone-walled under its turf roof, looking less like a house than like something the hill had grown and then thought better of.

But the smoke was wrong.

There was no smoke, first of all, though the hearth — she could feel it now, that prickle along her skin that seven years of suppression had dulled but never killed — the hearth had been lit recently. Ash-warmth ghosted from the chimney like breath held too long. And the wards—

Elodie stopped ten paces from the door and stared.

Every hedge-witch warded her threshold. It was the first lesson after control: salt in the sill, iron in the lintel, a charm above the door that said *known and welcome* to friends and *not today* to everything else. Ivenna's wards had been famous in their small way — layered, patient, woven over decades, the magical equivalent of a fortress built one brick a week.

They were broken. Not torn, not blasted — *unwoven*, each thread carefully picked apart and laid aside, the way you'd unpick a hem you intended to sew again later. Recently. Within days.

"She's coming back," Elodie whispered.

"Who?" Sera had her knife out; she'd drawn it somewhere between the hazel copse and the door, soundlessly, and Elodie was beginning to understand that Sera Vane's grief had made her fast rather than reckless. "The witch?"

"Nobody breaks wards like that except the woman who built them. And nobody builds wards like that and leaves them unpicked unless—" Elodie's throat tightened. Unless they mean to return. Unless they were interrupted. Unless they left in a hurry, taking only what mattered, leaving the rest for—

The map burned.

Not pulsed this time — *burned*, a stripe of heat straight through the cloth of her coat, and Elodie gasped and fumbled it out into the gray light. It unfolded itself, briskly, the way a bird shakes its wings, ink crawling across the parchment like ants deciding on a new route. And there, in the middle of the chart, where a moment ago there had been only the winding lines of the Gray Marches, a new mark was drawing itself:

A small square. A roofline. A chimney.

The cottage. The map was drawing Ivenna's cottage, stroke by stroke, in ink that gleamed wet and fresh, and beneath it letters were assembling themselves out of the crawling darkness, spidery and precise, in a hand Elodie would have known anywhere — a hand she had watched write a thousand lessons, a thousand recipes, one thirty-crown letter that had bought her freedom and her exile in the same stroke:

*If you have come this far, girl, then the map has chosen better than I dared hope. Come inside. We have very little time, and you have been lied to — though not, I think, in the way you believe.*

Sera read it upside down, and went pale. "Is that—"

"Yes," Elodie said. Her voice shook. The old wound had torn open all the way down, and at the bottom of it, impossibly, a light was burning. "It's her."

The cottage door, unwarded now, unlatched, swung inward on its own — slowly, courteously, the way a host opens a door — onto darkness that smelled of vervain and beeswax and waiting.

Elodie looked at Sera. Sera looked at Elodie. Somewhere down in the village, faintly, a church bell began to ring — not the hour, not the quarter, but the alarm pattern, over and over, urgent as a struck nerve.

Strangers in Alderbrook. Riders on the road. Someone had seen them.

"We have minutes," Sera said.

"Then we spend them inside," Elodie said, and stepped over her teacher's threshold.
