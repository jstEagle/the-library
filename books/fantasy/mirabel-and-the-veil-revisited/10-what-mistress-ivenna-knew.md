## 10. What Mistress Ivenna Knew

The village of Thistlebrook had not changed, which was somehow worse than if it had.

Elodie had expected ruin. She had half hoped for it — a burned-out shell she could walk through without feeling anything, a place the years had done to as thoroughly as they'd done to her. Instead the lane still ran crooked between the same leaning cottages, and the well at the crossroads still wore its crown of moss, and someone's goats still escaped through the same broken fence to stand in the road and stare at travelers with the flat insolence of their kind. Smoke rose from chimneys. A woman hung washing between two elms, humming. Children chased a hoop down toward the millpond, shrieking with the particular joy of children who did not know yet what it cost to hide.

Seven years ago Elodie had run from this place with a blistered heel and a stolen loaf and the certain knowledge that she would never come back. Now she came back with a fugitive's hood drawn low, a dead man's map pressed warm against her ribs, and three strangers who watched the lane like wolves watching a farmyard.

"We shouldn't stop," Sera said. It was the first thing she'd said since dawn that wasn't an accusation, and Elodie almost missed the accusations. "A village is eyes. Eyes are risk."

"Then you three can wait in the trees." Elodie didn't break stride. Her heart was hammering against the map like a second pulse, but her feet knew this lane the way her hands knew knotwork — without being asked. "I need something from the house on the hill. Ten minutes."

Tam Vess fell into step beside her, spectacles fogging in the morning damp, and peered up at the hill with the expression of a man reading a title page. "That cottage. The one with the rowan tree. Whose is it?"

"No one's. Not anymore." Elodie heard her own voice go flat and was distantly grateful for it. Flat was safe. Flat was seven years of practice. "It belonged to my mistress. Ivenna Underhill."

*Underhill.* Tam's eyebrows climbed above his spectacles. He opened his mouth — she could see the question assembling itself behind his eyes, all his cartographer's hunger for names and lines and connections — and then, to his credit, he closed it again. He only said, quietly: "Rowan trees keep off ill-wishers. Or so the old stories say."

"The old stories say a great many things." Elodie lengthened her stride. Behind them, Brannoc drifted back into the shadows of the hedgerow without being told, and Sera followed him after one last suspicious look at the village, arms crossed over her father's knife.

The path up the hill was overgrown but not abandoned. That was the first wrongness. Ferns had swallowed the stepping-stones, brambles had claimed the gate, and the rowan tree had grown vast and heavy-berried, its branches combing the thatch — but the garden wall stood whole, and the herb beds beneath their winter mulch were laid out in rows too neat for neglect. Someone had kept this place. Someone had meant to return.

Or someone had never left at all.

Elodie stopped at the gate with her hand on the rotten latch and made herself look at the cottage properly for the first time in seven years. It was smaller than memory had made it. Memory always built monuments out of the places that hurt us; standing here, she saw it was just a house, stone-footed and turf-roofed, its door painted the deep blue-green of beetle wings. The blue paint was flaking now. Everything else was exactly as she'd left it, down to the iron hare on the doorstep that Ivenna had made her polish every market day until she hated it.

"Ivenna?" Her voice came out small, swallowed by the garden. She cleared her throat and tried again, louder, hating herself for the hope in it. "Mistress Ivenna?"

Nothing answered but the wind in the rowan, and under the wind, faint and rhythmic, the map's warmth against her ribs — *tap, tap, tap* — like a finger tapping on glass. Like impatience. Like recognition.

She pushed the door open.

Dust lay thick on everything, silver-gray, undisturbed except by mice. The single room opened before her exactly as it had lived in her memory: the hearth swept clean, the kettle still hanging on its hook, the bundle of dried herbs above the lintel gone gray and weightless with age. Two chairs by the fire. The narrow bed in the alcove. And everywhere, everywhere, the smell — rosemary and woodsmoke and something underneath both, something green and bitter that Elodie's body remembered before her mind did and that made her eyes sting without permission.

Ivenna was not there. But Ivenna was *everywhere*, and Elodie stood in the doorway like a girl of thirteen again, waiting to be scolded for muddy boots.

"She's dead," Elodie told the empty room. Her voice sounded absurdly loud. "You know that, don't you? Whatever you are. Whatever you want. She's been dead or gone for seven years, and I'm the only one left carrying any of this, and I am *tired*."

The map tapped against her ribs. Patient. Insistent.

"All right," she said, to the map, to the room, to the ghost of a woman who had taught her everything worth knowing and then let her run away with nothing. "All right. What did you want me to find?"

* * *

She found the journals on the third day of searching, though it felt like the third year.

Not because the cottage was large — it was one room and a loft — but because she couldn't make herself search quickly. Every drawer she opened held a small ambush. The bone needles sorted by size. The tin of beeswax Ivenna rendered herself, still bearing the thumbprint pressed into its surface. The chipped blue cup that had been Elodie's, set apart from the others on its own shelf, kept, kept for seven years, kept as if its owner might come home and want tea.

Elodie sat on the floor with that cup in her hands for a long time and did not cry, and counted it a victory that she did not cry, and understood even as she counted it that this was a strange kind of arithmetic.

Brannoc came once, at dusk, stood in the doorway without entering, and looked at her sitting among the open drawers like a woman kneeling in a field of small graves. He didn't ask questions. He set down bread and a skin of water and a bundle of candles, and he said, "There's a dry loft. Cold up there, but dry," and left her to it. She was beginning to understand that this was how Brannoc Hale loved people: by making sure they had candles.

Sera did not come at all. Tam came twice, ostensibly to consult his notes, actually, Elodie suspected, to make sure she hadn't been turned into a newt or carried off by whatever dwelt in old witches' houses. On his second visit he lingered by the hearth, frowning at the chimney breast, and said, "These marks. The ones scored into the stone under the plaster. Do you know what they are?"

"Knot-marks. Warden-work." She said it before she thought, then froze. "I mean — I've seen similar. In books."

"Mm." Tam looked at her over his spectacles with terrible gentleness. "In books. Of course." He gathered his notes and left her alone with her lies, and the lies sat in her stomach like stones.

On the third day, having searched everywhere else, she finally climbed to the loft.

The ladder creaked under her weight the way it always had. The loft smelled of dust and stored apples gone soft in their straw. And there, in the corner where a girl had once been forbidden to go — *that corner is mine, Elodie, and you will respect it or you will find other lodging* — there was a chest.

Iron-banded. Lockless. As if Ivenna had known, all those years, that the only lock that mattered was a child's obedience, and had gambled seven years of secrets on it holding.

Elodie knelt before it for a long moment. Then she laughed — one short, ugly sound with no humor in it — because of course. Of course the lockless chest. Of course the forbidden corner. Ivenna had never needed chains. She'd had something better. She'd had the way Elodie was made.

She opened it.

Clothes first, folded with military precision: dresses in Ivenna's style, cut for a younger woman, cut — Elodie realized with a lurch — for *her*. For a grown Elodie. Dresses put away against a future in which a girl came home needing clothes that fit. Beneath them, letters tied in ribbon gone brittle. Beneath the letters, wrapped in oilcloth, a bundle of journals. Eleven of them. Leather-bound, hand-sewn, their spines cracked with use. The earliest was older than Elodie.

And on top of the whole pile, placed deliberately, face-up, as if set there yesterday, as if Ivenna had climbed this ladder on her last day in the world and left it where only a returning Elodie would find it — a single sheet of paper, covered in her mistress's sharp, slanted hand:

*If you are reading this, you have finally disobeyed me about something important. Good. Sit down first.*

Elodie sat down in the dusty loft with the apples rotting softly around her, and read.

* * *

*I never wanted you to be a hedge-witch.*

The handwriting was steady at first, the hand of a woman writing by good lamplight with time to spare.

*I took you as an apprentice because you had the talent, yes, but mostly because I knew your name. Underhill. I knew what that name used to mean, and I spent your entire childhood praying it had come to mean nothing at all. Every charm I taught you to fake, every trick of sleight and misdirection, every lesson in looking mediocre — understand, child, I was not teaching you to be less than you are. I was teaching you to be nothing. There is a difference, and it is the difference between living and dying, and I learned it too late to save anyone but myself.*

*Your mother wrote to me the last time eleven years ago. I keep her letters below. Read them or don't. But know this: the Underhills were not farmers who wandered. They were Wardens. Veilwardens, the last line of them, scattered and hunted these hundred years past, and every one of them died within sight of the border or on the road to it. Your grandmother. Your grandfather. Your mother, who deserved better than running, and your father, who never understood what he'd married and loved her anyway, which may be the bravest thing any Underhill has ever done.*

*They broke the line, Elodie. The witch-finders broke it, and the Crown paid them to, and the courts of Mirabel helped from the far side because a broken Warden-line serves everyone who wants the Veil weak. I hid what you were because hiding was the only spell left that worked. I let you believe your gift was a small shameful thing because a small shameful thing survives. A Veilwarden does not. Not alone. Not untrained. Not in a world with General Corvess's kind in it.*

*You will be angry with me. You should be. Be angry, and then read the journals, because anger is fuel and you will need every drop of it for what's coming. The map has found you — I felt it moving these last years, like weather changing. If it chose you, then the Veil is failing faster than I feared, and no amount of mediocrity will save you now. Nothing I taught you will be enough. But it will be enough to reach the border alive, if you're clever, and you are clever, and stubborn, which is better.*

*I am sorry. I am sorry for every lie, and I would tell every one of them again. That is the truth of loving someone in dangerous times: you become a liar, and you pray they forgive you, and you die not knowing.*

*Burn this after reading. (You won't. You never listened.)*

*Ivenna*

Elodie read it twice. Three times. The loft grew dark around her and she did not light the candles Brannoc had brought, because lighting them required hands that were not shaking.

Veilwardens. The word went through her like cold water through cloth. Tam had spoken it in whispers, reverent and horrified — *a Veilwarden chart, thought destroyed in the last war* — and she had thought it history, distant and dead, a story about other people. Not a story about a woman named Underhill. Not a story about her mother, whom she remembered only as laughter and flour-dusted hands and a habit of humming to bread while it rose. Her mother had *written letters*. Had written to Ivenna. Had known what Elodie was and — what? Chosen not to tell her? Died before she could?

Anger came up in her like floodwater, sudden and total, and she welcomed it, because the alternative underneath the anger was grief, and grief in this loft, in this house, alone, would drown her.

*She knew.* The thought struck sparks. *She knew what I was and she taught me to hide it. Seven years I starved in Corvane selling luck-charms to gullible merchants, terrified of my own blood, and she knew. She let me run. She let me believe I was nothing.*

But even as the fury crested, another voice inside her — Ivenna's voice, infuriatingly, speaking in memory with the exact cadence of the letter — said: *and where would you be otherwise? Dead on a border road at nineteen, like your grandmother. Burned in a market square, like the village Brannoc—* 

Elodie pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes.

Betrayal reframed itself, slowly, agonizingly, the way a dislocated joint reframes when it grinds back into place: it hurt more going right than it had going wrong. Because it wasn't betrayal, was it. It was protection. It was a woman spending seven years letting a child hate her rather than let a child die, and dying — or vanishing — without ever knowing whether the lie had held. And Elodie could not decide which was worse: that Ivenna had deceived her, or that Ivenna had been right to.

"You should have told me," she said aloud to the dark loft, and her voice cracked on it. "You should have told me, and I would have hated you, and at least I'd have hated you knowing why."

The map, against her ribs, pulsed once. Warm. Almost gentle. Almost like agreement.

"Don't you dare take her side."

* * *

She came down near midnight and found the company gathered in the cottage's main room, because of course they were; she'd heard them murmuring through the floorboards for hours and had been too proud to acknowledge it. Tam sat by the hearth with the last of the candles, surrounded by his notes. Sera stood by the window with her arms crossed, keeping watch on a village that posed no threat, because keeping watch was the only prayer she knew. Brannoc had found Ivenna's woodpile and was feeding the dead hearth back to life with the methodical tenderness of a man building a fire for someone else's comfort.

They all looked up when she came down the ladder with the journals clutched to her chest and Ivenna's letter crushed in her fist.

"Well?" Sera said. "What did the witch leave you?"

Elodie opened her mouth to say *nothing*, because saying nothing was seven years of habit, and then she looked at the three of them — the scholar who'd seen through every lie she'd told, the soldier who brought her candles, the grieving girl keeping watch over strangers — and she was so tired. She was so tired of being nothing.

"My family," she said. Her voice came out rough. "My family were Veilwardens. All of them. My mother. Her mother before her. Back a hundred years, back to the last war." She held up the journals. "Ivenna knew. She knew my whole life, and she hid it, and she trained me to hide it harder, and then she disappeared and left me a letter telling me to be angry about it."

Silence. Tam removed his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, which Elodie was learning meant his mind was moving too fast for his face.

"That explains," he said carefully, "why the map chose you."

"It doesn't explain why *she didn't tell me*."

"No," Tam agreed. "Though I suspect—" he caught himself, and Elodie, perversely, wanted to shake him. "I suspect the journals explain that. May I?"

He held out his hands for the oldest volume, and after a moment Elodie gave it to him, because her arms were aching and because some traitorous part of her wanted the words read aloud, wanted them witnessed, wanted them to stop being a secret she carried alone in a dark loft.

Tam read by candlelight, lips moving, and the fire Brannoc had built crackled, and outside the rowan tree combed the thatch with a sound like slow rain. Sera drifted closer despite herself. Even Brannoc paused in his feeding of the fire.

"Here." Tam's finger stopped on a page. His voice changed — flattened, the scholar's voice, the voice that forgot to be gentle because the facts were too loud. "'The anchor weakens. The Warden-line thins with each generation — the Crown sees to that, burning us faster than we breed. I have written to the last families. Fewer answers each year.'" He looked up. "This is dated thirty-one years ago. This is your grandmother's era."

"Keep reading."

"'A courier came through the Marches,' " Tam read, and Sera went very still by the window. "'He carried a chart I had thought destroyed with the rest. He would not say who sent him. He asked for shelter and salt and moved on before dawn. The chart is real. The anchor is real. And the line that must renew it is nearly ended.'"

Sera crossed the room in four strides and took the journal out of Tam's hands — no apology, no permission, just took it — and read the passage herself, twice, her lips shaping the words. When she looked up, her eyes were wet and furious.

"A courier," she said. "Thirty years ago. Through the Marches." Her voice shook. "That was my father. That was — he was young then. He told me once he'd carried something across the border country for a woman he never named, and I thought it was a lover's trinket, I thought—" She stopped. Pressed the back of her hand hard against her mouth. When she lowered it, the grief had rearranged itself into something harder and more useful. "He wasn't a thief. He wasn't even a smuggler. He was carrying *this* — your family's burden — and someone killed him for it, and I have been hunting the wrong answer for a month."

"Sera—"

"Don't." She thrust the journal back at Tam and turned to the window, shoulders rigid. "Don't comfort me. I'm working."

But her voice had cracked down the middle, and Elodie saw her wipe her face with a savage, angry motion, and understood — with a pang that had nothing to do with Ivenna — that they were all, every one of them, only now learning what their dead had actually been doing all along.

Tam had returned to the journals, turning pages faster now, hunting. "There's more. There has to be more — a Warden-line leaves records, rites, names of the—here." He went white. He actually went white, the color draining out of his face so fast that Brannoc stepped toward him instinctively.

"What?" Elodie said. "Tam. What is it?"

He turned the journal around so the candlelight fell across the page. The entry was short. The handwriting had deteriorated, hurried, the letters of a woman writing fast by bad light with fear in the room.

*"They have a name now. The witch-finders' general — Corvess — his grandfather signed the order that broke the Warden-line, and the son keeps the ledger. They are not hunting magic anymore. They are hunting the map. And they have a tracker attuned to it — blood-taken, God help us all, from the last Warden they burned. Which means, child, wherever you carry that thing, they can follow you like a wound bleeds."*

The fire popped. No one spoke.

Elodie stood very still in her dead mentor's kitchen, in the house that had been kept ready for her for seven years, holding eleven volumes of a dead woman's confession, and felt the map pulse against her ribs — *tap, tap, tap* — patient, insistent, pointing, as it had been pointing for days now, west. Toward the Marches. Toward the border. Toward the thin place where the Veil frayed like old rope.

Toward the thing that was hunting them, already on the road behind.

"They know where we are," she said quietly. "They've known since Corvane. Since the moment it chose me." She looked around the circle of firelight at the scholar, the soldier, the avenger — her company, her improbable, unwilling family — and heard herself say the words she'd sworn seven years ago never to say again. "We leave at first light. Before that tracker gets close enough to taste the map. We go west."

"And when we get there?" Sera asked, without turning from the window. "To the border. To the Veil. What then?"

Elodie looked down at the journal in her hands — at her grandmother's fear, her mother's letters, Ivenna's careful, cowardly, devoted lies — and felt the full weight of the inheritance settle onto her shoulders like a yoke fitted to her exact measure.

"Then," she said, "I find out what I actually am. And I find out who killed your father. Because I don't think those are two questions anymore."

Outside, far off in the western dark, something howled — long and low and wrong, a sound no Thistlebrook wolf had ever made — and the map burned hot against her ribs, and the rowan tree shivered though there was no wind.

First light was four hours away. None of them slept.
