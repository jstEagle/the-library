## 11. The Fae Exile

The inn at Grayhollow had a hearth that smoked, ale that tasted of regret, and a landlord who asked no questions — which was why Rue had been drinking there for three nights running, and why he intended to drink there for three more.

He sat in the corner with his back to the wall, as he always did, though the habit was older than walls. He wore the shape of a man: broad-shouldered, gray at the temples, with the kind of face that bartenders forgot by morning. It was good work, the shape. He had worn it for eleven years in this particular configuration, and before that other configurations, and if you added them all up you would arrive at a number that would make the landlord's ale curdle in its cask.

Rue did not add them up anymore. Counting years was a homesickness all its own.

He turned his cup slowly on the scarred wood, watching the firelight swim in the dregs, and let his senses stretch out past the smoke-stained rafters, past the frozen lanes of Grayhollow, past the human world's dull gray skin, to the thing beneath it all.

The Veil.

Most humans could not feel it. Most humans could walk their whole lives over the seam of two worlds and never once feel the stitch-work pulling at their soles. But Rue had been born on the far side of that seam, in a city where the seasons were kept like rooms in a great house and the moon came indoors when it pleased, and even exiled, even flesh-bound, even wearing this tired borrowed body — he could feel the Veil the way a man feels an old wound when the weather turns.

And lately, the wound had been turning.

* * *

It had started, properly, in the autumn. A warmth in the seam, south and east of him, faint as a voice through a wall. Then a flicker — something torn and hastily mended, the magical equivalent of a finger pressed over a hole in a dam. Rue had felt that mending from forty miles away and had sat down very suddenly on a milestone, because he knew what blood could do that, and there was supposed to be no blood left that could do it.

Veilwarden blood. The line broken, scattered, hunted to ribbons in the last war — he had *watched* it hunted, in the years when he still crossed freely, when he still told himself he was a scholar of borders rather than a traitor to one. He had stood in the ashes of the Underhill holding and felt the last thread of that great lineage go slack, and he had thought: *well. That is that. The door stays shut, and I stay shut out, forever.*

Forever, it turned out, was a negotiable term.

Now there was a bearer again. A new bearer, untrained, frightened, moving — he closed his eyes and read the map's passage across the land like reading weather — moving north and west. Toward the border. Toward the Gray Marches. Toward, eventually, whether she knew it or not, the anchor. And beyond the anchor, though she could not possibly know this either, the hidden gates, and beyond the gates—

Home.

Rue opened his eyes and found the landlord hovering at his elbow with the pitcher, giving him the wary look men gave strangers who smiled at nothing.

"Another?" the landlord asked.

"No." Rue slid a coin across the table — a coin he had made himself, silver, correct in every particular except that it would be a dead leaf by dawn. "You've been generous."

"I haven't," said the landlord, pocketing it anyway.

"No," Rue agreed pleasantly, "you haven't. But you will remember me fondly regardless. Isn't memory a marvelous thing? So *improvable*."

The landlord frowned, opened his mouth, closed it, and wandered back toward the bar wearing the expression of a man who had just mislaid a thought and suspected the stranger had picked it up off the floor.

Rue finished his ale. Outside, snow was beginning again, fat and unhurried, the kind of snow that meant to stay the week. He pulled his coat — wool, human-made, itching — around his shoulders and stepped out into the blue dusk of Grayhollow's single street.

The cold bit him the way it always did. Fae flesh remembered warmth the way widows remembered voices; every winter since his exile, the cold had felt less like weather and more like a message. *You do not belong here. You are a guest in a house that has never lit a fire for you.*

He walked to the edge of the village, where the fields gave up and the Marches began, and stood in the falling snow looking north.

* * *

People — humans, anyway — imagined exile as a door closing. A dramatic moment, a shouted sentence, a road leading away. Rue had learned that exile was not a door but a direction. You were exiled the way a river was exiled: continuously, at every moment, by the simple fact that everything sloped away from where you wanted to be.

His treason had been elegant, at least. He would grant himself that much. In a court that prized elegance above honesty, above mercy, above sense, he had committed the most elegant crime imaginable: he had told the truth, in public, with citations.

*The Veil is killing us,* he had said, standing in the Winter Court's hall while the chandeliers driched light like melting icicles and Lady Ashvine watched him from beneath her crown of black glass. *Slowly, but surely. We sealed ourselves away from the human world to escape their wars, and we are dying of the cure. Our magic thins. Our children come fewer and smaller. The anchor fails because no hand tends it from our side, and we have outlawed tending it. I propose we open the Veil. Carefully. Under law. Before it tears open without us.*

He had expected argument. He had received, instead, silence — that particular Mirabel silence, which is not the absence of sound but the presence of judgment — and then Ashvine's voice, soft as frost forming, saying the word *traitor* as though she were pronouncing a diagnosis.

They had not killed him. Killing was crude, and besides, Rue suspected, they had wanted him to suffer the way only a fae can suffer: by living. They had stripped his true shape and bound him into mortality's coarse garment, and they had put him over the border with nothing but the clothes he wore and a lifetime — a *human* lifetime, or near enough — to consider his errors.

Forty-one years ago. He had spent forty-one years considering his errors, and had arrived, after long and rigorous study, at the conclusion that he had been right.

It was the most bitter joke of his existence: exile had not cured him of his treason. It had refined it. Distilled it. What began as a scholar's proposal had aged, in the dark cask of four decades, into something with more teeth. He no longer merely wished the Veil opened under law, carefully, with councils and treaties and the slow machinery of diplomacy.

He wished it open. Full stop. And if the courts of two worlds lacked the wisdom to open it gently, then let it be opened roughly, and let both worlds learn what their cowardice cost. He was not proud of the wish. But he had stopped pretending it wasn't his.

All he needed was the map.

* * *

The map. He turned the thought over as he walked out into the Marches' edge, where the snow fell strangely — sometimes upward, sometimes pausing mid-air as though consulting itself. Even here, at the thin outer hem of the blight, the world forgot its manners.

A Veilwarden chart. Not paper that showed the Veil, but paper that *was* the Veil, in miniature, in ink — a living document, self-updating, keyed to the anchor itself. The last war had supposedly ended with its destruction. Corvess's ancestors had burned the Warden houses and smashed the charts and called it peace. And yet here, now, decades into his exile, the thing moved through the human world again like a rumor with legs, carried by a dying courier, and had chosen — *chosen*, the arrogance of it, the old arrogant magic of it — a new bearer.

Rue had chased rumors of it for thirty years. He had bribed hedge-witches and haunted archives and once, memorably, spent a season as a crow on the roof of the Royal Cartographic Office listening to Tam Vess lecture junior clerks about impossible geography. He had gotten close twice. Both times the trail had gone cold with a finality that smelled of witch-finders.

But now. Now the bearer lived, and breathed, and *moved*, and he could feel her the way he felt the Veil — a second pulse laid over the first, small and mortal and stubborn, dragging herself northwest through the winter with companions he could dimly distinguish. A mind like a knife wrapped in cloth — the scholar, surely; Vess had finally stopped talking and started doing. A heavy, guilty silence walking beside her — the soldier-shape of a man who had burned something he loved. And a third, sharp with grief, grief honed to an edge you could shave with.

And underneath them all, growing stronger by the day, the bearer herself. Elodie Underhill. He knew the name now; names traveled oddly along the Veil, arriving before their owners like servants sent ahead to book rooms. Underhill. Of course it was an Underhill. The universe had a taste for symmetry that Rue, in his darker hours, found frankly theatrical.

She was going to the anchor. She might not know it yet, but the map knew, and the map was patient, and maps always won arguments with their bearers eventually. He had seen it happen. He had *been* it happen, once, long ago, in another life, when he'd held a Warden chart in his own hands and felt it whisper to him of doors.

The memory rose unbidden: a library in Mirabel, candles that burned blue, a chart spread across a table, its ink crawling like living things, and his own young voice asking the archivist, *what does it feel like, to hold it?* And the archivist — old Vael, dry as pressed flowers — answering: *like being trusted with something too large for your hands.*

Rue stood in the snow at the edge of the human world and ached.

That was the truth of exile no one warned you about: not the hunger, not the cold, not the shrinking mortal span. The ache. Homesickness was a misnomer; it suggested a sickness, something to recover from. This was not an illness. It was an amputation. Mirabel was not a place he missed. It was a place he *was*, and he went on living daily in the wrong geography, a soul wearing the wrong world like a coat cut for someone else.

He could almost smell it from here, on nights when the Veil ran thin. Rain that fell upward. Streets that rearranged themselves out of politeness. The court's music, heard from the wrong side of the border, sounding like weeping.

*Soon,* he told the ache, as he had told it for forty-one years. *Soon, soon, soon.* The word had worn smooth as a river stone from handling.

* * *

There was a complication, of course. There were always complications; the world seemed constitutionally incapable of letting Rue have anything cleanly.

The witch-finders had a tracker. He had felt it two nights past — a cold, probing attention sweeping along the Veil's seam like a lantern passed over a wall, searching for the map's heat. Human craft, but fed by something older; somewhere in Corvess's machine there was a relic of the last war, a splinter of the anchor perhaps, or worse, a captured Warden artifact turned against its own purpose. The General was not the fool his reputation suggested. The fool believed in nothing; Corvess believed in exactly one terrible thing, entirely, and organized his whole life around it.

And Lady Ashvine would have felt the bearer too. The court's regent was many things — vain, brilliant, afraid in ways she would flay anyone who named — but she was not inattentive. If the map moved, Ashvine moved. She would want it sealed away, locked in some vault beneath the Hollow Crown, the Veil preserved in its elegant decay until Mirabel suffocated politely behind it. She would send knights. She might, if she were feeling sentimental, send something worse.

So: three players converging on one frightened hedge-witch in the snow. The witch-finders who wanted the map destroyed. The court that wanted it entombed. And Rue, who wanted it *used*.

He smiled at the empty fields, and the smile had nothing warm in it, though it was beautifully made — everything about him was beautifully made; it was practically the only inheritance exile hadn't touched.

* * *

He found the crossroads shrine an hour past dark, half-buried in drifts, a squat stone thing with a bowl for offerings and a saint's face worn featureless by weather. Humans built these along the old roads without knowing why; their grandparents had built them where the Veil ran close, little anchors of attention, prayers doing the work of wardens. Superstition was just forgotten engineering.

Rue brushed the snow from the bowl and set his palm flat on the stone.

It was a crude thing, what he did next — a cantrip, barely, the magical equivalent of whistling down a well — but sound carried strangely along the seam, and the bearer was close enough now, a day's walk, perhaps two, that he could reach her edges. He let a thread of himself run out along the Veil, thin as spider silk, and touched the map's awareness with the lightest possible pressure. Not a challenge. Not a claim. An introduction.

*I know what you carry,* he said, in the language that is not language. *I know what you are. And I know three roads meet ahead of you, little warden, and only one of them is mine.*

Somewhere north and west, he felt the map stir against its bearer's back like a sleeping animal hearing its name. Felt her startle — a spike of fear, bright and human and deliciously honest. Good. Fear kept mortals alive. Fear had kept *him* alive through forty-one winters of borrowed flesh.

He withdrew the thread, and the night closed back over the fields like water over a dropped stone.

Then Rue turned his collar up, settled his human shoulders into their human stoop, and began to walk north through the snow, following a pulse of waking magic toward a woman who did not yet know she was a door — past the shrine, past the last lamp of Grayhollow, out onto the road where, at the turn of the mile, he saw, far ahead, four small figures moving through the storm with the desperate steadiness of people who have stopped believing in safety.

And behind *them*, at the horizon's southern edge, a red glow that was not any dawn — torchlight, many torches, riding the same road.

Rue quickened his pace, and began, softly, to hum a tune no human ear had heard in forty-one years: a court song of Mirabel, about a traveler coming home.
