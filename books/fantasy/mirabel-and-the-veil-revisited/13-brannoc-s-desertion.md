## 13. Brannoc's Desertion

The fire had burned down to coals by the time Brannoc finally spoke, and he was glad of it. Embers were easier to face than eyes.

They had made camp in the hollow beneath a fallen oak, deep in the Gray Marches where the trees grew wrong — spiraled, their bark whorled like fingerprints pressed into wood by some enormous, patient thumb. The map lay folded in Elodie's pack, and even from across the fire it seemed to radiate a faint warmth, the way a hearthstone holds the day's heat long after the flames die. He'd been watching her guard it for three days now. Watching all of them circle it like moths pretending they weren't moths.

The scholar, Tam, sat sharpening a pencil stub with a knife that had seen better decades. Sera had taken first watch and stood at the lip of the hollow with her father's sword across her knees, silhouetted against a sky the color of old pewter. And Elodie — the map-bearer, the hedge-witch who flinched from her own shadow — sat closest to him, mending a strap on her boot with small, competent hands.

He'd been putting this off since the ford. Since he'd stepped out of the reeds with a soldier's blade and put down four witch-finders without breaking stride, and seen the question bloom on every one of their faces: *who is this man, and why does he fight like someone settling a debt?*

"You've been waiting to ask," he said to Elodie, not looking up. "All of you have. Three days of polite silence. It's worse than questions."

Tam's knife stopped moving. Sera's head turned a fraction, though she kept her eyes on the dark.

Elodie tied off her knot. "We assumed you'd tell us when you wanted to."

"That's the trouble with you witch-folk." He almost smiled. "Patient as graves."

* * *

He'd been twenty-two years old when they gave him the torch.

That was the detail that always came back first, the one his mind returned to the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth: not the village, not the screaming, but the torch itself — pitch-soaked burlap wrapped around an iron ring, heavy as a sleeping child, handed to him by Captain Dree with the same casual efficiency she'd use to hand him a dispatch or a bowl of stew.

*"Sergeant Hale. Your squad takes the east road through Hollowmere. There's a settlement there. You'll know what to do when you see it."*

He'd known what to do. That was the horror of it. He'd known exactly what to do, because he'd been trained to know, drilled until the knowing lived below thought, below conscience, in the marrow where soldiers keep the things they can't afford to examine.

Hollowmere wasn't on any map worth trusting. A scatter of cottages around a mossy millpond, tucked into a fold of hills two days beyond the last royal road. He remembered the smell of it — woodsmoke and wet wool and bread baking somewhere, because it was morning, gods help them, it was a bright cold morning and someone in that village was baking bread.

Captain Dree rode behind them with the witch-finders' contingent, six of them in their gray-and-crimson livery, and one of those six carried a case of instruments that clinked when he walked. Brannoc had never learned the instrument-man's name. He'd made sure never to learn it. The man had walked the length of Hollowmere's single street holding a forked rod of pale wood, and the rod had dipped and shivered at door after door after door, and each time it dipped, a witch-finder chalked a mark on the lintel.

Brannoc had counted the marks. Forty-one doors. Forty-one marks out of forty-six houses.

"Half the Crown," Captain Dree had said mildly, "would call that a nest of vipers."

And Brannoc, twenty-two years old and good at following orders, had said nothing, which was its own kind of answer.

It was the children that undid him. Not the burning — though the burning was bad enough, bad in ways he would spend the rest of his life failing to describe to anyone who hadn't stood in the smoke. But he'd seen burnings before, or the aftermath of them; the Crown's war on magic was old and thorough and he'd been its instrument since he was sixteen. What undid him was that the people of Hollowmere didn't run. They stood in their doorways beside the chalked marks, mothers and grandfathers and a miller with flour still white on his forearms, and they watched the soldiers come with the resigned faces of people who had always known this day would come and had stayed anyway, because staying was what their kind did, had done for generations, hiding small gifts under small lives — a charm against fever, a word that made cows calm, a grandmother who could find lost things — generation after generation, tending their little magics like candles cupped against wind.

And the children. There was a girl, seven or eight, standing in front of one of the marked doors with her arms spread wide, as if eight years of arms could stop a squad of the king's own. She had flour on her too. She'd been helping bake the bread.

Brannoc looked at her, and then at the torch in his hand, and something in him — something that had been bending for years under the weight of things unexamined — broke clean through.

"I'll need a moment," he'd told Captain Dree, and his voice had come out steady, which surprised him. "The wind's wrong for firing the east row. I want to check the direction."

Dree had nodded, bored, already turning to give the order elsewhere. And Brannoc Hale had walked to the end of the street, past the girl with her arms spread, close enough to see her eyes — brown, furious, terrified — and he had dropped the torch in the millpond and kept walking, out the far side of the village, up through the hills, and he had not stopped walking for two days.

Behind him, he knew, the fires went up anyway. Another sergeant took the torch. The Crown did not require Brannoc Hale specifically; it required only a hand, any hand, and there were always more hands.

But the girl had seen him drop it. He liked to believe she had. In the worst nights, in the years after, when sleep wouldn't come and the smoke smell rose in him like floodwater, he held onto that: that somewhere in the ashes of Hollowmere there was a child who had watched one soldier refuse, and might carry that forward instead of the flames.

He had no way of knowing if it was true. He carried it anyway. It was the only thing he'd saved from that morning, and he carried it the way other men carried medals.

* * *

The fire crackled. Somewhere out in the spiraled dark, a bird called — backward, wrong, its song running in reverse like water climbing a hill. Sera shifted at her watch.

"So," Brannoc said, staring into the coals. "You know how I deserted. Now you know why. What I haven't told you is the part that matters to you. To her." He tipped his chin toward Elodie, toward the pack with the map.

Elodie had gone very still. Tam had set down the knife entirely.

"The night I left Hollowmere," Brannoc said, "I didn't just walk away. I went back. Not to the village — there was nothing left to go back for. I went to the witch-finders' camp. While they were busy with their bonfire, I went through the instrument-man's cases."

"Why?" Sera asked from the edge of the light. Her voice was flat, but he'd learned in three days of travel that flatness was how she handled anything that hurt.

"Because I wanted to know how they found them. Forty-one doors out of forty-six. No village hides that badly — half those charms were generations old, buried so deep the folk themselves barely knew what they were carrying. Something found them. Something precise." He turned his hands over, palms up, as if the answer might still be written there. "What I found was worse than a dowsing rod."

Tam leaned forward. The disgraced cartographer had the look of a man who could not stop himself from being interested in terrible things. "What kind of worse?"

"There was a box. Iron-bound, warded — I felt it before I touched it, like putting your hand near an open oven. Inside was a piece of parchment. Old. Covered in ink that moved." He heard Elodie's breath catch. "I didn't understand it then. I understand it now, having traveled with you lot for three days and watched that thing in your pack crawl around like a living creature every time you unwrap it. The witch-finders have a fragment. A torn piece of a chart — the same kind of chart as yours, Elodie Underhill. Same ink. Same... hunger."

"That's impossible," Tam said, but his voice said he feared very much that it wasn't. "The Veilwarden charts were destroyed. All of them. After the last war, the Crown made a ceremony of it — public burning, witnesses, the whole theater of it—"

"They burned copies," Brannoc said. "Or they burned blank paper and called it charts, and everyone cheered because cheering was safer than asking. One fragment survived. Maybe more than one. And the fragment does what your map does — it reaches. It pulls. Only theirs is tuned like a hound on a leash, and the leash is held by the Witch-Finder General himself."

The name landed in the firelight like a stone in a well. Even Sera came away from her watch to sit closer.

"Corvess," Tam said quietly.

"You know him?"

"Every scholar knows the name. Corvess the Pious. Corvess the Thorough. He wrote the definitive text on the detection of residual enchantment — we used it at the Royal Academy, before I was expelled for suggesting Mirabel existed." Tam's laugh was short and had no humor in it. "I quoted that man for ten years. Gods. I quoted him while he was hunting my life's work with a piece of its own corpse."

Brannoc shook his head. "Not a piece of its corpse. A piece of its *body*. That's what I've come to understand, watching yours. These charts aren't documents. They're organs. Cut from something that was once whole and alive, and each piece still remembers being part of the whole, and each piece wants to be whole again." He looked at Elodie directly. "Yours has chosen you. Theirs has been chained. And right now, General Corvess is holding a compass needle that points at whatever remains of the Veilwarden line — and you are the only remnant left."

Elodie's hands had gone to her arms, wrapping herself, the gesture of a woman feeling suddenly cold. "How long," she said. "How long have they been able to follow us?"

"Since the city, I'd guess. Maybe since the stranger died in your stairwell. A chart waking after thirty years of sleep makes noise, if you've got the ear for it. And Corvess has spent his whole life learning to listen." Brannoc fed a stick of deadwood to the coals and watched it catch. "I deserted from the Third Crown Rangers eleven months ago. Before that, I served nine years. I know how Corvess moves, how he thinks, how he budgets his men and spends his informants. I know that he doesn't chase. He *herds*. He lets you run, and he closes the roads ahead of you, and he waits for you to tire yourself against the fences he's built. And I know—" he hesitated here, because the next part was the confession under the confession, the thing he'd swallowed even as he told them about Hollowmere— "I know that he offered me a commission. Two months ago. In a tavern in Marlow's Crossing, a gray-cloaked man bought me a drink and told me he knew who I was, knew what I'd seen at Hollowmere, and that the General forgave deserters who brought him something valuable."

The hollow went silent except for the fire.

"He wanted the map," Elodie whispered.

"He wanted the *bearer*," Brannoc corrected. "He said the map was only ink. He said what mattered was the blood that answered it. He said—" and here Brannoc's voice roughened, because he had heard these words spoken gently, reasonably, over a friendly drink, and they had been the most frightening words of his life— "he said the last war started because no one had the courage to finish what was begun. That the Veilwardens weren't protectors. They were the lock on a cage, and everything inside the cage — both sides of it — had forgotten they were caged, and his family had spent three generations trying to throw away the key."

"And you told him no," Sera said.

"I told him no with a table between us and my hand on a knife under it. Then I left through the window, because men like Corvess don't ask twice and don't forget." He met her eyes. "Your father knew that too, I think. Whatever else Vane was — thief, courier, fool — he knew what hunted him, and he ran in the right direction. Toward you." He nodded at Elodie. "Toward the blood."

* * *

For a long moment nobody spoke. The reversed bird called again, closer now, and Sera glanced at the dark with narrowed eyes before returning her attention to him.

It was Tam who broke the quiet, in the way of scholars, by attacking the problem rather than the emotion. "If Corvess has a fragment attuned to the Veilwarden line, then distance means nothing. Speed means little. He isn't tracking the map — he's tracking *you*, Elodie, and the map simply burns brighter the more you use it. Every time you touch it, every time it wakes, you're lighting a beacon."

"Then I'll stop touching it," Elodie said, too quickly.

"And starve it?" Tam shook his head. "You don't yet understand what you're carrying. Neither do I, fully. But I've read enough of the old accounts to know that a chart that has chosen a bearer doesn't take well to neglect. It woke for a reason. It will make itself heard."

"Wonderful," Sera muttered. "So we're chased by an army led by a man with a piece of living map, through a cursed borderland, toward a realm that may or may not still exist, guided by a deserter who waited three days to mention any of this." She looked at Brannoc, and her gaze was the sharpest thing in the hollow. "Why wait? Why tell us now?"

Brannoc considered lying. He was good at it; soldiers mostly were, in the small survivable ways. But he was tired, and the coals were warm, and he had spent eleven months carrying Hollowmere alone and discovering that a secret shared with no one grows heavier, not lighter, with every mile.

"Because at the ford," he said, "when the witch-finders had you surrounded and I could have walked away — should have, by every sensible measure — I didn't. And I asked myself why, and the honest answer is that I've been walking toward something ever since I walked away from that burning village. Away from the Crown, yes. But toward something. I didn't know what." He looked around the fire: the scholar with his ruined career, the daughter with her grief worn like armor, the hedge-witch with a dying godfolded in her pack and a bounty on her blood. "Then I saw the map move. And I understood that everything I've hated for nine years — the burnings, the chalk marks, the rods that dip at doorways — all of it comes from the same wound. The Veil. The broken inheritance. And the woman sitting across this fire is the only person alive who might mend it."

"That's a great deal to put on a stranger," Elodie said quietly.

"I'm not putting it on you. Corvess already did. Ashvine's kind already did. The world put it on you the day you were born Underhill." He shrugged, a soldier's shrug, blunt and unadorned. "I'm only telling you that you won't carry it alone. Not while I'm breathing. That's the whole of my confession, and it's late, and I'll take second watch."

He rose to move toward the edge of the hollow, but Elodie's voice stopped him.

"Sergeant Hale." She used the rank deliberately, he realized — not the deserter, not the drifter, the rank. "The girl at Hollowmere. The one with her arms out. Do you know what happened to her?"

He stood in the ember-light a long moment. "No," he said. "But I've decided to live as if she did. As if she saw one man refuse, and it mattered." He managed something that was almost a smile. "It's thin fuel. But it burns."

She nodded slowly, and something passed between them that needed no more words — the recognition of two people who had both spent years hiding, one behind mediocrity and one behind a mercenary's blank face, and who knew exactly what the hiding cost.

Brannoc took his watch at the lip of the hollow, blade across his knees, facing the dark.

It was near midnight when he saw it — or felt it first, a prickling along his scars, the old burn-sensitivity that had never quite left him. Out in the spiraled trees, far off to the northeast, the horizon glowed. Not dawn. Dawn was hours away, and dawn didn't flicker.

Fire. Distant, vast, methodical. Beacon-fires, lit in a chain, marching across the Marches the way Corvess marched across everything: patiently, in order, closing the roads.

He counted the lights. Seven. Then an eighth bloomed closer, and he was already moving down into the hollow, waking them, saying the words he'd hoped not to say for another week:

"They've built the fence. We're inside it. Move — now — and pray there's one gap left in the world that Corvess hasn't thought to close."
