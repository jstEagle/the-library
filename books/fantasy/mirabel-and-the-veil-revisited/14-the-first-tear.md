## 14. The First Tear

The Gray Marches did not end so much as they thinned.

For two days the company had walked through country where the world seemed to have forgotten its own rules — trees grown in slow spirals like fingers curling inward, streams that ran uphill for a dozen paces before remembering themselves, birds that flew backward with their heads turned toward wherever they had been. The map rode against Elodie's chest in its oilcloth wrapping, and it had grown warmer each hour, until she could feel it through her shirt like a second heartbeat pressed to her own.

She had stopped sleeping well. That was the truth of it, though she told no one. Every time she closed her eyes she felt the ink crawling again, that first night in the stairwell when the map had unfolded itself across her hands and something old and patient had poured up through her blood like water finding a crack in stone. Seven years of careful mediocrity, of selling luck-charms that were only thread and glass and confidence, of keeping herself small enough to slip through Corvane's notice — and all of it undone by a dying man's last gift.

"You're limping," Sera said, falling into step beside her.

"I'm walking."

"You're favoring your left leg and pretending you aren't." Sera's voice was flat as slate, but there was something underneath it now that hadn't been there when she'd first put a knife to Elodie's throat on the road out of Corvane. Not warmth, exactly. Something more like the grudging attention of someone who had decided, provisionally, not to hate you. "There's a stream ahead. Tam will want to stop anyway; he's been squinting at his notes since dawn."

Tam Vess was indeed squinting at his notes, walking with his nose in a battered journal while Brannoc steered him around obstacles by the collar like a nursemaid with a large, absent-minded child. The scholar had filled eleven pages since the Marches began — sketches of spiral trees, measurements of backward birds, long frantic passages about thin places and the historical record of Veilwarden patrols along exactly this route. He wrote the way other men prayed: desperately, and with total disregard for his own safety.

"He's going to walk into a bog," Elodie said.

"He's going to walk into a bog," Sera agreed, and neither of them moved to stop him. It had become a kind of game between them, this shared observation of Tam's self-destruction. It was the closest thing either woman had to friendship, and both of them knew it, and both of them pretended otherwise.

The stream was real at least — cold and quick over gray stones, running in the correct direction, which after two days in the Marches felt almost indecent. Brannoc dropped his pack with a soldier's economy and knelt to fill the waterskins. Tam sat down abruptly on a boulder, still writing. Sera checked the treeline out of habit, her father's short sword loose in its sheath.

Elodie crouched at the water's edge and did not drink.

Because the water was wrong.

Not visibly — at first glance it was only water, clear and ordinary, catching the pale light that filtered through the spiral pines. But when she looked at it properly, the way she had learned to look at things since the map had woken in her blood, she could see that the surface didn't quite match itself. There was a shimmer beneath the shimmer, a faint doubling, as if the stream were flowing through two worlds at slightly different speeds and couldn't decide which one to belong to.

"Tam," she said quietly.

He looked up. He followed her gaze to the water, and Elodie watched the color leave his face by degrees, like a tide going out.

"That's," he said, and stopped, and started again. "That's refraction. No — no, it isn't. Refraction doesn't *lag*." He scrambled off the boulder, nearly dropping the journal, and knelt beside her without touching the water. "The Veil is supposed to sit above the world, Elodie. Above it, like — like a roof. Like a held breath. It shouldn't be *in* the water. It shouldn't be anywhere. It should be between."

"Then why is it in the water?"

"Because somewhere near here," Tam said, very softly, "it isn't holding."

Brannoc had gone still by the waterskins. He didn't understand half of what Tam said about the Veil — Elodie suspected he understood more than he let on — but he understood the tone of a man who has just seen something terrible in the distance. His hand drifted to the axe at his belt, an old habit from a life he claimed to have left behind.

"How near?" Brannoc asked.

Tam looked at the map.

Everyone looked at the map. It hung against Elodie's ribs, warm as a sleeping animal, and she found she didn't want to take it out. Taking it out meant unfolding it, and unfolding it meant letting it look at her, and she was tired of being looked at by an object. But three faces turned toward her — the scholar hungry for knowledge, the soldier braced for violence, the daughter of the dead man watching her with those hard grey eyes that missed nothing — and she sighed, and drew the oilcloth bundle from her shirt.

The map was hot. Not uncomfortably, not yet, but the way a stone is hot that has sat too long in summer sun. She unwrapped it and let it fall open across her palms, and the ink moved.

It always moved now. She had stopped flinching. The lines of coast and road and river crawled like living things, redrawing themselves as the company traveled, and Tam had theorized at length about cartographic enchantment and self-updating survey-work, and Elodie had let him theorize because the alternative was admitting what she privately suspected: that the map wasn't recording the land. It was recording the *Veil*. And the Veil, here, was doing something maps should never have to draw.

Now the ink gathered itself into a knot east of the stream, a dark tangle of lines like a bird's nest made of scratches, and pulsed once.

"There," Sera said. "Northeast. Half a mile, maybe less." She was already standing, already checking the sword. "That's where my father was heading. I've been reading his letters wrong for months, but this—" she gestured at the pulsing blot of ink, "—this is the place he described. He called it a wound."

"A wound in what?" Brannoc asked.

Nobody answered him, because at that moment every bird in the forest went silent at once.

* * *

They went northeast in a loose line, Brannoc leading, because whatever else he was, the deserter had a soldier's instinct for ground and danger and the space between them. The trees grew stranger as they walked — the spirals tightening, the bark going smooth and pale as bone. The light changed too, thickening, taking on a quality Elodie had no name for except that it reminded her of the moment just before a fever breaks, when everything is too bright and too quiet and the air hums against the teeth.

The map grew hotter against her chest until she had to carry it in her hand.

And then the trees simply stopped.

It was not a clearing. Clearings are made by light or fire or the patience of axes. This was an absence — a circle perhaps forty paces across where nothing grew and nothing decayed, where the ground was gray and fine as ash and perfectly, unnaturally smooth. At its center, the air was torn.

There was no better word for it. Elodie had imagined tears in the Veil as gaps, holes, doorways — things with edges you could see. This was none of those. It was a seam of darkness maybe the height of a man and twice as wide, hanging in the air without support, and it did not reflect the light so much as drink it, and looking at it directly made her eyes ache the way staring at the sun does, except inverted, as if she were staring at a sun made of nothing.

"Oh," said Tam, in a small voice. "Oh, it's beautiful."

"It's a hole in the world," said Sera.

"It's a hole in the *wall between* the worlds, and yes, it's beautiful, and both things are true, and I would thank you not to reduce eight hundred years of theoretical cartography to—"

Something came through.

It came through the way smoke comes through a cracked window — sideways, unwillingly, as if even it did not wish to be here. It was roughly the shape of a wolf and roughly the size of a horse and it was wrong in every particular Elodie's eyes tried to fix upon it. Its coat was the grey of the ash underfoot. Its eyes were the gold of the map's oldest lines. Its shadow fell toward the tear instead of away from it, pooling back into the dark like water running uphill, and when it opened its mouth it made no sound at all, and somehow the silence of it was worse than any howl could have been.

Behind it, in the tear, other shapes stirred. Smaller ones. A great many smaller ones.

"Back," Brannoc said. Not loudly. He had drawn the axe without seeming to move, and his voice had the flat calm Elodie had heard exactly once before, on the road out of Corvane, when witch-finders had come out of the trees and he had killed four men in the time it took her to scream. "Slowly. Don't run. Tam — behind me. Sera, flank left, wide. Whatever it is, it's just crossed into a world it doesn't know. Give it a reason to be cautious."

The thing turned its head. The movement was fluid and terrible, like watching oil pour upward, and its gold eyes found Elodie first — not Brannoc with his axe, not Sera with her blade, but Elodie, standing at the edge of the dead circle with a burning map in her hands.

It looked at the map.

Then it made its first sound in this world: a low, grinding note, like a millstone turning, like something vast waking underground. And every shape behind it in the tear surged forward at once.

What happened next happened fast, and Elodie would spend the rest of her life piecing it together from fragments. Brannoc roared — actually roared, a battle-sound with no fear in it — and met the first of the small things as it spilled out of the tear, his axe rising and falling with a terrible economy. Sera's blade took one of them through whatever passed for its neck. Tam was shouting something scholarly and useless and magnificent about the tear's edges, about how it was closing on its own, slowly, how it needed only time—

And the great wolf-thing came for Elodie.

It did not gallop. It flowed, ash-grey and silent, covering twenty paces in the time it took her heart to beat twice, and she had time for exactly one thought — *it wants the map, it wants what I'm carrying, everyone always wants what I'm carrying* — and then it was upon her, and she did the only thing left in the world to do.

She cut her palm on the map's edge and pressed her bleeding hand against the tear.

Later, she would try to describe what happened then, and fail, and try again, and fail differently. The nearest she ever came was this: it was like pressing her hand against a wound in her own body that she had never known she had. The tear recognized her. There was no other word for it. The darkness shuddered at her touch the way flesh flinches, and something rose in her blood to meet it — the same something that had surged awake the night the map unfolded, but a hundredfold greater, a river where before there had been a stream. It poured out of her through the cut in her palm and into the wound in the world, and she felt the Veil itself, for one blinding instant, from the inside: a vast woven thing, older than kingdoms, stretched across the whole breadth of creation, and torn here, and torn here, and fraying here, and *fraying everywhere*, a tapestry coming apart along a thousand seams, and beneath it both worlds pressing against each other like armies leaning on a gate.

And she heard — or felt, or remembered — a single word that was not a word:

*Mend.*

So she mended.

She pulled the edges of the tear together the way her mistress Ivenna had once taught her to close a cut on a sick ewe — gently, firmly, against the animal's panic — and the darkness screamed, a soundless scream she felt in her teeth and bones, and the wolf-thing recoiled from her as if she had become suddenly, unbearably bright. The small things fled backward into the closing dark. The seam drew tight, and tighter, and for one moment she saw through it — truly saw — a sliver of another world: towers like white candles, a sky with two moons, a garden where the seasons stood side by side like rooms in a house —

Mirabel.

Home, said something in her blood, in a voice that was not hers, in a voice that was hers, in a voice older than either.

And then the tear sealed, and the power kept pouring, and would not stop.

* * *

She woke to rain.

Soft rain, gray and ordinary, falling on her face, and the smell of wet ash, and someone's coat draped over her, and voices arguing above her in urgent whispers.

"—shouldn't have moved her—"

"—ash is getting wet, she'll freeze, I don't care what your books say—"

"—her hand. Look at her hand, Tam. Just *look* at it."

Elodie opened her eyes. The sky above her was the Marches' pale ceiling, streaked with cloud. Four faces hovered at the edges of her vision: Tam, spectacles fogged, clutching his journal to his chest like a shield; Brannoc, spattered with something dark that Elodie chose not to identify, his axe still in his fist; Sera, kneeling closest, her grey eyes wide with an expression Elodie had never seen on her before and could not immediately name.

Fear. Sera Vane was afraid of her.

"What happened?" Elodie's voice came out as a croak.

"You sealed it," Tam said. He said it the way a man says *the sun rose in the west*. "You — the tear, the breach, the wound in the Veil — you put your hand on it and it *closed*. Do you understand what I'm telling you? The last recorded sealing was four hundred years ago, at the Battle of the Hinge, and it took seven Veilwardens working in concert, and it killed four of them, and you did it alone, in seconds, with a *cut palm*—"

"Tam." Brannoc's voice was gentle in the way of a man handling something explosive. "Breathe."

But Elodie had stopped listening, because she had lifted her right hand and was looking at it.

The cut on her palm had healed. That alone would have been remarkable enough — hedge-craft could speed a healing, but nothing closed a wound in moments. What held her was the scar it had left: a fine silver line, running from the base of her middle finger across the whole of her palm and up past the wrist, vanishing under her sleeve. When she turned her hand in the gray light, the scar caught it and gleamed, and for a moment she could have sworn the line continued — faintly, impossibly — up her forearm, tracing the path of a vein.

"It's the map," she whispered. "Look at the map."

The oilcloth lay beside her, fallen open. Across its face, where the tangled knot of ink had pulsed, the lines now ran clean and whole — and branching out from that place, following the roads and rivers, was a new line of silver-bright ink. It climbed the map's rivers northward. It branched along the old pilgrim road. And at the end of it, far to the north, in the deep heart of the chart where the ink gathered densest, it reached a symbol Elodie had not seen before: a tree, drawn in a single unbroken stroke, its roots torn and bleeding darkness across the parchment.

"The anchor," Tam breathed. "That's the anchor. That's — Elodie, the map is showing your power. Where it went. Where it *came from*. You didn't just seal a tear. You drew from the anchor itself, through the Veil, through—" He stopped. He took off his spectacles and cleaned them on his shirt with hands that were not quite steady, and when he spoke again his voice had gone quiet and careful, the voice of a man delivering a diagnosis. "You need to understand what that costs."

"I feel fine," Elodie said.

It was even mostly true. She felt hollowed, the way one feels after weeping — scraped clean, strangely light, wrung out. Her limbs answered when she asked them to. Her thoughts came slow but they came.

"You feel fine," Tam repeated. "Of course you do. So does a man who's bled a pint, for the first hour. The body lies beautifully about this sort of thing." He leaned forward, and behind the fogged spectacles his eyes were fierce with a pity she resented instantly and completely. "Seven Veilwardens sealed one tear four hundred years ago and four of them died within the year. Not in battle. *Withered.* The Veil takes its price from the ones who hold it shut, Elodie. It has to — it's a wall, and walls are held up by living stones, and living stones erode. You paid today. You may not see the bill for years. But it's coming."

Silence, except the rain.

Sera reached out — hesitantly, as if approaching a strange dog — and turned Elodie's scarred hand over in her own. She studied the silver line for a long moment. Then she said, quietly, "My father had a scar like that."

The words landed among them like a dropped knife.

"On his left hand," Sera went on, not looking up. "Silver. Old. He always wore a glove over it, even in summer. I used to think it was a burn." Her thumb traced the line on Elodie's palm, following it to the wrist. "He was a courier. He carried things for people who paid him not to ask questions. And I think — I think one of the things he carried was this map, and I think he held it the way you hold it, and I think—" Her voice caught, and steadied. "I think he knew exactly what was hunting him. And he brought it to you anyway."

"Why?" Elodie asked.

Sera looked up, and the grief in her face had rearranged itself into something harder and more terrible: certainty.

"Because you're the last of them," she said. "Whatever else you are, whatever you want to be — the map chose a Veilwarden, and it chose *you*, and my father died making sure it got there. So the question isn't whether you carry it anymore, Elodie Underhill. The question is whether you'll reach the anchor before the things tearing holes in the world reach it first."

Far to the north, beyond the spiral trees, thunder rolled — though the sky held no storm.

Against Elodie's chest, wrapped again in its oilcloth, the map pulsed once, warm as a second heart, and began, patiently, to pull.
