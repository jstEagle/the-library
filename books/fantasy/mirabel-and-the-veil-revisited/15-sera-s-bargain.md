## 15. Sera's Bargain

The witch-finders kept their garrison in the old tithe barn at Millbrook, and Sera Vane had been watching it for two days before she worked up the courage to walk through its door.

She told herself it was strategy. She told herself it was the only sensible thing left to do. The company had stopped at a drover's inn outside the village — Brannoc sleeping in the stable with his boots on, Tam buried in his notes, Elodie sitting by the fire with that cursed map folded against her chest like a child clutching a doll. And Sera had slipped out into the blue hour after sundown with her father's letters sewn into her coat lining and a lie already built in her throat.

Her father was dead because of that map. That much she knew. Whatever else was true — whatever Elodie Underhill claimed about ink that crawled and blood that sang — the beginning and end of it was Aldous Vane bleeding out in a stranger's stairwell with one word on his lips. *Mirabel.* He had carried that map across half the kingdom to keep it from someone, and someone had caught him, and now it sat warm against a hedge-witch's heart as if it had always belonged there.

Sera meant to take it back. And if she could not take it back, she would sell the truth of where it was to the only people in Corvane with the power to seize it — and buy her father's name back with it.

That was what she told herself, walking the ruts of the Millbrook road with her hood up and her hands jammed in her pockets so no one would see them shaking.

* * *

The tithe barn had been converted with the thoroughness of men who believed in their work. The great doors were replaced with iron-banded oak; the hay loft was now a watch post, and a boy of perhaps fifteen leaned out of it with a horn slung at his hip, watching the road. Lanterns burned behind shutters cut into arrow-slits. Over the doors, where once a farmer might have hung a garland at harvest, someone had nailed a board painted with the Crown's eye — the lidless circle that stared from every witch-finder's badge — and beneath it, in careful black letters: *THE KING SEES ALL.*

Sera stood in the road a long moment, looking up at that eye.

*He sees all,* she thought, *and he did not see my father die.*

She knocked.

The man who answered was not what she expected. She had imagined witch-finders as they appeared in market-day rumor: black coats, cold faces, voices like drawn blades. This one was gray and soft, middle-aged, with the watery eyes of a man who spent too many nights reading by bad light, and he looked at her the way a clerk looks at a petitioner — with weary, bureaucratic patience.

"Yes?"

"I need to speak with whoever commands here." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "I have information. About the Corvane matter."

Something shifted behind the watery eyes. Not warmth — nothing so kind — but interest, sharpening like a hook finding flesh. "The Corvane matter," he repeated. "You mean the dead man."

"The dead man," Sera said. "And what he was carrying."

* * *

They put her in a small room off the main hall — a tithe office once, judging by the shelf brackets still screwed into the walls — and gave her tea she did not touch. The gray man introduced himself as Lieutenant Quill, which struck her as either an honest name or a very private joke, and said the commander would see her shortly, and then left her alone for the better part of an hour.

She used the hour badly. She rehearsed her story until the words wore smooth and meaningless, then abandoned rehearsal and simply sat, listening to the sounds of the garrison around her. Boots on boards. A man coughing. Somewhere below the floor, muffled by stone, something that might have been weeping, or might have been wind in the eaves, and she made herself decide it was wind and did not think about it again.

When the commander finally came, he filled the doorway the way winter fills a valley.

General Corvess. She knew the name — everyone in the king's service had known the name, though her father had never served the Crown directly, and she had learned it the way children learn the names of wolves. He was tall without being large, silver at the temples, with a face built for composure: even-featured, unhurried, the face of a man who had never once needed to raise his voice because everyone in the room already understood who held the rope. He wore the black coat unadorned except for the eye at the collar, and he moved like a man who trusted floors to hold him.

"Miss Vane." He sat across from her without being invited, which was its own kind of message. "Quill tells me you've come about Aldous Vane."

Hearing her father's name in that measured voice did something to her chest. She had prepared for this moment and discovered, sitting in it, that preparation was a wall built of paper.

"He was my father."

"Yes." Corvess studied her with an attention that felt less like sympathy than like appraisal — a buyer turning a piece over to find the flaw. "I'm sorry for it. Genuinely. Aldous Vane was a difficult man to know and, I suspect, a more difficult one to lose." He folded his hands. "Tell me why you're here."

So she told her lie, and it went mostly the way she'd built it.

She was his daughter, estranged these three years — true enough, though the estrangement had been distance and silence rather than any quarrel, and grief had a way of rewriting those things. She had followed his route south after learning of his death, asking questions in Corvane's lower quarters until she found the trail. The man he'd died beside — the woman, rather, the charm-seller — had taken something from him. A document. Papers, maps, she didn't pretend to know precisely what, but her father had guarded it with his life and she wanted it back, and she wanted the people who killed him brought to account, and if the King's witch-finders could do both, she would tell them everything she knew.

"Everything you know," Corvess repeated softly.

"That's what I'm offering."

"And what is it you want in return?" He asked it mildly, almost kindly, and that mildness was worse than menace. Menace she could fight. Kindness from this man felt like a trap with the teeth hidden. "Not money. You didn't walk past my sentries for coin."

"My father's name." Her voice cracked on it and she hated herself for the crack. "He'll be remembered as a smuggler. A thief. Whatever your people write in their reports, that's what the record will say, and my mother will read it, and I will read it, and every time anyone says the name Vane they'll say it the way you wrote it. Clear it. Give him back his honor in the record, and I'll give you the woman and the map and everyone traveling with her."

Corvess was quiet for a moment. In the quiet, Sera heard again that sound beneath the floor — faint, rhythmic, unmistakably human this time — and saw, or thought she saw, the general's eyes flick toward it and away, unhurried, unsurprised, the way a man glances at weather.

"You love him," Corvess said. It was not a question, and there was something in it — some thin thread of recognition — that nearly undid her. "Good. Love makes people useful. It also makes them honest, eventually, when softer instruments fail." He rose. "Rest here tonight. Tomorrow I'll show you how we work, and you can judge whether we're the allies you want. I'd rather you chose us with open eyes than sold yourself blind. A bargain made in the dark sours in the light."

It sounded, Sera thought numbly, almost honorable.

That was the most frightening thing of all.

* * *

He showed her in the morning.

She had expected — she wasn't sure what. Interrogation rooms, perhaps, ledgers, the dry machinery of pursuit. Instead, Lieutenant Quill walked her down into the tithe barn's undercroft, lantern in hand, talking in the pleasant drone of a man giving a tour of an orchard, and the first thing she smelled was smoke that had soaked into stone so long ago it had become part of the stone.

"This wing was built in my grandfather's day," Quill said, gesturing along a corridor of cells. Most stood empty, doors ajar, straw still yellow in the corners. "We keep it ready. The Crown prefers readiness to improvisation."

At the third occupied cell, he paused, and raised his lantern, and Sera saw the prisoner.

He was young. That was the first thing — younger than her, maybe twenty, with a farm boy's broad hands and a burn scar running from his wrist halfway up his forearm, old and shiny. He lay curled on the straw, and when the light touched him he flinched away from it with a whole-body motion, the flinch of something that had learned what light preceded.

"What did he do?" Sera heard herself ask.

"Charm-work," Quill said comfortably. "Weather-charms, mostly. Little things. His mother taught him before we reached the family." He smiled at the cell, a clerk's smile. "He's cooperative now."

Sera stood very still. *His mother taught him before we reached the family.* The sentence arranged itself in her mind and refused to be unread. Before *we reached the family*. As in: the family had been reached. As in: somewhere in some green valley, a woman who taught her son to call rain had been —

"We don't enjoy this work," Quill said, and his voice was gentle, genuinely gentle, and somehow that was the worst thing yet. "People imagine we relish it. But order is a garden, Miss Vane. Gardens require pruning. Sentiment at the wrong moment lets the whole bed go to rot." He turned and continued down the corridor, expecting her to follow.

She followed. Her legs carried her because they had carried her through worse — through her father's funeral, through the long gray months after — and she watched the rest of it the way she had watched that: from slightly above herself, cataloguing, refusing to feel.

The undercroft opened into a workroom where a table stood crowded with instruments whose purposes she refused to guess at, and beyond it, through an iron door left open on a chain, a second chamber where a woman sat bound to a chair, awake, silent, her eyes fixed on the middle distance with the terrible patience of someone conserving themselves. A finder sat opposite her, reading aloud from a ledger in a low, reasonable voice, pausing now and then to ask a question, writing down her answers whether she gave them or not.

"That's the tracker," Quill murmured. "Attunement work. She can feel the object we're hunting — a chart, a very old one — when it's within a few days' ride. She felt it pass through Corvane. She feels it now, moving east." He glanced sideways at Sera, and his watery eyes were apologetic. "I shouldn't say more. Though I suspect you already know more than you've told me."

The woman in the chair turned her head. Just slightly. Her eyes found Sera through the doorway — found her, weighed her, and dismissed her, and in that dismissal there was a flicker of something Sera recognized from her own mirror over the last eight days: contempt for a bystander. For someone standing on the safe side of a door.

Sera's hands had gone cold.

*It's just a map,* she told herself. *My father died for a piece of paper, and these people are looking for a piece of paper, and the woman who has it is a stranger who let him bleed out on her stairs.*

But the woman in the chair had called rain for her crops, probably. Had taught her son little charms the way mothers teach bread. And the burn scar on the boy upstairs — the *cooperative* boy — had not been made by any hearth accident. Sera had grown up around soldiers' daughters and stablehands; she knew a brand when she saw one, even healed silver.

"Miss Vane?" Quill prompted pleasantly. "Shall we continue?"

"No," Sera said.

* * *

She walked out of the undercroft on legs that belonged to someone else, climbed the stairs, crossed the main hall where finders took their breakfast with the easy camaraderie of any garrison company — joking over porridge, complaining about the damp — and stepped out into the morning, and the morning was unbearable. Sunlight on frost. A cart of turnips rattling past. The world going on, ordinary and bright, while underneath the floor of the world women sat in chairs answering questions they hadn't been allowed to refuse.

She made it to the tree line at the edge of the village before she stopped and bent double and was sick in the frozen bracken.

Then she knelt there, breathing, and made herself think.

This was the trap of it — she could see the shape of the trap now, and admired, in a distant and furious way, how well it was built. Corvess hadn't shown her the cruelty to warn her off. He had shown it to bind her. Because now she knew, and knowing was a debt, and the general was a patient man who understood that people who had seen the machinery and walked away anyway would carry it forever — carry it as complicity, as fear, as the slow erosion of every excuse they'd arrived with. He had let her choose with open eyes, exactly as promised. And her choice, made in the dark of her own grief, had been to hand a frightened hedge-witch and her companions to this.

*My father's name,* she thought. *Is that what I'd be buying?*

No. She would be buying a line in a ledger written by these men. She would be buying the privilege of letting Aldous Vane be remembered however the King's eye decided to remember him — and she would be paying with the lives of five people, one of whom had held her father's hand at the end, if half of what Elodie Underhill said was true. One of whom had closed a dying stranger's eyes. Sera had seen it in her, that night on the road when knives came out: the woman fought like someone apologizing for it. Whatever else Elodie was, she was not the person who had killed Aldous Vane.

Someone else had caught him. Someone else had bled him in that stairwell — or he had run himself to death ahead of them — and the general downstairs, with his gentle voice and his garden metaphors, had known exactly what the dead courier had been carrying, because his people had been hunting it for years.

*She felt it pass through Corvane. She feels it now, moving east.*

The tracker. The attuned woman in the chair. They didn't need Sera's information at all. They had never needed it. Her bargain was a courtesy — a shortcut, a kindness extended to a grieving girl because grieving girls made such useful instruments afterward. Corvess would have cleared her father's name, too. He'd have written it beautifully. And Sera would have spent the rest of her life knowing what the beautiful line was printed on.

She thought of her father's letters, sewn against her ribs. She had read them a hundred times since the inn, hunting the cipher Tam swore was threaded through them, and she had begun to see it — the recurring phrases, the dates that matched no honest itinerary, the little marginal marks like birds. Her father had not been a thief. She was coming to believe it the way you come to believe dawn: gradually, then all at once. He had been a courier. A guardian. A man who carried a dangerous thing across a kingdom because someone had to, and who had taught his daughter, without ever saying it aloud, that some names are worth more than safety.

*Vane,* she thought. *Say it the way I say it.*

She got up. She walked back toward the garrison, and Quill met her at the door with his pleasant clerk's face, and she said, "I'd like to write a letter confirming the arrangement before I give anything further. My terms, in my hand. Standard practice, surely, for a bargain made in the light."

Quill hesitated only a heartbeat. Paper was harmless. Paper was what clerks understood. "Of course," he said. "The commander will want to review it, naturally."

"Naturally," Sera agreed, and smiled, and the smile cost her more than the sickening had.

* * *

They gave her a desk in the corner of the main hall — watched, of course, though the watcher pretended to inventory stores — and paper, and ink, and she wrote slowly, a daughter's careful terms, her father's honor itemized line by line in a fair round hand. Let them read it. Let Corvess initial it with his composed face. A bargain made in the light, exactly as he'd promised.

What she did not show anyone was the second sheet.

She wrote it in the afternoon, alone in the small room they'd assigned her, by the light of one candle, in the smallest hand she could manage. Then she folded it small, and waited for the change of the watch, and slipped out through the tithe barn's old side door — the one whose hinges Quill's people had oiled but never re-hung properly, because farmers' daughters did not count as flight risks.

The letter was addressed to no one. It was written to be lost, or found, or burned — it hardly mattered which, so long as it was never delivered. It named the garrison at Millbrook. It named the attuned woman in the chair, and the boy with the brand, and the cells under the floor, and General Corvess's habit of showing prisoners' suffering to grieving relatives as a means of binding them. It named the tracker's range, eastward, closing. If it fell into the right hands — a merchant's, a pilgrim's, anyone riding the east road — it might reach the company before the finders did. If it fell into the wrong hands, it was only a madwoman's scribble, unsigned, deniable.

It was not enough. She knew it was not enough. A letter was a pebble thrown at an army. But it was hers, and it was true, and truth was the only currency she had left that hadn't been debased.

Then she took her father's last letter to her — the earliest one, the one with the bird-marks in the margin, the one she had read so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth — and she held it over the candle.

She had meant to keep it. She had meant to decipher it with Tam, to wring every secret from it, to make it yield up her father whole. But sitting there in the witch-finders' guest room, with the smell of the undercroft still in her hair, she understood something with sudden, total clarity: the cipher wasn't in the margins. The cipher was the fact of the letters themselves — that Aldous Vane had written to his daughter regularly, warmly, boringly, for years, a courier so careful he hid his true cargo inside the appearance of an ordinary man's correspondence. The letters were the disguise. Which meant the letters, in the wrong hands, were the evidence — proof that the King's own courier had been playing a double game, proof that would damn his memory forever and hang whatever accomplices could be shaken out of it.

Tam could work faster without them. Or Tam could work better *because* of them, if Sera memorized what mattered first. Either way, they could never be found on her.

The flame took the corner. Bird-marks curling brown, then black, then gone to ash. Sera held it steady and watched her father's careful handwriting — *the weather holds fair here, sweetheart, eat something besides road bread* — dissolve into light, and she did not look away, and she did not cry, because she had done her crying in the bracken and what was left now was harder than tears.

When it was ash, she ground it fine between her fingers and scattered it out the window into the frost, where the wind took it east. East, where the map was moving. East, where a hedge-witch who apologized for fighting was carrying her father's burden without knowing its weight.

Sera Vane washed her face, packed her bag, and went down to tell Lieutenant Quill that she had reconsidered, that she wished to withdraw her offer, that she was leaving — and watched his pleasant face calculate exactly how much trouble one unarmed grieving girl could possibly be.

She hoped, for his sake, that he underestimated her.

Because tomorrow, when the company broke camp on the east road, she was going to ride back to them — to the woman she'd held a knife on, to the scholar, the deserter, the exile — and she was going to say the hardest words she'd ever said to anyone:

*I know where they're keeping the tracker. And I know how far behind us she is.*

And then, God help her, she was going to ask them to trust her.
