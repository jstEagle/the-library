## 16. The City of Mirabel

The gate was not where the map said it would be.

That was the first thing Elodie understood about Mirabel: it did not obey the rules of places. The map showed a hollow oak on the eastern lip of the Gray Marches, where a stream called the Liss split around a stone older than any kingdom — and they found the stone, and the stream, and no oak at all. Only a bare hillside, wind-scoured, with nothing in it but grass gone silver and a silence that pressed against the ears like deep water.

"It's wrong," Sera said flatly. She stood with her arms crossed against the cold, her father's knife sheathed at her hip, watching Tam as though he had personally misplaced the tree. "Your map is wrong."

"The map is never wrong," Tam murmured. He knelt beside the old stone, his ink-stained fingers hovering over its surface without quite touching. "It's *ahead* of us. That's what I keep trying to tell you all. It doesn't record the Veil. It participates in it." He glanced up at Elodie, and there was something in his face she had learned to dread over the weeks of travel — the scholar's look, hungry and reverent at once. "It's waiting for you."

Elodie's hand had been aching since dawn. Not the ordinary ache of cold or road-wear, but a deeper pull, as if the bones themselves were being gently rearranged. She unbound her glove — Brannoc watched the treeline while she did it, an old habit now — and held her palm over the stone.

The scar from the first tear, the one she'd sealed with her own blood a hundred miles back, flared white.

The hillside answered.

It began as a sound beneath hearing, a hum in the roots of things, and then the silver grass bent all at once in a wind that did not touch their faces, parting like a curtain drawn by careful hands. Where the grass parted, the ground darkened, softened, became loam rich and black and smelling of spring. And out of that loam, slowly, with the creaking patience of centuries compressed into a dozen heartbeats, an oak grew.

Not grew — *rose*. It came up whole, vast, its trunk wide as a chapel door, its branches hung with leaves in every color autumn owned. Bark split along one side, peeling back like a page turning, and behind the bark there was light. Not firelight, not sunlight. A light with weight to it, gold shot through with green, the color Elodie had seen only once before — bleeding through the tear in the Veil, in the moment before she closed it and collapsed into Brannoc's arms.

"Saints," Brannoc breathed. He had his hand on his sword without seeming to know it. In all the weeks since the ford, since he'd pulled them out of the witch-finders' ambush without a word of explanation, Elodie had never seen him look afraid. He looked afraid now. "That's a door."

"That's a *gate*," Tam corrected softly. "Doors open onto rooms. Gates open onto worlds." He turned to Elodie, and his voice dropped to something almost gentle, which from Tam was more alarming than shouting. "It's asking you, Elodie. It won't open for the rest of us. It's asking *you*."

She looked at her palm. The scar had shaped itself into a small whorl, like the ring a branch leaves when it's cut away, and the whorl pulsed in time with her heart.

Every instinct she had honed across seven years of hiding screamed at her to step back. Magic was chains and pyres; magic was Mistress Ivenna's face going cold and distant the day Elodie learned how much her teacher had hidden from her; magic was a dead stranger in her stairwell and witch-finders at the market and a life burned down twice over. If she walked through this gate, she would never be able to walk back into the person she had made herself become.

But the ache in her hand was not fear. It had never been fear. It was homesickness for a home she'd never seen.

Elodie pressed her palm to the bark.

The oak opened like a flower deciding, petals of wood folding outward, and light poured through — and beyond the light, impossibly, there was a city.

* * *

They stepped through together, because whatever else they were — fugitives, liars, a deserter and a heretic and a girl with a grudge — they had become a company, and companies cross thresholds as one.

The first thing Elodie felt was the air. It tasted the way her mother's voice sounded in the few memories she still trusted: warm, faintly sweet, old. The second thing she felt was the ground, which gave slightly underfoot like fresh bread, and the third thing she saw was everything else, all at once, too much, so much that her mind simply refused and started over.

They stood on a bridge of white stone that arched over a river flowing *upward*, a slow silver ribbon climbing through the air toward towers in the distance. The city spread below and above them both — that was the part her eyes kept stumbling on. Streets descended into valleys and then continued, seamlessly, up the sides of cliffs and along the undersides of arches, and people — people, or figures that were nearly people — walked them all, upright, unhurried, their shadows falling in three different directions at once.

The architecture was the work of minds that had never heard of right angles. Towers spiraled like shells, each floor a different width than the last, windows blooming where no window could logically be. Roofs were gardens; gardens were roofs. A cathedral-sized structure nearby appeared to be built entirely of glass grown like coral, and inside it Elodie could see — she squinted, disbelieving — snow falling, gentle and slow, while outside the same building the air was thick with the green heat of high summer.

"The seasons are stacked," Tam whispered. He had produced his journal and was writing as he walked, walking backward, tripping over a root, catching himself, never stopping the pen. "I theorized this. Twenty years ago. They laughed me out of the Royal Academy. Seasons as *districts* — climate as architecture — do you see, Elodie? Each quarter of the city holds its own season. They've *zoned the year*—"

"I see it," Elodie said. Her voice came out strange, hushed. "Tam. Why does it look like that?"

He followed her gaze and stopped writing.

Because for all its impossible beauty, the city was dying. She could see it everywhere once she knew to look. The upward river thinned to a trickle as it climbed, its upper reaches frayed into mist that never quite became anything. Whole spiraling towers stood dark, their windows empty, their gardens gone to gray bramble. The snow inside the glass cathedral fell on drifts no one had cleared, around doors sealed with ribbons of faded silk. And the figures on the streets — there were fewer of them than the size of the city demanded. A city built for hundreds of thousands, holding perhaps a few thousand, moving through its vastness the way embers move through a cooling hearth.

Even the light was thinner here. The gold-green radiance that had poured from the gate dimmed as they walked, stretched thin, like butter scraped across too much bread.

"It's real," Elodie said quietly. "All those years I told myself it was stories. Nursemaid tales about the fae. Heresy, Tam — you lost everything for saying this place was real." She turned in a slow circle, taking in the dying splendor. "And it's *failing*."

"The Veil fails, and this fails with it." Tam's pen had gone still. For once, the scholar looked less like a man collecting wonders than a doctor reading symptoms. "Mirabel isn't a country, Elodie. I think it's a *garden*. And the Veil is its wall, and its well, and its sun, all at once. When the wall cracks—" he gestured at the darkened towers, the frayed river, "—everything the wall protected starts to leak away."

Sera had said nothing since the gate. Now she spoke, and her voice was flat in the way Elodie had learned meant she was feeling anything but flat. "My father drew maps of this. All my life, I thought he was drawing fictions. Places to hide secrets in." She stared at the upward river, and something moved behind her eyes — grief rearranging itself, again, into a new and unwelcome shape. "He was drawing a *dying place*, and carrying the thing that might have saved it, and I hated him for leaving me."

"Sera—" Elodie began.

"Don't." But Sera's voice cracked on the word, and she turned away, and that was answer enough.

Brannoc alone watched the streets the way a soldier watches streets: exits, sightlines, crowds. "We're being seen," he said quietly. "Has been since we crossed. Don't stare back. Keep moving."

Elodie hadn't noticed. Now that he said it, she felt it — attention, layered and cool, like moonlight through water. Figures had paused on the far walks. A woman on a balcony above them leaned on a railing carved like sleeping swans and simply watched, her face beautiful and unreadable and terribly sad.

"They know what we are," Elodie said.

"No," Tam said. "Worse. I think they know what *you* are." He nodded ahead, down the great bridge, to where the street opened into a wide square — and where, gathered in the square, waiting with the patient elegance of people who had known of their arrival for days, stood perhaps two dozen figures in colors Elodie had no names for.

At their center, on a throne that seemed to have grown rather than been built — pale wood twined with living silver flowers — sat a lady.

* * *

They were escorted, though escort and honor guard and polite imprisonment blurred together in Mirabel. Fae knights flanked them — tall, luminous, armored in plates that looked like beetle-wing and frost — and the crowd in the square parted before Elodie with a sound like wind through wheat, a long collective intake of breath.

She heard the whisper pass through them. She didn't speak their language, but she didn't need to. Some words carry their meaning in their shape, and this one rippled from mouth to mouth like a spark down a fuse:

*Vailwarden. Vailwarden. Vailwarden.*

Her blood sang in answer, traitorous and warm, and Elodie clenched her jaw against it.

Lady Ashvine was not what Elodie expected, though afterward she couldn't have said what she had expected. The regent of Mirabel was small, fine-boned, dressed in gray silk the exact shade of the dying towers, with hair like smoke and eyes like the moment just before rain. Ageless was the word the stories used, but it was the wrong word. Ashvine wasn't ageless. She was *old* — old the way mountains are old, old the way grief is old — and she wore her age the way other women wore jewels.

"You are the Underhill girl," Ashvine said. Her voice was low and clear, a bell rung in an empty house. "The last of the line. Do you know how long I have waited for the last of the line?"

"I didn't know there was a line until a month ago," Elodie said. "So. No."

Something flickered across the regent's face — surprise, perhaps, or its courtly cousin. Behind Elodie, she felt Tam go very still, the stillness of a man memorizing.

"Then you do not know what you carry." Ashvine rose from her living throne, and the silver flowers turned to follow her like faces. "You do not know what your family *was*. What they held. What broke when they broke." She descended the dais steps, and the knights bowed as she passed, and she stopped before Elodie close enough that Elodie could smell her — winter apples and cold stone. "You have crossed the Marches. You have seen the tears. You have bled to close one — do not look surprised, child; the Veil keeps no secrets from me, only distances. So you already understand, better than most, that the wall between our worlds is coming down."

"I understand it's failing," Elodie said carefully. "I don't yet understand what anyone wants *me* to do about it."

"No," Ashvine agreed softly. "You don't. But you will. That is why you are here. That is why the map carried you here, across the world, past every hunter and hedge and grave in its path." She smiled, and it was a lovely smile, and it reached nothing above her cheekbones. "Mirabel has waited seven hundred years for a Veilwarden to come home. We can wait a little longer for you to understand why."

She gestured, graceful as a closing book. "You will be lodged in the Autumn Court. Rest. Eat. Be *seen* — my people need to look hope in the face, even borrowed hope." Her gaze swept past Elodie, touching Tam, Brannoc, Sera, lingering on each with a different flavor of calculation. "And tomorrow, we will speak of the anchor. Of what must be done. Of what your blood was always meant to do."

"And if I refuse?" Elodie asked.

Ashvine's smile deepened by a single degree.

"Child," she said, "no one has ever come this far to refuse. That is the tragedy of your family, and it will not be yours."

* * *

The Autumn Court was a palace folded inside a forest folded inside a single enormous tree, and Elodie stopped trying to map it after the third impossible corridor. Their chambers opened onto a balcony that overlooked a courtyard where it was eternally late October — golden light, red leaves falling forever without ever finishing falling, a fountain full of something that was water and wine and memory all at once. Servants brought food that tasted like the best day of whatever ate it. Everything was beautiful. Everything was generous.

Everything was a cage with the door politely left open, because everyone knew no one would want to leave.

Brannoc tested the balconies and stairwells anyway, silent and thorough, and reported back in his flat soldier's way: "We could leave. Probably. Tonight, maybe. But the streets change. Rue could do it." He paused. "If Rue were here."

"He'll find us," Sera said from the window, arms folded. "He's been following us since the Marches. You've felt it. The map's been *hot* since the ford." She glanced at Elodie. "Whatever game that creature is playing, we're on the board."

Tam, meanwhile, had claimed a corner and filled six pages with notes, muttering the whole while — about the seasons-as-districts, about the upward river, about the way the light thinned near the darkened towers. But near dusk he set down his pen and came to stand beside Elodie at the balcony rail, and for a while neither of them spoke. Below, the eternal leaves fell and fell and never landed.

"When I was disgraced," Tam said finally, "they read out my crimes in the Academy hall. Heresy. Fabrication. Corrupting students with fantasy." His mouth twisted. "I stood there thinking: but I've *seen* it. In the archives. Fragments of charts, fragments of treaties, a fragment of a war-journal that described the sky over Mirabel. I thought, I would rather be ruined for the truth than honored for a smaller lie." He looked out at the dying city, and his voice went quiet. "I never imagined the truth would be this sad."

"It's beautiful," Elodie said.

"It's both. That's what no one tells you." Tam tapped the rail. "Look at it properly, Elodie. This is what your blood built. Every tower, every season, every impossible street — the Veilwardens held the wall, and the wall let all *this* exist. Seven hundred years of it. And now the line is broken, the wall is cracking, and the garden is dying, and the people who live here—" he nodded at the servants, the knights, the distant figures on the far walks, all of them watching the Autumn Court with that same terrible hope, "—are going to ask you to save them. However they have to ask."

"I know."

"Do you? Because Lady Ashvine used the word *must* four times in five minutes, and I counted, because counting is what I do instead of screaming." Tam's eyes were sharp and kind at once. "There is a difference, Elodie, between a duty you choose and a duty that is chosen *for* you. Your whole life has been other people choosing for you. Ivenna. The map. The blood. If you let the regent choose too—"

"Then who am I?" Elodie finished quietly.

Below them, the leaves fell and fell and never touched the ground.

Night came strangely to Mirabel — not darkness but a deepening, the gold-green light banking itself like a banked fire, stars emerging that moved, slowly, in patterns no human astronomer had ever charted. From her chamber window, Elodie watched the dark towers of the city, and among them she saw lights kindle, one by one, in windows that should have been empty. Small lights. Defiant ones. People who had stayed in a dying place because it was home.

Her blood pulled toward them like a tide.

And in the deepest hour of the night, when the eternal autumn had gone still and even the falling leaves paused between moments, Elodie woke — or half-woke — to a sound at the balcony. Soft. Deliberate. The sound of someone landing who had chosen to make no sound at all.

She was on her feet with her knife drawn before her eyes fully opened, and there, perched on the balustrade in the starlight, elegant as a rumor, sat a figure she had felt on the edge of every mile since the Marches. Pale-haired. Amber-eyed. Smiling the smile of someone about to commit a crime he had committed many times before and intended to enjoy.

"Well," said the fae exile Rue, resting his chin on one hand. "You took your time. Shall we talk about what Lady Ashvine is really going to ask of you tomorrow — before she asks it?"
