## 17. Court of the Hollow Crown

The gate closed behind them like a held breath finally released.

Elodie felt it in her teeth first — a soft pressure, a change in the air's weight — and then the world of Mirabel unfolded around them with the slow arrogance of something that had never once doubted its own beauty.

They stood at the top of a stair that had no business existing. It descended from the hidden gate into a valley where three seasons lived side by side: autumn burning gold along the eastern slopes, spring unfurling pale green in the lowlands, and winter holding the high peaks in a silence so complete it seemed less like weather than like attention. Rivers ran uphill in places. Somewhere far off, bells rang in a key Elodie's ears kept trying to refuse.

"Steady," Rue murmured beside her. He had changed since they crossed — not in shape but in substance, as though Mirabel itself had remembered what he was and restored him to himself. His skin carried a faint sheen like moonlight on water. His eyes were brighter than they had any right to be, and unhappier too. "Don't stare too long at anything. The court will take it for rudeness, and rudeness here has consequences."

"What kind of consequences?" Brannoc asked. He walked with his hand resting on his sword hilt the way other men rested a hand on a friend's shoulder.

"The interesting kind," Rue said.

Sera said nothing at all. She had been silent since the crossing, her father's letters bound against her ribs beneath her coat, and she looked out over the valley with an expression Elodie couldn't read — hunger and grief braided together into something sharp enough to shave with.

Tam was the only one of them who looked delighted. The disgraced cartographer had stopped twice already to sketch, ink-stained fingers flying, muttering measurements that made no sense because the distances refused to hold still. "The perspective is *wrong*," Tam said happily. "Deliberately wrong. It's defensive architecture. You can't map a city that won't agree to be mapped."

"That's the point," Rue said. "Come. The regent will have been told we're coming since before we knew it ourselves."

* * *

The road down into the capital wound through orchards whose trees bore blossoms and ripe fruit on the same branch, and past fields where the harvest was being gathered by figures that were only sometimes people. Everything was beautiful the way a blade is beautiful — clean lines, perfect edges, and a coldness underneath that reminded you what beauty was *for* here.

Elodie kept her hand near the map.

It rode against her chest now, wrapped in linen beneath her coat, and it had grown warm again as they crossed into Mirabel — warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, with a heat that suggested awareness. All through the descent it had pulsed gently against her sternum, patient as a heartbeat, and she had begun to suspect that the map was not leading her to the fae court at all.

She suspected the map had led the fae court to *her*.

The city announced itself before they saw it: a smell of woodsmoke and night-blooming flowers, then sound — voices in a language that slid between Elodie's ears without catching, music from instruments that seemed to be played by the wind itself, and under all of it a hum she recognized with a lurch. It was the same hum the Veil made when it tore. Here, at the heart of Mirabel, that wounded note ran through everything like a crack through porcelain.

Then the city rose before them, and Elodie forgot to breathe.

It was built upward and inward and, in at least one place she could see, downward through the sky. Towers of white stone and living silverwood climbed into each other's seasons; bridges spanned gaps that had no ground beneath them; gardens grew on walls and waterfalls fell upward into clouds. And everywhere, threaded through the splendor, were the signs of decay that the guidebooks of legend never mentioned — towers with their upper floors simply *gone*, sheared off as if by a giant's indifferent hand; streets that ended in walls of gray mist; windows darkened with the same blight that scarred the Gray Marches, spreading like frost across beautiful faces.

"It's dying," Sera said quietly. It was the first thing she'd spoken since the crossing. "Your city is dying."

"Our city is *failing*," Rue corrected, and his voice had gone careful and flat. "There's a difference, and the court will kill you for confusing them."

* * *

They were met at the city's edge by an honor guard of six fae knights in armor the color of birch bark, mounted on horses that were only horses if you didn't look directly at them. Their leader dismounted with a fluidity that made Elodie's human joints ache in sympathy and inclined her head with exactly the degree of courtesy required and not one hair more.

"Bearers of the chart," she said. Her voice was pleasant and completely empty. "Lady Ashvine extends the hospitality of the Hollow Crown. You will be washed, fed, and presented."

"And if we'd rather not?" Brannoc asked.

The knight looked at him the way one looks at weather. "Then you will be washed, fed, and presented anyway, but less comfortably." She turned her horse. "Follow."

They followed.

Through streets where every passerby watched them with eyes of green and gold and violet and one unsettling shade of rust, through markets selling bottled songs and bolts of cloth woven from spider-silk and memory, up a great stair cut from a single piece of crystal that sang under their feet in a chord that changed with each climber's weight. Elodie noticed that hers made the crystal ring in a way the others' did not — a deep, resonant tone that made several of the honor guard exchange glances.

She pretended not to notice them noticing. Seven years of practice at being unremarkable, and here it failed her utterly. In Corvane she had hidden by being small. There was nowhere small left to be. The map beat against her chest like a second heart, announcing her to everyone with the sense to listen, and apparently everyone in Mirabel had the sense to listen.

The palace — if palace was the word for a structure that seemed less built than *grown*, silverwood and stone fused into spires and galleries — opened onto a hall that took Elodie's remaining breath away.

The throne room of the Hollow Crown was vast and dim and hung with banners from wars whose names had been deliberately forgotten; she could feel the forgetting like a pressure behind her eyes. Light came from no source she could find, falling in shafts of amber and green. And at the far end, on a dais of black glass, beneath a crown suspended in empty air — hollow, literally hollow, a circle of twisted metal holding nothing at all —

— sat Lady Ashvine.

She was not what Elodie expected, which Elodie supposed was the point. She was small. Slight, silver-haired, dressed in gray silk with a single garnet at her throat, she might have been someone's grandmother, someone's kindly neighbor, a woman you'd trust with your keys and your children. Only her eyes gave her away. They were the color of deep winter water, and they were very, very old, and they did not move when she looked at you so much as *arrive*.

"Welcome," Lady Ashvine said, and her voice filled the hall without effort or echo. "The last daughter of Underhill comes home at last."

Every word landed precisely where she wanted it. Elodie felt the trap in the sentence — *comes home* — and stepped around it carefully.

"I'm a charm seller from Corvane," she said. "I've never been here before in my life."

Something moved at the corner of Ashvine's mouth. Approval, possibly. Or appetite. With the fae, Elodie was learning, the two were difficult to tell apart.

"A charm seller," Ashvine repeated, tasting it. "Who carries a Veilwarden chart against her heart. Who sealed a tear in the Gray Marches with her own blood — yes, we heard of that; the Marches remember such things, and they gossip terribly. Who crossed my border escorted by my exiled traitor." Her gaze slid to Rue, and the temperature of the hall dropped by degrees. "Rue of the Autumn Gate. You have been gone a long time."

"Lady Regent." Rue bowed — deeply, gracefully, and with a hatred so well-controlled it might have been respect. "I see the court's hospitality hasn't warmed in my absence."

"Hospitality is for guests." Ashvine rose. She was even smaller standing, and somehow more enormous. "You are not a guest, Rue. You are a sentence that was never finished being carried out. That you stand in this hall breathing is either boldness or stupidity, and I have known you long enough to know it is both."

"I brought you something more valuable than my head," Rue said. "I brought you the bearer."

"You brought me a *problem*," Ashvine said, "wrapped in a girl from a city that burns witches." She came down the dais steps, and the court parted before her like water around a keel. She stopped an arm's length from Elodie — close enough that Elodie could smell winter and old stone on her — and studied her with those still, arriving eyes. "Let us not waste courtesy pretending we don't know why you're here. The anchor is failing. Your map knows it. My court knows it. The humans scratching at the border know it, or they soon will. So, Elodie Underhill: what do you intend?"

It was a test dressed as directness. Every answer Elodie could think of was a wrong one. *Seal it* would please the court and doom half the world. *Open it* would get them killed before dinner. *I don't know* would mark her as useless.

So she gave the truest answer she had.

"I intend to understand it first," she said. "Everyone keeps offering me choices, and nobody will tell me what the choices actually cost. I'm done choosing blind."

Silence. Then, somewhere in the assembled court, a ripple — not laughter exactly, but the place where laughter would live if the court permitted such a vulgar thing.

Ashvine tilted her head. "And if understanding shows you that sealing the Veil is the only mercy left? If the anchor cannot be saved, only closed — would you close it, knowing it would save my people and starve yours of magic forever?"

"Would *you*?" Elodie asked. "Knowing it would do the same to mine?"

"Mine are dying *now*," Ashvine said, and for one unguarded instant the grandmother mask slipped and Elodie saw what was underneath: not malice. Terror. Old, worn-smooth terror, the kind that has managed a kingdom for centuries. "You feel it in the streets. You smelled it at the gate. Mirabel is coming apart thread by thread, child, and when it goes, everything in it goes — every song, every name, every soul you see watching you with those hungry polite eyes. I have buried this realm once already in pieces. I will not bury it whole. If closing the Veil is the price of my people's survival, I will pay it, and I will sleep soundly after."

"And the human world?"

"Has survived without magic before," Ashvine said. "Yours will survive again. Grief is survivable. Extinction is not." She let that sit in the air, generous with her logic, letting them admire its terrible cleanliness. "But you did not come to hear my reasoning. You came because the map dragged you, and the map drags all its bearers toward the same grave eventually. So. You will rest tonight as honored guests — genuinely honored; I keep my courtesies sharp even when my intentions aren't — and tomorrow we will discuss terms."

"Terms for what?" Tam asked sharply, from the edge of the group. The scholar had been vibrating with barely-contained questions since the throne room doors opened, and now burst like an overripe fruit. "Lady Regent — with respect — the chart the young woman carries is a Veilwarden key-chart, third iteration, and I believe there's a deeper cipher layer that maps not the Veil itself but its *anchor mechanism*. If I might have access to your archives —"

"You might not," Ashvine said pleasantly.

"But —"

"My archives contain the reasons your royal colleges burned you, scholar, and I see no reason to repeat their mistakes by letting you confirm them." She turned back to Elodie, and the warmth in her voice was the warmth of a hearth in a house you were about to be locked inside. "Terms, Miss Underhill, for the future of two worlds. Rest well. Eat everything offered. Trust nothing that flatters you."

Her gaze flicked — briefly, deliberately — to Rue.

"And trust least of all the ones who flatter best."

* * *

The chambers given to them were beautiful beyond anything Elodie had ever slept in, which she understood immediately to be part of the message. Silk sheets. A window overlooking a garden where snow fell upward. A bath drawn with water that smelled of some flower that existed nowhere else in creation.

"They're showing us what we'd gain by cooperating," Tam said, pacing the sitting room, touching everything, cataloguing everything. "This is a bribe with a roof on it."

"It's also a cage with good taste," Brannoc said. He'd checked the door within thirty seconds of arrival. It locked from the outside. He hadn't commented on it aloud, but he'd moved his bedroll against the wall nearest it, and he sat now where he could see the window and the door at once.

Sera stood at the window, looking out at the dying-blooming city, saying nothing.

Elodie unwrapped the map.

It came alive in her hands the moment the linen fell away — ink crawling, coastlines shifting, the whole document straining eastward like a compass needle finding north. But here in Mirabel it did something new. Lines bloomed across its surface that she'd never seen, fine gold threads radiating outward from a single point deep in the chart's interior, and at that point the ink gathered and swirled and slowly, deliberately, formed a word:

**HURRY.**

"Well," Elodie said. "That's not ominous at all."

"It knows something we don't," Tam said, leaning close. "Or it knows something it thinks we should. Charts don't panic, Elodie. This one is panicking."

"Or it's being *made* to look panicked," Brannoc said grimly. "We're in a court full of creatures who can twist iron and lie with their faces. Maybe the map can be lied to as well."

The knock came at dusk.

A servant — a slim figure with bark-textured skin and moss in their hair, carrying a tray of food that smelled like every good memory Elodie had ever had distilled into sauce — entered, set the tray down, and withdrew without meeting anyone's eyes. Beneath the tray's napkin lay a folded slip of paper that had not been there a moment before.

Sera saw it first. She crossed the room in three strides and held it up between two fingers like something venomous.

"For you," she said, and handed it to Elodie.

The paper was heavy, cream-colored, sealed with gray wax bearing the sigil of the Hollow Crown. Inside, in handwriting so elegant it looked typeset:

*Miss Underhill —*

*A matter of history, briefly: the exile Rue was not cast out of Mirabel for treason against this court. He was cast out for treason against* me *— specifically, for discovering what I did to secure the regency, and preparing to speak of it. His crime was honesty. Mine was survival. We have both paid, though his bill runs longer.*

*I tell you this not to win your trust — flattery, as I said — but because tomorrow I will offer you a bargain, and you deserve to weigh it knowing what I am. I am a woman who exiled the only friend she had to keep a secret, and I would seal the Veil and orphan half of creation to keep my people alive. Judge me accurately. Then judge your options.*

*One further item, freely given: ask Rue, when you next speak privately, what happened at the Autumn Gate. Ask him what he was willing to open, and for whom. He has not told you everything. Neither have I. But only one of us wants you to reach the anchor alive.*

*— A.*

Elodie read it twice. Around her, the others watched her face.

"Well?" Tam demanded.

She looked at Rue's empty chair by the fire — he had gone to speak with old acquaintances in the city, he'd said, contacts, insurance; he'd promised to return before midnight — and she thought about the way he'd guided them across the Marches, the way he'd fought at the ford, the way he'd never once, in all these weeks, told them plainly why he wanted the map.

"He said the court exiled him for treason," Elodie said slowly. "He never said treason against *what*."

Outside, the upward-falling snow drifted past the window, and somewhere below in the singing crystal streets, the wounded hum of the failing Veil throbbed on — closer now, Elodie realized. Closer than it had been at the gate.

The map, warm against her ribs, pulsed once, hard, like a fist knocking on a door.

**HURRY**, it had written. And for the first time, Elodie wondered whether it meant hurry toward the anchor —

— or hurry away from whatever was waiting for them in the morning light.
