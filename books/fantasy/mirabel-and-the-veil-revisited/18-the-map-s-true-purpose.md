## 18. The Map's True Purpose

Tam Vess had spent eleven years in the royal archives of Corvane, and in all that time she had never once been permitted to touch anything as beautiful as the thing folded into Elodie Underhill's satchel.

She thought about that now, sitting cross-legged on the floor of a ruined shepherd's hut three days beyond the Gray Marches, with a stub of tallow candle guttering beside her and the map unrolled across her knees. The others slept — Brannoc at the door with his back to the wall and his sword across his thighs even in sleep, Sera curled like a drawn bow, Elodie on her side facing the wall as though she could refuse the map by refusing to look at it. Rue did not sleep. Tam had stopped trying to guess where the fae went when he stepped out of the firelight; she only knew he always came back, and that his coming back meant something he would never say aloud.

The candlelight moved across the parchment, and the ink moved with it.

That was the thing no one else seemed willing to sit with for long enough to understand. Ink did not move. Ink was fixed; that was its whole nature, its whole virtue. A chart was an argument made permanent — *the world is thus*, said the cartographer, and the world had better oblige or the chart was worthless. Tam had built her career, and then lost it, on charts that obliged.

This one did not oblige. This one negotiated.

She had watched the coastlines breathe since they'd left Corvane. She had watched mountain ranges shrug their shoulders by a hair's width over the course of an afternoon, the way real mountains did under frost and thaw, only faster, the way a man might shift in his sleep. The rivers ran. Not flowed — *ran*, a slow silver peristalsis along their courses, as though the parchment were skin and the water beneath it were blood. And always, always, the roads: thin dark threads stitching the human kingdoms to the borderlands and then stopping, fraying, unraveling into nothing wherever the land grew strange, so that the map looked less like a country than like a garment coming apart at every seam.

Everyone else saw a treasure map. Sera saw her father's killer. Brannoc saw a target painted on all their backs. Elodie saw a curse she couldn't put down.

Tam saw a text. And texts, whatever else they were, could be read.

* * *

The problem was the third layer.

The first layer was easy; any apprentice could have read it. Roads, rivers, ranges, cities — the human world rendered in a fine brown hand, annotated in a script Tam recognized from the oldest atlases in the royal collection, the ones chained to their shelves and consulted only by royal license. The second layer revealed itself to moonlight, which was why Tam had taken to sleeping with the map near the windowless gap in the eastern wall where the moon could find it: under lunar light, a second geography surfaced, faint as breath on glass. Fae roads. Fae holdings. The whole architecture of Mirabel laid over the human world like a second face pressed to the first, close enough to kiss, separated by nothing but the thinnest membrane of ordinary air.

And between the two layers, everywhere they touched, the membrane showed its wounds.

Tam had marked them over four nights of work: small places where the two geographies bled into each other, where a fae river crossed a human road and the crossing point shimmered with a sickly iridescence, like oil on water. There were nine of them. No — she counted again, lips moving — eleven. When they'd crossed the Gray Marches there had been seven. The tears were spreading. Whatever held the worlds apart was failing, and it was failing faster than anyone wanted to say out loud.

But the third layer — the layer beneath the geography, beneath even the fae architecture — that was the one that had kept her awake for two nights running, and it was the reason her eyes ached and her fingers were stained black to the second knuckle.

Because the third layer only appeared when the map was bleeding.

* * *

It had happened by accident, the way everything true happened. That afternoon, at the ford where Brannoc had cut his palm open on a wire strung across the shallows — old fae work, a snare left from some war older than any of them — Elodie had knelt without thinking and bound the wound with a strip of her own sleeve, and a drop of her blood had fallen on the corner of the map where it lay half-unrolled in the grass.

Tam had seen what happened next and had not told anyone, because telling people things before you understood them was how you got burned as a heretic. She knew that better than anyone living.

The blood hadn't soaked in. It had been *drunk*. The parchment had swallowed it the way dry earth swallows rain, and for perhaps ten heartbeats the entire surface of the map had gone dark — not black, but deep, deep green, the green of the underside of leaves, of deep water, of the moment just before dawn — and through that darkness something had shown. Not roads. Not rivers. Not any geography at all.

A structure. A single vast structure at the center of everything, both geographies at once, rendered not in ink but in light, like a tree seen from above — crown spread across half the map, roots reaching down through the parchment itself as though the paper were merely the topsoil of some larger world. And around the tree, written in a script Tam had never seen but whose grammar she understood the way one understands a mother tongue one was stolen from in infancy, a single line of text, repeated, ringing the tree like a fence:

*THE WARDEN HOLDS. THE WARDEN HOLDS. THE WARDEN HOLDS.*

Then the green had faded, the blood-light had died, and the map had been a map again, smug and silent, and Tam had sat down hard in the wet grass while Brannoc asked if she was ill.

She had needed two days to work up the courage to do it deliberately.

* * *

Now, in the ruined hut, with everyone asleep and the candle burned down to a thumb's width, Tam drew the little knife from her boot and opened the pad of her left thumb.

She was not a brave woman. She had never been a brave woman; she had been a careful woman who once, catastrophically, cared more about being right than about being safe, and the stocks and the heresy charge and the loss of everything had taught her precisely nothing, because here she was again, caring more about being right. But her hand did not shake as she pressed the bleeding thumb to the center of the map, over the place where, on the first layer, a range of hills called the Shepherd's Teeth marked the border between the human kingdoms and the blighted marches.

The map drank.

Darkness bloomed across the parchment like ink dropped in water, and the green came up through it, and there it was: the tree of light, vast and patient and *wounded*. Now that she looked deliberately, now that she wasn't reeling, she could see what she'd missed in the grass. The tree was damaged. Great limbs of its light ended in stumps. Its crown was patchy, thinning, translucent in places where it should have blazed. And at its base, where the roots plunged through the parchment, the light guttered like a lamp running out of oil.

*Failing,* she thought. *It isn't just weakening. It is dying.*

And the ring of text around it — she bent close, so close her nose nearly touched the luminous surface, and read it properly this time, syllable by syllable, using the grammar that lived in her bones and that she had spent thirty years pretending wasn't there:

*THE WARDEN HOLDS. THE WARDEN HOLDS.* And then, where the ring passed over the wounded places, the words changed. *THE WARDEN IS BROKEN. THE WARDEN IS BROKEN. THE WARDEN IS BROKEN.*

Tam sat back. Her thumb throbbed. The candle chose that moment to drown in its own wax and go out, and she let it, because the map gave off its own light now, soft and green and terrible, and she could see everything she needed to see.

"It's not a chart," she whispered to the dark. "You absolute fool, Vess. It was never a chart."

* * *

She worked until the sky behind the broken roof went from black to iron to the color of an old bruise. She worked because understanding was the only prayer she had ever known how to make. And by the time the first birds started up in the spiraled trees outside — singing their wrong, backward songs — she had the shape of it, the whole terrible shape, and she sat in the gray light with her hands flat on the cold floor and let it press the breath out of her.

The map was a key. Not metaphorically. Functionally. It was the externalized mechanism of the Veil's anchor — the tree, the anchor, the thing at the hinge of the world that the oldest heretical texts had called the Worldroot and the sanctioned histories had called a myth. The last Veilwardens had not carried the map because it showed the way to the anchor. They carried it because the map *was* the anchor's handle, its interface, the mortal-made instrument through which a mortal will could grip the immortal machinery and hold it shut. Without a bearer, the key turned nothing. Without the key, the bearer gripped nothing. Together they were the lock.

And the lock was breaking because there had been no bearer for seven years.

No — longer. Tam traced the dates in her head, the dates she'd reconstructed from banned chronicles and burned archives and one smuggled fragment of the Veilwarden genealogies that had cost a colleague her post and very nearly her life. The last confirmed Veilwarden had died twenty-three years ago. Since then the anchor had been running on residual strength alone, a dam with no engineer, holding because stone holds, failing because everything fails.

Twenty-three years of slow failure. Eleven years of accelerating failure — the timeline matched the tears, matched the blighting of the Gray Marches, matched the spiral trees and the backward birds and every wrongness they'd walked through. And the Crown's witch-finders, hunting magic-users through those same years, burning hedge-witches and charm-sellers and anyone whose blood ran even faintly toward the old inheritance—

Tam went cold.

They weren't suppressing magic. Or rather, they weren't *only* suppressing magic. They were pruning. Every witch-finder pyre, every family hunted down and scattered, every child of the old lines raised in ignorance of what they were — it was all one operation, whether the General knew it or not, whether he intended it or not. They had been systematically exterminating the only people who could ever hold the Veil shut. For a hundred years. Longer.

*And the last line,* said the genealogy fragment in her memory, in her colleague's careful hand, *the line of Underhill—*

The word arrived in her mind with the force of a physical blow, and she actually flinched, there alone in the ruin, and heard her own breath go ragged.

Underhill. The genealogies listed them last because they lasted longest. Wardens of the western approaches, keepers of the anchor-road, the final branch of the final line — hunted, scattered, whittled down decade by decade until the records simply stopped, the way records do when the people in them stop existing. Stopped existing, or stopped *being found*, which amounted to the same thing in the eyes of a state that kept lists.

Elodie Underhill, who hid her gift beneath careful mediocrity. Who had tried to burn the map. Who bled on it and it drank. Whose blood had woken a structure no scholar in three centuries had seen.

Whose family the witch-finders had hunted to the edge of extinction without ever knowing why their work mattered, without ever knowing they were sawing through the last rope holding two worlds apart.

"Oh," Tam said softly, to the sleeping room, to the gray light, to the girl facing the wall. "Oh, you poor, unlucky, extraordinarily lucky woman."

* * *

The map dimmed as dawn strengthened, folding its secrets away, becoming parchment again, innocent again. Tam rolled it with hands that were steadier than she deserved and sat for a long while watching Elodie sleep.

She should wake her. She should say it now, plainly, the way she had once delivered findings to the royal cartographers' council: clearly, completely, with citations. That was her function. That was the only thing she had ever been good for — seeing what was true and saying it out loud regardless of the cost, and look what it had gotten her, disgraced and branded and sleeping on floors. And still. Still she would say it, because someone had to, because the truth did not care who carried it and neither, apparently, did she.

But not yet. Let her sleep. Tam owed her that much — one more hour of being a charm-seller from Corvane who wanted nothing, before becoming the last warden of the world's hinge.

Around her the hut breathed with the others' breathing. Outside, a bird sang its song backward, note by reversed note, and somewhere far to the west — Tam felt it rather than heard it, a pressure behind the eyes, a wrongness in the teeth — something vast and ancient shifted in its sleep and did not quite settle again.

The Veil was failing. The key had chosen its hand. And the last of the Underhills lay sleeping an arm's length away, dreaming, Tam hoped, of anything at all besides what was coming.

She reached over and touched Elodie's shoulder.

"Elodie." Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "Wake up. I've read it. We need to talk about your family."

Elodie's eyes opened, wary and gray-green in the early light, and Tam watched the fear arrive in them a full heartbeat before she said a single word — watched the girl already know, the way one always already knows, that the life she'd been clinging to had just ended.

"There's something you need to hear," Tam said, "about what the witch-finders took from you."
