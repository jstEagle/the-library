## 19. Ashvine's Offer

They came for her at moonrise.

Not soldiers this time — no fae knights with their antlered helms and their blades like slivers of winter. Instead, a single handmaiden of the Hollow Court, gray-robed and silent as falling ash, who bowed at the door of the guest chamber and said only: *The Lady Ashvine requests the bearer's presence in the Night Garden.*

Elodie looked at Tam, who sat hunched over the map by candlelight, quill scratching notes he would never show the court. He looked up, and his eyes did the thing they always did when he was afraid but refused to name it — went very still, very bright, like a man reading a coastline for rocks.

"Go," he said. "Refusing an invitation is its own answer."

"That's what worries me."

"Everything here is an answer, Elodie. The trick is choosing which questions you want to be asked." He turned back to the map, but she saw that his hand had stopped moving. "Wear something warm. The garden doesn't have weather so much as it has *opinions*."

* * *

The Night Garden lay at the heart of the palace of Mirabel — or one of its hearts; the palace seemed to have several, stacked like thoughts in a dreaming mind, and the corridors rearranged themselves politely whenever anyone walked them twice. Elodie had spent two days lost in it and had begun, uneasily, to suspect that being lost was the point. A guest who could not find her own way out could not leave.

The garden opened around her all at once, the way a held breath releases.

It was night above her — a true night, deep and starred, though she had entered through a corridor lit by late-afternoon gold. Below the stars grew flowers that drank the dark instead of the light: blooms of smoked silver, of bruise-purple, of a black so rich it seemed cut out of the sky itself. Paths of white gravel wound between beds of it, and fountains ran not with water but with something slower, brighter, that hummed faintly when it fell — light, perhaps, or memory. The air smelled of cold stone and honey and, beneath both, something older and stranger, like the smell of a book left closed for a hundred years.

Lady Ashvine stood at the garden's center, beside a tree that had grown into the shape of a doorway and then, apparently, thought better of it.

She was not beautiful in the way human courtiers were beautiful. She was beautiful the way winter is beautiful — precise, total, indifferent to whether you survived admiring her. Her hair was the color of birch bark, bound with a circlet of living thorn, and her gown moved as if it were remembering water. When she turned to Elodie, her face arranged itself into courtesy the way a mask settles onto a stand.

"The bearer walks alone," Ashvine said. Her voice was low and resonant, a cello played in an empty hall. "Good. Secrets keep better between two."

"I wasn't aware we'd be sharing any."

"Everyone shares secrets in Mirabel, Elodie Underhill. Some sooner, some later. Some only after they've been opened like oysters." She gestured, and a bench of pale wood appeared where no bench had been — or had always been, waiting to be noticed. "Sit. I promise you will want your legs under you before this conversation ends."

Elodie did not sit. It felt important, suddenly, not to sit — one of the few small territories she still controlled. "Then say what you brought me here to say."

Ashvine studied her for a long moment. In the starlight her eyes were the gray-green of deep water over drowned things.

"You hate it here," the regent said at last, and there was something almost gentle in it. "The palace. The court. The way the halls move and the courtiers smile with too many teeth. You have spent seven years learning to be small and quiet and unremarkable, and now you are the most remarkable thing in a realm that eats remarkable things for breakfast. I know how that feels. I was mortal-adjacent once myself, long ago — half-blooded, tolerated, never quite belonging to either world." She smiled, thin as a paper cut. "So let us not waste each other's time with ceremony. I am going to make you an offer. And I am going to make it honestly, which in this court makes me either a fool or the most dangerous creature present."

"Both, possibly."

"Both, certainly." Ashvine began to walk, slow along the white gravel, and Elodie fell into step beside her without deciding to. The humming fountains kept their distance, as if eavesdropping were beneath them. "You have seen what Mirabel is now. Do not pretend you haven't. You have eyes, and you have blood that remembers what this place used to be."

Elodie had seen. They all had. The city of impossible architecture was dying by inches — seasons leaking out of their proper quarters, a district locked in eternal autumn whose leaves never finished falling, towers whose foundations had forgotten stone and drifted toward glass. The courtiers were elegant and hollow, feasting nightly on ceremonies that meant nothing, because meaning required a future and the future here had grown uncertain. Even the stars overhead, she realized now, were fewer than they should be. Someone had been counting.

"The Veil is failing," Ashvine said. "You know this. Your scholar knows it. The map has shown you, or you would not have come. What you may not know is *why* it fails, and *what* follows."

"Tam thinks—"

"I know what your scholar thinks. He thinks the anchor fails because the Veilwarden line broke. He is nearly right. The line broke, yes — hunted down by your witch-finders, scattered, burned, forgotten. But a broken line can be mended. Blood finds blood. Here you stand, aren't you proof?" She paused beside a bed of silver flowers and touched one with one finger, and it closed like a shy eye. "No. The anchor fails because it was built to be held by *many*, and for three hundred years it has been held by *one*. One bloodline. One warden at a time, spending herself against the dark, generation after generation, until the last of you was killed in a cellar in Corvane while the human kingdoms cheered."

The words landed somewhere old and bruised. Elodie thought of her mother, whom she barely remembered; of the journals in Ivenna's cottage; of the vision she'd had at the shrine, faces upon faces of women and men who had carried this weight until it crushed them.

"And you propose to mend it how?"

"By ending the need for mending." Ashvine turned to face her fully, and for the first time the mask slipped — not into cruelty, but into something rawer. Weariness. Terror, even, worn so long it had polished smooth. "Seal the Veil, Elodie. Seal it from this side, with the full power of the anchor behind you, and it will hold for ten thousand years. No more tearing. No more leaks. No more blighted Marches, no more backward birds, no more things crawling through the cracks into your world or mine. And no more war."

"People will die sealing it."

"Some. Fewer than will die if it tears." Ashvine said it flatly, without flinching, and Elodie hated how much she respected that. "I will not insult you with lies dressed as kindness. The rite requires a life given freely — not yours, necessarily. There are volunteers in this court who remember the last war and would leap at the chance. But someone must pay, and I will not pretend otherwise."

"And the other side of the Veil? My world?"

"Cut off." Ashvine spread her hands, and the gesture was almost tender. "Forever. Yes. That is the price, and I will not hide it behind pretty words. The human kingdoms lose magic entirely — which they outlawed anyway, if your witch-finders are any measure. They lose Mirabel, which they burned and plundered the last time the doors stood open. And we lose—" her voice caught, just slightly, on the word, and Elodie understood with a jolt that this, this single syllable, was the true center of everything Ashvine was — "*them.* We lose them. Their noise and their iron and their short furious lives. We lose the trade and the trespassing and the treaties and the wars. We lose the whole bleeding, brilliant mess of humanity, behind a wall no army and no ambition can ever breach again."

She stepped closer. This close, Elodie could see that the regent's age showed not in her face but in her eyes — centuries banked there like coals.

"Now hear my offer, bearer. Not the court's offer. Mine."

* * *

"You are tired," Ashvine said. "Do not bother denying it; I can smell exhaustion the way your kind smells rain coming. Seven years hiding. Seven years making yourself mediocre on purpose, shrinking your gift down to luck-charms and sleight of hand, because the world you were born into would kill you for the crime of your own blood. Then a dying man bleeds on your stairs and hands you a destiny, and you run — because running is the only skill they ever left you. You gather strays because you cannot bear to be alone and cannot admit it. You seal tears in the Veil with your own blood and collapse afterward and tell no one how much it cost. And every mile closer to the anchor, some small voice in you whispers: *what happens to me when this is over?*"

Elodie's throat was tight. "You've been watching me."

"I have been watching everyone. It is the whole of governing." Ashvine's voice dropped, went intimate, went warm — and the warmth was the most dangerous thing yet, because it sounded exactly like the voice Elodie had spent seven years longing to hear. "Here is what I give you, if you seal the Veil from my side. Belonging. Real belonging, not tolerance — a place in this court that no one can take from you, because you will have *saved* it. Power — the anchor's power, channeled through you, and unlike the Veilwardens' burden, it need not eat your years; the rite spends another life, not your own. A home in a realm where magic is not a crime but a citizenship. Where the thing you buried for seven years can grow in open air until it fills whatever shape you choose."

The words went into Elodie like water into cracked ground.

She hated that. She stood very still and let them soak in anyway, because refusing to feel them would have been its own lie, and she had promised herself — somewhere between the stairwell and the shrine — that she was done lying to herself even when the truth was shameful.

A home. Not a rented room above a charm shop with the shutters always drawn. Not a life measured in careful mediocrity, in charms sold to farmers' wives while she swallowed her own gift like a stone. A place where she could be *all* of what she was, loudly, in daylight, among people who would call it beauty instead of heresy.

And more than that — safety. The witch-finders walled away forever behind a Veil that would never crack. General Corvess and his pyres and his ledgers of the condemned, sealed on the far side of eternity. Brannoc's nightmares, Sera's grief, Tam's heresy charges — all of it locked away with the world that made them.

"You could stay here," Ashvine said softly. "After the sealing. Live in the Night Garden, if you like it. Grow things. Teach. Be *someone*, Elodie Underhill, instead of a woman perpetually halfway out a window with a packed bag." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "Or go back, if you must. Go back to Corvane, to mediocrity, to looking over your shoulder until the witch-finders finally get lucky. Go back to being nothing, in a world that has already told you what it does to people like you. The choice, of course, is yours."

The garden hummed. Somewhere far off, in the palace's shifting body, a bell rang a hour that did not exist.

"It isn't," Elodie said.

Ashvine tilted her head. "Explain."

"You're asking me to buy my freedom with half the world." Her voice came out steadier than she expected, and she clung to that steadiness like a rail in floodwater. "Every farmer's wife who bought a luck-charm off me. Every hedge-witch like Mistress Ivenna, every child born with sparks in their fingers who hasn't learned to hide yet. You're asking me to wall them in with the people who burn them — forever — so that I can have a garden." She laughed, and it came out cracked. "That's not belonging you're offering. That's a bribe. A very generous one. But I've been poor my whole life, Lady Ashvine, and I know what it looks like when someone offers you everything you've ever wanted and asks you to look away from where it comes from."

Something flickered across the regent's face — anger, or grief, or the place where those two waters met and could not be told apart.

"You think I do not understand temptation?" Ashvine said, and now the warmth was gone, and what remained was older and sadder. "I have wanted this — the sealing — for three hundred years. I watched the last war, child. I stood in a field in the Gray Marches after the human armies passed through, and I counted the dead of my own house until counting failed me. Eleven thousand. I was related to four hundred of them. When I say the Veil must never tear, I am not speaking politics. I am speaking the names of the dead." She drew breath, and the garden dimmed around her, the silver flowers closing all at once, as if the night itself were listening. "But hear the rest of it, since you insist on honesty. If the Veil tears — truly tears, the anchor failing, the hinge breaking — it will not be a door opening. It will be a wound. Both worlds will bleed into each other until neither can tell itself apart. Your witch-finders will get their war, and my knights will give them one, and it will end the way the last one ended, except this time there will be no Veilwarden left to close it. No one left at all, perhaps. I am offering you the only future in which *everyone you love survives* — just not everyone, everywhere. I am offering you arithmetic. Cruel, clean arithmetic."

"And I'm telling you arithmetic is how wars start." Elodie's hands had curled into fists without her permission. "Somebody always counts first. Somebody always decides which lives are the ones worth keeping, and it's never their own neighbors, never their own family, never *themselves* — it's always the ones on the other side of some line. You want me to draw the biggest line there is and call it peace."

"And what do you want, bearer?" Ashvine's eyes fixed on hers, and the full weight of the regent's attention came down like a change in pressure, like the moment before lightning. "Truly. Not the brave answer. Not the answer you'll perform for your little band of strays tomorrow. At three in the morning, in the dark, when no one is watching — what does Elodie Underhill want?"

And the terrible thing, the thing Elodie would carry out of that garden like a splinter under the skin, was that she knew the answer. Knew it instantly, completely, shamefully.

She wanted to stop running. She wanted a door that stayed where she'd left it, a room that was hers, a life that didn't require her to be smaller every year. She wanted to say *yes* so badly that her whole body ached with it, the way a starving body aches at the smell of bread.

"I want," she said slowly, "to live in a world where I don't have to be offered this bargain at all."

Ashvine held her gaze a moment longer. Then, quietly, she laughed — a real laugh, brief and startled out of her, the first honest sound Elodie had heard from her.

"Well answered," the regent said. "Wrong, I think. But well answered." She turned away, back toward the tree-shaped doorway at the garden's heart, and her voice cooled back into ceremony. "The offer stands until the anchor. Think on it. Sleep on it, if sleep will come. And bear in mind, Elodie Underhill, that refusal has consequences of its own. The court is patient, but patience is not the same as agreement. There are those among my knights who see no reason why the bearer should choose at all — why the choice should not simply be... *made for her.*"

Elodie went cold. "Is that a threat?"

"It is a weather report." Ashvine glanced back, and the mask was perfect again, and somehow that was worse. "I am telling you what is in the sky. Whether it rains is between you and the sky." She paused at the threshold of the tree-door, silhouetted against corridor-gold. "One thing more. A gift, freely given, no strings — judge for yourself whether I'm capable of such a thing. Your soldier, Hale. His wounds fester. Human medicine is failing him. I can have him healed by morning — fully, cleanly, by arts your world forgot. All you need do is ask."

* * *

Elodie walked back through the shifting corridors alone, and the palace rearranged itself around her with a courtesy that felt like surveillance.

Brannoc. Feverish for two days now, gray around the mouth, insisting he was fine in the voice men use when they are deciding how much time they have left. She could ask. One sentence, spoken to a servant, and by morning he'd be whole — and the debt would sit there between her and the court, unnamed but understood, softening her exactly the way Ashvine intended. Kindness as a leash.

Or she could refuse, and learn by morning whether deserters from the human world were permitted to die in the Hollow Court's guest chambers, and what message that would send to Sera, to Tam, to Rue — *this is what happens to those who follow her.*

There was no clean choice. Ashvine had built the trap out of mercy precisely because Elodie would walk around any trap built of threats.

She reached her chamber and found Tam asleep over the map, quill still in hand, ink dried on his fingers. Sera sat awake in the corner, whetting a knife she never seemed to use, and looked up when the door opened.

"Well?" Sera said. "What did the spider want?"

"To offer me everything I've ever wanted."

Sera snorted. "And?"

"And it costs half the world." Elodie crossed to the window. Far below, the dying city of Mirabel glowed under its thinning stars, beautiful as a lamp about to go out. "She also offered to heal Brannoc."

Sera's knife stopped moving. "For free."

"For one question, asked politely."

"Then don't ask it." Sera returned to the blade, but her voice had gone quieter. "Nothing in this place is free. My father learned that. Look where it got him."

Elodie looked at the window a long time. Somewhere below, in a fever-bed, a man who had once burned a village rather than burn it wrongly was fighting for his life, and she held in her throat the one word that could save him, and the word belonged to Lady Ashvine.

In the morning, she decided, she would find another way. There was always another way. There had to be.

She was still telling herself that when the bells of the Hollow Court began to ring — all of them at once, an alarm she hadn't heard in her two days in the palace — and Rue's voice came down the corridor, sharp and urgent, calling her name.
