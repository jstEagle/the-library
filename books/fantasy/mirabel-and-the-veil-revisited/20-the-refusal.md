## 20. The Refusal

The silence after Elodie spoke was the kind that had weight — a held breath in a room full of people who had not needed to breathe for centuries.

She stood on the dais steps of the Hollow Court with the map pressed against her ribs where Lady Ashvine could see it, and she said it again, because the first time had come out too quiet and she refused to let them mistake her.

"No."

The word fell into the great hall like a stone into still water. Around her, the court rippled. The fae did not gasp — they were too old and too proud for that — but their faces turned, all at once, like flowers following a sun that had just said something rude. Their beauty made it worse. A hundred flawless faces rearranging themselves around contempt, each one more elegant than the last, each one looking at her as though she were mud tracked across a marble floor.

Ashvine did not move. She sat upon the Hollow Crown's throne — a chair of white wood grown, not built, its branches bare and reaching — and she regarded Elodie with an expression that might have been patience in a younger face. On Ashvine it looked like a cat watching a mouse decide which direction to run.

"Consider what you refuse," Ashvine said. Her voice filled the hall without effort, the way cold fills a cellar. "You are a hedge-witch's leftover. A charm-seller who hides behind mediocrity in a city that would burn you for a fraction of what runs in your blood. And here I offer you—" she gestured, and the hall answered her, torchlight blooming gold along the walls, the stacked seasons turning overhead so that snow became blossom became high summer in a single slow breath, "—*this*. A home no human has been offered in four hundred years. Power your grandmother's grandmother could not have imagined. Belonging." Her gaze sharpened. "You have spent seven years belonging to nothing. I am offering you everything, and you say *no* as if the word cost you nothing."

"It costs me half the world," Elodie said.

Her voice shook. She let it. There was no point pretending steadiness she didn't feel, and besides — she had learned something in seven years of selling luck-charms to desperate people: honesty carried further than performance. "You're asking me to seal the Veil from your side. To cut off every farmer, every child, every ordinary soul on the other side of that border from — from whatever magic might have saved them, or healed them, or simply *wondered* at them. You're asking me to choose Mirabel over everyone I've ever known. Everyone I've ever *been*."

"I am asking you to choose survival," Ashvine said. "Yours. Ours. The Veil fails, Underhill girl. You have seen the tears. You know what comes through when it tears. When the anchor falls entirely, this realm dies — and when this realm dies, everything it leaks into dies with it, slowly, beautifully, over a hundred years. Sealing the Veil saves both worlds. It merely saves yours *without* us in it."

"And mine," Elodie said, "saves both worlds *with* us in it. Together. Or it tries to."

"Trying is how wars begin."

"Failing together is how wars end," Tam said quietly, at her shoulder.

Elodie didn't look at him. She kept her eyes on Ashvine, because looking away now would be surrender, and because some stubborn animal part of her understood that this was a contest of gazes as much as words. But she heard Tam shift his weight, heard Brannoc's hand settle near his sword hilt behind her — not drawing it, just *near*, the way a man stands when he has counted the exits and found none he likes. Sera stood on her other side, arms crossed, jaw set, radiating the particular fury of someone who had followed a stranger into a fairy court and intended to get her money's worth of grievance out of it.

"You are young," Ashvine said at last, rising from the throne.

The hall went very still. Fae did not rise for humans. Fae did not rise for anyone.

She descended the dais steps slowly, her gown moving like poured ink, and stopped one step above Elodie — close enough that Elodie could smell her, cold stone and night-blooming flowers and underneath it something older, something like the air before lightning. Up close her beauty was almost painful, and her eyes were the color of deep winter water, and they were *tired*. That was the thing no one had warned Elodie about. The regent of Mirabel was magnificent and terrible and exhausted, worn thin as old paper by centuries of holding a dying realm together with elegance and denial.

"I buried three hundred of my knights in the last war," Ashvine said, softly now, only for Elodie. "I watched the Veilwardens seal it and I thanked them, because it ended the dying. Do you know what sealing cost my people? We are creatures of crossing, little witch. Of thresholds and borders and the space between. They walled us into our own house and called it peace. Four hundred years we have been suffocating politely. And now the walls rot, and you stand there and tell me you will not even give us a clean death — you will give us *hope* instead. Hope is crueler."

"I know," Elodie said. "I hid from hope for seven years. It's why I'm standing here."

Something flickered across Ashvine's face — grief, or its aristocratic cousin — and then it hardened into something much worse.

"So be it." She stepped back, ascended two steps, and turned, and her voice rang through the hall with all its old cold authority. "The bearer refuses the crown's courtesy. Let the court consider what that means."

It meant, Elodie discovered, that the doors closed.

Not slammed — fae did nothing so vulgar — but the great doors at the far end of the hall swung shut with the soft finality of a book closing, and the torches dimmed to a blue-green gloom, and the courtiers began, one by one, to smile.

"We should go," Brannoc murmured. "Now."

"The doors—"

"Won't open for walking," Sera said grimly. "Everything here is a test or a trap. Usually both."

"Then we are honored guests until we find the door that isn't," Elodie said, and hated how thin her bravado sounded, and said it anyway, because the alternative was letting them hear her fear, and she'd spent seven years hiding things; she wasn't going to start handing them out free.

They walked. The court parted before them the way water parts around a drowned man — closing again behind, watchful. Somewhere in the gallery a string instrument began to play, slow and sweet, a melody Elodie's blood recognized before her mind did: a farewell song. A dirge dressed as a waltz. The fae were playing music for their departure, and the message in it was unmistakable. *Guests who refuse the host's gift leave by the river.*

There was a river under the capital. Tam had told them about it on the road — the Lethewater, the forgetting-river, where the court sent those whose presence had become inconvenient. Not killed. *Unmade.* Walked in whole, washed out blank, released into the mortal world as smiling strangers with no past and no map and no name.

"They mean to drown us in it," Tam whispered. "Politely. Over dinner, probably. There will be a toast."

"Then we skip dinner," Elodie said.

* * *

The capital of Mirabel did not have streets so much as suggestions.

Elodie had noticed it on the way in, through the hidden gate: corridors that curved when they should have run straight, staircases that arrived somewhere other than where their angle promised, windows that showed gardens on the third floor and rooftops on the first. She had thought it architecture. Walking back out, hunted, she understood it was immune system. The city knew who belonged and who did not, and it rearranged itself accordingly, gently, endlessly, the way a body shifts in sleep to expel a splinter.

An hour after leaving the hall — if hours meant anything here; the light through the high windows cycled between dusk and dawn with no loyalty to either — they were lost.

Properly lost. The corridor they had entered through led to a courtyard they had never crossed, and the courtyard's single gate opened onto a colonnade that bent left and kept bending until it met its own beginning. Twice they passed the same fountain, a dry basin carved with drowning figures, and the second time the figures' faces were different. The third time, the fountain was full, and the water flowed upward.

"This is deliberate," Sera said, kicking at a wall that rippled faintly under her boot like the skin of a drum. "The city's doing it. Or the court is. Either way we're rats in a maze with a sense of humor."

"Then we need a rat who knows the maze," Elodie said.

And there, leaning against a pillar at the colonnade's far curve as though he had been waiting for them since before they arrived — which, knowing him, he might have been — stood Rue.

He wore exile's gray, unadorned, and he looked as elegant in it as the courtiers did in their splendor, which was somehow an insult to all of them. His fox-sharp face held its usual expression of amused detachment, but his eyes moved over the group quickly, professionally: counting, assessing, noting Brannoc's sword-hand and Sera's knife and the way Elodie stood slightly in front of the map.

"Lady Ashvine offers you a clean death by dinner," he said pleasantly. "The Lethewater takes about a minute. Very peaceful. You forget your mother's face, then your own, then the difference between the two. Most guests thank the court afterward."

"You're one of them," Sera snarled, hand on her knife. "You sold us the audience. You brought us here."

"I did." Rue inclined his head, unbothered. "And now I'm offering you the way out. Which, incidentally, is the same door I offered you on the way in, except you were too busy gaping at the hanging gardens to mark it."

"Why?" Elodie asked.

It came out harder than she intended. She was tired — bone-tired, map-tired, tired down to the place where the Veilwarden inheritance lived in her blood like a banked coal. She wanted to trust no one. Trusting no one had kept her alive for seven years. But she looked at Rue — at the exile's gray, at the careful emptiness of his hands, at the way his gaze kept sliding, despite himself, toward the high windows where the stacked seasons turned — and she saw it again, the thing she'd glimpsed in the borderlands: homesickness like a wound that had never been allowed to close.

He was exiled from the only home he had. And Ashvine had done it. Tam had told them that much, in the hall's antechamber, in a whisper: *his treason was manufactured, or exaggerated, or invented outright — ask him sometime why the regent feared him enough to lie.*

"You want the map," she said. "That's why. You've hunted it for decades. This is just another way to take it."

"Yes," Rue said simply. "I want the map. I have wanted it since before your grandmother drew breath. And I could have taken it a dozen times on the road — did you truly believe the bandits at Graywater Ford found you by accident? Or the thing in the marsh? I tested you, bearer. Repeatedly. You passed every test I set, and several you didn't notice." He pushed off the pillar and walked toward them, unhurried, and Brannoc's blade cleared its scabbard halfway before Elodie raised a hand.

"What I want," Rue continued, stopping an arm's length away, close enough that she could see the faint silver tracery of old scars along his throat — exile's marks, cut by his own people, "is to go home. And I have finally, painfully, over the course of several extremely irritating weeks, accepted that the map will not open the Veil for me. It isn't a key to a lock. It's a key to a *door*, and doors have two sides, and the woman carrying it keeps insisting on opening rather than breaking it." His mouth curved, wry and bitter. "I have also accepted that Lady Ashvine does not intend to fix the anchor at all. She intends to let it fail — slowly, controlledly — while she searches for a bearer pliable enough to seal the Veil on *her* terms. You were never an offer, Elodie Underhill. You were an audition."

The courtyard around them shivered. Somewhere beyond the colonnade, bells began to ring — soft, silvery, wrong, a sound like the memory of bells. The light through the windows dimmed toward true dark, and the dark had texture to it, and shapes moving in it.

"They've stopped being polite," Brannoc said flatly.

"The court's patience lasts exactly as long as its curiosity," Rue said. "Yours ran out the moment you said *no* twice. Now. I can guide you out through the shifting streets — the city obeys the court, but it obeys older things too, and I remember the older things. Or you can stay and meet the Unmade, who are lovely conversationalists and terrible dancers." He extended one elegant hand, palm up. An offering. A gamble. "Well, bearer?"

Every instinct Elodie owned told her to refuse. Every instinct had been wrong lately — wrong about the map, wrong about Tam, wrong about Brannoc, wrong about Sera, wrong about the whole miserable beautiful terrifying truth that she could not do any of this alone. Seven years of solitude had taught her self-reliance, and self-reliance had brought her precisely here: surrounded, hunted, in a dying fairy city, weighing whether to accept help from a manipulator who admitted, to her face, that he'd been testing her like a lab specimen.

But he hadn't lied about wanting the door open. And neither did she.

She took his hand.

His grip was cool and dry and startlingly careful, as though she were something breakable he'd decided, against his better judgment, to keep intact.

"Don't make me regret this," she said.

"Oh, bearer," Rue said, turning and leading them at last against the colonnade's curve, into a passage that had definitely not existed a moment ago, "regret is the one thing in Mirabel that's free."

* * *

He moved them through the capital like a needle through cloth.

Where the streets had bent against them, they straightened for him — not obediently, but grudgingly, the way an old dog obeys a master it resents. He took them down stairs that descended into rooms higher than the ones they'd left. He walked them through a doorway that was a window from the other side. Once he stopped before a blank wall, pressed two fingers to a carving Elodie couldn't see, and murmured something in a language that made the map against her ribs heat and hum in answer, and the wall opened like a mouth.

Behind them, always, the bells. And beneath the bells, softer, worse: footsteps. Many. Graceful. Unhurried. The court did not run after prey. The court simply arranged itself ahead of it.

"They'll send knights to the gate," Rue said, taking a corner so sharply Sera nearly lost him. "The obvious exits are already closed. So we don't use the gates."

"Then what—" Brannoc began.

Rue smiled, and for the first time since Elodie had known him, the smile had no performance in it at all — just wildness, just delight, just a homesick creature finally running toward something instead of circling it.

"There's a door," he said, "that hasn't opened since the last war. The court forgot it because the court forgets anything that stops flattering it. It opens onto the pilgrim road — the Anchor Road — three days ahead of where we entered. It opens, specifically, for blood of the Veilwardens." He glanced back at Elodie, eyes bright and sharp as winter stars. "It has been waiting four hundred years for you, bearer. Try not to keep it waiting longer. Run."

They ran.

Through halls where the seasons bled into one another, autumn rain falling upward into spring. Through galleries where portraits of the old courts watched them pass, and Elodie would have sworn — would swear it later, on the road, in the firelight — that one painted woman with an Underhill jaw turned her head.

Behind them the bells stopped ringing all at once, and in the ringing's absence came a sound like frost forming, like a decision crystallizing, and Rue's face went grim and beautiful and afraid.

"They've loosed the Hunt," he breathed. "*Run faster.*"

Ahead, at the end of a corridor that had finally, mercifully, stopped lying about where it led, a door of pale living wood stood waiting — bare branches carved into its grain, and at its heart, glowing faintly awake, the shape of a tree of light.

The anchor. The map's promise. Everything.

And behind them, filling the corridor from wall to wall, the Hunt came howling.
