## 21. Rue's Treason

The exile's road had no milestones. That was the first thing Rue had learned about banishment, three centuries ago, when the gates of Mirabel closed behind him like a mouth finishing a sentence: the world of men did not mark the places where the fae walked through it, because the world of men preferred not to think about them at all. He had crossed from his own realm into this gray, stubborn country with nothing but the clothes he wore and a name that was no longer safe to speak, and in all the years since, he had never once found a signpost that said *this way home*.

He had made his own maps instead. Hundreds of them, drawn in ink mixed with his own blood so they would hold their shape against the Veil's slow breathing. He kept them rolled in a case of white leather across his back, and he consulted them now, standing on a hill above the pilgrim road where the company below had made its evening camp, and he admitted — not for the first time, but with more honesty than usual — that every map he had ever made was wrong.

Not inaccurate. Wrong. Wrong in the way a key is wrong for a lock it was never cut to fit.

Below him, the campfire burned small and disciplined, the way fires burned when a soldier tended them. Brannoc Hale sat closest to it, cleaning a blade with the mechanical patience of a man who had nothing else to say to the world. Tam Vess hunched over her satchel by the light, papers spread on her knees, muttering to the map as though it were a colleague who had disappointed her. Sera Vane stood watch at the edge of the light with a crossbow she held like an apology, scanning the dark for enemies she could name — witch-finders, fae knights, anything with a shape she could put a quarrel into.

And at the center of it, wrapped in a borrowed cloak, sat Elodie Underhill, turning her hands over and over as if checking whether they still belonged to her.

Rue watched her the way he had once watched the court of the Hollow Crown: cataloguing, measuring, waiting for the flaw. It was what exile taught you. Exile taught you that everyone wanted something, and that wanting was the only honest language left.

He had wanted, once. He had wanted so loudly that it had unmade him.

* * *

Three hundred years ago — or near enough; the fae counted time the way rivers counted stones, by what they wore away — Rue had been a courtier of the second circle, a maker of doors. Not carpenter's doors. *Thresholds.* In Mirabel, where seasons were stacked like rooms and a corridor might open onto midwinter or high summer depending on the mood of the stone, someone had to decide where one thing ended and another began. Rue had been very good at deciding. He had been so good at it that the regent herself had kept him close, and Lady Ashvine had smiled at him with that particular courtesy she reserved for useful things, and for two hundred years he had believed himself content.

Then he had walked the anchor road.

Every courtier made the pilgrimage eventually — out of piety, or curiosity, or the vague dread that came from serving a crown with nothing under it. The anchor lay at the hinge of the world, a tree of living light whose roots threaded down through both realms at once, holding the Veil taut between them like a drumhead. And Rue had stood beneath it, as thousands had before him, and felt it *straining.*

No one else seemed to feel it. Or perhaps they felt it and had agreed, over long centuries, to call the straining something else — tradition, sanctity, the natural order. But Rue had put his hand against the light and felt the whole vast membrane of the world tremble like a sail in weather, and he had understood two things in the same heartbeat: that the Veil was dying, and that everyone who mattered had decided to let it die slowly rather than admit it was dying at all.

Because the Veil was not merely a wall. It was a wound dressed as a wall. It had been raised after the last war — the war the human kingdoms remembered as a saga and the fae remembered as a funeral — and it had been raised *by* the Veilwardens, that doomed mortal line, who had bound themselves into its fabric generation after generation until there were almost none of them left. The fae called it wisdom. The humans called it peace. Rue, standing under the anchor with his hand burning, had called it a lie told so long ago that both worlds had forgotten it was a lie.

His solution had been elegant. Everything he did was elegant; it was the one vanity exile had never managed to scour off him. Open the Veil — not tear it, *open* it, deliberately, at chosen crossings, under law. Let the realms touch again in daylight instead of bleeding into each other in the dark. End the isolation that was strangling Mirabel season by season, magic thinning like wine cut with water, the court rotting into elegance because elegance was all that was left when a people refuse to change. And end, too, the human fear — for fear fed on ignorance, and ignorance fed the witch-finders, and the witch-finders burned hedge-witches in market squares while the fae behind the Veil pretended not to hear the smoke.

He had written a treatise. Of course he had. Forty-one pages, illuminated, argued, addressed respectfully to the regent. He had presented it at court with a bow of exactly the correct depth.

Lady Ashvine had read it in silence while the whole court watched, and then she had looked up at him with an expression he would spend three centuries trying to forget — not anger, but *pity*, the pity of a woman watching a child reach toward a stove — and she had said, gently, "You wish to open the door our dead died to close."

And then she had opened *his* door instead, and put him through it, and closed it behind him.

Treason, they called it. He had worn the word like a coat at first, defiantly, then like a scar, then simply like skin. Three hundred years among mortals, aging slower than any of them, burying acquaintances by the dozen, learning to eat their food and speak their languages and flinch from iron. He had tried everything. He had searched for the anchor's human-side approaches. He had hunted rumors of surviving Veilwardens. And for the last seven years — ever since a dying courier named Vane had passed through a village carrying something wrapped in oilcloth, something that *sang* to Rue's senses across half a country — he had hunted the map.

The Veilwarden chart. The key. The one object in either world that could find the failing places and, if the old rites were true, remake them.

He had wanted it to force the door open. He had told himself that was justice. He had told himself a great many things, in three hundred years of telling himself things.

* * *

What he had not told himself, until recently, was what he saw now, watching the firelight move across Elodie Underhill's face.

She was afraid. She had been afraid since the day the map chose her — he had felt her fear before he ever saw her, a bright thread of mortal terror running through the Veil-thin air of the Gray Marches, unmistakable as blood in water. And yet she had sealed a tear in the Veil with her own blood, knowing what it cost, knowing nothing, and then she had stood in the poisoned courtesy of the Hollow Crown's court and refused Lady Ashvine to her face — refused a home, a belonging, everything Rue had spent three centuries clawing at the walls of his cage for.

She had refused it and run, and Rue had run with her, and somewhere in the shifting streets of Mirabel's capital, dodging fae knights through corridors that rearranged themselves like a liar's story, he had understood that he was no longer running toward the map.

He was running toward whatever she meant to do with it.

That was the treason. Not the old one — Ashvine had already taken that title from him, spent it, made it cheap. This was a new treason, and it was against himself.

Because he knew what she intended. He had heard her say it around the fire, haltingly, defensively, the night after they fled the court: not sealing the Veil forever, not opening it wide, but binding it — binding it to a council, to living hands from both worlds, to *many* instead of *one*. A covenant instead of a bloodline. A door with locks that everyone could see, instead of a wall that everyone had to pretend was eternal.

It was, he thought, watching her rub her mortal hands in the firelight, a naive idea. It was impractical, unprecedented, politically impossible, and dependent entirely on the goodwill of two species that had spent a millennium teaching each other to be afraid.

It was also the only idea he had heard in three hundred years that did not require someone to lose.

Sealing the Veil forever meant Mirabel starved slowly behind its beautiful walls, and the human world forgot magic until it grew afraid enough to burn again. Opening it by force meant war — the old war, refought with thinner magic and sharper iron, ending in ash on both sides of the drumhead. Ashvine's way was a slow death. Corvess's way — and Rue had felt the witch-finder general's cold attention tracking them across the Marches like a hand on the back of the neck — was a fast one. Every path but hers led through a graveyard.

A self-serving creature, his mother's court had called him, back when he'd had a mother's court to be called things by. They had meant it as praise. Self-interest was honest; self-interest kept the courts running. And Rue had been self-serving for three hundred years, faithfully, diligently, the way other men attended church.

But self-interest, he was discovering, required a self worth serving. And his self — the elegant exile, the maker of doors, the traitor with the beautiful treatise — had wanted one thing for three centuries, and the wanting had hollowed him into a keyhole-shaped man, and now here was a tired hedge-witch with mortal hands and inherited blood saying aloud, in front of soldiers and scholars and a grieving girl with a crossbow, *no one has to lose* —

— and the terrible thing, the genuinely humiliating thing, was that he believed her.

* * *

He came down the hill as the moon rose, because exile had also taught him that if you must do a foolish thing, do it while your courage is fresh.

Sera saw him first — she always did; grief had sharpened her senses to a fine and unforgiving edge — and the crossbow came up, casual and constant, the way it always did when he approached. He had stopped taking it personally. Mostly.

"The perimeter holds," he said pleasantly. "Your witch-finders are half a day behind and arguing with each other. Your lady knight is further, and not arguing at all."

"Then why are you down here?" Sera said. "You hover up there like a heron. Herons have reasons."

"Perhaps I am a heron."

"You're a spy," Sera said, without heat. It was simply a fact she kept in inventory, like her quarrels. "We all know it. We keep you because Elodie says you know the roads."

"I keep myself," Rue said, "because I know the roads," and he stepped past her into the firelight before his nerve could reconsider.

They looked up at him — the soldier, the scholar, the girl, and the witch. Brannoc's hand had gone, without hurry, to the knife beside his knee. Tam pushed her spectacles up and studied him with the expression she reserved for texts with disputed provenance. Elodie simply watched, and her watching was the worst of it, because she watched the way the anchor had once watched him: as if she could feel him straining.

"I've been thinking," Rue began, and then stopped, because the speech he had prepared — three centuries of practice at courtly address, wasted — sounded, in this firelit circle of mortals, absurd. Elegant. Hollow.

So he said the true thing instead, which was the hardest thing he had said since the gates closed.

"When I wrote my treatise," he said, "I wanted the Veil opened because I wanted to go home. I told myself it was for both worlds. It was mostly for me. I have spent three hundred years telling myself it was mostly for me, and I have hated both courts for branding me a traitor for wanting it, and I have hated them more for being right about my reasons even when they were wrong about my conclusions." He drew a breath. Mortal lungs; he wore a mortal-seeming body these days, most of the time, because the full truth of his shape frightened horses and children. "I followed this company for the map. I will not insult you by pretending otherwise. I intended to take it to the anchor and use it, and I did not much care what happened to any of you when I did."

Tam said quietly, "And now?"

"And now I have watched her seal a tear with her own blood," Rue said, looking at Elodie, "and refuse a queen, and keep walking anyway, on legs that shake, toward a thing that is eating her years — and I have heard her say that no one has to lose, and I have discovered that I want it to be true more than I want my gate open. More than I want home." His voice did something unfamiliar on the last words; he let it. "That is my treason. The first one cost me Mirabel. I am telling you this one in advance, so you may weigh whether to trust me, because I am done choosing hope in secret. Hope chosen in secret is just ambition wearing a better coat."

The fire cracked. Somewhere in the dark, an owl called — or something that wished to be mistaken for an owl.

Brannoc spoke first, which surprised Rue; the soldier rarely spent words. "Why tell us now?"

"Because tomorrow we cross the borderlands where the Veil is thinnest," Rue said, "and what hunts us will stop hunting politely. Because you deserve to know what walks with you. And because—" he hesitated, and hated the hesitation, and finished anyway— "because I have learned, watching all of you, that secrets are how both my worlds ended up dying. I am attempting to break the habit."

Elodie had not spoken. She was still watching him with those gray eyes that had seen too much for twenty-some mortal years, and when she finally did speak, it was not the question he expected.

"What did it feel like?" she asked softly. "When the gates closed. When Ashvine sent you through."

Rue considered lying. It was his native language, and she had just given him a perfect opening to speak it fluently.

"Cold," he said. "Like a book closing with your hand still inside it."

Something moved across her face — recognition, old and worn smooth by seven years of hiding. She nodded slowly, and then she said the second thing he did not expect.

"Then you walk with us," she said. "Not as a guide. As one of us. If you mean it — if the treason's real — then prove it the same way we all have. By staying."

Rue bowed — not the courtly bow, the shallow one, the one that meant nothing. He bowed the way exiles bow: deeply, and longer than dignity allows.

"Then I should tell you," he said, straightening, and letting the last of the courtier fall away from his voice like a dropped cloak, "that the reason the witch-finders are arguing is that General Corvess has split his force. Half of them are riding for the anchor road ahead of us. He means to be there first."

Tam went pale in the firelight. "How far ahead?"

"A day," Rue said. "Perhaps less. And the other half—" he glanced at Sera, and gentled his tone in a way that cost him more than the confession had— "the other half is following a tracker. One attuned to the map. Which means, Miss Vane, that whoever they captured at the ford has given them what they needed."

Sera's face went white. "Tam."

"They have Tam," Brannoc said quietly, and set down the blade he had been cleaning, and reached for his sword instead.

The owl in the dark called again — closer now, and wrong, two notes where an owl makes one.

Rue turned toward the sound, and felt the Veil shiver along its whole thin length, like a drumhead remembering it could be struck.

"And," he said, "they are no longer alone on our trail. My former queen's knights have crossed the border. Ashvine marches."

The fire burned small and disciplined in the circle of faces, and far to the north, past the hills, past the Marches, at the hinge of the world, the anchor waited — and three armies were racing toward it, and only one of them carried a Veilwarden.

"We walk tonight," Elodie said, rising, and the map against her chest glowed faintly through her shirt like a heart deciding to beat faster.
