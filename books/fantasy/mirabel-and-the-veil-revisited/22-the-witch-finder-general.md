## 22. The Witch-Finder General

The candle in General Aldous Corvess's study had burned down to a stub, and still he did not call for a new one.

He sat in the dark with the ledger open before him, the way his father had sat before him, and his grandfather before that — three generations of Corvesses keeping the same accounts in the same careful hand. Not gold. Not grain. Names. The ledger held names: every hedge-witch burned, every charm-seller hanged, every child taken to the Foundling Houses to be scrubbed of whatever their mothers had passed to them in milk and blood. Four hundred years of entries. His grandfather's hand was cramped and fierce. His father's was neat as scripture. His own, he liked to think, was the cleanest of all — a soldier's hand, unhurried, certain that what it wrote needed writing.

He turned the page and found the entry he had been looking for, though he had known it by heart since he was nine years old, when his father had first set him on his knee at this very desk and read it aloud like a psalm.

*Underhill, Maren. Veilwarden line, confirmed by blood-rite. Executed at the Ashford pyre with two others of the order. Surviving issue: none recorded.*

His father had closed the ledger then and said, *Remember this name, boy. Remember what her kind cost us.* And Aldous had remembered. He had built his whole life inside that remembering, the way other men built houses inside walls.

Now he dipped his pen and added a new line beneath the old one, in ink so fresh it shone.

*Underhill, Elodie. Line presumed broken. Status: unconfirmed.*

The presumption troubled him. Presumption was where witch-finders went to die.

* * *

The war room beneath the Citadel of Corvane smelled of tallow, wet wool, and fear — the fear belonged mostly to the men standing along its walls, because no one stood in General Corvess's war room unless they had been summoned, and no one was summoned twice.

On the great table lay a map of the western provinces, weighted at the corners with lead seals. Corvess did not look at it. He looked instead at Captain Veyra, who stood at rigid attention and wished very much that she were somewhere else.

"Tell me again," Corvess said pleasantly, "what you told me this morning."

Veyra's throat moved. "That the raid on Tanners' Row produced nothing, sir."

"And now?"

"Now we have a dead man in the river district. No papers. No trade marks on his hands. A knife wound that a surgeon says was dressed by someone who knew what she was doing — field-dressing, army style, but not army thread." Veyra recited it like a confession. "And a witness. A dyer's widow on Candlemaker's Stair says she saw a woman carrying him up before dawn. Small woman. Brown hair. She sells charms at the Lamplight Market — luck-knots, fever-strings, nothing that would hang her under the low statutes."

"But?"

Veyra hesitated one breath too long.

"But," Corvess said for her, "the dead man bled on the stair. And when our reader went to take an impression of the blood—" he watched her face as he said it— "the blood had already been scoured away. Every drop. Scrubbed with lye and vinegar by someone who understood exactly why it mattered. Isn't that right, Captain?"

"Yes, sir."

"Do you know how many people in Corvane understand why a few drops of dried blood matter enough to scour a stairwell at risk of being caught at it?" He let the silence do its work. "I do. I can count them without taking off my boots. So either our charm-seller is a murderer with an education she shouldn't have, or she is something considerably more interesting." He straightened. "Bring her in. Gently. I want her comfortable, cooperative, and utterly unaware that she is being brought anywhere until the door closes behind her."

"And if she runs, sir?"

Corvess finally looked at the map on the table — at the western edge of it, where the cartographers' ink faded into a blank margin that had been blank for four hundred years, ever since the last war, ever since the Cleansing, ever since his family's finest hour.

"Then she confirms everything," he said, "and we hunt her properly. Have the hounds readied. The old ones."

Veyra went pale. "Sir — the attunement hounds haven't been loosed since my grandmother's day. The rites that made them—"

"Were performed by witches, Captain, which is precisely why they work, and precisely why we keep them muzzled and starved and only half-alive." Corvess's voice stayed gentle, which those who served under him knew to be the most frightening register he owned. "There is a relic moving through my city. It has been dormant for four centuries, and it is awake, and it has found itself a bearer. You may think of her as a fugitive. I ask you to think of her as a lit fuse walking toward a powder store. Now go."

She went. Corvess remained alone with the map and the darkening room, and after a while he unlocked the drawer beneath the table and took out the thing he kept there, wrapped in oilcloth older than the Citadel itself.

It was a sword hilt. Only the hilt — the blade had been snapped off at the guard four hundred years ago, on a battlefield whose name had been deliberately struck from every record his family could reach. Silver wire wound around leather gone black as tar. And set into the pommel, dull as a cataract eye, a stone that had once been white.

A Veilwarden's blade. Taken from the body of the last warden who died fighting, by a Corvess, in the last war.

His grandfather's grandfather had carried it home as proof. Proof that the wardens were mortal. That they bled. That the boundary between Mirabel and the world of men — that shimmering, treacherous membrane through which the fae had once poured like winter through a cracked window — could be guarded by flesh, and therefore could be ended by steel.

*We did not break the Veil,* his father had told him, kneeling beside the bed, the way other fathers taught prayers. *We broke the people who held it up. The Veil came down on its own, like a wall whose builders are dead. Never forget, Aldous: we are not the villains of that story. We are the ones who ended it.*

And Aldous Corvess had never forgotten. He had simply grown old enough to notice that stories need ending again.

Because the Veil was not entirely down. It never had been. It hung patched and sagging between the worlds like a curtain in a burned house, thinning at the edges, and lately — his readers reported it, his attuned instruments confirmed it, and three villages on the Gray Marches had stopped sending word at all — lately it was fraying fast. And when it tore, the fae would come through, and they would come as they had come before, beautiful and hungry and utterly without mercy, and the kingdoms of men would burn for a hundred years while children were stolen through holes in the sky.

Unless someone ended it. Truly ended it. Sealed the wound shut forever, cauterized magic out of the human world the way a surgeon burns out rot.

Four hundred years ago, his family had tried to do exactly that, and history called them butchers. This time, Corvess intended to finish the job so thoroughly that there would be no history left to argue about it.

He wrapped the hilt back in its oilcloth, gently, the way one handles a relic. Because that was what it was. A saint's bone, if you believed the right story.

He believed the right story. He had staked everything on believing it.

* * *

Two days later, the report came up from the city, and it was worse than he had feared, and better than he had hoped.

The charm-seller was gone. Gone cleanly — too cleanly — slipping out of Corvane through the Southgate on market-day morning amid a river of carts, with a man matching the description of Tam Vess, the disgraced royal cartographer, and a second man built like a siege engine whom the gate guards swore they'd never seen before and couldn't afterward describe. As if looking at him directly slid off the mind like rain off waxed canvas.

And the hounds — Corvess stood over the kennel-master's report and felt something cold and bright kindle in his chest, the feeling he imagined other men got from love or God.

The hounds had caught the scent.

Not the woman's scent. They cared nothing for her; she was meat and breath like anyone. What they had caught, what they were straining against their chains and keening toward, was the *other* scent underneath her — old paper, old blood, old power. The map. The Veilwarden chart. The relic his family had spent four generations hunting down the last copies of, burning them in pyres alongside the people who carried them.

One copy had survived. One. And it had slept for four hundred years, and now it had woken, and it had chosen a bearer with Underhill blood in her veins, and it was pulling her west like a fish on a line.

Toward the Marches. Toward the border. Toward the anchor.

Corvess laid the kennel-master's report down and allowed himself, alone in his study, the luxury of a long slow smile.

They thought they were fleeing. Fools. Nothing fled *toward* the Gray Marches. The map was not running from him; it was running home, and home was a fixed point, and a fixed point could be besieged. He did not need to chase a fuse across three provinces. He needed only to arrive at the powder store first, or failing that, arrive at the same moment, with more soldiers.

He pulled a fresh sheet toward himself and began to write orders in his clean, unhurried hand.

To Colonel Ashe at Westmarch garrison: double the patrols on the pilgrim road. To the river forts: hold the fords. Nothing crossed the Graywater without his seal — not cart, not pilgrim, not rumor. And to the Chapter of Readers, that cloister of half-tamed hedge-talents his predecessors had kept like hunting falcons: prepare the deep rite. The one requiring a warden's blood to anchor. The one his grandfather's notes described in a hand that shook.

He paused over that last order, pen hovering.

The deep rite needed warden's blood. And here was the exquisite joke of it, the irony he suspected even Providence might appreciate: the last warden's blood in the world was currently galloping toward him of its own accord, wearing the face of a small frightened charm-seller.

He signed the orders. He sealed them. Then he opened the ledger one more time, to the page with the psalm in it, and beneath *Underhill, Elodie* he wrote the correction he had been waiting four days to write:

*Line not broken. Bearer confirmed. Correction pending.*

Pending. Because a name did not move from the left column to the right column — from the hunted to the accounted — until the work was done. His father had taught him that too. Finish the sentence, or don't write it.

Outside his window, Corvane glittered under a hard frost, ten thousand chimneys smoking, ten thousand hearths warm and magicless and safe, and beyond the western hills the sun was going down over country he had never seen but knew better than his own heartbeat: the Marches, the thin places, the hinge of the world.

Soon, he thought. Soon he would stand at the hinge with fire enough to weld it shut forever, and when the histories were written — if anyone dared write them — they would say what his father had said at the bedside, what the whole long line of Corvesses had earned the right to say:

*We are not the villains of that story.*

* * *

Three days' ride west, in a ditch beside the pilgrim road, Tam Vess woke with frost in his beard and the map humming against his ribs like a struck bell, and knew immediately that something was wrong.

He had been sleeping with the thing wrapped in three layers of wool inside his coat ever since Elodie had grudgingly conceded that the scholar who understood it should carry it — a concession she had made in the tone of a woman handing a wolf to a neighbor for safekeeping. All night it had been quiet. Docile. Almost warm.

Now it was singing.

Not aloud. Nothing so useful. It hummed along his bones, a frequency of dread, and when he fumbled it out into the gray pre-dawn light and unfolded it a hand's width, the ink was moving — not crawling the way it did for Elodie, not alive the way it was alive for her, but agitated, the way a dog paces when thunder is still hours away. Roads redrawn themselves. The western margin, that blank space where Mirabel lived and died, pulsed faintly, like a bruise.

And near the Graywater ford, where their route crossed the river, a new mark was surfacing through the parchment as if rising through water.

Tam knew cartographic notation the way priests know Latin. This mark he had seen exactly once before, in a banned folio he had copied out by hand at nineteen and then burned at twenty-two when the King's men came asking. It was the survey-sign of the Order of the Pyre. The witch-finders used it to mark ground they intended to hold.

They were marking the ford.

"Brannoc," he whispered, and then, louder, when the big man didn't stir: "Hale. Wake up. We have a problem, and I use the singular loosely."

Brannoc came awake the way he always did — all at once, silently, one hand finding his knife before his eyes finished opening. He listened to Tam's whisper with the flat patience of a man who had heard bad news professionally for twenty years.

"How long?" he asked.

"If the mark means what I think it means? Days. Perhaps less. They're not chasing us anymore, Brannoc. They're ahead of us. Someone told them where we're going." He glanced across the dead campfire to where Sera Vane slept sitting up, as she always did, back against a tree, knife in her lap — and made himself stop looking, because suspicion was a poison he couldn't yet afford. "Someone who knows the roads."

"Or the map's telling them," Brannoc said. "You've been saying the thing's alive. Living things get followed."

"That," Tam said grimly, folding the chart away, "is among the several possibilities, all of which end with us needing to cross that river before the men holding the ford decide they'd rather we didn't." He looked west, where the sky was going the color of old pewter, and where — far off, past the last blue hills — the Gray Marches waited with their spiraled trees and their backward birds, and beyond them, the hidden gate, and beyond that, everything.

Behind them, faint but unmistakable on the morning wind, came a sound that raised the hair on his arms: dogs. Many dogs. Keening.

Not tracking.

Singing toward the same destination.

Tam went to wake Elodie, and found her already awake, standing at the edge of camp with her arms wrapped around herself, staring west, and he saw by her face that she had heard the hounds too — or heard something older than hounds, something in the blood the map had woken, some inherited instinct four hundred years of Corvesses had failed to burn out of her line.

"They know where we're going," she said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"The ford is marked," Tam said. "We can go around — north, through the high passes, add a week—"

"No." She said it fast, and then slower, as if surprised by her own voice. "No. If we turn aside now, we lose the road, and the road is the only thing the map trusts us with. We cross at the ford. Tomorrow night." She turned to him, and Tam Vess, who had faced down a royal tribunal and a decade of disgrace without flinching, flinched — because her eyes had gone the color of the map's western margin, that pulsing bruise-blue, just for a heartbeat, before fading back to ordinary brown.

"We cross at the ford," she said again, "and whatever is waiting for us there — we go through it."

Far to the east, in a warm study in Corvane, General Aldous Corvess dipped his pen and finished the sentence.
