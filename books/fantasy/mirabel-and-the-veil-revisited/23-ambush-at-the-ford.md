## 23. Ambush at the Ford

The river came on them the way bad news always did: gradually, then all at once.

For two days they had followed the pilgrim road through country that grew stranger with every mile — hedgerows blooming out of season, a chapel whose bell rang though no one pulled the rope, crows that watched them pass in perfect silence, as if under orders. The map rode against Elodie's ribs in its oilcloth wrapping, warm as a sleeping animal, and she had stopped pretending to herself that the warmth meant nothing. It was waking up. Whatever slept inside her alongside it was waking too.

"The ford's ahead," Brannoc said, reining in at the crest of a hill. He spoke little and when he did it was usually worth hearing, so the whole company slowed to listen. "Stone bottom, shallow this time of year. Half a day's march beyond it to the shrine valley." He squinted down at the water glinting between alders. "Or we go around. North crossing adds a day, maybe two."

"A day we may not have," Sera said. She sat her horse like her father had taught her, straight-backed and economical, and she had not slept properly since decoding the letters three nights ago. Grief had burned itself down to something harder in her — not rage anymore, exactly. Aim. "Corvess isn't behind us. He's *beside* us. He'll have sent riders ahead to hold every crossing on the road."

"He'd need to know which road," Brannoc said.

"He'd need only to guess," Tam murmured. "And Corvess is very good at guessing. I worked for him for six years; I know how his mind moves. He doesn't chase. He calculates where you must go, and he waits there, patient as arithmetic." Tam pushed his spectacles up his nose and looked, as he often did, slightly ill. "A ford is arithmetic. One road narrows to one line of men in open water. If I were planning an ambush—"

"You're not planning one," Elodie said. "That's the point."

"No," Tam agreed quietly. "I'm remembering one. There was a ford on the Vell, east of Corvane, where the General broke a rebel column in the autumn before my disgrace. I drew the map of it. I remember thinking, even then, what a *convenient* place a ford is to die."

They stood in the wind a long moment. Below them the river ran quick and silver-bright over pale stones, innocent as anything, willows trailing their fingers in the shallows. On the far bank the pilgrim road climbed away toward blue hills, and somewhere past those hills lay the anchor, and everything.

Elodie looked at the faces around her. Brannoc, scarred and unreadable, who had once held a bridge against thirty men because someone had to and no one else would. Sera, who had put a knife to Elodie's throat in their first meeting and now handed her the last of the dried plums without being asked. Rue, half-hidden in a gray traveling cloak that did nothing to disguise him — fae were fae, whatever they wore, and something about him made horses nervous and dogs go silent. And Tam, poor brave foolish Tam, who had spent his whole life believing that knowledge was safety, that if you could just name a thing correctly you could control it.

"We cross fast and spread out," Elodie said. "Brannoc first with Sera. Tam in the middle with me. Rue—" she hesitated.

"Rue will walk in the middle of the river and let the water hide what it can," Rue said, with a thin smile. "Fae don't love running water. It forgets us, and we don't like being forgotten." His voice was light as ever, but his eyes stayed on the far bank, moving from alder shadow to alder shadow. "Also, if anyone asks, I am a very tall pilgrim with a skin condition."

Despite everything, Sera snorted.

They went down.

* * *

The water was colder than the season justified. That should have told Elodie something right away — rivers borrowed their temperature from the land around them, and the land here had been leaning toward Mirabel for miles, the Veil wearing thin as old cloth — but her boots were wet and her mind was on the far bank and she thought about cold only in the ordinary way, the way anyone does.

The stones were slick underfoot. Brannoc reached the opposite side and turned, hand raised, scanning the treeline. Sera crossed beside him, low in the saddle. Tam came next, leading his mule, muttering cartographer's curses at the current. Elodie waded in behind him, water climbing her shins, her knees, the map pressing hot against her chest like a second heartbeat—

and then the willows on the far bank opened their eyes.

Not literally. But every shadow among them shifted at once, resolved into shapes, and the shapes were men — dozens of them, rising out of the fern-brake with the smooth unhurried motion of people who had been waiting a long time and were very good at waiting. Crossbows came up. Sunlight ran along sword-hilts. A horn sounded twice, short and flat, and the sound bounced off the hills and came back wrong, doubled, as if the hills themselves were repeating orders.

"Ambush," Brannoc said, in the same tone he might have used to report rain. "Off the road. Water. Now."

The first bolts came before the echo died.

Elodie saw one take Sera's horse through the neck — the animal screamed and went sideways, pitching Sera into the shallows — and saw Brannoc step off the bank into the river to meet the first soldiers coming down through the willows, sword out, planting himself in the current like something geological. And she saw Tam.

Tam had frozen. Of course he had. Tam Vess, who had faced down a royal tribunal with nothing but citations, who had argued heresy cases with archivists twice his rank, was not built for arrows. He stood hip-deep in the middle of the ford with his mule bolting past him and his arms full of falling books, watching the crossbowmen on the bank find their range, and Elodie understood with terrible clarity that he was going to die there, in the arithmetic, exactly where Corvess had calculated he would.

"TAM!" Her own voice sounded far away. "TAM, MOVE!"

He turned. He even tried. She would remember that — that he tried, dropping the books, splashing toward her, his spectacles knocked askew, reaching out one hand —

— and the bolt took him high in the shoulder and spun him around, and the second one caught his leg below the knee, and the river took him the way rivers take everything, gently, without malice, folding him under.

Something in Elodie tore loose.

She had spent seven years building walls around the thing inside her. She knew their shape intimately: the careful mediocrity, the charms sold deliberately flawed, the small controlled flickers passed off as sleight-of-hand. She had built those walls stone by stone after Mistress Ivenna, after Corvane, after every close call and every swallowed flare, and she had believed — she had needed to believe — that they would hold against anything.

They did not hold against seeing Tam drown.

It came up through her like a geyser breaking through winter ice. Not the little warmth she knew, not charm-magic, candle-flame magic, luck-in-a-pouch magic. This was the other thing, the deep thing, the inheritance, and it did not ask her permission. The map against her ribs flared hot enough to scorch through oilcloth and shirt, and the river *answered*.

Every drop of water in the ford stopped.

Not froze — stopped. Hung. The current stood up out of its bed like a startled animal, droplets hanging in the air by the thousands, each one catching the sun so the whole river became suddenly a field of floating glass, and in the middle of it Tam hung too, cradled in a fist of standing water, blood from his wounds unwinding in slow crimson ribbons that did not disperse but hung with him, suspended, beautiful and obscene.

The battle stuttered. Men on both banks stared. Even Brannoc, waist-deep in a river that had abandoned him, went still.

Elodie stood in the middle of it all and felt the power move through her like a second self, vast and ancient and *furious*, and she understood in one white-hot instant what she was holding. This was what her mother had carried. This was what the witch-finders had hunted the Underhills for, generation after generation, until almost none were left. This was why the map had chosen her, why it had lain folded and patient in a dying man's hands until it found the last living vessel of the last Veilwarden line.

It was magnificent.

It was the most frightening thing she had ever felt in her life.

Because it wasn't finished. She could feel it straining onward, past the stopping of the river, toward the men on the banks — she could feel what it *wanted*, the way you feel a dog strain at a leash. It wanted the crossbows to burst. It wanted the willows to reach down and the stones to rise and the water to come back down all at once over the soldiers on the far bank, all forty of them, drowning the ambush in the ambush. It wanted, with a patience older than kingdoms, to kill everyone who threatened its bearer, and it regarded them the way she might regard ants across a kitchen floor — without hatred, without anger even, just as obstacles to be cleared.

And the worst part — the part that would wake her sweating for weeks afterward — was that some small exhausted corner of her, the corner that had run for seven years and hidden for seven years and buried her best self for seven years, *wanted to let it.*

"Elodie." Brannoc's voice, low, cutting through the roar in her ears. He had waded to her side and his hand closed on her arm, and he alone of everyone on that river seemed to understand what he was touching. "Elodie. Look at me. Not them. Me."

"They shot Tam," she said. Her voice came out layered, doubled, hers and something vaster speaking underneath it, and that frightened her more than the power did.

"Yes." Brannoc didn't flinch. "And if you do what it wants, you become Corvess. You become the story he tells his witch-finders to justify the burning. *Look what they are. Look what they'd do.* Don't give him the truth he's built his whole life on."

The words landed like cold water. She clung to them.

"Not them," she whispered. "Me. Not — not their deaths. Just —"

"Just the water," Brannoc said. "Just bring him back."

So she did the hardest thing she had ever done, harder than sealing the tear with her blood, harder than any flight or any lie: she took the leash in both hands and hauled it back. She narrowed the world to the fist of standing water and the man inside it, and she pulled.

The river came down.

Not on the soldiers. It came down as a river comes down — all at once, everywhere, resuming itself with a sound like applause, and Tam came with it, dumped into the shallows at Elodie's feet, coughing, alive, bleeding, gloriously, impossibly alive. She dragged him up the near bank while the world resumed its chaos around them — Sera on foot now, fighting with a boat-hook she'd snatched from somewhere, vicious and precise; Rue finally abandoning pretense, moving through the melee with a speed that made the soldiers scream about demons; Brannoc holding the waterline alone, giving ground grudgingly, foot by foot, buying seconds with blood.

But the company was four fighters and a scholar against forty, and arithmetic was winning.

They got Tam behind a fallen willow. His shoulder wound was bad — the bolt had gone through above the collarbone, missing the lung by what Tam later described, feverishly, as "perhaps the width of a citation" — and his leg was worse, the shaft still in it, and his face had gone the gray of old paper. He gripped Elodie's wrist with a hand already going cold.

"The map," he rasped. "If they take me — Corvess will question me, and I'm not — Elodie, I'm not brave enough to hold out forever, whatever I'd like to believe. If they take me, they take everything I know. The anchor road. The rite. Everything." His eyes, unfocused behind cracked spectacles, found hers. "Don't come for me. Whatever you feel, don't—"

"Hush," Elodie said. "Save your breath."

"Promise me."

"I promise nothing," she said, "except that you will tell me yourself, over dinner, some night when we're old and insufferable." And she bent and kissed his bloody forehead, because he was her friend, and because she had learned in the last mile of her life that promises made in fear are the ones fate most enjoys breaking.

Then the soldiers crested the bank above them, and there was no more time for anything.

* * *

Afterward, Elodie could never assemble the retreat into a single memory. It came to her in fragments, the way disasters do:

Sera's boat-hook splitting a shield-rim, and Sera's voice, flat and deadly: *"Left! Left, you're drifting left!"* — the daughter of a courier, it turned out, had grown up learning to read a fight the way Tam read maps.

Rue stepping out of nowhere between her and a sword, catching the blade bare-handed with a hiss, black blood welling between his fingers, and his smile at the soldier — that smile, the one that made grown men drop their weapons and run — and saying softly, *"Now you've seen me. Isn't that unfortunate for you."*

Brannoc, everywhere, always, a wall that walked. Taking a cut along the jaw that would scar, not slowing.

The moment the tide turned — and it turned on something none of them planned. The map. It burned against her chest and *pulled*, hard, toward the river, and without deciding to Elodie obeyed it, dragging Tam's arm over her shoulders and stumbling back into the shallows, and the moment her blood touched the water the ford *recognized* her. The current rose around the company like a wall of glass, a curved shining rampart taller than a man, and the soldiers' charge broke against it, men recoiling from water that stood up and looked at them. Through the translucent wall they could see the far bank, the willows, the way out — and the river held the shape as long as she fed it, and she fed it until her vision went gray at the edges and her knees buckled and Brannoc caught her under the arms and half-carried her, and Tam, and Sera hauling Rue with his ruined hand, up the far bank and away into the hills while the wall of water groaned and shivered and finally fell, and the ford was only a ford again.

They ran until dark. They ran until the horns behind them faded and the road gave way to sheep tracks and the sheep tracks to bracken and stone. They ran until Tam fainted in the saddle of a stolen soldier's horse, and then they stopped, in a hollow ringed with rowan trees, and did what they could.

What they could do was not much. Sera cleaned and bound the shoulder wound with hands that shook only after it was done. Brannoc set the leg and splinted it, jaw tight, saying nothing, which from Brannoc was eloquence. Rue wrapped his burned palm in silence and watched the dark between the rowans with eyes that saw farther than theirs.

Tam woke once, feverish, near midnight. Elodie was sitting beside him, keeping the fire low, and he surfaced from the fever like a diver, gasping, and gripped her sleeve.

"I heard it," he whispered. "Under the water. When you stopped the river. There's a voice in the Veil, Elodie — I've theorized about it for twenty years, I wrote a paper the tribunal burned — and I heard it. It knows your name. It's been *waiting* for you." His eyes rolled toward the darkness where the map lay wrapped by the fire. "And Corvess has heard it too. That's why he's coming. Not for the map. For the voice. He thinks if he destroys the anchor he silences it forever, and he'll burn every living thing between here and the hills to do it."

"Sleep," Elodie said gently. "Tomorrow."

"There may not be a tomorrow in the direction we're walking." But his eyes were closing again, the fever pulling him under, and his last words came out slurred and urgent: "He has me now... he has what I know... you have to move faster than grief, Elodie. Faster than grief..."

He slept. The fire ticked. Somewhere far behind them, faint as memory, a horn sounded — once, short, flat — and the hills repeated it, patient as arithmetic.

Elodie looked at her hands in the firelight. Ordinary hands, ink-stained, charm-seller's hands. But she could still feel it coiled in her blood, the vast thing, the furious thing, sated for now and waiting, and she remembered with sick clarity how good it had felt, that one white instant, to stop a river.

Victory, she thought, looking at Tam's gray face, at Rue's bandaged hand, at the blood she'd washed from her own sleeves in a freezing stream. Victory that felt like loss. They had kept the map, kept each other, kept the road — and lost the thing she'd guarded most carefully for seven years, the comfortable lie that she was small, that her gift was small, that hiding was the same as being safe.

There was no hiding from what she'd felt on the water. Not from the power. And not — she glanced at Brannoc, sitting watch with his scarred jaw set, who had seen what she'd almost let happen and said nothing, only watched her now with something that was not fear and not quite forgiveness — not from herself.

"Faster than grief," she whispered to the dark, and began, in her head, to plan the rescue she had promised no one.

Behind them, far downstream, the ford lay empty. But the witch-finders had what they wanted. They had Tam Vess, alive and talking — and General Corvess, patient as arithmetic, already knew exactly where the last Veilwarden would go next.
