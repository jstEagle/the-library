## 24. What Tam Confesses

The cell had no windows, which Tam Vess found almost a comfort. Windows meant sky, and sky meant distance, and distance meant hope, and hope was the cruelest instrument of torture she knew. She had spent three days in a room of gray stone and gray dark, and in that time she had catalogued every inch of it the way she once catalogued coastlines: the drain in the floor that breathed cold air on an irregular schedule, suggesting a connection to the river; the iron ring bolted to the wall at wrist height; the single candle that was replaced by unseen hands when it burned to within a finger's width of its holder, always while she pretended to sleep.

She had also counted her interrogations. Four so far. The first two had been performed by a captain with a list of questions written in a careful clerk's hand — where had she obtained the chart, who else had seen it, what did the symbols along its western margin signify. He had asked them in the tone of a man reading someone else's grocery order, and Tam had answered none of them, and he had seemed neither surprised nor disappointed, and she understood that he was a prelude, not a performance.

The third interrogation had been conducted without words at all. Two men had come in with a basin of water and a length of cloth, set them down on the table, looked at her for a long moment, and then left again without touching either. It was the most honest conversation she'd had in years. She had shaken afterward, alone in the dark, not from fear exactly but from the strange intimacy of it — they had shown her the shape of her future, folded neatly, like a map.

The fourth interrogation was General Corvess himself.

She heard him before she saw him: boots on stone with a rhythm too even to be natural, as though the man had taught his own body to keep time like a drum corps. Then the door opened, torchlight spilled in, and Corvess stepped through with two guards who took up positions flanking the door and then became furniture.

He was not what she expected. She had imagined the Witch-Finder General as something out of a morality play — gaunt, fanatical, eyes like hot coals. Instead he was broad and comfortable-looking, silvering at the temples, with the ruddy complexion of a man who enjoyed his meals and the tired, patient eyes of a country physician. He carried a folding stool under one arm, and he set it down across from her bench and sat, unhurried, arranging his coat tails with the fussiness of an old maid.

"Tam Vess," he said. "Formerly Royal Cartographer to the Crown of Corvane. Convicted of heresy, sedition, and the falsification of official charts. Sentenced to the stocks, then to imprisonment, then — I believe the sentence read *at His Majesty's pleasure*, which is a legal way of saying forever." He smiled faintly. "I've read your file. All of it. It's very good work, your file. You were thorough even in your crimes."

"I was accurate," Tam said. Her voice came out rustier than she liked. "That's what they convicted me of."

"Yes." Corvess leaned back, lacing his fingers over his stomach. "You drew Mirabel on a royal survey map. You marked the Veil's crossings. You wrote a monograph arguing that the fae realm was not myth but geography, and that the Crown's entire border policy was built on a lie of omission. For this you lost your post, your name, and seven years of your life." He tilted his head. "Tell me, Mistress Vess. Was it worth it?"

Tam studied him. Around the candle's small flame, the cell seemed to breathe. She thought about the question honestly, because lying to this man would be like lying to a river — it would simply flow around whatever stones she placed in it.

"No," she said. "It cost me everything, and no one believed me, and the maps I made were burned. If I could go back, I'd do it again anyway, but I won't pretend that makes it worth it. Worth isn't the currency I was paid in."

Something flickered behind the general's patient eyes — recognition, perhaps, or its opposite. "Honesty," he said. "How refreshing. My captains tell me you've refused every question. Yet here you are, answering before I've even asked one."

"You didn't come here for answers I could give," Tam said. "A man in your position doesn't interrogate prisoners personally unless he wants something the ordinary questions can't reach. So ask me the real thing, General, and let's both stop pretending this is about my file."

Corvess regarded her for a long moment. Then he laughed — a short, genuine bark of sound that startled the guards into attention. "Oh, they were right about you. The scholars who denounced you, I mean. They said you were insufferably clever. They undersold it." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and the comfortable-country-physician mask settled into something older and harder underneath, like bedrock showing through topsoil.

"The real thing," he said, "is this. Six days ago, a Veilwarden chart walked out of a dead courier's hands and into the possession of a hedge-charm seller in the Lowmarket. That charm seller has since broken a prisoner from the stocks, fled the city under armed escort, acquired a deserter from the Third Regiment, and killed or maimed four of my men at the east ford. She is moving west, toward the Gray Marches, following a route that only one kind of document would reveal." He paused. "I have described your companions, Mistress Vess. And I have described you. Which means I already know the answer to the question my captain couldn't get out of you: you're not the map's reader. You're its interpreter. There's a difference, and it matters."

Tam kept her face still, though her heart had begun a slow, heavy drumming. *Four men at the ford.* She hadn't known. Elodie's power had gone off like a struck bell, and Tam had been running, and she had chosen not to look back at what the ringing left behind. Now the bill for that choice sat across from her in a folding stool, smiling gently.

"And what does the Crown want with me?" she asked. "You have the chart's bearer's route. You have trackers. What can a disgraced cartographer add?"

"Context," Corvess said. "The chart is a key. We know that much — my family has known it longer than any living archive admits. But a key is useless if you don't know what it opens, and how, and what happens when it turns. You spent seven years in prison thinking about nothing else. I want that thinking. I want the anchor's location confirmed. I want the mechanism of the binding explained. And in exchange—" he spread his hands, an easy, generous gesture, "—you walk out of this cell. Full pardon. Your name restored. Your charts reprinted with the royal seal. A chair at the new College of Survey, with students, with funding, with everything they took from you handed back with interest."

The words landed in Tam's chest like coins dropped down a well. She heard them fall and fall and never quite touch bottom. *Students. Funding. Her name spoken without spitting.*

"You should know," Corvess went on, mild as morning, "what we intend to do with the information. Not because I need your approval, but because I find that informed collaborators make better ones." He reached into his coat and produced a folded paper — not the map, Tam noted, just paper, though her whole body tensed at the sight of anything folded — and smoothed it on his knee.

"When the last war ended," he said, "the Veilwardens didn't simply die out. They were ended. Deliberately. By my ancestors, among others, acting under sealed royal warrant. Do you know why?"

"Because they held the border and the Crown wanted the border gone," Tam said. "Because an open Veil means trade and tribute and leverage over Mirabel, and a warded Veil means the fae courts can ignore human kings entirely."

"Half right," Corvess said. "That was the court's reason. My family's reason was simpler." He unfolded the paper. On it, drawn in faded ink by a hand Tam recognized with a jolt — her own hand, younger, from a chart confiscated years ago — was the anchor tree, rendered in cross-section, roots and crown. "We had seen what comes through when the Veil tears. Not the pretty stories. The other things. The things that wore soldiers' faces and asked, in perfect Corvanic, to be let home. My great-great-grandmother watched a garrison of two hundred men open their own gates to those things because they looked like their sons. The Veilwardens' answer to every tear was more magic, wider doors, deeper traffic between the worlds. They were meddlers, Mistress Vess. Noble ones, learned ones, but meddlers — and the toll of their meddling was paid in villages like the one I grew up in." He refolded the paper. "So when I take the anchor, I am not going to bind it, or claim it, or bargain with it. I am going to destroy it. Root and crown. Permanently. The Veil will seal itself shut — dead, inert, impassable in both directions — and both worlds will forget each other, and the war will never happen, because there will be nothing left to fight over. No magic crossing over. No fae slipping through. No chart, no key, no heir. Just two worlds, separate and safe, forever."

The candle flame stood perfectly still, as if listening.

"That's not safety," Tam said quietly. "That's amputation. The Veil isn't a wall, Corvess, it's a membrane — it breathes. Kill the anchor and you don't seal the boundary, you kill it, and a dead boundary doesn't hold things apart. It holds things *in*. Whatever's pressed against the far side when it dies gets pressed there permanently, and pressure like that doesn't dissipate. It waits."

"You sound like the monograph," Corvess said, unbothered. "'The Veil is a living system; severance will produce catastrophic rebound.' I read it. I had it read to me twice during your trial, in fact, because I wanted to be sure I understood the strongest version of your argument." He stood, retrieving his stool, and paused at the door. "And I still think you're wrong. But you'll forgive me if I prefer to be wrong with your expertise rather than without it. You have until tomorrow's sundown. After that, I proceed with what I have, and you return to His Majesty's pleasure."

The door closed. The guards went with him. The candle resumed its small, unsteady burning, and Tam Vess sat in the gray dark and discovered that her hands were shaking.

Not from fear. From arithmetic.

* * *

They gave her paper.

This was the bait inside the bait, and Tam admired its elegance even as she felt its hook. A collaborator would want tools; a collaborator drawing the anchor's mechanisms would need ink and vellum; and everything she committed to vellum would pass through Corvess's hands before it ever reached a wall. They brought her a lap desk, a pot of ink, six sheets of good rag paper, and a goose quill with the barbs trimmed, and left her alone with them in the breathing dark.

Tam sat for a long time without touching any of it.

She thought about the chair at the College of Survey. She thought about her name in print again, clean, with students leaning over her charts by lamplight, asking her questions she'd spent a lifetime being punished for answering. Seven years in the stocks' shadow had not cured her of wanting those things; it had only taught her to carry the wanting quietly, the way one carries a stone in the shoe — unnoticed by others, worn smooth by one's own walking.

Then she thought about Elodie Underhill standing at the east ford with light coming off her like heat off a forge, terrified of her own hands, and Brannoc Hale hauling Tam bodily out of the river, and Sera Vane's face when she'd finally said her father's name aloud instead of around it. She thought about Rue, who had once wanted the same thing Corvess wanted — the Veil forced, opened, decided by one willful party — and who had learned better at the price of everything, and had defected anyway, toward hope, which was the harder direction.

*Everyone in this story is trying to decide the Veil alone,* Tam thought. *Ashvine from fear. Corvess from fear. Even the old Veilwardens, maybe, from duty. And the map keeps choosing bearers who don't want to choose alone.*

She dipped the quill.

If she drew the anchor truly — its true location, its true mechanism — Corvess would march on it with certainty, and certainty would kill everyone she loved and half of two worlds besides. If she drew it falsely, he would discover the falsehood quickly; he had her original charts; he was not a stupid man. If she refused outright, she returned to the water basin and the cloth, and gave up the only weapon she had.

But a map, Tam had learned long ago in the royal archives, was never only what it depicted. A map was a language. And languages could say one thing to a reader and another thing to a listener, if the speaker was fluent enough in both.

She began to draw.

To Corvess's eye, over the next hours, the sheets would show exactly what he had demanded: the anchor's location confirmed against the pilgrim road, the chamber's approach, the root-crown structure rendered in careful cross-section, the binding points marked in her own precise hand. Accurate enough to survive his comparison with her confiscated work. Accurate enough to buy her pardon, if pardon were the point.

But Tam Vess had invented, in her heretical years, a notation system she had never published — a marginal shorthand built from surveyor's marks, contour conventions, and compass deviations, designed originally so she could annotate forbidden observations on official charts without the censor's clerks noticing. Every symbol had a legitimate cartographic meaning and a second, private one. A hachure cluster that read as steep terrain read, in her private key, as *danger, do not approach*. A dotted line that read as a projected route read as *this path is watched*. And a small circled cross — standard notation for a chapel or shrine — placed not at the anchor itself but at the shrine two days west of it, where she and Elodie had stopped, where Elodie had claimed her name — that circled cross, in the private key, meant *she has already begun; hasten*.

And beneath the anchor diagram itself, in the second layer, in deviations of contour lines too subtle for any eye but one trained by seven years of nothing else to see: *he means to destroy it, not bind it. He told me so. The seal he plans is death, not warding. Burn nothing. Trust the third road. — T.V.*

She worked until her candle guttered low, then worked by memory of the dark, which was a skill the stocks had given her along with everything else. When the finished sheets lay dry on the lap desk, she read them twice: once as Corvess would read them, and once as Elodie would, if the map chose to teach her the key — and the map would, Tam was nearly sure of it. The chart had chosen Elodie because Elodie listened. Maps spoke to people who listened.

The risk was enormous and specific: the message depended on the chart itself reaching Elodie's hands, and Tam had no idea whether Corvess carried the original with him or locked it in some vault. But she had noticed, during the general's visit, the faint smell of the thing — old vellum has a scent like warm dust and rain — clinging to his coat sleeve. He carried it. Men like Corvess carried their trophies, and their weapons, close.

Near dawn, the door opened and the captain entered with two soldiers to collect the drawings. Tam surrendered them with the weary gratitude of a broken woman, and let her shoulders slump, and asked in a cracked voice whether the pardon would include restoration of her library, and watched the captain write down *yes* with a clerk's neat hand, believing it.

Alone again, she put her back to the stone wall and closed her eyes and did the only thing left to do, the thing the stocks had trained into her body deeper than any craft: she waited, precisely, patiently, keeping time like a drum corps, and sent her silent message westward with all the force of a mind that had spent seven years learning how to speak without a voice.

*Burn nothing,* she thought at the map, at Elodie, at the thin bright line between worlds. *Trust the third road. And hurry.*

Far away, she could not know it, but the chart in General Corvess's coat pocket had grown warm against his ribs — warm as a sleeping animal dreaming of its keeper — and the ink along its western margin had begun, very faintly, to move.
