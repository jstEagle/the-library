## 26. The Anchor Road

The pilgrim road announced itself before they found it.

They had been walking through high moorland for two days — gray grass, gray stone, a sky the color of old pewter — when Brannoc stopped at the crest of a hill and said nothing at all, which from Brannoc was an announcement in itself. Elodie climbed up beside him and saw it: a line of white stones running across the moor like a stitch through cloth, each one taller than a man, each one carved with symbols worn nearly smooth by centuries of weather and pilgrimage.

"The Anchor Road," Tam breathed. She had come up behind them, her satchel of instruments clinking softly against her hip. "I've read about it. Every account says it was abandoned after the war. They pulled up the wayshrines. They burned the guidebooks." Her voice had that quality it got when scholarship and wonder fought for possession of her mouth. "But the road itself — you can't burn a road."

"You can try," Sera said darkly, coming up last with Rue drifting at her shoulder like a shadow that had learned to walk. "My father's letters called it the Weeping Way. He said the stones remember everyone who ever died on it."

"Cheerful," Elodie murmured. But she felt it too, even from this distance — a pull, faint and insistent, like a hand closed gently around her sternum. The map, folded small against her heart inside her coat, had grown warm again. It had been growing warmer every day since the ford, since the night she'd torn open her own power to save them all and woken with silver streaks in her hair that hadn't been there before.

She touched the coat over the map, the way she'd caught herself doing a hundred times now, and started down the hill.

* * *

The first waystone they passed still bore its carving clearly enough to read: two hands, one human, one long-fingered and fae, clasped around a single flame. Moss grew in the creases of both palms. Someone — pilgrims, once, hundreds of years ago — had pressed copper coins into the mortar at its base, and the coins had gone green as river water.

Elodie laid her palm against the stone without meaning to.

It hummed. Not sound, exactly — more a recognition, a vibration that traveled up her arm and settled somewhere behind her ribs, where the map's warmth lived. For one dizzying moment she saw the road as it must have been: crowded, candlelit, thousands of feet from both worlds walking side by side toward the anchor, fae lanterns bobbing above human torches, songs in two languages braiding together in the cold air.

Then the vision let her go, and she was alone on a dead road under a dead sky, and her hand was shaking against the moss.

"Don't do that," Brannoc said quietly. He'd come up beside her without her hearing, which was a skill of his she'd stopped resenting and started relying on. "Not until we know what it costs."

"It didn't cost anything," she said. But that wasn't quite true, and they both knew it. Since the ford, every use of the power left her a little thinner — not in flesh but in something harder to name, as if each working shaved a page out of the book of her. She hadn't told them how bad it had been that night. How for three days afterward she hadn't been able to remember her mother's face, only the fact that she'd had one.

Brannoc looked at her with those gray eyes that missed nothing, and said, "You'll tell me when it starts costing," and it wasn't a question, and she hated him a little, and loved him a little more.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a fallen waystone, its carving face-down in the earth as if the road itself had tried to hide what it was. Tam built a fire small enough to hide in a cupped hand. Sera took first watch without being asked; she always did, as if sleep were a debt she refused to owe anyone.

Elodie sat close to the flames and unfolded the map.

In the firelight it was beautiful and terrible. The ink moved when it thought no one was watching — rivers sliding like slow snakes, mountain ranges breathing, and along the eastern edge, where the Veil ran thinnest, a seam of gold thread that pulsed faintly, patiently, like a heartbeat waiting to be answered. At the road's end, past the last waystone, the map showed the anchor: a tree drawn in light rather than ink, its roots gripping the border between worlds.

And around it, drawn in a red so fresh it might have bled onto the parchment that morning, two armies.

"They're closer than yesterday," Tam said, settling beside her. She didn't touch the map — she never did anymore, not since it had flared and blistered her fingertips when she'd tried to measure it. "Corvess is a day behind us, maybe less. His outriders burned the ferry at Marlow's Crossing; we heard the smoke before we saw it."

"And Ashvine?"

Tam hesitated. It was Rue who answered, from the darkness beyond the firelight, his voice carrying that silken melancholy that never seemed to touch his eyes. "The Lady's knights do not leave tracks, little cartographer. They leave absence. Birds go quiet. Flowers close early. You will know they are near because the world will hold its breath." A pause, and then, softer: "She is frightened. That is what makes her dangerous. Frightened people march faster than angry ones."

"You speak about your own queen as if you know her heart," Sera said sharply from her watch-post.

"I knew her heart when it was younger and kinder. Hearts change. Courts change them." Rue's silhouette shifted, elegant even in stillness. "I would not be following us, girl, if I did not understand exactly why she follows. I simply hope, for all our sakes, that understanding arrives before her lances do."

Elodie folded the map away. Two armies, converging. One road, narrow as a needle. Somewhere ahead, an anchor failing, and her own blood the only thread that could reweave it — if the cost didn't unmake her first.

She lay down between the fire and Brannoc's watchful bulk and dreamed of a tree made of light, and of roots tearing, and of a voice — her mother's voice, though she could not have described it — saying *hold on, little warden, hold on.*

* * *

The second day on the road, the world began to hold its breath.

It started small. The moor birds, which had wheeled and cried overhead since dawn, went silent all at once, mid-note, as if a door had closed on them. Then the heather — out of season, blooming stubbornly purple along the waystones — began folding its petals shut, flower by flower, in a wave that rippled ahead of them up the road like a warning running before a storm.

Rue stopped walking. "Ah," he said.

"Ah?" Sera's hand went to her knife. "'Ah' is not a tactical assessment, fae."

"No," Rue agreed pleasantly. "It is a eulogy. For our head start." He turned his face eastward, where the moor fell away into a valley veined with mist. "The Lady's knights are in the valley. Perhaps three hours behind us now, perhaps two. They travel the way I taught them to travel, which is bitterly ironic, and they will reach the anchor within a day whether or not they catch us — the road goes there regardless. The question is only whether we arrive whole."

"Then we run," Brannoc said.

"We run," Elodie agreed, and they ran.

They ran the pilgrim road with their lungs burning and the waystones blurring past, and Elodie felt the map against her chest beating faster, eager, hungry for its destination, and beneath the fear she felt something worse: relief. Because running meant arriving. Arriving meant the choice. And the choice meant it would finally, finally be over — one way or another.

She stumbled on a loose waystone shard and Brannoc caught her arm without breaking stride, steadied her, let go. Seven weeks ago she hadn't known him. Now his hand on her arm felt like a load-bearing wall in the architecture of her courage. When had that happened? When had any of them happened? Tam, who complained about her boots every hour and mapped every mile in her head anyway, muttering coordinates like prayers. Sera, who spoke to her father's ghost through gritted teeth and had started, lately, speaking to Elodie without them. Rue, the exile, who walked at the rear and watched their backs with the fierce attention of a man guarding the first thing he'd chosen to protect in a century.

And Brannoc. Brannoc, who had deserted an army rather than burn children, and who now marched toward another army without hesitation, because this time he was walking toward something instead of away.

*We are not a company,* Elodie thought, watching them run ahead of her through the closing flowers. *We are a argument the world is having with itself, and somehow we are winning.*

Behind them, very far back along the road, she heard it: horns. Human horns, deep and disciplined, Corvess's army announcing itself to the empty moor. And beneath them, almost below hearing, a sound like wind through a keyhole — thin, sweet, wrong — that could only be fae war-horns answering from the valley.

Two enemies, converging. And between them, five people and a map, running for the hinge of the world.

* * *

They reached the shrine at dusk.

It stood where the road crested a final ridge before descending into the anchor valley — a small round building of pale stone, older than the waystones, its dome cracked but standing. Pilgrims had scratched prayers into its walls in two alphabets. Inside, someone had left offerings that had outlasted the offering: a child's wooden bird, a soldier's medal, a lock of hair gone to silk.

Elodie entered alone. She didn't decide to; her feet decided, and the rest of her followed, the way iron follows a lodestone.

Inside, the air was different. Warmer. The cracks in the dome let through the last violet light of evening, and the dust motes hung motionless, as if the room were holding its breath along with everything else. In the center stood a basin of black stone, dry for who knew how many centuries, and above it, carved into the wall, words in the old tongue that Elodie — to her own astonishment — could read.

*Here the road ends and the keeping begins. Lay down what you carried. Take up what you are.*

Her eyes stung. She pressed her hand flat against the wall, against the words, and felt the map answer through her ribs like a second heartbeat.

"Elodie." Brannoc's voice from the doorway, low. "There's firelight in the valley. A lot of it. Corvess has made camp at the road's foot — he means to reach the anchor by morning." A pause. "And there are lights in the sky above the eastern ridge. Green ones. Moving."

Ashvine's knights. Corvess's army. Both of them, hours away, converging on the same dying tree.

Elodie turned from the wall. In the doorway, framed against the deepening blue of dusk, stood the four of them — the scholar, the deserter, the avenger, the exile — looking at her with a question none of them would ask aloud, because they had already answered it in their bones. They would follow her down that road tomorrow. Into the valley. Between two armies. To the tree.

"Tomorrow," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect, "I stop being the girl who hides."

No one cheered. There was nothing to cheer yet. But Tam nodded slowly, and Sera's jaw set, and Rue smiled the sad, sharp smile of a man who had finally bet on the right horse, and Brannoc — Brannoc just looked at her the way he did when he was memorizing something he was afraid to lose.

That night, Elodie slept at the threshold of the shrine with the map open on her chest, and dreamed of her mother's hands teaching hers a knot she couldn't yet tie.

She woke before dawn to the smell of smoke on the north wind — woodsmoke and something fouler beneath it, oil and ash — and climbed the ridge alone to look back the way they'd come.

The horizon behind them was a line of orange. Corvess wasn't marching anymore.

He was burning the road.
