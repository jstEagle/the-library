## 28. The Price of the Rite

Brannoc Hale had stood in the burning doorway of Aldenmere and watched a village die, and he had thought — in the arrogant, broken way soldiers think — that he had already seen the worst thing the world could show him.

He had been wrong.

The shrine was older than the road it sat beside, a ring of standing stones furred with moss and hung with ribbons so old they had gone gray as cobwebs. The pilgrim road climbed toward it through pines that whispered even when there was no wind, and at its heart, where the stones leaned together like conspirators, Elodie Underhill knelt on bare earth with her palms pressed flat against the ground, and the rite took her.

He saw it happen. That was the worst part. He saw every moment of it.

It began with her breath. She drew one long, slow inhale, as if she meant to hold the whole night inside her lungs, and then she let it out — and what came out was not breath but light, thin and silver, curling upward into the dark like smoke from a fire that burned nothing. The map lay open before her, no longer ink on vellum but something alive, its lines rising off the page in threads of luminous script that wound around her wrists like bracelets, like manacles.

"Elodie—" Tam started forward, and Sera caught his arm.

"No." Sera's voice was steady, but her knuckles were white where she gripped his sleeve. "She said no one touches her. She said if we break the circle, we lose everything she's giving up."

*Everything she's giving up.* Brannoc turned those words over and found they did not fit anything he understood. You gave up coin. You gave up a post, a name, a country. You did not give up —

The threads sank into her skin.

She made no sound. That was somehow worse than screaming. Her back arched, her head tipped back, and the light poured *into* her now instead of out, filling her the way dawn fills a valley, and Brannoc could see — he swore to every god he'd stopped believing in, he could see — the shape of her beneath it, the small stubborn woman who sold luck-charms and lied badly to witch-finders, stretched thin around something vast.

And then the visions came.

He knew they were visions because Tam said so afterward, in his rapid scholar's voice, citing texts Brannoc had never heard of: *the rite shows the bearer the line, all of them, backward through time*. But knowing did not help. Watching Elodie's face flicker through expressions that were not hers — grief, triumph, terror, a mother's fierce tenderness — was like watching someone drown in other people's memories while the water kept coming.

Her lips moved. No sound came out. Once, only once, she spoke clearly enough for the words to carry across the stones:

"I see you."

Brannoc did not know who she was speaking to. He suspected she didn't either. Perhaps all of them. Every Veilwarden who had ever carried this weight and died carrying it.

* * *

Afterward, she slept.

They made camp in the shrine's shelter, because none of them could bear to move her, and because the stones seemed to hold the cold away — or perhaps that was the new power in her, radiating warmth like a banked hearth even in unconsciousness. Rue stood at the circle's edge with his arms folded, watching her with an expression Brannoc couldn't read and didn't try to. Fae faces were doors painted on walls.

Tam pored over Ivenna's journals by lantern light, cross-referencing passages with the frantic energy of a man trying to outrun a conclusion he'd already reached. Sera sharpened knives that were already sharp.

Brannoc sat beside Elodie and watched her breathe.

It was Tam who finally found it. Brannoc heard the journal snap shut, heard the silence stretch, and looked up to find the scholar pale as milk.

"What," Brannoc said. Not a question. A warning shot.

"The rite isn't a gift." Tam's voice came out flat, stripped of all its usual ornamentation. "It's a binding. The power doesn't live *in* her — it lives in the Veil, and she's become the channel. Every time she draws on it, every sealing, every working, the Veil takes its toll through her body. Through her years." He swallowed. "Ivenna wrote about the last warden. She called her 'the woman who burned bright and brief.' She was thirty-four when she died, Brannoc. Thirty-four, and she'd held the Veil for nine years."

The words landed in Brannoc's chest like arrows finding gaps in armor.

"How many years," he said quietly, "does she have?"

"I don't know. It depends on how much she uses. If she seals a tear like the one at the Gray Marches — that cost her days, maybe weeks. If she does what she's planning to do at the anchor—" Tam stopped. He couldn't finish it. He didn't have to.

Brannoc looked down at Elodie's sleeping face. The light had faded from her skin, leaving her ordinary again, human, mortal — except now mortality wasn't the vague distant country it was for everyone else. It was a ledger, and the Veil held the pen.

"She knows?" he asked.

"She has to. The visions would have shown her."

*She knows.* Of course she knew. That was the whole shape of Elodie Underhill, the whole infuriating architecture of the woman: she learned the price of a thing and paid it without haggling, because haggling meant delay, and delay meant someone else might suffer while she counted coins. Seven years she'd spent making herself small so no one else would burn for what she was. And now she'd found the largest possible version of herself and discovered it was a candle, and she'd lit it anyway.

Something in Brannoc Hale, which had been quiet since Aldenmere, began to roar.

* * *

She woke near dawn.

He knew the moment she surfaced because the warmth of her changed — sleep-warmth becoming waking-warmth, the small domestic heat of a living body becoming something else, something that made the nearest pine needles glitter with frost-melt though the morning was dry. She opened her eyes and looked up at him sitting beside her, and she smiled, tired and rueful, as if she'd merely overslept at an inn.

"You look terrible," she said.

"You look like a woman who traded her life away in the night," Brannoc said, "and thinks nobody noticed."

The smile faltered. Only slightly. She was too good at hiding things — seven years of practice — but he had spent seven years learning to read people who lied for their lives, deserters and smugglers and children in burned villages, and he knew the tell. The tiny stillness before the lie.

"So Tam told you," she said.

"He told me. Then he went off to be sick behind the stones, which is more composure than I managed." Brannoc leaned forward, elbows on knees, and made himself hold her gaze. "How much? How many years, Elodie?"

"It doesn't work like—"

"*How many.*"

She sat up. The movement was slow, careful, the movement of someone testing a body for damage. When she spoke, her voice was level, reasonable, the voice she probably used on frightened customers at her charm stall.

"It depends on use. Small workings, almost nothing. Sealing tears costs more. What I'll do at the anchor—"

"At the anchor," Brannoc repeated. "Say it. Say what it costs."

"Brannoc—"

"*Say it.*"

The sharpness of his own voice surprised him. It surprised her too; he saw it land, saw her flinch and then set her jaw, because she was stubborn down to the marrow, because she'd faced witch-finders and fae knights and the dying stranger's blood-soaked map with more grace than she was facing him now.

"A great deal," she said softly. "Possibly most of it. Possibly—" She stopped. Started again. "The last warden sealed the anchor during the war and died within the year. I know the odds, Brannoc. I've known them since the visions. I chose anyway."

"I know you chose." His hands had closed into fists without his permission. He looked down at them, scarred knuckles, a deserter's hands, hands that had failed to stop one fire and were now expected to sit calmly beside another. "That's what I can't — do you understand what it is you're doing? You're doing what I did. At Aldenmere. You're standing in a doorway watching something you love walk into a fire, and instead of dragging her back you're *handing her the torch*—"

"That's not fair."

"It's perfectly fair, because I'm not talking about you. I'm talking about me." The words were coming fast now, ugly and unpolished, seven years of guilt finding their target at last. "I stood in that doorway, Elodie. Captain Veyre ordered the village burned and I stood in the doorway of the first house and I watched, and I told myself I'd stop it, any moment, I'd step in and stop it — and I didn't. And everyone in that village died, and I ran, and I have spent seven years being a man who watches. So forgive me if I have *opinions* about watching you do this."

Silence. Somewhere beyond the stones, Sera had stopped sharpening. Tam had stopped pretending to read. Even the pines seemed to lean closer.

Elodie reached out and put her hand over his fist.

Her hand was warm. Warmer than it should be. The Veil's borrowed fire, banked in her blood.

"Brannoc," she said gently, and gentleness from her was worse than anger, "you didn't run from Aldenmere because you're a coward. You ran because staying would have killed you and changed nothing. There's a difference between watching and choosing. I'm not asking you to watch. I'm telling you what I chose."

"And I'm telling you it's a stupid choice."

"It might be." A ghost of her smile. "Most of mine are. Ask anyone."

"This isn't charm-selling, Elodie. This isn't lying badly to a witch-finder and running out the back door. This is arithmetic. Every tear you seal, every working, spends you. And when the anchor comes — and it's coming, Corvess is three days behind us and Ashvine's knights are two — you will stand in front of that tree of light and you will spend *everything*, because that is exactly the kind of choice you make, and I will be standing there watching, and I will not be able to stop it, and I will spend the rest of my long, cowardly, deserting life knowing I watched another village burn." His voice cracked on the last word. He hated it. He kept going anyway. "I lost everyone once. Do you hear me? Everyone. And I built nothing after, I built *walls*, and then you walked into my life bleeding other people's magic and refusing to leave, and I —"

He stopped. The rest of it stood in his throat, enormous, unsayable: *and I love you, and love is just the name we give the thing that makes losing unbearable.*

Elodie's eyes were bright. She didn't look away.

"Say it," she whispered.

"No," Brannoc said. "Not while you're planning to die. I'm not saying it as a farewell. You want the words, you stay alive and take them like a person, not a martyr."

A sound escaped her — half laugh, half sob. "You impossible, stubborn, honorable fool. You deserted your army and you won't desert an argument."

"Someone has to hold the line," he said. "Even if the line is just me, refusing to agree with you."

She squeezed his fist once, hard, and let go, and rose, and the morning light caught her and made her glow faintly at the edges, the Veil showing through her like sun through cheap cloth. She looked down at him, and for a moment her face was every Veilwarden's face, ancient and kind and grieving.

"I'm sorry," she said simply. "I'm sorry for the cost. I'm sorry you have to carry the watching. But I won't apologize for the choice, because it's the first fully mine I've ever made, and I find I rather like being the one who decides what I'm worth."

She walked to the edge of the stones, where Tam waited with the journals and Sera with the horses and Rue with his unreadable face, and Brannoc stayed kneeling a moment longer in the moss, breathing, letting the rage drain out and leave behind the only thing that had ever gotten him through anything: forward motion. One foot. Then the next.

When he joined them, Elodie was pointing east, where the pilgrim road crested a ridge and dropped toward a valley filled with morning mist.

"There," she said. "I can feel it now. Like a heartbeat under the world."

And then she swayed, just slightly, and pressed a hand to her side as if something inside her had twinged — a small motion, quickly hidden, the first flicker of the ledger being written — and Brannoc saw it, and Sera saw it, and no one said a word, and the company rode down toward the anchor with the mist closing behind them like a door.

Far to the south, faint as woodsmoke on the wind, Brannoc smelled ash on the air.

Corvess was coming.
