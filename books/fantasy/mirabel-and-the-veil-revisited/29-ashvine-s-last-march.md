## 29. Ashvine's Last March

The bells of the Hollow Crown had not rung in three hundred years.

Lady Ashvine stood beneath them in the dawn court, her hands folded at her waist, and listened to the sound she had hoped never to hear again. It was not a beautiful sound. The bell-metal had gone sour with age, and the note came out cracked, like a voice that had wept too long. But it carried. It carried through the stacked seasons of the palace — through the winter hall where frost bloomed on marble that remembered summer, through the autumn galleries with their leaves falling upward, down into the spring courts where the young ones danced without knowing why the music had stopped.

It carried out beyond the walls, into Mirabel itself, and every fae who heard it stopped what they were doing and went still, the way deer go still when they smell smoke on a wind that should be clean.

*The anchor is failing.*

Not the words themselves — no words rode the bell. But every soul in Mirabel was tied to the Veil by roots older than memory, and when the anchor shuddered, they felt it the way a tree feels lightning strike the earth a mile away: not pain, exactly, but the knowledge of it, arriving all at once in the marrow.

Ashvine had felt it first, three nights ago, in her sleep. She had dreamed of the anchor as she had seen it once, long ago, in her youth — a tree of living light standing at the hinge of the world, its roots drinking from both rivers, human and fae, holding the boundary between them like a cupped hand holds water. In the dream, the light had guttered. The roots had torn. And through the gaps, cold air had come pouring in from a country with no name, and everything it touched had simply *stopped* — not dying, for death is a kind of motion, but ceasing, the way a song ceases when the singer's throat is cut.

She had woken with her hand pressed flat against the wall of her chamber, as if she could hold up the sky by leaning on stone.

Now she stood in the dawn court, and the bells confirmed what her bones already knew, and around her the regent's council waited for her to speak.

They were a sorry assembly, and she loved them anyway, which was perhaps the most dangerous thing about her. Lord Vaelin of the Winter Gate, whose face had been beautiful once and was now a map of old frosts. The Lady of Thorns, who had not spoken aloud in eighty years and communicated instead through the roses that grew from her sleeves. Old Marrow, the archivist, so ancient he had begun to resemble his own books — leather-skinned, yellow-paged, dust sifting from him when he moved too quickly. And the knights of the Court, standing along the walls in their gray-lacquered armor, each one carrying a spear tipped with a splinter of the Veil itself, taken in the last war, when taking such things had still meant something.

"The bearer moves toward the anchor," said Marrow, in a voice like pages turning. "The mortal girl. She has performed the rite at the shrine of Alderhollow. She carries the full inheritance now."

"Then there is still time," said Vaelin, and his breath fogged the air though the hall was warm. "If the Veilwarden reaches the anchor and renews the binding—"

"If," said Ashvine, quietly, and the word fell among them like a stone into a well.

She let the silence stretch. She had learned, across four centuries of rule, that silence was the only honest language left to the powerful; everything else could be forged.

"You all felt the bell," she said at last. "You all felt what came before it. So I will not insult this council with comfort. The anchor is failing. Not weakening — *failing*, as a heart fails, unevenly, with long pauses between beats. The last Veilwarden line was broken two hundred years ago, and the binding has been running on residue ever since, on habit, on the stubbornness of roots that do not know they are dead." She turned her gaze along the row of them. "And now a girl of mixed blood, untrained until a month ago, walks toward the hinge of the world with an army of witch-finders behind her and a fae exile beside her, and we are asked to believe that everything will be well."

"She chose the third path," said the Lady of Thorns, and her roses rustled, and one of the knights translated the whisper of petals into speech: *"She refused the regent's offer. She refuses to seal the Veil. She means to open it."*

"Yes," said Ashvine. "She does."

Vaelin stepped forward. "Then we must stop her. If the Veil opens—"

"I know what happens if the Veil opens." Ashvine's voice did not rise. It never needed to. "I was there for the last war, Vaelin. Were you? No. None of you were, except Marrow, and Marrow was a child then, and children remember wars as stories." She looked at the archivist. "Tell them what it was. Not the battles. Tell them the rest."

Marrow's ancient eyes closed. When he spoke, it was with the reluctance of a man opening a book he had sworn never to read again.

"In the last years of the war," he said, "the Veil ran thin in a hundred places, and things crossed that were neither fae nor human — things that live in boundaries the way rot lives in walls. Whole villages on both sides went silent. Not destroyed. *Silent.* You could walk through them and hear your own heartbeat and nothing else. The mortals called it the Hollowing. We called it the same thing, in our own tongue, because grief needs no translation." He opened his eyes. "Two million dead, by the counting of either world. And the war did not end because anyone won it. It ended because both sides became too frightened to continue. That is the peace we have lived in since. A peace built on terror, sealed shut, and left alone in the dark for two hundred years."

"And now," said Ashvine, "a girl proposes to unseal it. To trust. To build a council of both worlds and hold the boundary open with living hands." She paused. "It is the most beautiful idea I have heard in four hundred years. And I am going to march against it."

The council stirred. Even the roses stilled.

"Regent—" began Vaelin.

"Hear me." She unfolded her hands at last, and let them see what she had kept hidden through the whole audience: that her fingers trembled. Not much. Only enough. "I offered her everything. Power, belonging, a home in Mirabel such as no mortal-born has held since the war. All she need do was seal the Veil from our side, and our realm would endure as it has endured — safe, separate, sovereign. She refused. She looked at me — a half-blood hedge-witch, a fugitive, a girl who sold luck-charms in gutters — and she refused a queen, politely, and walked away toward the anchor to gamble both worlds on hope." Ashvine's jaw tightened. "I have had four centuries to learn what hope costs. I buried the last generation that gambled on it. I will not bury this one."

"So we take the anchor ourselves," said Vaelin slowly. "We seal the Veil by force."

"No."

The word dropped like a blade.

"We do not seal it," Ashvine said. "Sealing it forever is slow death — you all know it, whatever lies we tell ourselves in the dark. The anchor drinks from both rivers. Cut off the mortal current, and Mirabel fades. Not quickly. Not dramatically. We would dim, century by century, like a lamp running out of oil, until the last of us was a story told by the last of us, and then nothing. I have watched the edges of our realm go gray already. You have too, if you are honest. The far orchards bloom later every year. The deep roads forget where they go." Her voice softened, and that softness was worse than any fury. "Sealing the Veil is how faeries die. Slowly, politely, with excellent manners, in a beautiful room."

"Then what does the regent propose?" asked the Lady of Thorns, through her knight's mouth.

Ashvine walked the length of the dawn court as she answered, past the pillars carved with the faces of every ruler since the Hollow Crown, past the window where the morning light came in the wrong color because even the sun over Mirabel had grown uncertain.

"I propose to reach the anchor before the armies do. Before Corvess and his witch-finders, who mean to destroy it outright — and do not doubt that he does, I have read the man's ancestors' letters, and destruction is the family trade. Before the mortal girl can attempt her binding and fail, or worse, succeed badly. I propose to stand at the hinge of the world with the authority of the fae court behind me, and I propose to *negotiate*." She stopped. "But I propose to negotiate from strength, with knights at my back and the anchor within my grasp, because hope without power is just a prettier way of begging."

"And if she refuses you again?" asked Marrow.

Ashvine was quiet for a moment.

"Then I will decide what must be done," she said, "when I am standing in front of the thing itself, and I can see with my own eyes how much light is left in it. I make no promises beyond that. Any ruler who promises you certainty in a hour like this one is lying to you, and I have lied to this court often enough in small matters that you deserve the truth in a large one: *I do not know what I will do.*"

She saw the fear move through them — the good fear, the honest kind, the fear of subjects who trusted their queen precisely because she admitted the ground was shaking. Vaelin bowed. The Lady of Thorns inclined her head, and a single white rose detached from her sleeve and drifted to the floor at Ashvine's feet, which was, in the old language of the thorn-court, a blessing and a warning in one gesture.

"Ready the knights," said Ashvine. "We ride within the hour."

* * *

She rode out through the Gray Gate at the head of three hundred knights, and the people of the capital lined the road to watch them pass, and Ashvine made herself look at every face.

That was the discipline she had set herself, four centuries ago, after the war: *look at them.* Rulers who stopped looking at their people's faces were the ones who started burning villages, on both sides of the Veil, in every age. So she looked. She saw the orchard-keepers with their stained hands. She saw the glasswrights of the winter quarter, carrying their impossible panes. She saw children lifted onto shoulders to see the knights go by, children who did not know that the bells had rung, who thought this was a parade, who waved.

One of them — a small girl with moth-wing ears and sap-green eyes, perched on a fruit-seller's cart — waved directly at Ashvine, with the fearless totality of the very young.

Ashvine raised her hand in answer, and kept her face composed, and inside her chest something folded up small and sharp and stayed there, like a splinter working deeper.

*This is why,* she told herself, riding on. *Remember this. Whatever you do at the anchor, this is why.*

Because here was the truth she had never said aloud in council, the truth under all her careful politics: Ashvine was not afraid for herself. Four hundred years was a long life by any measure, and she had spent it well, and if it ended at the hinge of the world she would go with her accounts more or less balanced. She was afraid the way a mother is afraid, except that her child was not a person but a *world* — this improbable, layered, luminous country where seasons lived in rooms and rivers ran uphill out of sheer delight and the oldest trees remembered being seeds twice. She was afraid of the gray creeping in from the edges. She was afraid of the silence in the far villages. She was afraid that the beautiful experiment of Mirabel, which had survived a war and two centuries of isolation and the slow rot of its own court, would finally die not in fire but in fading — and that it would be her watch when it happened, her name at the bottom of the ledger, the regent who presided over the long dusk.

And she was afraid — this was the sharpest splinter of all, and she had only admitted it to herself three nights ago, staring at the ceiling in the dark — that the mortal girl might be right.

Elodie Underhill. Hedge-witch. Charm-seller. Fugitive. A girl who had spent seven years burying her own gift to survive, and who had nonetheless walked toward the anchor rather than away from it, and who had looked a queen in the eye and chosen the harder mercy. Ashvine had read the reports. She had read Tam Vess's captured theories, and Sera Vane's intercepted letters, and Rue's treasonous correspondence going back decades, and somewhere in the accumulation of paper a portrait had assembled itself in her mind of a young woman who was not remarkable in any way the old chronicles valued — no prophecy, no prodigy, no sword — and who was remarkable in the only way that had ever actually mattered, which was that she kept choosing, again and again, at cost to herself, the option that left the most of the world alive.

A queen could hate such a person. It was easier than the alternative.

* * *

They made the border by nightfall of the second day, riding hard through country that grew stranger the closer they came to the hinge. The pilgrim road was ahead of them — Ashvine could feel it, a faint pull in her blood, the anchor calling its wardens home — and between her knights and the anchor lay the Gray Marches, where the Veil wore thin as old cloth.

At the edge of the Marches she called the halt, and rode forward alone, as her predecessors had done at every crisis since the founding, to look at the boundary with her own eyes.

She dismounted where the grass began to spiral. Trees grew in corkscrews here, their rings twisted like whorls in a fingerprint. A bird crossed the sky flying backward, wings sweeping the wrong direction, serene and unbothered. The air tasted of iron and rain that hadn't fallen yet. And beneath it all, faint but unmistakable, Ashvine felt the anchor's pulse — and counted the gap between beats.

It had lengthened. Three nights ago, in her dream, the rhythm had been like a sleeping heart. Now it was like a heart deciding, beat by beat, whether to bother.

She knelt in the spiraling grass and pressed her palm flat to the earth, the way she had pressed it to her chamber wall, as if she could hold anything up by leaning on it. The ground was cold in a way that had nothing to do with weather. Through the thin place she could hear — distantly, horribly — the other side. Mortal voices. Cart wheels. A dog barking at nothing. The ordinary gorgeous racket of the human world, separated from hers by a membrane wearing through like a worn knee.

And threaded through it, farther off, another sound: the cadence of marching men. Iron. Horses. The witch-finder army, coming from the south, toward the same hinge.

Corvess. Who believed, with the clean sincere conviction of a man reading his family's history as scripture, that the only safety for his kind lay in destroying the anchor altogether — in killing the heart so that neither world could ever use it again. He thought he was saving humanity. Ashvine had read enough of the last war's records to know that men who thought they were saving humanity had done most of its worst damage.

Three powers converging on one failing heart. A girl who wanted to share it. A general who wanted to kill it. A regent who wanted — what did she want? She turned the question over honestly, kneeling in the wrong-growing grass, because she had promised her court honesty and owed herself no less.

She wanted Mirabel to live. That was the root of it, clean and simple, and every branch that grew from it — the politics, the offers, the knights, the march — was shaped by that single taproot. She was not wicked. She knew that much, and knew also that knowing it changed nothing, because the last war had not been fought by the wicked either. It had been fought by people on both sides who loved their worlds and feared for them, armed with that love and that fear, pointed at each other like spears.

*I am not Corvess,* she told herself. *I do not want destruction. I want a door that holds.*

But wanting a door that holds, when the door was failing and the carpenters were at war — perhaps that, too, was just another way of pointing a spear.

She rose. Behind her, three hundred knights waited in the twilight, gray-lacquered, silent, the splinters of the old Veil glinting on their spearheads. Ahead of her, the Marches shimmered, and beyond them, faint as a star behind cloud, the anchor's light pulsed — paused — pulsed.

"Regent?" Vaelin had ridden up quietly. He followed her gaze to the horizon. "The mortal company cannot be more than a day ahead. The witch-finders, two days behind us. If we ride through the night—"

"We ride through the night," Ashvine said.

She swung back into the saddle, and for a moment she did not give the order. She sat looking east, where the light was, and let herself feel the whole weight of it — the splinter of the waving child, the fading orchards, the girl who had refused her politely, the general marching with fire in his family's blood, the great tired heart beating slower at the center of everything.

*Let there be another way,* she thought, with a sudden fierce clarity that startled her, because it was not a queen's thought at all. *Let the girl be right. Let someone, this once, find the other way.*

Then she lowered her hand, and the horns sounded, and the knights of the Hollow Crown rode forward into the Gray Marches, toward the anchor, toward the hinge of the world — and far ahead, at the edge of Ashvine's hearing, the pulse stumbled, caught, and beat again, a little weaker than before.
