## 30. Corvess's Last March

The road to the anchor had been a pilgrim's road once. General Aldous Corvess rode it at the head of three hundred men and remembered it as a child, when it had been nothing but a rumor his grandmother told to frighten him — *the old kings walked that way to bargain with the shining folk, and none of them came back whole.* She had told it by firelight with her hands folded in her lap like a woman praying, and she had never once smiled when she said it.

He was fifty-one years old, and he had spent forty of those years learning why she hadn't smiled.

* * *

His tent went up before dawn on the shoulder of a hill called, in the oldest survey maps, the Widow's Tooth — a name no one living could explain, though Tam Vess probably could, if anyone still trusted the word of a heretic cartographer. Corvess did not trust him. He had interrogated the man for two days now, in the careful, patient manner that had made his reputation long before his rank did, and what he had learned was this: Tam Vess would lie about small things to protect large ones, which meant every answer was a locked door wearing the costume of an open one.

But he had learned other things too. Things the man hadn't meant to give away.

The map was real. The bearer was real. And the bearer was a girl from Corvane who sold luck-charms under a false name and had been hiding a Veilwarden's blood the way other people hid a debt.

Corvess stood at the mouth of his tent and watched the sun come up over the Gray Marches, and thought: *a girl.* A shopgirl with hedge-magic in her veins and a dead man's chart in her hands, walking toward the hinge of the world with a deserter, a disgraced scholar, and — according to the reports that had begun arriving two days ago, reports he did not entirely believe — a fae exile walking openly at her side.

It should have comforted him, how small they were. It did not. Small things had ended his family once.

"General." Captain Ilsa Reyne ducked through the tent flap, helmet under her arm, frost in her close-cropped hair. "Scouts are back from the ford. The fae have crossed."

"How many?"

"Knights. Maybe sixty. They move like weather, sir — we couldn't get a clean count. They're on the pilgrim road ahead of us, moving toward the anchor." She hesitated, which Ilsa Reyne never did. "There's something else. The trees along their line of march. The men say the trees are *bowing*. Not bending in wind. Bowing. Toward them."

Corvess nodded slowly, as though this were ordinary intelligence and not a report from the edge of nightmare. "And our friend Vess?"

"Asleep. Under guard. He asked for ink again this morning."

"He can ask until the world ends." He pulled on his gauntlets, flexing his fingers against the leather. "We march within the hour. We will not let the fae reach the anchor first."

"And if we reach it second, sir? If they're already there when we arrive?"

Corvess looked at her. She was thirty, maybe younger, born after the last scare, raised on the official histories in which the War of the Shining Court was a footnote and the Veilwardens were traitors and the witch-finders were the reason there was still a human world at all. He had written some of those histories himself, in the years between field commands. He knew exactly how much truth a comfortable people could digest, and it was very little, and he had fed them accordingly for twenty years.

"If they're already there," he said, "then we do what my grandfather's grandfather failed to do, and we finish it properly."

Reyne held his gaze for a moment longer than rank strictly permitted. Then she put her helmet on and went to wake the camp.

* * *

They marched through country that remembered being wrong.

Corvess had crossed the Gray Marches twice before in his career — once as a young officer mapping the border forts, once fifteen years ago chasing a smuggler's route that turned out to be a smuggling route for *people*, hedge-witches and half-bloods fleeing north toward the thin places. Both times the land had unsettled him. This time it frightened him, and he noted the fact with clinical displeasure, the way a physician notes a symptom in himself.

The trees grew in spirals here. He had seen that before. What he had not seen before was the birds.

They flew backward. Not flying tail-first — flying *backward through the air*, wings beating in reverse, as though the sky itself were being rewound around them. The men had stopped pointing at things like that days ago. Now they just watched, silent, and marched faster.

At midmorning they passed a shrine at the roadside — one of the old pilgrim shrines, its stone worn smooth by centuries of hands that had come to ask the border for safe passage. Someone had defaced it recently. Not vandals; the carving was deliberate, skilled. A figure had been cut into the stone where the old blessing had been: a woman standing with her arms out, roots growing from her feet and light growing from her hands, and beneath it, in letters cut deep enough to last:

*SHE HOLDS IT FOR ALL OF US.*

Corvess reined in and looked at it for a long time.

"That's new," said Sergeant Odo Bram, riding at his left hand. "Wasn't there when we passed going east."

"No," Corvess agreed. "It wasn't."

"It's her, isn't it, sir. The girl. The bearer." Bram was a solid man, unimaginative, loyal in the way of soldiers who had decided long ago that thinking was above their pay. But even he had heard the rumors moving through the column like a fever — the girl who sealed a tear in the Veil with her own blood, the girl the map chose, the girl the fae were bowing their trees to. "Men are saying she's the last Veilwarden. Men are saying if she reaches the anchor—"

"Men are saying a great many things." Corvess urged his horse forward past the shrine, and did not look back at it. "What matters is not what she is. It's what the anchor does when she touches it. Vess confirmed it under questioning, whether he meant to or not. The chart is a key. The anchor is failing. And a failing lock, gentlemen, opens from either side."

He let that sit in the cold air behind him as they rode on.

Because that was the whole of it. That was the thing he had tried to make the Crown understand for twenty years while ministers smiled politely and filed his reports under *alarmism*: the Veil was not a wall. A wall keeps people out. The Veil was a *wound that had scarred over*, and scars fail from the inside as easily as the outside, and when it failed — fully, finally, catastrophically — it would not be an invasion. Invasions you could fight. Armies you could meet in a field. What came through a torn Veil would not be armies. It would be the *old war*, the one his grandmother had described without smiling, the one where the shining folk had treated human kingdoms the way children treat anthills — curious, then careless, then cruel, and never once sorry.

He had read the primary sources. That was the difference between him and the ministers, and he had paid for the difference with his career's slow climb and his wife's quiet departure and his daughter's estrangement, all of them prices he had catalogued and considered fair. He had read the monastic chronicles from before the burning of the archives. He had read what the fae courts had done to the borderlands in the last war, the century of it, the harvests taken and the children *changed* and the villages that simply ceased, unremembered, their names surviving only because a Veilwarden had scratched them into a chart before dying.

The Veilwardens had ended that war. He would grant them that much, and he granted it grudgingly, the way a man grants a debt he resents. They had built the Veil and anchored it and bled for it, generation after generation, and the borderlands had known four hundred years of peace because of them.

And then his own family had helped break them.

* * *

He thought about that more than he admitted to anyone, in the hour before sleep and the hour after waking, in the gray territory where a man's certainties go to be audited.

The Corvess name had not always meant witch-finders. Three hundred years ago it had meant *kings' men* — the Kingsbreakers, some chronicles called them, though the family preferred the cleaner term: the Crown's Hand. When the last Veilwardens had grown too powerful, too secretive, too *necessary*, when they had begun deciding policy at the anchor without consulting any throne, it had been a Corvess who led the king's soldiers up the pilgrim road to take the anchor away from them. It had been a Corvess who burned the warden houses. It had been a Corvess, so the private family record claimed, who struck the blow that broke the last warden's line — and the family record also claimed, in the same careful hand, that the man had wept while he did it, and had written afterward: *I have saved the kingdoms from their guardians, and I do not know if I have saved them.*

Three hundred years later, Aldous Corvess had inherited the question along with the name, and he had answered it the only way he knew how: by making certain the question never had to be asked again. No wardens, no anchor, no hinge. Burn the door and both houses stop burning each other.

That was the doctrine. That was the whole doctrine, and it was simple enough to teach to recruits, and he had taught it to thousands, and he believed it — he *did* believe it, he reminded himself, watching the backward birds wheel over the spiraled trees — the way a man believes in the load-bearing wall of his own house.

And yet.

Last night, interrogating Tam Vess by lamplight, he had asked the question he asked everyone: *why do you do this? Why throw away your life for a theory?* And Vess — exhausted, wrists chained, dignity long since surrendered — had looked at him with something worse than defiance. Pity.

"You've read the same chronicles I have," Vess had said. "You know what the fae did. You know better than almost anyone alive. So tell me, General — when your grandmother told you the story by the fire, did she ever once tell you what the Veilwardens' war actually cost *them*? Did she ever mention that the last warden died holding the door shut with her body while your ancestor's soldiers cut her down, and that the tear she was holding closed stayed open for nine days, and that everything that came through in those nine days is the reason you have a job?" He had leaned forward as far as the chains allowed. "Your family didn't save the world from the wardens, General. Your family killed the doctors and called the plague cured. And now the wound is opening again, and the last doctor is a frightened girl from Corvane, and you're marching to kill her too. You're not the hero of this history. You're just the newest chapter of the same mistake."

Corvess had ordered him gagged, and slept badly, and told himself the sleeplessness was the campaign.

* * *

They made camp that night within sight of the anchor valley.

He felt it before he saw it — everyone did. The horses first, ears flat, refusing to graze. Then the men, going quiet in a way that had nothing to do with discipline. And then Corvess himself, standing at the ridge line as the light failed, feeling something in his chest like a second heartbeat that was not his own, slow and vast and *faltering*, the way a great bell falters when its crack has gone too deep.

The valley below held a lake that reflected stars that were not in the sky. At its center stood a tree of light — he could see it even at this distance, even in the dark, a pillar of pale gold like a candle seen through fog, and around its base the light guttered. Flickered. Missed beats, like the heart of a dying man.

"There it is," Sergeant Bram said softly beside him. "Gods. It's beautiful."

"It's dying," Corvess said.

Below, across the valley, he could see the fae campfires — or what served the fae for fire, silver-white flames that cast no smoke — and among them, moving with terrible grace, the shapes of knights in armor that caught starlight and kept it. Sixty, the scouts had said. Perhaps more now. And beyond them, further off along the pilgrim road, the small stubborn lights of the girl's company, still moving, still ahead of everyone, a handful of souls walking toward the hinge of the world with the fate of two realms in a satchel.

Three powers converging on one dying tree. He had studied battles his whole life, and he had never once seen a battlefield shaped like this one, where every army wanted the same ground and no two of them wanted the same world.

"Send for Reyne," he said. "Full battle order before dawn. And bring me Vess. Ungagged."

Bram blinked. "Sir?"

"You heard me. Ungagged. If I'm about to do the thing my family has been preparing three hundred years to do, I want to hear every argument against it one final time — and then I want to watch the man's face when I prove him wrong."

He turned from the ridge and walked back down toward the fires, toward his three hundred men warming their hands against a cold that came from nowhere, toward the tents and the banners and the long machinery of the only future he had ever been able to imagine. Behind him the anchor flickered in its valley, and across the water the fae lights burned silver and patient, and somewhere on the dark road between them a girl he had never met was carrying a burden his family had made inevitable.

*Two peoples,* he thought, *armed with the same dread, marching to the same tree to save the world from each other.*

It was, he reflected, the oldest story there was. He had simply always assumed he'd die in a better chapter of it.

In his tent, by lamplight, Tam Vess waited with his chains and his pitying eyes — and Corvess sat down across from him, laid the interrogation ledger open, and asked the first question.

Vess listened. And then, instead of answering, the disgraced cartographer began, very quietly, to laugh.
