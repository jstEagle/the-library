## 31. The Siege of the Anchor

Elodie felt the anchor before she saw it.

It began as a warmth in her blood on the second morning of the Anchor Road — a low, patient heat, like a hearth-fire banked for the night — and by the time the pilgrim road crested the last ridge, that warmth had become a pulse she could feel in her teeth, her wrists, the soft hollow of her throat. The map had gone quiet against her ribs two days past, its ink still, its purpose spent. It no longer needed to guide her. She was the guidance now.

"There," Brannoc said beside her, and his voice came out strange, flattened, as though the ridge itself were holding its breath.

The world's hinge was not what the old stories promised. No mountain crowned in starlight, no gate of silver. Instead there was a valley — wide and green and utterly ordinary at first glance, ringed by hills the color of old bronze — and at its center stood the tree.

It was light, not wood. A tree of living radiance, tall as a cathedral spire, its branches spreading like slow white fire, its roots plunging into the earth in great luminous rivers that glowed beneath the grass for half a mile in every direction. Where the roots ran, the air shimmered faintly, as if seen through summer glass. Elodie understood without being told: this was the Veil made visible. Every thread of separation between Mirabel and the human world ran through those roots, and every thread was fraying. Whole limbs of the tree had gone dark, gray as ash, and where they hung over the valley the grass beneath grew in spirals, wrong and hungry.

"It's dying," Sera said quietly. She had not spoken much since decoding her father's letters; grief had burned down to something harder in her, something useful. "Gods. It's actually dying."

"Then we'd best hurry," Rue said. The fae exile stood at the ridge's edge with the wind pulling at his silver-dark hair, and for once there was nothing elegant in his posture — only urgency, raw and unhidden. "Because we are not the only ones who can feel it."

He was right. Down in the valley's southern mouth, where the pilgrim road descended from the human kingdoms, dust rose in a long brown banner. An army. Banners Elodie could not read at this distance but knew anyway — the black-and-crimson of the Crown's witch-finders, Corvess's host, marching with the terrible discipline of people who believed they were saving the world.

And from the north, out of the stacked impossible seasons of Mirabel, came another column — fae knights in armor the color of frost and dusk, moving without sound even at full march, their banners bearing the sigil of the Hollow Crown. Ashvine's last march.

Between them, at the center of everything, stood a dying tree of light and five people who meant to stand in front of it.

"Well," Tam said, pushing his cracked spectacles up his nose. His hands were shaking; they had been shaking since the ford, since his escape, since whatever he had endured in Corvess's tents that he would not speak of yet. But his voice was steady, the way it always went steady when he was terrified and thinking at the same time. "I've read about battles. I've never been in one. I should like to note, for the historical record, that I find the experience significantly worse than described."

"Nobody's making you stay," Brannoc said.

"Yes, well." Tam adjusted his pack. "Someone has to take notes."

* * *

They made their stand at the first ring of roots, where the light-rivers ran closest to the surface and the ground itself hummed with the anchor's failing strength. Elodie knelt and pressed both palms flat to the glowing grass, and the Veil answered her — a shudder of recognition running up her arms, the power she had carried since the map chose her rising to meet the source it had always been meant to serve.

She could feel everything. The tear on the Gray Marches she had sealed with her own blood, still scarred but holding. A hundred smaller wounds across the boundary, weeping. And beneath it all, the anchor itself — vast beyond comprehension and failing, its roots torn, its light guttering like a candle in a draft. It knew her. It reached for her the way a drowning thing reaches for a rope, and Elodie had to grit her teeth against the pull, because if she let it take everything it wanted now, there would be nothing left of her to bargain with when Ashvine and Corvess arrived.

"Elodie." Brannoc's hand on her shoulder, warm and solid, dragging her back. "Don't give it all. Not yet."

"I know." She sat back on her heels, breathing hard. Her fingertips had gone white as bone where they'd touched the light. "It's like standing in a river that wants to drink *me*."

"Then we hold the bank until you're ready." He crouched beside her, and for a moment the gruffness fell away and she saw the fear underneath — not fear of the armies, but fear of what the rite at the shrine had shown her, what they all now knew: that every act of her power cost her years, and that today would cost more than any day before it. "You remember what I said at the shrine."

"You said I was a fool."

"I said worse than that."

"And then you stayed anyway."

Brannoc looked out at the converging armies and said nothing, which was answer enough. The deserter who had run from one burning village rather than light it himself, and who had spent years believing courage was a thing he'd lost — he had found it again somewhere on this road, and it looked, Elodie thought, like refusing to leave.

Sera appeared at the crest of the rise, running low, her dark hair tied back with a strip of her father's coat. "Riders," she said. "From the south. Three of them, under a parley flag. They're asking for the bearer of the map."

"Corvess doesn't parley," Brannoc said.

"He does when he thinks he's already won." Sera's jaw tightened. "There's something else. There's a man with them — half-starved, in scholar's robes. They're saying he came to them voluntarily."

Elodie's stomach dropped. "*Tam?*"

"No." Sera shook her head slowly. "It's a trick of some kind. It has to be. He escaped three days ago — the fae scouts confirmed it. Why would he walk into their camp?"

"Because Corvess has something he wants," Elodie said grimly, standing. "Or because Corvess has something he fears. Either way, I'm going to hear it."

"That's madness—"

"Sera. If Tam's turned, I need to know before I bind my life to that tree. And if he hasn't—" She met Sera's eyes. "Then someone down there is wearing his face or his name, and I want to know why."

* * *

The parley was held at the midpoint between the root-ring and the southern army, close enough that Elodie could see the witch-finders' faces: hard, young, certain. General Corvess did not come himself. He sent a captain with a voice like gravel and, walking behind the guard, wrists chained, head bowed —

Tam Vess.

Except it wasn't. Elodie knew it the moment the figure lifted his head. The man wore Tam's robes and Tam's spectacles and Tam's stooped carriage, but his eyes were wrong — too steady, too empty, a puppet's patience. Magic clung to him like smoke. Fae-work, or something older, worn like a mask.

"Bearer of the chart," the captain said, unrolling a scroll with ceremonial slowness. "The Witch-Finder General extends terms. Surrender yourself and the artifact. The scholar will be released unharmed. Your companions may go free. The General desires no battle at this holy place."

"The General desires to destroy this holy place," Elodie said.

"The General desires to save humanity," the captain replied, without a flicker of doubt, and that was the most frightening thing she had heard all day. "The last war killed a million souls before the Veil was raised. Do you know what stands on the other side of it, girl? Do you know what the fae courts did to our grandmothers' grandmothers? The General remembers. Someone must."

Behind him, the false Tam smiled very slightly, and its mouth was full of too many teeth.

Elodie's hand drifted toward her belt, toward the knife she'd used to seal the tear on the Marches, and Brannoc's warning grip closed on her arm. *Not here. Not your blood. Not your years.*

"We'll consider your terms," she said aloud, cold and clear, buying hours she intended to spend differently.

As the parley party turned, the false Tam glanced back at her — and for one heartbeat the mask slipped, or was allowed to slip, and the eyes looking out of Tam Vess's face were desperate and human and *Tam's*, and his lips shaped three silent words with the careful precision of a man who had rehearsed them a thousand times in a cage:

*Ashes. Ledger. North.*

Then the mask closed, and the thing wearing her friend walked away.

"Ashes," Sera whispered, when they were safely back behind the root-ring. Her face had gone pale. "That's — my father used a cipher like that. Ashes means *burned*. Burned records. Burned evidence. Ledger means—"

"Proof," Elodie said. Something was kindling in her chest, fierce and fragile. "Corvess keeps a ledger. Tam told me once — royal cartographers audit the witch-finders' requisitions, it's how he first found the records of the Veilwarden purges. If Tam got loose long enough to reach Corvess's baggage train—"

"He didn't just escape," Brannoc breathed. "He escaped *with something*. And then he let himself be taken back so he could tell us where it is."

"North," Rue said softly. All of them turned. The fae exile was looking at the northern army, at Ashvine's silent columns, and his beautiful face had gone strange. "The baggage trains are north, between the armies. Which means to retrieve it, someone must cross the ground between two hosts about to collide." He smiled, thin and sharp. "Fortunately, you have a traitor who knows exactly how fae knights see in the dark."

* * *

Night fell, and the armies did not attack.

That frightened Elodie more than an assault would have. She could feel them through the anchor's roots — thousands of hearts to the south beating with righteous dread, hundreds to the north pulsing with cold court-fear — two peoples armed with the same terror, pointed at each other across a dying god. Dawn would bring the collision. Everyone on that field knew it. Corvess needed only to wait for light; Ashvine needed only to strike first.

So they went out into the dark instead.

Rue led them through the no-man's-land between the camps, and it was the strangest hour of Elodie's life: four shadows slipping between sleeping armies, past pickets whose lanterns burned green with fae-oil, past sentries murmuring prayers to saints who had never heard of Mirabel. The anchor's light lit the clouds above the valley like a false dawn, and beneath that glow the whole world held its breath.

The baggage train sat in a shallow fold of ground at the northern edge of Corvess's camp, guarded by six men and a locked iron wagon marked with the General's personal seal.

Six guards was three too many for stealth and two too few to matter, once Brannoc stopped trying to be stealthy. What followed was short, brutal, and utterly silent — the deserter moved like a man settling an old debt, and Elodie kept her magic leashed and hated every second of it, because using it here, now, would light her up like a beacon for both armies. Sera dropped the last guard with a sap and an apology she didn't mean.

The iron wagon's lock surrendered to Rue, who touched it and murmured a word in a language that made the metal forget it had ever been closed.

Inside, among requisition scrolls and salted meat and crates of witch-finder irons, they found a strongbox. Inside the strongbox, wrapped in oilcloth, Tam Vess — the real Tam, hollow-eyed, wrists scarred from irons, alive — sat clutching a leather ledger to his chest like a child with a doll.

"You came," he whispered.

"You absolute *idiot*," Elodie said, and pulled him out, and held onto him hard enough to hurt, because he was trembling and so was she. "You let them take you back. You let them put that thing in your skin—"

"It was the only way. They had wards on the camp; I couldn't get a message out, but a prisoner they think is theirs—" He laughed, wet and ragged. "Do you know what I found, Elodie? In the General's own records? While they thought I was broken?" He thrust the ledger at her, open to a page near the end, his finger stabbing at entries in Corvess's tight, pious hand. "Look. *Look.*"

By the anchor's distant glow, Elodie read.

Requisitions. That was all it was, at first — grain, rope, chains, lamp-oil. Then the other columns. Villages visited. Persons detained. Persons *processed*. Dates matched to places Elodie knew, market towns and river hamlets, and beside each entry a tally, and beside the tallies, in the margins, Corvess's own annotations: *no residue found. family line extinguished. no residue found. no residue found.*

Page after page. Year after year. Not the war against dangerous magic the proclamations described — a decades-long campaign of extinction against bloodlines, most of them carrying nothing but a grandmother's charm and a story. Including, on a page dated twenty-three years ago, in a village called Underhill's Rest:

*family line extinguished.*

Elodie's vision blurred. "He wrote us down," she heard herself say. "Like inventory."

"He believes every word of it is holy work," Tam said quietly. "That's what makes him dangerous. That's what makes this—" he tapped the ledger "—the only weapon that can stop him. Not power. *Proof.* His own army doesn't know what he's been doing. Show them this, and his holy war dies of shame."

A horn sounded from the south. Then another. Then the whole southern camp woke like a kicked hive, torches blooming across the slope, and far off, answering, the fae knights of the north began to move — silently, terribly, frost-colored armor catching the anchor's light.

"They're coming," Brannoc said. "Both of them. Now."

"Then we run," Sera said, hefting the ledger, "and we hope a book is worth more than an army."

"It isn't," Tam said. "But it might be worth enough."

* * *

Dawn broke over the world's hinge, and it was the color of iron.

Elodie stood at the first ring of roots with her company around her — Brannoc with a fallen knight's sword because his own had shattered at the ford, Sera with her father's knives, Tam clutching the ledger like a shield, Rue with his treason finally, fully chosen — and watched the two hosts come down upon them.

The witch-finders struck the valley floor first, a tide of black and crimson, and the fae knights met them at the third ring of roots, and the ancient war began again with a sound like the sky tearing.

After that, Elodie remembered it in fragments ever after: the flash of Corvess's vanguard breaking like a wave on fae steel. Sera at her shoulder, turning aside a blade meant for Elodie's throat, shouting something Elodie couldn't hear over the roar. Tam, madly, actually *reading aloud* from the ledger to any soldier who came near enough, hurling truth like stones. Brannoc, bleeding, laughing, holding ground that had no strategic value except that it was where Elodie stood. Rue moving through the melee like water finding every crack. And through it all the anchor blazing overhead, its dark limbs shedding ash-light, its roots drinking the spilled blood of both armies and dimming further with every drop, as if the land itself grieved what it had tried so long to prevent.

And through the chaos, cutting toward her with the certainty of a drawn line, came two figures.

One in frost-and-dusk armor, helm crowned with the sigil of the Hollow Crown, walking untouched through the battle as though the fighting were weather around her. One in black and crimson, unhelmed, gray-templed, his face carved with the serene conviction of a man arriving at the altar of his life's purpose.

Lady Ashvine, Regent of Mirabel, who would seal the Veil and save her people by amputating half the world.

General Corvess, Witch-Finder of the Crown, who would destroy the anchor and save his people by burning the bridge between them.

They reached the inner ring together, enemies for a heartbeat pausing in mutual recognition, and then both turned to her — to the last Veilwarden, kneeling at the roots of the dying tree with her blood already prickling beneath her skin, ready to pay whatever the binding cost.

"The anchor, warden," Ashvine said, her voice like bells over ice. "Choose."

"The anchor, girl," Corvess said, raising the pale length of a weapon Elodie's blood recognized with horror — Veilwarden steel, stolen, reforged to unmake what it had been forged to keep. "Choose."

The tree of light guttered above them all.

Elodie rose to her feet, and chose neither.
