## 32. Brannoc's Stand

The bridge had no name that anyone living remembered, and Brannoc Hale thought that was fitting. He had spent most of his life on bridges without names — the crossing points between one bad decision and the next — and this one, a humpbacked span of gray stone strung across a river the color of hammered tin, would be his last if he had anything to say about it.

He crouched at its northern end and tested the stone with his thumb, the way he'd once tested armor plate in the king's forges at Vaelmoor. Old work. Good work. The mortar was crumbled at the joints but the blocks themselves were river-granite, laid by hands that had expected floods and carts and maybe the occasional war. It would hold.

It would have to.

Behind him, up the pilgrim road where it climbed through black pines toward the anchor, Elodie Underhill was walking with her spine straight and her jaw set in the particular way she got when she'd decided to be brave about something and dared anyone to notice she was afraid. He'd seen that look on recruits before their first battle. He'd worn it himself, once, a lifetime ago, before he'd learned what battles actually cost.

*Buy her time,* Tam had said, gripping Brannoc's forearm with those ink-stained fingers. *That's all. The anchor chamber is close now — she can feel it, we all can. If Ashvine's knights catch her on the open road—*

*I know my trade, scholar,* Brannoc had said, and Tam had flinched, because Brannoc almost never said *I* about anything. He said *we*. He said *the company*. He said *someone should*. Twenty years of soldiering had taught him that the man who said *I* too often was either a fool or a dead man, and usually both.

But today he said *I*, because today there was no *we* that could fit on this bridge.

* * *

They came out of the trees an hour past midday, and they were beautiful, which was the worst thing about them.

Brannoc had fought fae twice before — skirmishes on the Marches, back when the Crown still pretended the border wars were rumors — and both times he'd come away with the same unsettled feeling, like he'd been struck by something that hadn't fully decided to exist. Ashvine's knights were worse than the border-raiders of his memory. They rode horses that might have been horses once, tall and pale as birch trees, their manes moving in a wind that touched nothing else. Their armor was silver-gray and seemed to drink the light rather than reflect it. Their faces —

Their faces were the problem. They weren't cruel. That was what he'd never been able to explain to men who hadn't seen it. Human soldiers going into a charge wore cruelty or fear or the numb blankness of men doing a job. These knights wore *sorrow*. They looked at him across the length of the bridge with eyes like winter water, and every line of them said: *we do not want this, and we will do it perfectly.*

There were nine of them. Brannoc counted twice, because counting was a thing you did when your hands wanted to shake.

Nine fae knights against one deserting soldier with a borrowed spear, a shield older than he was, and a body that had been telling him for years it was ready to retire.

"Well," he said aloud, to nobody, because talking steadied him the way prayer never had. "Could be worse."

It could not, actually, be worse. But lying to yourself was a soldier's oldest skill, and Brannoc had kept his edge.

* * *

He thought about Corvess's men instead of being afraid. That was the trick of it, the trick he'd learned at nineteen in the mud outside Harrowgate: you couldn't stop fear, but you could give it somewhere else to live. So he fed it memories.

He fed it the village. Thornmere, the reports had called it, though the reports had gotten everything else wrong, so why not the name. He'd stood in formation while Captain Dree gave the order, and he'd watched the flames go up over houses full of people whose only crime was keeping hedge-charms above their doorways, and he'd done nothing. Stood there. Held his spear. Been a good soldier.

Every night since, in whatever ditch or barracks or stolen bed he'd slept in, he went back to that hillside. Not to punish himself — punishment was cheap, punishment was easy, punishment let you feel bad and change nothing. He went back to remind himself what obedience cost when you paid it in other people's blood.

He had deserted the next morning. He had spent eleven years running from that hillside, and somewhere in the last few months — somewhere between Elodie pressing a hot map into his hands and Sera teaching him to laugh again and Tam arguing theology around a campfire until dawn — he had stopped running and started walking *toward* something.

Now the something needed time, and time was bought the old way.

"Nine of you," he called across the water, and his voice came out steady, which surprised him. "I've killed better odds than you."

The lead knight raised a hand, and the column halted. When he spoke, his voice was like frost forming — beautiful, and faintly sad. *"Soldier of the iron kingdoms. Stand aside. Our quarrel is not with you."*

"It is, though." Brannoc rolled his shoulders and set his feet. "Your lady wants the girl. The girl is mine to protect. That makes it my quarrel." He tapped the butt of his spear against the stone. "Also, I don't much like your horses."

A sound moved through the knights — not laughter, nothing so warm, but the acknowledgment of a joke properly made. The lead knight dismounted. Behind him, so did the others, and Brannoc felt his stomach drop in the old familiar way. Dismounted meant they meant to cross. Meant they meant to fight on foot, blade to blade, which was the only kind of fight where numbers told.

*"You cannot hold this bridge,"* the knight said, drawing a long pale sword that hummed like a struck bell. *"We are sorry for it."*

"Yeah," Brannoc said. "Everybody's sorry. That's the whole damn world these days."

And then the first three came across, and there was no more time for talk.

* * *

Fighting on a bridge was a butcher's arithmetic. One man could hold a narrow span against many, but only by spending himself, coin by coin, cut by cut, because there was nowhere to step back and breathe. Every exchange cost. Every parry rang up his arms. The fae swords were lighter than steel and stranger than steel, and when they touched his shield they left marks that smoked.

Brannoc had been good once. He found, with a kind of distant wonder, that he was still good. His body remembered what his conscience had tried to make him forget: the angle to turn a thrust, the half-step that stole ground, the short ugly counter-cut to the knee that ended arguments. He put the first knight into the river with a shield-bash that cracked something in the man's elegant chestplate, and the second he caught on the spear and held, held, *held*, feeling the fae's strength like a current trying to drag him off the span, and then he simply let go of the shaft and drew his knife and settled it the close way.

The third knight was faster than the first two. The blade opened Brannoc's arm from elbow to wrist before he saw it move, and the pain arrived late and enormous, and he roared — not from the wound but from fury at himself, at the slowness of his aging meat — and drove forward into the cut, inside the reach of the sword, and headbutted the knight off the bridge.

Three down. Six coming. Blood sheeting down his hand, making the spear haft slick.

Somewhere behind him, far up the road, he imagined he could hear Elodie running. Good. Run, girl. Don't look back. Looking back was how the dead got made.

* * *

The fourth and fifth came together, and they were better, and it stopped being a fight and became a negotiation conducted entirely in pain.

He gave ground — a pace, two — trading space for time, giving up the middle of the bridge inch by inch because inches were all he had to sell. A blade kissed his thigh. Another scored his shoulder. He broke a knight's wrist with his shield rim and took a gash across the scalp for it that turned the world red and ringing. He was aware, in the way a drowning man is aware of the shore, that he was losing. Slowly. Expensively. But losing.

*Hold until dark,* Tam had said. *Or until she reaches the gate. Whichever comes first.*

Dark was hours away. The gate was minutes. He did the arithmetic the way he'd done it at Harrowgate and Thornmere and every nameless ford since, and the answer was the same answer it had always been, and for the first time in eleven years the answer didn't taste like ash.

At Thornmere he had obeyed, and people burned. Here he disobeyed every instinct of survival drilled into him across twenty years, and someone lived. That was the whole ledger. That was everything. A man could build a life on the difference between those two hillsides, even if the life was short.

"You're not getting past," he told the knights, and spat blood onto the stones between them. "Not one of you. Not today. Write that down."

* * *

The sixth knight nearly killed him.

She came low and fast where the others had come high and formal, and her sword went through the meat of his left shoulder and pinned him — *pinned him*, cold metal grinding against bone — to the shield strap beneath, and for one long white moment Brannoc Hale hung on a fae blade like a moth on a pin and saw, very clearly, the end of the arithmetic.

He saw Thornmere burning. He saw his mother's kitchen in Vaelmoor, bread on the board, before any of it. He saw Elodie's face the night she'd sewn the gash in his arm from the Gray Marches, frowning in concentration, saying *hold still, you enormous man, I'm not losing you to infection after you survived witch-finders.* He saw Sera laughing. Tam lecturing. The fire, the road, the small stubborn family that had assembled itself out of runaways and failures and grief.

*Well,* he thought, with the strange calm of a man watching his own blood drip onto ancient stones. *This is a fine place to stop running.*

He let go of the spear.

The knight's eyes widened — fae eyes, winter-water eyes, and in them he saw surprise, and beneath the surprise something that looked horribly like respect. Brannoc grabbed the blade with his bare right hand, ignoring the way it bit, and hauled himself forward along it, closer, closer, into the kill-range where swords are useless and knives are gospel, and put his knife under the edge of her gorget and pushed.

She fell. He fell with her, shoulder tearing free of the blade, and lay gasping on the bridge stones in a spreading pool of his own blood while the remaining three knights stood at the southern end of the span and looked at him, and did not advance.

Why aren't they advancing, he thought. Then he heard it.

Hooves. But not fae hooves — the heavy, honest drumbeat of army cavalry, and the bugle-call he knew in his bones from twenty years of service, the Crown's triple-note, and rising over the river valley from the east came the banners of the witch-finders, General Corvess's black-and-silver, hundreds strong, pouring down the pilgrim road toward the bridge.

Toward the anchor.

Toward Elodie.

* * *

He got up. He did not decide to get up; his body simply did it, the way a wall keeps standing after the house around it has fallen. The three fae knights watched him rise, bleeding from a dozen wounds, and the lead knight — the sorrowful one, the one who spoke — made a gesture, and they withdrew a step. Only a step. Waiting. Letting the bigger wolf come.

Corvess's vanguard reached the southern end of the bridge in a clatter of harness and steel, and at its head rode a captain Brannoc recognized — Dree. Older, heavier, decorated. Alive and promoted, while Thornmere's ashes blew wherever ashes blow.

"Hale." Dree's voice carried the flat disgust of a man looking at a stain. "Deserter. Step aside. This doesn't concern you."

Brannoc planted his spear — someone else's spear, picked up off the stones, he'd lost his own somewhere in the red haze — and stood square in the middle of the bridge, and laughed. It hurt to laugh. He did it anyway.

"Dree. You remember a village called Thornmere?"

Something flickered in the captain's face. Good. Memory was a weapon too.

"I've stood on one side of a bridge or another my whole miserable life," Brannoc said. "Obeying. Stepping aside. And every single time, somebody burned." He set his feet. "I'm done stepping aside. Come and spend me, if you've got the coin."

Dree's jaw tightened. He raised his hand—

—and the world turned silver.

It came down the valley like weather: a pressure, a light, a vast slow pulse that Brannoc felt in his teeth and his wounds and the marrow of him, rolling out from the mountain ahead where the anchor slept. The river below the bridge lit from within, tin becoming mercury becoming starlight. The fae knights sank to one knee as one, heads bowed, and even the Crown horses screamed and shied.

And Brannoc understood, with a clarity that had nothing to do with thought, what it meant.

*She's there. She's reached it. She's begun.*

Elodie Underhill, hedge-witch, charm-seller, the woman who hid her gift under seven years of careful mediocrity, was at the hinge of the world, and the hinge knew her name.

"Too late, Dree," Brannoc said, and his voice cracked, and he found he didn't care. "Whatever you came to break — she's already holding it."

Then the fae rose from their knees, and the Crown troops leveled their lances, and the bridge that had no name became the only place in two worlds worth dying on, and Brannoc Hale — deserter, coward by his own long accounting, keeper of one burned village and one small stubborn hope — lifted his spear one final time.

He was still standing when the light took them all.
