## 35. What Corvess Cannot Forgive

The anchor chamber smelled of sap and lightning.

General Aldous Corvess stood at the threshold of the great root-hall with his sword drawn and his heart as steady as it had been on the morning of his first commission, thirty-one years ago, when he had sworn before the Altar of the Crown that no magic would pass unchallenged while he drew breath. The oath had cost him much. A wife who stopped asking questions. Sons raised on his silences. Nights spent reading the archives by tallow-light, learning what his grandfather's generation had done in the last war, and why the records grew thin and careful whenever Mirabel was named.

He had never doubted. That was the thing his enemies never understood about him, the thing Tam Vess had spat at him through the bars of a cell in Corvane: *you could learn the truth and still choose the lie.* No. Corvess had learned the truth — or what he believed to be the truth — and had chosen it every day since, because the truth he knew was written in ash. He had seen the border villages after the fae came through. He had carried children out of burning houses in the Gray Marches when he was young enough to still believe carrying them meant something.

The Veil must fall closed. There was no other arithmetic that ended in the living.

And now, here, at the hinge of the world, a hedge-witch from Corvane stood between him and that arithmetic with dirt on her hands and tears on her face, speaking of councils and covenants as though words could hold back a flood.

"You cannot mean it," she was saying. Her voice cracked on *mean*, and he noted, with the cold professional part of himself that never slept, that she was exhausted past sense — swaying on her feet, her palms scored with fresh blood where the anchor had taken its toll. "General. Look at it. Look at what you're pointing your fire at."

He looked. He had expected — the reports had promised — a weapon. A gate. A wound in the world dressed up as a tree.

What he saw was a tree of light, yes, vast beyond reason, its trunk a column of white-gold radiance rising through the chamber's open crown into a sky that belonged to no single season. But it was dying. He could see that too, and it unsettled him more than any menace would have: the great roots torn and blackened at their tips, light leaking from the wounds like blood from a butchered stag, leaves — if they were leaves — falling upward and dissolving before they touched the ground. It did not look like a fortress. It looked like an old soldier at the end of a long retreat, still standing because no one had told it the war was lost.

It changed nothing. He made himself say so, aloud, so his men would hear.

"It changes everything," Elodie Underhill said. "Or it can. If you let it."

"Madam." His voice was quiet. He had learned long ago that quiet carried further than thunder in rooms like this. "I have read every account of the last war that the Crown permitted to survive. I have walked the Marches. I have burned—" he paused, one heartbeat, and pressed past it, "—I have done things in the Veil's defense that would turn your stomach. Do not lecture me on what it costs."

Behind her, the others had arranged themselves without appearing to move: the deserter Hale on his feet by sheer refusal to lie down, gray-faced, one arm bound against his ribs; the exile Rue standing with that terrible fae stillness, hands empty and eyes not; the girl Sera Vane with her father's knife bare in her grip; the scholar Vess, freed, filthy, standing at the witch's shoulder like a conscience she hadn't asked for. Five people against two hundred of the Crown's best. It should have been nothing. It was not nothing, because the tree behind them breathed, and every breath of it moved the air in the chamber like a tide.

"Your men are outside," Corvess said. "They will not enter unless I call them. What happens in this room happens between us. I am offering you that much courtesy because you have earned it — you sealed the tear at the ford, you've bled for this thing already. Lower your hands. Step away from the anchor. And I will burn only the anchor, not the world."

"That's a generous way to say you'll kill half of everything alive."

"The alternative is the other half." He lifted the object he had carried up the pilgrim road at the head of his army: the sun-iron casket, long as a coffin, seamed with sigils his own grandfather had helped design — the last working fragment of the Hollow Lance, the weapon that had broken the Veilwardens a century ago. It hummed against his palms even through his gauntlets, hungry and patient. "You think I came all this way to negotiate. I came to finish what my family started. We were wrong to stop."

*We were wrong to stop.* The words tasted like iron filings. He had said them to himself a thousand times in the dark and they had always steadied him. Tonight, in this room, with the dying light of the anchor falling across the witch's upturned face, they wobbled — just once — like a compass near iron.

Elodie looked at the casket. Then at him. And something in her expression made his hand tighten on the release-seal.

"You're afraid," she said softly. "That's all this is. You're so afraid, and you've carried it so long you've built a cathedral out of it."

"I am not—"

"My mother saw you coming, General. Not you — your kind. Every Veilwarden for a hundred years has died knowing someone with your face would eventually climb this road with fire in his hands. She hid me in a city that hates magic and taught me to be small, and she died anyway, and the Veil kept failing anyway, and here we are. Fear didn't save anyone. It just made sure we were all alone when the end came."

He should have given the order then. Every discipline he owned told him to give the order then — thumb to the seal, the Lance's fragment waking, the anchor unmade, the worlds sealed apart forever behind a wall of dead light. Clean. Final. The arithmetic completed.

Instead he heard himself say: "There is no third path. There has never been a third path. Seal it or open it — those are the only doors out of this room, and I am telling you which door leads to your children living."

"Then show me your door," she said. "Show me what the last war actually looked like. Not the Crown's accounts. The real ones. Can you? Have you ever *seen* it?"

"I have read—"

"Reading isn't seeing." She turned her bleeding palms toward the anchor. "You want to destroy this. Fine. But you will look first. That's my price, General. Not surrender. Just — look. With your own eyes instead of your grandfather's."

Every instinct screamed that it was a trick, a Veilwarden's gambit, a hook of glamour baited with guilt. He was sixty years old; he had survived three purges and a court full of men who smiled like drawn blades precisely because he never took the bait.

He gave the order to his sergeant at the door — *"no one enters"* — and stepped forward alone, and told himself it was tactics. Keep the witch talking. Learn what the anchor could do before breaking it.

She pressed her bloody hands to the lowest root of the tree of light, and said, in a voice that was not entirely her own, grown suddenly deep and layered like a choir heard through a wall:

*"Witness."*

* * *

The chamber went away.

Corvess stood in a green country under a high bright sky, and for one absurd moment he thought the witch had simply conjured a pleasant fiction, some pastoral lie to soften him. Then he smelled smoke on the wind, and the sky in the east began to bruise.

He knew this country. Not this version of it — but its bones. The river ran where the Grayford ran now. The hills had the same shoulders. It was the Marches, a hundred years ago, before the blight, before the spiraling trees, green and whole and *inhabited* — human villages and fae hollows woven together along the riverbanks like threads in one cloth. He watched a human farmer trade bread to a fox-faced fae woman for weather-charms. Watched children — both kinds, indistinguishable except for the shine in the fae ones' eyes — chase each other through the shallows of the ford.

*This is the lie,* he told himself. *This is what they want you to believe it was.*

Then the sky tore.

Not metaphorically. He saw the Veil itself — a shimmer in the air like heat over a forge, running along the borderlands — and he saw where it ripped, and he understood with a lurching, physical nausea that the rip had not come from Mirabel. It had come from the human side. From an army. From *his* army — the banners were unmistakable, the crowned tower of Corvane, the same banner hanging in his own command tent a mile down the road. The last kings of the old line, terrified of a power they could not tax or conscript, marching on the Veilwardens' anchor with the Hollow Lance, demanding the boundary be sealed and all passage ended — and when the Veilwardens refused, when the fae courts refused, when the anchor itself resisted—

The Lance had struck the tree.

Corvess stood in the vision and felt the blow land in his own chest. The scream of the anchor was not a sound; it was a *removal*, a hole punched through the world's music, and everything downstream of it began to die. The green country browned in a season. The river silted. The fox-faced woman stood in her ruined hollow holding a weather-charm that no longer worked, looking at the sky as though waiting for it to apologize.

And the war everyone remembered — the fae invasion, the burning villages, the children carried from the flames — came *after*. Came *because*. Starved and maddened by the wounded Veil, cut off from their own realm's nourishment, bands of fae broke through the failing boundary into a world that had wounded them, and the human kingdoms met them with fire, and the histories were written by the survivors, and the survivors wrote themselves righteous.

He saw his ancestor — his grandfather's father, tall and stern in the same face Corvess shaved every morning — standing over the struck anchor with the Lance still smoking in his grip, and Corvess saw the man's expression, and it was not triumph.

It was horror. The horror of a man who has done a terrible thing for defensible reasons and has just realized the reasons were wrong. The man knelt in the anchor's dying light and pressed his hands to the wounded root, exactly as the witch had, and the vision gave Corvess his words, faint as breath:

*"I only wanted us safe. God forgive me — I only wanted us safe."*

The vision held him there. Made him watch the blight spread. Made him stand in the Gray Marches as it became the Gray Marches — the spiraled trees, the backward birds, the thin places where things leaked through and had to be put down by soldiers like the young Aldous Corvess, soldiers who never asked why the border was sick, because asking was heresy and the Crown had rewritten the question a hundred years ago.

Made him see, finally, the shape of his own life laid over his ancestor's like a tracing: the same fear, the same resolve, the same arithmetic, the same ash — and at the end of it, if he struck this tree, the same kneeling. The same words. Spoken by him, in another hundred years, to some descendant he would never meet, in a vision like this one.

*I only wanted us safe.*

The chamber returned.

* * *

He was on his knees. He did not remember falling. His sword lay on the stone beside him, and his hands — bare; he had stripped the gauntlets off somewhere inside the vision, trying to feel the real air — were flat against the floor as though bracing against a ship's roll.

The sun-iron casket stood where he'd left it, and Elodie Underhill stood between him and it, swaying, white-lipped, blood running freely from both palms now onto the roots of the anchor. The cost, then. She had paid something real to show him. He understood costs. It was the only language he had ever truly spoken.

"Kill it," she whispered. "If you still can. But you'll do it knowing. That's all I wanted. That's all my mother ever wanted anyone to know."

For a long moment the only sound was the anchor breathing, and his own breath, ragged, and far away the wind moving through an army that was waiting for an order that would not come.

Corvess looked at his hands. A soldier's hands. His grandfather's hands. Hands that had carried children from burning houses and later signed the orders that set the houses burning — and had never once, in sixty years, asked which came first, the fire or the fear.

"There were children," he said. His voice came out wrecked, unrecognizable to himself. "In the vision. At the ford. Human children and—" He stopped. Started again. "My whole life. My whole *life* has been guarding the door against the people my family locked out."

"Yes," Elodie said gently. Gently, which was somehow worse than hatred. "And now the door's falling down either way, General. The only question left is whether we build it again together, or whether we kneel in the ruins alone."

He reached for the casket. She flinched. Brannoc Hale lurched forward. Rue's stillness finally broke.

But Corvess only lifted the lid, took out the Lance's fragment — humming, eager, ancient hunger in a length of black iron — and drove it down against the stone floor with all his strength, and all his grief, and all sixty years of a life built on a corrected history. Once. Twice. On the third blow it shrieked like a living thing and came apart into slag and dust that smoked faintly, smelling of the last war, smelling of endings.

The silence afterward was enormous.

"I cannot forgive myself," Corvess said at last, still kneeling, not looking at any of them. "Understand that. Whatever comes next — whatever council you build, whatever peace you sign — I will carry what my family did until I die, and there is no rite, no oath, no absolution in either of your worlds that lifts it. That is not yours to offer and not mine to receive."

"No," Elodie agreed quietly. "But it might be yours to *use*. Get up, General. Your army is outside, and Lady Ashvine's knights are coming down the pilgrim road, and in a few hours there are going to be a great many frightened people at this door who trust exactly one voice among them. Yours."

He rose. It took longer than it should have. When he turned, the witch was watching him with an expression he had last seen on his wife's face, thirty years ago, on the day she finally stopped asking questions and started waiting for him to answer ones he couldn't.

Outside, horns sounded — not the Crown's calls, but a thinner, stranger music, silver and wrong, rolling down the valley from the north. Ashvine's vanguard. And beneath it, distantly, the drums of his own army answering, confused, leaderless, awaiting word.

Corvess picked up his sword, sheathed it, and walked to the chamber door to speak to his men — knowing that whatever he said next would decide whether they knelt in the ruins together, or alone.
