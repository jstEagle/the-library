## 37. Terms of the Council

Tam Vess had spent eleven years arguing that Mirabel existed, and three days discovering that proving a thing's existence was the easy part. Deciding what it *owed* you — or what you owed it — was the work of a lifetime.

The Concord was being negotiated in the long gallery of the anchor-hold, a chamber that had not been built so much as grown: walls of pale living wood, windows of amber resin through which the light of two skies mingled. The anchor tree itself stood beyond the eastern wall, visible through a great pane of woven glass, its branches still scarred from the siege, its roots still knitting where Elodie had poured out nearly everything she had to save them. Tam could not look at it for long. Every time he did, he thought of her lying in the grass afterward, breathing so shallowly the healers had argued over whether she was breathing at all.

He sat at the human side of the table, third seat from the end, with ink-stained fingers and a sheaf of drafts that had been rewritten so many times the paper had gone soft as cloth. Across from him, in a chair that seemed to have grown up around her like a thorned embrace, sat Lady Ashvine, Regent of the Hollow Crown.

She had conceded. That was the phrase everyone used, in whispers, as though saying it too loudly might undo it. Ashvine had conceded. But Tam, who had spent his career reading maps and the men who made them, understood that concession was a language with many dialects, and the regent spoke all of them fluently.

"Article Nine," she said now, her voice like water moving over stones in the dark. "You propose that passage between the worlds be open at four gates. I count three on your draft."

"The fourth gate is under discussion," Tam said carefully.

"It has been under discussion for two days." Ashvine's smile was a beautiful, terrible thing. "You mean the Gray Marches gate. The one your General Corvess burned his own soldiers to reach."

"He is not my general," Tam said. "And he is no one's general now."

That much, at least, was settled. Corvess sat remanded in the anchor-hold's deepest cell — not fae imprisonment, which would have been an insult the human kingdoms could never forgive, but held under guard of his own deserters and Sera Vane's cold-eyed oversight until his king could be sent for. Tam had seen him once, through the door grate, sitting perfectly straight on a stone bench with his hands folded, waiting with the patience of a man who believed history would eventually apologize to him. It had been the most frightening thing Tam had witnessed during the entire siege, and he had witnessed a bridge hold against forty fae knights.

"Then let us speak plainly," Ashvine said, "as plainly as your kind ever manages. You want four gates because four gates make the Veil a door rather than a wall, and a door can be forced. Three gates, each garrisoned by both courts, each answerable to the council — that is a door with locks."

"Locks are what started the last war," said Brannoc Hale.

He sat at the far end of the human side, arm still bound to his chest, a man who had been told by three different healers that he should be dead and had taken it as a personal affront. He rarely spoke in these sessions. When he did, everyone listened, because Brannoc had burned a village on Corvess's orders and had never once pretended otherwise, and there was a weight to a man who carried his sins openly.

Ashvine turned her gaze on him. Up close, in honest light, she was less terrifying than she was *old* — old in the way mountains were old, with the same patient indifference to the concerns of things that lived briefly.

"You were a soldier," she said. "You know what open gates mean. Your people breed like sparks from a fire. Ours do not. A century of free passage and Mirabel drowns in humanity; our forests become your farmland; our seasons become your weather." She spread her hands, rings glittering. "I am not offering generosity, cartographer. I am offering survival. There is a difference, and your side has never once acknowledged it."

"Your side burned the Marches," Sera said flatly, from her place beside Tam.

"Your side broke the Veilwardens," Ashvine returned, without heat, without mercy. "Shall we spend the afternoon trading atrocities? I have a longer list. I have had longer to write it."

"*Enough.*"

The word came from the head of the table, and the gallery went quiet the way a forest goes quiet — not silent, but listening. Rue had spoken perhaps six times in three days of negotiation, and every time, the room rearranged itself around his voice. He sat between the two delegations, neither side nor arbiter, dressed in traveling gray with his exile's brand still visible at his throat, and when he rose, even Ashvine's thorned chair seemed to lean back from him.

"We are doing it again," Rue said. "Both sides. Counting wounds like coins. I did this thirty years ago, Lady, when I stood before your predecessor and asked for open gates. I argued isolation was death. I was right, and I was branded for it, and I learned nothing, because I argued it as a grievance instead of a covenant." He laid one hand flat on the table, on the space between the drafts. "A gate is not a wound in a wall. It is a promise kept in both directions. If either side keeps a list, the promise dies. So. No lists. Not today. Today we decide only this: how many doors, and who holds the keys."

Tam looked down at his draft and found, to his private astonishment, that his hands were shaking. Not from fear. From something adjacent to hope, which was worse, because hope had betrayed him before — in the archives of the royal library, in the tribunal that had stripped his name, in eleven years of being right about Mirabel and punished for it anyway.

*Four gates,* he wrote in the margin. *Three garrisoned. The fourth —*

The fourth was the problem. The fourth was always going to be the problem.

* * *

They broke at dusk, if dusk could be said to exist in a realm where seasons were stacked like rooms and the light outside the amber windows came from a sun that set in the east. Tam took his drafts to the gallery's western end, where a balcony overlooked the anchor tree, and found Elodie already there.

She was wrapped in a blanket that made her look smaller than she was, watching the tree with the expression of a woman visiting a grave. Since the binding, since she had poured her inheritance into the anchor and woken mortal, she had been — Tam searched for the word and disliked all of them. Quiet. Faded. Present in every session, required by the terms of her own covenant, and somehow absent from all of them, a ghost attending the disposition of her own estate.

"The fourth gate," she said, without turning. "It's the Marches, isn't it. That's why they're fighting about it."

"The Gray Marches, yes." Tam leaned on the balustrade beside her. "Corvess massed his army there. Ashvine's knights died there. The ground itself is blighted — the Veil tore there first, and everything that grew after grew wrong. Neither side wants to build a door on a scar."

"Maybe that's exactly why it should go there." Elodie's voice was flat, tired. "Scars remember. Put the gate where everyone can see what happens when the Veil fails. Make them walk past it every time they cross."

Tam stared at her. Then he laughed — a short, disbelieving sound — and pulled out his drafts. "Say that again. Slower. I want to write it down and put your name on it."

"Don't." She finally turned, and her eyes were the old eyes, the ones that had lied to witch-finders across a market stall for seven years, sharp and wary and alive. "I'm not a signatory anymore, Tam. I gave that up. I'm just — the anchor's witness. Whatever that means."

"It means," said a voice behind them, "that you see truly, and the rest of us are obliged to pretend we don't find it insufferable."

Rue stepped onto the balcony with the unhurried grace of a man who had waited decades and could therefore wait minutes. Behind him came Sera, arms crossed, and behind *her*, moving stiffly, Brannoc — who had promised three separate healers he would rest and had broken the promise within the hour, as everyone had known he would.

"I heard the argument from the corridor," Rue said. "The Marches gate. It's elegant. It's also a trap."

"How?" Sera demanded.

"Because the Marches are mine." Rue's mouth curved, humorless. "My treason happened there. My trial. My branding. If the fourth gate opens on the ground where I fell, then every fae who walks through it walks over my name, and every human who walks through it walks into a story the court tells about traitors. Ashvine will concede the gate eventually — she'll dress the concession in a hundred courtesies — and then she will spend fifty years making certain nothing ever crosses it, and blame the blight."

"So we heal the blight first," Elodie said.

"With what?" Rue asked gently. "The Veilwardens are gone. The anchor holds, but it does not *reach*. That was always the price of the old system — one bloodline, one wellspring, and when the bloodline broke, the land around the break went bad forever." He paused, and Tam watched him arrive at the thought the way a man watches another man step off a cliff edge into darkness, trusting there is a floor. "Unless."

"Unless what?" Brannoc said.

"Unless the covenant works the way she designed it to." Rue looked at Elodie. "You bound the Veil to a council. Living hands, plural. The old magic answered one bloodline. Maybe the new magic answers many. Maybe the Marches aren't waiting for a Veilwarden, little witness. Maybe they're waiting for all of us."

The silence on the balcony stretched long enough that Tam could hear the anchor tree creaking as it grew, which it apparently did continuously, which he had decided not to think about too hard.

"That," said Sera, "is either the most hopeful thing anyone has said in three days, or the most insane."

"In my experience," said Brannoc, "those are usually the same thing."

* * *

The session resumed at what the fae called the turning of the second light and the humans called, with some irritation, "whenever Rue decides it's morning." Tam stood at the table with a fresh draft, and when the argument over Article Nine reignited — Ashvine's three gates, the delegation's four — he did what he had spent eleven years learning to do and one week learning to do honestly.

He told the truth.

"Lady Ashvine. You believe free passage will drown Mirabel. General Corvess believed open contact would extinguish humanity. Both of you built your politics on the same fear wearing different faces, and both of you marched armies to the anchor to make your fear into law." He turned the draft toward her. "Here is Article Nine as we propose it. Four gates. The fourth at the Gray Marches. And here is the clause no one has yet read, because I finished writing it an hour ago, standing on a balcony, taking dictation from a woman who claims she has no power left."

Ashvine read. Her expression did not change, which Tam had learned meant it had changed enormously.

*"The Marches gate shall open only when the blighted land surrounding it is restored,"* she recited, softly. *"Restoration shall be undertaken jointly, by labor and craft of both realms, under supervision of the Council of the Veil, and shall not be deemed complete until the Council's appointed witnesses affirm the land lives again."*

"Joint labor," said Sera. "Human farmers who know how to coax grain from bad soil. Fae gardeners who can teach a spiral-grown oak to grow straight. Years of it. Decades, maybe."

"And while they work," Tam said, "they will live in the same camps, eat at the same fires, bury their dead in the same ground. You cannot restore land together for twenty years and maintain a list of atrocities. The work itself becomes the treaty." He met Ashvine's ancient gaze. "You wanted a lock. This is better. This is a lock that has to be *earned*, by both sides, in the open, where no one can quietly rust it shut."

For a long moment the regent said nothing. The thorned chair creaked around her like a settling animal. Tam thought of all the histories he had read of treaties signed in triumph, sealed in a day, broken in a season, and understood that what was happening in this room was the opposite of that — slow, graceless, argued over line by line, the way peace actually got made when it got made at all.

"You are asking my people to kneel in dirt," Ashvine said at last, "beside the descendants of the men who hunted theirs."

"I am asking both peoples to kneel in the same dirt," Tam said. "Yes."

Something moved behind the regent's eyes — grief, maybe, or its fossil. Tam knew, from Rue, the shape of what she feared: not war but extinction, a realm fading season by season, a crown gone hollow because there was no one left to fill it. Terror wore courtesy in Mirabel the way it wore uniforms in Corvane.

"Three conditions," Ashvine said.

The room leaned in.

"One. The council seats equally — seven of ours, seven of yours, and the witness, who votes only to break ties, and whose vote, once cast, binds both sides absolutely." She glanced toward the balcony doors, where Elodie had re-entered unnoticed, blanket traded for a plain coat, and Tam saw the regent register her presence and choose, deliberately, to continue as though she hadn't. "Two. The Marches restoration begins within a year, or the clause voids and the gate closes permanently. I will not fund a perpetual promise. Three." She paused, and for the first time in three days, Lady Ashvine looked tired. "When the gate opens, I walk through it first. Into your world. Before any of my court. If my people are to kneel in your dirt, they will watch their regent do it first."

Silence. Then Brannoc, of all people, huffed something that was almost a laugh. "That's not a condition, my lady. That's leadership. We'd forgotten what it looked like."

"Accepted," said Rue, before anyone could object, in the tone of a man closing a door on a century of arguments. "Witnessed. Write it, cartographer."

Tam wrote it. His pen scratched through the soft paper, and around him the delegations bent over their copies, and the hard unglamorous machinery of peace began, clause by clause, to turn.

* * *

It took nine more hours. Tam remembered almost none of them clearly — only fragments, later, the way he remembered storms: Sera arguing border law with a fae herald twice her age and winning; Brannoc silently redrawing the gate diagrams because the fae draftsman kept making human figures too small; Elodie, called upon once to break a deadlock over custody of Corvess, saying only, "Send him to his king. Let him explain himself. That's a heavier sentence than anything we could pass," and the tie breaking like ice in spring.

Corvess, remanded, was taken out at the second light's turning. Tam watched from the gallery window as the witch-finder general crossed the courtyard between ranks of his own deserters, spine straight, chin high, a man rehearsing the speech he would give his king. At the gate he stopped, turned, and looked directly up at the window — directly, Tam was certain, at him — and inclined his head a single degree. Not surrender. *Deferral.* The look of a man filing the whole affair away for revision.

"He'll be back," Sera said beside him. "Men like him don't break. They wait."

"Perhaps," Tam said. "But men like him also need an army, and his army watched him order a charge into the anchor's light and saw the light refuse to burn them. You can't revise that. Some truths get into the blood." He hoped it was true. He wrote it down anyway, in the margin of the final draft, because a cartographer's habit was to record what he feared as carefully as what he knew.

By the time the last page was initialed — Ashvine's signature a curl of silvered script that seemed to move slightly when no one watched; Rue's a single unadorned stroke; Sera signing for the human company with her father's name written beneath her own, *courier's daughter, witness* — the mixed light through the amber windows had gone gold, then rose, then the deep blue of a Mirabel night full of unfamiliar stars.

Seven articles. Four gates. One council. One restoration that would outlive everyone in the room.

Tam set down his pen and found that his hands, which had shaken that morning, were steady now. Eleven years ago he had stood in a tribunal and been asked to recant the existence of Mirabel, and he had refused, and it had cost him everything, and he had told himself for a decade that the truth was worth it. He had never expected the truth to require *paperwork.*

"Cartographer." Ashvine paused at the door, the last to leave, her thorned chair already releasing her with a sound like a sigh. "Eleven years ago, your king's scholars declared you a heretic for drawing maps of a country they insisted did not exist."

"They did."

"And tomorrow," the regent said, "your king will receive a copy of the Concord, bearing the seal of the Hollow Crown, naming you its principal drafter." Something flickered across the ancient face — not quite a smile, but the memory of one. "I do hope your king enjoys being wrong. Mine never has."

She swept out. Tam stood alone in the growing chamber among the scattered drafts, the candle stubs, the cups of fae wine no one had dared finish, and let out a breath he felt he had been holding for eleven years.

Then he noticed the letter.

It lay on the table where no letter had been — a single sheet of heavy cream paper, sealed with wax the color of the anchor tree's light, addressed in a hand he did not recognize and yet somehow did:

*Tam Vess, drafter of the Concord — from a friend of the old Veilwardens. Come alone. There is a matter of the witness's price that the covenant does not mention, and she should hear it from someone who loved the last one.*

Outside, in the blue Mirabel night, the anchor tree creaked as it grew, and somewhere beneath its roots, Tam was suddenly very afraid, Elodie's borrowed years were counting down.
