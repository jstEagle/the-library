## 38. Homecoming

The grave was dug by hand, because Sera wanted it that way.

She had argued with the fae about it first — or rather, she had stood before the two Mirabel gardeners who offered to sing her father a resting place in an afternoon, roots parting the earth like water, and she had said no. Not unkindly. But firmly, the way she'd learned to say everything since the ford, since the anchor, since the world changed shape and refused to change back.

"He was a courier," she told them. "He carried things. He walked. If he's going into the ground, I'll be the one to put him there."

The gardeners had bowed — fae courtesy, elegant even in defeat — and lent her spades instead.

So on the third morning after the Concord was signed, Sera Vane knelt in the loam of a hillside above the city of Mirabel and dug her father's grave with her own two hands, while the seasons stacked themselves politely around her: spring on one slope, autumn gold on another, winter's hush at the crest where the light came down thin and silver through trees that had never known a human axe.

Aldous Vane had wanted to be buried at sea. She knew that. He'd said it once, drunk on cheap wine in a rented room in Corvane, laughing: *When I go, tip me over the side of something. Let me keep traveling.* But there was no sea here, and there would be no traveling now, and Sera had spent enough of her life chasing his ghost across half a continent to know that some wants you honor by doing them differently. He had carried the map toward Mirabel for twenty years. He had died three hundred miles short of it.

She would bring him the rest of the way herself.

* * *

They came, all of them, though she hadn't asked.

Tam Vess arrived first, of course — Tam was always first when there was work to be done and last when there was feeling to be felt — carrying a bundle of documents under one arm and looking, as always, like a man who had been personally offended by sleep. He stood at the edge of the grave site with his hands full of parchment and said nothing for a long while, which from Tam was practically a eulogy.

"I've been reading his letters," Tam said finally. "The ones you decoded. The courier logs."

"I know."

"Twenty years." Tam shook his head slowly. "Twenty years he kept it moving. Never twice along the same road. Never the same name twice. He outlasted three witch-finder generals, two purges, and an entire decade in which every other Veilwarden artifact on the continent was burned." He paused. "I wrote my heresy treatise about a map I thought was destroyed. Your father *was* the reason it survived. And I called him a thief."

"You didn't know."

"No," Tam said. "I didn't know. That's not the same as innocence, but it isn't guilt either." He set the documents down carefully on a flat stone. "I've drafted his rehabilitation. Under the Concord, the Crown has to hear it. Corvess's own records prove what he did — the man kept receipts for his atrocities, the pompous bastard. Aldous Vane will die in the histories as what he was. A protector."

Sera looked at the papers, and then at Tam, and found that her throat had closed around something too large for words. In the end all she managed was, "Thank you," and Tam nodded once, briskly, and turned away to fuss with the documents again, because that was how Tam Vess said *this matters to me more than anything I have ever written.*

Brannoc came next, moving slowly, favoring the side where the fae knight's blade had opened him at the bridge. He shouldn't have been walking yet — the Mirabel healers had said weeks, and Brannoc Hale had never in his life obeyed an instruction he could walk around — but he came up the hill with a shovel over his shoulder like a man reporting for duty, and when Sera opened her mouth to tell him to rest, he just looked at her, and she closed it again.

Some men you thank by letting them work.

Elodie came last, and quietest, wearing plain clothes and no circlet and none of the light that had poured out of her in the anchor chamber — just Elodie Underhill, charm-seller, hedge-witch's runaway, mortal again and still learning what that meant. She knelt beside Sera in the dirt without being asked and took the second spade, and they dug together in silence for a long time, two women who had met at knifepoint a season ago, turning the dark earth in rhythm.

It was Elodie who spoke first.

"My mother's grave is under a hawthorn outside Corvane," she said. "I haven't visited it in seven years. I couldn't, because visiting meant admitting she was gone, and admitting she was gone meant admitting what I ran from." Her hands kept moving. "I understand now why your father never came home. It wasn't that he stopped loving you. It's that the road he was on didn't have a way back until it was finished, and it never got finished."

"He could have told me," Sera said. The old anger rose, and she let it rise, because she had learned on this road that anger was not the enemy — it was grief wearing armor, and armor could be taken off. "Twenty years. One letter. One honest letter."

"He sent forty," Elodie said gently. "You told me. Coded. Because if any honest letter had reached you, it would have killed you both." She paused, spade resting on the earth's edge. "He spent twenty years choosing between protecting you and being known by you. He chose wrong, maybe. Or maybe there was no right choice, and he just — carried it. Like the map."

Sera drove the spade into the soil harder than she needed to. "And now he's dead and I'm burying him in a country he never saw, and the only reason I know who he was is because strangers read his mail."

"Not strangers," Elodie said. "Not anymore."

Sera stopped. Looked at her. At the woman she had hunted across the Gray Marches with a knife and a grudge, whom she had bound and threatened and betrayed and been forgiven by, whose blood had sealed a tear in the world and then given nearly all of itself to bind it forever.

"No," Sera said finally. "Not anymore."

* * *

They buried Aldous Vane at noon, when the light came slantwise through the stacked seasons and turned the whole hillside the color of old honey.

There was no priest. There was no rite, exactly — or rather, there were four rites, overlapping, from four people who each brought their own way of saying goodbye.

Tam read the rehabilitation decree aloud, formally, in the flat cadence of royal record: *Aldous Vane, courier of the Veilwarden line, faithful servant of the covenant between worlds, cleared of all charges, restored to honor, entered into the rolls of both courts.* It was, Tam noted afterward, the first document in human history countersigned by a fae regent and a human king's envoy simultaneously. Ashvine had signed it without hesitation. Even Corvess, broken and remanded home, had scrawled his mark at the bottom — the last thing he ever put his name to as a free officer of the Crown.

Rue spoke next, and Sera had not expected that. The exile stood at the grave's edge in his travel-worn coat, looking older than his centuries, and said: "Your father passed me on the road once. Years ago. Neither of us knew the other." His voice was quiet. "He was walking toward a country I had been cast out of, carrying the thing that could open its door. I was walking away from it, carrying nothing but my grievance. We were both couriers, in the end. Him of the map. Me of the hope that someone would come behind me and do better." He bowed — a real bow, deep, the first Sera had ever seen him give a human. "He did better. Rest, courier. The road is finished."

Then Brannoc. He didn't speak much — Brannoc never spoke much — but he took off the soldier's badge he'd carried for fifteen years, the one from the regiment that had ordered him to burn a village of hidden magic-users, the one he'd worn ever since as penance and proof, and he laid it in the grave on top of the folded courier's satchel.

"For the roads you walked alone," he said. "Nobody should carry that weight without relief coming."

Sera broke, then. Just a little. Just enough that Elodie's hand found hers and held it, and she let it, and the ground took her father gently, the way ground does when it is dug by hands that loved him.

Last, Elodie sang. Not a spell — she was mostly empty of those now, and besides, this wasn't magic. It was a hedge-witch's burial song, the kind taught to apprentices in kitchens, the kind sung over animals and strangers and anyone who died without family near. Simple words. A tune like a door closing softly.

Sera listened, and understood, at last, what gratitude felt like when it finished becoming grief: not lighter. Just held. Carried, now, by many hands.

* * *

Afterward, they sat on the hillside and ate bread and hard cheese and a jar of Mirabel honey that tasted, according to Rue, of "the summer of a year that never actually happened, which is the best kind," and Sera made her announcement the way she made all her decisions now: plainly, and without asking permission.

"I'm staying."

Tam looked up mid-chew. "Staying. Here. In Mirabel."

"As the human council's first envoy. They need someone. Someone who knows both worlds' paperwork, and both worlds' tempers." She glanced at Tam. "I was going to ask if you'd recommend me, but then I remembered you're constitutionally incapable of recommending anyone for anything you could claim credit for yourself, so consider yourself preemptively declined."

"That's—" Tam started.

"Accurate," said Brannoc.

"It's *accurate*," Tam conceded, wounded, "but it's also a mistake, because you'd be excellent and I'd be redundant, and I have never in my life been redundant—"

"Which is why," Sera interrupted, "you're going back to Corvane to run the archive of the new Concord. Both courts need a cartographer of the Veil who can't be bought, intimidated, or flattered. That's you. Congratulations. You're essential. Now stop making that face."

Elodie laughed — really laughed, the first time Sera had heard it sound easy — and Rue raised the honey jar in salute. "An envoy, a scholar, a soldier, and a witch," he mused. "It sounds like the beginning of a very bad ballad."

"It sounds," Sera said, "like a council."

* * *

Brannoc stayed on in Mirabel longer than any of them expected — long enough for the healers to stop scolding him, which took a month, and long enough after that for something else to happen, something slower.

It began with a patch of ground behind the lodging house where the company had settled during the negotiations. It was bare, weedy, trampled — the kind of ground soldiers know how to ignore. Brannoc stared at it for three days. On the fourth day he borrowed a hoe.

"What are you doing?" Sera asked, watching him turn the soil with the careful, deliberate movements of a man relearning what his body could do.

"Don't know yet," he admitted.

He planted anyway. Root vegetables first, because soldiers trust root vegetables. Then beans. Then, hesitantly, at the row's end, a handful of seeds Tam had procured from somewhere — flowers, of all things, small blue ones that grew in Mirabel's stacked springs and bloomed out of season, out of spite, out of joy.

The fae noticed. Of course they noticed; the fae notice everything. Children came first, peering over the wall at the human digging in the dirt, fascinated by the sheer strangeness of a man planting things he might not live to eat. Then their parents. Then an elderly gardener who had tended Mirabel's orchards for six hundred years and who stood at the fence one morning, watching Brannoc work, and finally said:

"In six hundred years, no human has planted anything here."

"I know," Brannoc said, not looking up. "Figured somebody ought to start."

The gardener was silent a moment. Then he climbed over the fence, picked up the spare hoe leaning against the wall, and began to weed.

By the time the first envoys arrived from the human kingdoms — nervous men and women clutching treaties like shields — the garden behind the lodging house was the most famous thing in Mirabel's lower quarter. Not the palace, not the anchor tree glowing on the horizon like a second dawn. A garden. A human soldier and a fae gardener, weeding side by side, arguing companionably about drainage in two languages neither fully spoke.

Sera watched it happen from her study window and understood that this, too, was statecraft. Maybe the truest kind.

* * *

On the eve of her departure — Elodie and Tam leaving for Corvane within the week, Brannoc staying on through the growing season — the four of them gathered in the garden at dusk, when Mirabel's stacked light went gold and long, and nobody talked about politics or treaties or the war that hadn't happened.

They talked about ordinary things. Tam complained about the Concord's indexing conventions. Elodie described, with relish, the shop she intended to open — "small, badly stocked, honestly labeled" — and the sign she'd already designed in her head. Brannoc reported on the beans with the gravity of a field commander. Rue, sprawled against the fence with his eyes closed, listened to all of it with an expression Sera had never seen on him before and finally identified, privately, as peace.

"We'll come back," Elodie said, at some point, to no one in particular. "For the harvest. For the anniversaries. For no reason at all, once the road's open and there doesn't need to be a reason."

"The road's already open," Rue murmured. "That was rather the point of everything."

"Yes," Sera said. "But roads only stay open if people walk them."

She looked around at them — the scholar, the witch, the soldier, the exile — these people who had been strangers and enemies and fellow fugitives and were now, by every measure that mattered, her family. Her father had spent twenty years keeping two worlds apart so they wouldn't destroy each other. She would spend whatever years she had helping them learn to live together. It seemed a fair inheritance. Better than fair. It was, she thought, the first inheritance in her family's history that didn't come wrapped in grief.

Well. Wrapped in grief, yes. But not *made* of it anymore.

* * *

It was late — the candles low, the garden fragrant with night-blooming things that existed nowhere else — when the messenger came.

A fae runner, breathless, which fae runners almost never were, standing at the garden gate with a letter sealed in green wax bearing a mark Sera didn't recognize: not Ashvine's sigil, not the council's, not any heraldry of either court.

"A rider brought this to the border gate," the runner said. "From the far side. From the human kingdoms. It names you, Lady Envoy. It says—" and here the runner hesitated, and Sera felt the old cold settle into her stomach, the courier's-daughter cold, the *something-is-wrong* cold—

"It says there are others. Other maps. Other anchors. And that someone in the human kingdoms has just learned their locations — and is gathering people to find them."

Sera read the letter twice by candlelight. Then she stepped out into the garden, where her family sat in the gold-and-green dusk, and held it up.

"Everyone," she said quietly. "We need to talk."
