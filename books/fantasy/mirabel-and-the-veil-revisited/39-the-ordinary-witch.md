## 39. The Ordinary Witch

The road into Corvane smelled the same as it always had: coal smoke and river mud and the sour-sweet rot of apples left too long in the market carts. Elodie stood at the top of the Wethering Hill with her pack on her shoulder and her hood down — her hood *down*, that was the first strangeness — and let the city look back at her.

Seven years ago she had crept through these gates with her collar up and her gift folded small beneath her ribs like a stolen thing. She had spent seven years being careful. Careful in the way she stirred a charm-broth so it looked like ordinary soup. Careful in the way she never met a witch-finder's eyes, never lingered near the pyre-notice boards, never let her hands do anything lovely when someone might be watching. She had built a life out of mediocrity the way other people built houses out of brick, one deliberate dullness at a time.

Now there was a notice board at the gate, and she stopped to read it because she could.

*By order of His Majesty's Court of Inquiry,* it began, in the same heavy black hand that had once signed death warrants, *the question of licensed magic within the city bounds shall be heard publicly at the Assize Hall through the winter sessions. All persons of craft are invited to petition for charter.*

*Invited.* The word sat strangely on the page, like a cat wearing a hat. Below it, someone had pinned a cheaper notice, hand-inked, slightly crooked: *CHARTERED CHARMERS' GUILD — FIRST MEETING, THE DROWNED BELL, MIDDAY TUESDAY. ALL CRAFTS WELCOME. NO ONE WILL BE BURNED.*

Someone had crossed out the last sentence and rewritten it twice, as if they couldn't quite believe it either.

Elodie read it three times. Then she laughed — a short, startled sound, half disbelief — and pulled her pack higher and walked down into the city with the wind at her back and nothing hidden anywhere in her body for the first time since she was nineteen years old.

It felt less like freedom than she had imagined. It felt more like taking off a boot you'd forgotten you were wearing, and discovering your foot had changed shape underneath.

* * *

Corvane had not forgiven itself yet. That was plain in the streets. The scorch marks on the Cheesemonger's Row posts had been scrubbed but not erased; the stone remembered. A woman selling roasted chestnuts outside the Assize Hall had hung a little bundle of rowan twigs from her cart handle, openly, brazenly, the way one might hang a ribbon for a festival — and beside her, a man in good wool glared at it as he passed, and his glare had fear in it still, and the chestnut woman saw the fear and lifted her chin, and both of them went on living in the same morning.

Elodie walked slowly. She had money now — not much, but honest, most of it earned mending charms along the pilgrim road on the long way home, and some of it given by Sera, who had pressed a purse into her hands at the parting and said, "You're terrible at accepting help, so I'm not asking," which was the most Sera farewell ever spoken.

She found a room above a cooper's shop on Tanner's Lane, the same street where she'd lived before, two doors down from her old stairwell. She stood in front of that stairwell for a long moment on her first evening, looking up at the crooked door where a bleeding stranger had died with a word on his lips and a map in his hands.

*Mirabel.*

She touched the doorframe. It was just wood. Just a door. Whatever the map had wanted from her, it had finished wanting. She had woken every morning since the binding half-expecting ink to crawl across her skin again, half-dreading and half-grieving it, and every morning there was only skin, only freckles, only the ordinary pulse of an ordinary woman.

"You're staring at my door," said a voice, and the cooper's wife — a broad, flour-dusted woman named Hesta whom Elodie did not know but who apparently knew everyone — was standing in the lane with her arms full of kindling. "People used to die in stairwells all over this city during the bad years. You've got the look of someone paying respects."

"Something like that," Elodie said.

Hesta squinted at her. "You're the charm-witch off the pilgrim road. My sister wrote to me about you — says you fixed her cow's milk-fever for two coppers and wouldn't take more." She shifted the kindling to her hip. "They say the Assize is going to grant charters by spring. Real ones. You going to apply?"

"I don't know," Elodie said honestly.

"Hm." Hesta considered her the way one considers a horse of uncertain temperament. "Well. If you do, my husband's shop needs its cellar blessed against damp, and he'll pay proper rates, and he won't cross himself while you work, which is more than you'd get from half this street." She thrust a stubby finger toward the empty shopfront at the end of the lane, its windows papered over. "That place is cheap. Landlord's desperate. Bad luck attached, he says."

"What kind of bad luck?"

"The kind that's only bad luck until someone moves in and changes it." Hesta smiled, and it was a shrewd smile, and Elodie felt something in her chest turn over — the old, dangerous, wonderful feeling of being *seen* and not flinching.

"I'll look at it tomorrow," she said.

* * *

She looked at it the next day, and the day after that she signed for it, and the day after that she stood in the middle of the dusty little room with light coming through the papered windows like light through bandages, and wondered what exactly she thought she was doing.

The shop was narrow and deep, with a counter of scarred pine and a cellar that did, indeed, need blessing against damp. There was a back room with a hearth and a hook for a cauldron, and stairs up to a loft where she could sleep, and a door that opened onto the lane, and — this was the part that made her stand very still for a long time — no back door. No second exit. No escape route.

Every place she had lived for seven years, she had chosen for its exits. She had slept lightly, packed ready, kept her boots by the bed and her savings sewn into her coat lining. She had been a woman shaped entirely by the shape of her running.

She rented it anyway. And when the landlord asked why she didn't want the back room's window barred, she said, "Because if I ever need to run out of here, I'll have made worse mistakes than a window," and the landlord looked at her oddly, and she laughed, and it was true, and it set her free in some small private way she couldn't have explained to anyone.

* * *

Opening a charm-shop turned out to be much harder than hiding a gift ever had.

Hiding, she had discovered, required only discipline. Craft — real craft, done openly, priced fairly, guaranteed to work — required skill, inventory, bookkeeping, nerve, and a tolerance for failure that seven years of careful mediocrity had left underdeveloped.

Her first week, she sold four items. One was a luck-charm to a nervous bride whose mother bought it for her; one was a damp-charm for the cooper's cellar, which worked beautifully and paid for a month's rent; one was a wart-charm that did nothing whatsoever, because warts, it turned out, answered to patience rather than magic, a fact she learned from Tam's letters, which arrived weekly in a handwriting like spiderwebs drawn by a scholar having opinions.

*Tam's letter that week:* *You cannot charm a wart. Warts are the body's own stubbornness made visible. Recommend honesty with the customer; scholars find honesty sells surprisingly well once people stop expecting to be cheated. Also, your ledger is wrong on page three. Also, I miss arguing with you. Also, Brannoc has planted cabbages. This is not a metaphor. He will explain it is not a metaphor at length.*

Elodie wrote back: *Fix my ledger then, if you're so clever. The shop is called Underhill's. I named it after myself, which felt enormous and took me three days. How does one price hope?*

Tam's reply came four days later: *Badly. Price it low and sell a great deal of it. P.S. Underhill is an excellent name. It sounds like somewhere the rain goes to think.*

She laughed alone in her shop at midnight over that one, and then sat for a while with her hands around a cup of tea, feeling the strange new weight of a life with no one hunting it.

* * *

The Assize Hall in midwinter was colder inside than out, all stone and echo and the breath of a hundred petitioners hanging in the air like fog. Elodie went on a grey Tuesday, mostly because Hesta bullied her into it ("If you don't claim a charter, someone worse will claim the trade, and then we'll all be buying love-potions made of pond water and lies"), and stood in a queue of people she would once have crossed the street to avoid being seen near.

There was a miller with a weather-sense he'd hidden for forty years. There were twin sisters who sang to bees. There was a boy of maybe fifteen who could make bread rise with a touch and who kept looking at the witch-finder guards by the wall with the particular blankness of someone who had learned young what eyes must not show.

The guards bothered her. Not their presence — their presence was almost gentle now, bored men leaning on halberds, assigned to a proceeding that had begun to feel ceremonial. What bothered her was the boy's blankness, because she recognized it. She had worn that face for seven years. She knew how heavy it got.

When her name was called — *Underhill, Elodie, hedge-craft, Corvane district* — she walked to the rail and gave her account to a panel of three: a magistrate, a priest, and, to her lasting astonishment, a grey-robed woman with a Veilwarden knot embroidered at her shoulder, one of the new council's liaisons, sent down from the Concord courts to observe.

The questions were simple. What craft did she hold? Where trained? Had she harmed any person with it?

"No," she said. "Never. That was rather the whole difficulty. I spent years making sure I did nothing at all."

The priest leaned forward. "And now? Now that it is lawful? What will you do with it?"

It was the right question, and it deserved better than the answer she'd prepared. Elodie looked at the panel, and at the boy in the queue behind her watching her with his careful blank face, and she heard herself say:

"Sell luck. Honestly. Which means telling people that luck-charms nudge, they don't command. Mend what's mendable. Teach what I know to whoever the council sends me, so the next generation doesn't have to learn it alone in stairwells." She paused. "And keep doing nothing at all whenever nothing is what's needed. That's a craft too. I'm very good at it. Seven years of practice."

Something flickered across the liaison's face — recognition, maybe, or memory of her own — and the magistrate stamped the paper, and that was that. Charter granted. Her name in a ledger, lawful, public, findable by anyone.

Walking out, she realized her hands were shaking, and that it wasn't fear. It was the opposite of fear, whatever that was. She decided not to name it. Some things spoiled when you named them.

* * *

Winter deepened. The shop found its feet the way a foal does — badly, repeatedly, then suddenly all at once.

She learned her trade's true shape. Luck-charms sold best on Mondays, when the week still seemed possible. Love-charms she refused to make, and said so loudly enough that refusing became her reputation, and her reputation brought her customers who wanted truth instead of flattery, which was a smaller crowd but a better one. She charmed a fisherman's nets against rot. She uncharmed a rival's spite-work from a baker's oven, her first real piece of craft-mending, and stood in the baker's kitchen afterward sweating and triumphant while the man wept over his loaves like they were children come home.

She was, by every measure, a mediocre witch. Her charms held for a season, not a lifetime. Her wards kept out damp and cats and most ill-wishing, but not, she suspected, anything truly determined. She overcharged no one and undercharged herself constantly and kept the ledger wrong on page three out of what she eventually admitted was superstition.

And she was happy. That was the astonishing part. Not the blazing happiness of the anchor chamber, of the oath and the light and the world remade — that had been happiness the way lightning is light. This was the other kind, the banked kind, the kind that lives in a warm room while rain works at the windows. She had spent so long believing that her magic and her safety could never live in the same house that she had never once imagined them sharing a kitchen, arguing mildly about whether the tea was stewed.

Some nights she still woke reaching for the map's heat under her ribs, and found nothing, and lay in the dark grieving a burden she'd have sworn on any altar she never wanted. Grief, she was learning, did not check whether its object deserved it.

On those nights she lit a candle and wrote letters. To Sera, in Mirabel, who wrote back about court politics with a soldier's contempt and a diplomat's growing skill, and signed her letters *your colleague, unfortunately*. To Brannoc, who wrote rarely, briefly, and entirely about cabbages, except for one line buried in each letter that was never about cabbages at all — *the garden keeps. So do I.* To Rue, who had appointed himself the open road's first ferryman and wrote in a hand so beautiful it was clearly showing off, describing the travelers he guided between worlds: human pilgrims gawping at stacked seasons, fae children pressing their noses to the Veil's edge like children at a sweetshop window, an old woman from the Gray Marches who walked through every year on the same day to leave flowers on the far side.

*They ask me,* Rue wrote, *why the road is open, who won, what it cost. I tell them a woman chose a third path because both offered paths were cowardice wearing armor. They never believe me. You should come see their faces when I introduce you.*

*I'm busy,* she wrote back. *I have a shop.*

*Yes,* he replied. *I know. It is the most Mirabel thing about you, that you saved two worlds and consider the shop the greater achievement.*

*It is,* she wrote. *Anyone can save the world. Try pricing hope.*

* * *

In the spring, the boy from the Assize queue came to her door.

He stood in the lane for a quarter hour, she learned later, working up the courage, and when he finally knocked he spoke so fast his words tripped over each other. His name was Pip. He could make bread rise. His mother was frightened of him — not of the gift, of what the neighbors might do, of what the neighbors *had done*, last autumn, before everything changed, when a mob was still a reasonable thing to expect. He'd heard there was a charter-witch on Tanner's Lane who taught.

"I don't teach," Elodie began, and then stopped, because the boy's face did the thing her own face had done at nineteen, the closing-down, the shutter going up, the careful blankness sliding into place like a mask being fitted. She had seen that expression in mirrors. She had promised a panel of strangers she would make sure no one learned it alone in stairwells.

She stepped back from the door. "Come in, Pip. First lesson's free. The lesson is tea, and the lesson is this: your gift is not a secret you keep. It's a fact you own. Those are different things, and the difference is the whole of everything I know."

He came in. He came back the next week, and the week after, and by midsummer there were three of them, then five, sitting around her back-room hearth on evenings while she taught them what Mistress Ivenna had taught her, and what Ivenna had failed to teach her, which was that inheritance is not a debt you pay alone.

She thought about Ivenna sometimes, more gently now. The journals were safe in her loft, wrapped in oilcloth, and she had not reread them since the anchor. She didn't need to. She understood them differently now — a frightened woman, hiding a child's birthright to keep the child alive, getting the arithmetic of love wrong in the way frightened people do. Elodie had cursed her for it on the road. She wasn't sure she'd been wrong to curse her. But she had stopped needing to be right about it, which was, she suspected, what forgiveness actually was: not a verdict, just the setting down of a case you no longer wished to prosecute.

* * *

On the first anniversary of the Concord, Corvane held a festival nobody had planned. It simply happened, the way spring happens — stalls in the square, lanterns on the river, the chestnut woman's cart draped in rowan and hawthorn, and in the Assize Hall a public reading of the Concord's terms, attended by hundreds who came less for the law than for the miracle of hearing it aloud without anyone being burned.

Elodie closed the shop early and went, and stood in the crowd, and listened to a clerk read words she had bled to make possible, recited in a bored administrative drone that made her want to laugh and weep in equal measure. *Limited passage under council law. Joint adjudication. Neither realm shall arm the Veil.* It sounded like a lease agreement. Two worlds, saved by paperwork. Tam would have loved it. Tam probably had loved it; Tam had likely drafted half the clauses and argued about the font.

Afterward she walked home along the river in the blue dusk, past lanterns, past children with sparklers they were legally permitted to hold, past a fae trader — an actual fae trader, tall and silver-eyed, selling glass flowers from a stall on the embankment while human children stared and their parents pretended to disapprove and bought anyway.

The trader caught her eye. Looked at her a moment longer than politeness required. Then inclined his head, very slightly, the way one greets a person one has heard described.

Elodie nodded back and kept walking, heart beating oddly, and told herself it meant nothing, and knew it didn't mean nothing, and let it mean whatever it meant.

At her door, she paused. The shop windows glowed warm behind her, the sign — *UNDERHILL'S, CHARMER, CHARTERED* — catching the last lantern-light. Inside, on the counter, the ledger lay open to page three, still wrong, still hers. Upstairs, Ivenna's journals waited in their oilcloth. In Mirabel, Sera was drafting an envoy's speech. In a garden outside a border town, Brannoc's cabbages were, by his own sworn testimony, not a metaphor. On the open road between worlds, Rue was showing off his handwriting to pilgrims.

She had spent seven years building a life designed to be abandoned. She had spent one year building one designed to be kept.

Elodie Underhill unlocked her door, went in, and lit the lamps — all of them, wastefully, gloriously, where anyone could see.

* * *

The knock came at dawn, three days later, when the shop was still dark and the river fog hadn't lifted.

She knew the knock before she reached the door — the particular patient rhythm of it, knuckle and edge of the palm, a traveler's knock, a road-knock. She had heard it at a hundred inns in a hundred towns, and her body remembered it the way it remembered old stairwells.

She opened the door on grey light and fog, and on the figure standing in it, travel-stained, hood pushed back, with a sealed letter in one hand bearing the council's knot in green wax.

"Elodie," said the visitor, and the fog seemed to lean closer to listen. "The anchor is singing. And it's asking for you — not as its warden." A pause, and a smile she couldn't yet read. "As its witness. We need you to walk the road again. All of us. Will you come?"

Elodie stood in her doorway, in her shopkeeper's apron, with her ordinary life warm at her back — and heard, beneath the fog and the river and the waking city, faint and unmistakable, something that might have been the far-off sound of a tree of light remembering her name.

She reached for her coat.
