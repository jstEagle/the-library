## 40. Mirabel and the Veil, Revisited

The summons came on a morning in late autumn, when Corvane's rooftops wore their first frost like a borrowed coat of silver.

Elodie was in the back of her shop, grinding dried rowan berries for a sleep-charm that would work only slightly better than a strong cup of tea, when she felt it: a warmth at her wrist, old and familiar as a hand closing around hers. She set down the mortar and pushed back her sleeve.

There, on the inside of her forearm, where the binding had left its mark seven years ago — a thin silver line, faded almost to nothing — the sigil had woken. It glowed softly, patiently, the way a lamp glows in a window for someone expected home.

"Well," she said aloud, to the empty room and the rowan dust and the cat asleep in the window box. "So it's time."

The cat opened one eye, considered the matter, and went back to sleep. Cats, at least, had not changed since the Concord.

She closed the shop early — hung the sign that read *Gone a few days. Charms still honest while I'm away* — and packed the way she had learned to pack in her years of running: lightly, quickly, with everything that mattered in one bag. But this time, standing in the doorway with the key in the lock, she noticed what was different. There was no fear in it. No looking over her shoulder for witch-finders, no rehearsed lies for the gate guards. Just a woman locking up her own shop before a journey, which was such an ordinary thing to do that it made her laugh out loud on the street, startling a fishmonger's boy.

Seven years ago she had fled this city with a dead man's map burning against her ribs and three strangers she did not trust. Now she walked out through the South Gate past a guard who nodded to her by name, because everyone knew the charm-seller who had been at the Anchor, and because half the guard bought their sweethearts' tokens from her stall.

The road between worlds began, as it always began, at the Gray Marches — though they were gray no longer.

* * *

Elodie remembered the Marches as they had been: blighted, spiraled, wrong. Trees grown in corkscrews, birds flying backward, the air tasting of copper and held breath. It had been the place where the Veil showed its wounds, the borderland where two worlds pressed against each other like badly mended skin.

Now she stood at the edge of it and breathed in, and the air smelled of rain and apples.

The trees grew straight here now — mostly. Here and there one still carried a gentle spiral in its grain, the way a healed bone carries the memory of its breaking, but the spirals were graceful things now, ornamental rather than agonized. The path wound through orchards that belonged to no kingdom and every kingdom, planted in the first years after the Concord by hands from both sides of the Veil. Human farmers and fae gardeners had argued cheerfully about grafting techniques for three seasons running, and the result was fruit that ripened in both worlds' light and tasted, according to Tam, faintly of whatever you missed most.

A waystation stood where the first tear had been — the small one, the one Elodie had sealed with her blood before she understood what she was. It was a shrine now, modest and well-tended, with a stone basin for offerings and a roof of living willow. Someone had left bread and honey and a sprig of rowan. Someone else had left a fae lantern, its glow soft as moth-wings, meant to burn until its bearer returned.

Elodie touched the basin's rim. The silver line on her wrist warmed, gently, like a dog leaning into a familiar hand.

*I'm not yours anymore,* she reminded it, the way she did every time. *I'm just visiting.*

But the Veil had never been very good at letting go of its wardens, even retired ones. And that, she had come to understand, was not a flaw. That was the whole point.

She walked on, and near dusk, at the ford where Brannoc had once held a bridge alone against nine fae knights, she heard voices ahead and knew them before she saw them.

* * *

Tam Vess had grown a beard, which suited him the way a footnote suits a poem — technically correct, slightly distracting. He sat on the flat rock in the middle of the ford sketching the water, because Tam could no more pass interesting geography without mapping it than he could pass a heresy without publishing it. His satchel, as always, contained more instruments than food.

"You're late," he said, without looking up.

"I'm precisely on time. You're early. You've been early to everything since the Concord."

"Because lateness is a choice, Elodie, and I refuse to make choices I can't defend in writing." He looked up then, and his face did the thing it always did — the gruff mask slipping just enough to show the gladness underneath. "You look well."

"I look old."

"We all look old. It's the finest thing we've ever done." He snapped his sketchbook shut. "Rue's already through. He said he'd meet us at the second milestone, which means he's actually waiting at the fourth and pretending he wasn't."

They crossed the ford together, boots in the cold water, and on the far bank Sera Vane rose from where she'd been sitting with her back against a milestone, sharpening a knife that had never once needed sharpening. Some habits outlive their reasons.

Sera had been the human envoy to Mirabel for five years now, and it had changed her the way a river changes a stone — slowly, thoroughly, into something smoother and harder both. She wore fae travel-clothes cut to human practicality and a courier's badge on a chain around her neck: her father's badge, restored posthumously, its dishonor struck from the rolls in ink that Tam had personally mixed.

"Underhill," she said.

"Vane." Elodie embraced her, because after seven years neither of them pretended anymore that they didn't mean it. Over Sera's shoulder, Elodie saw the milestone, and the words carved fresh into it — *MIRABEL, ONE DAY'S WALK, ALL TRAVELERS WELCOME* — and felt the old ache of wonder, worn smooth now into something like contentment.

"Your father's letters are in the archive at the anchor now," Sera said, pulling back. "All of them. Tam catalogued them last spring."

"I know. I visited them." Elodie smiled. "He was a better man than the Crown ever deserved."

"He was a stubborn man who carried things that weren't his," Sera said. "I've decided that runs in families." Her eyes flicked, brief and dry, to Elodie's wrist, where the silver line still glowed faintly beneath the sleeve. "Speaking of which. You felt it too, then. The summons."

"All of us did, I expect." Elodie looked down the road, toward the deepening violet of evening and the place where the light bent in ways light shouldn't. "That's what worries me. The anchor doesn't call its witnesses for nothing. And it hasn't called all four of us together since—"

"Since the binding," Tam finished quietly.

They stood a moment in the orchard-smelling dusk, three middle-aged people who had once changed the shape of two worlds, and none of them said the thought aloud: *the anchor is failing again.* They didn't need to. They had all done the arithmetic on the walk in.

Then a voice like poured honey came out of the twilight behind them, amused and exasperated in equal measure:

"If the three of you intend to stand there catastrophizing in perfect silence, I shall be forced to wait at the sixth milestone instead, and then where will we all be?"

* * *

Rue had not aged a day, because fae don't, and he had long ago stopped apologizing for it. He wore exile's black still — he claimed it was simply excellent fashion now that it was legal — and his beauty remained the dangerous kind, the kind that made you check your pockets afterward. But the hunger in it had gentled over the years, the way a river gentles when it finally reaches the sea it has always been running toward. Rue went home to Mirabel whenever he wished now. He had a house there, with a garden, and he complained about the gardening to anyone who would listen, which was how everyone knew he was happy.

"You felt it," Elodie said, when greetings were done.

"I felt it." For once, he didn't make it pretty. "Three nights ago. In my garden, of all places — I was arguing with a rosebush, and the mark woke, and the whole garden went quiet. Not dead-quiet. Listening-quiet." His fingers found the sigil on his own wrist, twin to hers, one of the first pledges signed at the anchor. "It has never called us *all*. Not once in seven years."

"It called the council," Tam said. "Not just us. Ashvine's people will have felt it. The human signatories too. Corvess—" He paused on the name, as everyone still paused on the name. "Corvess is in the king's custody in the south. But his daughter holds his seat on the council now, and she'll have felt it."

"Then we walk faster," Sera said, shouldering her pack, "and argue slower."

They walked. And as the stars came out — stars that belonged to both worlds at once, here, where the sky couldn't decide and had stopped trying — Elodie found herself falling into step beside Brannoc Hale, who had appeared out of the dark the way he always did, silent as a man who had spent years being hunted and never entirely unlearned it.

He was grayer now. broader through the shoulders, slower to smile and surer when he did. His garden in the Marches foothills was famous among travelers; people said the vegetables tasted of safety. He had planted it the spring after he recovered from the bridge, on the advice of a healer who told him that men who had burned villages needed to learn to grow things, and Brannoc had taken the prescription so seriously that he now supplied half the waystations on the road between worlds.

"You felt it too," Elodie said. It wasn't a question.

"Two nights back. Middle of harvesting turnips." He shrugged. "Dropped the basket. Stood there like a fool with dirt on my hands, listening."

"To what?"

"That's the thing." He was quiet for a few steps. "It didn't feel like danger. Every other time the anchor's spoken, it's been like a wound talking. This was different. This was—" He frowned, hunting the word with the same care he gave to everything. "Like someone clearing their throat before a toast."

Elodie laughed despite herself, and the laugh loosened something in her chest that had been tightening for three days. "A toast."

"Aye. Which is either very good news or very strange news, and with that tree it's usually both."

The anchor was, and had always been, a living tree of light at the hinge of the world — and living things, Elodie had learned, were never simple. Living things grew. Living things changed. And living things, occasionally, surprised you.

They walked through the night and the next day, four companions and one fae moving through country that belonged to both worlds and neither, past farms where human children chased fae lanterns and fae children lost arguments to human dogs, past shrines tended jointly, past a market straddling a stream that ran through two skies at once. And everywhere, woven through everything, Elodie felt the Veil — not as a wall, not as a wound, but as a fabric. Something held. Something held by many hands.

That had been her third path, seven years ago, chosen in the anchor chamber with Ashvine's blade on one side and Corvess's weapon on the other: not to seal the Veil forever, not to let it stand open and trust to luck, but to bind it to a living council of both worlds. To make its keeping a covenant instead of a bloodline. She had signed first, spending nearly everything she had, waking mortal and ordinary and hollow. Rue had signed second. Sera third. Then Ashvine, to her own visible astonishment. Then forty others in that first year, and hundreds since — human and fae, farmer and scholar and soldier, each of them carrying a thread of the keeping, each of them mortal or long-lived but none of them alone.

Her own power had gone into that first signature. What remained in her was small: enough to warm a charm, enough to hear the Veil when it spoke, enough to be called. A witness, the council had named her, in the formal language of the Concord. Not its warden. Its witness.

She had spent seven years learning that witness was not a consolation prize.

* * *

They reached the anchor at moonrise.

The valley had changed, as everything had changed. Once it had been a hidden place at the end of a pilgrim road, guarded by secrecy and desperation. Now it was open — terraced gardens climbing the slopes, halls of human stone and fae-grown wood sharing the same roofs, and at the center, in a ring of white pillars that had been raised by hands from both worlds working side by side, the tree.

It was taller now. That was the first thing. Seven years ago it had been a dying thing, beautiful and failing, its roots torn, its light guttering. Now it rose higher than the pillars, higher than the hills seemed to permit, and its light was steady — not the desperate flare of something about to go out, but the deep, patient radiance of something established. Leaves of light drifted from its branches and dissolved before touching ground, and where they dissolved, the grass grew greener.

And the tree was full of people.

That was the second thing, and it stopped Elodie mid-stride. The great roots spread wide across the valley floor, and among those roots, in niches and alcoves and seats grown from the living wood itself, sat figures — dozens of them, scores — each one dimly luminous, each one wearing the mark. Signatories. Wardens of the covenant, gathered from both worlds, and they were not merely sitting. They were *attending*, the way a choir attends a soloist, and the tree's light moved among them like music moving through voices.

"They're holding it," Tam breathed beside her. "All of them. All at once. Look at the weave — it's not one thread anymore, it's a *tapestry*—"

"I can see it, Tam."

"Why didn't anyone tell us? We should have been notified. There should have been correspondence. I *catalogue* the correspondence—"

"Maybe," Rue said softly, and for once there was no irony in him at all, "it wanted us to see it ourselves."

Ashvine was there.

The regent of Mirabel stood apart from the others, beneath the eastern pillars, and she had aged — fae age, glacially, but visibly now, a fine silvering in her hair, a new depth carved around her eyes. Terror had marched her armies once. Time had done what terror could not: it had made her a gardener of the thing she'd feared. She turned as they approached, and inclined her head — not the poisoned courtesy of the Hollow Court, but the plain respect of one keeper to another.

"You felt the summons," she said. It was not a question either. Everyone in this valley had felt the summons.

"What is it?" Elodie asked. "What's happening to it?"

"Nothing is happening *to* it," Ashvine said, and there — there it was, the thing in her voice that Brannoc must have heard from miles away, the cleared throat before the toast. "That is what none of us understand yet. Come. It's been waiting for you specifically. It has been very insistent, and I do not enjoy being insisted upon by a tree, however magnificent."

They walked together — the five of them, and Ashvine, and a scattering of other signatories drawn by the same pull — up the gentle rise to the tree's heart, where the great trunk split into living arches and the light pooled like standing water. And there, in the oldest alcove, grown into the wood itself, Elodie saw what the summons was for.

A new seat. A thirteenth root-throne, freshly formed, its wood still pale and unfinished, waiting.

Above it, written in drifting motes of light that assembled and reassembled like breath on cold glass, hung words in no language and every language, and Elodie read them not with her eyes but with the faded silver line on her wrist, which had begun to burn like a brand:

*A NEW HAND SEEKS THE COVENANT.*

*IT IS NOT HUMAN. IT IS NOT FAE.*

*IT IS THE VEIL ITSELF, LEARNING TO HOLD ITS OWN WEIGHT.*

*BUT IT CANNOT SIGN ALONE. NO ONE SIGNS ALONE. THAT IS THE LAW YOU MADE.*

*BRING YOUR WITNESSES. TEACH IT WHAT YOU KNOW.*

*THE THIRD PATH MUST NOW LEARN TO WALK WITHOUT ITS MAKERS.*

Elodie stood a long time in the light, reading it again and again, feeling the truth of it settle into her bones the way the map's inheritance had once settled — unwelcome, enormous, and unmistakably hers to answer. The covenant they had built was growing up. The Veil, bound to so many living hands for so long, had learned from them. Learned to reach toward keeping itself. And like any child on the edge of its majority, it needed witnesses — the ones who had made the law — to stand beside it when it took its first steps.

Behind her, she heard Brannoc exhale slowly. Heard Tam already muttering about precedent and documentation. Heard Rue say, with wondering laughter caught in his throat, "*It wants to join us.*" Heard Sera say nothing at all, because Sera never wasted words on things that were obvious.

Elodie stepped forward to the unfinished throne and laid her hand on the pale wood, and the silver line on her wrist flared bright as a wedding band, and all around the valley the gathered keepers lifted their heads as one.

"Well then," she said, to the tree, to the covenant, to the five people who had become her family on the worst road of her life. "Let's teach it everything we know. We'd best begin with the most important lesson."

Brannoc raised an eyebrow. "Which is?"

She smiled — the smile of a woman who had once sold false luck-charms in a city that feared her, and who had finally, after everything, stopped hiding.

"That no one signs alone."
