## 1. The Charm Seller's Winter

The trick to being nobody, Jonah Fairweather had learned, was to do it thoroughly.

Not half-nobody, not mostly-nobody — those were the men the Ministry found. Half-nobodies kept a lucky coin in their pocket and told themselves it meant nothing. Mostly-nobodies flinched when a crier mentioned the word *gifted*, and flinching was a signature as legible as any name written in a register.

Jonah had spent twelve years becoming a complete and perfect nobody, and he had gotten so good at it that some mornings he woke up and had to lie still in bed for a full minute, counting his own heartbeat, before he could remember who he was pretending to be.

His name in Greyhollow was Jonah Fairweather. That much was true, or true enough. A man could keep one true thing about himself like a coal banked under ash.

Everything else was craft.

* * *

His shop opened at dawn because dawn was when the honest poor came buying, and because the Ministry inspectors never rose before the second bell. It was a narrow slot of a place wedged between a chandler's and a shop that sold nothing but buttons, on Cordwainer Lane, where the fog came off the harbor thick enough to chew. The sign above the door read FAIRWEATHER'S CHARMS & CURIOSITIES in paint that had been allowed to fade exactly to the point of shabbiness. Too fresh a sign invited questions. So did too ruined one.

Inside, the shelves were arranged with the precision of an apology.

Luck-knots hung from the ceiling beams in braided rows — red cord, three loops, tied left over right over left. They were beautiful. He made them beautifully, because ugly luck-knots would have been suspicious, and they were completely, deliberately useless. A knot held no more fortune than a shoelace. He knew this the way a locksmith knows the inside of a safe: from the making. There was no working in them, no whisper of intent, no thread of the old language pulled taut through the braid. Just cord, and patience, and the human appetite for believing that something small might hold back something large.

The fever-charms sat in a glass case by the till: chips of river-stone wrapped in copper wire, each one blessed by no one, charged with nothing, warmed only by his hands. Mothers bought them for sick children at two coppers apiece, and Jonah always threw in a third charm free, and told them what every wise-woman in the city told them: keep the child warm, keep the water boiled, and let the charm do the rest.

The charm did nothing. The warm and the boiled did everything. But the mothers needed something to hold in the long dark hours of a fever, something that felt like action, and Jonah had decided long ago that a harmless lie shaped like a stone was kinder than most truths available on Cordwainer Lane.

It had taken him four years to learn how to make magic that wasn't.

That was the part no one understood about hiding. Anyone could bury a gift. Burying it was easy; you simply stopped. What nearly killed him was learning to counterfeit its absence — to build a whole trade out of the shape of power without the substance, so that if an inspector ever ran his thumb along a luck-knot hunting for the hum of worked intent, he'd find only cord and a craftsman's pride. His counterfeits were his camouflage. Every useless charm he sold was another brick in the wall between Jonah Fairweather and the storm.

And beneath the floorboards, beneath the false bottom of the till, beneath twelve years of careful smallness, the real thing slept.

He felt it the way a man feels a bad tooth — not always, but always somewhere. On still days it was quiet. On days when thunder walked the hills beyond the city walls, it turned over in its sleep, and Jonah would stand at his counter with a smile fixed on his face and sweat sliding down his spine, and tie knots, and be nobody, nobody, nobody.

* * *

The first customer of the day was Mistress Ibbet, the chandler's widow, who came in for her monthly luck-knot and stayed, as she always did, to deliver the news.

"They say the king's been strange," she said, watching him braid. "Strange even for kings."

"Kings are strange by profession, mistress."

"You know what I mean." She lowered her voice the way people in Greyhollow lowered their voices whenever the palace came up, which was often, because fear and gossip shared a root. "My sister's boy works in the kitchens up there. Says His Majesty asked a serving girl her name three times in one dinner. Same girl. Same question. And then there's the edicts coming down from the Ministry — new ones every week now, all signed with the king's own seal, and half of them don't read like anything Aldric ever wanted. Curfews. Registries." She paused. "Lists."

Jonah's hands did not pause on the braid. Twelve years of practice lived in his hands; they could tie a luck-knot through an earthquake with a corpse at his feet and never show it. But something behind his ribs went very still, the way a hare goes still when the hawk's shadow crosses the grass.

"Lists of what?" he asked, mild as milk.

"Oh, you know how it is. Tax delinquents. Debtors. Dissidents." She waved a hand. "The usual categories grow."

"The usual categories always grow," Jonah said. "That's what makes them usual." He finished the knot, bit the cord clean, and held it out. "There. Fortune's yours."

"And worth every copper," she said, and paid him four instead of three, and left.

Jonah stood alone in his shop with the luck-knots swaying faintly overhead in the draft from the door, dozens of small red lies turning on their strings, and told himself that lists were just lists. That Greyhollow was the largest city in the realm and the easiest place in it to be small. That the Ministry cared about smugglers and seditionists and hedge-mages selling real workings in the Warrens, not about a tired man on Cordwainer Lane whose charms wouldn't cure a headache.

He was almost able to believe it. Then the second bell rang, and the knock came at his door, and it was not a customer's knock.

* * *

Ministry inspections followed a liturgy, and Inspector Hask performed it with the flat devotion of a man who had done ten thousand of them and would do ten thousand more.

There were two of them. Hask in his dove-grey coat with the Order's brass eye pinned at the collar, and a younger one — Vess, by his name-tag — who carried the instrument case and had the particular hungry stillness of a dog that has not yet been taught what it is allowed to want.

"Routine," Hask said, already inside, because routine meant he didn't need permission. "Cordwainer Lane, quarterly sweep. You know the form, Fairweather."

"I know the form," Jonah said, and fetched it.

While Hask filled out the header in his small dry hand — name, trade, years resident, next of kin (Jonah wrote *none*, and had written *none* for twelve years, and the word had worn a groove in him) — the young one, Vess, drifted along the shelves with the instrument case under his arm.

The instrument was called a quench-needle, though it looked like nothing so much as a long silver knitting needle with a drop of black glass at the tip. Held near worked magic, the glass clouded. Near dead matter, it stayed clear. Every licensed charm-seller in Greyhollow got the needle run across their stock twice a year, and every year Jonah watched the black glass pass over his life's work and stay clear as ice, clear as a winter sky, clear as the eye of a storm that has decided, for now, not to look at you.

Vess was slow about it. Slower than the ritual required. He lifted each luck-knot on its string and passed the needle along the braid, and watched the glass, and watched it not cloud, and Jonah understood — with the weary clarity of long experience — that the boy was disappointed. New inspectors always were. They wanted the needle to sing. They'd joined the Ministry of Order because somewhere in their childhoods someone had frightened them with a story about the gifted, and they wanted, badly, for the world to justify the fear.

"Clear," Vess said, on the fortieth knot, sounding like a man reading a eulogy.

"They're cord," Jonah said pleasantly. "Cord runs clear."

"Mm." Vess moved to the fever-charm case, and here he did something no inspector had done in six years: he set down the needle and picked up a charm with his bare hand.

Jonah's heart performed a single hard strike against his ribs and settled.

"River-stone," Vess said, turning it. "Copper wire. Where's the stone from?"

"Graywater shallows. South bend."

"You go collect it yourself?"

"I buy from a tides-man. Cheaper than the walk."

"Huh." The boy weighed the charm in his palm, and Jonah watched him hunt for something to find, because finding things was how a young inspector became an old inspector with a pension and a reputation. "You know what I heard, Mr. Fairweather? I heard there was a woman up in Kettle Row last month. Sold charms same as you. Nice little shop. And the needle said clear, clear, clear, right up until the night her neighbor saw light coming under her door at midnight. Blue light." He let that sit. "They took her at dawn. She'd been hiding it for eleven years, they say. Eleven years, and the needle never once caught her."

"What happened to her?" Jonah asked. He kept his voice at the temperature of the room.

"She's not in Greyhollow anymore." Vess set the charm down, aligned it precisely with its neighbors, and smiled with nothing in it. "Funny, isn't it? All that work to seem ordinary. Seems like a lot of effort, just to stay alive."

"It does," Jonah agreed. "But then, breathing's a lot of effort too, and we all keep at it."

Hask looked up from the form. "Fairweather. Years resident?"

"Twelve."

"Trade unchanged?"

"Unchanged."

"Any contact with unregistered persons? Any purchases of reagents above household quantity? Any—" and here Hask recited it in the toneless voice of a man reading words he'd read ten thousand times, "—any awareness of persons displaying gifts of working within your district?"

"No," Jonah said. "No. And no."

Hask stamped the form, collected his copy, and paused at the door with the fog rolling grey behind him. For a moment — half a moment, less — his eyes moved across the shop the way a man reads a page he has read many times, and Jonah felt the inspection tilt on its axis, felt the whole grey machinery of the morning pause and consider him.

Then Hask said, "Quarterly sweep. Next visit after solstice," and the bell above the door gave its small cheap jingle, and they were gone.

* * *

Jonah locked the door. Then he stood with his forehead against the wood and breathed, and counted, the way he did after every inspection. One for the lungs. Two for the heart. Three for the hands, which had not shaken, which had not once in twelve years shaken where anyone could see.

Blue light under a door in Kettle Row. Eleven years of hiding, undone not by a needle but by a neighbor and a sleepless night. That was the arithmetic of his life, and he knew it by heart: survival was not a wall you built once. It was a fire you fed hourly, daily, in a hundred invisible ways — the faded sign, the useless charms, the *none* written in the kinship line, the smile calibrated to the temperature of the room. One cold snap, one careless flame, one blue light under a door, and twelve years burned to nothing in a morning.

He thought about the storm sleeping under his floorboards. He thought about it the way you think about a grave you dug yourself.

On still days he could almost pretend he'd buried it deep enough.

Outside, the fog was lifting off the harbor, and somewhere beyond the city walls, far off, so distant it might have been his imagination, thunder muttered against the hills.

Under the floorboards, something turned over in its sleep.

Jonah went back to tying knots.

* * *

He should have gone to bed. Instead he climbed the stairs to his rooms above the shop and took down the loose brick behind the stove, and took out the oilcloth packet, and unwrapped the single page of his mother's grimoire that he had allowed himself to keep.

One page. He'd burned the rest twelve years ago, the night he crossed into Greyhollow with ash still in his hair — a chapter at a time, feeding them to a tavern fire while the landlord's back was turned, watching weather-craft older than the kingdom curl and go orange and become nothing. He'd kept this one because it was the least dangerous thing in the book: a drawing of his family's mark, the storm-spiral, inked in his mother's own hand, with a note beneath it in her slanted script. *The storm is not in us. We are in the storm.* He'd told himself he kept it as a lesson in what to avoid. He'd told himself a great many things.

He held it now in the lamplight, and the ink looked fresher than it had any right to, and outside the window Greyhollow lay quiet under the first snow of the season, white and soft and law-abiding, a city that believed itself safe because it had outlawed everything it feared.

A city built on the idea that if you made people small enough, they'd stay small.

Jonah wrapped the page away, reset the brick, and stood a moment longer at the window, watching the snow come down on Cordwainer Lane — on the chandler's, on the button shop, on the dark windows of the poor and the careful and the hidden, hundreds of them, thousands, all keeping their fires low.

Somewhere out in all that whiteness, a crier was ringing his bell at the corner of Saltmarket, calling the hour. And then the bell stopped, and the crier's voice changed — dropped its practiced rhythm, took on a new cadence, a proclamation's cadence — and though he was too far off to catch the words, Jonah had lived in Greyhollow long enough to know the difference between an announcement and an edict.

He stood very still in the dark, and the snow fell, and the voice rang out over the rooftops, saying something he could not yet hear.

He would hear it by morning. Everyone would.
