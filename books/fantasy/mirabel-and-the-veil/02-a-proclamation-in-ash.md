## 2. A Proclamation in Ash

The criers came at dawn, and by noon the whole of Greyhollow smelled of ash and wet wool.

Jonah heard the first bell from his stall in Tanners' Row, where he was pretending to sell a luck-knot to a woman who didn't need one and wouldn't get one. The bell was the deep one, the harbor bell, the one they rang only for fires and royal decrees—and Greyhollow hadn't burned in years.

"Edict," said the woman, dropping the knot back on his table like it had bitten her. "Another edict."

"Probably tolls," Jonah said. "King's short on coin."

She left without arguing, which told him she didn't believe it either.

He closed the stall early. That alone felt like a confession—everyone knew charm-sellers worked through lunch, working every hour the light allowed because their margins were thin and their customers thinner—but his hands had started doing things before his mind agreed to them, an old habit from a life he'd buried. When the Ministry knocked, you wanted to be found doing something boring. Boring was armor.

The criers stood at the fountain in Cobbler's Square, three of them in royal grey, their voices pitched to carry over rooftops. A crowd had gathered the way crowds do—loose at the edges, dense at the center, everyone pretending they'd only stopped to rest.

Jonah stopped to rest.

*"By decree of His Majesty King Aldric the Third, Beloved of the Veil,"* the nearest crier read, unrolling a scroll with ceremonial slowness, *"let it be known throughout the city and its holdings: all persons possessing the unsanctioned gift—hereafter named the Taint—are commanded to present themselves at the Ministry of Order within ten days for registration, examination, and disposition."*

Disposition. The word went through the crowd like a fishhook through soft flesh. Jonah watched people flinch around him—a baker's boy, an old widow, a dockhand chewing a splinter—all of them flinching at nothing, at a word, which was somehow worse.

*"Those who register freely,"* the crier continued, *"shall be examined and, if found harmless, released to their lives under watch. Those who conceal themselves shall be hunted. Those who shelter the concealed shall be hunted alongside them. There is no middle road. The king grieves that it has come to this. The king asks your forgiveness even as he asks your obedience."*

The king grieves. The king asks. It was well-written; Jonah could hear the palace scribes in it, the careful cadence, the sorrow folded into the threat like poison into honey. Whoever had drafted this understood something important about fear: that it works best when it sounds reluctant.

The crowd began to murmur, and the murmurs were the wrong kind—not angry, not defiant. *Relieved*, some of them. *Good*, said a man near Jonah, quietly, to no one. *About time someone did something.* A woman beside him nodded. Jonah catalogued their faces out of habit and hated himself for the relief he felt that neither of them had ever looked twice at his stall.

He walked home the long way, past the shrine to the Veil where old women lit tallow candles, past the Ministry annex where clerks were already hanging fresh parchment in the windows. REGISTRATION BEGINS TOMORROW. BRING PROOF OF RESIDENCE. He read it three times, looking for the loophole, the mercy clause, the small print. There wasn't any. There never was.

Twelve years he'd been careful. Twelve years of selling knots that held nothing but string and suggestion, charms that cured fevers because fevers break anyway, luck that arrived because people looked for it harder after paying. Twelve years of being small, being dull, being a man whose hands shook only when no one watched. And in one morning, a scroll and a bell had made all of it worth exactly nothing.

Because here was the thing the criers didn't say, the thing every gifted person in Greyhollow understood in their bones: *examination* meant a Ministry reader laying hands on you and listening for the hum. And Jonah Fairweather didn't hum. He *thundered*. They'd hear him from across the room. They'd hear him from across the street.

Registration was not a door. It was a trap with the bait painted on.

* * *

His neighbor Tam Vetch was watering his herbs when Jonah got back to Candlemaker's Lane.

Tam was sixty, maybe older, with a stoop like a question mark and a garden that shouldn't have survived the salt air but did—feverfew and comfrey and a row of stubborn roses, all of it thriving on hedge-craft so old and so gentle that half the time Tam himself seemed to forget it was magic at all. He touched a wilting leaf and it perked up. He sang off-key to his bees and they stayed through winters that should have killed them. He'd set Jonah's broken wrist once, twelve years ago, the week Jonah arrived, wrapping it with poultices that worked faster than poultices had any right to.

"You heard," Tam said. Not a question. His voice was steady the way a rope is steady while it frays.

"Heard."

"They'll come for me first, you know." Tam kept watering. His hand didn't shake, which impressed Jonah more than anything else that day. "Hedge-healers. We're on every list they've ever kept. Half the lane owes me for birthing babies or burying their dead, and the other half resents it." He smiled, small and crooked. "Either way, I'm known."

"Don't register."

"I'm not going to register, boy. I'm eighty-one years old. I'm not starting a new life in a Ministry cell." He set down the watering can. "But you should think about what you're going to do. You've got more years in you than I do, and whatever it is you're hiding—" he'd never once asked directly, in twelve years; Jonah suddenly loved him for it and for the asking now, both at once, "—it's bigger than mine. Isn't it?"

Jonah said nothing.

"That's what I thought." Tam picked a rosehip, turned it in his fingers. "There's talk, down at the wet docks. Smuggler tunnels. People who've gone under the city. If it comes to it—" he pressed the rosehip into Jonah's palm, absurdly, like a coin, like a blessing, "—you run. Don't be brave. Bravery is for people with nothing left to lose, and you've got a whole life you haven't lived yet. Go live it somewhere they can't read your name."

"I've already run once," Jonah said. "It doesn't take."

"It takes better than staying does." Tam went back to his roses. Conversation over; the old man rationed his words like flour. But as Jonah climbed his stairs, Tam called up softly: "Lock your window tonight. And Jonah—the feverfew on your sill. Take it inside. Frost coming early this year."

Frost coming early. From anyone else, weather-talk. From Tam, a warning shaped like gardening, the last kindness he knew how to give.

They took him that night.

* * *

Jonah woke to boots.

Not many—six, maybe eight, moving with the unhurried coordination of men who had done this before and would do it again before dawn. He lay in his bed in the dark and listened, mapping them: two at Tam's front door, three circling to the garden, the rest holding the alley. No battering ram. No shouting. Tam's door opened with a knock, and Tam answered it dressed, coat on, as though he'd been expecting them since noon. Because he had been.

Through the wall, Jonah heard everything and nothing. Voices, low. A word he couldn't make out. Then Tam's voice, clear and calm and terribly kind: "Mind the roses, lads. They're worth more than I am."

Someone laughed. Someone else didn't.

The boots moved off toward the Ministry annex, and Jonah lay in his bed until the sky went grey, doing absolutely nothing, and the nothing was the hardest work he had ever done. Every nerve he owned screamed at him to go to the window, to go into the street, to call down something with teeth. Twelve years of discipline held. He lay there and let an old man be taken fifty feet away from him, and when dawn finally came he went out and looked at the garden.

The roses were fine. Of course they were. Tam had warded them against frost, against blight, against everything except the thing that had actually happened. Jonah stood among them in the cold and let himself feel it—one clean minute, no more, because grief you indulged got people caught—and then he went inside and made tea and began to plan like a coward, which was the only way he'd ever survived.

By full dark the next evening, the lane had already rewritten Tam out of itself. His shutters were closed. His name was spoken in the lowered voices people use for the dead. The baker's wife, whose twins Tam had delivered, said loudly, to no one, that she'd always thought there was something odd about that garden. The illusion of safety hadn't just died—it had been murdered, and the neighbors were helping bury the body.

Jonah went home and took his mother's grimoire down from behind the loose brick in the chimney.

* * *

It was a small book. That was the joke of it, the cruelty of it—twelve years of terror guarded by a book that fit in one hand. Wren's handwriting filled the first third, round and impatient, copying their mother's spells at fourteen, fifteen, sixteen: cloud-shaping, rain-calling, the storm-lure that had been the pride of the harvest-guard. Their mother's hand filled the rest, tighter, older, the letters leaning like trees in wind. On the last page, in the margin, in ink gone brown: *For my son, who will be greater than me, God keep him from needing to prove it.*

God hadn't kept him from proving it. One night, one storm, one mistake, and the proving had cost everything. He'd carried the book out of the village in his coat, over his heart, because burning it then would have meant admitting Wren was really gone, and he hadn't been ready. He still wasn't. That was the whole truth of the last twelve years: he had never been ready for anything, so he had simply waited, and waiting had felt like living.

He built a small fire in the hearth. Closed the shutters first. Checked them twice.

Then he sat down with the book and a pair of tongs, and he burned it page by page.

Not all at once—he couldn't have explained why, except that it mattered. Each page he held a moment longer than he needed to, reading it like a eulogy. *Cloud-shaping, first form: breathe low, breathe slow.* Wren had drawn a lopsided cloud in the margin with a face on it. He held that page until the corner browned, until the face curled, until there was nothing left to hold. *Rain-calling for the dry fields.* Their mother's note: *Never call what you cannot thank.* He fed that one to the flames and thanked it, absurdly, silently, and watched it go.

The fire ate his family in order: sister first, then mother, then the margin note last of all, the ink flashing bright before it blackened. He stirred the ashes until they were cold. He washed the soot from his hands. He put the tongs away and swept the hearth and sat in the clean, empty quiet of a house where no evidence remained that Jonah Fairweather had ever been anything but a seller of useless knots.

It should have felt like safety. It felt like amputation.

He was still sitting there, in the dark, ash on his sleeves, when the sound came.

A hum. Faint, rhythmic, coming from everywhere and nowhere—from the walls, from the floor, from the space behind his ribs. For one insane moment he thought it was his own storm, rising unbidden from twelve years of grave-silence, and his heart slammed against his sternum.

Then he saw the light. Thin, blue-white, flickering under his door like spilled water.

And beneath the hum—so faint he might have imagined it, might have wanted it, might have been dreaming already—came a second sound. Small feet on his stairs. A breathless, ragged whisper, young and terrified and utterly certain:

"Please. Please, they're coming, please—"

His window exploded inward.
