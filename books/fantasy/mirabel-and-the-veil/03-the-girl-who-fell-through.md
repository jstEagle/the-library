## 3. The Girl Who Fell Through

The city of Greyhollow taught you its rules fast or it ate you. I'd learned them in nine days, which I considered a personal triumph.

Rule one: never sleep in the same place twice. Rule two: the bells that rang at dusk meant run, not pray. Rule three: when the Ministry wagons rolled with their lanterns burning white instead of yellow, you got small and you got gone, because white lanterns meant hunters, and hunters didn't want your coin—they wanted the thing under your skin.

I had a thing under my skin.

It lived on my left wrist, a mark like a lightning bolt caught mid-fall, silver-blue and faintly warm, the way a stone stays warm after sun even on a cold day. I kept it wrapped in strips of linen stolen from a rag cart, and I told anyone who asked—nobody ever asked—that I'd burned myself stealing from a baker's oven. It was a good lie. Lies were the only inheritance I had left.

Nine days in Greyhollow. Nine days since the thing at the crossroads outside Harrowfen had pressed a locket into my hands with fingers like cold candle wax and said, *Carry this to the storm-caller. He'll know what it means.* Nine days since I'd stopped being whatever I was before and started being a courier for a dead woman's errand.

Except she wasn't dead. That was the worst part. The locket hummed against my sternum where I wore it under my shirt, and when I held it too long, I heard her—not words exactly, more like weather. A pressure before rain. A voice trying to remember how voices worked.

*Find him,* the weather said. *Jonah Fairweather.*

So I'd found him. Or found his shop, anyway—a narrow little place wedged between a chandler's and a shop that sold nothing but buttons, with a hand-painted sign reading FAIRWEATHER CHARMS: LUCK-KNOTS, FEVER-WRAPS, SAFE TRAVELS. I'd watched it for two days from rooftops and doorways, learning his rhythms. He opened at dawn. He closed early. He spoke to customers in a flat, careful voice, like a man reading lines someone else had written. And every evening, before he shuttered the windows, he stood in the middle of the shop with his eyes closed and his hands loose at his sides, just breathing, like he was listening for something far away.

He looked wrong for a storm-caller. He looked like a man who'd been folded up small and stored in a drawer for years. Gray already at the temples though he couldn't have been older than five-and-thirty. Careful hands. Eyes that flinched from loud noises.

Tonight I meant to go in. Tonight the timing finally felt right—he'd blown out his lamp an hour ago, and the street was quiet, and I'd counted three patrols passing without stopping.

Then the white lanterns came, and everything went sideways.

* * *

They came down Candlemaker's Row in a wedge formation, six of them, boots striking cobbles in unison so the sound rolled ahead of them like thunder's ugly cousin. I flattened into the gap behind a rain barrel and pulled my knees to my chest and did the breathing Wren—the weather-voice, the woman in the locket, whoever she was—had shown me once, in a dream that wasn't quite a dream. In through the nose. Out slow. Become a stone. Stones don't get hunted.

The patrol stopped four doors down.

"Ministry inspection," said the one in front, a big man with a voice like gravel settling. "Open in the king's name."

There was a long pause. Then a lamp flickered alight behind the charm-shop window, and Jonah Fairweather opened his door wearing a nightshirt and an expression of perfect, practiced boredom.

"At this hour?" he said. "The sign says dawn to dusk, gentlemen."

"The sign says a lot of things." The gravel-voice man pushed past him into the shop. "Edict of Cleansing. Every residence and business subject to search. You'll stand there and keep your hands visible."

I should have run. That was rule zero, the rule underneath all the others: never be near the thing they're hunting. But the locket had gone hot against my chest, hot as a coal, and the weather-voice was pressing against the inside of my skull like a face against a window, and it wasn't saying *run*. It was saying *look*.

So I looked.

Through the gap between barrel and wall, I watched them turn over jars of dried feverfew and bundles of knotted red thread. I watched Jonah stand with his hands out like a man showing he held nothing, which, I supposed, was true. I watched the gravel-voice man pick up a luck-knot, sniff it, and sneer.

"These are garbage," he said.

"They're meant to be comforting," Jonah said mildly. "Comfort is also garbage, if you ask the Ministry."

Something in his voice made the hunter's head come around sharp. For a moment nobody moved. The lamplight lay across Jonah's face, and I saw what I hadn't seen from the rooftops: he wasn't bored. He was terrified. Terrified the way water is wet—totally, structurally, all the way down. He'd built his whole careful life on top of that terror and furnished it nicely and hung curtains over it, but tonight, with a Ministry man close enough to touch, the terror showed through like bones through thin skin.

"You're nervous," the hunter said.

"I'm a shopkeeper being searched by armed men at midnight," Jonah said. "Nervousness is the appropriate response. Would you prefer I laughed?"

The hunter stared at him a beat longer. Then something crashed upstairs—a shelf, maybe—and two of the other hunters whistled their man back down, muttering about the next house, and the wedge formation re-formed and rolled on down the Row, white lanterns swaying.

Jonah stood in his doorway until they turned the corner. Then he went inside, and I heard, very faintly, the sound of a grown man sitting down on the floor.

The locket cooled against my chest. The weather-voice settled, satisfied, like a cat curling up.

*Tonight,* it said. *Go to him tonight.*

"Yeah, well," I whispered to it, "your timing's garbage."

Because here was the problem, the problem I'd been chewing for two days while I watched him from the roofs: the weather-voice wanted Jonah Fairweather, but Jonah Fairweather was a coward. I could see it from a mile off—I knew cowards; I'd been raised among them; I was half one myself. Cowards didn't take in stray girls with dangerous wrists. Cowards called the Ministry themselves, if a stray girl knocked, because turning her in would prove how harmless they were. Cowards survived by being small, and I was the smallest, most dangerous thing that could possibly knock on their door.

But the voice in the locket had carried me nine days through snow and hunger and one genuinely horrifying encounter with a toll bridge, and it had never once been wrong about anything that mattered.

So I climbed the drainpipe at the back of the button shop, walked the eaves like I'd done a hundred times in a hundred towns, and dropped onto the roof of the charm shop, right above the little dormer window with its cracked pane.

And that's when the second patrol came around the corner, and I slipped.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about falling through a window: it sounds enormous from the inside. From my side it was a rush of cold air, a splintering crack, a tangle of curtains, and then floor—hardwood, dust-smelling, lit by one dying banked fire. From his side, I'd learn later, it sounded like the end of the world.

I hit the floor rolling, came up in a crouch with a shard of glass in my fist—it's amazing what the body decides to do without asking you—and found myself face to face with Jonah Fairweather, who had come off the floor where he'd been sitting and was now pressed against the far wall holding a poker like it might apologize for him.

"What—" he said. "What—who—"

"Shut up," I said, because the boots were still on the street below, and the white lantern-light was sliding up the wall opposite the broken window, and every instinct I owned was screaming. "They're coming back. They saw the glass."

"No," Jonah said faintly. "No, no, no—"

"Window's broken, genius. Glass on the cobbles. They'll check." I was already moving, low, scanning the room. Back door: probably locked, definitely audible. Chimney: too narrow, and I wasn't eight anymore. Cellar hatch under the rug in the corner—I could see the seam. "Is there a way out of the cellar that isn't the cellar door?"

"How would I—" He stopped. Something crossed his face, some old reflex waking up after years asleep. "Coal chute. It's barred, but the bar's rotted, I've been meaning to—"

"Meaning to what?"

"Fix it." His voice cracked on the word. "For twelve years I've been meaning to fix it."

"Then tonight's your lucky night." I ripped the rug back and hauled at the hatch. Stuck. Of course stuck. Boots in the street now, close, and a voice calling, "You there! Lights! Someone's broken a window on the Row!"

Jonah crossed the room faster than I'd have guessed. He didn't help me with the hatch. He went to the hearth, swept the ash aside with his bare hand, and pulled up a floorboard I would never have found, revealing a second hatch beneath—newer, oiled, fitted with a proper iron ring.

"There's a real way," he said. "Under the ash. There's always a real way, if you're willing to live like a rat."

"Get in the hole," I said.

"You first."

"I'm not the one they're looking for!"

"You don't know that," he said, and there was something strange in his voice, something that wasn't fear for himself. "You're a child alone in a curfew with a broken window and blood on your sleeve. They'll take you just for asking questions. Get in the hole."

Boots on the step now. Knuckles on the door. "Open in the king's name!"

And Jonah Fairweather—coward, careful man, keeper of useless charms—did the single bravest thing I would see anyone do in the whole long winter that followed. He picked up the poker, walked to his own front door, and opened it wide, standing in the frame with the light behind him.

"Sorry, sirs," he called out, loud and aggrieved. "A cat knocked a pot off my sill. Gave me quite a fright. Nothing worth troubling the king's men over."

"It sounded like breaking glass," said the gravel voice.

"Cats," Jonah said, "sound like many things."

There was a silence I could feel through the floorboards, the particular silence of a man deciding whether tonight was the night he arrested somebody. Then: "You'll report the damage to the Ministry office. All property damage is logged under the Edict."

"Yes, sir. First thing tomorrow."

The boots receded. The white light slid off the walls and away.

I let out a breath I felt like I'd been holding for nine days. Jonah closed the door, set down the poker, and leaned his forehead against the wood for a long moment. When he turned around, his face had rearranged itself into something stern and exhausted and much older than his years.

"Now," he said, "you're going to tell me who you are, why you're in my house, and why you broke my window, in that order, and then you're going to climb back out of it however you climbed in."

"Or what?" I said. "You'll call the Ministry? They just left. Be a waste of a trip."

His jaw tightened. "You're eleven years old."

"Ten and a half."

"You're ten and a half years old, alone, at midnight, during a curfew, and you fell through a second-story window like you've done it before." He crouched down to my level, which I hated, adults doing that, like height was respect. "Who are you running from?"

"Everyone," I said. "Same as you."

That landed. I watched it land—watched it hit something in him that had been waiting years to be hit. He sat back on his heels.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got." My wrist throbbed where I'd gripped the glass. I realized I was still holding the shard and put it down carefully, the way Wren's voice in my head insisted: *never leave a blade where a fool can find it.* "Look. I'm not here to rob you. I'm here because—" and I stopped, because how did I explain the locket? How did I explain a voice made of weather, a dead-alive woman, a crossroads and cold candle-wax fingers? Any word of it and he'd throw me out, or worse, he'd believe me, and believing was worse.

But the locket had gone warm again. Insistent. Almost annoyed.

*Show him,* it said.

"Fine," I muttered—to it, not to him—and yanked the linen wrap off my left wrist.

* * *

I've thought a lot, since, about what he saw. I've had a year to think about it, sitting across breakfast tables and lesson books from him, and I still can't fully picture it, because I've never seen my own mark the way he saw it in that firelight: silver-blue, forked like a river delta, like a lightning bolt frozen in the act of deciding where to strike.

What I saw instead was his face.

It did several things very quickly. First it went blank, the way faces do when they're protecting themselves. Then it went pale. Then something happened around his eyes that I can only describe as a door blowing open in a house that had been shut up for years—dust and grief and old cold air all coming out at once.

He reached for my wrist. I jerked back.

"Don't touch it," I said. "Nobody touches it."

"I'm not going to—" He stopped himself. Put his hands flat on his knees. Breathed. "Where did you get that mark?"

"I was born with it. I think. I don't remember not having it."

"Who are your parents?"

"Don't have any. Had a woman in Harrowfen who fed me sometimes. She's gone now. Everyone's gone now." I said it flat, the way you say things you've decided not to feel yet. There'd be time to feel them later, probably at an inconvenient moment. That's how feeling works.

"And the locket." His eyes had gone to my shirt, to the shape of it under the cloth. "You're wearing something under there. It's humming. I can hear it from here."

"You can hear it?"

"I can hear—" He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "Twelve years. Twelve years I've heard nothing, felt nothing, been *nothing*, and now there's a child in my shop with my sister's mark on her wrist and a locket that hums like a storm front, and the Ministry is hunting people like us, and I am standing in my own home having a conversation I buried a lifetime ago."

"My sister's mark," I repeated. "You said—"

"Wren." He said the name like it cost him. Like pulling it out hurt. "My sister. She died. Twelve years ago. I killed her." His voice went strange and level, reciting something learned by heart, worn smooth from handling. "There was a storm. I called it. I was young and stupid and full of power I didn't understand, and the harvest-guard died, and Wren died, and I ran, and I have spent every day since making sure I could never do it again."

The locket was burning now. Not painfully—warm, urgent, like a hand gripping mine.

*I didn't die,* the weather said, and this time it wasn't weather, it was almost words, it was the closest to words it had ever come, and I think it came through to him too, because Jonah Fairweather went absolutely still, the way animals go still when they hear something impossible.

"She's alive," I said. "Isn't she. Whoever's in this locket. She's alive, and she sent me."

He looked at me for a long, long moment. The fire ticked. Somewhere out in the city, another patrol bell rang, distant and indifferent.

"You're going to catch your death in that hole under the floorboards," he said finally, in a voice like a man agreeing to lose a war. "There's a blanket on the chair. Don't touch anything else."

"That's not a yes."

"It's not a no." He dragged a hand through his hair. "It's the middle of the night, and the Edict says harboring the gifted is treason, and I have spent twelve years building a life specifically designed so that no one would ever fall through my window needing me." He looked at the broken glass, at the cold pouring through it, at me. "And my sister's mark is on your wrist."

"So that's a yes."

"That's a *maybe*," he said, but he was already pulling the blanket off the chair, and we both knew.

I slept that night in the hidden space under the hearth, curled around the locket like it was a coal I couldn't let go out, listening to Jonah Fairweather pace overhead for hours. And just before dawn, when I was nearly asleep, the weather-voice rose one last time, clear as a bell across water:

*Tell him what the locket remembers. Tomorrow. Tell him about the king.*

Above me, the pacing stopped.

He'd heard it too.
