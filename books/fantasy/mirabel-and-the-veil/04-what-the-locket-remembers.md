## 4. What the Locket Remembers

The girl slept like something that had learned to sleep fast.

Jonah watched her from the doorway of his back room, a chipped mug of barley tea going cold in his hands. She'd claimed the cot beneath the window—the one with the bad latch—and curled into a ball no bigger than a bundle of kindling, one fist closed around the locket at her throat even in sleep. Her breathing came quick and shallow, the rhythm of an animal that had never once trusted the dark to hold.

The storm-mark on her wrist caught the lamplight. Even from here, even across the room, it seemed to move—a slow curl of silver-blue lines, like lightning frozen mid-decision.

Wren's mark. His sister's mark, drawn on a stranger's skin.

He'd told himself a dozen lies since dawn. That it was a coincidence. That storm-marks were common among the gifted, a family of sigils, and plenty of people wore this one. That the girl was nobody's messenger and he was nobody's brother anymore—twelve years of being small had taught him how to be nobody thoroughly.

None of the lies held. He set down the tea and crossed to the cot.

Up close, sleeping, she looked younger than she had awake. Awake, Mirabel was all edges—feral eyes, dirt-ground into knuckles, a jaw set like she expected the world to swing at her and had decided to swing first. Asleep, she was just a child with chapped lips and a healing scab over one eyebrow, and Jonah felt the old grief shift its weight inside him, the way a wound shifts when you finally stop bracing against it.

He crouched beside the cot. Slowly—she'd shown him what happened when he moved too fast near her, a flash of teeth and a hand darting for the knife she kept in her boot—he reached out and touched the locket.

It hummed.

Not warmth. Not cold. A hum, low and steady, like a struck bell heard through water, like the moment before thunder when the air itself holds its breath. He'd felt that hum once before, long ago, standing at the edge of the Veil with Wren's hand in his and the harvest burning behind them.

His stomach dropped through the floorboards, through the cellar, through the bedrock of Greyhollow itself.

*Don't open it,* said twelve years of careful living. *Lockets are traps. Lockets are evidence. Lockets get hedge-healers dragged into the night and neighbors who suddenly can't meet your eye.*

But Tam was already taken. The Edict was already read from every corner in the city. The illusion of safety had died two nights ago in a crier's flat voice, and a man with nothing left to lose could afford to be curious.

Jonah thumbed the clasp.

* * *

The world went white, then blue, then somewhere that was neither.

He stood in a garden he had never seen but somehow knew—knew the way you know a face from a dream. Roses gone heavy with late summer. A fountain trickling somewhere out of sight. And a woman laughing, head thrown back, dark hair escaping its pins, while a tall man in plain clothes—plain clothes, though Jonah's memory supplied the crown anyway—chased a small girl between the hedges.

*Aldric.* The knowledge arrived whole, unasked-for, the way locket-memories do: not seen but *remembered*, as if they'd always lived in him and the locket had only turned the key. King Aldric Vane, third of his name, and he was laughing in his own garden in his shirtsleeves because his daughter had hidden his boots and he couldn't very well summon the Royal Guard to find them.

*Elira,* said the memory, tender as a bruise. *My Elira.*

The queen. Dead three years now, the criers had said, though the criers said it the way they said everything, flat as slate. In the memory she was alive and ridiculous, holding the stolen boots above her head while the princess shrieked with delight, and Aldric caught Jonah—caught *himself*, the remembering self, the king's own vantage—watching them both with an expression so nakedly grateful that Jonah's throat tightened.

*I don't deserve them,* the king thought, in the memory, in his own voice. *And I will spend my whole life trying to.*

The garden dissolved.

Snow next. A border of white pines, and beyond them—not darkness exactly, but an absence, a place where the world forgot to continue. The Veil. Jonah knew it instantly, the way you know your own scar tissue. He'd stood at its edge once, at nineteen, with his hands full of storm and his sister's blood on his sleeves.

Aldric stood alone before it. No guards. No crown—no, wait, there was the crown, carried under his arm like a hat, like a burden he'd grown tired of wearing. He looked older than the garden-memory. He looked hollowed already, though not yet eaten.

"I know what you are," Aldric said to the Veil. His voice cracked on it. "I know what you want. And I am telling you—" he stepped forward, close enough that the snow around his boots stopped falling, hung suspended, *listened* —"you may have my memories. Mine. Every year of them, take them slow, take them as tribute. But you will not touch my people. You will not touch the gifted. They are under my protection, and I will pay for their protection with myself."

Silence. Then the Veil answered—not in words, in *certainty*, a pressure behind the eyes, a bargain sealed without signature:

*So be it. One memory per season. Begin with her laugh.*

"No," Aldric whispered. "Not that one. Not first—"

But the Veil did not negotiate. It took what it took.

The memory shattered—

* * *

Jonah came back to himself on the floor of his back room, cheek against cold boards, the locket swinging shut above him. Mirabel crouched over him, knife drawn, eyes wide.

"You fell down," she said. Accusatory. As if he'd done it at her.

"How long?"

"A minute." She sheathed the knife, deciding he wasn't a threat, or wasn't worth the effort. "You made a noise."

"What noise?"

She considered. "Like the kettle. Before it screams."

He sat up slowly. His hands were shaking. Outside, Greyhollow went about its morning—cart wheels, a vendor crying the price of salt fish, all of it ordinary, all of it obscene, given what he'd just seen.

Because he'd seen it. Not imagined it. Locket-memories didn't lie; they were cut from the mind that made them, true as scars. King Aldric Vane had stood before the Veil three years ago and traded himself, piece by piece, to buy his people's safety.

And now his people were being hunted by royal decree.

"Do you know whose memories these are?" Jonah asked.

Mirabel shrugged, wary. "They're loud sometimes. At night. A man talking to snow." Her chin lifted, defiant, daring him to call her mad. "I thought everyone heard lockets."

"Where did you get it?"

Another shrug, but smaller. "I woke up with it." She wrapped her arms around her knees. "Two years ago. There was a fire—I think there was a fire—and after, I was walking, and it was around my neck. I don't remember before the fire. I don't remember much of anything." Her voice hardened, pre-empting pity. "I manage."

*Wren sent her.* The thought arrived with the weight of certainty, and Jonah pushed it down, because if he let it stand he would have to face what it meant—that his sister lived, that she'd spent twelve years on the other side of the Veil, that she'd found a fire-orphaned child and pressed a piece of a dying king into her hands and aimed her at Greyhollow like an arrow. Later. He could face that later. Right now there was a simpler horror, and it needed naming.

"Mirabel. Listen carefully." He held up the locket. "This belonged to King Aldric. The real one. These are his memories—his garden, his queen, the bargain he made at the Veil. Do you understand what that means?"

"That he lost them?"

"That someone *took* them." Jonah stood, pacing now, three steps and turn, three steps and turn, the way he paced when the storm under his skin stirred. "Three years ago, Aldric paid the Veil to protect the gifted. Tribute. Himself. And the Edict we heard two nights ago—the Cleansing, registration, hunters in the streets—that Edict is the exact opposite of everything he bargained for. It's the opposite of *him*. The man in those memories wouldn't hunt magicians if his crown depended on it. His crown *did* depend on it, and he chose us instead."

Mirabel frowned. "Then why is the king doing the hunting?"

"Because," Jonah said quietly, and the word came out of him like a stone dropping into deep water, "the thing giving the orders isn't the king."

He stopped pacing. The lamplight steadied, or maybe his hands did.

"Think about it. Think about what the Veil takes. Memories. Selves. It eats them—it ate a piece of Aldric every season, and Aldric *let* it, because he believed it would keep its word. But things like that don't keep bargains. Things like that *nest*." He heard his own voice go flat and certain, the voice of a man reading his own death warrant and finding the handwriting familiar. "Something crossed over. Something wearing his memories, wearing his face, wearing his crown. And it's afraid of us—of the gifted—because we're the ones who can see it. Who can feel the wrongness. So it turned its fear into law and called it cleansing."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, a customer knocked at the shop door, tried it, gave up. Jonah barely noticed.

"And the real king?" Mirabel asked. For all her feral wariness, she followed fast—faster than most adults, faster than Jonah liked.

"Alive, I'd guess. Or what's left of him. Locked away somewhere while the thing that ate his life rules in his name." Jonah looked down at the locket, humming faintly against his palm, and understood at last why Wren—if it was Wren—had sent it. Not as a keepsake. As *evidence*. As a weapon. As proof that the hunt was built on a lie, and lies could be broken if you were brave enough, and foolish enough, to carry the truth into the light.

Dread had been his constant companion for twelve years, a dog at his heel. But dread was formless, and formless things could be outrun. This—this had a shape now. A shape with a stolen crown and a hunger that would not stop at Aldric, that would not stop at Tam the hedge-healer, that would not stop until every gifted mind in Greyhollow was devoured or driven into the snow.

Fear with a shape could be fought.

"Mirabel." He crouched to her level. "The night you came through my window. The men chasing you. Did they say anything? Anything about the locket, about where you'd been, about—"

"They said 'the little thief has the king's face-words.'" She recited it flatly, the way children recite nightmares they've decided not to be frightened of anymore. "And the captain—the one with the grey eyes—he kept saying, 'Give it back, give it back, give it back,' like a prayer. Like it hurt him to say it."

Captain Ives. Jonah had seen the name on the Edict broadsheets, inked beneath the royal seal: *By command of Captain Ives, First Hunter of the Crown.* A man begging a child to return a locket, in words that sounded like grief.

A man half-eaten already, maybe. A man who remembered, in some buried chamber of himself, that the face he served was not the face he'd sworn to.

Jonah closed his fist around the locket. The hum traveled up his arm and settled behind his sternum, where the storm lived, and for the first time in twelve years the two sensations did not feel like enemies.

"We can't stay here," he said. "They'll come back for you. They'll come back for me, once they trace the window, the street, the charm-shop with a storm-marked girl in the back room. And when they come, we need somewhere to be." He was thinking aloud now, plans assembling themselves out of panic and purpose. "Greyhollow is older than its maps. Old cities have old ways underneath them. Smugglers' tunnels, forgotten cisterns, doors that aren't on any survey the Ministry ever drew. If there are others like us—unregistered, unhunted-so-far—they'll be underground. Literally."

Mirabel's eyes had gone sharp. "You know tunnels?"

"I know a woman who knows tunnels." He did not say that the woman was Sable Voss, one-eyed ferrywoman of the Undertow, who owed him nothing and whom he'd avoided for twelve years precisely because she saw too much. He did not say that asking Sable Voss for shelter meant stepping back into the current he'd spent half a lifetime swimming against. "First, we eat. Then we pack light. Then—"

The knock came at the shop door again. Harder this time. Three raps, deliberate, spaced like a countdown.

Jonah went still. Through the wall he heard the door test, the handle turn, and then a voice—official, bored, carrying the particular flatness of a man who says the same sentence forty times a day:

"Ministry inspection. Open in the name of the Edict of Cleansing."

Mirabel was off the cot and silent in the same heartbeat, knife drawn, moving to the corner where the shadows pooled deepest. Her eyes found Jonah's, and in them was no fear at all—only the flat readiness of a child who had been hunted before and intended to be hunted less successfully this time.

The storm-mark on her wrist glowed faintly in the dark, silver-blue, patient, *waiting*—and Jonah Fairweather, charm-seller, coward, keeper of useless luck-knots, reached beneath the floorboard where his mother's grimoire ashes lay mixed with the dust of twelve careful years, and made his choice.

"Not through the front," he breathed. "There's a way down to the cellars. Stay behind me."

Outside, the knocking stopped.

Inside, something far worse began: the soft, deliberate sound of a key turning in the Ministry's lock.
