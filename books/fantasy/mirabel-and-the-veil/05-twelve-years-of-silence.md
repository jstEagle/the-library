## 5. Twelve Years of Silence

The girl slept like the dead.

Jonah had watched her for an hour from the doorway of his back room, a cup of willow-bark tea going cold in his hands, and she hadn't moved except to breathe. She was small enough that she barely dented the mattress he'd dragged in from the loft. Her boots were ruined—soles worn through to the shape of her feet—and her hair was a matted brown thicket with twigs in it, as if she'd come through a hedge rather than a window. Maybe she had.

On her left wrist, just above the pulse point, the storm-mark caught the lamplight.

He'd told himself it wasn't what it looked like. He'd told himself that all night, while he'd barred the door and shuttered the windows and sat in the dark listening for hunters on the stair. A birthmark. A burn scar. Ink, done badly by some traveling tattooist who charged children two coppers for a crooked line. There were a hundred explanations that weren't the one his heart kept insisting on.

But the mark didn't care about his explanations. It lay on her skin in the old pattern—the spiral that opened into three branching lines, like lightning frozen mid-fall—and it *moved*, very slightly, the way water moves under ice. When she breathed out, the branches brightened. When she breathed in, they dimmed. It was keeping time with her. It was alive on her arm the way his own mark had been alive on his, once, before he'd learned to starve it.

Wren's mark. His sister's mark, exact down to the third branch's fork, which no other storm-caller in their line had ever carried. He'd held Wren's wrist in his hands when they were children and traced it with a fingertip and she'd laughed and said, *It tickles, Jonny, stop it, you'll wake it up.*

You'll wake it up.

He set the tea down because his hands had started shaking, and shaking hands near a storm-mark was a bad idea even now, even after twelve years of practice at being nothing.

* * *

Twelve years ago, on the last night of harvest, Jonah Fairweather had killed six people.

That was the sentence he carried. Not "six people died." Not "there was an accident." He had killed them, and he knew the difference the way a man knows the difference between rain falling on him and rain called down by his own hand.

The village of Thornmere sat in a river valley where the storms came off the western hills every autumn, fat and slow and mostly harmless, and for four generations the Fairweathers had stood in the fields during those storms and talked the worst of the lightning elsewhere. It wasn't heroics. It was chores. His mother had treated it like chores—"Take out the storm, Jonah, and then bring in the buckets"—and Wren, three years younger and twice as gifted, had treated it like a game, running the field rows barefoot with her arms out, laughing at the thunder like it was telling jokes.

The last night of harvest, the storm came down the valley wrong.

He'd known it was wrong before anyone else did. Storms had textures, and this one had a texture like a hand closing. Later—much later, in the long years of not-thinking-about-it—he would learn there was a word for what he'd felt: something on the far side of things, hungry, leaning against the membrane of the world. But that night he was seventeen and tired and half-drunk on cider, and when the sky over Thornmere went the color of a bruise and the air pressure dropped so hard his ears popped, he did what he'd always done. He reached up and took hold of it.

And it took hold of him back.

He remembered it in pieces, the way you remember any wound. The storm wasn't a storm; it was a mouth. It poured itself down his arm and into the mark on his wrist and it *asked*, in a language older than words, to be let all the way in, and some part of him—some part that was tired of being small, tired of being the boy who did chores—said yes.

What came out of him then wasn't weather. It was a column of white fire from cloud to earth, and it struck the center of the harvest-field where the whole village stood celebrating, where the harvest-guard had formed their traditional ring with torches, where Wren was running barefoot with her arms out, laughing.

Six people. The guard captain. Two farmers. A boy of fifteen whose name Jonah had never let himself remember and never managed to forget. And Wren.

They said afterward that there was nothing left to bury of her but her boots. They said it gently, because grief makes people gentle, and Jonah had stood in the ash circle in the rain with his mark burned black and silent on his wrist and understood that he would never call so much as a breeze again for as long as he lived.

His mother didn't speak to him. That was the exile—not a decree, not soldiers at the gate. Just his mother turning away from him in the doorway of their house, and his father saying, quietly, "Go, son. Before she has to look at you again." And Jonah walking out of Thornmere in the rain with nothing, letting the storm wash the road behind him clean of his footprints, walking until he reached a city where magic was illegal and therefore, he'd reasoned, safe—because in Greyhollow, no one would ever ask him to be what he was.

He'd sold useless charms ever since. Luck-knots that were only knots. Fever-charms that were only dried mint and a pretty rhyme. Every day a small performance of harmlessness, every night a small death, and he'd told himself twelve years of small deaths added up to penance.

They didn't. They added up to hiding. He'd known the difference for a long time. He'd just never had a reason to do anything about it.

Until a girl fell through his window wearing his sister's skin-mark and carrying a dead king's memories in a locket.

* * *

She woke at dawn, silently, the way animals wake—one instant asleep, the next fully upright on the mattress with her back to the wall and her eyes tracking the room.

"Easy," Jonah said from the chair he'd never actually gone to bed from. "No one's here but me."

Her eyes found the door, the window, the shutters, the poker by the hearth. Then him. Then, lingering longest, his hands—as if hands were the thing she'd learned to watch first.

"I'm not going to hurt you," he said.

"You locked the door," she said. Her voice was rough, unpracticed, like a hinge that hadn't swung in months. "From the inside."

"So the hunters can't get in."

"They already know I'm here." She said it flatly, without fear, the way you'd report the weather. "They always know. That's what the whistle-dogs are for. They smell the locket."

"The locket," Jonah said carefully. "Where did you get it?"

She looked at him for a long moment. He watched her decide something—watched the calculation move behind her eyes, quick and hard, a child who had learned that information was currency and spent it carefully.

"A lady gave it to me," she said finally. "In a dream. Or I dreamed the lady. I don't know anymore which way round it goes." She touched her wrist without seeming to notice she was doing it. "She said, *find the charm-seller in Greyhollow, the one who stopped listening.* She said you'd know her name."

The room got very quiet. Outside, Greyhollow was waking—cart wheels on cobbles, the Ministry bell tolling the hour, someone shouting about the price of coal. Inside, Jonah sat very still and felt twelve years of careful silence develop a crack straight down the middle.

"What name?" he asked. His voice came out wrong. He made himself say it again, steadier. "What name would I know?"

Mirabel—the girl, Mirabel, though she hadn't told him that yet—pulled the locket out from under her shirt. It hummed. He could feel it humming from across the room, a faint pressure behind his teeth like the moment before lightning, and the storm-mark on his own wrist, dormant and scarred and starved for twelve years, *ached* in answer.

"She said," Mirabel recited, in a cadence too old for her, a cadence borrowed from someone else's mouth, "*tell my brother the storm didn't take me. Tell him I've been holding the door.*"

The crack in the silence widened, and everything Jonah had buried came up through it at once—not as memory, as flood. The field. The white fire. The boots. His mother's turned back. Twelve years of being small, being harmless, being nothing, all of it built on a grave that might be empty.

"No," he said.

"It's true," Mirabel said, with the terrible flat certainty of children. "I don't lie about her. She's the only person who ever came back for me."

"You don't understand what you're saying." He was on his feet now and didn't remember standing. "I saw it happen. I *did* it. There was nothing left of her but—"

"Boots," Mirabel said. "I know. She told me that too. She said, *he thinks he killed me, and the guilt is the lock on his cage, and whoever forged that key wanted him exactly here—small, and silent, and alone in a city with no sky.*"

Jonah's legs gave out and he sat down hard in the chair.

Because that was the part that had never fit. In twelve years of turning the night over and over, looking for the flaw in himself that could explain it, there had always been one detail that refused to make sense: the texture of the storm. Storms had textures. He'd known that since he was five years old. And the storm that came down the valley that night had a texture like a hand closing—a texture no natural storm had ever had—and he had felt, in the last instant before it poured into him, something on the far side *choosing* him. Choosing him the way you choose a key.

And now a girl sat on his mattress quoting a dead woman who said the guilt was a lock, and locks are forged by someone, and the Regent of Rust wore a king's face and ate memories and hunted the gifted—

"—wanted him exactly here," she'd said. Small, and silent, and alone.

"Oh," Jonah said aloud, to nobody, to the room, to the ghost of a seventeen-year-old standing in an ash circle in the rain. "Oh, God. It wasn't me. It was never only me."

Mirabel slid off the mattress and crossed the floor and stood in front of him, close enough that he could see the exhaustion under her defiance, the grime in the creases of her neck, the fact that she was eleven years old and had crossed half a kingdom alone because a voice in a dream told her to. She held out the locket.

"She wants you to open it," Mirabel said. "All the way. Not the little bits that leak out when I sleep. All the way. She says there's something inside it you have to see before winter ends, or everyone like us dies in the dark."

His hands were shaking again. He looked at them—these hands that had once held a storm, these hands that had spent twelve years tying knots that meant nothing—and he thought about the Ministry clerk's bored eyes, about Tam the hedge-healer dragged into the dark, about the proclamation nailed to the chapel door in letters that smelled faintly of ash. About a king who was not a king, eating the realm's memory one mind at a time. About a sister who might be alive beyond a Veil he'd only heard of in his mother's oldest stories, holding a door.

The past had refused to stay buried. Fine. Then he would dig it up himself, and see what was underneath, and let it name him whatever it wanted. Coward. Killer. Brother. He could survive being named, now that he knew the naming might have been done to him.

He took the locket.

It was warm. Of course it was warm—it had been warm all night, sitting on the table, humming its low storm-hum, and he'd refused to touch it, and now his thumb found the clasp and the humming climbed into a whine and the marks on both their wrists lit together, twin spirals, same fork in the third branch, and the room filled with the smell of rain on hot stone.

"One condition," Jonah said, and his voice was steady now, steadier than it had been in twelve years, because purpose is a kind of ballast. "Whatever's in there—whatever she shows us—you tell me everything after. No more rationing it out. We're past rationing."

Mirabel nodded solemnly, an eleven-year-old sealing a treaty.

Jonah opened the locket, and the light that came out of it was the light of the last night of harvest, and his sister's voice said his name.

* * *

Later—much later, when the light had shown them what it showed, and Jonah had wept the way he hadn't wept since he was seventeen, ugly and kneeling on the floorboards while Mirabel patted his shoulder with the awkward competence of a child comforting a large broken animal—they sat at his little table with the shutters still closed and planned.

Because the light had shown him Wren. Not dying. Standing in a place that was not a place, at the base of a door made of storm-fronts, aged barely a day per year, her mark blazing, her face older and calmer and *hers*. Holding the door. Holding it shut from the far side, against something that pressed and pressed and pressed.

And she had said, in the voice that had said *it tickles, Jonny*: "Come to the Veil. Bring the storm home. And Jonah—bring help. You can't carry what I'm carrying, and you can't cross alone. Find the ones who live under the city. Find the ferrywoman. She owes our mother a debt, and she doesn't even know it yet."

Jonah stared at the cold tea, at the girl, at the locket lying open between them like a small sleeping mouth.

"The Undertow," he said slowly. "Smuggler tunnels under the harbor. I've heard the name whispered for years and pretended I hadn't." He rubbed his face. "Unregistered gifted folk. Which means, as of two nights ago, criminals. Which means if I go asking after them, I'm not a charm-seller anymore. I'm a hunted man."

"You were always a hunted man," Mirabel said. "You just hadn't noticed yet."

He almost smiled. Almost. Then he looked at the window, at the grey light pressing against the shutters, at the city where he had spent twelve years being small—and he thought of Sable Voss, one-eyed ferrywoman of rumor and nightmare, and of a debt owed to a dead woman, and of a harbor that would freeze within the month.

"Get your boots," Jonah Fairweather said, standing, feeling the silence of twelve years break around him like ice off a roofline. "We're going under the city."

Somewhere above them, distantly, a whistle-dog began to sing.
