## 6. The Undertow Beneath Greyhollow

The tunnels smelled like the inside of a drowned man's pocket.

Mirabel had told Jonah that on purpose, right before she dropped through the grate behind the tannery, because she wanted to see his face when he landed in it. He'd made a sound like a cat being stepped on, and for one whole minute after that he'd stopped looking at her like she was a problem to be solved and started looking at her like she was maybe something else.

She liked that better.

"Keep your hand on the wall," she said now, her voice bouncing off wet stone. "Left side. If you touch the right side you'll get tar on your sleeve, and Sable hates tar. She says it means somebody's been crawling where they shouldn't."

"And people crawl where they shouldn't down here often?"

"All the time." Mirabel skipped over a puddle that was more oil than water. "That's kind of the whole point of tunnels."

Jonah's lantern threw their shadows huge against the brick — hers small and quick, his tall and hunched, like a heron trying to make itself a smaller bird. He'd been hunched since the curfew sweep three nights ago. Since her. She'd noticed that about him early: Jonah Fairweather folded himself up whenever the world got loud, like a man who'd learned that taking up space was the same as asking for trouble.

She knew a lot about not taking up space. She'd been very good at it for two years, before the mark started humming and the locket started whispering and everything went wrong in the specific way that ended with her crashing through a charm-seller's window with hunters on her heels.

"You said," Jonah said carefully, "that you've been down here before."

"I said I know the way."

"That's not an answer."

"It's better than an answer. Answers are just words." She ducked under a low pipe, heard him clunk his head softly behind her, and grinned into the dark. "The way is the way."

He muttered something that was probably a prayer or a curse. With storm-callers, she was learning, it was usually both.

The truth — which she wasn't going to give him yet, because he hadn't earned it, because trust was a thing you paid for in pieces — was that Wren had shown her these tunnels. Not in person. In the locket. Nights when Mirabel couldn't sleep, the locket would open itself a crack and pour out little floods of memory that weren't hers: a woman with storm-grey eyes walking this exact passage, counting her steps, pressing her palm to a stone shaped like a sleeping dog. *Forty-one steps past the dog,* the memory always said, *and knock twice, then once, because they'll be listening.*

Mirabel had walked it in her head so many times that her feet already knew it. That was the strange thing about borrowed memories. They didn't feel like learning. They felt like remembering something you'd somehow forgotten, like your body had done it all along and your mind just hadn't been invited.

*Come find the Undertow,* Wren's voice had said, the first night, soft as snowfall. *Tell them the Veil is thinning. Tell them the ferrywoman still owes me.*

Mirabel didn't know who the ferrywoman owed or why. But debts were the one currency Greyhollow never stopped trading in, even underground. Especially underground.

* * *

Forty-one steps past the dog-shaped stone, Mirabel stopped at a wall that looked exactly like every other wall — black brick, weeping damp, utterly boring. Behind her, Jonah shifted his weight and said nothing, which she'd decided was his way of screaming.

She knocked twice. Then once.

Silence. Then, from somewhere inside the stone, a voice like gravel poured slow:

"*Password.*"

Mirabel glanced back at Jonah. His face did something complicated.

"I don't have a password," she said. "I have a message."

"A message from who?"

"From someone who knows why the harbor ice hasn't frozen right for three winters running."

Another silence, longer. Somewhere far off, water dripped in a rhythm almost like speech. Then the wall shuddered — a whole section of it, brick and mortar and old ship's timber, swinging inward on hinges greased with something that smelled like fish and secrets.

A woman filled the doorway. Tall, broad as a door herself, grey braid coiled on top of her head like a rope ready to be thrown. A leather patch covered her left eye; her right eye was the color of shallow water over sand, and it went from Mirabel's face to Jonah's lantern to Jonah's face and stayed there.

"Well," the woman said. "You brought me a Fairweather."

Mirabel's stomach dropped. "How do you—"

"The walk." The woman tapped her own nose. "Storm-callers walk like the ground owes them money. Twelve years I've waited for one of you to come down my stairs, and here you are, smelling of cheap luck-knots and fear." She stepped aside. "In. Both of you. And douse that lantern — light travels down here like gossip."

Jonah hesitated on the threshold. Mirabel watched him do it — watched him stand at the edge of the dark with his jaw tight, some argument happening entirely inside his own head. She was starting to collect these moments, cataloguing them the way other kids collected buttons. *There goes Jonah, deciding whether to run. There goes Jonah, deciding not to.*

"In," the woman said again, gentler this time, which somehow made it worse. "Nobody's made you do anything in twelve years, Jonah Fairweather. Nobody's making you now. But the girl's half-frozen and the hunters swept the Tannery Quarter an hour ago, so whatever you're deciding, decide it warm."

That did it. Jonah stepped through, and the wall closed behind them with a sound like a book shutting.

* * *

The Undertow was not what Mirabel expected, mostly because she hadn't let herself expect anything. Expecting things was how you got hurt. She'd learned that lesson in a dozen cities before Greyhollow, and she'd learned it so well it had grown into her like a root.

But the tunnel opened into a cavern the size of a church, and the church it most resembled was one where everybody had decided, all at once, to stop pretending.

Lanterns hung from rusted chains, dozens of them, burning low and gold. Rope bridges crossed overhead between ledges cut into the rock. There were stalls — actual market stalls — selling bread and boots and bottled eels, and there were children, actual children, chasing each other around a rain barrel, and there was an old man sitting by a fire with his hands cupped around a flame that burned blue and cold and cast no heat at all, just light, just for him, just because he liked how it looked.

Magic. Everywhere. Open, unhidden, ordinary as breathing.

Mirabel stood very still and felt something crack in her chest, quiet, like ice giving way in spring.

Nobody was hiding.

An old woman stirred a pot with a wooden spoon and hummed, and the steam off the pot curled into shapes — birds, mostly, little paper-thin birds that flew up and dissolved. Two men argued cheerfully over a game of tiles while one of them moved the pieces without touching them, cheating outrageously, both of them laughing. A girl not much older than Mirabel sat cross-legged on a crate, talking to a rat the size of a loaf of bread, and the rat was clearly talking back.

"They're not afraid," Jonah whispered beside her. His voice came out strange, scraped thin.

"No," the one-eyed woman said. "We gave that up. It was costing too much."

She led them down a stair cut into the living rock, past a shrine where someone had left offerings — a child's shoe, a lock of hair, a brass button polished bright — and into a low room lit by a single hanging lamp. Maps covered the walls. Not city maps, though there were those too, marked in red ink. These were maps of weather. Wind charts, tide tables, a great faded drawing of the coastline with arrows showing where the storms came from, and one arrow, long and careful, pointing straight at Greyhollow and labeled in a firm hand: *HE COMES THIS WAY EVERY WINTER. WHY DOES NO ONE ASK?*

The woman lowered herself onto a bench that groaned under her and looked at Mirabel with her one eye.

"Now. You said you had a message."

Mirabel's mouth went dry. All the way down here she'd known what she had to say, and now that it was time to say it, the words stuck like fishbones.

She pulled the locket out from under her shirt instead. It caught the lamplight, dull silver, worn smooth. The woman's eye fixed on it and her whole face changed — not dramatically, nothing so easy as shock, but the way a harbor changes when the tide turns. Everything the same, and everything different.

"Where did you get that?"

"It was given to me." Mirabel's voice came out steadier than she felt. "By the woman who owns it. Sort of owns it. She—" How to explain? How to say *she lives behind the sky* without sounding like the mad thing the hunters said she was? "She can't come herself. So she sent me. Her name is Wren. Wren Fairweather."

The silence that followed had weight. It pressed down on the room like deep water.

The woman rose slowly from the bench. She crossed to Mirabel, and Mirabel made herself not flinch, made herself stand still the way Wren had taught her — *stillness isn't fear, little fish, stillness is choosing* — and the woman took the locket in fingers scarred thick as ship's cable, and held it, and listened to it hum.

"Sable Voss," Jonah said quietly. "The ferrywoman."

"At your service, when there's service to give." Sable didn't look away from the locket. "Wren Fairweather pulled me out of the Veil-tide twenty years ago, when I was fool enough to go fishing where the memory-realm leaks into the sea. It took my eye. It took my crew. It did not take me, because she stood on the water and argued with it." Her thumb moved over the locket's seam. "I've owed her a debt since before you were born, girl. I thought it was a debt to a ghost."

"She's not a ghost," Mirabel said, too fast, too fierce.

"No." Sable's eye lifted to hers. "Ghosts don't send children. Ghosts don't choose hope. Only the living do that, and only the stubborn." She closed her fist around the locket, gently, and gave it back. "Welcome to the Undertow, Mirabel. Both of you. You'll be fed, you'll be warmed, and you'll be asked exactly one question tonight, and it's this—"

She turned to Jonah. And Mirabel saw the big woman's voice change registers, the way a ship changes sails, from friendly to working.

"—the Edict says every gifted soul registers by month's end or the hunters come. We have sixty-one people down here. Eleven of them are children. Winter ice closes the harbor in six weeks, and when it does, the sea-gate seals, and whatever's inside these walls when it seals stays inside until spring." She spread her hands. "So. Jonah Fairweather. Last storm-caller on this coast. Are you staying, or are you passing through?"

* * *

Later — after stew that tasted like onions and mercy, after a blanket that smelled of woodsmoke, after the blue-flame old man had shown Mirabel how to fold firelight into little wings — Mirabel found Jonah sitting alone on a ledge overlooking the cavern, watching the life below with the expression of a man watching a fire he both needed and feared.

She sat down next to him without asking. That was another thing she'd learned: you didn't ask permission to sit beside someone. You just did it, and if they moved away, you learned something too.

"You're going to say no," she said.

"I haven't decided."

"You've decided. You do this thing with your hands when you've decided and you hate it." She nodded at his hands, which were laced together tight enough to whiten. "Sable's going to ask you to call a storm. For the sea-gate. Before the ice."

Jonah flinched like she'd touched a burn. "Twelve years," he said. "Twelve years I haven't—"

"I know. You told me. Twice."

"Then you know why I can't." His voice cracked on it, just barely, and he smoothed it flat again, fast. "The last time I called weather, people died, Mirabel. Good people. My—" He stopped. Swallowed the rest of the sentence whole. She watched it go down like a stone.

"My sister died," he finished, quieter. "And I swore I'd never touch the storm again. And I've kept that oath for twelve years, and keeping it has kept everyone safe, and you want me to break it for a gate."

"For sixty-one people," Mirabel said. "Eleven of them kids."

"I know the arithmetic."

"Do you?" She hugged her knees, chin on top, watching the lanterns sway below. "Because I think you're doing bad sums. I think you're counting yourself as zero."

Jonah looked at her. In the lamplight his eyes were the exact color of the sky before hail.

"Wren sent me," Mirabel said. "She sent me to you specifically. Out of everyone in the world, out of everywhere, she sent me to a charm-seller in Greyhollow who sells luck-knots that don't work. You think that's an accident? You think she spent—" Mirabel stopped herself. Almost said too much. Pulled it back. "You think she wasted her one chance on a mistake?"

Below them, the blue-flame man let the fire-birds loose, and a dozen children shrieked with delight and chased them into the dark, and the dark, for once, didn't seem like it was winning.

"I don't know what I think," Jonah said finally.

"That's the first honest thing you've said all week." Mirabel stood, brushing off her skirt. "Sable wants you at the sea-gate tomorrow, low tide. Wear boots you don't like."

She was three steps down the ledge when his voice caught her.

"Mirabel."

She turned.

"The locket." He was standing now too, and his hands had come unlaced, hanging open at his sides like he'd finally set something down. "When it shows you things. Does it ever show you—" He stopped. Started again, slower, like each word cost him. "Does it show you her? Wren? Is she—"

Mirabel held his eyes and felt the truth press up against the backs of her teeth, the whole enormous truth, the one Wren had sworn her to keep until the time was right, and the time was not right, and it might never be right, and she could not carry it alone much longer anyway.

"She's alive," Mirabel said. "That's all I can tell you tonight. She's alive, Jonah. And she needs you to remember how to be what you are."

She left him standing there in the lamplight, and she didn't look back, because looking back was how you saw whether your words had landed, and some things you had to throw and trust.

Down in the cavern, the last fire-bird circled twice and went out, and somewhere above them — faint, impossible, filtering down through forty feet of stone and city — Mirabel heard thunder.

Clear sky. No clouds. Just thunder, once, like something answering.
