## 7. Sable's Bargain

The ferrywoman's boat smelled of tar and old fish and something else—something that made the back of my teeth ache like a storm was coming. I hadn't felt that ache in twelve years. I'd built a whole life on never feeling it again.

"Sit," Sable Voss said, and I sat.

The Undertow lived beneath Greyhollow the way roots live beneath a tree. We'd come down through Mirabel's smuggler tunnels—she'd known every turn, every rotten ladder rung, every place where the brick gave way to older stone—and emerged into a cavern where the harbor tide breathed in and out through a gap in the rock. Lanterns hung from iron hooks. Laundry lines strung between pilings. A woman mending nets by candlelight. A boy no older than ten practicing letters on a slate. Somewhere deeper, someone was singing, low and off-key, a song about the sea giving back what it takes.

Maybe forty people. Maybe more, hidden in the warrens beyond the lantern light. All of them gifted. All of them unregistered. All of them, as of three nights ago, criminals under the Edict of Cleansing.

Mirabel sat beside me on an upturned crate, swinging her legs, still wearing that ridiculous oversized coat she'd crashed through my window in. The storm-mark on her wrist caught the lantern light—three silver lines, branching like lightning frozen mid-fall. My sister's mark. Wren's mark. I'd spent twelve years telling myself I'd never see it again except in memory, and here it was on a child who picked her teeth with a fishhook and called me "old man" like it was my name.

Sable Voss stood at the tiller of her boat even though the boat wasn't going anywhere. She was tall the way a mast is tall—all weathered wood and rope muscle—and she wore a leather patch over her left eye, and her right eye was the pale grey-green of harbor water in February. She looked at me the way harbormasters look at ships coming in during bad weather: measuring whether I'd sink or hold.

"You're Fairweather," she said.

"I'm a charm-seller."

"You're Fairweather." She said it flat, no question in it, which meant someone had already told her. Which meant word had moved through the tunnels faster than we had. "Storm-caller. Exile. The one whose storm killed the harvest-guard at Millbrook."

"The one whose storm—" I stopped. Swallowed. "Yes."

She nodded slowly, like she was weighing each word before letting it settle. "And you brought her here."

"I didn't bring her anywhere. She fell through my window."

"She led you through my tunnels," Sable said. "Don't flatter yourself, charm-seller. That girl knows this city's bones better than I do, and I've been crawling through them for thirty years." Her good eye flicked to Mirabel. "You vouch for him?"

"He burned his mother's grimoire," Mirabel said. "Page by page. In the fireplace. He cried but he did it anyway."

I hadn't realized she'd been awake for that. I'd thought she was asleep on my floor, curled up like a cat, while I fed twelve years of my family's history to the coals one page at a time so the Ministry wouldn't find it. Apparently she'd watched the whole thing. Apparently that was her idea of a character reference.

"That's not vouching," Sable said.

"It's true, though."

Sable almost smiled. Almost. It crossed her face like weather over water and was gone. Then she turned back to me, and whatever softness had visited her was replaced by something harder and much more honest.

"Here's how it is, Fairweather. You're sitting in the last warm room in Greyhollow. Every person in this cavern is worth a bounty now. A registered hunter gets paid by the head. Captain Ives's people get paid by the head *and* keep whatever they find. And you walked in here carrying the two most dangerous things in the city: a child with a king's memories locked around her neck, and a storm-caller who thinks he's a shopkeeper."

"I'm not—"

"You are. You just don't know it yet." She hopped down from the tiller seat, landed light for a big woman, and came close enough that I could smell the salt in her coat. "Twelve years ago you were the strongest weather-worker on the coast. My brother saw you call a squall off Cape Verity that turned a fleet of privateers around without sinking a single ship. Turned them around. Do you understand what control that takes? What *power*?"

"I know what my power does," I said. My voice came out lower than I intended. "It kills people I love."

The cavern had gone quiet around us. Not silent—the tide still breathed, the net-mender's needle still clicked—but the people had stopped their work to listen. They knew who I was. Of course they knew. News travels underground faster than above, and exiles always recognize each other.

Sable let the silence sit for exactly as long as it needed to. Then:

"Winter ice closes the harbor in six weeks. Maybe five. When it does, the Undertow is sealed in with whatever food we have stored, and right now that's eleven days' worth, because half our suppliers got scared after the Edict and stopped running the routes. There's an old sea-gate in the north breakwater—a gate the city built two hundred years ago and then abandoned when the harbor silted. It opens a channel wide enough for supply boats to reach us from the open water, past the ice line. The mechanism's rusted shut. Hasn't opened in a century. But there's a story that it was built to answer to storm-work—that the founders keyed it to weather-callers because they wanted a gate no army could force and no bureaucrat could sell."

"And you want me to open it."

"I want you to *try* to open it. Before the ice. If you fail, we starve slow instead of fast, and I'll have learned something about you either way."

"And if I succeed?"

"Then you and the girl get a bunk, a ration card, and a place at the table. Nobody touches you. Nobody sells you. Nobody breathes your name to a soul outside these tunnels. That's the bargain."

I looked at Mirabel. She was watching me with those sharp grey eyes, and there was something in her face I couldn't read—hope, maybe, or its crueler cousin, expectation. She believed I could do it. Children believe things like that. Children haven't stood in a burning field holding a dead friend's hand and felt their own gift humming in their chest like it was pleased.

"Sable," I said carefully, "do you know why I was exiled? Do you actually know, or do you just know the story?"

"I know the story."

"Then let me tell you the part the story leaves out." My hands had started shaking. I put them flat on my knees, an old trick, pressing the tremor into my thighs where no one could see it. "My storm didn't go wild because I was careless. It went wild because I was *angry*, and grief makes anger, and anger makes weather, and weather doesn't care who it loves. The harvest-guard died because I was fourteen years old and hurting and I reached for the sky without thinking about who was standing under it. My sister was standing under it. The man who taught me to fish was standing under it. Six people. Six graves. And the thing inside me that did it—it wasn't sorry. It *enjoyed* it. That's what nobody tells you about storms. They're not forces. They're appetites."

"So don't feed it," Sable said.

"It's not a dog. It's not a thing I can choose to feed or not feed. It's—" I searched for the words and hated every one I found. "It's me. The worst me. The me that wants. And I buried it, Sable. Twelve years. I sold luck-knots that were just knots. I felt rain coming and told myself it was my knees. I have spent twelve years being small on purpose, and it worked, and the proof that it worked is that I'm alive and everyone else who didn't hide is in a dungeon or a grave."

"Everyone else who didn't hide," Sable repeated. She looked around the cavern—at the net-mender, at the boy with the slate, at the singer in the dark. "You see all this? None of these people hid well enough. Tam the hedge-healer hid for twenty years. He grew herbs in window boxes. He kept his head down so hard he walked bent over. They took him anyway, second night of the Edict. His daughter's in the back warren right now, sleeping in a hammock because her bed is evidence." Sable's voice didn't rise. It didn't need to. "Hiding worked, Fairweather. Right up until it didn't. And now it doesn't anymore—for anyone. So the question isn't whether staying small will save you. Staying small is dead. The question is what you're going to be instead."

* * *

They gave us a corner of the cavern to sleep in—two hammocks slung between pilings, close enough to the tide-gap that we could hear the water all night. Mirabel fell asleep almost immediately, the way children do, all at once, like a lamp blown out. I lay awake listening to the harbor breathe.

In. Out. In. Out.

Twelve years ago I would have found that rhythm comforting. Now it sounded like a countdown.

I thought about the sea-gate. I'd seen it once, years ago, from the top of the north breakwater, back when I still walked the walls at dawn because walking was the only exercise hiding allowed me. It was a great arch of black stone set into the breakwater's root, big enough to drive a cart through, crusted with barnacles and salt-stain, and set into the arch were channels—grooves cut deep into the rock in spiraling patterns, like the inside of a shell. I'd assumed they were decorative. Everyone assumed they were decorative. But Sable said the founders had keyed it to storm-work, and looking back at it now through memory, those grooves didn't look decorative. They looked like instructions. Like a lock waiting for a very specific key.

Like a lock waiting for me.

I turned the thought over and over and it cut me every time I touched it.

Because here was the truth I hadn't said out loud, not to Sable, not to Mirabel, not to anyone: some part of me wanted to say yes. Some part of me had been wanting to say yes for twelve years, lying awake in my shop above the chandler's, feeling pressure systems move across the coast through the soles of my feet, feeling the storm in my chest turn over in its sleep like something dreaming. I had told myself the burial was peace. It wasn't peace. It was suffocation, done slowly enough that I'd mistaken it for calm.

And here was the other truth, the one underneath: I was terrified that if I reached for it—if I let it out even a little, even for a gate, even for a good reason—I wouldn't be able to put it back. That the appetite would wake up hungry. That I'd stand in front of forty people who trusted me and feel that old pleasure rising in my chest, the pleasure I'd felt at fourteen, the sky answering me like a lover, and that this time there'd be no field, no fire, no exile to stop me from taking everything the storm offered.

Power doesn't corrupt. I'd heard that saying my whole life and never believed it until I'd had power and lost it and spent a decade learning what its absence cost. Power doesn't corrupt. Power *reveals*. It shows you what you'd do if nothing could stop you, and at fourteen, nothing could stop me, and what I'd done was kill six people and run.

But Sable was right too. That was the trap of it. Both things were true. Hiding was dead. Smallness was dead. The Edict had made sure of that—every gifted person in Greyhollow was going to be found, registered, chained, or worse, and the only variable left was whether we met it alone or together, kneeling or standing.

And Mirabel. God help me, Mirabel. She was asleep ten feet away wearing my sister's mark, carrying a locket full of a king's stolen life, hunted by men paid by the head. I could not keep her safe by selling charms. I could not keep her safe by being small. Being small was how Tam had died. Being small was a strategy with a body count, and the next body it counted might be hers.

* * *

Near dawn, I gave up on sleep and climbed down from the hammock. The cavern was dim, the lanterns burned low, and the tide was pulling out with a long sighing sound. I found Sable at the mouth of the tide-gap, drinking something from a tin cup, watching the grey water.

"Couldn't sleep either?" she asked, without turning.

"I slept fine."

"Liar. Charm-sellers are terrible liars. Too used to customers who want to believe." She handed me the cup. It was tea, barely, mostly hot water and hope. I drank it anyway.

"I'll do it," I said.

She didn't answer right away. She took the cup back, looked at the water, looked at me.

"You sure?"

"No."

"Good. Sure men get people killed. Scared men check their work twice." She drained the cup and crushed it flat against her thigh with one hand, an idle display of strength that I suspected was not idle at all. "When?"

"When the ice starts forming. Not before. If I open the channel early, it silts shut again within the month, and the hunters will have noticed a hundred-foot arch of black stone suddenly working. We open it late, we use it fast, we get the supplies through, and by the time anyone asks questions the ice has closed the door behind us."

Sable grunted. "You've thought about this."

"I've thought about nothing else since you said it."

"One condition." She stepped closer, and her good eye held mine with the flat patience of a woman who had buried friends. "You don't practice. You don't test it. You don't stand on the breakwater feeling the wind and 'seeing what comes.' The first time you touch your storm in twelve years will be at that gate, with a plan, with people positioned, with boats ready, and with me standing next to you holding a knife—not for you, for *it*, if it takes you. There's a rite for that. Old one. Weather-workers used to carry it like sailors carry a final prayer. If the storm takes you, I end it quick, and your body goes to the sea, and no one blames anyone. Can you live with that?"

Can I live with that. The question was so absurd I almost laughed. Live with a stranger's knife held in reserve against the thing living in my chest. Live knowing that the price of trying to save forty people might be dying on a rusted gate with salt water in my lungs. Live—

"Yes," I said. And found, to my surprise, that I meant it. Because that was the thing about terror: once you agreed to carry it, it got lighter. It was the fighting that made it heavy.

Sable studied me a moment longer, then nodded once, sharp, like a knot pulled tight. "Then we have a bargain. Five weeks. I'll get you the gate's old schematics from a man who owes me. You'll learn that gate the way you'd learn a face. And Fairweather—" she paused at the mouth of the tunnel, half-turned, the lantern light catching the edge of her eyepatch, "—the girl stays with me while you work. Not as hostage. As student. That mark on her wrist isn't decoration either, and if you're going to bring a storm home, somebody ought to teach her what storms cost."

Behind me, from the direction of the hammocks, a small voice said, "I already know what storms cost."

Mirabel stood barefoot on the cold stone, hair a black tangle, the locket clutched in one fist against her chest. She must have been awake the whole time. She was always awake the whole time. She looked at me with an expression far too old for her face, and she said:

"My storm cost me everything too. That's why I'm here."

And then, before either of us could answer, the locket in her fist began to hum—loud, insistent, a sound like a struck bell underwater—and the tide at the mouth of the cavern stopped breathing, and every lantern in the Undertow guttered at once, as if something vast had drawn a breath on the other side of the world.
