## 8. First Thunder

The locket's hum faded the way a scream fades—reluctantly, leaving silence behind like a bruise. The lanterns steadied. The tide remembered how to breathe.

Sable Voss stood at the mouth of her own cavern, one hand braced on the hull of her ferry, and looked at me with her single eye as if I were a ledger that had finally balanced.

"Well," she said. "That settles it."

"Settles what?"

"The sea-gate." She jerked her chin toward the black water beyond the harbor mouth, where winter was already laying its first thin skin of ice across the swells. "You heard it same as I did. That thing out there knows she's here. Knows what she's carrying. Which means we've got maybe three days before it stops sending hunters and starts sending something worse. Three days to open the gate, bring in the winter stores, and seal this place up tight." She turned that eye on me fully, and I felt it like a hook through the chest. "Three days for you to call your storm, Fairweather."

"I told you no."

"You told me you were afraid. Different thing."

"It's not different," Mirabel said quietly.

We both looked at her. She stood with her arms crossed, bare feet still on the cold stone, and there was something in her voice that wasn't defiance exactly. It was recognition. The way you'd recognize your own handwriting on a stranger's letter.

"You're afraid of it because it wants things," she said. "I know. Mine does too. It sits under my ribs at night and it *wants*, and if I let it, it would take and take and take." She shrugged, a small sharp motion. "But wanting isn't the same as doing. And hiding isn't the same as being safe. You taught me that. Or you will. I've seen it."

"I haven't taught you anything yet," I said.

"No," she agreed. "But I remember it anyway."

The locket did that sometimes—spilled other people's yesterdays into her mouth. Sable and I exchanged a look over the girl's head, and in that look we were briefly allies: two adults standing at the edge of something neither of us wanted to name.

* * *

They gave me two days to refuse.

I spent them the way I'd spent twelve years: uselessly, deliberately, with great care. I mended nets that didn't need mending. I re-sorted my charm stall inventory by a system only I understood. I sat with Tam's widow, Marta, who had come down into the Undertow after the hunters took him, and drank tea that tasted of seaweed and grief while she told me stories about a man who healed fevers with nothing but boiled willow and stubbornness.

"He never once used the gift," she said. "Not in thirty years. Said magic was a debt you pay with interest." She wrapped her hands around her cup. "He registered anyway, in the end. Thought honesty would protect him." Her laugh was terrible. "Honesty."

I didn't tell her about the locket, or the Regent, or the thing wearing Aldric's face. Some truths are just cruelty wearing a coat.

On the second night, Mirabel found me on the ferry dock, sitting where the water lapped against the pilings, watching ice form in slow motion at the harbor mouth—a lacework of white creeping outward from the rocks, sealing the Undertow's throat one crystal at a time.

She sat down beside me without asking. She was getting good at that.

"My village drowned when I was six," she said. "Before Wren found me."

I went very still.

"She pulled me out of the water herself. Carried me three miles to the edge of the Veil-lands, and she was bleeding the whole way, and she never once put me down." Mirabel swung her feet above the dark water. "She talked the whole time so I wouldn't be scared. About storms. She said storms weren't angry, they were just *lonely*. That they gathered and gathered because nobody ever asked them what they needed."

My throat closed. Of course she'd said that. Wren had been saying it since we were children, back when our mother's grimoire lay open between us and we took turns pressing our palms flat against the pages, feeling the weather move underneath like a sleeping animal.

"What else do you remember?" I managed.

"That's all. Just that. It's the only memory I have that's mine from before." She glanced sideways at me. "Sable says you'll do it tomorrow. At slack tide. She says you'll do it because you're the kind of man who does the frightening thing when children are watching."

"Sable is wrong."

"Sable is never wrong. It's infuriating." A pause. The ice creaked, spreading. "Jonah. What happens if you call it and it doesn't stop?"

And there it was—the question I'd been swallowing for twelve years, laid out plain between us by an eleven-year-old with my sister's mark burning faintly blue on her wrist.

"Then people drown," I said. "People drowned last time. People I loved. The storm doesn't distinguish, Mirabel. It's not a tool. It's a *hunger* with my face on it, and every time I've fed it, it's come back for more."

She considered this with the gravity of someone twice her age.

"Wren said storms are lonely," she said finally. "Maybe yours has been lonely twelve years. Maybe that's why it's hungry." She stood, brushed off her knees, and looked down at me with those too-old eyes. "Tomorrow, when you call it—don't call it like a weapon. Call it like you're sorry. Like you're asking it home."

Then she walked back up the dock toward the lantern light, small and straight-backed, and left me alone with the ice and the water and the sound of my own heart trying to climb out of my chest.

* * *

Slack tide came gray and windless, the worst possible hour, the sea lying flat as hammered tin as if it already knew what was coming and wanted no part of it.

The smugglers assembled at the sea-gate in silence—nine of them, hard-faced men and women who'd survived the Edict by being quick and quiet and lucky, now betting everything on a charm-seller who hadn't touched weather in twelve years. They loaded the first longboat in the dark, crates of salt-pork and lamp-oil and medicine stacked to the gunwales. Beyond the gate, the ice sheet stretched a hundred yards to open water, thick enough to walk on, thick enough to trap them if it closed again behind.

Sable stood at the gate mechanism, hand on the ancient winch that would raise the portcullis of black iron once the way was clear. Her eye found mine across the water.

"Fairweather." No mockery in it now. "Whatever happens—thank you for coming."

"Don't thank me yet."

"Wasn't going to." She almost smiled. "Was going to say: aim for the middle of the ice. Crack it wide. We'll handle the rest."

Mirabel stood beside me on the gate platform, close enough that I could feel the heat of her, the strange static hum that lived in her skin. The mark on her wrist glowed faintly, patiently, like a banked fire.

"How do I start?" I asked her. Not Sable. Not the smugglers. Her.

She thought about it seriously, the way she thought about everything.

"When I'm scared," she said, "I put my hand somewhere warm and I breathe until the scared gets smaller than the wanting." She held out her wrist. "Here."

I took her arm. The mark flared under my palm—not hot, not cold, but *familiar*, the way a song is familiar, the way a house you grew up in is familiar even after the roof falls in. And beneath my skin, deep down where I'd buried it under twelve years of useless charms and careful smallness, something turned over in its sleep.

It opened one eye.

*I said don't—* I began, and then stopped, because the old words were wrong. All the old words were wrong. This wasn't a leash or a cage or a weapon in the cellar. This was—

*Lonely,* said the storm, in the wordless way storms say things. *So long. So long.*

"Oh," I breathed. "Oh, you poor thing."

I raised my free hand, and I called it home.

* * *

I want to tell you it was gentle. I want to tell you the clouds gathered politely and the rain fell softly and everyone clapped.

What happened was this: the sky tore open.

Not gradually. Not with warning. One moment the gray ceiling hung low and dead, and the next a column of cloud spun down out of nothing like a fist closing, and the pressure dropped so fast my ears popped, and the first thunderclap didn't roll—it *detonated*, directly overhead, close enough to feel in my teeth, and every soul on the dock hit the stone except Mirabel, whose grip on my arm tightened and whose eyes went wide and bright and utterly unafraid.

The wind came next. It came the way grief comes—all at once, from every direction, screaming. It snatched the tarps off the longboat, snapped the mooring lines of the ferry, sent crates skidding across the dock. Rain followed, horizontal, needling, and then the lightning, and the lightning was the worst part, because it wasn't striking down at the sea.

It was striking *toward me*.

Fork after fork, crawling out of the boiling cloud, bending midair, curving home to the place where my hand met the air like iron filings to a lodestone. The storm knew me. The storm had been waiting twelve years in whatever country storms wait in, and it had *opinions*, and the opinions were: *more, more, give me more, I have been empty so long, fill me, fill me, FILL ME—*

"Jonah!" Sable's voice, raw with alarm. The ice at the harbor mouth was groaning, heaving—but not breaking clean. The swell was rising, waves stacking up under the sheet, and the longboat, half-loaded, was lifting and slamming against the dock timbers with cracks like rifle shots. A smuggler went down; another grabbed his collar and hauled him back from the edge as a wave broke green over the gate platform. "It's too much! You're drowning us—you have to pull it back—"

*I can't.* The thought arrived cold and certain. *I can't pull it back. I never could. That's the whole story of my life—I opened the door and the door ate the harvest-guard and it ate Wren and it will eat these people too, it will eat them all, this is what I am, this is what I've always—*

And then Mirabel stepped in front of me.

Small. Barefoot. Rain streaming off her chin. She put both hands flat against my chest, right over my hammering heart, and the mark on her wrist blazed blue-white, and she said—not shouted, *said*, in a voice that cut through the gale like a blade through silk:

"*Listen to it.*"

And I did.

Beneath the hunger, beneath the greed and the roar and the twelve years of starvation, I heard it. The thing underneath. The small true note at the center of the storm's vast noise, and it sounded like this:

*You left. You left, you left, you locked me away and you left, and I thought you died, I thought everyone always dies, please, please don't leave again—*

It wasn't rage. It had never been rage.

It was a dog scratching at a door.

"Oh," I said again, and this time the word broke in the middle. My eyes were stinging and it wasn't the rain. "I'm here. I'm here. I'm not leaving. But you have to be *gentle*, do you understand me? Gentle, like—like holding something alive."

The storm shuddered. The whole sky shuddered, a vast convulsion of cloud and light—and then, slowly, like a great animal settling itself down limb by limb, it began to *listen*.

The wind dropped from a scream to a shout to a voice. The rain softened from needles to sheets to a steady, drumming fall. The lightning stopped reaching for me and went instead where I asked—down, and out, and *into the ice*, one measured bolt, then another, striking the harbor sheet dead center with cracks that ran outward in beautiful branching rivers, white fractures blooming across the frozen surface like frost-flowers opening in fast-forward.

The ice groaned. Split. Heaved apart with a sound like the world's largest door unlocking.

Open water. Black and breathing, stretching from the sea-gate all the way to the horizon, steaming faintly where the warm rain struck the cold swells.

For a moment nobody moved. Nine smugglers stood soaked and staring, and Sable Voss stood with her hand still frozen on the winch, and the rain fell gently, gently, on all of us, warm as a blessing.

Then Sable started to laugh—a low, disbelieving bark that cracked into something almost like tears. "Well," she called over the water. "There's a sight. Twelve years I figured you for a coward, Fairweather. Turns out you're worse." She was already moving, shouting orders, the longboat pushing off into the open channel with the first load of supplies. "Turns out you're a man who makes friends with the weather!"

Mirabel hadn't let go of my chest. I looked down at her, drenched and radiant, and she looked up at me, and something passed between us that had no words in it—or rather, had words I wasn't ready to say. Because her mark was fading now, dimming back to its quiet ember-glow, and as it dimmed I saw—just for a heartbeat, just a flicker, there and gone—

Another mark. Beneath hers. Older. The same spiral, the same storm-shape, but drawn in a surer hand.

Wren's hand.

"Mira," I started. "Your wrist—"

"Later," she said.

The word landed like a stone in still water. Later. Which meant *there was a later*. Which meant there was something to tell, and she knew what it was, and she was choosing the moment, the way she chose everything—with a patience far too old for her body.

Above us, the storm rumbled off toward the open sea, sated and subdued, trailing soft thunder like a cat carrying its kill. The longboats moved through the broken channel, one after another, laden and living. The Undertow would eat this winter after all.

I stood on the gate platform in the warm rain with a child's hands still pressed against my heart, and I understood, with terrible clarity, that everything had changed. The storm knew me now. It would come when I called, and it would come *hungry*, and someday soon I would have to call it for something larger than a channel through the ice.

And Mirabel was keeping a secret shaped like my sister's name.

She smiled up at me, rain-bright, and said:

"Told you. Ask it home."

Behind us, far out past the harbor mouth, where the new-open water met the gray horizon, the surface of the sea bulged once—briefly, silently—something vast turning over in the deep, taking notice.

Then it sank, and the horizon was only a horizon, and the rain kept falling, and none of us saw it but the storm.
