## 9. Hunters at the Door

The Undertow smelled like wet stone and lamp oil and, underneath it all, like people who had decided to keep living anyway.

Mirabel learned its smells before she learned its names. That was how she did everything—nose first, then eyes, then words. Words were the last thing she trusted about a place, because words could be taught to lie. Smells couldn't. The ferrywoman's tunnels smelled of river silt and tallow and someone's stew gone cold on a ledge, and when Mirabel pressed her palm flat against the tunnel wall she could feel the harbor breathing through it, slow and salt-deep, like something enormous asleep.

She had been in the Undertow for nine days. She was counting, though she didn't tell anyone she was counting. Nine days of sleeping on a hammock strung between two pipe-stanchions instead of in doorways. Nine days of eating stew that had meat in it more often than not. Nine days of Jonah Fairweather looking at her wrist when he thought she wasn't watching, his face doing that thing where it tried to be three faces at once and failed at all of them.

Nine days of belonging somewhere, which was the part that scared her worst.

"You're brooding again." Sable Voss dropped onto the crate beside her, one eye catching the lamplight, the other a puckered seam of old scar. She was mending a net in her lap without looking at it, her fingers working the knots the way other people worked prayer beads. "Brooding's allowed down here, mind. It's practically currency. But you do it loud."

"I don't do anything loud," Mirabel said.

"Girl, you breathe loud. You sit loud. You've been sitting on that crate for an hour glaring at the water like it owes you money." Sable bit off a thread. "What is it?"

Mirabel didn't answer right away. Below them, along the flooded channel that gave the Undertow its name, the ferrywoman's people moved through their evening: a fire-eater teaching two children to hold flame in cupped palms without burning; a hedge-witch stirring something in a cauldron that steamed purple and smelled like sleep; Tam's cousin, a broad quiet man named Doss, hauling crates up from the supply skiff that had come through the sea-gate Jonah had opened. Jonah's storm had opened. The thought still made her chest feel strange, like a door swinging on rusted hinges.

"The locket hummed last night," Mirabel said finally. "It hasn't done that since we came down here."

Sable's hands went still over the net. Just for a moment. Then they kept moving, steady as tide. "Hummed how?"

"Like before. Like when the hunters were close in Greyhollow." Mirabel touched the locket through her shirt. It lay warm against her sternum, warmer than skin had any right to be, and inside it the king's stolen memories shifted and murmured like fish under ice. "Something's coming. Or looking."

"Looking for what?"

"For me." She said it flatly because saying it any other way would have cracked her open. "For this place. I brought it here, Sable. I led Jonah straight down into the one hole in Greyhollow they haven't searched yet, and now it's going to get searched."

Sable set the net down. When she turned, her whole face turned with her, scarred side and unscarred side together, and her one eye held Mirabel with the patience of deep water. "You listen to me, girl. I've been running this undertow for nineteen years. You know how many times hunters have come close? Eleven. You know how many times they found us?" Her mouth twisted. "None. Not once. And every time, it wasn't because we hid well. It's because we watched well. There are forty pairs of eyes between here and the surface, and every one of them belongs to somebody the Edict already took everything from. Nobody down here misses footsteps."

"But if they find us—"

"If they find us, then we were always going to be found, and it wouldn't have mattered whether you'd come or stayed in a doorway freezing." Sable picked the net back up. "That's the arithmetic of hiding, Mirabel. You can't spend guilt you don't owe. Now go find Jonah. He's been pacing the east landing like a man waiting on a letter, and you're the only thing that slows him down."

Mirabel slid off the crate. At the mouth of the east passage she stopped, because stopping there had become habit in nine days, and looked back. Sable was already mending again, lamplight pooling gold in the hollow of her collarbone, and around her the Undertow went on with its small stubborn ceremonies of living—the fire-children laughing, the cauldron steaming, Doss singing something low and off-key—and Mirabel thought, with a clarity that felt like falling: *I could lose all of this. I am the reason it can be lost.*

She didn't know yet how right she was.

* * *

Jonah was on the east landing, and he was indeed pacing, and the moment he saw her his whole body changed direction like a compass needle finding north.

"There you are." He crouched to her level, which he always did, which had taken some getting used to after twelve years of adults who only ever looked down. "Sable sent you?"

"Sable said you were pacing."

"I was conferring with the floor."

"It looked like pacing."

"It was a very serious conference." But he wasn't smiling properly, and she could see the tiredness sitting behind his eyes like sediment. He'd been sleeping badly since the sea-gate. Since the storm. She'd felt it through the wall of the room they shared—him turning over, turning over, the way a man turns when his own skin doesn't fit. "Mirabel. The locket. Has it done anything today?"

"Hummed. Last night. Around the third bell."

He exhaled slowly through his nose. "Mine too. Well—not mine. My charms. All forty of them in my stall stock, humming at once, like struck bells. Something above us is pulling, and everything with magic in it is ringing." He rubbed his jaw. "Ives is sweeping the river district. Tam told me—I mean, before they took him, Tam said Ives had a method. He walks a grid. He lets the gifted feel him coming, because fear makes them glow, and then he follows the glow."

"So we run."

"And go where?" Jonah gestured vaguely upward, toward the city, toward the world. "There's no out anymore, Mirabel. The Edict closed the roads. The ports are watched. Every town between here and the mountains has a Ministry office now." His voice dropped. "This is the last dry ground. If it goes under, we swim or we drown."

"Then we should decide which," Mirabel said, "before the water decides for us."

Jonah looked at her then—really looked, the way he did when she said something older than her years, which was often, because her years were strange ones—and whatever he saw made him nod slowly. "When did you get so sensible?"

"When everyone started hunting me."

* * *

They came at the fourth bell of the morning, when the Undertow slept deepest.

Mirabel woke to silence, which was wrong, because the Undertow never slept silent—it breathed, it murmured, it snored and muttered and turned over in its hammocks. The silence had a shape, and the shape was absence, and she sat up in her hammock with her heart already hammering before she knew why.

Then the lamps died.

Not flickered. Died. All of them, down the whole length of the flooded channel, in one breath, as if a great hand had pinched out forty flames at once. And in the dark Mirabel heard the locket begin to sing—a thin high note, keening, the king's memories thrashing inside it like drowned men—and she heard, far above, through sixty feet of stone and earth and brick, the sound of boots.

Many boots. In step.

"No," she whispered. "No, no, no—"

She was moving before she finished the word, bare feet slapping wet stone, down the passage toward the main channel, and she collided with Jonah in the dark and grabbed his arm hard enough to bruise. "They're here. Jonah, they're *here*, they came at fourth bell, they killed the lamps—"

"How—" His voice was stripped calm, the calm of a man falling back into an old emergency. "How many?"

"I don't know. Many. In step. Soldiers' steps."

Above them, muffled by stone, a voice rang out with the flat authority of a proclamation: *"By order of His Majesty King Aldric, in accordance with the Edict of Cleansing: surrender the unregistered gifted. Surrender them, and the crown's mercy is yours. Resist, and the crown's justice is yours instead."*

And then, closer, another voice—dry as paper, patient as rot:

"They're below the waterline. Old smuggler runs off the ferry stairs. Seal the stairwells. Flood the side channels if you must. Nothing leaves by water tonight."

Mirabel knew that voice. She had heard it through a window in Greyhollow, through a door, through her own nightmares. Captain Ives. The man whose eyes had gone pale and empty as boiled glass, the man who ate memories the way the Regent ate kingdoms. He was directly overhead. He was going to flood the channels.

"Jonah." Her voice cracked. "The water. He's going to bring the water in."

* * *

The Undertow woke all at once, the way a hive wakes when smoke hits it.

Lamps relit in stuttering clusters—someone had charm-spares, someone always had charm-spares—and by their light Mirabel saw the community assembling itself out of sleep and terror: Doss hauling children up from the lower berths, the fire-eater pressing flame-charms into every free hand, the hedge-witch throwing warding-salts across the passage mouths where they burst into weak blue rings that would hold maybe a minute against a determined soldier. Forty people. Maybe fifty. Half of them children or old. Against a captain who could smell memory and a garrison of Ministry regulars with sealed exits and flooding channels.

Sable Voss stood at the center of it on the ferry platform, and she had never looked calmer.

"Listen!" Her voice carried the way a bell carries, cutting through panic like a blade through cloth. "Side channels flood in minutes—we go up the main run, the old coal chute, single file, fast and quiet. Doss takes point with the little ones. Hedge-witch, salts on the rear. Everyone else, you carry someone or something. We have done this drill. We will do it now." She caught Mirabel's eye across the crowd, and her one eye held steady. "Girl. Jonah. With me."

They fought their way to her through the press of bodies. Up close, Mirabel could see what the calm cost her—Sable's hands were shaking, just slightly, as she pulled a ring of iron keys from her coat.

"Coal chute comes up behind the ropewalk, half a mile east," she said rapidly. "You'll come out in a tanner's yard. There's a cart. There's always a cart." She pressed the keys into Jonah's hand and closed his fingers over them. "Get her out of the city. North road, then off it—there's a chapel ruin at Harrow's Bend, eight miles, the roof still stands. Wait for word."

"Wait for—" Jonah's face went white. "Wait for *word*. Sable, you're coming with us."

"I'm staying." She said it simply, the way you'd say the tide was coming in. "Someone has to stand on this platform and look like the whole Undertow is still down here while fifty people climb a coal chute. Someone has to buy the time. I'm the one they'll want to question." Her mouth quirked. "One-eyed ferrywoman, known associate of every smuggler on the coast. I'm *fascinating*. They'll spend hours on me. Hours you'll need."

"No." Mirabel grabbed her sleeve. "No, you can't—they'll take you, they'll—"

"Girl." Sable crouched, slow, until they were eye to eye, and her hand came up and cupped the back of Mirabel's head the way nobody had since before she could remember. Her palm was rough and warm and it trembled, just barely, and Mirabel understood suddenly that the trembling wasn't fear. It was everything Sable wasn't letting herself feel, held in one small shaking place so it wouldn't spill. "Nineteen years I've kept this place. Nineteen years, and do you know what I learned? A home isn't a hole in the ground. A home is whoever climbs out of it alive." She pressed her forehead briefly against Mirabel's. "You climb. You live. You make me worth the price. That's the whole bargain."

"I can't—"

"You can. You're storm-marked. You're the most important person in this tunnel and you don't even know why yet." She stood, and the trembling was gone, packed away, and she was the ferrywoman again, iron and tide. "Now GO."

Boots on the ferry stairs. Blue warding-rings flaring and dying. Somewhere a child screaming and Doss's big hand muffling it gently, lifting, carrying. The hedge-witch hurling her last salts in a sheet of blue fire that bought thirty seconds and cost her everything she had left. And Sable Voss walking toward the stairs with her lantern raised high, her one eye blazing, her voice ringing out clear and carrying in the dark:

"DOWN HERE, MINISTRY! Your quarry's on the ferry platform—come and get your mercy!"

* * *

The coal chute was black and narrow and smelled of a hundred years of coal, and they climbed it in the dark by touch and terror, fifty souls breathing in the walls of it.

Mirabel went third from the front, Jonah behind her with a child on his back, and all the way up she could hear, faint through the stone, the sounds of the Undertow dying. Boots. Shouting. The crash of the ferry platform being torn apart. Once, horribly, Sable's voice—defiant, laughing, buying seconds with every word—and then a sound like the air itself being wrung out, and silence where the laughter had been.

Mirabel climbed faster. She didn't let herself think about what that silence meant. She would think about it later, or she would never think about it, or it would live in her chest forever like a swallowed coal. Later. Climb. Live. Make her worth the price.

They came out of the chute into freezing pre-dawn dark, into a tanner's yard stinking of lime and hides, and there was a cart, because there was always a cart, and Doss loaded children into it with hands that shook and a face like carved wood. Jonah counted heads twice, lips moving, and Mirabel watched him arrive at the number and watched the number break something in him.

"Fifty-one," he said hoarsely. "We were fifty-three."

The cart rolled east through streets gone grey with ash-snow, past Ministry proclamations nailed to every post, past criers already shouting the morning's news—that the notorious smuggler queen of the Undertow had been taken alive, that the hunt continued, that the king's mercy was infinite and the king's patience was not.

Mirabel rode in the cart bed among the children, holding a small fire-eater girl who cried herself to sleep against her shoulder, and she stared at the ash falling soft and endless out of a sky the color of old bruises, and she did the arithmetic Sable had forbidden.

*I led them here. I brought the glow. I was the coal that lit the seam.* The locket lay cold and silent against her chest now, its work done, its song sung, and she hated it, and she hugged it tighter. *They took her because of me. Because I fell through a window and wouldn't stay small.*

The girl in her arms stirred. "Is the ferry lady dead?" she whispered.

"I don't know," Mirabel whispered back, because lying to children was a luxury she refused, even now. "But she told us to live. So we're going to live so hard it makes her furious."

Ahead, at the edge of the city, the north road opened white and empty under the falling ash, and somewhere eight miles along it a ruined chapel waited with its broken roof full of sky. Jonah sat at the front of the cart with Sable's iron keys clenched in his fist, staring back the way they'd come, and Mirabel saw his lips move once, shaping a name.

In her pocket, unnoticed by anyone, the locket had begun—very softly—to hum again.

A different tune this time. Almost like a lullaby.
