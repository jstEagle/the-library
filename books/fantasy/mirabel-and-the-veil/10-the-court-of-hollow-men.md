## 10. The Court of Hollow Men

The clerk's coat smelled of someone else's sweat and someone else's ambition. Jonah had bought it off a dying man in the Undertow's lower rooms—Sable Voss had called it a loan, though the man was too far gone with fever to ever ask for it back—and it fit him the way a lie fits: snug enough to pass, loose enough to strangle him if he moved wrong.

Greyhollow's palace had been a lighthouse once, back before the city grew up around it. You could still see the bones of it, the great spiral stair that wound where the beacon-stair used to climb, the windows angled to catch a sea that no longer touched them. Now it caught clerks instead. Hundreds of them, pouring through the gates at dawn with ledgers clutched to their chests like shields, all of them gray-coated, gray-faced, gray-eyed with the particular exhaustion of men who processed other people's ruin for a living.

Jonah joined the river of them at the Fishgate, head down, ink-stained fingers—stained that morning with soot and walnut oil, a trick from his mother's grimoire, one page he'd saved from the burning for exactly this kind of use—and a ledger under his arm filled with nothing. Blank pages. The most honest thing he'd carried into that building.

*You are Tobias Merle,* he told himself, matching his stride to the clerk ahead of him, a stoop-shouldered man who coughed between every third step. *Third-floor records. You have been doing this for nine years. You are invisible.*

Invisible. He'd spent twelve years becoming invisible in Greyhollow, small and dull and harmless, a charm-seller whose charms never worked because working charms left traces. He had thought himself an expert on being no one. But walking through those gates, past guards in the king's black-and-silver who checked faces against lists, past a crier still hoarse from reading the Edict of Cleansing, Jonah understood he had only ever been *hidden*. This was different. This was walking into the wolf's mouth wearing the shape of something already swallowed.

The guard at the inner door barely looked at him. "Records?" the guard said, and Jonah said "Records," and that was the whole conversation. Nine years of Tobias Merle's tedium bought more safety than twelve years of Jonah Fairweather's caution ever had. There was a lesson in that, and he didn't like it.

Inside, the palace breathed. That was the word for it—he couldn't find another. The corridors were warm in a way stone shouldn't be, warm like a throat, and the air had a taste to it, faint and metallic, like a coin held too long in the mouth. Like rust. He passed a window and saw the snow falling outside, black-flecked, wrong, each flake drifting down as if it had all the time in the world and intended to use it.

He had come for two things: confirmation, and a way in. Sable Voss sat in a cell beneath this building, and Mirabel hadn't slept since, and Ives—the captain, half-eaten, half-himself—had whispered his warning through the bars of a smuggler's door three nights ago: *It means to finish. It means to wear us all.* Jonah could not fight what he could not see. So he would see it.

The Regent held court on Thursdays. Petition day. Every seventh day the thing wearing Aldric's crown descended from its apartments and listened to the grievances of Greyhollow, and every seventh day the city held its breath and hoped its king would be gentle.

Jonah followed the flow of clerks upward, into the long gallery where petitioners waited, and found a place against a pillar where a records-man might plausibly stand with his blank ledger and his blank face.

* * *

They brought the king out at midmorning.

Jonah heard him before he saw him—a sound like wind through a keyhole, thin and high, and he realized it was the court itself going quiet, hundreds of people inhaling at once and forgetting to exhale.

Aldric came down the beacon-stair slowly, and the court bowed, and Jonah bowed, and then Jonah made the mistake of looking up.

He had prepared himself for a monster. He had spent three sleepless nights building the thing in his mind: scales, or smoke, or eyes like drowned lanterns, some obvious horror he could name and hate cleanly. What stood at the foot of the stair was worse than any of that, because it was almost right. A tall man, broad-shouldered, wearing the king's black-and-silver and the iron circlet of Greyhollow. A face that was, by every measurement, the king's face.

But it fit badly. That was the only way Jonah could describe it, even inside his own skull. The face fit the man the way a coat fits a scarecrow—as if there were something underneath that wasn't quite the shape the clothes expected. When the Regent smiled at the first petitioner, the smile arrived a half-second late, like thunder after lightning, like an echo that had traveled too far to still believe in the voice that made it.

And the eyes. The eyes were the color of weathered iron, and they did not move when the Regent looked at things. They stayed fixed while the head turned around them, the way a portrait's eyes stay fixed, and Jonah felt the hair rise on his arms because he had seen eyes like that before—in the fever-charms he sold, in the little painted tokens grieving mothers bought, eyes that looked without seeing, crafted to comfort the living by pretending the dead still watched.

The Regent was not pretending. It was watching. It watched everything, and it kept none of it, and somehow that was worse.

"Petitioner," it said, and its voice was Aldric's voice, deep and measured, but played slightly too fast, like a song remembered by someone who'd forgotten the tune. "Step forward."

The petitioner was a fishwife, broad and sunburned, holding her hat in both hands. Her husband's boat had been seized under the Edict—her son was gifted, she said, just a little, just weather-sense like his grandmother, and the hunters had taken the boy and the boat and the nets besides, and could His Majesty say where the boy had been taken, could he say if the boy yet lived?

She got through maybe half of it before her voice broke. And Jonah, pressed against his pillar, watched the Regent of Rust listen to a mother beg for her child, and he watched it do the cruelest thing he could imagine.

It leaned forward. It attended. Every line of that borrowed body bent toward her with perfect, practiced compassion, and its iron eyes drank her in—not her words, Jonah realized, not her grief, but the *memory* of it. He could see it happening, could see the faint shimmer in the air between them, the way heat shimmers over a summer road, except this shimmer flowed one direction. Toward the throne. The woman's face went slack as she spoke, her words losing their edges, her anger softening into confusion, her grief into a vague, pleasant fog. By the end she was smiling. She thanked the king for his kindness. She could not remember why she had come.

"Your petition is granted," the Regent said warmly, to a woman who no longer had one. "Next."

The gallery exhaled. Somewhere, a clerk wrote: *Petitions heard: four. All resolved amicably.*

Jonah pressed his knuckles against his teeth and thought about Tam the hedge-healer, dragged from his rooms in the night. Thought about the boy on the fishwife's boat, wherever the hunters kept such boys. Thought about a king who ate his people's sorrow so thoroughly they thanked him for the meal.

*I came for confirmation,* he told himself. *You have it. Now find the tower and go.*

He did not go. Because the Regent turned its head—just slightly, just toward the pillar where a gray clerk stood with a blank ledger—and for one terrible moment its iron eyes rested on Jonah, and Jonah felt something brush across the surface of his mind like a hand trailing through still water. Not seizing. *Tasting.* Checking whether anything here was worth swallowing.

Jonah did the only thing he could think of. He gave it boredom. Twelve years of practice served him now: he let his mind fill with the grayest inventory he owned, ink prices, ledger columns, the ache in his lower back, the specific tedium of copying shipping manifests. *Nothing here,* he offered up like a plate of ash. *Nothing but a tired man and his arithmetic.*

The attention lifted. The Regent blinked—slowly, like a lizard—and turned away, and Jonah discovered he had been holding his breath long enough to see stars.

* * *

He should have left then. Instead he followed the procession.

Court ended at noon, and the Regent withdrew, and the courtiers scattered like startled birds, and Jonah—because he was a fool, because Mirabel was sleeping in a cellar without Sable, because Wren's mark burned on a girl's wrist somewhere behind him—slipped from the gallery and trailed the royal party at a distance no one questioned, because clerks carrying things are furniture, furniture is invisible.

The Regent did not return to its apartments. It climbed. Up past the council chambers, up past the old beacon-room, up a narrow stair that the guards did not guard because no one was supposed to know it existed, and Jonah followed on legs that had stopped asking his opinion, past a landing where the rust-taste in the air grew thick enough to chew, to a corridor that ended in a single door of black oak banded with iron.

Two guards stood outside it. They did not stand like guards. They stood like men who had forgotten they were men, faces smooth, eyes faintly shimmered, and Jonah recognized the slackness of them with a lurch—the fishwife's look, the pleasant fog. Eaten. Hollowed out and set to door-duty like coats hung on hooks.

The Regent stopped before the door. And then it did something Jonah had not seen it do in the gallery, something that made his blood run cold and hot at once.

It hesitated.

Its hand rose to the door and stopped short of touching it. The borrowed shoulders drew together. For a moment—three heartbeats, four—the thing wearing the king stood before a locked door like a man before a confession booth, and if Jonah hadn't known better he would have sworn he was looking at shame.

Then it opened the door and went in, and closed it behind it, and the hollow guards resumed their staring, and Jonah stood in the shadows of the stairwell with his heart hammering out a storm-rhythm against his ribs.

Through the door, muffled by oak and iron, he heard voices. One was the Regent's—Aldric's voice, but gentler now, slower, almost tender. The other was thinner. Frayed. An old man's voice, or a sick man's, repeating something over and over, patient as water dripping on stone:

*"Her laugh was like—"*

A pause. A longer pause. The sound of a man grasping at smoke.

*"Her laugh was like... it was like..."*

And then the Regent's voice, soft as a nurse settling a child: *"Yes. Tell me again."*

Jonah backed away down the stair, one step at a time, his hand flat against the cold wall. He understood now—or he understood the shape of it, the way you understand a storm by the pressure in your ears before it breaks. The thing upstairs wasn't keeping the king prisoner the way a jailer keeps a prisoner. It was keeping him the way a larder keeps meat. Feeding slowly. Drawing out the meal. And the worst of it, the part that lodged under Jonah's ribs like a splinter, was the tenderness. It *liked* him. Whatever the Regent of Rust was, it had developed a genuine affection for the flavor of the man it was unmaking, and it visited him daily to savor the last of him a spoonful at a time, and the king—what was left of the king—told it his memories again and again because a fading man will tell anyone who listens.

*Her laugh was like—*

Jonah reached the bottom of the stair and stood in the gray light of the clerks' corridor, and he thought of Wren. Of how he had spent twelve years refusing to remember her laugh because remembering hurt, and how he would give anything now—all his buried storm, all his hidden years—to hear it again, even secondhand, even scratched into a tower wall by a dying man's fingernail.

There was a real king in that tower. Fading, but not gone. Clinging to something. And if a man could reach him—if a man could learn what the king remembered about the Veil, about bargains, about the thing that wore his face—

Jonah straightened Tobias Merle's collar, tucked his blank ledger under his arm, and walked out through the Fishgate past the guards who never looked twice at furniture.

Behind him the black snow fell on Greyhollow, patient as hunger. Above the palace, in a locked room at the top of the world, a king was forgetting his own name one syllable at a time—and whispering it, Jonah would learn later, to the walls, so that some part of him would remain to be found.

He had come for confirmation and a way in.

He had both now. All he needed was a plan, a miracle, and a girl with a locket full of a dead queen's laughter—which, he suspected, was exactly what the king in the tower had been trying so hard to remember.
