## 11. A King Who Forgets His Name

The king of Veyland woke on the floor again.

He had gone to sleep in the bed—he was almost sure he had gone to sleep in the bed—but the bed stood across the room like a piece of furniture in another man's house, and Aldric's body had chosen the cold flagstones instead, curled against the wall beneath the window where the light came thinnest and earliest. His hip ached from the stone. That was good. Pain was reliable. Pain came back every morning and told him who he was: an old man, a hurt man, a man with a body that still reported for duty even when everything above it had begun to desert.

He lay still and took inventory, as he did every morning, though some mornings he forgot to do it until noon, and one terrible morning last month he had not done it at all, had simply existed without asking himself any questions, and the hours had slipped past like water through open fingers.

*My name is Aldric.* He said it aloud, because saying it aloud made it heavier, harder to lose. "My name is Aldric."

It sounded right. It sounded like his name. But it had sounded right yesterday too, when he'd spent four hours convinced he was called *Aldebrand*, and had scratched *ALDEBRAND* into the wall beside the window before the true name had come swimming back up through the dark like a drowned man surfacing.

He got up. His knees complained. He crossed to the wall—the eastern wall, the one with the scratches—and found the morning's work waiting for him, as it always waited, patient as scripture.

Three memories. That was the rule. Three memories, no more, because more than three could not be defended. A garrison of three could hold a wall; a garrison of thirty would scatter and die in the field. He had learned this the hard way, over years, by losing things. He had once tried to keep forty. He had written them all down, named them, rehearsed them nightly like a general reviewing troops: the coronation, his father's hands, the taste of river trout, the color of the palace gardens in autumn, the name of his first horse, the smell of his mother's bread—

All gone now. Every one. Eaten or faded or simply *left*, the way soldiers desert a losing cause, quietly, in the night, until one day you count your ranks and find the field empty and cannot even remember what you were fighting for.

But three remained. Three held.

* * *

The first memory was his queen's laugh.

Her name was Maerin. He knew this the way he knew his own name—by daily maintenance, by ritual, by scratching it into stone. *MAERIN. SHE LAUGHED LIKE RAIN ON A TIN ROOF.* The words were cut deep now, deeper than they needed to be, because shallow scratches faded faster than memory did, and he had learned to out-carve the forgetting.

He closed his eyes and reached for it.

For a long moment there was nothing. This was the worst part, the part no one warned you about—not that remembering hurt, but that remembering was *work*, that a memory was not a thing you owned but a thing you tended, like a fire, like a garden, like anything alive. And his garden had a wolf in it. Something came every night, something patient and vast and wearing his face, and it ate.

But then—there. Faint, like a candle seen through fog: a kitchen in the old summer palace, before the crown, when they were only two young fools playing at being married. She had been laughing at something. What? He couldn't remember what—jokes went first, jokes were the leaves that fell in autumn—but the laugh itself remained, and it was exactly as he had carved it: rain on a tin roof, quick and bright and slightly too loud, the laugh of a woman who had never learned to be embarrassed. She had laughed that way at their wedding, in front of two hundred courtiers. She had laughed that way the day their daughter was born, exhausted and bleeding and furious with joy.

Maerin was dead eleven years now. He had buried her under the rowan tree she'd planted herself. He remembered the funeral. He thought he remembered the funeral. Some days the funeral was a warm, solid fact, and some days it was a story someone had told him about a stranger, and those were the days he scratched the wall twice.

"SHE LAUGHED LIKE RAIN ON A TIN ROOF," he read aloud, tracing the letters with one cracked fingernail. "Maerin. My wife. I loved her. I love her." Present tense. He had decided, long ago, that love did not expire just because its object did. The wolf could eat his past. It would not eat that.

* * *

The second memory was the lullaby.

His daughter's name was—here he stopped, and pressed his palm flat against the wall, and breathed, because this was the one that fought him. The lullaby itself came easily; melodies burrowed deeper than names, deeper than faces, down in the marrow where the wolf's teeth couldn't reach. But the name of the child he had sung it to slipped through his fingers like minnows.

*Elira.* No. *Elin.* No—

He stood there, an old man in a locked room, mouthing silent guesses at his own blood, and hated himself with a clean and efficient hatred, the way you hate weather.

Then it came. Not summoned—*arrived*, the way dawn arrives, all at once and obvious. *Seren.*

"Seren," he said aloud, tasting it. Yes. Seren. Named for the evening star, because she'd been born at dusk and Maerin had insisted, half-delirious, that the first star out was watching. Seren, who had a gap between her front teeth and a habit of falling asleep mid-sentence. Seren, who was grown now—or he believed she was grown; the years since had gone strange and soft-edged, and when he tried to picture her adult face he got only a blur, a smear of light where a woman should be.

But the lullaby. The lullaby was whole.

He sang it. Quietly, because the walls had ears—there was a grate in the ceiling through which food arrived and, he suspected, listening happened—but he sang it, because singing it was the keeping of it. Words first: *Sleep, my starling, the wind is folded / Sleep, my starling, the sea is told...* Then the tune underneath, a rising third and a falling fifth, the little turn at the end that Maerin had invented and Seren had demanded every night for six years, holding one finger up even while her eyes closed: *one more turn, Papa. One more.*

His voice cracked on the high note. It always cracked now. Once it had been a good voice; Maerin used to say he sang like a man who meant it. Now he sang like a man who was afraid of stopping, because stopping was how things ended, and ending was how the wolf won.

When he finished, he added a fresh scratch beneath the old ones: *SEREN. THE LULLABY HAS ONE MORE TURN. ALWAYS ONE MORE TURN.*

* * *

The third memory was the promise.

This one he did not sing or speak. This one he only touched.

It sat deepest of the three, and strangest, because it was not a memory of his life but of its edge—a memory of standing somewhere that was not quite anywhere, before something that was not quite anyone, and making a bargain whose terms he could feel in his bones but no longer fully parse, the way you can remember that you once understood mathematics without being able to do it anymore.

There had been a Veil. There was a Veil—it was in the word itself, *VEIL*, which he had carved so many times the stone was worn smooth as glass in that spot. A boundary between the living world and the realm of memory. And he had stood before it—young? old? he genuinely could not say—and something on the far side had spoken to him, and he had promised.

Promised what?

He pressed his forehead to the wall. The stone was cold. Cold helped; cold was honest; cold didn't lie to flatter you.

Fragments, then, drifting up: *a debt. a door. a hunger that must be fed or it will feed itself.* A voice like rust. A warning about—about storms? About a storm-caller? Someone with lightning in their blood whom he had sworn to protect, or to find, or to forgive—

And beneath all of it, the shape of the promise itself, which he could hold only the way you hold water: *I will remember what must not be forgotten, even if remembering is all I have left.*

That was it. That was the whole of it. He had promised to remember. And whatever else the wolf took—and it took so much, it took so much, it had taken his father's face and his coronation oath and the name of every servant who had ever been kind to him—it could not take the promise, because the promise was not a memory *of* something. The promise was the vessel. You could drain the cup, but the cup remained.

Some nights he wondered if that was why the wolf kept him alive. Why wear the king's face and lock the king in a tower, instead of simply killing him? Because a dead man's promise dies with him, perhaps. Or because—as the thing sometimes murmured through the walls, in a voice horribly like his own—*you are the last copy, Aldric. I am nearly finished with the original. But a copy should be kept, shouldn't it? In case of errors.*

He did not answer it. He had learned not to answer it. Answering was feeding.

* * *

Midday came through the window, gray and thin. Winter was close; he could smell it, iron and ash under the cold, and lately the ash smell had been growing stronger, which troubled him in a distant, underwater way, the way a man on a sinking ship is troubled by the pitch of the deck.

Food arrived through the grate: bread, broth, an apple. He ate sitting on the floor with his back against his wall of scratches, because the table felt like furniture for a different man, a man with a kingdom, and he was trying to be honest with himself about what he had left. He had a wall. He had three memories. He had a fingernail that split and bled when he carved too deep, and he carved too deep most days.

He studied the wall while he chewed.

It had become, over the years, a kind of terrible autobiography. Rows and rows of the same words, cut and re-cut, layered like sediment: *MAERIN. SEREN. VEIL. THE LULLABY HAS ONE MORE TURN. MY NAME IS ALDRIC. MY NAME IS ALDRIC. MY NAME IS ALDRI—*

Here and there, the false entries. *ALDEBRAND.* A line that read *I HAVE A BROTHER NAMED CORWIN* which was wrong, he had no brother, he had checked, he had spent a full day checking, turning the fact over and over like a coin to test whether it was counterfeit, and it was counterfeit, and yet the scratch remained, because he could not bring himself to deface his own record even when the record lied. Lies were evidence too. Lies showed where the wolf had bitten.

And near the floor, low, where a kneeling man would carve: a single line he did not remember cutting at all. *DO NOT OPEN THE DOOR FOR THE MAN WITH YOUR FACE.* He touched it sometimes. It felt like his hand's work. It frightened him more than anything else in the room—that his future self had apparently considered this warning necessary, and urgent enough to carve blind, in the dark, perhaps while the wolf watched.

Afternoon. The light turned. He did his rounds.

Name. Queen. Daughter. Lullaby—one more turn. Promise. Veil. Name again, to close the circuit, because a circuit unbroken was a self maintained. He walked the perimeter of himself like a sentry walking a wall at dusk, lantern in hand, checking every stone.

And it was during the evening round, on his knees before the smooth-worn spot where *VEIL* lived, that he heard it.

A voice. Below him—far below, floors down, muffled by stone, almost certainly imagined. He had heard voices before. The wolf sent voices. The wolf had once spent an entire week impersonating Maerin, and he had wept and answered and answered, and in the morning the words *MAERIN IS DEAD, MAERIN IS DEAD, DO NOT TRUST HER VOICE* had appeared on the wall in his own handwriting, and he had no memory of writing them.

But this voice was not Maerin's. It was rough, and tired, and it was *reading*.

Reading aloud. Slowly, carefully, the way a man reads who does not know what the words mean but understands they matter: *"MAERIN. SHE LAUGHED LIKE RAIN ON A TIN ROOF."*

Aldric went very still.

*"SEREN. THE LULLABY HAS ONE MORE TURN."*

The voice paused. When it came again, it was closer to wonder than fear, and it was speaking not the carved words but new ones, its own:

"Your Majesty? Can you hear me? My name is Jonah Fairweather. I'm going to come back tomorrow night. And the night after. I'm going to read your wall to you, every night, as many times as it takes."

Aldric knelt in the dark with his hand pressed to the worn stone, and felt something he had not felt in years: the sensation of a door opening inside him onto a lit room. Someone knew. Someone had found the wall. Someone was coming back.

*One more turn,* he thought, giddy and terrified. *Always one more turn.*

Above him, faint through the grate, something laughed—softly, patiently, in a voice exactly like his own.
