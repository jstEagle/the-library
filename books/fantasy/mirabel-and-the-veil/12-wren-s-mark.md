## 12. Wren's Mark

The locket woke up on the ninth night.

Mirabel had been sleeping in the corner of Jonah's back room, curled in a nest of blankets that still smelled like the Undertow—brine and lamp oil and Sable Voss's pipe smoke—and she woke because her wrist was burning. Not the storm-mark itself. The locket, hanging from its cord around her neck, had gone hot as a coal against her chest.

She sat up in the dark. The room was small and close, shelves of useless charms on every wall, and through the window she could see Greyhollow under curfew, the streets empty except for the Ministry lanterns swinging like slow yellow pendulums. Somewhere a dog was barking at nothing. Somewhere a hunter was probably listening to it.

The locket pulsed. Once. Twice. Like a heart that wasn't hers.

She'd carried it for two years without it doing anything but hum. It hummed when she slept, hummed when she dreamed, hummed sometimes when she thought too hard about how she'd gotten it—which was never, because she couldn't remember. That was the hole in her head, the one Jonah had stopped asking about after the third night, when she'd cried so hard she'd made the lamps flicker. She remembered the road. She remembered walking. She remembered cold and hunger and the sound of her own name—Mirabel, Mirabel, like someone calling her across water—but before that there was just white, and a voice saying *go now, go quickly, don't look back*, and a hand pressing this locket into hers.

She'd assumed the hand belonged to whoever she'd been before. Some mother. Some stranger.

The locket pulsed again, harder, and this time it *spoke*.

Not words. Not exactly. It was more like remembering something that hadn't happened to you—a smell of rain on hot stone, a woman's laugh, the feeling of standing at the edge of something enormous and bright. Mirabel gasped and grabbed it with both hands, and the images came faster, tumbling over each other like water over a weir:

*A garden gone silver with frost. A door made of weather.*

*A woman with grey eyes and a scar across her knuckles, pressing the locket closed with both hands, saying: "He'll know what it means. Make him listen."*

*And on the woman's wrist, plain as morning—*

—the storm-mark.

Mirabel knew it the way you know your own face in a mirror. She'd spent two years staring at hers, tracing its spiral in candlelight, wondering whose it was and why it had chosen a girl who couldn't remember being anyone. And here it was, identical, coiled on another wrist, on an arm attached to a woman with Jonah's exact jaw and Jonah's exact stubborn mouth and eyes the color of a sky about to break.

"Wren," Mirabel said out loud, and the name came out of her like it had been waiting years to be said. "Wren Fairweather."

The locket flared white.

* * *

Jonah came awake fast—that was a thing Mirabel had learned about him, that twelve years of hiding had turned his sleep into a shallow pond he could stand up out of in one motion—and found her sitting cross-legged on the floor with the locket blazing in her hands like a captured star.

"Put it down," he said, already moving, already reaching—

"No." She pulled it against her chest. "No, you have to see. You have to *see*."

"Mirabel—"

"She's alive." Her voice cracked straight down the middle. "Jonah. Your sister is *alive*."

The silence that followed was the loudest thing Mirabel had ever heard. Jonah stood in the middle of his own back room in his nightshirt, half his face lit by locket-light, and Mirabel watched twelve years move across him like weather. Disbelief first. Then hope, raw and terrible, the kind of hope that hurts worse than grief. Then something colder underneath, the thing he used to hold all the other things down.

"That's not possible," he said quietly.

"It's her mark. It's *your* mark, it's the same one, look—" She held out the locket, and when he didn't take it, she took his hand and pressed it flat against the metal. "Look at what it's showing me."

The light spilled up between their palms. Jonah made a sound Mirabel had never heard a grown man make—a small, wounded thing, like a door opening on a room that had been sealed since childhood.

Because the locket was showing him too. She could feel it now, understood it now: it wasn't speaking to her specifically. It had been waiting. Two years of humming, and all that time it had been waiting for her to bring it home.

They saw together: the frost-silver garden. The door of weather, tall as a cathedral, stitched out of cloud and lightning and the memory of rain. And Wren—older than Jonah remembered her, lines around her eyes, hair going iron at the temples, but unmistakably, impossibly *Wren*—standing before the door with her palm raised, holding it shut the way you'd hold shut a gate against a flood.

"She stayed," Mirabel whispered. "She's on the other side of the Veil. She's been there the whole time."

"No," Jonah said. But his hand hadn't moved from the locket. His fingers had curled around it instead, gripping hard enough to go white. "No, I *saw* her die. I felt it happen. My storm—" His breath went ragged. "My storm killed her. That's why I—"

"You *believed* it killed her," Mirabel said. The words were coming out of her steady and strange, like they were being fed to her, like Wren was speaking through the metal and using Mirabel's mouth to do it. "That's what you were meant to believe. That's what *it* wanted you to carry."

"What do you mean, *it*—"

"The thing in the palace." She watched him flinch; Sable's people had told them everything by then, the Regent of Rust, the hollowed king, the hunt built out of one creature's fear. "It was there. Twelve years ago. At the harvest-guard. It fed your storm the death and let you keep the guilt, because a man who thinks he killed his own sister will bury his gift so deep he'll never use it again. And it put *her* beyond the Veil, where she couldn't tell anyone the truth."

Jonah sat down. Not gracefully—he went down like a man whose legs had been cut out from under him, onto the edge of his cot, in the little room full of useless charms, and he stared at the locket in their joined hands like it was a ghost.

Mirabel almost pitied him. Almost. But there was a fire in her chest that had been building since the moment the mark appeared on the other woman's wrist, and pity didn't have any room next to it.

Because here was the thing Mirabel hadn't said yet. Here was the thing sitting under her ribs like a swallowed stone.

*Wren sent me away.*

* * *

The locket kept talking through the small hours. It had a lot saved up.

Mirabel learned that Wren hadn't died at the harvest-guard—she'd been *taken*, snatched sideways through the thin place in the world at the exact moment the storm peaked, pulled into the memory-realm before the lightning ever touched ground. She'd woken on the far side of the Veil in a country made of everything forgotten, and she'd learned, slowly and painfully, what lived there. What had been living there a long time. A hunger older than the crown it would eventually wear.

She learned that Wren had fought it. That the fight had cost her everything except the one weapon she had left: herself. The Regent's hunger needed a cage, and a storm-caller's mind, freely given, was the strongest cage there was. So Wren had built herself into the lock. Had stood at the door of weather for twelve years, aging barely a day per year, holding the hunger inside so it couldn't finish crossing over.

She learned that the cage was failing. That the black snow falling on Greyhollow was what leaked through the cracks. That Wren had spent the last of her strength outside the lock crafting one message and one messenger, folding them into a girl who couldn't remember being anyone, and sending her through the last thin place before it sealed.

*"Come to the Veil,"* the memory said, Wren's voice frayed but fierce, her grey eyes filling the whole world. *"Bring the storm home. Tell my brother—"*

The memory broke apart there, every time, like a wave hitting rocks. Tell my brother—what? The locket didn't say. Maybe Wren hadn't finished it. Maybe she'd run out of time. Maybe some part of her had known that whatever she said next, Jonah wouldn't hear it right until he heard it in person.

By the time the grey light of morning came through the window, Mirabel knew everything except the one thing she most needed to say.

Jonah hadn't moved from the cot. He sat exactly where he'd fallen, the locket now dark and ordinary in his open palm, and he looked ten years older than he had at midnight. He looked, Mirabel thought, like a man who had been handed back a corpse and told it was breathing.

"I mourned her," he said finally. His voice was gravel. "Twelve years. I built my whole life on her grave. Every charm I sold wrong on purpose, every day I made myself small—I did it *for* her. Because I thought I owed a dead girl a quiet life." He laughed, and it was the worst sound Mirabel had ever heard. "And she was alive. The whole time. On the other side of a door I could have—"

"You couldn't have opened it," Mirabel said. "You didn't even know it was there."

"I could have *looked*. I could have asked questions instead of hiding. One question, Mirabel. One honest question in twelve years and maybe—"

"And gotten yourself eaten like Tam?" It came out sharper than she meant. "Gotten yourself taken like Sable? You hid because hiding was the only thing that worked. Don't you dare turn that into another sin to carry."

He looked up at her then, startled. She was eleven years old and she'd just scolded a grown storm-caller in his own house, and she found she didn't care at all. There was a fury in her that had been waiting two years for a shape, and now it had one.

"Say it," Jonah said quietly. "Whatever you're not saying. I can see it sitting on you like a coat."

Mirabel hugged her knees. Outside, the curfew bells rang the hour, and somewhere below a Ministry wagon rolled past on the wet cobbles, and the whole city held its breath the way it had been holding its breath for weeks.

"She left me," Mirabel said. "That's the part I can't—everyone keeps talking about how brave she is, how she sacrificed herself, the cage, the door, all of it, and fine. Fine. But she *made* me. Out of whatever I was before. She took a girl and emptied her and filled her up with a job and sent her through alone, and I don't even remember who I was! I don't remember a mother or a bed or a birthday or anything, I just remember *walking*, for two years, walking toward a man I'd never met because a voice in a locket told me to!" Her voice had climbed without her permission. The lamp on the shelf buzzed and flickered. "And everyone's going to say she had no choice. And maybe she didn't. But I didn't either, and nobody asked *me*."

The room went very still.

Jonah set the locket down on the blanket between them, carefully, like it might bite. Then he did something Mirabel didn't expect. He slid off the cot onto the floor, onto the cold boards beside her, and sat the way she was sitting—knees up, arms wrapped around them, small. Making himself small, the way he did. Except this time it didn't look like hiding. It looked like coming down to her level.

"You're right," he said.

Mirabel blinked. "What?"

"You're right, and I should have said it hours ago, and I'm sorry it took me this long." He rubbed his face with both hands. "Everyone in this story keeps getting used. Me, by the Regent. Aldric, by the Regent. Sable, by the war. And you most of all—you've been carrying a dead woman's errand since before you could remember having a name, and every adult you've met since has treated you like a package that happens to talk." He exhaled. "Including me, some days. I'm sorry."

Mirabel hadn't known she was going to cry until she already was. It was humiliating and enormous and it came up out of her like the storm used to come up out of Jonah, all at once, two years of walking and hunger and fear breaking over her in a back room that smelled like lamp oil. Jonah didn't tell her to stop. He didn't pat her head or tell her she was brave. He just sat there on the cold floor next to her and let her do it, which was somehow the only right thing anyone could have done.

When she finally ran out, the window had gone from grey to gold. Morning, real morning, the first one in weeks that didn't feel like a countdown.

"So what do we do?" she asked, wiping her face on her sleeve. Her voice was wrecked and she didn't care. "About Wren. About the door."

Jonah picked the locket back up. He turned it over in his fingers, and Mirabel watched him watch it, and she saw the moment the grief finished turning into something else. Something with edges.

"The solstice eclipse is in nine days," he said. "Sable told me. It's when the Veil thins thinnest—it's why the Regent chose this winter. It'll be the only chance to cross." He closed his fist around the locket. "I'm going to get my sister back. And I'm going to make the thing that wore her death pay for every year it stole."

Something in Mirabel's chest went tight and bright. Joy and fury, tangled together so completely she couldn't tell them apart anymore—maybe they'd always been the same feeling wearing different faces.

"There's a problem," she said. "The eclipse. Crossing over. How?"

Jonah was quiet a long moment. When he answered, his voice had dropped into a register she'd only heard once before—at the sea-gate, in the thunder.

"I don't know yet," he said. "But I know someone who might. Aldric made a bargain at the Veil once, before the Regent hollowed him. Whatever he promised, whatever he paid—he's the only living soul who's stood at that door and come back." He stood, and began pulling clothes off their hooks, moving fast now, the exhaustion burned clean off him. "Which means tonight, I'm going to break into the palace tower."

Mirabel scrambled up after him, the locket warm against her sternum, humming again—not the old aimless hum, but something with direction now, something pulling north toward the palace like a compass needle finding its true bearing.

"And me?" she said.

Jonah paused at the door. For a second she thought he was going to tell her to stay, to hide, to be small—and she watched him strangle the words before they got out.

"You're her hand and her voice," he said. "Wren chose you for this. So you're coming."

The locket flared, once, like a heartbeat answering.

They went out into the ash-falling morning together.
