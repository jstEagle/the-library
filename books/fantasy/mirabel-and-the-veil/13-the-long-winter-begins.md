## 13. The Long Winter Begins

The snow came wrong.

Jonah noticed it first as a smell—burnt sugar and cold iron drifting through the shutters of his shop, sharp enough to wake him an hour before dawn. He lay in the dark listening to the wind work at the eaves and thought: *that's not snow-smell. Snow smells like nothing, like held breath.* This smelled like something had died in the sky.

He went to the window and pulled the curtain back a finger's width.

The flakes falling through the lamplight were grey. Not white touched with soot from a thousand chimneys—Greyhollow's winters had always been grey with coal smoke—but *grey through and through*, each flake dense and slow, falling like ash shaken loose from a great burning page. Where it settled on the sill it didn't melt. It sat there, fine and dry, and when Jonah touched it with one fingertip it clung to his skin like static, like a memory trying to stick.

His storm-sense, buried twelve years under deliberate silence, stirred anyway. It always stirred for weather. It was the one part of himself he'd never managed to kill completely—the way a man who has cut out his tongue can still feel rain on his face.

And what it felt now made his stomach drop.

The air over Greyhollow wasn't just cold. It was *thin*. He could feel the Veil the way you feel a draft under a door—a whisper of wrongness running along the rooftops, a pressure behind the eyes, a sense of vast attention leaning close. The barrier between the living world and whatever waited beyond it had been fraying since the Edict, he'd known that much. But this morning it felt less like fraying and more like peeling, like skin off fruit, like someone on the other side had finally found the corner of it and begun to pull.

Behind him, Mirabel slept curled in the storage room among the ribbon spools, the locket clenched in one fist even in sleep. She'd been dreaming loudly again—small sounds, whimpers, once a word that might have been *Wren* or might have been *run*. Jonah had learned not to wake her. Waking her mid-dream brought the storms in her blood too close to the surface, and once she'd cracked every pane of glass on Copperlane with a scream.

He let her sleep. He stood at the window and watched the ash-snow bury Greyhollow, and he understood, with the flat certainty of a man reading his own death warrant, that hiding was over.

* * *

By midmorning the whole city knew something was wrong, because the criers went out twice.

The first proclamation was expected: the hunt accelerating, registration deadlines moved up, rewards doubled. Jonah stood in the crowd outside the Ministry annex with his collar up and heard the words read in a voice that didn't sound human anymore—it sounded rehearsed by something that had studied human voices from a distance. *All persons harboring the gift of unlicensed craft will be seized for the crown's protection. All persons concealing such persons share their guilt. There is no sanctuary. There is no winter long enough.*

But it was the second proclamation that emptied the streets.

A herald in black walked the length of Copperlane alone, unescorted, which no herald had ever done, and nailed a sheet of vellum to the notice post without ceremony. The paper was the color of old teeth. The writing on it wasn't ink; it looked burned in from behind, letters surfacing like drowned things rising.

It said only:

**THE VEIL THINS. THE GIFTED ARE ITS DOOR. BRING THEM TO THE PALACE AND THE COLD WILL END.**

No seal. No signature. No claim of royal authority—which was itself the horror, because everything else the Regent did wore Aldric's face and Aldric's seals, and this thing didn't bother pretending. It was done hiding too.

An old woman near Jonah crossed herself with a charm-sign no priest sanctioned. A man spat. Most people just looked at the notice the way you look at weather coming—helpless, calculating how much wood they had, whether the roof would hold.

Jonah looked at it and did arithmetic.

*The gifted are its door.* He'd assumed the hunt was fear, or hunger, or the slow cruelty of a usurper consolidating power. But this reframed everything. It wasn't hunting magicians despite the Veil thinning. It was hunting them *because* of it. Every gifted mind in Greyhollow wasn't a threat to be removed.

It was an ingredient.

* * *

Mirabel met him three streets from the shop, materializing out of an alley with the particular silence she'd perfected—no footfall, no breath, just suddenly there, tugging his sleeve. Her hood was up. Under it her face was pale and set, and there was ash-snow caught in her lashes.

"Undertow's gone quiet," she said. "Sable sent word. Not good word."

"Sable's always bad word. That's half her charm."

"Not like this." Mirabel pulled him into the alley mouth, out of the sightline of the Ministry patrol trudging past through the grey fall. Up close, Jonah could see her hands shaking—not fear, he'd learned, never fear, but the static of her gift looking for somewhere to go. "The tunnels are humming. Everyone can hear it. Like something big moving under the ice. And the ferry—" She stopped, swallowed. "The ferry won't cross anymore. Sable says the water remembers being watched."

Jonah exhaled slowly. "Get us home first. Then talk."

They walked the long way, heads down, two more grey shapes in a city of them. The ash-snow thickened as they went. By the time they reached the shop the streetlamps were lit at noon and the light through them was the color of weak tea. On their doorstep, someone had left a dead wren.

Not a message. Just a bird, small and brown, laid neatly on the stone with its feet pointing toward the door. Hunters' work, or hunters' humor—or the Regent's idea of a joke aimed at him specifically, because the thing wearing the king's face surely knew by now whose shop the storm-caller hid behind.

Mirabel stared at it, and Jonah saw her jaw tighten, and he picked the bird up gently before she could, and buried it in the garden under the frost-killed rosemary, because grief needed somewhere to go and he would not let hers go sideways today.

Inside, he barred the door, lit no lamps, and told her everything the notices had said while she sat on the counter swinging her heels like the child she almost wasn't anymore.

"So it's eating them," she said at last. "The gifted. That's why it wants us all in one place."

"That's what it looks like."

"And if it eats enough..." She frowned, working it through with the fierce practicality she applied to everything. "It's already wearing his body. His face. So what's missing?"

"Its self." Jonah heard his own voice go quiet. "Bodies are easy. It took the king's body months ago. What it doesn't have is a *mind* worth inhabiting—memories deep enough, a soul heavy enough, to make the embodiment permanent before the Veil closes for the season. A hollow man in a stolen coat." He thought of Aldric in his tower, scratching fading memories into stone, and felt the pity come again, unwelcome and enormous. "And it's decided we're the stuffing."

Mirabel was silent a moment. Then, in a smaller voice: "Wren's on the other side of the Veil. If it thins all the way..."

"Then we stop it before it does."

"You keep saying *we* like it's simple."

"It isn't. It's the least simple thing I've ever said." He looked at her—at the storm-mark circling her wrist like a bracelet of blue lightning, at the locket against her sternum, at the whole improbable fact of her, delivered through his window by fate or by his sister's hand. "But I've spent twelve years being small, Mirabel, and small stopped working around the time the snow turned black. So we're going to need help, and luck, and probably a miracle, and I'd rather owe all three to people I trust than spend them alone."

She nodded slowly. Then her head snapped up, toward the front of the shop, and Jonah heard it a half-second later: footsteps on the porch. Heavy ones. More than one set. And beneath them, faint but unmistakable, a sound like sand pouring through an hourglass—the sound he'd heard once before, in a palace corridor, and never forgotten.

The sound of memories leaking out of a man who was losing them.

* * *

He put Mirabel in the cellar behind the false wall and told her, pressing her shoulders, *whatever you hear, whatever you feel, do not come up*, and then he opened the door before they could break it, because broken doors invited searches and open doors invited assumptions.

Captain Ives stood on his step with four hunters fanned out behind him, and Jonah's breath caught, because Ives was dying in a way Jonah hadn't seen in the palace.

Half the captain's face was still a hard, competent soldier's face—grey-stubbled, scarred, tired. The other half had gone *smooth*, featureless as an egg, the flesh there remembering no history at all. One eye remained, dark and wet and desperately human. The other socket held nothing but a faint rust-colored shimmer, like light through a keyhole into a furnace room.

"Ives," Jonah said carefully.

"Fairweather." The voice was mostly intact. Mostly. "You're going to invite me in, because I have information, and because my men are watching a different door right now, and because I have perhaps"—he paused, visibly counting, visibly losing count, starting over—"perhaps days of myself left. Fewer, maybe. I forget how forgetting works."

Jonah stepped aside. The hunters peeled away into the snow without a word—they were Ives's own, loyal to the man rather than the crown, and Jonah understood that loyalty was the last organ the Regent hadn't eaten yet.

In the shop, Ives stood with his one eye closed, breathing, orienting himself by smell. Charm-ribbons. Old paper. Woodsmoke. "I used to know this street," he said. "I can't find it in my head anymore. There's a hole where Copperlane should be." He opened his eye. "So listen fast, because the holes are getting bigger.

"The Regent isn't gathering the gifted to punish them. You've worked that out—I can see it on your face, and your face is the last honest thing on this street. It's gathering them to *eat*. Every mind with craft in it. It needs the weight. A soul is heavy, Fairweather, heavier than a body, and its borrowed one is shedding mass every day. When the Veil is thinnest—at solstice, during the eclipse—it means to walk out of the palace wearing a completed king, stuffed full of every gifted mind in Greyhollow, and it will not need to hide ever again. It will simply *be*, and the world will adjust around it, the way the world adjusts around mountains."

"How many?" Jonah asked. "How many minds does it need?"

Ives smiled, and it was the worst thing Jonah had seen all winter, because the smile was fond. "It doesn't think in numbers anymore. It thinks in *enough*. And here is the part you need: it knows about the girl. It has tasted the edges of her through the locket—she carries fragments of the king's true memories, and those fragments are the one meal it cannot reach on its own. They're anchored. Loved. It can't digest what someone loved hard enough." His eye fixed on Jonah. "It wants her brought in alive. Everything else it takes as it finds. But her, it wants *whole*."

The floor seemed to tilt. Somewhere below, Jonah knew, Mirabel was pressed against the false wall, hearing every word.

"Why tell me?" Jonah said. "Why burn your remaining days walking across a hunted city to warn a charm-seller?"

Ives was quiet for a long moment. Ash-snow ticked against the windows like fingernails.

"Because it ate my daughter's name yesterday," he said finally. "I woke up and reached for it and the place where it lived was smooth as the side of my head. I don't know what her face was. I know she existed. I know I loved her. I know her name was—" His jaw worked. His one eye shone. "*Was*," he finished, and the word broke in half. "That's what it does. That's what it *is*. And every hour I stand in its ranks I'm a longer arm for it, and I would rather be a short dead man than a long arm. So." He straightened, the soldier reassembling himself out of habit and spite. "Solstice. Six days. The eclipse. The palace will pull its hunters inward to guard the feeding—that's the one night the Undertow's tunnels run empty. If any of you are going to do anything, it's then, or it's never."

He walked to the door. Stopped. Didn't turn around.

"There was a boy in my village once," he said, "who pulled a drowning man out of the millrace and then apologized for getting his boots wet. I've been trying to remember why I remembered that. I think it's because I'm standing in front of him." He shook his head slowly. "Or I think it's because the Regent left it in me on purpose, as bait. I honestly can't tell anymore. Either way, Fairweather—six days."

The door closed behind him. His footsteps faded into the hush of the falling grey.

Jonah stood alone in the dark shop, heart hammering, doing the only arithmetic that mattered now. Six days until solstice. Six days until the eclipse. Six days until the Regent walked out of the palace complete, unless someone crossed the Veil first and unmade it from the inside—and the only person who knew how to open the Veil was a king locked in a tower, forgetting his own name one scratch at a time.

From the cellar, softly, came the sound of the false wall sliding aside.

Jonah turned. Mirabel stood in the doorway with the locket glowing faintly against her chest, and her eyes were dry and absolutely certain, and she said the words that ended the last twelve years of his life as surely as any edict could have:

"Then we go tonight. We get the king. And I'm not staying in any cellar ever again."
