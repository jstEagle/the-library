## 14. What We Bury, What We Keep

The refuge smelled of tallow and wet stone, like a church that had given up on God. Mirabel sat cross-legged on a crate that wobbled every time she breathed wrong, watching the survivors of the Undertow do the strangest thing she had ever seen.

They were giving their memories away.

Not all of them. Not the whole selves of them. Just pieces — a spoonful here, a spoonful there — passed from hand to hand in a circle of eleven people around a single candle. An old man with river-silt in his voice held his palms out flat, and a woman with a burn-scarred jaw pressed her fingertips to his, and between them something moved. Mirabel couldn't see it exactly, but she could feel it, the way you feel weather coming through a shut window: a pressure, a warmth, a faint electric itch along her storm-mark.

"What are they doing?" she whispered.

Sable Voss was not in the circle. Sable Voss was in a cage in the palace dungeons, and every hour Mirabel spent in this warm room was an hour she owed to that. The one-eyed ferrywoman's second-in-command — a broad, gray-bearded man everyone called Dock, though Mirabel suspected it wasn't his name so much as his personality — crouched beside her with a tin cup of barley tea he kept trying to make her drink.

"Trading," Dock said. "Or sharing, if you want to be kind about it."

"Sharing what?"

"Memories." He nodded at the old man, who now wore the burn-scarred woman's memory of her wedding day like a coat two sizes too big, smiling at nothing. "The Regent eats people whole, girl. That's how it works. It doesn't kill you first. It hollows you out until there's nothing left to fight with. Captain Ives is what happens when it takes most of you. But if your memories live in more than one head—" Dock tapped his own temple with a knuckle like tapping a barrel— "then when it comes for yours, it gets a mouthful of other folk too. Other voices. Other griefs. Nothing it can swallow clean."

Mirabel turned this over. "So nobody owns anything."

"Everybody owns everything," Dock corrected. "That's the trick of it. You give away the memory of your mother's face, and now six strangers carry your mother's face for you. Lose your mind tomorrow, and you don't lose her. She lives on in people who never met her." He shrugged, huge shoulders rolling like the harbor tide. "It's not tidy. Some nights I've got a fisherman's fear of drowning sitting in my chest like a swallowed hook, and I've never been further into the sea than my own bathtub. But it keeps us *us*."

The candle guttered. Someone fed it a fresh wick. In the corner, a boy no older than Mirabel — twelve, maybe thirteen, with ink-stained fingers and a stutter he only lost when he was remembering aloud — recited another person's childhood: a red door, a dog named Turnip, a father who whistled through his teeth. Half the room closed their eyes to hold it better.

Mirabel's chest did something complicated.

She had carried things alone her whole life. That was the shape of being feral; you learned it before you learned letters. Every scrap of Wren — the locket, the mark, the humming weight of a dead queen's lullaby and a king's broken promise — lived in her skull and nowhere else, and she guarded it the way a dog guards a bone it can't chew: fiercely, uselessly, alone.

Nobody had ever offered to carry any of it.

"Your turn," Dock said.

"I don't have anything worth trading."

"Everyone says that." He held out his hands, palms up, patient as a dock waiting for a ship. "Doesn't have to be big. Doesn't have to be happy. Just has to be true."

Mirabel looked at his open hands. At the circle. At the candle, which was doing its small stubborn work against all that dark.

And she thought about Wren.

*Come to the Veil,* the message hummed inside her, worn smooth by months of carrying. *Bring the storm home.* She'd said it to Jonah once, in the bad hours after the raid, and watched him go white as gull-bone. She hadn't said it since. It felt like a knife she kept handing him handle-first and then wondering why he bled.

But maybe that was the problem. Maybe it wasn't meant to be carried by one person. Maybe that was the whole lesson of this smoky room full of borrowed mothers' faces.

"Okay," she said, before she could talk herself out of it. "But mine's heavy."

"Heavy ones keep best," Dock said.

She closed her eyes. She found the memory the way you find a stone in shallow water — by feel, by weight — and lifted it out: the night on the salt flats outside Greyhollow, three weeks after she'd run, when the locket had gone hot against her sternum and Wren's voice had come through it clear as a bell over ice. *Come to the Veil. Bring the storm home.* And underneath the words, the feeling of them: urgency braided with love, and under *that*, something Mirabel had never told anyone, because she hadn't understood it until this exact moment.

Wren hadn't sounded afraid.

She'd sounded like someone standing on a shore she'd chosen, calling to the people she loved across water.

Mirabel opened her eyes and pressed her palms into Dock's.

The giving felt like the sea-gate storm in reverse — not pouring out but handing over, careful as passing a full cup. Dock went still. His eyebrows climbed. When she let go, he sat back on his heels and breathed once, long and slow, like a man who'd just set down luggage he'd forgotten he was holding for somebody else.

"Well," he said, gruff and gentle at once. "Hello, Wren Fairweather."

Around the circle, heads turned. One by one, without a word, people reached out to Dock — the burn-scarred woman, the old river-voiced man, the boy with the ink stains — and took the message from him the way you pass bread down a table. Mirabel felt each hand-off as a small loosening in her own chest, a knot untying strand by strand. By the time it came back around to her, the words were the same but the weight was different. Lighter. Held by eleven pairs of hands instead of two small cold ones.

"That's how it works," Dock said quietly. "You don't lose it. You just stop being the only one who'd die if it did."

Mirabel wiped her nose with her sleeve. She was eleven years old and she refused to cry in front of a man called Dock, and she mostly succeeded.

* * *

Jonah found her afterward, in the alcove off the main chamber where the Undertow kept its maps — smuggler charts of Greyhollow's underbelly, inked on sailcloth, the palace marked with a child's scrawl of a crown. He was holding two cups of tea and wearing the expression he always wore lately, which was a man trying to look calm while standing in a field during lightning season.

"You gave it away," he said.

"Not away." She took a cup. "Around."

He drank. Said nothing. The lamplight caught the gray at his temples, the scar splitting one eyebrow, all the small damage of twelve years of being small. Mirabel had learned to read him the way she read weather: the tightness at his jaw meant guilt, the stillness of his hands meant fear, and when he looked at her too long it meant he was seeing Wren, and hurting about it, and sorry for hurting about it.

Tonight his hands were still.

"The Regent's going to come for these people," he said finally. "For everyone who traded. A room full of shared memories is a feast laid out on a table."

"Then we'd better finish eating before it does," Mirabel said, and was surprised at how steady her voice came out.

Jonah almost smiled. It didn't survive. "Ives came back. Sliver of himself, thanks to you and that locket, but enough. Enough to tell us what's coming." He set down the cup. His fingers had started moving again — drumming, restless, the way they did when his storm wanted out and he wouldn't let it. "Black snow within days. The Veil thinning over the whole city. And the Regent doesn't want arrests anymore, Mirabel. It wants *minds*. Every gifted one it can reach, swallowed whole, to finish making itself real. This refuge, the tunnels, all of it — it's a pantry, and the door doesn't lock."

"So we run."

"We can't run far enough. Not with winter sealing the harbor, not with hunters on every street." He rubbed his face. "There's one door left. You know the one."

The Veil. *Come to the Veil. Bring the storm home.*

Mirabel looked at Jonah — at the man who had burned his mother's grimoire page by page rather than be caught with it, who had spent twelve years selling luck-knots that didn't work because working magic was a death sentence, who had taken her in anyway, mark and all, because love apparently outranked survival even when he pretended otherwise.

He was going to make himself walk toward the thing that had ruined his life. She could see him deciding it right now, in the set of his shoulders, in the way he stopped drumming.

And she realized she was tired of watching him bleed.

"Sit down," she said.

"Mira—"

"*Sit down*, Jonah."

He sat. She stood in front of him in the map-lit alcove, heart hammering, and did the bravest thing she had ever done, braver than running from hunters, braver than crashing through his window: she reached into herself, into the place where Dock and ten strangers now helped hold Wren's voice, and she pulled the memory up whole — not a spoonful, the entire cup — and she took Jonah's scarred hands in hers.

"You need to hear it the way I heard it," she said. "All of it. Not just the words. The *feel*. Because you've been hearing 'come to the Veil' like it's a summons to an execution, and it isn't. It isn't, Jonah. Let me show you."

"Mirabel, if this hurts you—"

"It won't. There are eleven people in the next room carrying it with me. That's the whole point." She squeezed his hands. "That's what they taught me tonight. You don't have to be the only one who'd die if it broke. Neither do I. So take it."

She gave.

It poured through their joined hands like current through wire — the salt flats, the hot locket, Wren's voice ringing clear over ice — and Mirabel felt him receive it, felt the exact moment the words landed in him properly for the first time. His breath caught. His grip tightened hard enough to hurt, and she let it.

Because she could feel what he was feeling now, the edges of it bleeding back through the link: not dread. Something older. Underneath twelve years of guilt and terror, some buried boy in him heard his sister's voice and *recognized* it, and the recognition went through him like sunrise through a shuttered house.

"She chose it," Jonah whispered. His eyes were wet and furious and lit from inside. "I always thought — all these years I thought I'd sent her somewhere terrible, that she was trapped, that she was *waiting*—" He laughed, a cracked, disbelieving sound. "She sounded like the day we flew kites off the harvest bluffs. She sounded *happy*."

"She sounded like someone with a plan," Mirabel said. "And the plan needs you. Your storm. *Bring the storm home*, Jonah. That's not a punishment. That's a job offer."

He stared at their hands. At her wrist, where his sister's mark shimmered faintly under the skin like a fish under ice.

"I haven't called weather in twelve years," he said. "And what I'm hearing is that I have to call all of it. Everything I ever buried. Pour it into a door at the edge of the world while a thing made of eaten memories tries to unmake a king." He shook his head slowly. "You're asking me to become the exact thing I've spent half my life pretending not to be."

"No," Mirabel said. "I'm telling you that you already are that thing, and it's the best thing about you, and it's time you stopped apologizing for it." She leaned in, fierce, close enough to see herself reflected twice in his eyes. "Wren didn't send me to save you from your storm. She sent me to bring it *home*. There's a difference. Home isn't a cage, Jonah. It's where a thing belongs."

For a long moment the alcove was silent except for the lamp hissing and, beneath the city, beneath everything, the deep slow pulse of the sea against the old gate.

Then Jonah Fairweather straightened his spine, and something in his face changed — not the calm mask he wore for Ministry inspections, not the careful blankness of a man being small. Something larger. Weather-shaped.

"Get Dock," he said. "And the maps. If the Regent wants a feast, we're going to give it a fight." He looked down at his own hands, flexing them, and for the first time since Mirabel had known him, they weren't trembling. They were *waiting*. "First thunder was practice. Tell the Undertow I'll need everything they know about the solstice eclipse — and tell them to start saying goodbye to their fears."

"Why?"

"Because in four days," Jonah said, and the lamps flickered though no wind had moved, "we're going to open the Veil."

Above them, muffled by stone and distance, the first flakes of black snow began to fall on Greyhollow.
