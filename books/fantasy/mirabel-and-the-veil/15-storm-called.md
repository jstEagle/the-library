## 15. Storm-Called

The refuge beneath the old salt warehouse smelled of tallow smoke and wet stone, and Jonah had not slept in two days when Mirabel put her hand on his arm and said, "Show me."

"Show you what?"

"The storm." She said it the way other children asked for supper. "You keep it folded up so tight I can hear it buzzing through your sleeves. Show me."

He almost laughed. Almost. But there was nothing funny in her face—only that terrible patience children have, the kind adults spend years learning to fake.

"It's not a trick, Mirabel. It's not a knot I can untie on command."

"I know." She sat down across from him on an upturned crate, knees drawn up, the storm-mark on her wrist catching the candlelight like a bruise made of lightning. "Wren told me about you. Before she sent me through. She said you didn't lose your gift. You locked it in a room and swallowed the key."

Jonah looked at his hands. Charm-seller's hands now, ink-stained, rope-burned, ordinary. Twelve years of making them ordinary.

"She would know," he said quietly. "She was there when I did it."

* * *

They trained in the flooded lower gallery, where the Undertow kept its boats, because water was honest. Water remembered what it touched. Sable had cleared the space for them at dusk and said only, "Don't sink my skiffs," which was as close to a blessing as the ferrywoman gave anyone.

Jonas stood at the edge of the black water with his boots off, because some part of him still believed you called weather barefoot, the way his mother had taught him, skin to the world.

"I can't do it gently," he warned. "That's the whole problem. That's always been the whole problem."

"You're thinking about the harvest-guard again."

He flinched. The girl missed nothing. She had a way of looking at him that felt less like being seen than being read.

"Every time I reach for it," he admitted, "that's what's there. Wren's face. The field burning. The sound the guard made when—" He stopped. Twelve years and he still couldn't say it. "The storm doesn't come when I call. It comes when I remember. So I stopped calling. I stopped remembering. I made myself small enough that even the sky forgot me."

Mirabel was quiet for a moment. Then she waded into the shallows, hem of her stolen coat darkening, and held out the locket.

"Then don't call it alone," she said. "I've got half a king's memories in here and a mark I don't understand, but when you're near me, things go quiet. When you were scared at the sea-gate, I wasn't. When I panicked in the tunnels, you weren't. Sable says marks like ours are tuned to each other. Like strings." She shrugged, eleven years old and matter-of-fact about miracles. "So hold the string."

* * *

It should not have worked. Jonah knew everything that shouldn't work about it—the age of her, the danger of her, the way every gifted thing they did together painted a target on them both. He knew it and he took her hand anyway, because twelve years of smallness had taught him exactly one lesson worth keeping: the safe thing and the right thing almost never lived on the same street.

Her palm was warm. The mark pulsed once against his skin, and he felt it answer something deep in his chest, a second heartbeat he'd pretended for over a decade was indigestion or guilt or fear.

"Now," Mirabel said. "Don't remember the fire. Remember the rain after."

And Jonah reached.

* * *

The storm came up out of him like a diver breaking surface.

Not the ravening thing of his nightmares—not first. First it was just pressure, a gathering, the candle flames leaning toward him though no wind moved. The water in the gallery shivered into rings. Somewhere above them, muffled by stone and street, Greyhollow's clouds began to mutter.

"There you are," Mirabel whispered, delighted, and the delight went through their joined hands and into the heart of the thing, and the storm *liked* her. He could feel it turning toward her warmth the way cold rooms turn toward open doors.

"Careful," he breathed. His voice shook. "It's eager. It's been waiting."

"Let it wait a little longer. Feel around it. What do you see?"

He closed his eyes and did something he had never dared: instead of clamping down, he opened. And the storm opened back.

What he saw nearly dropped him to his knees.

The storm had a shape. Not a mind, exactly—storms didn't think—but a memory-structure, a pattern worn into it like a path worn into grass. Every storm he'd ever called had walked that path. And at the center of the pattern, wound through it like wire through a cable, was a thread that did not belong. A thread of someone else's making.

Dark. Patient. Tasting of rust.

"Jonah?" Mirabel's grip tightened. "Your nose is bleeding."

He knelt slowly in the shallows, one hand still in hers, and followed the thread.

* * *

He saw the harvest-field again. Of course he did. The thread led straight through it, because the thread had been laid there.

But this time he saw it whole, and from outside himself—and the sight broke something that had been broken wrong for twelve years.

He saw himself, seventeen years old, wild with grief and terror, calling a storm to save the harvest from blight-wind. He saw the storm rise obedient and enormous. And then he saw the thread *pull*. Saw something vast and hungry lean against the fabric of the evening from the far side of the Veil and shove—shove the lightning a hand's width off its true course. A hand's width. The difference between the guard-post empty and the guard-post full.

He saw Wren run toward the burning post, not away. He saw her raise her own small mark against the falling bolt. He saw the Veil *take* her—saw the air fold around her like a page turning, saw her vanish mid-scream into somewhere else, while the world below recorded only a body that was never found and a brother who believed he'd killed her.

And beneath it all, under the thunder, under the grief, he heard it. A voice like a key dragged through a lock. Amused. Satisfied.

*There. Now he will carry it. Now he will make himself small. Now the strongest storm-caller in three generations will bury himself in a city without magic and stay buried until we need the sky.*

The Regent of Rust had authored his shame. Had written his exile line by line, the way Jonah wrote novels—except this book had a boy for an inkwell and a village for a page.

* * *

Jonah came back to himself screaming.

Not crying. Screaming—a raw, animal sound that bounced off the flooded gallery walls, and the storm came with it, tearing loose, the candles dying, the water rising in a standing wave around his knees, lightning crawling the ceiling beams like white roots.

Mirabel did not let go of his hand.

"Jonah." Small voice, iron core. "Jonah. Look at me. It lied to you. Look at me."

"It killed them." The words tore coming out. "It made me—it used my hands—"

"It *tricked* your hands." She climbed onto the crate beside him, into the wind, into the light, eleven years old and utterly unafraid of him, which was perhaps the most healing thing anyone had ever done. "There's a difference. Wren tried to stop the bolt. Wren *knew*. She grabbed the storm with her mark—I've seen it, in the locket, I've *seen* her do it—that's why the Veil took her, because she touched it while it was crossing. You didn't kill your sister. You didn't kill the guard. Something wearing the king's crown murdered them with your arms, and it has spent twelve years letting you believe—"

She stopped, because he had gone very still, and the storm had gone still with him.

The rage didn't burn out. It *condensed*. Jonah Fairweather had carried guilt like a stone in his chest for twelve years, and now the stone turned in his hand and he saw it had never been a stone at all. It had been a hook. Placed there. Baited there. And rage at the fisher was a different weather entirely.

The lightning on the ceiling gathered itself into a single slow ring above them, patient, attentive, *his*, and for the first time since he was seventeen years old, Jonah was not afraid of it.

"Oh," he said softly. "Oh, it made a mistake."

"What?"

"It left the thread in me." He laughed, and the laugh came out with thunder in it. "Twelve years it's been sitting in my storm like a splinter, and tonight I finally looked at it—and a thread runs both ways, Mirabel. A thread runs *both ways*."

He closed his fist. Far above, beyond stone and street and law, every cloud over Greyhollow lit silver at once, silent as a held breath, and every hunter on every corner felt the hair stand up on his neck and did not know why.

* * *

They stayed in the gallery until the small hours. He learned his gift again the way a man relearns a language—stumbling, laughing, swearing at his own clumsiness. Mirabel taught him as much as he taught her; her mark harmonized his surges the way a keel steadies a sail, and by the time the false dawn greyed the water, he could raise fog, call a soft warm rain, spin a whirlpool the size of a washbasin. Small magics. Honest ones. Weather as relationship, not weapon—his mother's oldest lesson, finally understood.

He was wrung out and lighter than he had been in years when boots sounded on the stair.

Tam's widow came down two steps at a time, gray-faced, a scrap of paper crushed in her fist.

"News from the palace," she panted. "Smuggler's whisper, third-hand, but the man who carried it has never lied. The prisoners from the raid—they're alive. Sable Voss is alive. They're in the deep cells under the court, and Jonah—" Her voice cracked. "They're being *drained*. A little more every day. The Regent's feeding on them slow. There's maybe a week before there's nothing left to save."

Jonah looked at Mirabel. Mirabel looked at Jonah, and her chin came up in a way that was pure Wren, though she'd never known it.

"We just got our storm back," she said.

"Yes," Jonah said, already reaching for his coat, feeling the sky lean down close over Greyhollow like a friend bending near. "Let's go introduce it to the king."
