## 16. The Rescue of Captain Ives

The palace dungeons smelled like rust and forgetting.

Mirabel had imagined them a hundred ways on the walk down—iron doors, screaming, the Regent's servants with their gray hands. What she found instead was worse in a quieter way: rows of cells where people sat very still, staring at nothing, their mouths slightly open like they'd forgotten what mouths were for.

"They've been drained," Jonah whispered beside her. He was dressed as a records clerk, gray coat, gray satchel, his face made forgettable by one of Sable's smuggler salves. "Not eaten whole. Just... skimmed. Like cream off the top."

"Can they get it back?"

"Some of it." His jaw tightened. "The locket's the only vessel I know that holds memory without eating it. The Regent takes and keeps. Wren's charm gives back."

Mirabel touched the locket through her shirt. It had been warm ever since they crossed into the palace's shadow, warm like a bird cupped in both hands. *Hurry,* it seemed to say. *Hurry, hurry.*

Sable was in the third cell from the end.

She sat on the stone floor with her back to the wall, one eye closed, the other fixed on the door with an expression Mirabel had learned to read as patience sharpened into a weapon. When the lock clicked open under Jonah's borrowed keys, she didn't move.

"Took you long enough," she said.

"You're welcome," Jonah said.

"I wasn't thanking you. I was making a point about punctuality." She hauled herself up by the bars, and Mirabel saw what the cell had done to her—the fine tremor in her hands, the way her good eye kept sliding to the corner of the room as if something lived there. They'd taken things from her too. Not everything. Enough.

"Twenty-three of us," Sable said. "They started with the strongest. The ferry-blessed boy, Tam, the twins from the tannery row. Ives comes every evening at the change of watch. He sits outside my cell and asks me questions I can't answer anymore, because he's already drunk the answers." Her voice didn't shake. That somehow made it worse. "He cried while he did it. Did you know that? The captain cries when he feeds."

"I know," Mirabel said quietly.

Of course she knew. She'd seen Captain Ives three times now—once across a burning square, once through a cellar grate, once close enough to count the gray threads spreading through his eyes like frost through a windowpane. He was being eaten from the inside, and he knew it, and some stubborn drowned piece of him kept reporting for duty anyway because duty was the last thing the Regent hadn't swallowed.

"Here's how it goes," Sable said, and for a moment she was entirely herself, the ferrywoman who'd run the Undertow for thirty years. "There are twenty-three cells and two of you and one of me, and the change of watch is in forty minutes. So we do this fast, quiet, and in order. The strong ones first—they can carry the weak. And if anyone asks about the man in the clerk's coat, he's from the Ministry of Records and deeply boring."

Jonah almost smiled. "Deeply boring. I can do that."

* * *

They moved through the dungeon like water finding cracks.

Mirabel carried the keys. It was her job because she was small enough to pass for a servant girl even without the salve, and because Jonah said her hands shook less than his did, which was a lie, but a kind one. Cell after cell she unlocked: a fishwife whose gift was calling eels, a boy of maybe fourteen who could mend broken glass with a touch, an old man who'd spent sixty years whispering rain onto rooftop gardens. The drained ones came slow and blinking. The stronger ones took their arms and shoulders and half-carried them, and the procession crept along the drainage corridor toward the old well shaft Sable had mapped from memory—from the part of her memory the Regent hadn't reached yet.

Twelve out. Fifteen. Eighteen.

Then, at the twentieth cell, the corridor went cold.

Not winter-cold. *Other*-cold, the cold that came off the black snow, the cold that meant the Veil was thin here and something on the far side was breathing through it. Mirabel pressed against the wall and looked down the passage and saw him.

Captain Ives stood at the far end, alone, no torch, no guards. He was looking at the ceiling as though reading something written there. The gray in his eyes had spread since she'd last seen him. It reached his temples now, silvering his hair in streaks, and when he lowered his gaze and found her standing there with a ring of stolen keys in her fist, he didn't shout. He didn't draw his sword.

He said, in a voice like a man talking in his sleep:

"Are you the one who keeps humming?"

Mirabel's heart slammed. Behind her, Jonah froze mid-step, twenty freed prisoners stacked in the dark behind him like a held breath.

"The lullaby," Ives said. He took one step closer. His boots made no sound, which was wrong, which was the Regent moving him like a hand moves a glove. "I hear it in the places he's emptied. A woman singing. A daughter. He keeps it at the bottom of himself like a coin at the bottom of a well, and when I reach for it, it burns." Another step. His sword-hand twitched at his side, fighting itself. "Who taught you that song?"

"No one," Mirabel whispered. "It came with the locket."

Ives stopped. For a long moment he just stood there, and Mirabel watched something impossible happen: the gray receded, just slightly, like a tide losing its nerve. His eyes focused. His own eyes, brown and tired and human.

"You're the Fairweather girl's messenger," he said. "The one from beyond the Veil." And then, urgent, leaning toward her like a drowning man: "Listen to me. There isn't much of me left that's mine. But there's enough. There has to be enough." His hand found the front of her coat—not gripping, holding, the way you hold a rope. "He means to finish before the solstice. Every gifted mind in Greyhollow, poured into him at the eclipse, so he never has to leave that body again. And the king—" his voice cracked— "the king is still in the tower. Still scratching his name into the wall. I put him there. I carried him up those stairs myself and told myself it was for the realm's safety, and every day I remember less why that felt like mercy."

"Ives," Jonah said softly, from behind her. "Brannock Ives. You arrested my sister twelve years ago. You gave her your cloak because it was raining."

Something broke open in the captain's face.

"I did," he breathed. "Wren. The storm-girl. I liked her. Gods help me, I *liked* her, and then the orders came and I stopped liking anything, I couldn't, he took—" He sagged against the cell bars. "Take them and go. All of them. The east postern gate—the guard rotation has a gap at the fourth bell, I built the gap myself months ago and I don't remember why until right now."

"Why?" Mirabel asked. "Why build the gap? Why fight him?"

Ives smiled, and it was the saddest thing she'd ever seen, because only half his mouth remembered how.

"Because some things sink too deep to drink," he said. "My mother sang that song. Before any of this. Before the queen, before the palace, before him. He reached for it and it burned him, and he left it, and whatever he leaves—" his grip tightened on her coat— "whatever he leaves still belongs to me."

* * *

They got nineteen prisoners through the gap at the fourth bell.

The last four were too weak to climb the well shaft, so Jonah stayed behind with them, hidden in the records room among forty years of confiscated ledgers, while Mirabel went ahead with the others to the Undertow. She hated leaving him. She hated it the way you hate a splinter—small, constant, always there when you press on it.

She came back before dawn, through the drainage tunnels with Sable's map inked on her forearm, and found the records room empty except for Jonah and one other man.

Captain Ives sat on a stool between two shelves of ledgers, gray-eyed and swaying, his sword laid across his knees like something he was guarding rather than carrying. Jonah stood over him with the locket in his open palm.

"He walked in an hour ago," Jonah said. "Put the sword down and asked me to do it. Whatever 'it' is." He looked at Mirabel over the captain's head, and his face was careful in the way it got when he was frightened. "He wants you to try. Says the song reaches further than anything else we've got."

"It might eat what's left of him," Mirabel said. "If I pour memories in wrong—"

"It might," Ives rasped. His voice was mostly gone now, sanded down to gravel. "But here's the arithmetic, girl. By solstice, he'll have all of me anyway. Every scrap. So the worst case is I lose four days early." He lifted his chin, and beneath the frost-silvered ruin of his face Mirabel saw the man who'd given a soaked storm-child his cloak in the rain. "And the best case, I get to be the one who opens the tower door instead of guarding it. Do it."

Mirabel knelt in front of him. Opened the locket.

Inside, the fragment of King Aldric's memory still glowed—that thread of lullaby, of a queen's laugh, of a promise made at the Veil—but around it, caught in the charm-work like flies in amber, were other things now. Small things. Things the locket had gathered on its journey: a smell of rain on a barracks roof. A brown-eyed woman singing. A young soldier handing his cloak to a shivering prisoner.

Wren's charm-work, Jonah had explained once, didn't store memories. It *recognized* them. It found the shape of a self and offered it back.

"Captain Brannock Ives," Mirabel said, not knowing why she used his full name except that it felt like ringing a bell. "Your mother's name was Maren. She sang on the stairs of your childhood house because the echo was better there. You have two brothers and you haven't thought of them in three years because he ate the years, but they're still yours. They're still *yours.*"

She pressed the open locket to his forehead.

The gray flared white.

For one terrible moment Ives arched backward, mouth open, silent, and Mirabel thought she'd killed him, thought she'd poured a river into a cracked cup—and then the light settled, dimmed, sank into him like rain into drought-cracked ground, and he slumped forward and began, very quietly, to weep.

"My brothers," he said. "Toml and Edric. Toml's got the farm. Edric never writes." He laughed through the tears, a wrecked, astonished sound. "Edric never writes. I'd forgotten that he never writes. How does a man forget his own brother doesn't write to him?"

"The same way kings forget their names," Jonah said gently.

Ives wiped his face with the heel of his hand. When he looked up, his eyes were still threaded with gray—half the frost remained, would always remain—but the brown around it was fierce now, awake, a man standing in the ruins of his own house and refusing to leave.

"How much of me is still his?" he asked.

"More than half," Jonah said. "You'll feel him pulling. You'll hear orders in your head that aren't yours. Can you live with that for four days?"

"I've lived with worse for four years." Ives stood, picked up his sword, and slid it home in its scabbard—and the gesture was different now, Mirabel saw. Not a glove being worn. A choice being made. "Solstice night, the eclipse, the eclipse-watch changes at moonrise. I command it. I'll unlock the tower stairs myself and look the other way while you go up." He paused at the doorway, silhouetted against the gray light of coming morning, and turned back.

"The girl," he said. "The Fairweather girl. Is she truly alive? Beyond the Veil?"

"She's waiting at a door of storms," Mirabel said. "She sent me to bring the storm home."

Ives nodded slowly, and Mirabel saw him file that away somewhere deep, below the frost, where the Regent's reach couldn't follow.

"Then I'll see the tower opened," he said, "and may whatever gods are left forgive the rest of us."

He walked out into the morning, back straight, a hollow man walking hollowly toward a crown he meant to betray—and for the first time since the hunt began, Mirabel understood what Sable had been building all these years in the dark under Greyhollow.

Not an army. Not a rebellion.

A place where broken people could remember themselves long enough to choose again.

* * *

They surfaced into the first true snowfall of the season that evening—white snow, clean snow, falling soft over the rooftops of Greyhollow like the city was being forgiven. In the Undertow, the freed prisoners slept in heaps of blankets, and someone had started a soup, and Tam the hedge-healer was telling the story of his own rescue to anyone who'd listen, making himself braver in the telling, which Mirabel thought was probably how most stories worked.

Jonah found her on the steps by the sea-gate, watching the snow come down over the black water.

"Four days," he said, sitting beside her. "Eclipse at moonrise. Ives opens the tower. And then we go up and meet the thing that eats memories, wearing a king's face, on the longest night of the year." He laughed, short and disbelieving. "When you fell through my window, I was selling luck-knots."

"You're going to give it your storm," Mirabel said. It wasn't a question. She'd known since the tower plan was drawn, known the way you know a lullaby. "All of it. I've watched you practice. You're not training to win. You're training to spend."

Jonah was quiet a long time. Snow gathered on his shoulders like the beginning of a shroud, or a blessing; with him it was hard to tell the difference.

"Wren stayed behind that door for twelve years," he finally said. "Caging that thing's hunger with nothing but herself. If she can pay that price, I can pay mine." He looked at her then, and his eyes were wet and steady. "Besides. I'm not doing it alone. That's the whole trick, isn't it? That's what you taught me."

Above them, at the rim of the harbor, the wind changed.

It came off the water in a long low sigh, threading through the sea-gate's iron ribs, and it carried a hum—a familiar hum, a melody rising and falling, patient as tide. Mirabel's hand flew to the locket. It was burning warm now, answering, harmonizing, and the snow around the gate's arch melted in a perfect circle though the air was freezing.

Jonah stood very slowly.

"That's not the wind," he whispered.

"No," Mirabel said, her heart hammering joy and dread in equal measure. "It's not."

The Veil was thinning. And something—or someone—was singing them in.
