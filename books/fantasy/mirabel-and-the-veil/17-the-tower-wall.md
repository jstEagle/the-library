## 17. The Tower Wall

The tower had no door on its first three landings. That was the point of it, Jonah supposed. A king who forgets his own name cannot climb down a ladder he doesn't remember exists.

Jonah climbed anyway, by the light of a shuttered lantern, his clerk's grey coat stiff with someone else's sweat. The rope Ives had left knotted at the fourth-floor window still held—bless the captain's methodical, half-eaten mind—and Jonah hauled himself up through the dark with his palms burning and his heart doing something worse than burning.

The top room was round and cold and smelled of stone dust and old candle smoke. There was one window, barred, facing north toward the mountains where winter was already stacking itself like plates. One bed, unmade for what looked like years. And the walls—

The walls were covered in writing.

Not inked. Not painted. *Scratched*, gouged into the granite with something small and desperate—a spoon handle, maybe, or a belt buckle worn to an edge. Words in a shaking hand, some of them carved deep and confident, others so faint Jonah had to hold the lantern close enough to scorch his sleeve.

*Her laugh was like water over stones.*

*Mira's lullaby. The one about the moon going fishing. She made it up herself. Do not lose this one. DO NOT LOSE THIS ONE.*

*I promised her I would come back whole.*

The same phrases, over and over, dozens of times, some rewritten atop older versions until the letters blurred into each other like footprints in snow. A man carving his own name into the world every day before the tide of forgetting could wash it away.

And there, near the bed, in letters deeper than all the rest:

*My name is Aldric Vane. My name is Aldric Vane. My name is Aldric Vane.*

The man himself sat on the floor beneath that last inscription, knees drawn up, staring at nothing. He was thinner than the coins suggested—the palace minted his face on silver, but no one had told the silver he'd stopped eating properly. His hair had gone white at thirty-five. His hands were ruined, fingertips split and scarred from years of scratching.

He looked up when the lantern light touched him, and Jonah braced for fear, for a cry, for guards.

Instead the king said, quietly: "Are you the new one? They send a new one every few months. You always leave."

"I'm not sent by anyone," Jonah said.

"They all say that." Aldric's eyes were pale, washed-out blue, like sky after rain that never quite clears. "You have ink on your fingers. Clerks have ink on their fingers. Clerks leave."

Jonah crouched down slowly, keeping his hands visible, the way you'd approach a spooked animal—or a spooked child. Mirabel would have known how to do this. She had a way with frightened things, being one herself.

"Your Majesty," he said. "I'm going to read your walls to you."

Aldric blinked. "What?"

"The words you scratched. I'm going to read them aloud. Every night, if I can get back up here. Because I think—" Jonah swallowed. "I think you already know why that matters."

For a long moment the king just stared. Then something moved behind his eyes—not recognition exactly, but the ghost of it, the shape recognition leaves behind when the rest is eaten.

"You sound like my wife," Aldric whispered. "She used to read to me. Before." His hand drifted up, touched the wall, traced the words *her laugh was like water over stones*. "Before the other one came."

*The other one.* So even hollowed, even half-devoured, some part of Aldric knew. Knew what wore his face downstairs, knew what fed at his table and signed his edicts in his hand.

"Yes," Jonah said. "The other one. I'm here because of him."

"Don't say his name in this room." Aldric's voice cracked sharp, sudden, and for one instant there was a flash of the king he must have been—command hardening his spine, grief sharpening his voice. "He hears his name. He grows toward it. Like a plant toward a window." Then the hardness guttered out and he was small again, hunched, ashamed. "Please. Just read."

So Jonah read.

*Her laugh was like water over stones.* He read it twice, three times, letting the syllables settle into the cold air. Aldric closed his eyes. When Jonah reached the lullaby line, the king's lips moved along silently, shaping each word a half-second ahead, like a man walking a path he could no longer see but whose stones his feet remembered.

*She made it up herself. Do not lose this one.*

"She has a good voice," Aldric murmured, when Jonah finished. "Mira. My daughter. She sings it wrong on purpose now, to make me laugh. Or she did. Did she?" His brow furrowed, reaching for something and finding only smooth wall where a memory should be. "How long has it been?"

Jonah thought of the locket humming in his pocket, of Mirabel asleep in the Undertow with a storm-mark on her wrist and a dead queen's ring hidden in her boot. He thought of Sable in a dungeon cell, of Ives's flickering, mending mind.

"A while," he said gently. "But she remembers you. I promise you that."

Something in Aldric's face loosened. He leaned back against the wall beneath his own name and, for the first time since Jonah entered, seemed to actually see him.

"You're not a clerk," the king said.

"No."

"You're one of the marked ones. The ones he hunts." It wasn't accusation. It was almost wonder. "You came *up* here. To the tower. Where he keeps the thing he's eating." A ragged breath of a laugh. "Either very brave or very stupid."

"My sister says the two run in the family," Jonah said, and was surprised to find he meant it as a joke, and more surprised when Aldric laughed too—a real laugh, brief and rusty, startling them both.

Then the king's face changed. Went still. Focused.

"How long can you stay?" he asked, and his voice had dropped into something low and urgent, the voice of a man who knows his lucidity is a candle burning down. "Because I can feel him pulling. He'll notice you're here soon. He notices everything that comes near me—it's why he starves me of visitors. Every mind that touches mine gives me somewhere to... hold on." His ruined fingers curled against the stone. "So if you have questions, ask them *now*. While I'm still mostly me."

Jonab had carried a hundred questions up the tower stairs. Now, faced with the hour he'd begged the universe for, they all jammed in his throat. He forced out the one that mattered most.

"The Veil. How do I open it?"

Aldric went very quiet. When he spoke again, each word came slow and deliberate, a man laying stones across a river.

"You've seen the Regent feed. You know what he takes. Memories. Selves. He eats them because he has none of his own—he's a hunger wearing a shape, and a shape needs mortar." His eyes fixed on Jonah's. "The Veil between the living world and the memory-realm isn't stone. It's not magic either, not really. It's *agreement*. An old bargain, sealed in blood and storm, that says the dead-and-remembered stay on their side and the living stay on theirs. Someone paid enormously to set it. To open it, you don't break it. You *unmake the payment*. You give back what was given."

"What was given?"

"Everything." Aldric's mouth twisted. "That's the cruelty of it. The Veil was sealed by a storm-caller who poured their entire gift into the binding—every storm they'd ever carry, every drop of the power in their blood, spent in one act. That's why it's held for so long. That's why it will take the same to unmake it. A storm-gift, given wholly, freely, to the other side." He paused. "There are perhaps four people alive in this kingdom with that much weather in them. Maybe fewer now, with his hunters busy."

Jonah felt the floor tilt under him, though he hadn't moved. Four people. He could name two without trying: himself, and the girl sleeping below the harbor with his sister's mark on her wrist.

"And the cost," he said, though his mouth had gone dry. "Beyond the gift itself."

Aldric watched him with those washed-out eyes, and Jonah understood suddenly that the king had been waiting for this question the way a condemned man waits for the reading of a sentence.

"The gift isn't separate from you," Aldric said softly. "That's the lie we tell children, that magic is a tool you put down. It's woven through what you are. Pour it all out and what's left..." He lifted one ruined hand, let it fall. "You become what I am becoming. Hollowed. Not eaten—*emptied*. You'd keep your memories, your loves, your name. But the part of you that called the storms, the part that sang to thunder—that would be gone. Forever. And the silence where it lived..." He shook his head. "Most people couldn't bear it. Most people who try don't survive the trying."

*Come to the Veil. Bring the storm home.*

Wren's message, carried in a girl's memory across a decade and a veil. *Bring the storm home.* Not *bring yourself*. Not *come die*. Bring the storm.

Maybe Wren had found another way. Maybe she'd found a door that cost less. Or maybe she'd sent for her brother knowing exactly what it would cost, because she trusted him to pay it anyway, and that trust was its own kind of terrible gift.

"There's another way," Jonah said. "There has to be."

"If there is, I never learned it." Aldric pulled himself upright against the wall, wincing. "But I'll tell you what I *do* know, clerk-who-isn't-a-clerk, and you'll want to sit down for it, because it's the reason he keeps me alive instead of finishing me."

Jonah sat.

"The binding that holds the Veil shut has an anchor," Aldric said. "A keystone. Something on *this* side that the whole agreement hangs from. If the anchor is destroyed, the Veil doesn't just open—it *collapses*, both realms bleeding into each other, the memory-realm pouring through into the world of the living. Everything the Regent has ever eaten would come home at once. Every stolen self, every devoured mind, loose in Greyhollow like a flood." He smiled, and it was the saddest smile Jonah had ever seen. "He wants that. Did you understand that? He *wants* the Veil open. He's been starving me, hunting the gifted, hoarding stolen minds—all of it building toward one moment when he can crack the seal and pour himself into the world with everything he's swallowed inside him. Your hunt, your Edict of Cleansing, your black snow—it's all one long inhale before the exhale."

"So opening the Veil—"

"—is exactly what he wants, done his way. Unless." Aldric raised one finger. "Unless it's opened *small*. A crack, not a collapse. Wide enough for one person to slip through, narrow enough that he can't follow, can't pour through. And that requires the storm-gift given freely—but *held*, controlled, metered out like blood from a wound rather than spilled all at once. Which requires a caller strong enough to hold their own death in their hands and pour it slowly." He met Jonah's eyes. "Which requires someone who has spent twelve years practicing being small. Practicing holding back. Practicing *not* spending."

The tower was silent except for wind worrying the barred window.

"You knew," Jonah said. "Somehow, you knew I'd come."

"I didn't know anything." Aldric laughed again, that rusty, startled sound. "I scratched words on walls and hoped. But my wife believed things find their way to where they're needed. She said the world is full of doors and most of them open for whoever knocks hardest." His gaze drifted to the inscription above him. "I miss her. I miss her so much I've carved her laugh into stone forty times and it's not enough. Is she—do you know if she—"

"She's remembered," Jonah said carefully. "By people who loved her. She's safe."

It wasn't the whole truth. The whole truth was humming in his pocket next to a fragment of this man's own memories. But the hour was short, and some mercies were worth their incompleteness.

Aldric nodded, accepting, and then his face did something complicated—grief and resolve wrestling behind the exhaustion.

"One more thing," he said. "When you open your crack in the Veil. When you go through, or send someone, or whatever you're planning—you should know what's on the other side. What he's afraid of." His voice dropped. "There's a keeper. Someone who went through long ago and stayed. Someone who's been holding the far side shut from *within*, all these years, keeping the Regent's hunger caged from behind. He fears her. He fears her more than he fears armies. If you find her—"

The words stopped. Not trailed off—*stopped*, cut clean, like a snipped thread. Aldric's eyes went wide, then glassy, the focus draining out of them like water from a cracked cup.

"No," the king whispered. "No, not yet, I was *talking*—"

His hand scrabbled at the wall, finding the grooves of his own name, and Jonah saw the terror of a man drowning in a room he'd lived in for years.

"Your name is Aldric Vane," Jonah said loudly, firmly, gripping the king's shoulder. "Your wife's laugh was like water over stones. Your daughter made up a lullaby about the moon going fishing, and she sings it wrong on purpose to make you laugh. You promised her you'd come back whole. *Listen to me.* Your name is Aldric Vane."

The king's breathing slowed. His fingers stilled against the stone. Slowly, the panic receded, leaving behind something exhausted and grateful and terribly, terribly fragile.

"You read well," Aldric murmured. "For a clerk."

"I'll come back tomorrow night," Jonah said. "And the night after. As long as it takes."

"They all say that." But the king's hand caught Jonah's sleeve as he rose, grip surprisingly strong. "Clerk. The keeper on the far side. Find her. Whatever else you do—find her first. Because if the Regent reaches her before you do, there won't *be* a far side to come back to."

"I will," Jonah promised.

At the window, rope in his hands, he paused and looked back. The king had already turned to the wall, fingers finding the grooves, re-tracing his own name in the dark. Keeping himself alive the only way left to him—one letter at a time.

Jonah climbed down into the freezing night with the cost of everything sitting in his chest like a stone: a storm given freely, held and metered, poured out slow. A crack in the world. A keeper on the far side who feared nothing except being reached too late.

A keeper. Someone who went through long ago and stayed.

Someone aged barely a day per year, guarding a door of storms.

*Wren.*

He stood in the shadow of the tower, snow beginning to fall—white tonight, ordinary, almost kind—and let himself feel it fully for the first time: not guilt, not grief, but the wild, terrified hope of a man who has just learned his sister is alive, and that saving her might cost him everything he is.

Tomorrow night he would climb again. Tomorrow night he would read the walls.

Tonight, he had a school of gifted children to warn, a girl with a storm-mark to tell, and a storm of his own to begin learning how to spend.

The solstice eclipse was nine days away.

Nine days to learn how to die slowly, and mean it.
