## 18. Crossing the Veil

### Jonah

The eclipse began at dusk, and the city held its breath.

Jonah stood at the sea-gate with Mirabel on his left and Captain Ives on his right, and above them the moon slid into the sun's throat like a coin dropped into dark water. The light went strange—copper, then bruise-purple, then a gray that made no sense at all, the color of a world remembering it had once been something else.

The Veil thinned with it. He could feel it the way you feel a change in pressure before a storm: a tightness behind his eyes, a hum in his teeth. Twelve years he had kept his storm buried so deep he'd almost convinced himself it wasn't there. Now it rose in him like water finding its level, and he let it.

"You're sure about this," Ives said. It wasn't quite a question. The captain's face was still wrong in places—the memory-eaten patches where the Regent had fed, leaving gaps that filled with whatever expression was nearest—but his eyes had come back to himself since Mirabel gave him the sliver of the locket. His eyes were tired and human and afraid, and Jonah trusted them more than he'd trusted anything from the palace.

"No," Jonah said. "But we're going."

Mirabel laughed, short and sharp, the way she did when she was scared and refusing to let it show. "That's the most honest thing anyone's said all week."

The sea-gate was older than Greyhollow itself, a ring of black stone set into the harbor wall, carved with waves that might have been words if you squinted. Sable Voss had told him about it in the weeks after her rescue, in the Undertow's lamplit refuge, her one eye bright with an old grief: *My grandmother's grandmother used it, back when the gifted didn't have to hide. It opens twice a year. Solstices, when the sun forgets which side of the sky it belongs to.*

Beyond the gate, the harbor ice groaned. The long winter had not stopped falling just because Aldric had woken enough to repeal the Edict; the Regent still nested beyond the Veil, still hungry, still wearing its stolen years. And somewhere past all of it, Wren.

*Come to the Veil,* she had said, through a memory carried by an eleven-year-old girl who'd fallen through Jonah's window like a thrown stone. *Bring the storm home.*

"Jonah." Mirabel tugged his sleeve. She was wearing every layer she owned and still shivering, and her wrist, bare below the glove she refused to wear tonight, showed the storm-mark plain: the same spiral of silver lines that had lived on Wren's skin, that had lived on Jonah's own forearm before the exile, before the shame, before he'd learned to keep his hands in his pockets like a man apologizing for existing. "Whatever's over there. Whatever it looks like. You're not allowed to do the thing where you decide it's your fault and stop talking to us."

"I don't do that," Jonah said.

"You absolutely do that," Ives said.

"I've known you two months and I know you do that," Mirabel said.

The eclipse deepened. The last crescent of sun thinned to a wire, to a spark, to nothing, and the world went dark at noon-dark, stars blooming wrong across a blackened day—and Jonah reached out with everything he had spent twelve years not being, and touched the Veil.

It felt like touching the surface of a frozen lake from underneath.

He called the storm. Not the wild thing that had torn the harvest-guard apart when he was nineteen—not the screaming gale of his nightmares—but the small, precise, listening storm he'd learned at the sea-gate, the one that answered because he asked instead of demanded. Wind gathered in the ring of black stone. The carvings lit up pale as moonlit foam. And the air between the gate's archway split along a seam no one else could see, peeling open like the skin of the world was only ever a curtain someone had hung there to keep the cold out.

Through it: snow. Snow that fell upward. A horizon the color of old paper.

"Oh," Mirabel breathed.

"Stay between us," Ives said, and put his hand on his sword even though they both knew steel meant nothing where they were going. "Both of you. If we get separated—"

"We won't," Jonah said, and stepped through the door of the world.

* * *

### Mirabel

The other side was winter the way a story is winter—all the shape of it and none of the warmth, or maybe none of the cold either, just the idea of cold stretched out forever.

Mirabel had expected darkness. What she got was white: a plain of snow that glittered without light, under a sky like frosted glass, and everywhere, everywhere, *things*. Half-buried in the drifts. Sticking up from the ice like trees, except they weren't trees. They were—

"That's a house," she said. "That's somebody's whole house."

It was. A cottage, tilted, its windows dark, furred over with frost—and as she looked at it, she *knew* it. Not recognized it. Knew it, sudden and complete: the smell of bread in its kitchen, a woman singing off-key, a dog named Bristle who was afraid of thunder. None of it hers. All of it inside her head anyway, the way a splinter is inside a thumb.

"It's memories," Jonah said hoarsely. "Everything it ate. It doesn't digest them. It just—" He turned in a slow circle, and Mirabel watched his face do something complicated and terrible. "It keeps them. Frozen. Whole."

The plain went on farther than a plain should. Houses, ships, a carousel with painted horses stiff under ice. A wedding dress hanging in a drift like a ghost deciding what to haunt. A library, doors flung open, books spilling out and freezing mid-fall. Somewhere distant, a bell that never finished ringing, tolling one note over and over like a heart that couldn't move on.

And people. Or the shapes of people. Some of the shapes stood motionless in the snow, faces smooth as eggs, waiting to be remembered again.

Mirabel's hand found Jonah's sleeve without her asking it to. "Are they alive?"

"They're *kept*," said Ives quietly. He had gone very still, staring at one of the standing figures—a soldier, by the coat, frost on his shoulders like epaulettes. "I know him. Private Ellery. He died at Marrow Ford." The captain's jaw worked. "I drank to forgetting him for six years. And here he is. Still standing post."

"The Regent doesn't kill what it takes," Jonah said. "Killing wastes. It just... puts things on a shelf."

"Then we take them off the shelf," Mirabel said, and was surprised by how steady her own voice came out. Eleven years old, four foot ten, armed with a locket and a bad attitude. But the locket hummed against her sternum, warm as a held bird, and it had been humming louder the deeper they walked, pulling them forward like a fish on a line. "Wren's out here somewhere. And Wren's been out here twelve years, keeping that thing caged by herself, and she sent *me*, so the least we can do is walk fast."

They walked fast.

The snow took no footprints. That was the detail that finally frightened her properly, more than the frozen houses, more than the smooth-faced watchers: their passage left nothing. In this place they were already half-memory themselves, already the kind of thing this country could swallow without noticing. She thought about saying so and decided against it. Jonah was doing the thing where his shoulders crept up toward his ears. Ives was counting his steps under his breath, some soldier's habit, holding onto numbers the way the real king held onto names scratched into a tower wall.

You hold on to what you can. That was the first lesson the Undertow had taught her, back when she'd been nobody's daughter and everybody's problem. Sable Voss had said it with her one eye narrowed: *Nobody gets to keep everything, girl. But nobody gets to take what we share.*

The locket flared hot.

"There," Mirabel gasped, pointing.

Ahead of them the plain broke apart. A chasm opened in the ice, wide as a street, deep as a well—and rising out of it, impossibly, a door. Not a door in a wall. A door made of storms: a frame of slow-turning wind and lightning folded quiet, thunderclouds packed dense as timber, rain that fell in place and never landed. It stood alone in the white, ancient and furious and patient, and before it, small against all that weather, stood a woman with her back to them.

She was facing the door the way a guard faces a gate. Shoulders squared. Feet planted. One hand raised, palm out, holding back a storm the size of a cathedral, and even from fifty yards Mirabel could see the silver spiral of the storm-mark crawling up the woman's bare arm, glowing faintly, spending itself hour by hour, year by year, like a candle burning from both ends.

Jonah made a sound Mirabel had never heard a grown man make.

"Wren," he said.

The woman didn't turn. Her voice came back across the snow, worn thin as paper but unmistakable, a voice Mirabel had heard a hundred times in borrowed memories and never once with her ears:

"Don't come closer, Jonah. Please. If it sees you, everything I've done—"

"You're *alive*," Jonah said, and it cracked in the middle, and he was moving, running, the snow taking no prints beneath him, and Mirabel ran too because there was no force in any world that would have kept her back, and Ives swore and followed.

"Wren! *Wren!*"

And the woman at the door of storms turned around at last.

* * *

### Jonah

She looked exactly as he remembered her and nothing like it at all.

Twelve years. He had aged twelve years—gray coming in at his temples, lines around his eyes, a body that ached in the mornings now. Wren had aged barely a day per year, Sable had said, and Sable had been right: she was nineteen still, or nearly, his little sister with the crooked grin and the ink-stained fingers, wearing a coat that had been mended a hundred times by someone who loved her, her face thin and frost-pale and *alive*.

But her eyes were old. That was the difference. Nineteen-year-old face, ninety-year-old eyes. Eyes that had stood at a door alone for twelve years.

"You're dead," Jonah said stupidly, because his mind had shattered somewhere around the word *alive* and was reassembling itself out of whatever pieces lay nearest. "I killed you. I—I saw the field burn, I carried you, I—"

"You carried what was left of my body," Wren said gently. "I know. I watched you do it. I'm sorry, Jonah. I'm sorry I couldn't tell you. When the harvest-guard burned, I was already—already pulled through. The Veil takes what storms break. It's the price of the door, and the door has to be paid." Her voice caught. "I've had twelve years to write you a hundred letters and I never managed one, and now all I can say is that none of it was your fault, and I know you won't believe me yet, and that's all right. We have time."

"Do we?" Ives said quietly.

The word landed like a stone in still water. Jonah dragged his eyes from his sister's face—dragged them, it cost him—to look at the door of storms, and understood what he was seeing.

The Regent was on the other side.

He couldn't see it. He could feel it: a vast attention pressed against the far side of the storm-door like a face against a window, patient beyond all human patience, hungry beyond all human hunger. The door wasn't a barrier the way a wall was a barrier. It was a cage the way a dam was a cage—with everything behind it straining, always straining, and one exhausted woman holding the spillway shut with her bare hands and her birthright and her life.

"Twelve years," Jonah said. "Alone. Holding *that*."

"Not alone lately." Wren's eyes moved to Mirabel, and something in her face came undone, soft and grieving and proud all at once. "Hello, little storm. You got my message."

"You *sent* a child," Jonah said. The fury arrived late, the way it always did, behind the awe. "You tore a hole in the world and threw an eleven-year-old through it—"

"I sent my hand and voice, because it was that or watch the door fail while the Regent wore a king and hunted every gifted soul in Greyhollow to make itself a body." Wren didn't flinch. "I weighed it. I'll answer for it. But not today, and not here, because—" and for the first time her composure slipped, and Jonah saw the nineteen-year-old underneath, terrified, exhausted, *relieved*, "—because it knows you've come. It's felt you since you crossed. Jonah, it's been waiting for you specifically. It has something of yours."

"What?"

"Your exile," Wren said. "Your worst night. The truth of what happened in the harvest-field. It's been holding it for twelve years like bait on a hook." Her old eyes held his. "And I don't know what you'll do when it offers to give it back."

The locket at Mirabel's throat sang out, one clear ringing note, and the door of storms shuddered—and from the far side, through the thunder, through the rain that never landed, something vast and patient and pleased began, very softly, to laugh.
