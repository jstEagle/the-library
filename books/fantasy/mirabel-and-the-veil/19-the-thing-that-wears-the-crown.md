## 19. The Thing That Wears the Crown

The heart of the memory-realm was a city that had never existed.

Jonah walked its frozen streets with Mirabel's hand in his and Ives a half-step behind, boots crunching on snow that was not snow—each flake a memory with nowhere left to go. The buildings rose around them in impossible architecture: a bakery whose windows showed a wedding no one had attended, a schoolhouse with lessons still chalked on the board in a dead woman's handwriting, a harbor full of ships that had never been launched, their sails stiff as paper.

Everything here was something the Regent had eaten.

"It keeps what it takes," Ives said quietly. His voice sounded better out here than it had in the dungeons—less hollowed, though his eyes were still wrong, still full of other people's weather. "It doesn't digest. It hoards. Like a dragon, but the gold is us."

"Then we're standing in the dragon's bed," Jonah said.

Ahead, the streets converged on a hill that should not have been there, crowned by a tower of white stone—and at its top, a door made entirely of storm.

Jonah stopped walking.

He knew that door. He knew it the way you know your own name, the way you know the shape of your own hands. It was built of cloud and lightning and the green-black pressure of a sky about to break, and it hummed with a frequency he felt in his teeth, in the scar tissue of his chest where his storm lived coiled and waiting.

"That's—" Mirabel started.

"I know what it is." His voice came out rough. Twelve years of silence, and every storm he'd ever swallowed was behind that door, or something very like it. "That's a Fairweather door. My mother built doors like that. Wren used to help her."

*Wren.*

She was there. He could see her now, small against the vastness of the door, one hand pressed flat to the lightning as if greeting an old friend. She hadn't aged—not really. A day per year, Sable had said, and the math of it made Jonah's throat close: twelve years gone, and she looked barely more than the girl who had died in his arms in a burning field, if she had died at all.

Because she hadn't. That was the thing he still couldn't make his mind hold. The harvest-guard's death, the fire, the guilt he had carried like a stone sewn into his ribs for twelve years—all of it a lie, or worse than a lie, an *edit*. The Regent had fed him that memory the way you feed a dog a pill wrapped in meat.

And Wren had let him believe it. Had let him carry it. Had stayed here, beyond the Veil, while her brother rotted himself alive with shame in a city without magic.

Anger came first. Anger was easier.

Then they crested the hill, and anger became impossible, because the Regent was waiting for them.

* * *

It sat on a throne of fused memories at the top of the tower steps—a shape that kept trying to be a man and couldn't quite commit. Its face was King Aldric's face, but worn, like a coin rubbed smooth by too many hands. Beneath the skin, things moved: glimpses of a queen laughing, a child's lullaby, ten thousand mornings that belonged to other people, all of them churning behind its eyes like fish under ice.

It wore the crown. Of course it wore the crown. Even here, even in a realm of its own making, it wore the crown.

"Jonah Fairweather." The voice was Aldric's voice, warm and reasonable, the voice of a man who had once been loved by his people. But underneath it ran something older, something like rust and like hunger. "The storm-caller. I've been looking forward to this."

"You have my sister," Jonah said.

"I have nothing." The Regent spread its hands, and the gesture was so perfectly human, so perfectly *Aldric*, that it turned Jonah's stomach. "I am not a thief, Jonah. I am a guest. A very old guest, in a house that was empty when I arrived. Your king opened the Veil himself—did you know that? Grief does that. His queen had just died, and he went looking for her, and he found me instead. And I was so hungry, and he was so full."

Mirabel's grip tightened on Jonah's hand. "You ate him."

"I *kept* him." The Regent's head tilted, and for a moment the mask slipped and Jonah saw what was underneath—not a face at all, but a doorway, and through the doorway, falling. "There is a difference. Everything I take, I preserve. Nothing is lost with me. Nothing is ever lost. Ask him—he remembers his wife's laugh better than he ever did while she lived. I gave him that."

"You gave him a cage," Ives said. His hand had found the locket at Mirabel's neck, or the idea of it; his fingers moved over empty air, remembering. "You gave me a cage. I know what's inside them now. I remember enough to know."

"Yes," the Regent said, and sighed, and the sigh was genuinely sad, which was somehow the worst thing yet. "Captain Ives. My finest work. You were so afraid of forgetting yourself that you forgot yourself. Fear is such an efficient key." It turned back to Jonah, and its attention settled on him like a physical weight, like deep water. "But I didn't call you here to gloat. Gloating is for creatures with time. I am running out of time, Jonah Fairweather. The long winter is nearly finished falling, and when it finishes, I finish with it—unless I complete my embodiment. Unless I come all the way through."

"And you need minds for that," Jonah said. "Gifted minds. That's why the Edict. That's why the hunt."

"The gifted are doors," the Regent said simply. "Every one of you is a crack in the wall between here and there. Twelve years ago there were hundreds of cracks in Greyhollow alone. Now? A handful. You, girl. The ferrywoman. A few frightened healers hiding in cellars. Not enough. Never enough. But you—" and here its ruined-Aldric face did something almost tender, "—you are not a crack, storm-caller. You are a gate. Your family built gates. Your mother built the door your sister is leaning against right now. If I had *you*, whole and willing, I would not need the others at all."

The snow around them shivered. Somewhere in the frozen city, a bell that had never rung began to ring.

"So here is my offer," the Regent said. "Listen to all of it before you refuse. You deserve that much."

* * *

It stood, and the throne dissolved behind it into a drift of small memories—first kisses, last words, the smell of bread—and descended the steps until it stood close enough that Jonah could see the churn of stolen lives behind its eyes.

"I will give you back everything," it said. "Your erased memories. All of them. Do you understand what I'm offering? You don't, because I took them—but I will show you. There was a night, twelve years ago, when you almost understood what your storm truly was. When you touched the truth of it. And I reached through the Veil and I cut that night out of you, because a storm-caller who knows what he is cannot be broken, and I needed you broken. I needed you small. I needed you to spend twelve years selling luck-knots to people who don't believe in luck."

Jonah's breath went out of him.

He had always known something was missing. Not a memory—a *room*. A room in the house of himself with the door bricked over, and sometimes in dreams he stood outside that room and heard weather on the other side.

"There is more," the Regent said softly. "Your sister. You want her free. I can do that. I can open the Veil from this side—I have always been able to open it from this side; it is only the living side that is sealed against me. Wren walks out of here tonight. Whole. Alive. Home. And the Edict ends—the hunt ends—the real Aldric wakes in his tower with his name in his mouth, and Greyhollow forgets the long winter ever threatened it. Peace. Family. Truth. Everything you crossed the ice to win."

The world narrowed to those words. Jonah felt them working on him the way water works on stone—not violently, just patiently, just inevitably. *Wren walking out of the Veil. Wren in the kitchen of the charm-shop, arguing with him about tea. Wren teaching Mirabel the storm-mark's old songs.* He wanted it so badly that his vision blurred, and he hated himself for how badly he wanted it, and he wanted it anyway.

"What's the price?" he managed.

"Such a suspicious question." The Regent smiled Aldric's smile. "The price is the same price I have asked of everyone: the gifted minds of Greyhollow. All of them. Freely given—that is the part that matters, freely given, because a door must open from the inside. You are their gate, Jonah. Open yourself, and bring them through after you. They will not suffer. Nothing suffers with me. Nothing is ever lost. They will live here, in the snow, in the preserved dark, perfect and unchanging and *mine*, and you will have your sister, and your memories, and your peace."

"No," Mirabel said.

They both looked at her. She was eleven years old and shaking, and her chin was up, and her wrist-mark glowed faintly blue-white against the frozen air.

"They wouldn't be alive," she said. "You keep saying 'nothing is lost,' but that's not true. I've got the locket. I've got pieces of the real king, and you know what? He's *sad* in there. Everything you take gets sad. Because it's not being lived anymore. It's just being kept. Like jam." Her voice cracked. "You're not a guest. You're a pantry."

For one instant—one flickering, terrible instant—the Regent's face came apart entirely, and what looked out of it was not hungry but *lonely*, so lonely that Jonah's chest ached in sympathy before he caught himself.

Then the mask reassembled, colder than before.

"The girl has spirit," it said. "I'll enjoy keeping her."

"Wren," Jonah said.

The word came out of him like a blade leaving a wound. He stepped forward, past the Regent, past the offer still hanging in the frozen air like smoke, toward the door of storms and the woman standing before it.

"Wren. Look at me."

* * *

She turned.

Twelve years, and her face was her face. Older by twelve days, Sable had guessed, and it was true—there were faint lines at her eyes that hadn't been there in the burning field, twelve days of aging laid down one per year like rings in a tree. Her hair was longer. Her eyes were the same grey-green as his, and they were full of a sorrow so old it had become architecture.

"Hello, Jonah," she said.

"You're alive." His voice broke on the second word. He had rehearsed this reunion across a thousand sleepless nights in Greyhollow, and in none of those rehearsals had he managed anything better than this—this naked, undignified wreck of relief. "You're alive and you *let me think*—"

"I know." She didn't flinch from his anger. She walked into it, took his hands, and her hands were cold as the snow but steady. "I know what I did to you. I have had twelve years to be sorry for it, and sorry doesn't cover it. So let me tell you the truth instead, all of it, and then hate me if you still need to."

Behind them, the Regent waited with the patience of a thing that had eaten centuries.

"The night of the harvest-fire," Wren said, "your storm didn't kill anyone. No one died, Jonah. Not the guard, not the villagers, no one. The Regent *wrote* those deaths. It reached through the Veil—it was already reaching through then, thin places, our valley was a thin place—and it rewrote the ending. Because it had found me. I was standing too close to the Veil, and I was gifted, and I was *open*, the way our family runs open, and it saw in me exactly what it sees in you now. A gate."

"But the bodies," Jonah whispered. "I *carried*—"

"You carried what it showed you. Grief with weight and temperature. It's very good at that. It has had a long time to practice." Her thumbs moved over his knuckles, the way she'd done when they were children and he'd skinned his knees. "And then it offered me a choice. It does that, when it finds someone strong enough. It offers a choice, because a door must open from the inside—its one law, its one limitation. It said: give me your passage, stay here, be my cage, and I will spare your brother. I will leave him his grief but take his guilt's *cause*, and he will live a small safe life in a city where nothing can reach him."

"You caged it." Mirabel's voice, small and fierce. "That's why you're here. You're not its prisoner. You're its—"

"Lock," Wren said. "Its hunger is bottomless, but its *passage* isn't. It needs a gate to pour itself through, all the way, into the living world. As long as I stand here—as long as a willing gifted soul holds this threshold—it can only reach through in threads. Whispers. Edits. The deaths it wrote. The fear it fed into a king's dreams. Threads, Jonah. It has spent twelve years trying to turn threads into ropes, and the Edict, the hunt, the long winter—all of it is rope-making. But the knot is still me. I am the knot."

Jonah stared at her. The frozen city rang its impossible bell. And slowly, horribly, he understood.

"If you walk out of here," he said, "the knot comes undone."

"Now you see the shape of the gift I'm offering," the Regent said behind him, gentle as a physician. "Freedom for your sister. Truth for you. All it costs is everyone else."

* * *

Jonah turned to face it.

His storm was awake inside him—fully awake for the first time since the sea-gate, since Mirabel's mark had harmonized with his and shown him that power was a relationship and not a weapon. It pressed against the walls of him like a tide against a seawall, and it sang, low and furious, and every note of it said: *this thing edited your grief, authored your shame, starved your king, hunted your neighbors, and put a child on an ice floe to do its errands.*

But the Regent's offer still glittered. That was the obscenity of it. The offer was *real*. Wren could walk out. The room with the bricked-over door in the house of himself could be opened. He could be whole, and have his sister, and end the hunt—with one word.

*Yes.*

He thought about Tam the hedge-healer, taken in the night for growing feverfew too well. About Sable Voss, one-eyed and iron-spined, rotting in a dungeon for the crime of ferrying the frightened. About Aldric in his tower, scratching his wife's laugh into stone so he wouldn't lose the last of himself. About the Undertow's survivors sharing memories in candlelight like communion wine, so that no one could be stolen whole.

About Mirabel, standing beside him with her chin up, calling the devourer of worlds a *pantry*.

"Here's my counter-offer," Jonah said.

The Regent's ruined face sharpened with interest.

"You keep what you've taken," Jonah said. "All of it. Every memory, every stolen morning, every scrap of the king. Keep it forever. I won't ask you to give any of it back—you couldn't anyway, could you? That's the truth under the truth. You don't preserve. You *can't let go*. That's not hunger, that's a hole, and no amount of eating fills a hole." He stepped closer, and his storm rose in him until frost steamed off his shoulders and the frozen city's lights guttered. "So I'm not going to take anything from you. I'm going to give you something instead. I'm going to give you somewhere to *put it all down*."

The Regent went very still.

"You want embodiment. You want through. You want to be a self instead of a swarm of other people's selves. Fine. There is a way. Not your way—*our* way. A binding that anchors instead of devours. A lullaby that gives every stolen soul inside you somewhere to return to. A king who wakes up whole because the storm that sealed him also set him free." Jonah's voice didn't shake. For the first time in twelve years, nothing in him shook. "My sister held the knot alone for twelve years. She shouldn't have had to. None of us should have to hold anything alone. That's the whole secret, you rusting, starving, miserable thing. That's what you never learned, eating your way through eternity. You don't fill the hole by taking. You fill it by being *held*."

"You would bind me," the Regent said. Not angry. Almost curious. Almost, terribly, hopeful.

"With love," Jonah said. "Which is the one thing you've never been able to steal, because it only works when it's given."

Silence. The bell stopped ringing. In the white quiet, snow that was memory drifted down between them, each flake somebody's wedding day, somebody's last words, somebody's ordinary Tuesday, waiting to be either treasure or burden depending on what happened next.

Then the Regent laughed—a sound like a palace settling, like rust advancing, like a king waking from a dream he couldn't remember.

"Then come and try," it said, and the frozen city began, all around them, to melt.

Behind Jonah, Wren pressed her palm flat against the door of storms, and the door began, slowly, to open—and through the widening seam came a sound Jonah had not heard in twelve years and would have known blindfolded, drunk, dying, or dead:

Thunder. His thunder. Coming home.
