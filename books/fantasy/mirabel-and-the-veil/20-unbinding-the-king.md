## 20. Unbinding the King

The Regent of Rust wore Aldric's face the way a coat wears a hanger.

It stood at the heart of the memory-realm, in a cathedral built from everything it had ever eaten—a nave of frozen laughter, pillars of first kisses and last words, a ceiling of stolen childhoods glittering like frost. And in the center, on a throne that had once been a bed where a queen died, sat the thing that had ruled Greyhollow for twelve years in a dead man's shape.

Jonah walked toward it across the ice, and his storm walked with him.

He felt it now the way he hadn't since he was nineteen—every cloud over the living world leaning toward him like a dog hearing its name. Twelve years of being small, of selling luck-knots that did nothing, of flinching from rain. All of it ended here, on this white plain where the Regent kept its feast.

"You brought it," the Regent said. Aldric's voice, but wrong—the way an echo is wrong, arriving a half-beat late to itself. "The storm. I've been so hungry for it, Jonah Fairweather. Do you know how long I've waited? I planted you like a seed. I fed your sister's death into your hands and watered you with shame, and now you've grown all the way back to me."

"I know what you did." Jonah's breath came out as lightning-fog. Behind him, Mirabel's small hand gripped his coat. Ahead, past the throne, Wren stood at the door of storms—his sister, twenty-nine years old and barely changed, her storm-mark burning silver on her wrist like a brand of office. "You wrote my exile. You ate the harvest-guard's deaths into my memory until I couldn't tell what I'd done from what you'd done to me."

"Yes," the Regent said simply. "That was my best work. Grief is such a rich soil." It rose from the throne, and its shadow was not a man's shadow but a swarm—thousands of shapes pressed flat together, every gifted soul it had ever swallowed, folded into its darkness like letters never sent. "And now I offer you terms, because I am generous. Give me the city's minds—all of them, every witch and hedge-healer and storm-caller in Greyhollow—and I will give you back what I took. Your true memories. Your innocence. And Wren, whole, walking out of this place beside you. Everyone lives. Isn't that what you've wanted? To stop paying?"

Jonah looked at Wren.

She shook her head slowly. *Don't*, her eyes said. But she was smiling too, the way she used to smile before thunderstorms when they were children, standing on the headland daring the sky to touch them.

"It's a lie," Jonah said. "You don't free anyone. You just file them."

"Everything is filed eventually," the Regent said. "Kings. Queens. Little girls who fall through windows." Its hollow eyes slid to Mirabel, and the temperature dropped so hard the ice sang. "I remember her. She was delicious."

Mirabel stepped forward.

She was eleven years old and her boots were too big and she had been running for most of her life, and she planted herself between Jonah and the thing wearing the king's face with her chin up and the locket clenched in her fist.

"No," she said. "You don't get to eat anybody else. Not ever again. Because we figured something out, me and Sable and everyone in the Undertow—we figured out that memories you *give away* can't be stolen. So we're giving them back. All of them. Every single one you took."

She opened the locket.

* * *

The lullaby began the way all the best magic begins: quietly.

It was the queen's song—the one Aldric scratched into his tower wall each morning as it faded, the one Mirabel had carried in a humming locket through smuggler tunnels and hunter raids and the long black snow. Wren's voice joined hers from the door of storms, and the two voices braided together, high and low, and the frozen cathedral *shivered*.

Because the lullaby wasn't just a song. It was an address. A place where a self could stand up and be counted.

Every stolen memory inside the Regent heard it.

Jonah saw it happen. The swarm-shadow behind the thing's shoulders began to stir—not unraveling yet, but turning, the way sleeping people turn toward a window at dawn. Somewhere in that dark press of shapes, a hedge-healer named Tam remembered his mother's kitchen. A ferrywoman's laugh found its own name. Thousands of eaten selves, held flat for years, began to remember they were three-dimensional.

"What are you doing?" the Regent snarled, and for the first time its voice cracked down the middle, two voices, ten, a thousand. "Stop *singing*—"

"Come home," Wren called over the song, to the shadows, to the eaten ones. "All of you. There's room. There was always room—you just had to be invited."

And Jonah raised his hands and called the storm.

* * *

It came through the Veil like a cavalry charge.

Not the small polite weather he'd practiced at the sea-gate—this was everything he'd buried for twelve years, every swallowed thunderhead, every night he'd lain awake pressing lightning down into his chest like a cork in a well. The sky above the memory-realm tore open, and his storm poured through, and it was *glad*. That was the terrible, beautiful truth of it. It had never stopped loving him. Not through the exile, not through the shame, not through the years of useless charms. It had only been waiting for him to ask.

"Jonah!" Wren shouted. "The binding! Now!"

The binding was a thread—he'd seen it in Aldric's one lucid hour, described in the king's cracking voice: a filament of storm-light running from the heart of the memory-realm down through the Veil, through the palace, into the wasting body of the real king in his locked tower. The Regent had spun it to feed. But a thread runs both ways.

Jonah seized it.

The moment he touched it, the Regent's whole history flooded into him—every mind devoured, every fear converted into law, the long patient centuries of hunger wearing borrowed faces—and he understood why Aldric had lasted even one year against this. Because underneath the feeding, underneath the rust, the Regent was nothing but terror. An old, old fear of ending, of being forgotten, of going dark. It ate memories because it could not bear the silence where its own should have been.

*You're just a hole*, Jonah thought, pouring himself into the thread. *A hungry hole wearing a crown.*

And then he gave it everything.

His storm left him the way a river leaves a canyon—not all at once but completely, scouring him clean. He felt it go: the charge in his blood, the pressure behind his eyes, the low animal hum he'd carried since birth. He felt it run down the binding-thread like lightning down a mast, and he aimed it with the last of his intent at one point, one anchor, one sleeping man in a tower who had scratched his wife's laugh into a wall so he wouldn't lose it.

*Hold on*, Jonah told him, across the impossible distance. *Your queen is laughing. Your daughter is singing. Come back.*

The thread blazed white.

* * *

The Regent screamed with a thousand voices, and then, gradually, with none.

It came apart the way frost comes off a window when a warm hand touches it—not violently, but inevitably, each stolen self peeling away along the path of the song. Mirabel watched them go: shapes rising out of the swarm-shadow like sparks off a bonfire, each one flickering briefly into focus—a young woman with dye-stained fingers, an old man holding a fiddle, Tam, *Tam*, whole and blinking and confused—before streaming down through the thinning Veil, home to their bodies, their beds, their waiting lives.

The thing on the throne shrank. The coat lost its hanger. By the end there was only a small gray knot of hunger crouched on the steps, no bigger than a child, clutching at the last scraps of itself.

Wren knelt in front of it.

Jonah, empty and swaying, saw his sister cup the remnant in her hands the way you'd hold a dying bird. He couldn't hear what she said. But he saw the gray knot listen. And he saw it let go—unclenching, at the very last, into a fine rust-colored dust that the storm-wind carried up and scattered across the frozen plain, where, here and there, tiny green shoots were already breaking through the ice.

The lullaby ended.

Silence, real silence, fell over the memory-realm for the first time in centuries.

Then, far away—through the Veil, through the city, faint as a bell under water—a sound Jonah had never heard: the great bells of Greyhollow, ringing without orders. And beneath them, a roar. Ten thousand voices waking at once, remembering themselves, remembering their neighbors, remembering a king.

In a locked tower, Aldric Vane opened his eyes, whole, and read the words on his wall, and wept because he already knew what they meant and because he still knew his own name.

"He's awake," Mirabel whispered. She was crying without any fuss about it, the way she did everything. "Jonah. He's *awake*."

Jonah tried to answer and found he had no voice left either. His storm was gone. All of it. What remained was just a man on his knees in the snow, forty-one years old, cold in the ordinary way, lighter than he had ever been in his life and grieving it already.

Wren came to him.

She crossed the ice with her storm-mark dimming to embers—her work nearly done, the door of storms behind her guttering—and she knelt and took his face in her cold hands, and for a long moment neither of them said anything, because there are no words for getting someone back twice.

"You gave it all," she said finally. Her thumb wiped lightning-dust from his cheekbone. "Oh, Jonah. You absolute idiot. You brave, stubborn, *Fairweather* idiot."

"I had to." His voice came out wrecked. "It was the only thread strong enough. And—" He laughed, or sobbed; from the outside they sounded the same. "I wanted to. That's the strange part. Twelve years I thought my gift was a curse, and the second I spent it saving someone, it was the easiest thing I've ever done."

"That's what I tried to tell you," Wren said softly. "Power isn't what you have. It's what you *hand over*."

Behind them, the Veil shimmered.

* * *

They saw it together—the three of them and the locket and the green shoots and the ringing bells on the far side of everything.

The Veil was closing. The Regent's death had cut the wound's edges loose, and the membrane between the living world and the memory-realm was drawing shut like skin over a healed cut, slow and silver and irreversible. Through it they could see Greyhollow: the ash-snow turning ordinary and white, hunters dropping their chains in the streets, a one-eyed woman in a cell lifting her head as her own memories came flooding home to her.

But the Veil closed from one side only.

"The far side," Wren said quietly. She was looking at it the way a sailor looks at weather. "Someone has to be here to pull it shut. If it closes crooked—if it tears instead of seals—everything the Regent was will leak back through. Every hungry scrap of it. And the next one won't be stupid enough to wear a crown."

"How long?" Jonah asked, though he already knew.

"A year," Wren said. "Maybe ten. Time moves strangely here—I've aged a day for every year out, and I can hold the seam that long if I'm careful." She turned to him, and she was smiling, and it was the worst smile Jonah had ever seen, because it was her real one, the headland smile, the dare-the-sky smile. "I stayed willingly, Jonah. I chose this door. Don't you dare make my choice smaller by making it tragic."

"I'm coming back," Jonah said. "When it's sealed. When it's safe. I'll find a way—"

"Maybe," Wren said. "Maybe not. That's the bargain, and we both know it, and we're going to be honest with each other for once in our stubborn lives." She crouched down to Mirabel's height and took the girl's hands, locket and all. "So here's what happens instead. You"—she tapped the girl's storm-mark, and it flared silver, harmonizing with the ghost of Jonah's emptied one—"you take my brother home. Feed him. Make him sell charms that actually work, he'll be terrible at it. And every solstice, you come to the sea-gate, and you sing the lullaby, and I will listen. I promise you that. I will *always* be listening."

Mirabel threw her arms around Wren's neck and held on the way drowning people hold driftwood, and Wren held her back, and neither of them let go until the Veil's light began to pulse faster, warning them, hurrying them.

Jonah kissed his sister's forehead, storm-less, ordinary, a man saying goodbye.

"Bring the storm home," he echoed—the message she'd sent him, years ago, through a runaway girl and a humming locket. "I didn't bring it home, Wren. I gave it away."

"That *was* bringing it home," she said. "Home was never a place, you fool. Home is whoever you'd give it up for."

She stepped backward through the door of storms as it narrowed to a seam, to a thread, to a line of silver light in the ice—and the last thing they saw was her hand, raised, storm-mark blazing, waving goodbye with her fingers spread wide the way she had waved from the headland when they were children, daring the sky, unafraid.

The Veil closed.

The snow over the memory-realm began, very gently, to melt.

And on the living side, in a city waking up free, Jonah Fairweather stood in the falling white with an eleven-year-old girl's hand in his, listening to the bells ring in a new age—and felt, faint and far and unmistakable, a hum in the wind that sounded almost exactly like his sister, singing.
