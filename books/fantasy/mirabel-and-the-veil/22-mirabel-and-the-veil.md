## 22. Mirabel and the Veil

One year later, and Greyhollow still smelled of salt and bread in the mornings, which Mirabel had decided was the smell of being safe.

She woke before the bell the way she always did—old habit, the feral part of her that had never quite learned to trust a roof—and lay listening to the school breathe around her. The old charm-shop on Lantern Row had grown into something Jonah never quite admitted he'd wanted: three classrooms where there had been one, cots in the attic for children whose families couldn't know what they were, a yard behind the building where the frost sometimes arranged itself into spirals no one asked it to make. Above the door, someone—Sable, probably, with her one eye and her terrible taste—had hung a sign that read FAIRWEATHER'S SCHOOL FOR THE GIFTED AND OTHERWISE INCONVENIENT.

Mirabel was eleven going on twelve now, and she had learned to braid her own hair over the mark on her wrist, though she didn't hide it anymore. Nobody hid anything here. That was the whole point of the place.

Downstairs, she could hear Jonah moving through the kitchen the way he did every morning: kettle first, then the bread, then the long pause at the window where he checked the sky. He still checked the sky. He would check the sky until he died, Mirabel suspected, even though his storm was gone, even though the Edict was ash, even though King Aldric himself had stood on the palace steps last midwinter and named the gifted protectors of the realm while snow fell clean and white for the first time in a year.

She padded down the stairs. Jonah stood at the window with two cups of chicory, and he handed her one without looking, because he always knew when she was on the stairs. Twelve years of hiding had made him a man who noticed everything; a year of family had made him a man who used it for better things.

"Sky's clear," she said.

"Mm." He drank. "Clear skies make me nervous."

"Clear skies make you nervous, storms make you nervous, fog makes you nervous."

"I contain multitudes," Jonah said, "of nervousness."

But he smiled when he said it, and Mirabel counted that smile the way she'd once counted exits from a room. It was a good morning. She could tell by the set of his shoulders, by the fact that he'd already laid out six bowls instead of five, because Tam the hedge-healer was bringing his granddaughter today, and Jonah always set an extra bowl for whoever might arrive hungry. He'd done it every single day since the school opened. Some days nobody came. He set the bowl anyway.

That, Mirabel thought, was the difference between the man who had sold useless charms and the man who ran this school. Same hands. Different weather.

* * *

The morning lessons went the way mornings went: chaos wearing the costume of curriculum.

Mirabel taught the youngest ones now—reading, arithmetic, and the third subject Jonah had added to the schedule himself, written on the board in his careful clerk's hand: *Knowing What Your Gift Is Before It Knows What You Are.* Six children sat in a half-circle on the floor, and Mirabel held up a luck-knot, one of the old useless charms from Jonah's shop days, kept as a teaching tool.

"Now," she said, in her best imitation of Sable Voss's ferrywoman growl, "who can tell me why this knot is a lie?"

A small boy named Pip, whose gift was making candles burn blue, raised his hand so hard he nearly fell over. "Because it doesn't do anything!"

"It doesn't do anything," Mirabel agreed, "because the man who made it was hiding. But here's the question." She turned the knot over in her fingers. "If I tie this same knot *meaning* something—if I tie it for luck, really meaning it, the way my—" she paused, the way she still paused, "—the way Wren would have tied it—does it work then?"

The children considered this with the ferocious seriousness of the very young.

"Maybe," said Pip.

"Maybe," Mirabel said, "and that maybe is the whole lesson. Magic isn't just power. It's relationship. That's what Jonah says. Power is what you can do. Relationship is who you're doing it *with*." She set the knot down. "A storm alone is just destruction. A storm with someone standing beside you—that's weather. There's a difference."

Pip frowned. "What's the difference?"

"You'll know it someday," Mirabel said, "when you find your person. Or your persons." She thought of a smuggler tunnel, a one-eyed woman laughing, a broken captain learning to be whole, a man setting out an extra bowl. "You'll probably get more than one. They come in bunches, like grapes."

At noon Jonah took over, and Mirabel slipped out to the yard, where the frost spirals grew thickest in the corner by the wall. She crouched and pressed her palm flat against the ice, over the mark on her wrist, and let herself feel for the hum.

It was faint this time of year. A thread of sound under the world, like a finger run around the rim of a glass. But it was there. It was always there.

*Come solstice,* the hum seemed to say, in no words at all. *Come solstice, come to the water.*

"We'll be there," Mirabel whispered. "We're always there."

* * *

They went to the sea-gate on the evening of the winter solstice, the way they had gone every solstice since the Veil closed.

The harbor had changed. The ice that had once crept toward sealing it forever now broke apart each spring and reformed each winter in clean white sheets, and the smuggler tunnels beneath Greyhollow had become something almost respectable—the Undertow ran supply routes openly now, Sable Voss at the helm of an honest fleet with dishonest origins, and the sea-gate itself stood open to the winter air, its great iron leaves pinned back by order of the crown. Aldric had wanted to tear it down. Jonah had talked him out of it. *Some doors should stay doors,* Jonah had said, *even if we never walk through them again.*

So they walked through it anyway, once a year, because it wasn't a door anymore. It was a window. And windows were for looking through.

Jonah carried a lantern. Mirabel carried the locket.

The locket had been empty since the night the Regent unraveled—its stolen memories gone home to their owners, its silver shell just silver now. But Mirabel wore it anyway, because it had carried a king's memories and a sister's message and a sliver of Captain Ives's mind, and things that have carried that much shouldn't be thrown away. Sable had said that once, drunk on plum brandy: *Vessels remember what they've held. That's why ships are haunted and cups are lucky.* Mirabel believed her about the cups.

They stood at the gate's edge where the stone met the water, and the wind came off the harbor carrying salt and the far-off sound of the city's solstice bells, and they waited.

It didn't take long. It never did.

The hum rose out of the wind—not from any direction, but from everywhere, from the space between the air and the water, thin and bright and unmistakably *hers*. Wren's hum. The sound Mirabel had heard through the locket a hundred times before she ever knew whose it was, the sound that had led a feral girl through a window and into a life.

Jonah closed his eyes. He did this every time, and every time his face did the same thing: grief and joy shaking hands and refusing to let go.

"She sounds stronger," he said quietly. "Than last year."

"She does." Mirabel tilted her head, listening the way she'd learned to listen—with her wrist, with her blood, with whatever piece of Wren lived in the storm-mark they shared. "There's more of her in it. Like she's not holding the door so hard anymore. Like it's gotten easier."

"The Veil settles," Jonah said. "Aldric's scholars say the binding deepens every year. Maybe holding it gets lighter the longer it holds." He opened his eyes and looked out at the dark water, at the horizon where the memory-realm lived on the other side of everything. "Or maybe she's just gotten good at it. She was always better than me. At everything. Don't tell her I said that."

"She can't hear you."

"She can *always* hear me," Jonah said. "That's what little sisters are."

And the hum—Mirabel would have sworn this, later, would have sworn it on the locket itself—the hum stuttered. Just slightly. Just the way a laugh stutters out of someone trying to hold it in.

Jonah heard it too. His breath caught, and then he laughed, rough and wet, and wiped his face with his sleeve, and said, to the wind, to the water, to the door that was only a window now: "Hello, Wren."

The hum wrapped around them both, warm as a blanket, brief as a visit, and then it settled back into the ordinary sound of the sea, and the bells rang the solstice hour across Greyhollow, and that was the ritual entire. No speeches. No miracles. Just a brother and a daughter-figure and a sister-on-the-other-side, keeping an appointment.

On the walk home, Mirabel took Jonah's hand. He let her, which he hadn't, a year ago. He'd flinched from touch then, the way people flinch from things they think they've lost the right to. Not anymore.

"Pip asked me today what happens when he's grown," she said. "Whether he has to leave the school."

"What did you tell him?"

"That he could stay forever if he wanted, but that most people don't want to, eventually. That leaving isn't the same as losing." She kicked a crust of ice off the cobbles. "Was that right?"

Jonah was quiet for half a block. Then: "When I was your age, I thought my gift was a thing inside me. A weapon I was carrying around. Something to be buried or aimed." He squeezed her hand. "It took me thirty years and losing everything twice to learn it's not a thing at all. It's a thread. And threads don't live inside you—they run *between* you and everyone you love. You can't bury a thread. You can only cut it, or follow it."

"So Pip—"

"So Pip will leave, someday, and the thread will stretch, and it won't break. None of ours have." He glanced down at her, and in the lantern light his face was the open one, the one she'd had to earn, the one she'd earned. "Even hers. Even across the Veil. Especially hers."

* * *

Back at the school, the children were asleep, and Sable was awake, because Sable was always awake, occupying the kitchen like a ship at anchor. She poured them plum brandy without asking—watered for Mirabel, unwatered for Jonah—and looked them both over with her one eye, which missed nothing and forgave most things.

"Well?" she said. "How's our lighthouse keeper?"

"Stronger," Mirabel said. "Happier, I think."

"Hm." Sable turned her cup slowly on the table. "I sailed past the gate at dawn today. Before anyone was up." She paused, and for once the old ferrywoman looked almost shy. "Thought I heard singing. Real singing, not just humming. Three voices, maybe four. Couldn't make out the words." She shrugged, elaborate and unconvincing. "Probably the wind."

"Probably," Jonah said softly.

"Definitely the wind," Sable said, daring them to argue, and Mirabel didn't argue, because some things you keep quiet and carry close, like a locket, like a knot tied with meaning, like the knowledge that somewhere beyond a sealed door, a woman who had given up everything was somehow—impossibly, wonderfully—finding company in the dark.

Later, in the attic room that had been hers for a year and would be hers for all the years after, Mirabel sat at the window with the locket open in her palm, watching the solstice moon.

She thought about the girl she had been, crashing through a stranger's window with hunters at her heels and nothing in the world but a stolen locket and a mark she didn't understand. That girl had thought magic was survival. Then she'd thought it was sacrifice—Wren behind the Veil, Jonah giving up his storm, everyone paying and paying.

Now she knew better. Now she knew magic was a bowl set out for someone who might not come. A knot tied with meaning. A thread between people, stretching but never breaking. A hum in the wind that meant *I'm still here, I'm still listening, come next solstice.*

Below her window, the frost in the yard was arranging itself into spirals again, delicate and deliberate, reaching up toward the glass like handwriting.

And Mirabel Fairweather—she'd taken the name in spring, and Jonah had cried, and pretended he hadn't—leaned her forehead against the cold pane and read what the frost wrote, the way she was learning to read all the small languages of the world:

*Not the end,* the frost said. *Just the turn of the year. Come spring, come learn what grows.*

She smiled, and blew out her candle, and slept without dreaming of running.
