## 1. The Ledger of Small Mercies

The debt ledger opened with a name that was not mine and a sum I could not read without my hands shaking.

*Blackwood, Tamsin — principal, 4,100 crowns. Interest accrued, ten years: 2,880 crowns.*

My mother's debt. My inheritance. The crown's clerks had written it in their flat, official hand, the kind of handwriting that never apologizes for anything, and every quarter-day I came to the temple scriptorium's back counter and copied the figure into my own book, because copying it made it real, and making it real was the only way I knew how to fight it.

Four thousand crowns bought a lot in Vharen. It bought a merchant's house on the Lantern Row and a season of good wine and, if you were King Aldric's tax-farmer, a new set of boots. What it had bought my mother, before the fever took her, was a coffin with unplaned edges and a daughter who would spend her twenties paying for the privilege of grieving.

I dipped my pen. I did not write *4,100*. I wrote *3,940*, which is what remained after the morning's sales, and I wrote it small, in the margin, where only I would look.

Then I opened the shutters and began selling mercies.

* * *

The scriptorium of Saint Vessa's smelled the way all scriptoriums smell: ink, tallow, dust, and the faint vinegar tang of old vellum. By law it belonged to the temple. By practice it belonged to whoever could pay the temple's fees, and by eight bells I had claimed my corner table by the eastern window, laid out my wares, and become exactly what everyone expected me to be.

A scribe. A charm-seller. Harmless.

"Harmless" was a word I had spent ten years building, stone by stone, like a wall around myself. Harmless charms: knots against nightmares, inked sigils for safe travel, love-tokens that worked no better than luck but cost less than heartbreak. I sold them at three coppers apiece, four if the buyer looked wealthy, two if they looked hungry. I never promised miracles. I never promised anything at all. That was the first rule, and beneath it sat the second rule, the one that mattered:

Never let them see what the ink does when you mean it.

Because here was the truth I carried under my tongue like a hot coin: the charms worked. Not always, not grandly, but they worked, and they worked because of me. When I drew a warding knot, I didn't just draw it — I poured something into it, something that lived in me the way blood lives in a vein, quiet until cut. My mother had called it the gift. The temple called it abomination. And as of last month, King Aldric called it treason.

I kept my prices low and my promises lower, and I watched the door.

* * *

The morning's customers were the usual procession of small fears. A fishwife wanting a blessing for her nets. A boy with a stutter who'd saved six coppers for a charm to steady his tongue before his guild examination — I gave him one for four and told him the words mattered more than the ink, which was true, though not in the way he thought. An old man asked me to write his will and wept twice doing it, once over his daughter's name and once over the price.

By midday I had cleared eleven coppers and copied out two contracts for the temple, and my hands ached in the ordinary way, and I let myself believe, for eleven coppers' worth of an hour, that today would be like every other day.

That was when Tam Rell came in.

I knew him from the tannery row — a big man gone soft at the middle, with burn scars up one forearm and a laugh you could hear across a market square. He'd bought a joint-ease charm from me twice. He always paid full price and always apologized for haggling anyway. Today he did not laugh. Today he stood in the doorway of the scriptorium like a man standing at the edge of deep water, and when he saw me his face did something complicated and terrible.

"Scribe Blackwood." His voice came out low. "Can we— I need to talk to you. Not here."

"Everything can be said here," I said pleasantly. "Everything I sell, anyway."

He crossed the room anyway, ducking under the low lintel, and stood over my table dripping river-damp, and I saw that his hands were shaking. Not palsy-shaking. Fear-shaking. I had seen fear before; I had worn it like a coat for a decade. But I had never seen it on Tam Rell, and it frightened me more than any inquisitor's mask could have.

"It's my girl," he said. "Liss. She's seven." He swallowed. "She took a fever off the tannery water. The physickers say—" He stopped. Started again. "They say there's nothing left to try. They said it this morning, and she's still breathing, so I thought—"

"No," I said.

"You don't know what I was going to ask."

"I know what you were going to ask." I kept my voice level, kept my eyes on his scarred knuckles instead of his face. "And the answer is no. I sell charms, Master Rell. Knots against bad dreams. Ink against bad luck. If your daughter is ill, take her to Saint Ferren's hospice — tell them I sent you, they'll waive the fee for—"

"The hospice is where they're going to die," he said, and now his voice cracked clean through. "You think I haven't been? You think I don't know what the physickers do? They hold her hand and they talk about the Merciful Dark like she's already halfway there." He leaned closer, and lowered his voice to almost nothing. "I've heard things. About you. About your mother. About what the ink does when the right hands hold the pen."

The scriptorium went very quiet around us. Somewhere behind me a temple clerk turned a page. Outside, a cart rattled past, and the light through the eastern window lay across my wares in a long gold bar, illuminating everything I was supposed to be.

"Who told you these things?" I asked softly.

"It doesn't matter."

"It matters enormously." I made myself smile, the harmless smile, the wall-smile. "It matters because whoever told you has put you in danger, and me in danger, and most importantly your daughter in danger, because if you go about Vharen asking scribes to work miracles in the year of Aldric's coronation, you won't get a miracle. You'll get a knock at your door after dark, and masks, and questions your family can't answer."

Tam Rell stared at me. His jaw worked. "So that's it. She dies, and you sell your little knots, and you sleep fine."

"I sleep fine," I said, "because I am careful. Because I have spent ten years being careful, and being careful has kept me alive and out of the wolf-masks' ledgers, and I will not unlearn it now, not for—"

Not for anything, I almost said. But Liss Rell was seven years old, and the sentence died somewhere between my pride and my throat.

"I'm sorry about your daughter," I said instead. "Truly. Take the hospice fee. Take it, please." I pushed three coppers across the table toward him, which was stupid, which was charity I couldn't afford, and he looked at the coins like they were an insult.

He didn't take them. He straightened up, and something in his face closed, the way a door closes, and he said, "I heard you were decent, Scribe Blackwood. I heard wrong, then."

He turned and walked out, ducking under the lintel, and the bell above the door gave its small idiot jingle, and I sat there with my useless copper coins and my harmless charms and my careful, careful life, and I hated every piece of it.

* * *

Here is what I did not tell Tam Rell, because telling him would have been suicide:

I could probably have saved his daughter.

Not certainly. The gift doesn't obey commands; it obeys something deeper than command, some current in me that rises or doesn't. But when I draw with intent — true intent, whole intent — fever breaks. Wounds close. I had done it exactly twice in ten years, both times in absolute secrecy, both times leaving me drained and terrified and giddy, both times worth it, both times nearly fatal in ways that had nothing to do with exhaustion.

The third time, I had sworn, would never come. The second time had taught me what vigilance costs. Vigilance is not a wall you build once. It's a wall you rebuild every hour, every day, forever, and I had been rebuilding mine since I was sixteen years old, and I was tired down to the marrow, and tired walls crack.

I packed up my stall at dusk. I counted my coppers. I wrote *3,928* in the margin of the ledger, and below it, because I could not stop myself, I wrote nothing else, absolutely nothing else, and went home through streets that smelled of rain and frying onions and, faintly, of woodsmoke from the direction of the proclamation boards.

There was a crowd gathered at the board on Tanner's Square. I should have walked past. Crowds at proclamation boards meant taxes, or levies, or curfews — the usual weather of Aldric's reign. But the crowd was too quiet, and quiet crowds are worse than loud ones, so I stopped, and I read over a fishwife's shoulder, and the words went through me like cold water.

*By order of the King: henceforth, any soul found practicing sorcery, witchcraft, or the hidden arts shall be taken, tried, and burned. Any soul harboring such, or concealing knowledge of such, shares their guilt. Informers will be rewarded. The hunt begins at dawn.*

Dawn.

I walked home fast, not running, because running attracts eyes. In my room I lit no lamp. I sat in the dark with my mother's debt ledger open on my knees, and I thought about Tam Rell's closed door of a face, and the things he'd heard, and who he'd heard them from, and whether fear makes men grateful or makes them hungry, and I knew — I knew with the sick certainty of a woman who has spent ten years studying exactly how her life could end — that I had seen the first crack in my invisibility, and that dawn was coming whether I was ready or not.

Somewhere across the city, a seven-year-old girl was breathing. Or not breathing. And somewhere closer, a big soft man with burn scars was deciding what his silence was worth.

I blew out the candle I hadn't lit, out of habit, out of ritual, out of the old prayer of unseen things, and I began, quietly, to pack.
