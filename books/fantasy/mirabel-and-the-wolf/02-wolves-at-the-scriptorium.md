## 2. Wolves at the Scriptorium

The proclamation went up on the temple doors at dawn, and by noon my neighbors had learned to look at each other differently.

I read it twice, standing in the cold with the morning crowd, my breath fogging the vellum's gilt letters. *By order of King Aldric, First of His Name: all bearers of unlicensed magic shall surrender themselves for judgment. Any who harbor, conceal, or aid a magic-bearer shares their guilt. Reward offered for information leading to discovery.* Below the words, stamped in black wax, the seal of a wolf's head.

Someone behind me whispered, "The baker's boy. Didn't his mother always say he had the sight?"

Someone else said, "My cousin's wife. She reads fortunes. Just for coins. That can't count."

Just for coins. I thought of my own stall in the scriptorium's side gallery, the little charms laid out on dyed cloth — knots against fever, folded prayers for safe roads, inked sigils that were *only* ink, only paper, only harmless. Ten years I had kept them harmless. Ten years of measuring every stroke of my pen so nothing living slipped through.

And ten years of knowing it wouldn't be enough forever.

"You've gone pale, scribe," said Ossa the candlewoman, squinting at me. "Bad breakfast?"

"Bad news," I said. "For everyone."

She grunted and turned back to the proclamation, and I walked into the temple with my hands steady and my heart going like a trapped bird.

* * *

Brother Halvard found me at my desk an hour later. He was seventy, bent as a question mark, head scribe of Vharen's temple for longer than anyone could remember, and he had never once in ten years asked me what I really was. He set down a stack of ledgers beside my elbow and did not leave.

"Mirabel." His voice was low. "The client who came to you last week. The one with the sick daughter."

"Maren Cotter."

"She was taken this morning. Inquisitors. They walked her through the market square and she pointed at this temple." He paused. "At you."

The quill in my hand left a blot on the ledger page. A small black bloom, spreading.

"I refused her," I said. "I told her I couldn't help. I told her—"

"I know what you told her." He sat down across from me, slowly, joints cracking like old rope. "But she saw something anyway. They always see something, in the end. You cannot hide a fire by making it smaller, child. Only by putting it out, and yours was never going out."

I looked at the blot. At ten years of careful, cramped, invisible work drying in stacks around me. "How long do I have?"

"An hour. Perhaps less. They'll want witnesses gathered first — Aldric likes an audience." He said it flatly, the way men say things they have decided not to weep about. "There is a postern gate behind the reliquary. The lock is rusted; I will oil it myself."

"Halvard—"

"You will go east," he said, as if I hadn't spoken. "Past the river, past the toll-roads, until you reach the free cities. And when you get there—" He stopped. Looked at me for a long moment with eyes gone milky at the edges but still sharp underneath. "Come with me first. Before you run anywhere."

* * *

The reliquary smelled of dust and lamp-oil and, beneath both, something older — stone that remembered being a tomb before it remembered being a temple. Halvard barred the door behind us and crossed to the far wall, where a saint's sarcophagus stood on carved feet. He pressed his palm against its base, muttered a word I'd never heard him use, and a section of stone swung inward with a breath of cold air.

Inside, on a shelf cut into the rock, lay a single object.

A stone no larger than a plum, dark as garnet, veined with threads of silver that moved — *moved*, slowly, like blood through a heart — when I looked at them directly.

"What is it?" My voice came out strange.

"The oldest thing this temple owns," Halvard said. "Older than the temple. Older than the kingdom, maybe. It is called a memory-stone, and it holds the last recollection of Queen Serevanne — the last true queen of the old line, murdered in her own hall eleven years ago while Aldric burned her banners." He lifted it with both hands, reverent, trembling. "It is proof, Mirabel. Of what Aldric did. Of the lie his whole crown is built on. The free cities would rise if they saw it."

"And no one has carried it east in eleven years because—"

"Because everyone who tried died," he said simply. "Because the last man who held it was my brother. Because carrying it means every wolf-mask in the kingdom hunts you from the moment you take your first step." He turned and pressed it into my hands, closing my fingers over it with his own spotted ones. Its warmth startled me — body-warm, pulse-warm, alive. "You are the only person left in this city I trust with it. Not because you are brave. Because you are careful. Careful people survive long enough to finish things."

"I sell charms," I said. "Knots against fever. I am nobody. I owe the crown four hundred crowns I will die owing—"

"Then you'll owe them running," he said, and for the first time in ten years I saw him smile, and it broke my heart. "Go. Now. The gate's oiled. Don't stop before the river."

Somewhere above us, muffled through floors of stone, a bell began to ring wrong — fast, arrhythmic, panicked. Then shouting. Then the particular splintering crash of a door that has been forced.

Halvard's hands tightened over mine once, hard, and then he pushed me toward the dark passage behind the shelf and turned back to bar the way.

* * *

I did not escape cleanly. No one does.

The postern gate opened onto the temple's herb-yard, and the herb-yard opened onto a lane, and the lane was already full of people running the wrong way — toward the temple, drawn by smoke and shouting, faces lit with that awful appetite crowds get when righteousness is on offer. I pulled my hood down and went with the current's edge, past the chandler's, past the well, and that was where I heard Maren Cotter's voice, high and cracked, coming from the temple steps.

"—her, it was her, she touched my daughter's fever and it *moved*, I saw it move—"

They had given her a red ribbon to wear. Collaborators wore red ribbons now; someone had decided it was kinder than a badge. Her face was swollen where someone had struck her, and she was pointing at the temple doors, and I hated her with a clean, exhausting hatred that lasted exactly as long as it took me to remember her daughter's gray lips, her own shaking hands, the way she'd begged. Then I hated Aldric instead, which was more useful, and kept walking.

Behind me, the wolf-masks came out of the temple two by two, tall in their grey lacquer, snouts smooth and eyeless. One of them carried something bundled in a scribe's robe, and the bundle was not moving, and I understood with a lurch that Brother Halvard had not followed me through the gate. That he had never intended to.

I made it three streets before my legs gave out and I crouched in a tanner's doorway, shaking, the memory-stone burning warm against my sternum where I'd tucked it inside my shirt. Above the rooftops, smoke rose from the temple's east range — the scriptorium, my scriptorium, ten years of my careful invisible life going up in a column of black.

A woman passing yanked her child close as she saw me. "Wolves took the temple," she hissed, not unkindly. "Get off the street, girl."

*I know,* I wanted to say. *I know what they took.*

Instead I got up, and I walked east, and I did not stop before the river, because a dead man had told me not to, and it was the only instruction left in my life that made any sense.

* * *

By nightfall I was in a hay-barn outside the toll-road, wet to the knees, eating stolen bread, and finally — finally — alone enough to take the stone out and look at it.

It pulsed in my cupped palms like a second heart. The silver veins brightened when I breathed on it. And underneath the warmth I felt something else now, something I hadn't felt in Halvard's hands: a pressure, faint and patient, like a voice waiting behind a door for permission to speak.

Eleven years dead, and the queen was still in there. Still remembering.

I thought about what Halvard had said. *Everyone who tried died.* I thought about Captain Veyre's name, which even I knew — the inquisitor who never lost a quarry, whose wolf-mask was said to fit so closely no one had ever seen her face. Somewhere behind me, tonight, she would be reading Maren Cotter's description of a scribe with ink-stained fingers. Somewhere behind me, the hunt had already begun.

I should sell it, I thought. Find a fence in some river town, trade a dead queen's memory for four hundred crowns and a new name and a life.

The stone pulsed against my palms, patient as a debt.

I wrapped it back in cloth, tucked it away, and lay down in the hay to listen to my own heart hammer, knowing that whatever slept inside that garnet-dark thing had just chosen me — and that come morning, everything in the kingdom with a wolf's mask or a reward-list would be choosing me too.
