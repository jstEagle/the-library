## 3. What the Stone Remembers

Three days east of Vharen, the road narrowed to a thread of mud between black pines, and Mirabel finally let herself stop running.

Not because she was safe. Because her legs had stopped negotiating.

She sat down on a fallen log at the edge of a charcoal-burner's clearing, and Dain—Dain, who had followed her out of the burning scriptorium with nothing but a satchel of stolen bread and an expression of pure, stubborn disbelief—dropped beside her and started building a fire like it was any other evening.

"You're going to open it," he said. Not a question. He'd been watching her hold the stone for three days the way a man holds a wound he's afraid to look at.

"I'm going to sleep," she said. "For six hours. Possibly nine."

"Mirabel."

She pulled the memory-stone from her coat anyway. It sat in her palm like a river pebble, grey and unremarkable, warm in a way that had nothing to do with body heat. Head Scribe Ondrel had pressed it into her hands while the temple burned above them, his blood already pooling under the ledger desk, and had said only: *It remembers what they killed to forget. Carry it or bury it, but don't you dare sell it.*

She'd been carrying it. She hadn't decided about the rest.

"It's just a stone," Dain said, which was the kind of thing people said when they wanted to be told otherwise.

"It's not just a stone." She turned it over. Faint veins of silver moved beneath the surface, slow as blood in frost. "It's a recollection. The old kind—the kind they made before the war, when magic wasn't contraband but craft. Someone poured their last hours into it. Everything they saw. Everything they knew." She hesitated. "Ondrel said it belonged to Queen Sarelle. The last of the old line."

"The queen Aldric hanged."

"The queen Aldric says he *inherited* from." Mirabel's mouth went dry. "That's the whole foundation of his claim, Dain. That Sarelle named him heir before the end. Every edict, every purge—it all rests on that one piece of paper."

"And if the paper is a lie?"

"Then everything he's built is standing on nothing." She closed her hand around the stone. "And I'm carrying the proof."

The fire cracked. Somewhere behind them, far back along the road, a dog barked twice and stopped—too deliberate, too far from any farmstead. Dain's head came up. They'd both learned that sound in Vharen.

"We keep moving," he said quietly. "You can open it tomorrow."

But that night, wrapped in her cloak in the hollow of a dead oak's roots, Mirabel held the stone against her sternum the way Ondrel must have held it once, and whispered the unsealing words before she could talk herself out of them.

* * *

The world folded.

She was standing in a corridor of white marble lit by a hundred candles, and she was not herself. Her hands were older, ringed with gold. Her lungs ached with smoke. She was Queen Sarelle of Vharen, and it was the last night of her life.

*Mirabel-the-scribe watched through Sarelle's eyes as soldiers hammered at the doors below. She felt the queen's fear—not panic, but the cold, methodical fear of a woman doing arithmetic with minutes. Sarelle knelt at a writing desk, sealing wax dripping red onto parchment, and Mirabel felt her own throat tighten, because she recognized the document. A declaration of inheritance. A name written in the queen's own hand.*

*The name was not Aldric.*

*Aldric's father was on that page—but not as heir. As the man who had sworn fealty for the eastern provinces, witnessed and countersigned. Sarelle's heir was a girl of seven, her daughter, sent north to the free cities two years before. The queen wrote fast, pressing the seal into cooling wax, and then she took up a second sheet and forged nothing at all: she simply wrote the truth, plainly, in language even a farmer could read, and tucked it inside the lining of a memory-stone's case.*

*"They will burn my letters," Sarelle murmured—and Mirabel felt the queen's lips move, felt the words as her own. "They cannot burn what lives in stone. Whoever finds this: I did not yield my crown. My daughter lives. And the man who takes my throne has taken it with ink and lies."*

*The doors broke. Sarelle rose, drew a knife too small to matter, and turned to face the men in wolf-masks—and the last thing she thought, the thought she poured into the stone with her final breath, was not of vengeance. It was:*

***Let it be carried, not kept. Truth hoarded becomes a weapon. Truth given becomes a dawn.***

*And beneath that, older, half-erased, another voice—not Sarelle's, something vast and weary that had been sleeping in the stone since long before her: a warning, worn smooth by centuries. The stone remembered being fought over. It remembered cities burning the first time, a war so old its name had been forgotten and only its scar remained. It remembered that every hand that seized it had believed itself righteous.*

Mirabel came back to herself gasping, cheek pressed into frozen leaves, dawn bleeding grey through the pines. Dain was shaking her shoulder, saying her name, saying it again.

"You were gone," he said. "Two hours. Your eyes were open."

She sat up and told him everything, her voice steadier than she felt. When she finished, Dain was silent for a long moment.

"So we take it to the free cities," he said. "To the council. They'll—"

"They'll do exactly what everyone always does with this thing." She looked at the stone lying in the leaves between them, ordinary as a river pebble. "Sarelle spent her last breath trying to give it away, and four hundred years of kings have spent theirs making sure no one ever hears it. If I hand it to the council, they'll lock it in a vault and argue for a decade. If I sell it—" her voice caught, "—if I sell it, some merchant buys a crown's worth of leverage and I buy a boat and a new name."

"That's not what you want."

"No," she admitted. "But it's what I can afford."

* * *

The ambush came at midday, at the ford where the road crossed the Ellow Water.

Mirabel saw the trap a heartbeat too late—the charcoal burner's cart abandoned at the near bank, the wrongness of still water, the crows lifting off the far willows all at once. Then the reeds stood up wearing wolf-masks.

"Dain—run!"

He didn't run. He threw himself sideways into the first inquisitor's knees, and they went down together in the shallows, and Mirabel ran the other way because that was the only gift she could give him now. Behind her: shouting, a splash, the flat crack of a weighted net. She reached the alder thicket, the bramble, the steep red bank beyond—

A woman stepped out from behind the alders. Tall, grey-cloaked, unhurried. Her wolf-mask was pushed up on her head, revealing a face that was calm and almost kind, which was somehow worse.

"Captain Veyre," Mirabel breathed.

"You've cost me three days and two good men's dignity." Veyre's eyes went to Mirabel's fist, clenched white around the stone. "The girl from the scriptorium. The charm-seller. You know what interests me most about you? Everyone who betrays you ends up loyal. Even your client, in the end—she wept when she gave up your name." Veyre extended one gloved hand. "Give me the stone and the debt ledger burns tonight. Every copper your family owes the crown, ash. You walk free. You've seen what happens to the alternative."

From the ford, faintly, Dain called her name—once, then not at all.

Mirabel looked at the offered hand. Freedom, itemized and priced. Ten years of small compromises, and here was the largest one, gift-wrapped.

Her fingers loosened.

Then she heard Sarelle's voice inside her skull, patient as water: *Truth hoarded becomes a weapon. Truth given becomes a dawn.*

Mirabel closed her fist tighter and did the only reckless thing left. She lifted the stone high over her head, over the water, over everything—and shouted, loud enough for the whole ford to hear:

"This holds the true testament of Queen Sarelle! Aldric's inheritance is a forgery, and I can prove it—and if you take me, you take me *with* it, and every ear in the free cities will hear why!"

Veyre's composure cracked, just barely, just around the eyes.

"There," Mirabel said, heart slamming, "is the problem with killing me now, Captain. You've just realized it too."

Behind Veyre, at the top of the bank, Dain limped out of the reeds—bruised, soaked, alive, dragging a dropped inquisitor's blade—and grinned at her like a madman.

The captain's hand drifted toward the horn at her belt.

Mirabel ran.
