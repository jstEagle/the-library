## 4. The Unsealing

They let me bathe before the council. I want to believe it was mercy and not theater—a clean witness reads better than a filthy one—but the water in the basin went gray in the first minute, and the woman who brought the towel wouldn't meet my eyes.

The chamber they gave me had a window with a view of the harbor and no latch on the door. I sat on the bed with the memory-stone cupped in both hands and listened to the gulls argue over the free city of Solmere, and I understood that I was not a guest here. I was an exhibit awaiting its plinth.

Three days I'd been in Solmere. Three days since Captain Veyre's wolves lost me at the river ford and I walked the last forty miles alone, hollow-eyed, wearing a dead man's coat. Three days since the gate wardens took one look at me—mud to the knees, a satchel clutched like a child—and sent word up the hill that the fugitive from Vharen had arrived, and she was carrying something.

Now the summons came. A page in council blue, too young to hide his curiosity. "They'll see you at dusk," he said. "The Hall of Nine Voices."

I asked him if I'd be permitted to speak.

He looked genuinely puzzled. "You're to answer questions."

* * *

The Hall of Nine Voices was built for argument. It was a great ring of tiered benches beneath a dome pierced by nine skylights, one for each free city, so that even at dusk the floor stood in a wheel of falling light. Delegates filled the tiers in their city colors—Solmere's sea-blue, Corvane's iron-gray, the rust-red of little Dunmark—and above them all, in a gallery set apart, sat the observers: merchants, priests, and one or two faces I recognized from wanted bills as bounty-men, taking their leisure.

They put me in the center of the light wheel, alone, and for a long moment nobody spoke. The silence had weight. It pressed on me like deep water.

A woman rose from the Solmere bench. Delegate Ondrey—I knew her name; everyone did. Silver-streaked, shrewd, famous for ending a grain war with a signature instead of a siege. She looked at me the way a jeweler looks at a stone she suspects is cut glass.

"Mirabel Blackwood," she said. "Temple scribe of Vharen. Debt-bound. Convicted—in absentia—of unlicensed magic." She let that last word sit in the hall like a dropped knife. "You come to us claiming to carry proof of King Aldric's forgery. Proof that could give nine cities cause for war."

"Not war," I said. My voice came out smaller than I intended. I made myself stand straighter. "Cause for truth. There's a difference."

"There is always a difference," Ondrey said, "until the armies are moving. Then there is only one thing." She descended two steps, her robes whispering. "Show us what you carry."

I lifted the memory-stone from my satchel.

It had changed in the weeks since the head scribe Sarelle pressed it into my dying hands. Then it had been dull, a river pebble with a secret. Now it caught the falling light and held it, turning slow colors behind its surface—the old queen's last hours still burning inside, patient as banked coals. The hall saw it and stirred. They had heard rumors for three days. Rumors are cheap. The stone was not.

"A memory-stone of the royal line," someone breathed from the Corvane bench. "We thought them all destroyed."

"All but this one," said a delegate from Dunmark, an old soldier with half his face gone to scarring. "And we know what happened the last time one was opened. The War of Inheritance burned three cities to their foundations. Half a million dead over a rock full of somebody's dying afternoon." He spat toward the floor, an old habit he didn't bother to disguise. "You bring us a spark, girl. We live in a room full of oil."

"Then hear what's in the spark before you decide to fear it," I said.

Ondrey raised a hand. "There is another matter first." She turned, addressing the whole ring now, and I watched her do something terrible and skillful: she made the entire hall complicit with a single sentence. "Vharen has offered terms. Return the stone to crown custody, surrender the bearer for judgment under Aldric's law, and the free cities keep their trade routes open through the winter. No war. No famine. No half a million dead." She looked back down at me, and there was no cruelty in her face, which somehow made it worse. Only arithmetic. "Some among us find those terms reasonable. You must understand, Miss Blackwood—you are asking nine cities to gamble everything they have built on the word of a convicted charm-seller and a rock full of ghosts."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you will be held until the terms can be negotiated without your cooperation. The stone will be surrendered either way. That much has already been agreed." A murmur ran the tiers—agreement reached before I ever entered the hall, decided while I bathed in gray water upstairs. "I am sorry. Truly. But you were never going to leave this chamber a free woman. The only question left is whether you leave as a prisoner of Aldric or a guest of ours."

The wheel of light was cold on my shoulders. Somewhere above, a gull cried against the dome, absurd and alive.

I thought about running. There was nowhere to run to. I thought about the amnesty I might have bargained for, the quiet life, the ledger of small mercies balanced at last. Ten years I had survived by making myself small, by selling harmless charms and hiding the living fire beneath my ink-work, by refusing a frightened client the healing she begged for because safety mattered more than mercy. And where had it gotten me? Betrayed anyway. Hunted anyway. Standing in a ring of strangers being told that my life was a line item in someone else's peace.

Sarelle had died pressing this stone into my hands. Her blood had been warm on my wrists. *Carry it home,* she'd said, and home had never meant anywhere safe.

"You're right about one thing," I said, and my voice carried now, clear across the hush. "I was never leaving free. So I'll choose what I'm arrested for."

I knelt and set the stone on the pale floor at the center of the light wheel, and I laid both hands upon it, and I did the thing I had hidden for ten years, the thing they had branded me for in absentia, the thing worth killing for.

I opened it.

Magic moved through me like dawn through a window. The stone drank my gift and gave back what it held, and I did not merely witness the old queen's last hours—I became the vessel through which the hall witnessed them. I spoke the memory aloud as Sarelle had taught me it must be spoken, every word paid for out of my own strength, and the dome filled with a voice that was mine and not mine: Queen Isolde Vane, betrayed, poisoned by her own brother's men, naming the forged inheritance even as her sight failed—*the seal is false, the bloodline lives, let no one say Aldric was chosen*—and the hall heard it, all nine cities heard it, merchants and priests and bounty-men, the truth rolling around the ring of benches like a bell that cannot be unrung.

It cost me everything. When the last word left me I was on my hands and knees on cold stone, nose bleeding freely onto the floor, hands shaking so hard I could not have lifted the memory-stone again if my life depended on it. It no longer glowed. It was a river pebble once more, empty, its work done.

Silence. Then the old soldier from Dunmark stood, slowly, like a man rising from a grave he'd been lying in a long time. "I fought at Redford Ford," he said, to no one and everyone. "I buried boys for a lie I didn't know was a lie. I'll be damned before I hand that lie back its crown." He turned to Ondrey. "Vote."

Delegate after delegate rose. Not all—there would be arguments for months, I knew that—but enough. Enough voices saying *witness*, saying *proof*, saying the word that changed everything: *enough.*

Ondrey came down into the light herself. She crouched beside me, studied my bleeding face, and did the first honest thing anyone in power had done for me in ten years.

"You understand you're still a magic-bearer under our own laws," she said quietly. "Unlicensed. Convicted. By rights I should have you held tonight."

"I know."

She sighed, and signaled to her guards—and then, as they approached, she said, "Hold her under *our* jurisdiction. Protective custody. Let Aldric's wolves come ask us for her." To me, almost gently: "You claimed justice outside the law today, Miss Blackwood. The least we can do is make the law catch up."

They helped me to my feet. Through the high windows I could see the harbor, and beyond it the dark road east, along which, sooner than any of us wished, wolf-masks would be coming.

Let them come. I had carried the truth home. Now it would learn to walk on its own.
