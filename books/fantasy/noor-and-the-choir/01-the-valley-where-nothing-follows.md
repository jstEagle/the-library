## 1. The Valley Where Nothing Follows

There are two things you should know about the Valley of Vell before you envy or pity me, and I will tell you both honestly, because honesty is the one commodity in this country that isn't taxed.

The first thing: nothing here is free.

The second thing: nothing here works the way it's supposed to, and everyone has agreed not to notice.

I'll start with the ink, because ink is where my days begin and end. Temple ink comes in three grades — Ledger Black for debts, Scripture Brown for consolations, and a thin watery grey called Pauper's Grey that we scribes are issued for our own correspondence, if we can afford paper to correspond on. We cannot. So Pauper's Grey mostly dries in its pots while we write other people's fortunes in finer colors than we will ever see.

My name is Tamsin Thackeray, and I owe the Temple of the Balanced Scale four thousand, six hundred, and eleven marks. This is not my debt. It belonged to my mother, who left it to me the way other mothers leave lockets or recipes or the shape of their hands. She left no locket. She left no recipe. She left a number, entered into the Great Ledger in her own handwriting, which I know because I have copied that page of the Ledger eleven times in my years at the scriptorium, and each time I have recognized the slant of her letters the way you recognize a voice through a wall.

Four thousand, six hundred, and eleven marks. At my current wage — room, bread, candle-allowance, and one mark per hundred lines copied — I will be free when I am sixty-one. The temple considers this generous. The temple has a pamphlet about it.

* * *

The scriptorium sits above the tithe barn like a thought sitting above an appetite. Forty desks in rows, forty stools, forty little brass lamps, and forty of us bent over vellum from first bell to last, copying out the accounts of a valley that runs, as far as anyone can tell, entirely on accounts.

You might ask — as I once asked, naively, at fifteen, and was made to stand in the cold corridor for the length of two bells — what a temple needs with so many ledgers. The answer is that in Vell, the temple does not merely record debt. Debt is the temple. Every mark owed is a prayer the temple has answered in advance, and every payment is a hymn sung back in coin. The Ledger-Kings, who sit in their counting-house upriver where the air smells of tallow and certainty, call this economy. The priests call it grace. I call it what it is, quietly, in the privacy of my own skull, which is the only property I hold outright: it is a machine for turning longing into arithmetic.

Here is how a morning goes. First bell, we file in. Overseer Pellam reads the day's assignments off a slate. Second bell, we dip. Then there is nothing but the sound of nibs — a sound like insects eating something precious — until noon gruel, then more nibs, until last bell, when we file out and sleep in cells barely wide enough to turn around in, which the temple deducts from us at half a mark a night. Rent for a coffin you haven't earned yet.

I am good at the work. This is the worst thing about me, some days. My hand is steady, my letterforms are praised by Pellam in his flat way — "adequate," he says, which from Pellam is a parade — and I can copy a merchant's ledger of nine hundred entries without a single transposition. Diligence, they call it. I have learned to wear diligence the way a drowning person wears water: completely, and without ever having chosen it.

Because here is what diligence actually is, in me. It is wanting. It is all the wanting I am permitted, folded very small and pressed flat under a ruling line. When I copy the account of the wool-factor Ossen, whose daughter is being schooled in the capital at a cost of thirty marks a term, I am diligent. When I copy the funeral expenses of the cooper's widow — itemized, down to the candles, because in Vell even grief arrives with an invoice — I am diligent. I copy other people's lives so carefully that sometimes I live in them for a line or two, and then the bell rings, and I come back to mine, which fits on a single ledger row: *Thackeray, T. — balance outstanding.*

* * *

Now the second thing you should know, the strange thing, the thing every child in this valley learns before they learn their letters, though no one teaches it and no one explains it.

No magic crosses our border.

I don't mean magic is forbidden. Forbidden things happen anyway; that's most of what makes them interesting. I mean it *cannot*. Somewhere up along the rim of the valley, where the border stones stand in their leaning gray ranks like tired shepherds, there is a line, and across that line the world is full of wonders — so the caravans say, so the few travelers say, so the smuggled storybooks say — and on our side of it, nothing follows.

That's the phrase the old people use. *Nothing follows.* A charm spoken in Vell stays a sentence. A blessing given here is just breath with good manners. A curse is a rude noise. Midwives keep charms in their kits out of habit and reach for them and remember, and put them away, and deliver the baby with hot water and swearing like everywhere else. There are no hedge-witches in Vell because there is nothing for a hedge-witch to be. There are no miracles, no omens, no signs. The stars here are only stars.

And yet — and this is the part nobody discusses at supper, the part that lives in the valley like a stone lives in a shoe — the prayers still get answered. Sometimes. Quietly. In ways that arrive exactly on time and cost exactly what you have.

The temple says this is the working of the Balanced Scale: you pray, you pay, you receive. Cause, consideration, effect. Clean as bookkeeping. And for most of my life I believed it, the way you believe the floor, without thinking about it, right up until the floor moves.

But travelers — real ones, from over the stones — look at our valley strangely. One of them, a spice-merchant with a limp and a laugh like a hinge, told me once, when I was carrying his ledgers to the customs desk, that outside, prayers are wild things. They go up and scatter and maybe land somewhere, maybe not, and the gods who catch them are capricious and magnificent and mostly absent. He said in Vell it was different. He said in Vell the prayers went up and came back down like coins into a slot, and he had never seen a country where heaven kept such punctual books.

He meant it as a compliment. I have thought about it nearly every day since, and I have decided it was a warning.

* * *

Let me give you a scene, since scenes are what I hoard instead of money.

It was the third week of Harvest-Tithe season, and the scriptorium smelled of lamp oil and cold mutton. Outside, the light was doing that thing it does in late autumn, coming in low and gold through the south windows and laying long warm bars across the desks, so that half of us worked in sunlight and half in shadow, and I had drawn the shadow seat, as usual. Fate and Pellam agree on many things.

I was copying the tithe rolls. Page after page of what the valley owed: Ossen the wool-factor, so much; the mill at Colder's Bend, so much; the shrine-tenders of the Little Saints, whose shrines are swept and candle-lit and utterly silent, so much. The numbers marched. Nibs scratched. Somewhere below us, in the tithe barn, sacks were being dragged and counted, and the sound came up through the floorboards like a slow heartbeat under the whole building.

And I was diligent. I want you to understand that. Whatever happened next, I was, at that moment, doing exactly what I was made for.

Because my mind wanders — it must, or I'd have died of this work years ago — and while my hand wrote *Ossen, balance carried forward,* my mind went over the border stones. It always goes the same route. Up the shepherd tracks, past the leaning gray sentinels, over the saddle of the pass where the wind comes through smelling of rain that has touched other countries. And on the far side, in the country I have never seen, my mother is walking. That's where she went, you see. Through the stones, twenty-two years ago, owing four thousand marks and carrying nothing but a bundle and, I have always suspected, a song. They told me she went to escape the debt. Debts don't cross the border either — that's law, that's written, that's the one mercy carved into the whole system: your obligations die at the stones, even if you don't. But she left hers to me anyway, signed and witnessed, which means either she could not carry it and chose to give it, or she would not carry it and chose to leave it, and I have spent twenty-two years unable to decide which of those I could forgive.

So my mind was over the pass, and my mother was walking in a country where magic followed, and my hand was writing *carried forward* —

— and the pen stopped.

Not because I stopped it. Because the ink stopped. The line ran dry mid-word, which happens, except that when I looked down, the word wasn't *forward.* The word on the page, in my own handwriting, in Ledger Black, was *listen.*

I sat very still. Around me, forty nibs went on scratching, and the light lay in its bars, and below us the sacks thumped their slow heart-beat against the barn floor.

I looked at the word. *Listen.* My letters. My slant. Except that I had not written it, and I knew the difference the way you know the difference between your own reflection and someone standing behind you wearing your coat.

The sensible explanation: fatigue. The hand wanders; the scribe doesn't notice; the word goes down unbidden. Scribes' manuals are full of warnings about it. I reached for the sensible explanation the way you reach for a banister in the dark, and found it wasn't there.

Because the ink in my well was full. Full to the brim, blacker than Ledger Black has any right to be, and moving. Not sloshing — moving the way a crowd moves, the way a congregation shifts on wooden benches when the door opens. Turning toward something.

I did the only thing a debtor may safely do with a miracle, which is nothing. I closed the well. I set the pen down parallel to the page, the way Pellam taught us, evidence of order, and I sat with my hands in my lap and my heart going like a bird in a shuttered room, and I waited for the bell.

When it rang, I filed out with the others. I ate my gruel. I was diligent.

But that night, in my cell, in the dark, with the candle deducted and therefore unlit, I lay on my pallet and listened. To the wind on the stones, miles off. To the barn settling. To the silence of the Little Saints' shrines, which is a silence I have heard my whole life and never once questioned — the silence of altars with no one home.

Except that tonight, underneath it, faint as a word drying on a page, I could have sworn — I could almost swear — something was humming.

And the terrible, wonderful, unspeakable truth of it, the thing I pressed my face into the blanket to keep from saying aloud, was this:

In the valley where nothing follows, it sounded like something had finally caught up.

* * *

I did not sleep. I want that recorded somewhere, even if only here, in the one ledger I keep for myself, which is this telling. I lay awake until first bell doing sums, because sums are my rosary, and the sum I kept arriving at was this: whatever spoke through my pen tonight broke a law older than the temple, deeper than the Ledger-Kings' counting-house, older than the border stones themselves. Nothing follows, the valley says. Nothing crosses. That is the foundation the whole country stands on, the reason we are safe, the reason we are poor, the reason my mother's debt outlived her and landed on me like a second skeleton.

If something followed me home tonight, then the floor has moved. And everything built on the floor — the temple, the tithes, the arithmetic of my entire life — is standing on air.

At first bell I rose. I washed my face in water I paid for. I filed into the scriptorium with the others and took my seat in the shadow, and Pellam read the assignments, and I dipped my pen.

The task was a confiscated prayer book. This happens a few times a year: some family caught keeping old devotions, some grandmother's hymnal turned in by a neighbor with a debt to reduce. Confiscated texts are copied into the archive before burning — the temple destroys nothing without recording it first, because in Vell even destruction must be accounted for — and transcription duty falls to whoever's numbers are cleanest.

Mine are cleanest.

So I opened the little book — cheap paper, soft with handling, smelling of smoke and lavender and somebody's kitchen — and I began to copy the first prayer onto archive vellum, in Ledger Black, in my adequate hand.

It was addressed to no saint I knew. No name of the Balanced Scale, none of the Little Saints. Just a single word at the head of the page, worn nearly smooth by thumbs, repeated down the margin in a child's handwriting, over and over, the way children practice the names of things they love.

The word was *Noor.*

And as my pen touched the vellum, the ink in my well rose up to meet it — and the page, before I had written anything at all, was already answering.
