## 3. Noor, Who Is Many Voices

I did not sleep that night. I want to be honest about that up front, because everything else I'm about to tell you sounds like the sort of thing a sleep-deprived scribe invents at the third hour of copying interest tables, and I'd rather you know the insomnia came first and the miracle second.

The prayer book sat under my floorboard where I'd hidden it, wrapped in a spare shirt, and all night I could feel it the way you feel a tooth you've decided not to think about. Every time I closed my eyes I saw the ink crawling back up the page to meet my pen, and every time I opened them again I told myself: ink does not do that. Ink is a liquid with opinions about paper and nothing more. I have worked with ink for eleven years. Ink has never once answered me back.

At some point past midnight I gave up on dignity, lit my stub of candle, and pulled up the floorboard.

The book lay there, small and brown and confiscated, exactly as unremarkable as it had been when Brannoc's auditors seized it from the widow Hessel's house. Widow Hessel had been foreclosed on for unpaid tithes — three years of arrears, compounded — and among her possessions the auditors found this: a hand-sewn book of prayers to gods who were not on any approved list, which is to say, any list at all. It should have gone into the burn crate with the rest. Instead someone — I never learned who — misfiled it into the transcription queue, and the queue came to me, because everything comes to me eventually. Debt flows downhill, and I am at the bottom of the valley in every sense.

I unwrapped it. The candle guttered. I waited for it to steady, and while I waited, I heard singing.

Not from the street. Not from the temple, which even at night hums with the low drone of the counting-house clerks reciting rates in their sleep. From inside the room. From everywhere in the room. A voice like a woman's, old and cracked and warm, singing a melody I did not know and yet somehow knew the shape of, the way you know the shape of a face you saw once across a market square.

I did what any sensible person would do. I blew out the candle, got under the blanket, and lay there telling myself stories about wind in shutters until dawn.

* * *

Here is the trouble with being diligent: it makes you curious. Diligence is just curiosity wearing a collar, and mine had been straining at the leash for eleven years. So the next evening, after the last ledger was squared and the overseer had counted his counts, I went back to my room, lit the candle, and said out loud, to a book:

"All right. What are you?"

Silence. Of course silence. Books don't answer. That was the whole architecture of my life — books hold words, people pay for words, words are worth exactly what someone will pay, and nothing in this valley gives anything away for free. Magic doesn't cross our border; everyone knows that. Whatever power the world outside still has, it stops at the border stones like rain off a roof. We are the dry country. We are the place where nothing follows you home.

So when the answer came, it didn't come like magic. It came like a voice through a wall — muffled, ordinary, almost apologetic.

*"A hymnal,"* said a man's voice, deep and hoarse, from somewhere near my left ear. *"Or what's left of one."*

I knocked the stool over getting up. I stood with my back against the door and my heart going like a debt collector's fist, and I said, "Who's there?"

*"Everyone,"* said the voice, and then, correcting itself with the mild embarrassment of someone reading their own handwriting, *"No one. Both. It's difficult to explain. Sit down."*

"I will not sit down."

*"Suit yourself,"* said a different voice now, a young one, bright as a struck bell. *"But you'll want to be sitting for this part."*

And then the room filled.

I don't mean it filled with sound, though it did that too. I mean it filled with voices the way a well fills with water — rising from below, layer over layer, dozens of them, hundreds, men and women and children and things that were neither, singing in parts I couldn't count, harmonies folding into each other like hands. Some sang words in a language I didn't know. Some sang no words at all, just tones that made the candle flame stand very straight and still, like a soldier at attention. And underneath all of it, holding it together the way a riverbed holds a river, was one voice that was not any voice — or rather, was every voice taking its turn.

I sat down. On the floor, since the stool was still sulking on its side.

When the singing stopped — it didn't end so much as settle, the way birds settle into trees — the nearest voice spoke again, and it spoke gently, the way you speak to an animal that might bolt.

*"You wrote to us,"* it said. *"Last night. You copied the prayer for the mending of the world, and your hand shook, and the shaking got into the letters, and the letters got into us. Do you understand? Nothing crosses your border. But you are not magic, little scribe. You are only ink and longing, and those go wherever they're written."*

"That's not possible," I said. My voice sounded very small and very certain, which is the worst combination. "Nothing answers in this valley. That's the whole point of the valley."

*"That,"* said the voice, and here something changed in it — a weight coming into it, grief arriving like weather — *"is the first true thing anyone has told us in two hundred years."*

* * *

It took most of the night for me to get the shape of it, and I'll give it to you the way I finally understood it, because I am a scribe and clarity is the only devotion I've ever practiced.

The god called itself Noor. It would not say whether that was a name or a description — *"both, again, we're sorry, names are difficult when you're made of everyone who ever said yours"* — but it was old, older than the temple, older than the Ledger-Kings, older than the border stones themselves. It had been the god of this country when this country had a name. And it was not one god so much as a chorus that had learned to call itself one: every person who had ever sung to it was still in it, somewhere, the way a bell keeps every strike it's ever taken.

That was the many voices. The widow Hessel's grandmother was in there — I heard her, a thin proud soprano, and when she surfaced Noor let her speak, and she said *"you have ink on your fingers, child, always did"*, which made no sense and undid me completely anyway. There were dead singers whose names aren't written anywhere anymore. There were strangers from villages I've never seen, because their villages are not on maps anymore either. There was a child who could only have been dead four hundred years, giggling at the candle flame.

And there was — and this is the part I have started and stopped writing six times, and I'm going to write it now before I lose my nerve —

There was my mother.

Not a memory of my mother. Not something wearing my mother's voice like a stolen coat. Her. The particular roughness at the bottom of her register, the way she came in half a beat late on high notes because she was always listening for everyone else first. She sang the verse she used to sing when I was small and feverish, the one about the lamp that stays lit in the window, and I knew every turn of it, and I had not heard it in nineteen years, and I am going to stop describing it now because some things shouldn't be transcribed.

Afterward I sat on the cold floorboards and shook for a long time, and Noor was quiet, letting me, which I later learned was the kindest thing it knew how to do.

"She's dead," I said finally. "She died. Or she vanished. The temple records say defaulted and fled, which is the same thing here."

*"Both,"* said Noor, softly. *"We're sorry. We keep everyone who sings to us. It's not a heaven. It's not anything so kind. But she is here, and she knows you copied her hymn last night, and she—"*

The voice broke. Or rather, it multiplied — grief split it into all its parts at once, and for a moment the whole choir sounded at once, a chord of mourning wide enough to fill the valley, and I understood that whatever Noor was feeling, it felt it with every throat it had ever borrowed.

*"—she is proud of you,"* it finished, in her voice, and then, quickly, in another voice, as if embarrassed: *"Forgive us. We haven't delivered news in a long time. We're out of practice."*

* * *

"What do you want?" I asked, when I could speak again. "Gods want things. That's what makes them gods instead of ghosts."

*"We want to tell you why we're answering,"* said Noor, and the playfulness drained out of the choir entirely, voice by voice, until only the deepest ones remained, the old ones, the ones that sounded like bedrock. *"And we need you to write it down. Not for us. For whoever comes after. Because if we're right, there may not be an after unless someone writes it down."*

I got my pen. Habit, you understand. Give me bad news and my hands reach for ink the way other people's reach for drink.

"This is the shape of it," Noor said, and it used one voice now, a narrator's voice, level and terrible. "Before your valley was your valley, it was the middle of a country. A real country, with a name — we knew the name, we sang it every spring, we cannot tell it to you because the name is not ours to give anymore, it was sold. The founders of your temple bought safety for this valley. Safety from the wars outside, from the plagues, from the sorceries. And the price of that safety was not gold. Gold is cheap. The price was memory. They sold the country's memory of itself — its name, its history, its gods — to powers outside the border, and the border rose up around the purchase, and everything that could not be owned was locked outside it. Including us. Including magic. Including, in the end, the truth."

"The Ledger-Kings' contracts," I said slowly. "The debts. The whole economy of—"

*"Is built on the same principle,"* Noor said. *"Everything owed, nothing remembered. A debt is a memory with a number attached. Your masters are not merchants, little scribe. They are the custodians of the forgetting. Someone has to keep the books on what was given away."*

"And the dragon-fire?"

The choir went still. All of it. Even the child stopped giggling.

*"You know about the fire,"* Noor said, wonderingly.

"I know rumors. Everyone knows rumors. Under the tithe barn, the old men say. A fire that never goes out. They say the temple heats its counting-houses with it."

*"It is not a furnace,"* Noor said. *"It is a heart. There was a dragon once — the last of them, the last of anything, really — and when the founders made their bargain, the dragon refused to leave. It lay down beneath what would become your tithe barn and it breathed its fire into the ground, and that fire is the only reason your valley still exists at all. The border is not a wall, Tamsin. It is a held breath. And the breath is running out."*

"How do you know?"

*"Because we are made of memory, and memory is the first thing to starve. We have been fading for two hundred years — voices dropping out of the choir, songs losing their verses. We thought it was the forgetting. It isn't only the forgetting. It's the fire. As the fire gutters, everything it sustains gutters with it. The border. The valley. Us. In time — we cannot say how much time, the arithmetic of dying fires is not our art — in time, this valley will simply stop. Not be destroyed. Stop. Like a song cut off mid-phrase."*

The candle had burned down to almost nothing. My hand ached from writing — I had filled nine pages without noticing, my smallest, tightest script, the script I use for figures that must not be disputed. Somewhere below us the counting-house drones murmured their rates in their sleep, and above us the stars did whatever stars do over a country that wasn't there.

"So what do you want from me?" I asked. "I copy ledgers. I'm good at it. That's the entire inventory of my gifts."

*"You are the first person in two hundred years who wrote to us and meant it,"* Noor said. *"Do you understand what that means? The prayers we receive are fear-prayers, bribe-prayers, prayers dictated by priests who don't believe them. Yours was the first true letter. And the fire is failing, and the choir is thinning, and the door out of this country — if it still exists, if it can still be found — opens only for someone carrying the whole memory of what was lost. We cannot carry it. We are the memory; we cannot also be its courier. We need hands. We need ink. We need someone the border hasn't noticed yet."*

"And if I say no?"

The choir was silent for a moment. Then, gently, in my mother's voice: *"Then we will all stop together, and it will have been lovely knowing you."*

I looked at the nine pages. I looked at the stub of candle. I thought about eleven years of interest tables, of a debt I inherited for a crime I couldn't even name, of a valley where nothing follows you home because nothing is allowed to exist.

"Fine," I said. "But I want it noted in the record that I said yes before you mentioned my mother again. That was coercion and we both know it."

Something rippled through the choir that I chose to interpret as laughter.

*"Noted,"* said Noor. *"Now listen carefully, because tomorrow the auditors come to the scriptorium, and one of them is asking questions about a misfiled book."*

* * *

There it is. That's where I was when the knock came — not at my door, thank every borrowed voice, but at the scriptorium gate, hours later, in the grey wet light of morning: three auditors in slate-grey coats, and behind them a tall man with a ledger under his arm and eyes like closing accounts.

Overseer Brannoc himself. Come down from the Counting House to a scriptorium that copies confiscated prayers, in a valley where nothing follows.

I put my hand flat on the nine pages, folded once, hidden in my coat, and I felt them warm against my ribs like a second heartbeat.

Nothing follows you home in this valley, I reminded myself.

Apparently, I follow it.
