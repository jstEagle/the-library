## 4. The Ledger-Kings Take Notice

I have been called many things in my thirty years with the Ledger: instrument, hound, abacus with a badge. My favorite insult came from a debtor's wife in Millrow who spat at my boots and said I was "a man who counts other people's grief." She was not wrong. I simply pointed out that her husband had signed for the grief himself, in triplicate, and that the interest schedule was on page four.

My name is Overseer Brannoc. I keep the arithmetic of the valley honest, or at least consistent, which is what passes for honest in a place where nothing follows.

That last part matters, so let me say it plainly for anyone reading this report who was not raised inside our borders: no magic crosses into this valley. Whatever runs wild beyond the border stones — the singing hills, the talking rivers, whatever it is the old stories get misty about — stops dead at the boundary like a courier refused entry. The founders built it that way. Paid for it somehow; the founding contracts are sealed and I do not ask about seals. Inside the valley, everything is done by hand, by ink, by debt. Which means everything can be counted. Which means everything can be owed.

A country where magic cannot follow is a country where nothing happens unless someone has agreed to pay for it. I find this comforting. Most people find it suffocating. The difference between us is that they signed nothing and I signed everything.

* * *

The trouble began, as trouble usually does in my line of work, with an anomaly in the receipts.

Every temple in the valley files a weekly tithe reconciliation. Candle-wax sold against candle-wax stocked. Prayer-parchment issued to petitioners against prayers recorded as received. It is dull work and I am very good at it, which is the same thing as saying I am trusted.

On the fourth day of Harvestmonth, the Scriptorium of Saint Vellum-and-Chain — the monks were never good at names — filed a discrepancy so small that any ordinary auditor would have waved it through. Three prayers recorded as answered. Not performed, mind you. Performed prayers are routine; you pay your fee, the monk mumbles over you, the prayer is logged as delivered whether anything happens or not, because nothing ever happens. Delivered is a billing category, not a result.

These three were marked *answered*. With results noted. A fever broken overnight. A well that cleared. A lame mule walking sound by morning.

Three answered prayers, entered without corresponding payment.

I sat with the ledger open in front of me for a long time before I touched it. In the valley where nothing follows, an answered prayer is not a miracle. It is an error. And errors, in my experience, are never innocent. Somewhere there is always a hand — a sloppy monk, a bribed clerk, a petitioner clever enough to forge a blessing rather than pay for one. Forgery of sacrament carries a surcharge of two hundred percent plus forfeiture of the petitioner's remaining years of expected labor. I know the schedule by heart. I helped write the current revision.

But three in one week, from one scriptorium, all genuine-looking, all unpaid?

I pulled the prior twelve months of the same ledger. Nothing. I pulled the neighboring parishes. Nothing. Then I did something I almost never do, because it wastes hours and smells of superstition: I walked down to the scriptorium myself and asked to see the original entries.

* * *

The scribe who had written them was a young thing named Thackeray. Debt-inherited, third generation, the kind of indenture we used to call "legacy accounts" until someone in the home office decided legacy sounded too warm and renamed them "perpetual." Tamsin Thackeray copied ledgers fourteen hours a day and would die owing more than their parents had borrowed, if the actuarial tables held, which they always do.

I watched them work for an hour without announcing myself. That is a technique. People write differently when they think no one is counting.

Thackeray copied cleanly. Too cleanly. There is a particular quality to the handwriting of the hopeless — a small surrender in every letter, a willingness to let the descenders trail off because what does it matter, the debt will outlive the hand. Thackeray's letters stood up straight. Every stroke accounted for. It was the penmanship of someone still planning to be somewhere else someday, and I remember thinking, with the mild irritation one feels toward a ledger that won't balance: *this one is going to be a problem.*

Then I examined the three prayer entries.

They were written in Thackeray's hand. Same slant, same pressure, same ink batch — I checked the ink against the scriptorium's issue log to the dram. And yet. There was something in the letters themselves, some faint wrongness I could not then name and cannot fully name now, except to say that the words seemed to sit slightly deeper in the parchment than ink has any right to sit. As if the writing had been *answered* rather than merely written. As if someone on the other side of the page had pressed back.

I held the leaf up to the light. On the reverse, faint as breath on glass, there were marks that matched no known script in the valley's registry of licensed scripts. We license scripts, by the way. All forty-one of them. Unlicensed writing is contraband.

I did not feel fear. Let the record show that. What I felt was the specific displeasure of a man whose columns refuse to sum. Three unpaid answers, an unlicensed script ghosting through the paper, and a scribe with suspiciously upright handwriting. The numbers were telling me a story, and the story was impossible, and I have learned that when the numbers tell you an impossible story, you do not disbelieve the numbers. You go looking for the term you've left out.

* * *

Back at the audit-house I opened the restricted correspondence and wrote to the Ledger-Kings themselves. Not the regional factors — the Kings. Nine men and one woman who own, between them, every debt in the valley, which is to say every future the valley will ever live. They communicate by ciphered letter and consider even that a concession.

I reported the anomaly in the proper form: nature of irregularity, estimated fiscal exposure, recommended response. Estimated exposure is the language they hear best. An answered prayer, unlogged and unpaid, is not a wonder to the Ledger. It is leakage. If the valley learns that prayers can be *answered* — truly answered, free of charge — then within a season the tithe-houses empty, the debt-economy stalls, and the entire architecture of ownership we have spent three hundred years building comes down like wet paper. Hope, unpriced, is the single most dangerous commodity in existence. Everything else we can tax.

Their reply came back in four days, fast for them, which told me how seriously they took it. The substance was one line:

**"Answers require invoices. Find the issuer."**

Beneath it, the authorization seal: full auditor dispatch, discretionary force, expenses unreceipted. When the Ledger-Kings stop requiring receipts, you understand the size of what frightens them.

* * *

I dispatched six auditors. I chose them myself, from the roster, avoiding the ones who pray privately — yes, we track that; hypocrisy is a manageable risk but devotion is not — and avoiding the ones who enjoy force too much, because force enjoyed is force escalated, and escalation generates paperwork. I wanted auditors who were like me: instruments. Men and women who could stand in a room with a weeping family foreclosed down to their bedsheets and record the inventory without their hands shaking.

Auditor Vess. Auditor Kellam. The twins Ostra and Osten, who interview witnesses in tandem and cross-check each other's notes like living ledgers. Auditor Maren, who once talked a mob off the tithe-barn steps using nothing but a repayment schedule read aloud in a calm voice. And Auditor Pell, junior, sharp, ambitious, the kind I keep close because ambition is predictable.

Their instructions were precise. Locate the source of the unanswered-for answers. Identify all persons exposed to it. Do not harm the source — the Kings want it *invoiced*, not destroyed; you cannot bill a corpse, though heaven knows we've tried. Document everything. And above all: no sermons, no speculation, no talk of gods.

That last instruction I wrote myself, and I wrote it twice, underlined.

Because here is the thing I did not put in the report, the thing I have not put in any report, the thing I am setting down now only because a man who keeps everyone else's accounts ought to keep his own somewhere, even if only in his head, even if only once:

When I held that prayer-leaf up to the light and saw the unlicensed script pressing through from the other side, I did not think of fraud. I thought of my mother.

* * *

She sang. That was her crime, if crime it was — she sang hymns to gods with no registered names, in a kitchen that smelled of barley and lamp-oil, quietly, after the audit-house closed, as if the songs were laundry to be folded away before morning. I was six. I remember standing in the doorway watching her mouth move around words I wasn't permitted to learn, and I remember thinking her slightly mad, and I remember — this is the part I keep in the locked drawer — I remember that the candle flames leaned toward her when she sang. All of them, together, like a congregation turning to face the reader.

No magic follows, in this valley. Everyone knows it. The founders paid for that.

So the flames must have been leaning toward something else. Or the rule must have a seam in it. Or my memory is a forgery, which would be fitting, since forgery is the family trade now, in a sense. I count things that aren't true and make them true by recording them.

I was eight when the auditors came for her. Different auditors, older uniforms, but the same seals. They took the hymnal and they took her account books and they levied a fine she could not pay, and my father assumed the fine, and the fine compounded, and the compounding became my inheritance, and that is how I came to spend my life on the collecting side of the desk. People assume enforcers are made of conviction. Most of us are made of arithmetic that started as somebody else's grief and got handed down.

I have not thought about the leaning candles in twenty-five years. I thought about them for exactly the length of time it took to seal my report to the Kings, and then I put the drawer back, and locked it, and went to dinner, and ate everything on the plate, because appetite is also a discipline.

* * *

The auditors moved out on the fifth day of Harvestmonth, in pairs, plain-clothed, carrying portable scales and wax-sealed warrant blanks. Their first circuit covered the scriptorium's supply chain: the ink-monger, the parchment factor, the candle-renderer, the well-diggers who'd reported the cleared water. Standard procedure — trace the materials, trace the money, and somewhere along the chain the anomaly will reveal itself as an ordinary crime, because it always does.

Except.

Except that on the second evening, Auditor Pell sent back a note that I have read perhaps forty times since, and which I reproduce here from memory because the original went into the fire, officially as a security measure and unofficially because I could not decide which drawer to lock it in:

*"Overseer — interviewed the mule's owner re: item 3 (lame beast restored). Subject confirms healing, confirms no payment, confirms no knowledge of mechanism. Subject then asked me, unprompted, whether I had ever lost anything, and said — quote — 'ask nicely, sometimes they're listening again.' I reminded subject that solicitation of unlicensed intercession is a Class II offense. Subject smiled. Note: I experienced an involuntary recollection of a lullaby. Unable to identify its origin. Recommend continued observation. Also recommend, though I know better than to recommend it: someone should check whether the rule about nothing following still holds everywhere it's supposed to."*

Listening again. *Again.* As though the listening were an old habit resumed, not a new crime begun.

I sat in my office with Pell's note and the week's tallies and the portrait of order I have spent my life maintaining, and I did the sums, because sums are what I have instead of prayers. Three answered prayers, exposure rising. Six auditors deployed, cost recoverable. One mother, levied, gone. One son, employed by her leviers, promoted twice.

The columns balanced. They always balance. That is the trap of arithmetic — it balances whether or not the world underneath it deserves to.

I authorized a second wave. Twelve more auditors, widening the circuit, parish by parish, door by door. Find the issuer. Find the singers, if there are singers. Find whoever is giving away what we sell.

And in the warrant margins, in my own hand, small enough that only a fellow counter would notice, I added an instruction I have never written on a warrant before and do not intend to explain to anyone:

*"If any auditor experiences memories inconsistent with their personal records, report them to me directly. Not to the Kings. To me."*

Justice, in this valley, is arithmetic. I have believed that since I was old enough to hold a stylus. But an equation has terms, and I am beginning to suspect that somewhere in the founding of this country, someone left one out — a term so large that everything we have counted since is wrong by exactly that much.

The auditors ride at dawn. Somewhere in this valley where nothing follows, something is following anyway, and it is answering people who cannot pay, and the Kings want its name on an invoice.

I want it too. Though if I am honest — and a man who keeps accounts should permit himself honesty in the margins, nowhere else — I find I want it for a different reason than they do.

I want to ask it about the leaning candles.

I want to ask it what my mother was paying, all those years, and to whom, and why the fine they charged her never once appeared in any ledger I have ever been able to open.

Find the issuer, the Kings said.

I intend to. God help the finding — whichever god turns out to still be taking questions.
