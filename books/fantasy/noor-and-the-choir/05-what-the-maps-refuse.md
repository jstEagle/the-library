## 5. What the Maps Refuse

The Cartography Hall smelled of vinegar and old men, which is to say it smelled of preservation — of things kept past their usefulness because no one had the heart or the authority to throw them out. I had copied ledgers in the temple scriptorium for six years, and I knew every smell of institutional neglect the way a fisherman knows weather. Vinegar meant copperplate etchings being cleaned. Old men meant the Guild of Surveyors still employed three of them, and all three were asleep by the second bell after noon.

I want to be clear about something before I go further: I am not, by nature, a thief. I once returned an overpayment of two coppers to the tithe office, and the clerk looked at me as though I'd handed him a live snake. My mother used to say I had a scrupulous streak wide enough to drive a cart through. But my mother also sang hymns that got her erased from the rolls, so perhaps her judgment on matters of law was not the most reliable compass.

Still. A thief would have felt less guilty about what I did that afternoon.

It began with Noor, of course. Everything lately began with Noor. The choir had gone quiet for two days — not silent, exactly, but *distant*, like voices heard through a wall — and when they came back, they came back with a single instruction, repeated in a dozen borrowed mouths:

*Find the edges. Find where you end.*

"Where *I* end?" I'd asked aloud, in my own room, at midnight, feeling like a madwoman talking to her ceiling. "I end at my skin, thank you very much."

But the choir didn't mean me. They meant the valley. And I understood, eventually, why a god who lived on memory would ask such a question. If someone had tried to erase your country — if every chart refused its name — then the first thing you'd want to know was whether the erasure had worked. Whether there were edges left to find.

So I went to the Cartography Hall, wearing my scriptorium sash, which opened more doors in this valley than any key. Scribes are invisible. That's the whole point of us. We write down what other people do, and in doing so we become furniture — necessary, ignored, and assumed to be honest, because who would bother lying with ink when you could lie with money?

The plates were kept in the long gallery behind the reading room: copper etchings of every survey made in the last hundred years, each one stamped with the Ledger-Kings' seal, each one showing the valley and its tributary villages rendered in loving detail — roads, mills, tithe barns, the temple itself drawn with a cartographer's flattery, all spires and symmetry.

And around the valley? Nothing.

That's what I'd always assumed. The maps showed the valley sitting in a blank margin, surrounded by white space, and I had grown up believing that white space the way you believe the sky — as simply how the world was. Beyond the border stones there was nothing worth drawing. Other countries existed, of course; traders spoke of them, and the caravans came and went through the pass twice a year. But the maps never drew them. The maps showed our valley like an island floating in nothing, and I had never once thought to find that strange.

I thought about it now.

I pulled the oldest plate in the gallery — dated a hundred and twelve years back, before even Overseer Brannoc's grandfather had learned to count — and held it under the gallery lamp. The valley was there. The river, the pass, the villages, all of it. And around it, instead of white space, instead of nothing—

There was writing.

Faint, because the plate had been re-etched at some point, the old lines burnished down and new ones cut over them. But under the lamp, tilted just right, the ghost of the older engraving rose up like a bruise. Mountains. Rivers. Towns with names I half-knew from hymns — *Alderfall*, *the Grey Steps*, *Merrow-on-the-Wind*. A whole country, drawn in confident strokes by someone who had stood on those hills and measured them.

And across the middle of it, in letters that had been deliberately struck through but not fully effaced, a single line of official text:

**THE SOVEREIGN TERRITORY OF NOOR'S REACH — STATUS: DISSOLVED BY CONTRACT. NO LONGER EXISTS.**

I set the plate down very carefully, the way you set down something that might be dreaming.

"No longer exists," I said to the empty gallery. "I'm standing in it."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, one of the old men snored.

I pulled another plate. Then another. Then six more, laying them across the reading table in chronological order, and I watched a country die in copper. The earliest surveys named everything — every hill had a title, every stream a history, the border stones marked not as the edge of the world but as the edge of a province, with friendly neighbors beyond. Then, in a survey dated ninety years back, the names beyond the stones vanished. Not faded. *Vanished*, replaced mid-decade with clean white space, as though a hand had passed over the plate and unmade them. The next survey showed the same, but now the white space pressed closer, eating into the margins. By the most recent plate, the valley floated alone in nothing, and the legend read:

**THACKERAY VALLEY AND DEPENDENCIES. FORMERLY PART OF: [RECORD WITHHELD].**

[Record withheld]. Four little words. Somebody had been paid to put those four words there, and somebody else had been paid to check their work, and I had spent my whole life inside the withheld record without ever once asking what had been withheld.

Here is the vertigo, since you asked. It isn't falling. Falling has a direction. This was worse — it was discovering that the floor you've stood on all your life is a painting of a floor, hung over an actual floor, several stories down, and everyone agreed to look at the painting and call it ground. Every map I'd ever trusted. Every lesson in temple school about the valley's place in the world. The traders' talk of "other countries" that somehow never appeared on any chart. All of it a coordinated act of looking away, maintained for longer than anyone alive had been paying attention.

My home wasn't remote. My home was *redacted*.

And underneath the vertigo, cold and patient as water finding a crack, another thought: if the country could be unmade on paper — if paper could do that — then paper could undo it. Paper had done it. Which meant the prayer book that answered me, the god assembled out of remembered voices, the hymns my mother bled for — none of it was madness. It was *bookkeeping*. The gods hadn't died. They'd been written off.

I laughed. I couldn't help it. It came out of me in the empty gallery, high and unhinged, and I clapped both hands over my mouth because the sound echoed off the copper plates like something escaping.

Because here was the thing I couldn't stop seeing, now that I'd seen it: the ledger logic was everywhere. My debt was a record. My mother's erasure was a record. The country's dissolution was a contract. The Ledger-Kings didn't rule with soldiers; they ruled with the agreement that whatever was written down was true, and whatever was unwritten had never happened. They had built a prison out of consensus and called it arithmetic.

Well. Two could keep books.

I took the oldest plate. I know — theft, sacrilege, all of it. But I told myself, in the tone of a woman negotiating with her own conscience, that a record withheld from the people it describes belongs to the people, and I was merely repossessing it. My mother would have called that sophistry. My mother also once stole a whole ham from the tithe barn on festival day, so her opinion on the matter carried limited weight.

Wrapping the plate in my sash, I slipped out through the reading room, past the snoring surveyors, into the blue hour before evening bell. And that was when the choir found me again — close now, urgent, crowding in against my ears like family at a sickbed:

*Tamsin. Hurry. You're seen.*

I turned. At the far end of the colonnade, lantern light bobbed — two figures, gray-cloaked, moving with the unhurried purpose of men paid by the hour. Auditors. Brannoc's hounds. One of them raised a hand, and light flashed off a seal-ring, and he shouted something down the colonnade that I didn't wait to hear.

I ran.

I ran through the temple courts with a stolen country under my arm, past the tithe barn where the auditors' boots rang on stone behind me, past the well, past the bell-tower, cutting through the drying-yard where sheets snapped in the wind like startled birds. The choir sang in my skull the whole way — not hymns now, but directions, sharp and specific, in the voice of a woman I didn't know: *Left. Through the gate. Down the stair. Don't look back, girl, you run like your mother ran.*

"You knew my mother?" I gasped, taking the stair three at a time.

*Everyone knew your mother,* said the choir, and there was grief in it, layered like sediment. *Now run.*

I came out through the wash-gate into the tanner's lane, and the lane was empty, and the shouting behind me faded into the ordinary evening noise of the valley — carts, dogs, someone beating a rug. I leaned against a wall with the plate hugged to my chest, breathing like a bellows, and looked up at the sky going dark over the border stones.

Somewhere out there, past those stones, was a door. A real one, in a real border, of a country that officially did not exist. And somewhere beneath the tithe barn — though I didn't know it yet — something old was sleeping, keeping a fire lit on almost nothing, waiting for someone to remember its name.

The choir murmured, softer now, almost tender:

*You have the first piece. Bring it home.*

"Home," I said bitterly, pushing off the wall. "Which doesn't exist."

*Not yet,* said Noor, in my mother's voice, and I walked home through the dark carrying the proof otherwise.
