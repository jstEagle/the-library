## 6. Debt of the Mother

There is a particular cruelty in inherited debt that the temple never bothers to disguise: they make you copy out your own ledger.

I learned this in my third year at the scriptorium, when Overseer Pellam — a man whose face suggested he had been assembled from leftover accounting errors — set before me a folio and said, "Transcribe this one carefully, Thackeray. It concerns you."

It was my mother's account. Every line of it. The principal: forty marks owed to the Temple of Ordered Accounts for services rendered, which turned out to mean *for existing within our jurisdiction without sufficient collateral*. The interest, compounded quarterly with a devotion I could only admire from a distance. The annotations in Brannoc's neat, merciless hand — though he was not yet an Overseer then, just another auditor sharpening his arithmetic on people like us. And at the bottom, in the space where a settlement should have been recorded, a single word:

*Absconded.*

Not *deceased*. Not *discharged*. Absconded. As if she had slipped out of the ledger like a coin through a torn pocket, and the tear was my inheritance.

So yes. I copied my own ruin four hundred times over the years, in triplicate, with margins ruled true. If you want to know why my handwriting is so beautiful, that's why. Grief makes some people weep. It made me precise.

* * *

The night after Noor first spoke to me — after the prayer book answered back and I lay awake listening to my own ink dry like it might start whispering too — I did something I had never done in nine years of diligent servitude.

I went looking for her file.

The archive beneath the scriptorium is organized with the loving attention of a place that believes paper is holier than flesh. Debts are filed by household, households by district, districts by tithe-year, and somewhere in that great patient machinery of record-keeping there is a drawer with my family name on it, and inside that drawer, everything the Ledger-Kings ever knew about Merrin Thackeray.

I brought a lamp and a stolen key — borrowed, I told myself, since I intended to return it, which is the kind of lie scribes tell themselves professionally — and I knelt on the cold flagstones and pulled the folder into my lap like a child pulling a wounded bird close.

Her debt was not what I'd been told.

That was the first thing. The number in my working ledger — the one I served against, the one that grew like mold no matter how many pages I copied — bore only a passing resemblance to the original sum. Someone had been adjusting it. Adding fees. A "border-proximity surcharge" I had never heard of, applied retroactively across eleven years. An "unverified memory assessment," which sounded less like accounting and more like a threat wearing a clerk's collar.

And then there were the attachments.

Confiscated effects, itemized. One shawl, grey wool. One copper kettle. One hymnal — *illicit text, burned*. And beneath that, in a different hand, older, almost furtive:

*One (1) song, unpriced. Subject observed singing at border stones, hour of dusk. Song identified as pre-Unification liturgical fragment. Value: incalculable. Disposition: subject warned; song unrecoverable.*

Unrecoverable. Except it wasn't, was it? Because I had heard it. All my life, in fragments, hummed under breath while kneading bread or mending hems — a tune I thought she'd invented, the way mothers invent things, small private music made for one child. She hadn't invented it. She'd smuggled it. Out of a country that had sold its gods, she had carried a piece of one in her mouth like a jewel held between her teeth, and she sang it to me at bedtime, and I fell asleep to it, and I never once asked where it came from.

I sat in that archive with her file open on my knees and felt something I didn't have a name for yet. It started as anger — clean, familiar anger, the kind I kept like a banked coal under the floorboards of myself — but it curdled as I read, page after page, because the anger needed a target and the targets kept multiplying. The temple that fined her for singing. The auditors who followed her to the border stones to *observe*, taking notes while she sang. The country itself, which had made a law that a song could be a crime, and then made a second law that the crime could be inherited.

And underneath all of it, quieter and worse: anger at her. For leaving me the bill instead of the explanation.

* * *

You should understand what I remember of that night. Memory in our valley is a strange currency — everyone has it, nobody trusts it, and the exchange rate changes depending on who's asking.

I was eleven. There had been warnings before — notices nailed to our door, polite men with polite voices explaining that Mother's *behavior* constituted an ongoing liability. She would nod and sign and smile the way you smile at weather. But that last evening she was different. She braided my hair, which she hadn't done in years, her fingers moving in the rhythm of that forbidden tune, humming it low like a secret told to my scalp.

"Where are you going?" I asked, because she had put on her good boots, and good boots meant leaving.

"Out past the stones," she said. "Just to look."

"You can't. They'll fine you."

"They've already fined me everything I have." She tied off the braid and kissed the top of my head, and her lips were cold, which I noticed and did not understand. "Tamsin. Listen to me. If anyone ever asks you what I owed —"

"Merrin Thackeray owes nothing," said a voice from the doorway, and there stood Auditor Brannoc, young then, his coat buttoned to the throat, two other auditors behind him holding lanterns that threw our kitchen into long theatrical shadows. He was not cruel. That's what I want to say about him, even now, even after everything. He was *precise*, which in the valley passes for mercy. "Merrin Thackeray owes forty marks, plus assessed interest, plus penalties accrued. Which is to say she owes everything, forever, unless she settles."

"And how do I settle?" my mother asked pleasantly. "You don't take labor. You don't take goods. You won't take the song — you tried, and it wouldn't come out of me, did it? You put your little instruments on my throat and the song went somewhere your instruments couldn't follow." She laughed, and it was not a nice laugh, and I loved her so much in that moment that it frightened me. "What's left, auditor?"

Brannoc looked at her for a long moment. Then at me. Something crossed his face — I have spent years trying to name it and I still can't, except that years later, when I saw him again across a seizure-yard, I recognized it instantly: the look of a man doing arithmetic and getting an answer he doesn't like.

"Go back inside, boy," he said to me. "This is adult business."

I went inside. I sat on my bed with my hands over my ears, which of course did nothing, because the business was conducted in the yard, in voices, and then in footsteps, and then in silence.

She walked to the border stones that night. Witnesses saw her — the valley being what it is, witnesses see everything and report selectively. She stood at the stones at dusk and she sang, loud, the whole hymn, every verse, the one I now know is called *Noor's Name in Seven Lines*, and the auditors wrote it down in their instruments and got nothing, because the song wouldn't be written, and then she stepped past the stones.

And the valley did what the valley does.

Nothing followed her. That's the property of this place — nothing crosses. No magic, no god, no blessing, no curse. I used to think it meant nothing of hers could leave either. But she left. People leave all the time; the border repels magic, not grief. What the records say — what Brannoc's own attachment says, in that furtive older hand — is that when she stepped past the stones, the song stopped mid-verse.

Mid-verse. Like a door closing on a note.

They found her boots on the near side, placed neatly together, facing outward. No body. No trace. The auditors searched for a week and then, with the efficiency of men whose time is billed hourly, they closed the file, transferred the debt to next of kin, and let the valley forget her at its usual speed.

But here is the thing I understood only now, kneeling in the archive with a dead woman's paperwork in my lap: the song didn't stop. It stopped *here*. On this side of the stones. Because nothing follows — and that means nothing leaves either. Whatever she carried past that border stayed behind, the way heat stays in a wall after the fire's gone.

It stayed in me. I am the wall. I have been humming my mother's debt since I was eleven years old and calling it a lullaby.

* * *

I climbed out of the archive at the hour the temple calls Matins and the roosters call a lie, and I stood in the scriptorium among the sleeping desks, and I did not go to my cot.

Instead I took out the confiscated prayer book — the one that had answered me, the one I should have burned or reported or at the very least stopped keeping under my shirt like a second heartbeat — and I laid it open on my desk, and I said, in a whisper pitched for no one and therefore everyone:

"She didn't abandon the debt. She refused to pay in the only currency you accept."

The pages did their trick again. Ink moved. Words surfaced in the margin like fish rising, in a hand that was not mine and not any single hand at all — letters assembling themselves out of a hundred different scripts, a choir deciding on a melody:

*We remember her.*

"You don't," I said, and my voice cracked on it, which embarrassed me even with no audience but a book. "Nobody does. That's the whole architecture of this place. You sell the remembering and buy the forgetting, and the difference is called safety, and people like my mother get foreclosed on for humming."

*We remember her,* the book wrote again, patiently, the way you repeat yourself to someone shouting. *She sang the fourth line wrong. She always sang the fourth line wrong. She added a note that isn't in the hymn. We loved her version better.*

I put my hand flat on the page. The vellum was warm. Books aren't supposed to be warm. Nothing in this valley is supposed to be anything at all.

"That note," I said slowly. "The one she added. Teach it to me."

And the ink gathered, and the choir leaned close — I could feel them leaning, dozens of borrowed voices, dead singers and strangers and once, horribly and tenderly, my own mother's timbre folded into the chorus like a thread of gold in grey wool — and they taught me. Four notes. A rise where the old hymn falls, a hold where it hurries. Her note. My inheritance, the real one, the one no auditor could assess because it had never been entered in any ledger, because it lived in the one place the Ledger-Kings' instruments couldn't reach:

A voice. A memory. A thing given freely, which is the one commodity this entire economy runs on refusing.

I sang it once, alone, at midnight, in a room full of ledgers. It was not beautiful. I am a scribe, not a singer; my voice has the same relationship to music that Brannoc's has to mercy. But the lamps flickered anyway. The candles bent toward me like listeners. And somewhere far below the tithe barn — I know this now, though I didn't know it then — something vast and starving shifted in its sleep, the way a fire shifts when a door opens somewhere in the house.

I closed the prayer book and held it against my chest and made a decision, there in the dark, with the taste of my mother's note still in my mouth.

For nine years I had paid my debt the way the temple wanted: quietly, precisely, in triplicate. I had believed diligence was a virtue and patience was a plan. But my mother hadn't vanished. She had *withdrawn* — taken herself out of an account that was robbing her, gone looking for whatever lies past the edge of a country that isn't on any map. And she'd left me the one asset they couldn't seize, hidden in the worst possible vault: my own throat.

Anger is a fine fuel but a poor compass. What I felt walking back to my cot that morning wasn't anger anymore. It was colder and steadier. Resolve, maybe. Or arithmetic of my own: they had taken her boots, her kettle, her shawl, her name from every record but one, and charged me interest on the loss. Fine. Then I would settle accounts the way she had — not by paying, but by leaving. And I would not go empty-handed. I would carry out what she'd tried to carry: the whole song, the whole memory, every line of it, even the ones the founders sold.

Even the ones that cost the singer.

I slept two hours and dreamed of border stones, and woke to bells, and went to my desk, and began copying ledgers with beautiful, obedient, treasonous precision — because a scribe who is watched must look exactly like a scribe who has decided nothing.

At noon, Overseer Pellam stopped at my desk, consulted a slip of paper, and frowned.

"Thackeray," he said. "Someone has requested your file. Full history. Priority seal."

"Whose request?" I asked, keeping my pen moving.

He checked the seal and his frown deepened into something almost like sympathy, which from Pellam was indistinguishable from alarm.

"Overseer Brannoc's," he said. "He's coming to the scriptorium himself. Tomorrow."
