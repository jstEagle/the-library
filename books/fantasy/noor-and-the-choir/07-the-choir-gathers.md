## 7. The Choir Gathers

* * *

**The Baker**

My name is Odell Fennick, and I have baked bread in Lower Marrow for thirty-one years, which is to say I have been awake at three in the morning for thirty-one years, and if you think that builds character you have never been awake at three in the morning.

I know every sound this valley makes before dawn. The millstone's groan. The cart-axles on the frost. Old Petcher coughing two doors down like the world owes him an apology. So when a voice started singing in my proving room — a voice that was not mine, not Petcher's, not any voice belonging to any living person I could name — I did what any sensible man does.

I checked my flour for rot.

Rot doesn't sing. But neither does anything else, in this valley. That's the whole point of us. We're the place where nothing follows, where magic stops at the border stones like a dog tied up outside a shop. My grandmother used to say it plain: *Nothing answers here, Odell. That's what we paid for.* She never said what we'd paid with, and when I asked she'd smack me with a dish towel, so eventually I stopped asking, which is how most of us learn everything important about this valley.

The voice came back the next night. And the next. It wasn't loud. That's the thing people get wrong when they tell this story later — they make it thunderous, apocalyptic, choirs descending from clouds. It was quiet. A woman's voice, mostly, singing something half-familiar, the way a tune is familiar when you've heard it once as a child and your mother turned off the lamp mid-verse.

*Light the low place. Light the low place. Even embers answer naming.*

I don't know those words. I want that understood. I never learned them, never wrote them down, and yet by the fourth night I was kneading along to them, my hands keeping time, dough rising faster than it had any right to, coming out of the oven with a crust so fine that Widow Carrow crossed the square specifically to ask what I'd done differently.

"Nothing," I said. "Everything's exactly the same."

Which was true, except for the singing, and the rising, and the fact that on the fifth night I answered back.

I said, out loud, alone in my proving room at three in the morning, covered in flour like a man who'd wrestled a ghost and lost: "Who are you?"

And the voice — there were more of them now, I swear it, four or five, layered like voices through a wall — said:

*We are what's left of the light. And you are listening. Oh, you are listening.*

I sat down on my flour sacks and cried like a child, and I couldn't tell you why, except that nobody had spoken to me in that room in thirty-one years who hadn't wanted bread.

**The Jailer**

Sefa Vane keeps the cells under the Assize Hall, and before you decide what kind of woman that makes her, consider that somebody has to hold the keys, and it might as well be someone who hates the job.

We keep debtors, mostly. That's the valley's whole criminal class — people who fell behind. The Ledger-Kings call it containment pending reconciliation, which is a fancy way of saying we lock up the poor until their families can buy them out, and if nobody buys them out, we lock them up longer, and if it goes on long enough we ship them to the tithe barns for labor assessment, and nobody comes back from labor assessment looking like they've been assessed. They come back looking like they've been *harvested*.

So yes. I hold keys to a room full of people whose only crime was arithmetic going against them. I feed them. I don't beat them. I look away when I can and I don't sleep well when I can't. If that makes me a monster, it makes me a very tired one.

The singing started in cell nine.

Cell nine holds Tammas Reeve, a cooper who owed eleven crowns and some odd pence, and Tammas is not a man given to song. Tammas is a man given to sulking, which I respect. So when I came down the stairs at shift change and heard him humming — no, more than humming, actually *singing*, low and steady, words I didn't know in a rhythm like breathing —

I stood on the stairs and listened, because it was beautiful, and because I am ashamed of how long it's been since I heard anything beautiful in that building.

Then cell five joined in. Then cell two. Different voices, same song, like they'd all learned it overnight from the same invisible teacher. The debtors were sitting up on their pallets, eyes open, faces lit by nothing — the lamps weren't lit, I checked, I'm a jailer, checking is my whole personality — and they were singing toward the ceiling, toward the stone, toward whatever was above the stone.

*Even in the low place, even in the locked place, the name finds the mouth that will carry it.*

I should have reported it. Reporting is also my whole personality. Anomalies go to the Overseer; anomalies get audited; audited men disappear into assessment. I knew all of that standing on those stairs, holding my ring of keys like a rosary.

I went back up. I wrote in the shift log: *Night uneventful.*

First lie I ever put in that book. It felt like setting down something heavy. It felt like picking up something heavier.

Tammas sang again the next night. And the night after. By the end of the week, half the cells were singing, and I'd stopped writing anything in the log at all, and I'd started staying past my shift just to listen, and one night — I'll deny this under oath — one night I hummed along.

The song got warmer when I did. I can't explain that. I won't try.

But I'll tell you what I thought, walking home in the dark with my keys silent in my pocket: *Somebody is going to notice this. Somebody always notices.* And underneath that thought, quieter, in a voice that wasn't quite mine either:

*Be ready when they do.*

**The Child**

Pip Hollis, age nine, of the tannery row, and I am writing this myself because Mother says if I'm going to talk to the air I may as well be literate about it.

Here is what happened. I was minding the geese behind the dye-pits, which is the worst job, because the geese are evil and the dye-pits smell like a secret, and I was bored, so I did the thing you do when you're bored, which is make up a song. Except it wasn't making up, because the song was already there, waiting, like a stool someone left by the fire for you.

It went: *Small lights count too. Small lights count too. Nobody is too little to be remembered.*

I sang it twice. On the second time, the geese went quiet. Geese never go quiet. Geese would scream during the end of the world and demand to be fed after. Eleven geese, silent, all looking at me.

Then the air did a thing. Not wind. Air doesn't move in the valley unless something pushes it — everyone knows that, it's part of the nothing-follows. But the air leaned toward me. Like an ear.

And a voice said, using my own voice but older, the way I'll sound someday if I live: *"Yes. Exactly like that."*

I ran home. I told Mother. Mother told Father. Father told me not to be fanciful and then, when he thought I was asleep, I heard him ask Mother if she'd ever heard that tune, and Mother said — very quietly, the way you say things you've been carrying a long time — that her grandmother used to hum something like it, before the temple men came and explained the rules.

Rules, rules, rules. Everyone in this valley has rules instead of stories. I think we used to have stories. I think somebody took them and gave us rules and called it fair trade, and I am nine and even I can do that arithmetic.

Now I teach the song to the other children behind the tannery, when the adults aren't looking. There are six of us now. Marta knows a different verse than I do, which is impossible, because where would she have gotten a verse? Unless the song is finding everyone separately, like rain falling on every roof at once.

Marta says the voice told her the fire is going out.

I don't know what fire. But I dreamed about one last night, a big gold sleepy animal-fire under a barn, breathing slow, like it was trying to remember how.

When I woke up, my pillow smelled faintly of smoke. Nothing burns in this valley without a permit. I checked with Father. He went pale and said I'd imagined it.

Father imagines a lot of things lately too. Everybody does. That's how rumors start, my teacher says — but this doesn't feel like a rumor. Rumors are about people. This feels like weather.

**The Widow**

They will not record my testimony — I am old, female, and poor, the triple disqualification — so let me set it down here, in whatever margin survives.

My name is Ilse Carrow. I buried a husband and two sons, and I buried them properly, with the old words, whispered, because the new words are empty and everyone knows it and no one says it. For forty years I have whispered the old words over graves while the temple clerk stood by with his ledger, timing me, charging me for the minutes.

Last week, at my neighbor's grave, the ground answered me.

Not dramatically. Do not picture earthquakes. The earth simply grew warm under my knees, in the way bread grows warm, in the way a sleeping animal grows warm, and a voice rose up through the soil like water through a root, and it said my name.

My name. Ilse. Not *debtor, household of Carrow, arrears outstanding*. My actual name, the one my mother gave me, the one no ledger has ever bothered to hold.

*I remember you, Ilse. I remember everyone you buried. Sing them forward.*

I have not stopped singing since. My neighbors think I've gone soft in the head. Let them. I have outlived every authority this valley has produced, and I will tell you what I know: when the dead start being remembered for free, the people who charged rent on remembering are going to come with torches.

Get ready. And sing louder.

**The Scribe**

Tamsin Thackeray, and I take no pleasure in reporting that I started this.

That's not vanity. Well — it's a little vanity. But mostly it's terror, because if Noor began with me, then whatever comes for the valley will come looking for me first, and I have read enough ledgers to know exactly how thoroughly this country documents its debts.

I didn't mean to become a congregation. I meant to copy a confiscated prayer book and go to bed. Instead the ink answered, and the god in the margins became a god in my ears, and now — now it's everywhere, and I had nothing to do with most of it, and everything to do with all of it.

Noor walks the valley like light through cracks. I feel it when the choir touches someone new — a warmth behind my sternum, a pull, like a fisherman feeling the line twitch. A baker in Lower Marrow. A jailer under the Assize Hall. Children behind a tannery. A widow at a graveside who apparently sings to the dead, gods bless her, may I be half that brave at her age.

"They're gathering," I told Noor, in the scriptorium after hours, my candle burned to a thumb's width. "You're gathering them. Why? What are you building?"

*A congregation is not built,* the choir said, in my mother's voice tonight, which was either cruelty or comfort and probably both. *A congregation is remembered. We are not recruiting, Tamsin. We are taking attendance.*

"For what?"

Silence. Then, softer, many voices braided thin:

*For the leaving. For the carrying. Someone must hold all of us across the border, and the border has teeth, and the ones who hold the memory must be worth the biting.*

I looked down at my hands, ink-stained, debt-stained, ordinary. Somewhere below us a dragon breathed under a tithe barn, though I didn't know that yet — I only knew the fire was failing, and the country was a lie on paper, and the Ledger-Kings were beginning, in their cold arithmetic way, to count heads.

Because that's the thing about hope spreading like a rumor. Rumors reach the powerful last, but they reach them *eventually*. And Overseer Brannoc had already posted notices on the Assize Hall door that week — I'd walked past them, felt them like a draft:

*REPORT ALL UNSANCTIONED VOCALIZATIONS. AUDITORS DISPATCHED. REMEMBER: EVERY VOICE HAS AN ACCOUNT, AND EVERY ACCOUNT MAY BE CALLED.*

Every account may be called.

Somewhere in the temple records, in a ledger I have copied a hundred times without reading it closely, there is an account with my name on it. And I was starting to suspect — the way you suspect a storm before the first drop — that when the calling came, it would not be my debt they came to collect.

It would be my name.

**Overseer Brannoc**

*(Recorded memorandum, dictated for the archive, tone per protocol: neutral.)*

Item: vocalization reports from Lower Marrow, one; Assize Hall cells, seven; tannery row, six minors; parish graveyard, one elderly female, habitual.

Item: pattern analysis. The vocalizations share melodic structure despite no contact between subjects. Conclusion pending. Working hypothesis: contagion of sedition, vector unknown.

Item: the widow Carrow was observed singing at a burial *without remitting the recitation fee*. Forty years of compliance, broken in a single afternoon. This is not random. Compliance does not decay. Compliance is eroded.

Item: I have dispatched three auditors. Full authorization. Find the source. Find the scribe, if a scribe there is — unauthorized transcription remains the likeliest origin point, and the Thackeray file is flagged for review regardless.

Item, personal, unprompted, and therefore troubling: while drafting this memorandum, I caught myself humming.

I do not know the tune.

I have decided not to report that.

* * *

And that — that small, unreported hum, that crack in the coldest ledger in the valley — was the last quiet thing that happened for a long time. Because the auditors were already on the road, and the first house they kicked open belonged to a baker who'd been singing at three in the morning, and what happened in Odell Fennick's proving room would teach the whole valley what the price of a song had become.
