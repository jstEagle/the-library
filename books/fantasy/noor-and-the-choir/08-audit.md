## 8. Audit

There is a particular pleasure in a well-balanced column. I will not apologize for it. Most men find their joy in wine or women or the smell of rain on hot stone; I find mine in the moment when the figures close — when every debt owed matches, to the copper, every debt recorded, and the world for one clean instant makes sense.

Overseer Brannoc, they call me. Third Chair of the Audit, which sounds grander than it is. What it means is that when prayers get answered without payment, I am the man sent to find out why.

And this season, prayers were being answered without payment.

The reports came in like moths against a lamp. A widow in Lowmarsh whose dead husband's cough was cured by nothing. A ferryman who swore his rope had knotted itself against the current. And now, three villages along the western terraces where people had been heard *singing* — singing in the old manner, unlicensed, unrecorded, and worst of all, unpaid.

"You understand the problem," Master Quillon had said to me that morning, not looking up from his abacus. Quillon never looks up. Looking up costs time, and time, in the Ledger-Kings' arithmetic, is the only currency that appreciates. "A prayer answered freely is a prayer answered *outside* the ledger. One such prayer is an anomaly. Two is a trend. Three—"

"Is a contagion," I finished for him.

"Is a *market failure*," he said, with the mild disgust of a man correcting my vocabulary. "Contagions are for physicians. Markets are for us. Take two auditors and the standard writs. Find the source. Foreclose as needed."

Foreclose as needed. Four words that have paid for my house, my coat, my reputation. I have said them so many times they have worn smooth as river stones, and like river stones they no longer cut anything, least of all me.

Or so I believed on the morning I rode west with Auditors Pell and Vesh and a wagon for seized goods.

* * *

Pell is young and believes everything, which makes him efficient. Vesh is older than me and believes nothing, which makes her thorough. Between them they make a serviceable instrument, and I have learned not to think of them as anything else, because instruments do not ask questions and neither, ideally, do I.

The first village was called Harrow's End, though the maps call it Terrace Settlement Nine, because names are a form of wealth and these people had none worth recording. We arrived at midday, when the fields were empty and the lanes were full, and we did what auditors do: we set up the table, unfurled the writ, and waited for the guilty to identify themselves.

They always do. Not because they are guilty — most of them aren't, not really; their debts are arithmetic accidents, bad harvests multiplied by bad luck — but because guilt is the only language we offer them, and people learn to speak whatever language is spoken at them.

"By order of the Ledger-Kings," Pell read, in his clear young voice, "all persons in this settlement found to have received value without recorded payment are subject to immediate assessment and foreclosure of assets."

An old woman spat near our table. Not at it. Near it. There is a difference, and the difference is courage, and I noted it the way I note everything: without comment, in the appropriate column.

It took until evening to find them. The singers. They weren't hiding, which was the strange part. We followed the sound down past the millpond — six voices, maybe seven, rising out of a barn with its doors open, singing something in a mode I did not recognize and yet somehow did, the way you recognize a face in a crowd before you know why.

Vesh looked at me. I nodded. We went in.

They stopped when they saw the seals. Six of them — a stonemason, two sisters who kept bees, a boy no older than fourteen, a woman with flour still on her hands, and an old man holding a lantern he didn't need yet. They stood in the lamplight of their own barn and did not run.

"Names," said Pell, opening his book.

They gave them. That was the saddest part, how readily they gave them. The mason was Coram Hale. The bee-sisters were Odile and Fern. The boy was Tammas. The flour-handed woman was Sefa — no, wait, that name comes later, keep it straight, Brannoc — the flour-handed woman was Maret. The old man gave his name as Ede and then added, unprompted, "I owe eleven marks already, if that speeds things along."

It did not speed things along. Nothing speeds things along. That is the first thing they teach you: the audit is not meant to be fast. It is meant to be felt.

We assessed. It is not complicated work. You take what has value, you record what has none, and you leave enough that the household does not die outright, because a dead debtor repays nothing. This is called mercy, in our ledgers. I have signed the word mercy more times than any priest has pronounced it.

From the bee-sisters we took the hives — two of four, per regulation. From the mason, his tools, which is a cruelty dressed as procedure, since without tools he cannot work and cannot pay, but the ledger does not concern itself with next year. From the old man Ede, the lantern, which was brass and therefore worth taking, and I watched him hand it over with the courtesy of a man donating to a temple, and I did not enjoy that.

The boy Tammas owned nothing. We took his shoes anyway, because there is a category in the writs for *items of sentimental value*, and sentimental value, unrecovered through payment, constitutes an asset in default. I wrote it down. My handwriting did not shake. I checked.

* * *

It was the second village where the crack came. But I am getting ahead of myself, and a good auditor never gets ahead of himself; the record must follow the event, not precede it.

Between Harrow's End and the second village — Wetherlow, Terrace Settlement Seven — we made camp, and I sat up with the seizure manifests, balancing them, because balance is the closest thing I have to prayer. Pell slept. Vesh kept the first watch and smoked a pipe she was too disciplined to smoke more than twice a year.

"You've gone quiet," she said.

"I'm always quiet."

"You're quiet like a closed ledger. Tonight you're quiet like an open one." Vesh has been reading me for thirty years and considers it a hobby. "Something in that barn bothered you."

"The boy's shoes bothered me," I said, because lying to Vesh is a waste of both our evenings.

"And the rest?"

"The rest was procedure."

"Mm." She drew on the pipe, once, and put it away. "I foreclosed on a fiddle in Kellin's Ford twenty years ago. Belonged to a girl who played at weddings. Her family owed nine marks. The fiddle was worth twelve. I remember thinking the ledger had done her a favor." She looked at the fire. "I don't remember the girl's face. I remember the fiddle's. Isn't that something."

"It isn't something," I said. "It's wear. Instruments wear. So do we."

She laughed at that, dry as an audit stamp, and said nothing else, and I balanced my columns, and every figure closed, and I felt nothing whatsoever, and I want that on the record: on the night between the two villages, Overseer Brannoc felt nothing whatsoever.

* * *

Wetherlow was worse prepared than Harrow's End, which is to say they had been warned, which is to say they had done nothing, because what can you do? You cannot hide from arithmetic. We set the table in the square and the square emptied around it like water around a stone, except for one knot of people outside the chapel who would not disperse, and who were, when we approached, singing.

Four of them this time. Three women and a man, standing shoulder to shoulder on the chapel steps, singing in that same unrecognized-yet-recognized mode, and here is the detail that matters, the detail I have turned over every night since like a coin I suspect is counterfeit: they were singing *the same song*. Different voices, different village, forty miles of bad road between them — the same melody, the same words, word for word, note for note.

Songs travel. Of course songs travel. Peddlers carry them, weddings trade them, soldiers spread them like lice. But these were not traveling songs. These were the old hymns, the forbidden ones, the ones burned out of circulation two generations ago, and two villages that had no commerce with each other were singing them identically, newly, as if remembering rather than learning.

"Disperse," said Pell, and they did not disperse.

"By order of the writ," said Vesh, and they did not stop singing.

So we did what the writ says. We assessed them on the spot. The man was a cooper; we took his adze and his iron hoops. One woman owned a loom; the loom went on the wagon. The second woman had nothing but a ring, her mother's, and she held out her own hand for it to be taken, which is a thing I have seen perhaps five times in thirty years and have never once gotten used to.

The third woman was old. Old the way trees are old, weathered into permanence. She had no assets worth the ink to list them. Pell flipped forward in his book to the section on persons of negligible estate, and I saw him find the line, the one that reads: *persons of negligible estate may be detained for public example.*

"Detention," Pell said.

"No," I said.

Pell looked up. In thirty years I have countermanded an auditor in the field perhaps three times, and each time I had a procedural justification ready, airtight, filed. This time I had nothing. I just said no, the way you say no to a child reaching toward a stove.

"She has no assets," Pell said carefully. "The writ specifies—"

"I know what the writ specifies. Detention requires feeding. Feeding is a cost. Cost without recoverable asset is a loss entry." I invented that reasoning on the spot, and it was even true, and I hated how smoothly it came, how the machinery of justification works in me even when the rest of me has gone somewhere else entirely. "Release her. Note it as cost avoidance."

He noted it as cost avoidance. The old woman looked at me while he wrote, and her eyes were the color of tea left too long, and she did not thank me, and I understood that she knew exactly what I was, and that being spared by a thing like me is not a kindness, it is a delay.

Then she hummed.

That is the whole of it. That is the crack, the entire crack, laid bare: an old woman, released, turned to go back into her chapel, and as she went she hummed four notes under her breath, half a phrase, barely anything, and the tune went through me like a splinter finding a wound I did not know I had.

I knew it.

Not recognized — *knew*. The way you know your own front step in the dark. Four notes, rising, then falling on the fifth, and somewhere behind them a kitchen, a low fire, a voice — a woman's voice, tired, off-key on the high part, humming while she worked, and small hands, my hands, sorting beans on a table, and the smell of—

Nothing. It stopped there. Thirty years of careful accounting and the memory had been written off, depreciated to zero, and all that remained was four notes and the shape of a feeling with no object attached.

"Overseer?" Pell was watching me. So was Vesh. So, damnably, was the old woman, half-turned on the chapel steps, humming, waiting.

"Note the hymn," I heard myself say. "Lyrics if obtainable. Melody notation. The Kings will want the pattern documented."

Which was true. Which was procedure. Which was also, I realized as I dictated it, the first time in my career I had ever ordered evidence gathered because I wanted to look at it again.

* * *

We rode out of Wetherlow at dusk with the wagon heavier and the manifest complete, and I did not balance the columns that night. I told myself it was fatigue. Fatigue is an acceptable explanation; auditors are permitted fatigue. What auditors are not permitted is the other thing, the thing sitting in my saddlebag in the form of a melody transcribed in my own hand, four notes rising and falling, humming back at me from the page whenever I looked away from it.

Because here is what I could not stop calculating, and I use the word *calculating* deliberately, because calculation is my native tongue and I went looking for refuge in it and found none:

The song predated the ban. The ban predates my birth. My mother — and I use the word *mother* with caution, since the memory has no face, only a voice — my mother sang a banned hymn to me in a kitchen I cannot picture, in a childhood I cannot otherwise access, and no one ever taught me that hymn, and no ledger records that I ever heard it.

Which means the memory exists outside the ledger.

You must understand what that sentence does to a man like me. Everything is in the ledger. Every debt, every payment, every asset, every life reduced to entries — that is not cruelty, whatever the pamphleteers say, that is *honesty*. The ledger does not lie. The ledger does not forget. The ledger is the only fair thing in the valley, because it treats the earl and the bee-keeper identically: as sums.

And now there was a sum in me that no ledger contained. Four notes, carried free of charge, inherited without contract, owed to no one — and answering, tonight, in two villages at once, in voices that matched each other across forty miles of road like columns that had been balanced by some hand other than ours.

Prayers answered without payment, Quillon had said. Market failure. Contagion.

But it was not spreading outward like a contagion. It was surfacing inward, like water returning to a well that someone had pumped dry — as if the songs had never left, only been *entered wrong*, and something was now correcting the record.

On the third day we reached the third village on the list, and found the whole settlement in the square, singing, and among the faces turned toward our wagon was a young scribe in temple gray with ink-stained fingers and the look of someone carrying something heavy at a run.

Pell reached for his writ. Vesh reached for her irons.

I reached, without deciding to, for the melody in my pocket, and held it there, four notes against my palm, like a coin I had not yet decided whether to spend.

"Wait," I said.

They waited. The singing did not.
