## 9. Running the Border Stones

There is a particular kind of fool who believes that knowing a thing is impossible makes attempting it easier. I had been that kind of fool my whole life — the scribe who read the ledgers and thought understanding the cage was the same as having a key to it.

So when Noor told me we were leaving, I packed a bag like a sensible person instead of laughing until I cried, which was the other option.

"You understand," I said, lacing my boots by candlelight in the scriptorium's back alcove, "that no one leaves. Not debtors, not pilgrims, not corpses, if the gravediggers are to be believed. The valley keeps what it takes."

"The valley," said Noor, in my mother's voice, which was either cruelty or comfort and probably both, "keeps what it is paid to keep."

"And who pays?"

Noor didn't answer with words. The candleflame leaned sideways, all at once, as though something enormous had exhaled in a room with no windows. Outside, far off toward the eastern ridge, I heard — or imagined I heard — a sound like a bell struck underwater. Once. Then nothing.

"That," said Noor, "is what is left of the payment."

I should have asked more questions. A sensible scribe would have. But I had spent three weeks transcribing prayers that answered back, learning that a god made of remembered voices could sit in the margins of my confiscated hymnal like a cat that had decided the house was its own, and I had learned that Noor answered questions the way rivers answer stones: eventually, and on their own schedule.

I took the hymnal. I took a stub of graphite, a knife, two days of bread I had bought honestly with wages I had earned copying the very ledgers that bound me, because irony apparently came free in this valley even when everything else was mortgaged. And at the hour when the temple bells went quiet and the auditors' lamps had all guttered down to embers, I walked out of the scriptorium where I had served eleven years of an inherited debt, and I did not look back, because looking back felt like something out of a ballad, and ballads always ended badly for people who looked back.

* * *

The border stones stood along the eastern ridge like teeth in a jaw that had never quite finished closing. You could see them from the valley floor on clear days — grey monoliths taller than a man, older than any chart, each one carved with symbols so worn they might have been weather or might have been writing; the distinction, I would learn, mattered enormously.

Sefa had told me about them once, years ago, in her jailer's flat voice, the way she told prisoners about the weather. *They tried to carry old Marta out when she died in the cells. Four men. They got her halfway between the third stone and the fourth and then they came back carrying nothing, and none of them would say what happened, and one of them never spoke again.* She'd said it the way you report a leaky roof. I had filed it away under *things the valley does* and gone back to my ink.

Now I stood at the foot of the first stone with my bag on my shoulder and my heart going like a debtor's knock at midnight, and I understood that Marta had been the lucky one. Dead things don't feel what comes next.

"Noor," I whispered. "If this goes wrong—"

"It will go wrong," said the choir, from everywhere, gently. "Then it will go differently. That is how leaving works."

"Comforting."

"I am not a comforting god. I am a remembered one. There is a difference, and the difference is mostly honesty."

The night was windless. The stars over the ridge seemed dimmer than stars should be, as though someone had drawn a curtain partway across them — and I remember thinking, with the detached clarity that sometimes visits right before disaster, *that's not cloud. Clouds move.*

I stepped between the first stone and the second.

For six paces, nothing. Grass. Frost. My own breath hanging in front of me like a small ghost that hadn't yet been assessed for tax.

At the seventh pace, the world refused me.

I don't know how else to describe it. There was no wall, no hand, no beast. There was simply a place where the air stopped agreeing to be air. It pressed against me the way deep water presses, except it pressed inward from every direction at once, and it did not want me dead — that was the horror of it, it was not malicious — it wanted me *sorted*. Filed. Returned. I felt myself being read, thoroughly, the way an auditor reads a ledger line by line looking for the discrepancy, and somewhere behind my ribs a voice that was not mine said, with terrible bureaucratic calm, *this item does not appear on the manifest.*

I screamed. I am not ashamed of it. Anyone who tells you they wouldn't scream has never been read.

And then Noor was there — not beside me but *in* me, or around me, a choir of the dead and the borrowed wrapping itself over my skin like a cloak of voices, singing something old enough that the syllables had worn smooth, and for one blazing moment the pressure eased, the reading stuttered, and I stumbled forward another pace, and another, close enough to see the frost on the far side of the border, close enough to imagine the smell of grass that belonged to some other country—

The border *hit* me.

It came up through the ground, through the soles of my boots, through the bones of my legs, and it threw me. Not pushed — thrown, the way a door slams, the way a ledger closes. I went backward through the dark and landed hard on the valley side, skidding on frozen grass, and the last thing I saw before the world whited out was the space between the third and fourth stones lighting up with lines of fire — not flame, exactly, but *script*, burning script, vast letters written in something gold and starving, and the letters said, in a language I somehow knew without ever having learned it:

**PAID IN FULL UNTIL THE FIRE FAILS.**

Then the light snapped out, and I lay in the frost with blood in my mouth and my bag split open and my mother's hymnal lying face-down in the grass beside me, and I laughed, because the alternative was screaming again and I had used up my ration.

*Paid in full until the fire fails.*

"Well," I said to the sky, wheezing. "That's ominous."

* * *

Noor found me before I'd finished picking myself up. Or rather, Noor arrived — the temperature dropped, the frost sang faintly underfoot, and every sound in the night took on a second layer, a harmonic, as though the world were being performed by an orchestra hidden just out of sight.

"You knew," I said. I meant it as accusation. It came out as exhaustion. "You knew it would do that."

"I suspected. Knowing and suspecting are different debts." The choir shifted voices — my mother, then a stranger, then someone young and bright whose voice made my chest ache for reasons I couldn't name. "But now we know together, and that is worth a broken rib."

"Nothing's broken."

"Your fourth rib disagrees. It has filed a complaint."

I sat down hard in the grass, which the rib appreciated about as much as you'd expect. The hymnal lay open where it had fallen, pages bent, and in the margin of the first psalm — the psalm my mother used to hum while she mended clothes, the one that got her called a heretic and worse — fresh ink was blooming. Not my ink. Ink the color of banked coals.

*You saw the writing on the stones,* Noor wrote, in that same coal-colored hand. *Tell me what it said.*

"'Paid in full until the fire fails.'" I traced the words in the air with one finger, wincing. "Which means someone paid. Once. For the whole border. For—" and here the thought arrived, slow and cold, like ice forming across the surface of a pond, starting at the edges and closing inward, "—for the whole *valley*. Everything. The magiclessness. The keeping. The way nothing follows us in or out."

"Yes."

"And payments don't last forever."

"No."

"So what happens when it runs out?" I asked, and then, because I was a scribe and therefore constitutionally incapable of leaving a question half-asked: "What is the payment *made of*?"

The choir went silent. All of it. Every borrowed voice at once, and the silence was worse than the border-stone's blow, because the border had only wanted to file me away, and this silence was grief — old, enormous, patient grief, the kind that has been waiting centuries for someone to finally ask the obvious question.

Noor answered in one voice only. Mine. My own voice, taken from wherever gods keep such things, speaking words I had never said:

"There is a dragon beneath the tithe barn, Tamsin Thackeray. There has always been a dragon beneath the tithe barn. Its fire is the payment. It has been paying since before your temple had a name, and it is nearly empty, and when it gutters out, the border will not merely fail." A pause, long enough for me to hear my own heartbeat stumble. "It will *collapse*. And everything the spell holds apart will come together, and the valley — the country that was unmade, the country your maps refuse — will be erased so completely that even the memory of having been erased will be sold to pay for the erasing."

I stared at the hymnal. At the coal-dark ink. At the border stones standing black against the dimmed stars, still humming faintly with the residue of that golden script.

"They're not guarding us in," I heard myself say. The wry voice, the scribe's voice, the voice I used for everything unbearable. "They're keeping the fire *banked*. We're not prisoners. We're a *reserve*."

"You are collateral," Noor said, kindly, which was somehow the cruelest available option. "All of you. Every soul in the valley is a line item securing a loan against a dying dragon."

I thought of Brannoc's auditors with their brass seals and their arithmetic smiles. I thought of the Ledger-Kings in their high houses, calling in debts, foreclosing on singers, burning prayer books — and suddenly every cruelty of the last eleven years reorganized itself in my head into a single coherent document, and I understood, with the vertigo of a man who finds his whole life was a footnote, that the Ledger-Kings weren't exploiting the valley's poverty.

They were *managing the estate*. And the estate was us.

"So the fire must be fed," I said slowly, working it through like a difficult column of figures, "or freed. Fed, and the cage stays up forever and we all stay collateral until the end of the world. Freed—" I stopped. "What happens if it's freed?"

Noor's answer came in many voices at once, a chord rather than a word, and underneath the chord, almost too quiet to catch, ran a melody I knew. My mother's psalm. The heretic's hymn. The song she'd been singing the night she walked east and never came home — walking, I realized now, not away from me, but *toward* these very stones, toward the seam in the world, toward whatever door she'd believed was waiting on the other side.

"If it is fed freely," the choir said, "the country remembers itself."

"And if it's freed?"

"If it is freed," said Noor, in my mother's voice, soft as banked coals, "then someone must carry what the fire cannot. Someone must walk out through a door that is about to stop existing, carrying the whole memory of a nation — and the door opens only for the one who carries it, and it exacts a price that I will not lie to you about tonight, because tonight you have bled enough."

I looked at the border stones. I looked at the hymnal, and the coal-dark ink was already fading, saving itself, hoarding its strength like everything else in this valley.

Eleven years I had copied ledgers. Eleven years of inherited debt, of other people's numbers in my hand, of a life assessed and filed and held as security against obligations I never chose. And now the largest ledger of all lay open in front of me, written in fire on standing stones, and the balance outstanding was a dragon's dying breath, and the creditor was everyone I had ever known.

I picked up the hymnal. I tucked it inside my coat, against the rib that had filed its complaint, and I turned my back on the border — on the door my mother had died reaching for — and faced the long dark slope down toward the valley, toward the temple, toward the tithe barn.

"Beneath the tithe barn," I said.

"Beneath the tithe barn," said Noor, and every voice in the choir, all at once, began very softly to sing.

I started walking. Behind me, the border stones hummed their golden warning — *paid in full until the fire fails* — and ahead of me, under the frozen fields, under the granaries and the tithes and the eleven years of my life, something vast and starving turned over in its sleep, and dreamed, I think, of being owed.
