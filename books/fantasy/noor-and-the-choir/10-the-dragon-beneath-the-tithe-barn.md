## 10. The Dragon Beneath the Tithe Barn

I had copied the tithe barn's dimensions eleven times in my life as a scribe — length, breadth, height of the rafters, capacity in bushels of wheat, bushels of barley, bushels of *obedience* (that last one was my own private joke, never written down, because the Overseers read everything). Eleven times I had recorded that the barn's floor was packed earth over fieldstone, and eleven times I had believed it.

The twelfth time, I noticed the floor was warm.

Not warm like sun-baked stone at noon. Warm like a sleeping animal. Warm like the space just above a candle flame, if the candle were the size of a cathedral and burning very, very slowly, somewhere far below.

"You knew," I said to Noor, who was currently occupying the voices of three dead choirboys and my grandmother's sister, a combination that made every word sound like it was being sung by someone who'd rather be anywhere else. "You've known since the border stones."

"The fire is close here," Noor said, in a voice like a hymn sung through water. "We did not know what held it. We only knew it was not free."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning nothing in this valley burns unless someone is paid for the burning."

I stood in the dark of the tithe barn with a stolen lantern and a stolen key and a stolen sense of purpose, which was the only kind I owned outright. Above me, the rafters sagged under sacks of grain that would never feed anyone — tribute grain, contract grain, grain measured and sealed and shipped out through the pass to pay debts no one alive had incurred. Below me, something breathed.

The breathing was the worst part. It was slow. Slower than any animal has any right to breathe. One breath took so long that between the exhale and the inhale there was a silence long enough for me to doubt it entirely, to convince myself I'd imagined the whole thing, that the warmth was a fever, that Noor was a hallucination brought on by ink fumes and heresy. And then the inhale would come — a vast, tectonic drawing-in, the floorboards flexing almost imperceptibly, dust rising from the cracks in little sighing columns — and doubt would die again.

"How do we get down?" I whispered.

"There is a stair," said one of the dead choirboys, helpfully. Dead people are always helpful. They have nothing left to lose and it shows.

There was indeed a stair. Of course there was. There is always a stair beneath a temple; temples are mostly stair with a little roof on top. This one was behind the grain racks, hidden behind a false wall of planks so old they'd gone grey as driftwood, and when I pried the first board loose, the warmth came through the gap like a hand pressed flat against my face.

I want to say I hesitated. I want to report some moment of mortal caution, some sensible voice saying *Tamsin Thackeray, debt-bound scribe, perhaps do not descend into the earth toward an enormous source of heat that the most powerful institution in the valley has seen fit to hide.* But the truth is that I had spent twenty-nine years being sensible, and sensibility had gotten me a cell, a ledger, a bounty on my head, and a god living in my ears. Caution and I were through.

I went down.

* * *

The stair went further than the barn was tall. That should have been my first clue about how much of this valley was a lie built on top of another lie. Forty steps, sixty, eighty, the walls changing from fieldstone to dressed granite to something older, something that wasn't quarried so much as *grown*, veined with mineral streaks that caught my lantern light and threw it back amber.

At the bottom was a door. Not a grand door — a clerk's door, oak and iron, with a lock that cost more than my mother's funeral. Sefa's keys opened it on the third try. She'd insisted I take the full ring, all forty-seven keys, even though they clanked like a one-woman percussion section. "You'll need them," she'd said, "or you'll need one I didn't think of." Jailer logic. I was beginning to respect it.

Beyond the door was a room that was not a room.

It was a cavern, but *cavern* is too generous a word, too wild. This had been engineered. The walls rose in smooth curves to a height I couldn't judge, ribbed with iron bands bolted into the living rock, and between the bands ran pipes — copper pipes, brass pipes, hundreds of them, thick as my torso, all converging from the darkness above and descending into the floor around a single central structure.

The structure was a cage. Or rather: the structure had been a cage once, and then someone had done arithmetic, and now it was a harness.

The dragon lay inside it.

I have read every description of dragons in the temple archive. I have copied bestiaries until the word *draco* lost meaning and became just a shape my pen made. The bestiaries say: vast, winged, scaled in bronze or crimson or the green of deep water, eyes like furnaces, breath like the end of the world. They say dragons are pride and hunger given wings. They say a dragon's hoard is its heart kept outside its body.

They do not say what a dragon looks like when it has been milked for two hundred years.

This one was grey. Not silver-grey, not storm-grey — the grey of ash after the fire has moved on. Its scales hung loose over a body that had folded itself as small as its bones allowed, wings clamped tight and visibly wasted, the membranes thin and scarred where the harness straps crossed them. Its ribs showed through the scale-gaps like barrel staves through a rotten cask. Chains ran from its limbs to the iron bands in the walls, but the chains were slack — not because the dragon could have broken them, but because breaking things requires strength, and strength requires food, and food requires fire, and the fire was being drawn off through those beautiful copper pipes before it could ever reach the dragon's own body.

The pipes hummed. That was the sound I'd been hearing since the second landing — not machinery, not wind. A hum like a struck bell that never stops ringing, the sound of heat being harvested, metered, sold.

And above the dragon's head, mounted on the iron band like a plaque on a magistrate's bench, was a contract.

I walked toward it without deciding to. My legs did it on their own. The contract was written on vellum, in the high formal hand they use for founding documents, and it was long — very long — but I am a scribe, and reading terrible documents quickly is the one superpower my life has granted me.

*Be it known,* it began, *that the Fire of the Last Dragon of Vael, being the sole remaining warmth of the realm, is hereby entered into perpetual covenant...*

Vael. The name landed in me like a dropped stone hitting still water. Our country had a name. Not *the valley*, not *the district*, not *the territory formerly comprising* — a name. Vael. I mouthed it silently, tasting it, and felt Noor go utterly still inside my skull, the way a person goes still when someone says their name in a crowd.

*...the Fire shall be drawn at a rate not exceeding the covenant schedule, Schedule A appended, such draw to fund the Bonds of Foundation, the Wards of the Border, and the Stipends of the Founding Families in perpetuity...*

In perpetuity. Perpetuity is a favorite word of contracts. It means *forever*, and forever, in contract language, always means *until the thing paying runs out*. The draftsmen who wrote this knew exactly what they were doing. They wrote *in perpetuity* knowing the fire was finite, knowing the dragon was mortal, knowing that someday — soon, judging by the grey of those scales — the schedule would exceed the supply, and the whole elegant machine would seize, and everyone downstream of the pipes would discover what cold really meant.

But that was the founders' problem, wasn't it? Forever is always past the signature date.

*...and the Dragon, being party to this covenant by its freely given oath, shall be maintained in comfort proportionate to its output...*

I looked at the emaciated body. The wasted wings. The slack chains. The ash-grey hide stretched over a skull that had once, presumably, been magnificent and was now the shape of a horse's skull left in a field.

*Comfort proportionate to its output.* Output near zero. Comfort near zero. The clause was technically honored. Everything in this valley was technically honored. That was the whole genius of the Ledger-Kings: they never broke a law, never missed a payment, never violated a single clause. They just wrote the clauses so that honoring them was indistinguishable from cruelty.

"Freely given oath," I said aloud. My voice cracked on the second word. "Freely given. What did they hold over you? Your eggs? Your—"

"They held the country," Noor said quietly, and all the borrowed voices said it together, choirboys and grandmothers and strangers, and for once none of them sounded reluctant. "The founders came to the dragon when the wars were ending. They said: give us your fire and we will ward the valley, and nothing will ever hurt this place again. No plague. No invasion. No magic but ours, controlled, metered, safe. And the dragon —" the voices caught, harmonized, broke apart again — "the dragon loved this country. Do you understand? It was the last of its kind in the world, and this country was the only place that had ever made room for it. It said yes. It thought it was buying us a forever."

"And they turned its love into a revenue stream."

"They turned everything into a revenue stream. That is what a ledger is. It does not care what it counts. It counts, therefore it owns."

I stood there in the humming dark, reading clause after clause, and something happened in my chest that I can only describe as a key turning. All my life I had understood the valley's economy as weather — a thing that simply was, unfair the way droughts are unfair, but natural, structural, nobody's fault in particular. My mother's debt. My inherited servitude. The prayer tax. The memory levy. The way the poor borrowed against next year's labor at rates that guaranteed next year belonged to someone else. Weather. Structure. Fate.

It was not fate. It was a *schedule*. Appendix A, line item by line item, the fire of the last dragon in the world apportioned out to keep a handful of families rich and a country asleep. My mother hadn't died of bad luck. She had died of a line item. Every debtor in the temple cells was a rounding error in a two-hundred-year-old extraction scheme, and the thing being extracted wasn't wheat or labor or coin.

It was the country's own heartbeat, piped out through copper, sold back to us as wards and stipends and the privilege of forgetting.

I tore the contract off the wall.

I don't remember deciding to. I remember the sound — vellum ripping free of its mountings, nails shrieking out of ancient iron — and I remember the dragon's eye opening.

* * *

An eye the size of a cartwheel, opening eight feet away from you, is an education. The bestiaries say furnace. They undersell it. The eye was a furnace, yes, but a banked one, a hearth raked down to coals, and in the coals there was awareness — old, exhausted, and so sad that my fury stumbled mid-stride and nearly fell over.

The pupil contracted. Fixed on me. On the lantern. On the torn contract hanging from my fist like a dead bird.

"Don't," I said — to myself, absurdly, warningly, as though I were both the reckless party and the responsible one, which, in fairness, has been true my entire life. "Don't look at me like that. I'm angry enough already and you're making it worse."

The dragon exhaled. Even banked, even starved, the exhale moved my hair and smelled of hot stone and, underneath that, of something sweeter — incense, maybe, or the memory of incense, the smell of every temple fire that had ever been lit from this same source. Because that was the other thing the contract told me, buried in clause fourteen: every sacred flame in every temple in the valley was kindled from the covenant draw. Every candle I'd ever prayed over was secondhand dragonfire, rented, metered, taxed. The gods' altars had been running on the dragon's blood this whole time, and no one had told the gods, and no one had told the dragon.

"Noor," I said. "Can it hear you?"

A pause. Then, softly, in a voice I hadn't heard Noor use before — hushed, reverent, the voice of someone entering a sickroom: "It can hear anything that remembers it. Ask it its name."

"That seems personal."

"Tamsin." All the voices at once, gentle. "It has not heard its name spoken aloud in two hundred years. The contract calls it *the Asset*. The overseers call it *the Source*. The founders called it *the Covenant Partner*. Ask."

My throat was dry. I stepped closer — close enough to feel the heat coming off the great grey flank, heat being siphoned away even as it was made, a body warmed by a fire it was never allowed to keep — and I looked up into the banked-furnace eye, and I asked.

"What's your name?"

The silence afterward lasted four of the dragon's slow breaths. In the fourth exhale, something changed in the quality of the air — a pressure, a gathering, the feeling before thunder — and the dragon spoke.

Its voice was not a roar. Roaring is for creatures with strength to spare. Its voice was a creak, a groan, a hinge two centuries un-oiled, and it came up through the floor and the pipes and my own breastbone more than through the air:

"*Ember-of-the-First-Morning,*" it said, and each word cost it visibly, the great sides heaving. "*Who Was To Be Kept In Comfort.*"

The bitter precision of it. Two hundred years of silence, and the first thing it chose to say was its grievance, quoted verbatim from its own contract. I laughed — one broken, furious note — and then I was crying, which was less dignified, and then I was angry again, angrier than I had ever been in my careful, copious, compliant life, because the machinery of it was so *clean*. No cruelty for cruelty's sake. Just schedules. Just perpetuity. Just a loving creature, promised comfort, milked to ash, while above it clerks like me copied the bushel-counts and never once wondered why the floor was warm.

"I'm going to burn that contract," I told the dragon. "I don't know how yet. I don't know what happens to the wards, or the borders, or any of it. But I'm going to burn it, and I'm going to do it in front of the men who wrote it."

The eye regarded me. Coals shifting. And then, faintly, impossibly, the corner of that vast ruined mouth curled — not a smile, nothing so simple, but the ancestor of smiles, the thing smiles were descended from.

"*Small scribe,*" said Ember-of-the-First-Morning, "*who Was To Be Kept In Comfort,*" and I realized it had read my debt-brand, the ledger-number seared into my wrist at birth, and had translated it into its own native tongue of contractual grievance. Kinship, dragon-style. "*Bring me your god. We have an accounting to settle.*"

Above us, muffled through eighty steps of stone and a floor of lies, the tithe barn doors banged open, and boots came down across the warm boards — many boots, in step, unhurried, the cadence of men who owned the building and everything under it.

Noor's voices went sharp as a blade: "Auditors. Four of them. They followed the heat readings — Brannoc has begun measuring the valley's temperatures against the covenant schedule, and we are *not on schedule*."

I looked at the key ring on my belt. Forty-seven keys. Somewhere among them, presumably, was the one to the cage.

Somewhere behind me, at the top of the stairs, was the lock those boots were walking toward.

"*Choose quickly,*" rumbled the dragon, and drew its next long, starving breath.
