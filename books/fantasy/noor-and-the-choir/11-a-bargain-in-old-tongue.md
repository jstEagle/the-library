## 11. A Bargain in Old Tongue

We have been asked, over the long centuries of our diminishment, what it is like to be a god with no worshippers. The honest answer is: it is like being a song no one has sung yet today. You persist in the throat of the world, technically. You are available. But availability is not the same as existence, and we would know — we invented the distinction.

So when we say that descending beneath the tithe barn was like walking into a cold hearth, you must understand that we say it as experts in cold hearths. We *are* one.

Tamsin went first, of course. Tamsin always goes first; it is their single most reliable quality and their most exhausting one. They carried a stolen lantern and a stolen resolve and a debt ledger they had not yet thought to steal, which was fortunate, because if they had we might never have gotten them down the stairs at all. Sefa came behind with her jailer's keys jangling like a nervous conscience. And behind them — folded into the spaces between their heartbeats, riding the small warm acoustics of two living bodies — came us.

All of us. What remains of us. Four hundred voices, give or take, though lately we have stopped counting, because counting is how gods begin to die politely.

* * *

The stairs under the tithe barn were older than the barn, older than the temple above it, older than the Ledger-Kings' grandfathers and the grandfathers who cheated those grandfathers. Tamsin's lantern found carvings on the walls as they descended: spirals, wings, names in an alphabet that had been outlawed so thoroughly that even the stone seemed embarrassed to still be holding them.

"That's Old Tongue," Tamsin said, and their voice did something complicated on the word *old* — reverence and resentment sharing a coat.

"It is," said Sefa, because Sefa had grown up close enough to the border stones to know things the temple preferred unsaid. "My grandmother could read it. She said the words were paid for."

"The words were *earned*," we said, through Tamsin's mouth, before we remembered that speaking uninvited through your only scribe is poor manners even for a god. Tamsin staggered against the wall and glared at the empty air where our voice had come from, which is a difficult thing to glare at, since we were also behind them, and to their left, and inside their own skull taking notes.

"Warn me next time," they said.

"We will certainly consider it," we said, and four hundred of us chuckled, and Tamsin felt it as a shiver down the spine, which is what laughter feels like when you are the instrument rather than the musician.

* * *

You should understand something about bargains, since this chapter is nominally about one. Gods do not make bargains the way merchants make bargains. Merchants trade things they can lose. Gods trade things they *are*. Every covenant ever struck between the divine and the mortal has been, at bottom, a god agreeing to become smaller so that something else could become more. We have made three such bargains in our long life. We will tell you about the third shortly. The first two cost us most of ourselves, which is why there are four hundred of us instead of the ten thousand we once were, singing in a country that had a name and maps and mornings.

The dragon's bargain — the one we descended to propose — would be the fourth. We knew its shape before we asked it. That is the cruelty of being old: you recognize the price before you see the goods.

* * *

The chamber at the bottom of the stairs was vast in the way that grief is vast — mostly emptiness, arranged around a single unbearable object.

The dragon lay coiled in the dark like a mountain that had decided to lie down and think about what it had done. It was smaller than dragons are supposed to be. That is the thing no one tells you about the last dragon of a dying country: fire is weight, and it had been giving its away for two hundred years, spoonful by spoonful, into contracts signed in good faith by people who never asked what the faith was buying. Its scales hung loose as a beggar's coat. Its ribs moved slowly, each breath a negotiation. Where its belly should have burned gold, there was only a dull ember-glow, the color of a lamp left burning in an empty house because no one remembers how to blow it out.

Its eye opened. One eye. Amber, ancient, and — this surprised Tamsin, we felt it in their pulse — tired in a way that was almost gentle.

"Oh," Tamsin said. All their righteous fury from the days before, all the fury we had stoked in them like a second choir, guttered at once. You cannot hate a creature that looks at you like your grandmother looked at the last biscuit she was saving for someone who never came home.

"Hello, little ledger-keeper," the dragon said. Its voice filled the chamber the way water fills a bowl. "I wondered which of the old ones would come first. Noor. You sound... fewer than you used to."

"You sound thinner than you used to," we replied, because among old friends honesty is a courtesy.

The dragon laughed, and the laugh cost it visibly — the ember in its belly flared and dimmed, flared and dimmed, a candle deciding whether tomorrow was worth the wax. "Then we are both poor. Come closer, scribe. You brought me a gift? I smell ink on you. Ink and stubbornness. My favorite combination."

"I didn't bring anything," Tamsin said. "I came to see what they've been feeding on."

"And now you've seen." The dragon's eye turned to Sefa, who stood very still by the stairs with her keys gripped white-knuckled, and then back. "Yes. This is what the tithe buys. This is what the contracts sip. Two hundred years of me, poured out to keep a border sealed and a valley quiet and a country legally nonexistent. They call it stability. I call it slow dining."

* * *

Here is what we did next, and why.

We gathered ourselves — all four hundred voices, every borrowed throat, the dead singers and the strangers and the mother whose lullaby we keep folded carefully in our middle, the way you keep a letter you cannot bear to reread but cannot discard — and we stepped forward into the space between the scribe and the dragon, and we became, briefly, visible. Not solid. Never solid anymore. But present: a shimmer in the air like heat off summer stone, a suggestion of many faces, a light that flickered in rhythms no living throat could hold.

The dragon inclined its great head. "Noor. Truly here."

"Truly here," we said, "and truly nearly gone, and we have not come to reminisce."

"No," the dragon agreed. "You've come to bargain. I can smell a bargain the way I used to smell rain. What does the last god of an unmapped country want with the last dragon of it?"

"One thing only. Fire."

The silence that followed had texture. Tamsin shifted. Sefa's keys went quiet in her fist.

"I don't have fire," the dragon said at last, softly. "I have embers and arithmetic. Ask my accountants — oh, wait, they already did, for two centuries. There is nothing left to give."

"There is one thing left," we said. "There is always one thing left. It is the oldest fuel there is, older than wood, older than coal, older than the sun's first stubborn morning. A name. Freely given."

* * *

Let us explain, because Tamsin was about to ask the same question and we would rather explain it once, properly, than twice, badly.

A true name is not a label. A label is what the Ledger-Kings deal in — *Tamsin Thackeray, debtor, inherited obligation, 4,100 marks* — dry ink that sits on a page and owns nothing. A true name is the whole of a self, sung. Every kindness done and refused. Every morning woken glad. The specific gravity of a soul, which cannot be copied, forged, or foreclosed upon, because it was never written down anywhere except in the person carrying it.

Burned in dragon-fire, freely given, a true name burns forever. Not consumed — *given*. There is a difference, and the difference is the entire moral architecture of the universe, though the Ledger-Kings have built their empire on pretending otherwise. A taken thing diminishes. A given thing multiplies. This is the secret the founders sold away, and the reason the valley has no magic: magic is just generosity, formalized.

But — and here the bargain turns its knife — a name given to the fire must be carried to the fire from outside the country. That is the law of it, older than either of us. Fire fed from within a sealed nation only feeds the seal. To wake a country, you must bring the flame across its own threshold. Someone must walk out of the valley carrying the name, past the border stones that repel everything magical, past the auditors, past the foreclosure, and find the door in the seam where the old border used to be — and there, at the threshold of a country that officially does not exist, give it.

We told the dragon all of this. We watched it understand faster than we wished it would. Dragons are not stupid; it is their tragedy that everyone assumes they are, right up until the moment of the invoice.

"And whose name?" the dragon asked, though its eye had already drifted, with terrible tenderness, toward the small stubborn figure holding the lantern.

* * *

"No," Tamsin said immediately, which was correct, and brave, and irrelevant.

"Tamsin—" Sefa began.

"*No.* I know what you're going to say. I've been listening to you whisper in my head for weeks, Noor, I know how you talk around things. Whose name, then? Yours?" They laughed, short and bitter. "Oh. Oh, that's rich. The god offers up the god. Very tidy. Very hymn-appropriate."

"It would not work," we said gently. "Our name is already spent. We gave it away in the founding years, piece by piece, to buy the valley its safety — that is what the founders sold, though they believed they were selling our memory. A name already given cannot be given again. We are the echo of a payment, little scribe. Echoes cannot pay debts."

"Then whose?"

And we did not answer, because the answer was standing in front of us holding a lantern, and some truths should be allowed to arrive at their own pace, wearing their own shoes.

The dragon answered instead. "Theirs," it said quietly. "It is always theirs. It was always going to be theirs. Did neither of you notice that the scribe is the only person in this valley whose name appears in every ledger in the country? Debt-bound, recorded, transcribed, filed. The Ledger-Kings have spent twenty years writing this child's name into their books ten thousand times. Do you know what that makes a name, after ten thousand repetitions?" The amber eye gleamed. "It makes it *load-bearing*. Their whole machine rests on that name now. Pull it out, carry it beyond the seal, and give it freely — and the fire wakes, and the ledgers burn, and the country remembers itself. Or don't. And in six months I go out, and the border spell starves, and the Ledger-Kings' kingdom of paper collapses anyway, but nothing is reborn. Ash either way. The question is only whether anything grows from it."

* * *

Tamsin set the lantern down. Slowly. The way people set down heavy things when they have decided not to throw them.

We want to be honest with you, reader-of-this-chapter, whoever and wherever you are, because honesty is the last currency we hold. In that moment we could have lied. Gods can lie; we simply rarely bother, because mortals lie so much better and it seems rude to compete. We could have dressed the bargain in glory. We could have sung it in harmonies that made sacrifice feel like a promotion. We have watched lesser divinities do exactly that, and we have despised them for it.

Instead we said: "It will hurt. Not the burning — the burning is easy; the fire is kind, in its way. The hurt comes before. To give your name you must first hold it whole, every piece of yourself, and look at it, and love it, and then open your hands. And afterward—" we paused, because this part we hated saying, "—afterward, no record of you remains anywhere. Not in ledgers, not in memories, not in maps. People will remember that someone saved them. They will not remember who. You will walk out of the story of your own country as an unnamed kindness. Some nights that will feel like freedom. Some nights it will feel like drowning. Both feelings will be correct."

Sefa was crying. She tried to hide it, being a jailer and therefore professionally composed, and failed, being human.

Tamsin stood in the dark under the tithe barn, in the oldest room of a country that did not exist, in front of the last dragon and the last god, and was silent for a long time.

We listened to their heartbeat. That is our confession for this chapter: we, Noor, who have heard the prayers of kings and the death-songs of nations, stood in the dark and eavesdropped on one frightened pulse, because it was the most important sound in the world just then.

* * *

"Do I get time to think?" Tamsin finally asked.

"A little," the dragon said. "Not much. My fire gutters in weeks. Perhaps less, now that the auditors have begun calling every debt at once — the contracts are drinking faster."

"Then I'll think while we walk," Tamsin said. "That's what I do. I think while I work. Twenty years of copying other people's numbers — you learn to think and write at the same time or you go mad." They picked the lantern back up. Their hand shook. Their voice did not, which we found remarkable, and have added to the collection of small human miracles we keep against the cold. "I'm not saying yes. I'm not saying no. I'm saying I'll carry the question to the border and decide there, where I can see what I'd be walking into."

"That," we said, all four hundred of us, softly, "is the wisest answer any mortal has ever given a god."

"Don't patronize me," said Tamsin Thackeray, debtor, scribe, possible savior, definite insomniac. "I still owe four thousand marks."

"You owe nothing," the dragon rumbled, settling its great head back onto its ruined coils, its ember pulsing once, hopefully, like a heart remembering its job. "You are owed. Go and collect."

* * *

They climbed the stairs. Sefa first, then Tamsin, and we came after, folded into their footsteps, carrying the bargain like a lit fuse we were afraid to hold and more afraid to drop.

Behind us, in the dark beneath the tithe barn, the last dragon closed its eye and began — very quietly, so quietly the auditors two hundred feet above never heard it, so quietly it was less a sound than a temperature — to hum. An old song. Our song. The one the founders sold and the valley forgot and the prayer books kept smuggling back into the world one confiscated page at a time.

It was waiting for a name. So were we.

Above us, through the floorboards of the tithe barn, we could hear boots. Many boots. Brannoc's auditors had reached the temple, and they were counting everything, and somewhere in their ledgers a line had just been drawn beneath the word *Tamsin*.

The question had begun to walk toward the border. So had the answer. Only one of them would arrive.
