## 12. The Jailer's Hymn

I have been a jailer for nineteen years, and in that time I have learned three things about people: they lie about why they're here, they tell the truth to the walls, and they sing when they've stopped being afraid.

The third one is the problem.

* * *

The cells under the Assize Hall were built to hold debtors, which means they were built cheaply and with contempt. Debtors aren't dangerous. Debtors are just arithmetic that went wrong — or arithmetic that was done to them, which the ledgers never bother distinguishing. So the cells are damp little boxes of grey stone, and the locks are old, and I could pick every one of them with a hairpin if it came to that, though it never has, because picking locks is theft and theft adds years to a sentence and I am not a thief. I am a jailer. There's a difference. It's written down somewhere, probably.

For most of those nineteen years, my prisoners were quiet. They wept, they slept, they counted their debts aloud like rosaries. Nobody sang. Singing in the Assize cells had been against regulations since before I took the keys — Regulation Nine, posted on the wall by the duty desk, in that official hand that makes even cruelty look tidy: *No hymns, no chants, no unlicensed vocalization.* Unlicensed vocalization. Somebody got paid to write that. I used to wonder what their dreams looked like.

Then, four months ago, a woman named Odile Voss came through intake singing.

She wasn't singing when she arrived. She arrived the way they all do — hollowed out, clutching her foreclosure notice like a talisman that had failed her. But somewhere around the second night, alone in cell six, she started humming. Low at first, like she didn't know she was doing it. Then words. Then harmony, which is an impressive trick for one woman in a stone box, except it wasn't one woman, because by the third night the man in cell five had picked up the line, and then the boy in cell two, and then the widow in nine, and suddenly my quiet little dungeon had become something else entirely.

I should have reported it. Regulation Nine. I stood at the top of the stairs with the report form in my hand and listened to eleven debtors sing a song none of them had ever been taught, in a key no choir master would sanction, and I put the form back in the drawer.

I told myself it was mercy. That's the story I'd have given you, if you'd asked me then. Mercy, or laziness, or simple human weakness — any of those would do. What I couldn't have told you, what I didn't understand until much later, is that I stayed my hand because I recognized the song.

Not the words. Not the tune, exactly. Something underneath them, the way you recognize a face in a crowd from behind, by the set of the shoulders. A song I'd heard once, long ago, sung by someone whose name I had spent nineteen years not thinking about.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. Jailer's habit — always narrating toward the crime.

* * *

The song spread the way rumors spread, which is to say faster than any law can walk. By the time Odile Voss served out her ninety days and walked free, half the debtors who passed through my cells had heard of her, and a good number left humming. I confiscated nothing. I wrote nothing in the duty log. There is a particular kind of cowardice that looks exactly like diligence from the outside: I kept immaculate records of everything except the one thing that mattered.

The auditors noticed before I did. Of course they did. Auditors are paid to notice, and I was paid not to, and between us we achieved a perfect equilibrium right up until the night Overseer Brannoc's men came down my stairs with lanterns and warrants and took three of my singers away to the foreclosure wing.

I signed the transfer papers. Let that sit a moment. Three names — Odile's cousin Pellam, the boy Wren, the widow Ansa — and beside each one, a space for the jailer's mark, and I made the mark. My hand didn't shake. That's the part that shames me most, looking back. Not that I did it, but how easily I did it, the way you sign for a delivery of lamp oil. Nineteen years of practice at making my hand into a tool of other people's arithmetic.

Wren looked at me as they led him past the desk. Sixteen years old, foreclosed on his father's baking debts, and he looked at me — not with hatred, which I could have borne, but with the patient curiosity of someone watching a machine work, trying to figure out what it was for.

"You know the second verse," he said. "I heard you humming it. Tuesday last."

I hadn't known I'd been humming. That's the thing about songs from childhood; they live below the waterline, and they surface when you're not paying attention, and by the time you notice your own mouth moving, some part of you has already chosen.

"I don't know what you mean," I said, and stamped his paper, and sent him up into the dark.

That night I sat at my desk with the duty log open and wrote: *All quiet.* And underneath, so small it was nearly invisible, so small I could pretend later it was a smudge: *all quiet all quiet all quiet*, three times, like a spell, like a prayer to a god I didn't believe in yet, asking to be let off the hook I had built myself.

* * *

My mother sang.

There. I said it, or wrote it, which for a jailer amounts to the same confession. My mother sang, in the kitchen, in the yard, walking to market with her basket on her hip — sang the old songs, the ones without authors, the ones the temple called unlicensed before there were regulations to call them anything. She had a voice like a low fire, warm and a little smoky, and when she sang our neighbors' shutters would open, just a crack, just enough to listen.

I was nine when the auditors came for her. I remember the arithmetic more than the tears: forty-one marks owed, assessed retroactively, for *unlicensed vocalization over an estimated period of eleven years*. They had worked out a per-song fine. Someone had sat at a desk and calculated the price of every lullaby. My mother laughed when they read it out — laughed the way you laugh when the number is too big to be real — and then they took the house, and then they took her, and I went to an uncle who taught me that the safest place in the valley was inside the law, even at its ugliest edge. Especially there. Be the hand that holds the stamp, he said, and no one will ever stamp you.

So I became the hand. And for nineteen years it worked, the way a splint works, the way a tourniquet works — it kept the bleeding internal.

Here is what I never let myself ask: where did my mother go after the foreclosure wing? There are no records. I checked, once, eleven years ago, drunk enough to be brave and sober enough to remember checking. No record of release. No record of death. Just a fine assessed, a debt entered, and then a blank page, as if the ledger itself had swallowed her.

I thought, for a long time, that the blankness meant corruption — some clerk pocketing the paperwork. It didn't occur to me, then, that the blankness might be the point. That in this valley, people don't only disappear from their homes. They can be disappeared from memory itself.

* * *

Three nights after they took Wren, the singing started again, and this time it was coming from the empty cells.

Cell six, where Odile had begun it all. Cell nine, where the widow had harmonized. Empty, swept, doors standing open on darkness, and out of that darkness — voices. Not echoes. Echoes repeat what was given to them; these voices were adding verses I'd never heard, in arrangements no one alive had written, and underneath them all ran a bass note like a heartbeat in a hillside.

I did what any sensible jailer does when confronted with the impossible. I checked the locks. All sound. I checked the grate. Sealed. I stood in the corridor with my lantern shaking in my hand and listened to dead air sing, and I heard — clear as my mother's kitchen, clear as the day the auditors came — the second verse.

Wren had been right. I knew it. Knew it the way you know your own front door in the dark.

And the verse said — I'll give it to you straight, because it deserves straightness — the verse said *you were not forgotten, you were sold,* and something in my chest that had been braced for nineteen years let go all at once, like a knot cut rather than untied.

I sat down on the cold flags of the corridor and I cried like a nine-year-old, which is to say completely, and without any plan for stopping.

When it was done, the singing paused — politely, the way a congregation pauses for the latecomer — and a voice spoke out of cell six. Not Odile's voice. Not anyone's voice I knew, and somehow everyone's, layered like cloth.

"Sefa," it said. My name. Not Sefa the jailer, not the name on my employment seal. Just Sefa, the way my mother had said it, calling me in from the yard.

"What are you?" I asked. My voice cracked on the second word, which undid whatever authority the first word had managed.

"We are what's left of being remembered," the choir said. "We are Noor. And we need a jailer."

"I'm the last person you need," I said. "I'm the lock."

"That's why," it said. "Locks know their own keys."

* * *

I want to be honest about the next part, because honesty is the only currency I have left that isn't counterfeit. I did not heroically decide to free my prisoners. I lay awake for two nights first. I made lists of reasons to stay: my pension, my uncle's warning, the sheer animal comfort of a routine, even a rotten one. I calculated — that's the word, calculated, the Ledger-Kings' own verb living in my head like a tapeworm — I calculated the cost of defiance versus the cost of complicity, and complicity was winning on points, because complicity has better odds and pays out small but steady.

What broke the calculation wasn't courage. It was Wren.

They moved him to the foreclosure wing, which I wasn't cleared to enter, but I've been a jailer nineteen years and there is no door in the Assize Hall that doesn't eventually open for someone carrying keys and a bored expression. I found him in a holding room awaiting audit-transfer, thinner, quieter, and humming. Still humming. They'd taken everything else and left him that, like taking a man's coat and boots and leaving him his shadow.

"They're going to erase the debt," he told me, when I brought him water I wasn't authorized to bring. "That's what happens in the wing. The debt gets erased." He smiled, and it was the worst smile I've ever seen. "Not forgiven. Erased. Like it never existed. Like *I* never—" He stopped. He looked at his hands. "Jailer," he said, "do you think they keep the singing? When they erase you? Or does that go too?"

And I thought of a blank page in an eleven-year-old record, and a woman with a voice like a low fire, and I thought: *she might still be in there. Somewhere in the machinery. Singing into the dark where nobody writes it down.*

I have done many things wrong in my life, but I refuse to add one more entry to that ledger: *stood outside the foreclosure wing and did the arithmetic.*

"Listen to me," I said to Wren, and my voice came out steadier than it had any right to. "In three days, the assizes sit, and the hall empties for the procession, and there will be one guard on the wing instead of four, and that guard will be me. Can you get word to the others?"

He stared at me. "You're going to open the doors."

"I'm going to open the doors," I said, and heard, distantly, absurdly, beneath the words, a faint chord of approval, like a choir drawing breath.

* * *

Procession day dawned grey and smelled of rain that wouldn't commit. The assizes emptied the hall as predicted; the temple bells rang the hour of tithe; and I walked the foreclosure wing with a ring of keys heavy as sin in my pocket, doing the last inventory of my old life.

Pellam first. Then Ansa. Then the others scattered through the lower rooms — seven in all, plus Wren, eight souls, which is either a riot or a congregation depending on your point of view. I opened each door and said the same thing, the only honest sentence available to me: "I should have done this sooner. Go."

Most of them went. Ansa paused at the wing door and looked at me with the flat assessing gaze of a woman who had spent ninety days in one of my cells. "You'll hang for this," she said.

"Probably," I said.

"Good," she said, and then, softer, almost against her will: "Thank you." And she was gone into the grey morning, and I felt lighter than I had in nineteen years, which tells you something damning about how heavy the lawful life had been.

Wren was last. He stood in the doorway of his holding room and didn't move.

"The choir wants you," he said. "It's been saying your name for two days. Well — not your name. Your mother's songs. It says them in your voice sometimes. Did you know that? It knows your voice."

"I know," I said. "I've heard it."

"It's gathering people," he went on, urgent now, leaning close like a conspirator, which I suppose we both were. "A scribe from the temple scriptorium — Tamsin Thackeray, the debtors talk about them like a saint already — and a god made of voices, and something about a dragon, and a door. It sounds mad. Everyone who hears it says it sounds mad, and then they start walking toward the tithe barn anyway." He grinned, and this time the grin was sixteen years old and furious and alive. "Are you coming, jailer? Or are you going to stand here holding the keys to a country that erased your mother?"

I took off my duty belt — the ring of office keys, the stamp, the seal — and hung it on the hook by the wing door, where it swung gently, ridiculous, orphaned. From the drawer at the desk I took instead the older ring, the master ring, the one every senior jailer keeps and no senior jailer admits to keeping: the keys to the outer gates, the canal paths, the smugglers' stair behind the Assize Hall. Nineteen years of knowing every route in and out of this valley's iron little heart.

Guilt I kept. You don't put guilt down; you carry it forward and make it useful. Keys, routes, guilt — that was my dowry, such as it was.

"Come on, then," I said to Wren, and locked the empty wing behind us out of pure habit, and then laughed — actually laughed — at the absurdity of a freed jailer locking an empty prison, and didn't unlock it, because some habits deserve to be kept as jokes.

* * *

We came up out of the smugglers' stair into the rain finally committing, and across the square, through the wet, I heard it: singing. Not one voice, not a cell's worth — dozens, scores, ragged and untrained and utterly certain, drifting from the direction of the tithe barn like smoke finding the chimney. Debtors and freed prisoners and, God help me, a baker still in his flour apron, and a child on someone's shoulders beating time with a wooden spoon.

And threading through all of it, unmistakable, in a voice like a low fire:

My mother's second verse. Sung forward. Carried.

I stood in the rain with a master ring of stolen-official keys in my fist and my whole body shaking, and Wren touched my arm and said, "There. By the barn door. The scribe."

Tamsin Thackeray turned at the sound of our footsteps — ink-stained, exhausted, hunted, and burning like a lamp — and looked at me, and at the keys, and at the nineteen years of shame standing upright behind them.

"You're the jailer," they said.

"I was," I said. "Now I'm the way out. Tell me where you need doors opened, and God forgive me, but I know every one of them — and so do the Ledger-Kings, which means we'd best hurry, because come morning, Brannoc calls a total audit, and when he does, there won't be a single lock in this valley left closed to them."

Above the tithe barn, through a gap in the clouds, something vast exhaled — a thread of gold, thin as wire, rising from the stones like breath through teeth.

The dragon was waking. And it was listening for names.
