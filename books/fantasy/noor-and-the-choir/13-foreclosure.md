## 13. Foreclosure

I have served the Ledger-Kings for twenty-two years, and in that time I have learned one truth above all others: arithmetic does not flinch. A number is a number. A debt is a debt. When you write *owed* beside a name, the name stops being a person and becomes a column, and columns are easy to carry because they weigh nothing.

That was the theory, anyway.

The summons came at dawn, delivered by a courier who did not dismount his mule, which was itself an omen. Couriers always dismount. They stretch, they complain about their backsides, they drink our terrible tea and tell us how much better the tea is in the capital. This one handed me a sealed tube, said "Total Audit," and turned his mule around before I'd even broken the wax.

*Total Audit.* Two words I had read about in training manuals and never once expected to see outside of them. The manuals described it the way a surgeon's manual describes amputation: technically, thoroughly, with diagrams, and without any mention of what the patient screams.

I stood in my office above the audit-house and read the decree three times while the ink dried on my understanding.

Effective immediately: all debts called. Every account in the valley, settled or unsettled, due or not due for years — called. All at once. No grace periods. No payment plans. The whole country's ledger, closed in a single stroke, like a book slammed shut on someone's fingers.

And beneath that, in the Ledger-Kings' own hand, the part that made me sit down:

*All confiscated devotional materials to be destroyed by fire. Witnessed. Certified. Immediately.*

Burn the prayer books.

I want to be clear about what those books were, because I know what they'll say in the histories, if there are histories. They were contraband. That was the legal term, and I used it myself often enough: *confiscated devotional materials*. What they actually were was paper and ink and other people's hope, bound in leather, seized from homes and stalls and schoolrooms over forty years of quiet enforcement. We kept them in the cellar of the audit-house because burning them had never been ordered before — only confiscation. You take a woman's prayer book and she weeps, but she pays her tithe. You burn it, and something else happens. Something the manuals did not diagram.

There were nine hundred and forty-one of them down there. I knew the number because I had counted them myself, twice a year, inventory being inventory. Nine hundred and forty-one books that people had knelt over, hidden under floorboards, taught to children in whispers. And now a decree with a seal on it wanted them ash by nightfall.

Arithmetic does not flinch. But that morning, standing over the decree, I noticed my own hands were not entirely steady, and I found this professionally embarrassing.

* * *

By mid-morning the valley had heard. Of course it had. A total audit is not a secret; it is a bell rung over every rooftop at once. I walked from the audit-house to the granary office to post the schedule of foreclosures, and I saw the valley doing its arithmetic too — every face I passed working the same sum: *what do I owe, what do I have, what will they take.*

A baker named Ollen Vetch stood in his doorway with flour still on his forearms, staring at nothing. I had audited him personally two years ago. Modest debt, honestly incurred, paid on time like a metronome. He looked at me as I passed and did not look away, which was new. People used to look away from me. It was a courtesy, or a surrender, or both.

"Overseer," he said. "Is it true? Everything? Even debts already paid?"

"Called, not owed," I said. "The distinction is—" and then I stopped, because I heard myself. *The distinction is* what? Meaningless? Cruel? A sentence I had recited so many times it had worn smooth as a river stone, and now, suddenly, I could feel its edges again?

"The distinction is in your decree," I finished, and walked on, and hated the sound of my own boots on the cobbles.

Here is the thing nobody tells you about being an instrument: instruments are tuned. Someone tunes you, note by note, year by year, until you produce exactly the tone required and no other. For twenty-two years I believed I had chosen this work, chosen these methods, chosen this life of ledgers and seals and clean, cold justice-as-arithmetic. Standing in the market square watching a valley full of people calculate their own ruin, I understood for the first time that I had been tuned, and that whatever part of me was doing the noticing had never once been consulted.

* * *

The order came down formally at noon: the burning would happen at dusk in the audit-yard, witnessed by two senior clerks, certified under my seal. I signed the certification form without reading it, which I had never done in twenty-two years. Reading was my religion, insofar as I had one. Read everything. Verify everything. Trust nothing but the document in front of you.

I didn't read it because I knew what it said. I had written forms like it. *Received into custody for destruction: 941 items, assorted.* Assorted. As if they were buttons.

In the afternoon I went down to the cellar alone. Officially I was conducting a final inventory. Unofficially I don't know what I was doing, and I've decided not to examine it too closely even now.

Nine hundred and forty-one books on pine shelving in the damp dark. Some of them were beautiful — illuminated, hand-sewn, the work of lifetimes. Most were cheap things, homemade, copied out by candlelight by people who could barely spell. One was a child's copybook, the letters wobbling, a hymn written out eleven times as punishment or devotion or both, and on the twelfth attempt the letters stopped wobbling because the child had learned them, and I stood there holding a dead person's homework and felt something shift in my chest that I did not have a ledger-column for.

I opened another at random. A prayer, handwritten, the ink browned with age:

*Noor, who hears the unpaid, remember us when we cannot pay.*

I put it back on the shelf very carefully, the way you put down something sleeping.

"Noor." I said the name aloud in an empty cellar, testing it, and it tasted strange in my mouth — not foreign, exactly. Familiar in the way a word is familiar when you knew it long ago and were made to forget it. Like the smell of a house you lived in before you can remember living anywhere.

And underneath the word, faint as a watermark, something hummed.

I want to be precise here, because precision is all I have left. I did not hear singing. I am a senior officer of the Ledger-Kings and I did not hear singing in a contraband cellar. What happened was: the silence in that room acquired a texture. A grain. The way silence has a texture when somewhere, far off, someone is singing and the walls are carrying it to you whether they mean to or not.

I climbed the stairs faster than dignity allows.

* * *

Dusk came gray and early, the way it does this time of year, and the yard filled with clerks and braziers and the two witnesses, and the carts began coming up from the cellar loaded with nine hundred and forty-one books, and I stood at the head of it all with my seal in my pocket like a stone.

I had performed executions of property before. Seized goods, counterfeit weights, a printing press once, in my younger years, that had been turning out pamphlets about interest rates. You burn the thing, you certify the burning, you go home and eat dinner. The thing burns. Things are good at burning. It is people who make it difficult, and there were no people in the yard, only clerks, only colleagues, only men and women as well-tuned as I was.

Except.

Except that the first armful of books hit the flames and the fire made a sound I had never heard fire make.

Not crackling. Not roaring. Singing.

I have tried since that evening to describe it in terms that would survive an inquiry, and I cannot, so I will simply tell you what happened. The pages caught, and the smoke rose, and where the smoke went, sound followed — voices, dozens of them, hundreds, layered like strata, like sediment, like every voice that had ever read those prayers aloud was stored in the paper and the heat was opening it all at once. A hymn. An old hymn, in an old tongue, rising off the pyre in columns of black smoke against the gray sky.

The clerks stopped working. Braziers guttered. Somewhere beyond the yard wall, a dog began to howl, and then another, and then — this is the part I keep returning to — people. Voices beyond the wall, joining. Badly. Out of tune. From memory they couldn't possibly have had, singing words most of them had never been allowed to learn.

Half the valley heard it. I learned that later. Half the valley stood in doorways and fields and stairwells and heard a fire sing, and instead of running from it, they sang along.

And I stood in my own yard, in front of my own clerks, wearing my own seal, and I did not stop it.

Understand what that means. I could have ordered the pyre doused. I could have cleared the yard, sealed the cellar, postponed the certification pending review — I had the authority, I had used such delays a hundred times as administrative leverage. One word from me and the singing stops, the books drown, the decree proceeds tomorrow under tighter conditions.

I said nothing. I stood there with my mouth shut and let the fire sing, and some vast and ancient part of me that predates my tuning stood up inside my chest and *listened*, and I recognized — God help me, I recognized the melody. Not from any archive. Not from any manual. From a kitchen. From a woman's voice. From before.

My mother used to hum it. I had thought it was just a tune she liked.

* * *

Afterward — after the pyre burned down to certified ash, after the witnesses signed, after I pressed my seal into the wax with a hand that shook only slightly, complicity documented in triplicate — I dismissed the clerks early and sat alone in my office with the lamp turned low.

Nausea is an imprecise word for it. Nausea is the body rejecting what the stomach has been given. What I felt was the soul's version: a full and violent rejection of what I had spent twenty-two years swallowing. Every foreclosure. Every seizure. Every family I had unmade with a signature and called it justice because the numbers balanced. Every time I had watched a debtor's face go through the stages — disbelief, bargaining, the flat gray acceptance — and noted it in my report as *subject compliant.*

Subject. Compliant. I had written human beings in the language of livestock for two decades and called it professionalism.

And beneath the nausea, worse than the nausea, the melody kept playing. My mother's kitchen. Her humming. Her hands in flour or dishwater, keeping time. She died owing nothing — I checked, years ago, quietly, the one sentimental audit of my career — and I had assumed that meant she was honest. Now I wondered if it meant something else entirely. If she had known things. Sung things. If the tune she gave me, without knowing she was giving it, was contraband all along, smuggled into the one place no auditor ever thinks to search: a child's memory.

The knock came at midnight. A courier, again, no dismounting, no tea. New orders, sealed with the high seal, the one reserved for decrees that end careers or countries.

I broke the wax expecting more of the same — escalate, seize, suppress. Instead I read:

*Overseer Brannoc: reports indicate unauthorized congregations forming across the valley. Singers identified and listed herein. You are instructed to proceed tonight with seizure of all named individuals. Failure to execute will itself constitute default.*

Default. They had found a way to phrase disobedience as debt. Of course they had. It was the only language they spoke.

The list was long. Names in the Ledger-Kings' cramped clerk-hand, addresses, assessed values. I read down it with my lamp pulled close, and halfway down, in the middle column, between a weaver from Lowmark and a stonemason from the quarry road, I found a name that stopped my breath:

*Sefa, jailer, valley lockhouse. Debt: inherited. Status: noncompliant.*

Sefa. I knew Sefa. Everyone who worked enforcement knew Sefa — she held the keys to the cells where we put the worst debtors, and she did it without cruelty, which in our trade was rarer than honesty. I had shared a table with her at two audits' end-of-year reconciliations. She drank her tea bitter and her ale sweet and she once told me, mildly, over the rim of a cup, that she kept the keys because somebody decent ought to.

Somebody decent ought to. And the Ledger-Kings wanted her seized tonight, along with a hundred others, and they wanted *me* to do it, and if I refused, the machinery would simply roll over me and send someone less decent, someone still fully tuned, someone whose hands wouldn't shake.

I sat with the list for a long time. Outside, the valley lay dark under a sky still faintly stained with the smell of burnt prayer, and if I listened — really listened, past the ringing in my ears, past twenty-two years of tuning — I could almost believe the silence out there had a texture to it. A grain. A hymn waiting in it like water under ice.

Arithmetic does not flinch. I had built my whole life on that foundation, and tonight, holding a list of names and a seal gone warm in my pocket, I finally asked the question I should have asked twenty-two years ago:

Flinching from *what*?

Because numbers don't flinch, true. But numbers also don't choose. And somewhere between the counting and the counting-out, I had mistaken the instrument for the hand, and the hand for the law, and the law for the right — and the fire in my own yard had sung the difference to me in my mother's voice.

I took a fresh sheet of paper. I did not write a refusal; refusals get filed, and filed things get found. Instead I wrote what I had written ten thousand times: a schedule. Routes. Timings. Assignments.

Then I altered every one of them.

Wrong streets. Reversed orders. Seizure points that placed my teams precisely nowhere near the names on the list. It would buy hours, maybe a day, before anyone cross-checked. A day is not salvation. But I had watched enough ledgers in my life to know how fortunes begin: not with a fortune, but with a single entry, entered wrongly, on purpose, by a man who has finally decided which side of the column he stands on.

I blotted the falsified schedule, sealed it, and set it out for the morning couriers. Then I took the original list of singers, folded it small, and put it inside my coat, against my chest, where a man keeps the things he is no longer willing to lose.

Somewhere out in that dark valley, the singers were already running, or hiding, or — the thought arrived unbidden and stayed — gathering. And somewhere beneath all of it, under the tithe barn, under everything, something old was waiting to find out whose side the instruments would take.

For the first time in twenty-two years, Overseer Brannoc intended to find out first.
