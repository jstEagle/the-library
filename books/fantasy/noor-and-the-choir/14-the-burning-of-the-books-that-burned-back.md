## 14. The Burning of the Books That Burned Back

They built the pyre in the temple square at dawn, which was a courtesy, I suppose. The Ledger-Kings wanted witnesses. A burning you conduct in secret is an apology; a burning you conduct at dawn is a lesson.

I stood in the crowd with my hood up and Sefa's shoulder pressed against mine, and I watched men in auditor grey stack forty years of confiscated prayer books into a pyramid as neat as any ledger column. They'd soaked the lower layers in lamp oil. Someone had thought about this. Someone had sat at a desk with a cup of tea and calculated exactly how much oil it takes to make sure a book stays dead.

"You're shaking," Sefa said.

"I'm cold."

"It's summer."

"Then I'm shaking for other reasons," I said, and she had the grace not to laugh.

Here is what nobody in that square knew except me, and Noor, and — I hoped — the books themselves: every one of those volumes had passed through my hands. Not all of them; there were hundreds over the years, carted into the scriptorium by auditors with inventory lists. But I had transcribed the confessions of most of them. That was part of the foreclosure process, back when foreclosures were small and personal: a family's prayer book seized, its hymns copied out so the temple could prove what contraband it contained, then the original destroyed or locked away. I used to think copying them was a kind of mercy. Keeping the words alive somewhere, even in a government archive.

Now I understood I had been building an index. And indexes, Noor had told me three nights ago in the voice of a dead stonemason, are how you find everything worth burning.

"They know," I whispered to Sefa. "Brannoc knows. This isn't just punishment. He's cutting the choir's throat."

"Then we'd better hope throats aren't all they have," she said.

* * *

Let me tell you what a prayer book sounds like when it burns, because no one had told me, and I have decided that this is the kind of information people should carry.

It does not sound like paper. Paper pops and sighs and is done. These books had been prayed out of for generations — spines cracked from being held open during births, margins soft where thumbs had worried at promises — and when the flame reached them, they did not pop.

They harmonized.

The first note came from somewhere low in the pyre, a bass hum like a door swinging on oiled hinges, and I felt it in my teeth before I heard it properly. Then a second voice joined it, higher, wavering — a woman's voice, or something that remembered being one — and the two notes found each other the way notes do when someone has sung them together ten thousand times. By the time the fire climbed past the third layer of books, the whole pyramid was singing.

Not one song. All of them. Every hymn ever confiscated in this valley, layered and tangled and rising through each other like water finding its level, and the terrible beautiful thing — the thing I will hear until I die — was that none of it was chaos. It was a choir that had never rehearsed together discovering, mid-performance, that it already knew the piece.

The auditors stopped feeding the fire. One of them dropped his oil can. Another made a gesture I recognized from childhood, quick and involuntary, the sign old women made against ill luck before the temple taught everyone that ill luck was just unmanaged risk.

And the valley heard.

I don't mean the square. I mean the valley. Sound doesn't travel that far — I know this, I've copied enough treatises on acoustics to bore a room full of scholars — but this wasn't traveling. It was arriving. The song came up out of the ground in the terraced vineyards, out of the well-water in the lower town, out of the stones of the border road itself, because the whole country was one instrument and someone had finally touched it. Later, people would describe hearing it in their cellars, in their chimneys, in the fillings of their own teeth. A baker told me the loaves rose differently that morning, lighter, as if the yeast had been listening too.

In the square, the crowd did the thing crowds do, which is nothing, magnificently. Two thousand people standing absolutely still while the impossible happened directly overhead. You could smell the fear coming off them like rain off hot stone.

Then a child started to sing along.

She couldn't have been more than six. She stood near the front with her mother clutching her wrist, and she didn't know the words — how could she, no one under fifty knew the words — but she knew the shape of the melody the way you know the shape of your own name, and she opened her mouth and put her small clear voice straight into the gap where the next line should go.

And the gap filled.

That was Noor. I felt it happen, felt the god pour itself into the space the child left open, and suddenly the melody had words again, old words, in the old tongue that isn't written anywhere anymore except in the places books keep their secrets:

*We were not forgotten. We were taken. There is a difference, and the difference has a price, and the price is coming due.*

The mother snatched the child up and ran. Half the crowd flinched toward flight.

But the other half — and this is the part Brannoc did not plan for, could not have planned for, because his arithmetic had no column for it — the other half took a step forward.

An old man near me began to sing. Badly. Off-key, half the words wrong, substituting market-day nonsense for verses he'd lost sixty years ago. It didn't matter. The pyre caught his errors and corrected them gently, the way you'd steady a child learning to walk, and folded his voice into the whole. A woman in a dyer's apron sang next. Then a pair of brothers who worked the quarry. Then more, faster, a contagion of singing, and I stood there with Sefa's grip crushing my arm and realized my mouth was open too, that I was singing a hymn I had only ever read silently, transcribed in a forbidden hand, and that my voice — my thin, untrained, debt-bound voice — was being carried by the fire like a leaf on a river.

Terror, if you feed it enough witnesses, changes species. It becomes defiance. Nobody decides this. It happens to you, the way frost happens to a window.

* * *

Brannoc came out onto the temple steps while the singing was still spreading.

I want to be fair to him here, because fairness is a discipline I learned from copying ledgers, where every figure gets its line even the ones you hate. He did not look like a man enjoying himself. He looked like a man watching his own house burn and knowing he'd lit the match for reasons that had seemed sound at his desk. His hand rested on the balustrade. His auditors were looking at him. The crowd was looking at him. The fire was looking at him, if fire can be said to look, and I believe it can, because that fire had opinions.

"Disperse," he said, and his voice carried the way trained voices carry, flat and official. "This assembly is unlawful. Disperse to your registered places of residence."

Nobody dispersed. The song rolled over his order like water over a rock, wearing it down without even noticing it was there.

He tried again. "Every person remaining in this square after the count of one hundred will be assessed a disturbance fine of—"

A woman laughed.

It was the dyer, I think, or someone dressed like her, and it was not a nice laugh. It was the laugh of a person who has just realized the emperor's arithmetic is naked. "Assessed," she called up at him. "By whom? With what? You going to write it in the ledger, Overseer? Write it loud, then. Write it where the fire can read it."

And the fire — I swear this on whatever's left of my credibility — the fire swelled on the word *ledger*, a great orange breath, and the singing surged, and somewhere in the depths of the pyre a voice that sounded disturbingly like my mother sang a line I hadn't heard since I was seven years old, the closing verse of the Lamentation of the Unpaid, the one that goes:

*Count us, count us, count us true —*
*we were counted once, and we counted you.*

Brannoc went very still. I watched him from thirty feet away and I saw something happen in his face that I would spend the next weeks trying to understand: not fear, exactly. Recognition. As if the song had reached into him and turned a page he didn't know was there.

He withdrew. Ordered, I later learned, the square sealed rather than cleared — a retreat dressed as tactics. But he withdrew, and the singing did not stop when he left, and that was the moment I understood we had crossed some line that doesn't appear on any map, including the maps that refuse to show our country at all.

* * *

The pyre burned for three days.

Not because there was that much fuel. Because it refused to go out. On the second day the temple sent men with buckets and they came back damp and shaken, reporting that the water steamed into shapes before it landed. On the third day the fire simply settled down to embers, patient as a banked hearth, and stayed there, glowing, in the middle of the square, and no one dared sweep it, and after a while no one wanted to. People started leaving offerings around it. Bread, mostly. Flowers. Once, memorably, a copy of a tax assessment, which burned with what I can only describe as enthusiasm.

Sefa and I watched it from the jailer's quarters above the old lockup, eating bread that tasted of woodsmoke.

"It's still singing," she said. "Quietly. Can you hear it?"

I could. Under the crackle, under the wind, a thread of melody persisted, the way a bell keeps humming after the strike. "Noor's in there. What's left of the books is holding the door open."

"How many voices does your god have left?"

"Noor says it's not about how many." I tore the bread, gave her the bigger half without thinking about it, which tells you something about how far we'd come. "It says a choir isn't a number. It's a decision. Enough people deciding to sing the same direction."

"That's not theology," Sefa said. "That's a riot with better manners."

"Sefa. You were a jailer for eleven years. Everything you believe is a riot with better manners."

She smiled at that, briefly, and then the smile went away, because we both heard it at the same time: bells. Temple bells, but not the hour-chime pattern. The alarm sequence. Three fast, one slow, three fast — the signal reserved, per the emergency protocols I had once been paid four coppers an hour to transcribe, for *total audit conditions*.

Down in the square, the embers flared.

Across the valley, doors opened and people stepped out into the streets to look at the sky, where riders were already moving, carrying sealed orders to every ledger-house: all debts called at once, all accounts frozen, all movement between districts prohibited. The valley locking its own jaw shut.

And beneath the tithe barn, I felt — not heard, felt, the way you feel weather coming in a scar — the dragon shift in its sleep, one vast slow heartbeat answering the alarm like a second set of bells.

Noor's voice arrived in my ear, borrowed from the baker's daughter, from six miles away, and it was not gentle anymore.

"Tamsin. They are going to try to starve the fire before it spreads. And the first place they will look for the memory we carry — the last verse, the name — is the scriptorium. Your records. Your ledger-line." A pause, and in the pause, almost tenderly: "Run."

I looked at Sefa. She was already pulling the key ring off its hook.

"Well," I said. "At least I finally have somewhere to be."
