## 15. What Memory Costs

We hid in the root cellar of a house that had been empty so long the ivy had taken out a lease. Sefa said it belonged to a family foreclosed on two winters back, and that she'd locked the door herself, and that if anyone came asking, we were to say the house was condemned.

"Everything in this valley is condemned," I said, settling onto a crate of turnips gone soft as grief. "It just hasn't been audited yet."

Noor laughed — three voices at once, an old man's chuckle, a girl's snort, and something vast underneath both, like a bell heard through a mountain. The choir had grown since the burning of the prayer books. Half the valley had heard them sing from the pyre, and every ear that heard became another throat Noor could borrow. They were louder now. Richer. And heavier, too, the way a full purse is heavier than an empty one, though I'd never owned enough coin to know that firsthand.

I knew debts. I could speak to their weight with authority.

Sefa kept watch at the top of the stairs with her ring of keys and her jailer's habit of counting things — footsteps, heartbeats, the seconds between distant hoofbeats on the road above. She counted because counting calmed her, she told me once, and because in her old life counting was how she kept people safe: so many prisoners, so many meals, so many breaths until morning. I didn't ask what it cost her to have been good at that job. Some questions you save for the fire you'll build later, when there's time to sit beside it.

"Tamsin," Noor said, and now they used my mother's voice, which they did when they wanted me to actually listen instead of merely hearing. It should have been cruel. It wasn't. It was the closest thing to tenderness a god made of borrowed voices could manage. "You've been copying ledgers since you were nine years old. You can read a contract faster than Brannoc's own auditors. So read this one."

"There's no contract here. There's a turnip."

"Look again."

The cellar darkened — not dimmed, *deepened*, the way water gets darker over depth — and the walls of it changed. The damp stone took on lines, columns, clauses. The whole cellar became a page, and I was standing on it, small as a comma.

I am a scribe. I have spent my life making marks that other people must obey. But I had never stood inside a document before, never felt a contract the way a debt feels it: from below, from within, as architecture.

At the top of the page, in the high formal hand they taught only to temple archivists — a hand I recognized because I'd spent four years forging it under Overseer Vell's whip-counted quotas — was written:

**THE ACCORD OF FOUNDING, REVISED AND SEALED**

And beneath it, parties to the agreement:

*The Founders of the Valley, being of sound mind and desperate circumstance,* and *the Gods of the Country, being numerous.*

I read the first clause twice before my eyes would accept it.

*In consideration of protection absolute — that no magic shall cross our borders, that no spell shall find us, that no dragon-fire, no curse, no wonder of the outer world shall touch these lands or the generations thereof — the undersigned gods do hereby sell, assign, and forever relinquish all rights to their own remembrance.*

"They sold themselves," Sefa said from the stairs. She'd come down without my noticing; even Noor's page couldn't hold a jailer who'd decided to move. Her voice was flat in the way I was learning meant fury banked like a stove overnight. "They sold *themselves*, Tamsin. To be forgotten. That was the price of the valley's safety."

"Not sold." Noor corrected, gently, in a voice I didn't recognize — someone's grandmother, maybe, someone dead and fond. "*Assigned*. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole wound. Sold means we were goods. Assigned means we agreed. We signed. Every god of this country put our names to paper and let the founders carry that paper into the world, and the founders traded it — clause by clause, name by name — to buy the border. Do you understand what a border made of forgetting costs? It costs remembering. Someone has to pay in memory, Tamsin. Magic always takes its price in the thing most precious to the payer."

The page turned itself beneath my feet. More clauses. I read them because reading was the one thing I had ever been good at, and because stopping would have meant feeling.

*Clause Seven: In exchange for the remembrance of the god Ashkaval, lord of thresholds, the border shall repel all enchantment of passage.*

*Clause Eleven: In exchange for the remembrance of the Nine Sisters of the Deep Water, the valley's wells shall never fail nor poison.*

*Clause Fourteen: In exchange for the remembrance of Noor, who is many voices, the valley shall be hidden from every map, chart, ledger, and index of nations, and shall not be found by any who seek it.*

My stomach dropped through the floor of the page and kept going.

"That's why the maps refuse you," I whispered. "That's why the country doesn't exist. You're the hiding. Your name bought the invisibility."

"My name bought the *safety*," Noor said. "Hiding was how safety was priced. And here is the clause your temple has never copied, because it appears in no book the Ledger-Kings permit—" the page turned one last time, and the final clause was written in a hand I knew better than any archivist's. A scribe's hand. My mother's hand.

*Final Clause: The Accord holds while the fire that sealed it holds. When the last dragon's flame gutters, all clauses revert. All protections end. All memories return — including the memory of what was given up, and by whom, and why. Let the country remember itself or perish unremembered entire.*

There it was. The whole shape of it. Not a curse. Not an accident. A *contract*, with a maturity date, and the date was now.

I sat down on the turnip crate because my legs had resigned without consulting me.

"You knew," I said. "All along. From the first night your voices answered me in ink, you knew the fire was failing and the contract was coming due, and you needed a carrier, and you picked the scribe with the biggest debt and the least to lose."

"I picked the scribe who *answered back*," Noor said. "That's different. That's everything different. But yes. I knew."

"Why hide it?"

"Because truth delivered too early is just weight with nowhere to go." They paused, and when they spoke again it was in many voices at once, the full choir, and the cellar shook faintly with it, dust sifting down between us like the first snow of a long winter. "You want to know the worst of it, Tamsin? Ask me. Ask the question that's been sitting in your chest since the pyre. I can hear it. It rhymes."

So I asked it. My voice came out small and mean and human, and I'm not ashamed of it, because shame is a luxury the indebted can't afford.

"If they gave up their memories willingly — if the gods *signed* — then who do I hate? Because I've built my whole life on hating the Ledger-Kings, and if the founders and the gods made this bargain together, then my mother didn't vanish through a broken border. She walked into a trap the *victims* built. Everyone I've been angry at was either a buyer or a seller, and nobody was—"

"Nobody was innocent," Noor finished. "Yes. That's the cost of memory, Tamsin. Not the forgetting. The *returning*. When a country remembers itself, it remembers everything, including its own signature on its own ruin. The founders weren't villains. They were terrified people in a burning age who bought their grandchildren twenty generations of peace and paid with the gods who loved them. And the gods weren't martyrs. We were proud, and frightened, and we believed — truly believed — that being forgotten was a small price, because we assumed someone would remember us anyway. Love assumes that. Love always assumes it will survive its own arrangements." A pause, and then, quieter, in my mother's voice: "It doesn't. Not unless someone carries it across."

"And the Ledger-Kings?" I asked. "Where do they fit in the sacred bargain? Did they sign too?"

"No. They're what grows in the space where a promise goes unkept. The founders promised the gods would be remembered *eventually* — there's a reversion clause, a day of return, written in the margins in faded ink. The Ledger-Kings are the ones who found the margin and burned it. They didn't make the prison, Tamsin. They discovered it, and they became its wardens, and they charged rent. Debt is what fills a vacuum where forgiveness was supposed to go. Your anger at them isn't wrong. It's just aimed at the symptom. The disease is older. The disease is the bargain itself — and bargains, unlike kings, can be renegotiated."

I sat with that a long time. Above us, Sefa counted hoofbeats — six, then silence — and the turnips exhaled their slow rot, and somewhere far away, so far it might have been imagination, a dragon breathed.

Here is what I understood, sitting in a condemned cellar on a crate of softening vegetables, holding a god's confession in my hands like a page I'd finally been allowed to copy:

I had spent my life believing I lived inside a story about thieves. Thieves above, thieves below, everyone skimming, everyone stealing, and me the honest fool keeping the true accounts. But that wasn't the story. The story was about *debtors*. Everyone in this valley, from the founders to the gods to the Ledger-Kings to me — everyone owed something to everyone, and the interest had compounded past any hope of payment, and the only way out was the one clause nobody had ever dared enforce.

The Final Clause. Revert everything. Remember everything. Feed the fire or perish unremembered entire.

"The door," I said slowly. "The door out of a country that isn't there. It's not an escape route, is it. It's a *collection notice*. The country has to deliver its own memory beyond its own borders — the one place the Accord says nothing can go — or default entirely."

"Now you're reading like a scribe," Noor said, and there was pride in it, in a dozen borrowed voices, and pride from a god is a heavy thing to receive. "The founders wrote the Accord assuming no one would ever be mad enough to carry the whole memory out. Memory is heavy, Tamsin. It's the heaviest cargo there is. Most people can't carry their own."

"I've carried my mother's for fifteen years."

"Yes," Noor said softly. "Which is why I chose you. Which is why I lied to you by half, and I will spend whatever eternity I have left apologizing for the half."

"Don't apologize yet." I stood up. My legs had reconsidered their resignation; anger does that, real anger, the kind that has finally found its correct address. "Apologize after. Because if the enemy is the bargain, then bargains can be broken, and I want to know — Noor, who is many voices, who signed away your own remembrance — *do you want it broken?*"

The cellar went very quiet. Even the turnips seemed to listen.

"We have wanted one thing for twenty generations," the choir said, all of them, the dead singers and the strangers and my mother and a girl who snorted when she laughed and a bell heard through a mountain. "We want to be remembered. Not worshipped. *Remembered.* The way you remember rain. The way you remember your own name. And we will burn the Accord ourselves, clause by clause, if it means this country gets to say it once more with feeling."

Above us, Sefa stopped counting.

"Tamsin," she called down, and her voice had gone strange — not afraid, exactly. Awed, maybe, or appalled. "Tamsin, you need to come up here. Now."

I climbed the stairs two at a time, Noor humming at my shoulder like a second heartbeat, and looked out through the gap in the boarded window where Sefa stood rigid.

Down the valley road, moving fast, torches bobbing like a procession of angry stars, came the Ledger-Kings' riders. Dozens of them. And at their head, on a white horse that seemed embarrassed by the company, rode Overseer Brannoc — and he wasn't carrying arrest warrants.

He was carrying a horn. The kind used for one announcement only, in the whole history of the valley's law.

"Total audit," Sefa breathed. "He's calling total audit. Every debt, called at once. They're going to lock the valley down before the fire fails — before anyone can run, or sing, or—" she swallowed. "Tamsin. They're going to burn the prayer books. All of them. Tonight."

Behind us, in the dark of the cellar, the pages of the Accord turned one final time, and Noor's choir went silent — not absent, but listening, the way a country listens when it hears its own name spoken for the first time in twenty generations.

The march on the tithe barn would have to happen tonight.
