## 16. March of the Unpaid

I have held keys to every cell in the Undercroft for eleven years, and I never once thought about what it means to hold a key until the morning I used one to open a door that wasn't mine to lock in the first place.

That's the thing about being a jailer. You tell yourself you're just part of the machinery. The debt goes unpaid, the law says lock them up, and you lock them up. You don't ask whether the law is right any more than the lock asks whether the door should be shut. A key doesn't have opinions. I told myself that for eleven years, and then a woman named Tamsin Thackeray walked into my gaol with a god humming in her coat pocket, and suddenly the key had opinions, and so did I.

* * *

We gathered at the well-square before dawn, which was Brannoc's first mistake, if you can call a man like Brannoc capable of mistakes. He expected us scattered. Desperate people are scattered by nature — that's what desperation is for, from the Ledger-Kings' point of view. A debtor alone will bargain. A debtor alone will sign anything. But two hundred debtors standing shoulder to shoulder in the grey light with nothing left to repossess except their breath?

Two hundred debtors are a congregation.

I stood at the front because nobody else would, and because guilt makes a fine kind of courage when there's nothing else in the pantry. Behind me: the weaver Osla, whose loom had been seized over four marks and who still wore her spindle at her belt like a knife she'd decided not to use yet. The mason Dree and his three apprentices, whose hands were worth more than everything they owed. Old Pellan, who'd spent six years in my cells for a debt his dead father signed, and who looked at me that morning with an expression I couldn't read and didn't deserve to.

And the singers. That was the strangest part. They weren't debtors, most of them. They were bakers and tanners and a fishwife named Cora who'd heard Noor's fragments in her smokehouse and come out singing. Half of them had never met each other before that morning. But when they opened their mouths, the pieces fit.

That's how I understood what Noor actually was. Not a voice. A harmony waiting for enough throats.

* * *

Tamsin found me at the well. They looked like they hadn't slept, which was fair, since neither of us had. There was ink on their fingers still — temple ink, the black kind they use for debts — and they kept rubbing at it like it might come off with friction and remorse.

"The tithe barn," Tamsin said. "You know the way in."

"I know every way in and out of every building the Ledger-Kings own," I said. "Eleven years. It's the only useful thing about me."

"That's not true."

"It's mostly true. Let me keep it."

They almost smiled. Then they didn't, because down the square, the auditors came.

There were eight of them, grey coats, grey faces, carrying their seal-rods the way soldiers carry pikes. And behind them — this was the part that made my stomach drop — behind them came the criers, reading the proclamation. Total audit. Every debt called at once. Every account foreclosed. I watched a woman near the fountain hear her name read out and go white, because she'd been paying on her mother's debt for nine years and now the whole balance was due by sundown or her children went into bond-service.

"They're not trying to stop us," Tamsin said quietly. "They're trying to make us too frightened to move."

"Is it working?"

Tamsin looked at the crowd. At Osla with her spindle. At old Pellan. At Cora the fishwife, who had started humming under her breath, low and tuneless, the way you hum when you don't know you're doing it.

"No," Tamsin said. "Ask them why."

So I did. I turned around and shouted it, actually, loud enough that my voice cracked on the second word, which embarrassed me deeply and worked completely: "Why are you here? Nobody's paying you. Nobody's forgiving your debts for this. Why?"

And Cora stopped humming and answered, clear as a bell across cold water:

"Because somebody finally asked."

* * *

We moved at first light, and I'll tell you honestly: it didn't feel like a march. Marches are orderly. This was more like a river deciding where it wanted to go. We flooded down the processional road toward the tithe barn, two hundred strong by the time we passed the granary, three hundred by the time we passed the shrine of the Nine — the official gods, the ones the temple kept fed and fat and thoroughly domesticated — and somewhere in that stretch, the singing started.

It started with a child. Of course it did. A girl of maybe seven, riding her father's shoulders, picked up the fragment Cora had been humming and gave it words. I don't know where the words came from. Neither did she, I'd wager. That's the nature of remembered things — they surface like drowned coins, worn smooth, still spendable.

*The fire was lit when the world was young,*
*the fire was fed with names—*

She got that far and stopped, because she didn't know the rest. And then a tanner behind us picked it up from a different angle, a different verse, something about *morning* and *unpaid light*, and the two fragments braided together mid-air, and the crowd went quiet for one full stride, and then everyone was singing, badly, beautifully, in parts that shouldn't have harmonized and did.

The dragon heard us. I'm certain of that now, though I didn't know it then. All I knew was that as we came up the last hill before the tithe barn, the ground under my boots felt warm. Not sun-warm. Warm from underneath, like standing on a sleeping animal's flank.

Which, it turns out, is exactly what it was.

* * *

Brannoc was waiting for us at the barn doors with forty auditors and a line of writs pinned to boards, and I want to record this fairly, because whatever else he was, he wasn't a coward. He stood in front of the doors with his seal-rod grounded like a halberd and he said, in a voice pitched to carry:

"This assembly is unlawful. Disperse, and individual accounts may be renegotiated."

Renegotiated. As if our lives were a price of wool and he was feeling generous at market. Old Pellan laughed — actually laughed, a dry rusted-hinge sound — and stepped forward, and I thought, oh, no, old man, don't, but he did anyway.

"Renegotiate," Pellan said. "My father signed a bond for twelve marks in the year the river froze. I've paid back thirty-one. Your arithmetic says I owe nine more. Whose sums do you trust, Overseer? Yours? Or the ones written in a book none of us were ever allowed to read?"

Brannoc didn't answer. And here is what I saw, and I've turned it over a hundred times since: his hand tightened on the seal-rod. Not the grip of a man readying a weapon. The grip of a man holding onto something to keep steady.

He knew. Some part of him already knew the ledgers were rotten. You can't audit a country for that long without finding your own name in the falsified columns.

But knowing isn't turning. Not yet. So he raised the rod, and the auditors leveled their writ-staves, and the crowd — three hundred, four hundred now, swollen with every soul between the well-square and the barn who'd heard the singing through their floorboards — the crowd did not run.

They sang louder.

That's the sentence I want carved somewhere, if anyone ever carves anything about this day: *they sang louder.* Auditors advanced and the crowd closed ranks around the singers and the verses kept coming, fragment answering fragment, and I felt it in my teeth, in the fillings of my back molars, a vibration like the world clearing its throat.

Then the barn doors shuddered.

Not from the crowd. From inside.

* * *

I've broken up fights in my cells where two men hated each other with their whole histories, and even those sounds didn't prepare me for what came through the timbers of the tithe barn. It was a groan, deep as a landslide deciding to be patient, and beneath it a heat that made the frost on the door hinges bead and run. The auditors nearest the doors stumbled back. One of them dropped his writ-staff and I swear on my mother's grave — and my mother deserves better than to be sworn on, but needs must — I swear the staff smoked.

Tamsin pushed through to the front, and Noor was with them; you could tell because the air around them had gone strange, layered, like a room full of voices heard through a wall. And Tamsin put both palms flat against the burning-warm wood of the doors and said something in a language I didn't know, and the choir behind them answered in it, hundreds of voices mangling the old tongue together into something that was somehow still true.

The doors split down the middle.

What came out wasn't fire, not at first. It was light — a slow gold exhalation, like the last breath of something enormous that had been holding it for a very long time. And in that light I saw the shape of the dragon, emaciated, vast, its scales dull as old coin, its ribs showing like the beams of a burned house. It had been down there all along, under the tithes, under the contracts, under eleven years of my keys and locks and lawful obedience, and it was dying, and everything we'd ever paid had gone into keeping it too weak to object.

It lifted its head. It looked at the crowd. And I would stake my freedom — what's left of it — that it looked at each of us individually, the way a jailer learns to look at prisoners, and found us familiar.

The singing changed. I can't explain how. The fragments locked together, all at once, the way a lock's tumblers answer the right key, and the whole song stood up in the air complete for the first time in living memory, and the dragon breathed in.

Frost formed on the auditors' coats. The fires in the watch-lamps guttered toward the barn, pulled like water toward a drain. Everything loose leaned toward that open doorway — dust, hats, my own hair — and the dragon drank the song the way the valley had never let anything drink before.

Magic doesn't follow in this valley. Everyone knows that. Everyone's always known that.

Everyone was wrong. Magic doesn't follow here because it's been chained under the tithe barn, and the chain was singing loose.

* * *

Brannoc stood very still through all of it. I was close enough to see his face, and what was on it wasn't fear. It was recognition — the look of a man hearing a song he half-remembers from childhood, which, as it happens, is precisely what he later told us he was hearing. His mother's voice, in the crowd's hundred voices. A lullaby from before the ledger got him.

He lowered the seal-rod.

One inch. Two. An instrument of the law, lowering, while the law itself stood in the doorway of a barn shedding its chains of gold light.

And then he did the thing that saved us all hours of blood, maybe years: he reached into his coat and took out his own childhood ledger — the little debt-book every orphan of the temple wards carries — and held it up over his head where everyone could see it.

"My name," he called out, and his voice broke in the middle of it, which I will deny describing tenderly, "is in this book. Read it to me. Someone read it to me, because I have carried it since I was six years old and I have never once been allowed to check the sums!"

The crowd went quiet enough to hear the dragon breathing.

Osla took it from him. She, whose loom was seized over four marks. She opened the little book in the dragon-light, and her face changed, and she read aloud, and what she read made Brannoc sit down in the mud like a puppet with cut strings, because the numbers were forged, had always been forged, and the boy who became the Overseer's hound had been bought with a lie.

The auditors looked at their rods. They looked at their master on his knees in the dirt. They looked at the dragon.

Most of them lowered their staves. Three ran. I don't blame the runners. Courage is easier when you've practiced.

* * *

Here is where I should end the telling, and here is where I can't, because the dragon did not rise. That was the terrible grace of it. All that song, all that light, and the beast could only lift its head and look at us with eyes like banked coals, and the light was already dimming, the warmth fading off the door-timbers, the frost creeping back.

Tamsin understood first. I saw it land on them like a dropped stone.

"It's not awake," they said. "It's remembering. There's a difference, and the difference is—" They stopped. Their hand went to their chest, to the place where a person keeps their name.

Noor's choir stirred around them, all those borrowed voices, and I heard grief in the harmony, plain as a minor chord. The god knew too. Whatever bargain had been struck down in the dark of that barn, the price of it was standing right there, rubbing temple ink off fingers that would soon not belong to anyone.

The dragon exhaled once, slow, and spoke — not in words, but in the way a fire speaks, in the crack and settle of a thing becoming itself — and every singer in that crowd heard the same sentence in the marrow of their own bones:

*One name, freely given, carried beyond the border, before the fire gutters.*

Tamsin turned to me. And I have faced down drunks and knife-fighters and one genuinely memorable escaped murderer, and none of it prepared me for the look on that scribe's face, which was terror and relief in equal measure, the face of someone who has finally read to the bottom of their own contract.

"Sefa," Tamsin said. "How fast can you get me to the border stones?"
