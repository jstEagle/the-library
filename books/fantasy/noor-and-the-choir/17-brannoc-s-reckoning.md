## 17. Brannoc's Reckoning

I have fired on crowds before.

Let that sit on the page a moment, because I intend to be honest in the way that only matters at the end of things: I have done it twice, and both times the arithmetic held. Eleven debtors, one auditor injured by a thrown turnip — we docked him for the turnip, incidentally; property is property — and the valley's ledgers balanced another quarter. The Ledger-Kings teach you early that a crowd is not people. A crowd is an aggregate of outstanding balances, and aggregates can be dispersed with lead as cheaply as with patience, and lead is faster.

So when Overseer-General Vess handed me the writ — *total audit declared, all debts called, unauthorized assembly at the tithe barn to be suppressed by any means* — and when she said "by any means" while looking directly at my hands, I did what I had always done. I said yes. I said it in the flat voice we are trained into, the voice of a man reading a column of figures aloud, and I took the writ, and I went down to the yard where forty auditors stood waiting with their seals and their pistols and their faces like closed ledgers.

That was the last time saying yes was easy. After that, everything got difficult, and the difficulty began, as most of my difficulties have, with arithmetic.

* * *

The crowd at the tithe barn was larger than the writ anticipated. Writs always anticipate smaller crowds; hope inflates them. There were debtors in their gray smocks, freed prisoners still wearing jail straw in their hair, bakers with flour to the elbows, children on shoulders. And they were singing.

I want to be precise about what that singing did, because I have spent thirty years believing that nothing moves a man who has been properly trained, and I would like the record to show exactly where that belief failed me.

They were singing fragments. That's all Noor ever gives anyone — a scrap of melody, half a name, a phrase in an old tongue that tastes like your grandmother's kitchen. The baker woman sang one line over and over. A man near the front sang something else, and where the lines met they fit, the way two debts offset, and the crowd found the harmony without being taught it, which should be impossible, and was impossible, and was happening anyway.

My second-in-command, Auditor Pell, stood at my shoulder with her pistol drawn and her jaw set. "Orders, sir?"

"The orders," I said, "are clear."

They were clear. That was the problem. For thirty years I had mistaken clarity for correctness, the way you mistake a clean ledger for an honest one, and standing there with four hundred singing debtors in front of me and a writ in my hand, I understood — not gradually, not nobly, but with the sick lurch of a clerk finding a transposed digit — that I had never once checked my own account.

* * *

Here is what I remembered, standing in that yard with the singing rising like water:

When I was seven years old, my mother hummed a song while she mended my coat. She hummed it badly. She was a laundress with no ear and a great deal of lung, and the song was old, older than the temple hymns they let us sing, and she told me it came from her mother, and that her mother had learned it from a choir that used to stand where the tithe barn stands now, before the barn, before the ledgers, before.

Then the auditors came for her. Not for debt — we were poor but current, my father's accounts were kept in his own careful hand — but for the song. Unauthorized devotion, they called it. Unrecorded worship. They fined her, and the fine became a debt, and the debt became my apprenticeship to the Ledger-Kings, because that is how the machine works: it does not punish you, it *finances* you.

I rose through the ranks on the strength of my arithmetic. I foreclosed on widows with a steady hand. I burned prayer books without flinching, because burning is just accelerated depreciation. And every year on the anniversary of her seizure, my mother hummed that song in my head, badly, beautifully, and every year I filed the memory under *non-operational* and went back to work.

The crowd was singing her song now. Badly. Beautifully. Four hundred people humming my mother's bad humming, and the god inside the song knew her name, or names like hers, names the ledgers had eaten.

"Sir," Pell said again. "The writ authorizes suppression."

"I know what the writ authorizes," I said. "I wrote the template."

And then I did the thing that ruined me, and I recommend it to anyone: I opened my own ledger.

* * *

Every auditor carries a copy of their personal account — it's doctrine, *know your balance, know your leash*. Mine lived in the inner pocket of my coat, next to my heart, which the Ledger-Kings would find a sentimental placement if they ever thought about hearts at all. I had read it a hundred times. Inherited obligations, converted fines, service credits accrued against principal. I knew the number the way you know your own weight: intimately, hopelessly, without ever questioning the scale.

But I had never audited it. Auditing is for them, not for us. We are the instruments; instruments do not check their own calibration.

I stood in front of four hundred singers and I unrolled my ledger and I checked the scale.

The handwriting changed halfway through the first page. That was the first thing. My father's entries — payments, small and stubborn, made in copper and bartered labor — were in his hand, cramped and honest. Then, after the year of my mother's seizure, the entries continued in a different hand. Smoother. Official. Temple-trained. Entries I had never seen: fees assessed, penalties compounded, services rendered by the temple that no one in my family had requested. A purification surcharge. An archival fee for records concerning my mother — records I had never been permitted to see. Interest calculated at a rate that did not appear anywhere in the published schedules, a rate that existed, so far as I could determine, in exactly one place: this ledger, and presumably the ledgers of every family like mine.

I did the sums right there in the yard, standing up, with the crowd singing and Pell watching me like I'd gone mad. It took me perhaps four minutes. I am very good at sums.

My family's debt had been forged. Not exaggerated — forged. Fabricated line by line by someone with temple training and a steady hand, sometime in the year I turned eight, in order to convert a free household into an indebted one, and out of that indebted household to make an instrument. To make me.

Four hundred people watched Overseer Brannoc, terror of the debtor quarters, stand in the mud doing long division and go slowly, comprehensively white.

* * *

"Sir," Pell said, a third time, and there was something new in her voice. Not insubordination. Fear. The specific fear of a person watching a load-bearing wall develop cracks. "Sir, if you're unwell—"

"I'm entirely well," I said. "That's the horror of it. I've been well for thirty years. Read this." I held the ledger out to her. "Check my figures. You know I don't make errors."

She didn't take it. So I read it aloud instead.

I want to tell you that I chose to read it aloud for dramatic effect, for the crowd, as an act of defiance. That would be tidy and it would be false. I read it aloud because my voice was the only part of me still obeying orders, and the orders it knew were the ones drilled into every auditor from the first day: *all findings must be announced publicly before enforcement.* I had enforced against thousands of people on the strength of announced findings. Now the finding was mine, and my mouth did what it was trained to do.

"Purification surcharge, assessed without petition," I read. "Archival fee, records sealed from account holder. Interest at eleven and three-eighths percent, schedule unpublished, authorization absent." My voice carried. The singing faltered, then hushed, then stopped altogether, four hundred people leaning in to hear the law eat itself. "Signature of assessing officer—" and here I stopped, because the signature at the bottom of the forgery was one I knew. Everyone in the valley's administration knew it. It was on the founding charters. It was on Vess's own commission.

It was the mark of the first Ledger-King himself, applied to a laundress's fabricated debt fifty years after his death, which meant either the dead were signing our books or the living were signing with a dead man's seal, and both possibilities were, by the Ledger-Kings' own doctrine, capital fraud.

A woman in the front row — the baker, flour to the elbows, the one whose single line of melody had been threading through everything all morning — said, quietly, "Read the rest."

So I read the rest. Every forged line. Every phantom fee. And when I finished, I did the thing I will be remembered for, if I am remembered at all, and it took me less time to decide than it takes to initial a receipt.

I turned to my forty auditors, and I said: "Check your own ledgers. Now. Out loud if you find what I found."

* * *

You would think they would refuse. You would think forty trained instruments would stand fast while their senior officer defected in real time. But here is the thing about instruments: they are honest. It is the people who aim them who lie. Give an auditor a discrepancy and a mandate to verify, and she will verify, because verification is the deepest thing we are taught, deeper than loyalty, deeper than fear. The Ledger-Kings built us to find errors. They never imagined we would point the machinery inward.

Pell unrolled her ledger with shaking hands. Her father had been a ferryman. Her entries changed hands too, in the same smooth official script, the same phantom fees, the same dead king's signature. She read it aloud in a voice that started as procedure and ended as grief.

Auditor Marsh found his. Then Osk. Then the twins, Ferren and Ferrelle, whose family debt turned out to be a single original forgery copied twice, like a plate run off in duplicate. By the end of the hour, thirty-one of my forty auditors stood in the mud holding proof that the debts that owned them were fiction, and the other nine stood holding ledgers that were clean — genuinely, honestly clean — and looked almost disappointed, and then looked at the nine clean ledgers, and at the thirty-one forged ones, and understood what kind of lottery they'd been living in, and crossed over anyway, because a system that forges some debts and honors others is not a system. It is a dice game with the dice weighted, and we had all been playing blind.

The crowd had gone very quiet. Then the baker woman began to hum, and the humming spread, and this time I knew the song, all of it, every bad beautiful note of it, because it was mine before it was theirs.

* * *

Vess arrived at dusk with the reserve column and the writ of suppression in her fist, and found her suppression already thoroughly self-suppressed.

I will give her this: she did not bluster. She looked at the crowd, at my auditors with their open ledgers, at me standing in front of the tithe barn with my mother's song still hanging in the air, and she said, "Brannoc. Your account."

"My account is fraudulent," I said. "Assessed under the founder's seal, posthumously. I have thirty-one corroborating cases and I invite your audit."

"You invite—" Something moved behind her eyes, and I recognized it, because I had worn it for thirty years: the flicker of a person who has just been asked to audit the one book they swore never to open. She crushed it. "The writ stands. Disperse the assembly or I disperse it for you."

"With what?" I asked, and held up my hand, and in it I held the seals.

Here is the part I confess with actual pleasure. Every auditor carries a validation seal — the stamp that authenticates assessments, foreclosures, executions of writ. It is the highest instrument of the Ledger-Kings' authority, and its whole power rests on one doctrine: *the seal cannot question; the seal confirms.* What is stamped is true. That is the entire theology of the ledger in a single wax impression.

But a seal, I had realized somewhere between my mother's song and my mother's forger's signature, is a tool, and tools do not care whose hand holds them. And a validation seal pressed onto a finding of fraud makes that finding *true*, by the Ledger-Kings' own law, irrevocably, in the eyes of every court and every clerk in the valley.

Thirty-one auditors had already validated each other's findings — I had organized it while Vess rode, methodically, in the mud, witnesses countersigning, wax and stamps, the whole liturgy performed correctly for the first time in its existence. Thirty-one certified findings of systemic fraud, each one carrying the full authority of the office that issued it. Their own law. Their own wax. Their own arithmetic, turned around at last to face its makers.

"There is a writ of total audit," I told Vess, "already in effect. You declared it yourself this morning. We are merely executing it." I held out the stack of validated findings. "Your move, Overseer-General. Though I'll note the arithmetic is no longer on your side."

Behind her, the reserve column shifted, and I saw the thing I had spent my life preventing from being seen: doubt moving through ranks of trained men like a transposed figure through a column of sums, corrupting everything below it. Two of them were looking at their own coat pockets, where their own ledgers slept.

Vess looked at the crowd, at the singing, at the tithe barn beneath which — though she did not know it, though almost no one knew it — something vast and starved and patient lay sleeping with its fire siphoned into other people's contracts. She looked a long moment. Then she said, very quietly, "This changes nothing. The Kings will call in everything. Every debt, every bond, every name in every book. If the valley burns, it burns in balance."

"That," I said, "is what I'm counting on."

Because here is what none of us had said aloud yet, what the crowd seemed to know in its bones even as we discovered it line by line: the Ledger-Kings' whole power lives in the books. Burn the books, void the debts, and the machine has nothing left to enforce with. And there was a fire under this valley older than any contract, a fire that had been paying for everyone's safety while the Kings skimmed the flame, and it was waking up, and the singing was the wick.

Vess turned her horse. The reserve column followed, except for four who did not, who walked instead to where I stood and asked, in the flat procedural voices we were all raised in, whether we were accepting additional validations.

We were. We worked by lantern light into the night, thirty-five auditors and a growing crowd of debtors, checking figures, pressing wax, announcing findings. Somewhere past midnight, Pell looked up from her ledger, ink to the wrists, and said, "Sir — the original plates. The master copies. If the forgeries trace back to the founding charters, the evidence is in the archive under the Counting House."

"And the Counting House," I said, "is guarded by people exactly like us, who have not yet checked their pockets."

The crowd stirred. The baker woman smiled at me like I was a slow student finally showing promise. And far beneath our feet, faint as a banked coal breathing, I felt — or heard, or remembered — a heat begin to rise, as if something enormous had rolled over in its sleep and found, for the first time in a century, that the chains on it had been stamped invalid.

At dawn we would march on the Counting House. But that night, for the first time since I was seven years old, I slept without owing anything — and woke to horns, and smoke on the wind from the direction of the archive, and the sound, rising from four hundred throats and one enormous hidden fire, of a country beginning to remember its own name.
