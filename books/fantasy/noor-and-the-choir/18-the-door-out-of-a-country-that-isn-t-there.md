## 18. The Door Out of a Country That Isn't There

I had imagined, in the small hours when imagining was all I could afford, that a dragon waking would sound like thunder. Like the world cracking open like an egg. Something vast and simple and loud.

What it actually sounded like was a choir clearing its throat.

The dragon rose out of the ruined tithe barn on wings that had been folded for longer than my grandmother's grandmother had been alive, and the sound it made was not a roar but a hum — a low, resonant note that passed through the ground, through my boots, through my ribs, and settled somewhere behind my sternum like a tuning fork finding its pitch. Around me, the March of the Unpaid — bakers and jailers and debtors and one very confused former auditor — went silent, and then, one by one, began humming back.

I did not hum. I was too busy trying to remember how to breathe.

The dragon was emaciated. I want you to understand that, because everything else about it was so enormous that enormity threatened to become the whole story. Its wingspan swallowed the sky above the temple district. Its scales were the color of old copper, of banked coals, of the inside of a bell. But it was thin — gaunt in the way the starving are gaunt, all architecture and no flesh, the great bones of it pressing against its hide like the ribs of a house with the walls stripped for firewood. The Ledger-Kings had been siphoning it for a hundred years, and it showed. It looked like a cathedral that had been mined for its stones.

It looked, in other words, like the valley. Like all of us.

"Where," Sefa said beside me, very quietly, "did it *go*?"

"Underneath us," I said. "Apparently always underneath us. Under the tithe barn. Under the ledgers. Under everything we owed."

"That's a joke, isn't it. That's the universe making a joke."

"The universe has a very dry sense of humor and no sense of timing."

Above us, the dragon turned its head. Its eyes were furnaces banked low — not the white-hot blaze I'd expected but a deep, patient orange, the color of a fire that has been carefully, cruelly rationed for a century. It looked down at the crowd of us, at the singers and the debtors and the freed prisoners still holding their cell keys like relics, and I felt its attention pass over me the way you feel a hand pass over a candle flame — not touching, but aware of exactly how much heat you carry.

Then it turned toward the western hills, toward the border stones, and breathed.

I have no adequate words, so I will borrow the ones Noor gave me later: *it did not burn the border. It burned the lie about the border.*

The flame came out of the dragon not as fire but as light — a torrent of it, gold and white and the particular violet you see only in the last second before sunset — and it struck the line of standing stones that had repelled me so violently in chapter nine, that had thrown me back into the dirt like a dog hitting the end of a chain. The stones did not explode. They *remembered*. That's the only way I can describe it. They remembered what they had been before they were border stones, and they stopped being a wall and became, instead, a seam — a long, shimmering line in the air where something had been stitched shut and was now, thread by golden thread, being unpicked.

And through the seam, I could see —

I could see the country.

Not the valley. The *country*. The one that had been legally unmade, erased from every chart, struck from every registry until even its own children thought the valley was the whole world. It stretched out beyond the seam in every direction: rivers with names, mountains with names, roads running to cities I had never heard of because no one had ever been allowed to hear of them. And over all of it, faint as breath on glass, hung the shapes of things — spires and bridges and orchards in blossom — like a memory trying to reassemble itself out of fog.

The crowd made a sound I had never heard a crowd make. Not cheering. Something older than cheering. A sound like ten thousand people inhaling at once and forgetting, collectively, to exhale.

"There it is," Noor said. The choir of voices came from everywhere and nowhere, from the crowd and the dragon and the seam itself, dead singers and strangers and — once, briefly, unbearably — my mother. "There is what they took from you. Not your gold. Not your labor. Your *there*."

"It's not solid," I said. "It's — it's like a painting of itself."

"It is a memory of itself. Memories need a carrier, little scribe. That is the whole of the problem, and the whole of the price."

The dragon folded its wings and settled, slow as a landslide, onto the ruins of the tithe barn, and I noticed then — with the particular clarity that arrives only when something terrible has finished assembling itself in the back of your mind — that the fire in its eyes had dimmed. Not gone. But dimmed. The waking had cost it. The burning of the seam had cost it more. It was a lamp with perhaps a cup of oil left, and everyone in the valley was looking at it the way you look at the last candle in a blackout.

I walked toward it. I don't remember deciding to. Sefa started to follow and Noor — gently, in a voice that was not my mother's but was adjacent to my mother's — asked her to stay with the crowd. "This part," the choir said, "is for the scribe."

The dragon lowered its head until we were eye to eye, and up close I could smell it: smoke and old paper and something like the inside of a locked room that has been locked for a very long time.

"You're dying," I said. It seemed important to say it plainly. Dragons, I felt, deserved plain speech.

*I am spent,* it said, and its voice was the hum, was the note behind my sternum, was every hymn my mother ever sang in the dark. *I was lit once, freely, by a people who understood what fire costs. I have been milked since. There is a difference between being given and being taken, and my whole body knows the difference, and now, at the end, so do you.*

"The seam," I said. "The door. Noor said — the door opens only for someone carrying the whole memory. What does that mean? What's the whole memory?"

The dragon looked at me for a long moment. Furnace eyes, banked low. And in them I saw reflected a small, thin, ink-stained woman with her mother's cheekbones and her mother's habit of standing too close to dangerous things.

*You know,* it said. *You have known since the burning books sang. You are a scribe. Tell me what a record is.*

"A record is —" I stopped. My mouth knew the answer before my courage did. "A record is a debt of attention. You write it down so it can't be denied. So it *has* to be remembered. So someone, somewhere, has to carry it."

*And who carries the memory of this country?*

"The gods," I whispered. "The gods were the memory. That's why they were sold. That's why they were unremembered — not to make the valley safe, but to make the valley *portable*. Small enough to hide. The founders sold the memory of the gods to buy the border, and the border is the spell, and the spell is fed by the dragon, and —" I was talking fast now, the way you talk when you're running downstairs ahead of your own understanding. "And the door out opens only for someone carrying the whole memory. The whole memory is the gods. The gods are a choir of voices. Noor is a choir of voices. Noor is the memory."

*Yes,* the dragon said, with terrible gentleness. *And Noor cannot cross. Noor is the country's memory, and the country cannot carry itself out of itself. A memory needs a vessel with feet.*

The wind came through the seam from the country that wasn't there, and it smelled like rain on roads I had never walked, and I stood very still in it while the last piece of the arithmetic finished itself in my chest.

Noor would have to be poured into a person. Into one person, one vessel, one scribe with ink-stained hands and an inherited debt and a mother who had once tried to cross this same border singing. And a person is a record. A person is a name in a ledger, in a temple registry, in the great book of who-owes-what-to-whom. And you cannot pour a whole country's memory into a vessel that is already full of itself.

*The name that feeds the fire,* Noor had said, in the old tongue, in the barn beneath the tithe barn. *One true name, freely given.*

I had thought it meant the dragon's name. I had even hoped, briefly, that it meant the Ledger-Kings'.

It meant mine.

"Tamsin Thackeray," I said out loud, to the dragon, to the seam, to the faint and patient choir that was everywhere in the air now, waiting. "That's the name in the records. That's the name that would have to go."

The dragon said nothing, which was the loudest confirmation I have ever received.

Behind me, the crowd was still singing — fragments, half-remembered hymns, the baker's off-key descant, a child's clear thread of melody — and the seam was holding, and the country beyond it was a breath away, and all of it, all of it, depended on a debt-bound scribe doing the one thing no scribe is ever asked to do: striking her own name from the book.

Sefa was pushing through the crowd toward me now, and Brannoc was holding the auditors back at the far end of the field with his own seals raised like a man holding back the tide with a broom, and I had perhaps a minute, maybe two, before someone said something kind enough to make this impossible.

I looked at the door in the seam — and there it was, now that I knew to look: not a seam in the air but a door, an actual door, old wood and iron hinges, standing free in the golden light where the border stones used to be. The kind of door you'd find in a farmhouse. The kind my mother had walked through and never come back from.

*It opens for a carrier,* the dragon said. *It will not open for a name. Names are the Ledger's. You would walk through unrecorded, unwritten, unknown — and the country would walk through with you, remembered, whole, alive. That is the trade. I will not pretend it is a small one.*

"No," I said. "No, I don't suppose you will."

And the terrible thing — the thing I will carry, if I carry anything at all, into whatever I become on the other side of that door — is that I understood it. Perfectly. A scribe knows what a record costs. I had spent my whole life paying down a debt that was never mine, copying other people's names into books, and I had known, somewhere under the diligence and the quiet suffocation, that someday the ledger would come for my own page.

I just hadn't expected to be the one holding the pen.

The choir began, very softly, to sing the first line of a hymn I had copied out as contraband in a scriptorium that smelled of dust and tallow — the hymn my mother sang, the hymn that started everything. And I realized, with a clarity that felt almost like relief, that I had been walking toward this door my entire life. Every ledger line, every confiscated prayer book, every night I'd spent listening for an answer in the ink.

The door was waiting. The country was waiting. The fire had perhaps a cup of oil left.

"Tamsin," Sefa said, reaching me at last, out of breath, her keys jangling at her belt like a frightened heartbeat. "Tamsin, what did it say to you? What's the price?"

I looked at her — at the jailer who had chosen redemption late but chosen it, at the crowd of the unpaid standing in the shadow of a dying dragon and a door out of a country that isn't there — and I opened my mouth to lie to her, because I loved her, and lying to the people you love is the last courtesy of the condemned.

But the choir was in my throat now, and Noor has never once let me lie.

"It's my name," I said. "Sefa. The price is my name."
