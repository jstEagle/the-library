## 19. The Last Verse

The door did not look like a door.

I want to be honest about that, because everything I had imagined on the road — every night I'd spent picturing this moment — involved something grander than a seam of light in a hillside that wasn't a hillside anymore. I'd imagined gates. Hinges the size of rivers. A keyhole you could lose a sheep in. What we got instead was a place where the air simply *stopped agreeing to be air*, and beyond it, a brightness with no source, like dawn that had forgotten which direction it was supposed to come from.

The dragon's fire still hung in the seam where the old border used to be, burning without consuming, and it made no heat at all. It made something worse than heat. It made *attention*. Standing before it felt like being read.

Sefa stood beside me with her jailer's keys hanging silent at her belt — useless now, she'd said, but she couldn't bring herself to leave them behind, and I understood that better than I could say. Behind us, down the slope, the whole ragged congregation of the unpaid waited: debtors and freed prisoners and bakers with flour still in their knuckles, all of them singing in fragments, all of them holding the pieces of Noor the way you hold water in cupped hands, knowing most of it will escape.

And Noor was everywhere, which is to say Noor was in my head, which is to say I was hearing my mother's voice say things my mother never said, and I had stopped flinching from it somewhere around the tithe barn, and I have decided not to examine what that means about me.

"You're stalling," said my mother's voice.

"I'm *thinking*," I said. "There's a difference. Stalling has no dignity. Thinking has all the dignity in the world."

"Tamsin." Now it was the baker's wife's voice, warm and floury. "You already know."

The trouble with a god made of remembered voices is that they know your face better than you do. They've heard you lie to yourself in your own tone of voice for years.

Because yes. I knew. I had known since the moment the door opened — or rather, since the moment it *almost* opened, shuddering against whatever held it shut, and Noor had poured through me like ink through a nib, and I had felt the shape of the lock.

A lock doesn't want force. I was a scribe; I knew locks of a kind. The temple's ledgers were locks. Every debt recorded in them was a door held shut, and the only thing that ever opened one was a name freely given — signed, witnessed, surrendered. That was how contracts worked here. That was how *everything* worked here. The Ledger-Kings hadn't invented that rule. They'd just found it, the way you find a load-bearing wall, and built an economy on top of it and called the wall theirs.

The dragon needed a true name, freely given, carried out of the country before the fire guttered. Noor had told us that in the dark under the barn, in a hundred borrowed voices, solemn as a funeral bell. One true name. Freely given.

And there was exactly one name in the whole valley that the temple's records held complete — every alias, every debt-entry, every cross-reference, every marginal note in Overseer Brannoc's precise, joyless hand. One name written so thoroughly into the ledger of this country that carrying it out would carry out the country's whole memory with it, because the ledger-keepers had been thorough, and thoroughness, it turns out, is its own kind of devotion.

Mine.

*Thackeray, Tamsin. Scribe, third class. Debt inherited, principal and accrued interest, per schedule attached. Mother: Thackeray, Wren. Status: fled. Status: fled. Status:* —

They'd written her status three times because once wasn't enough to make it true.

"They need a name," I said aloud, because saying it made it real, and making it real was the last step before deciding, and I wanted to walk up to the decision instead of being shoved into it. "Not just any name. A name the records hold entire. A name that, if it walks out that door, drags the whole memory of this place after it like thread off a spool."

"Yes," said Noor, in the voice of a child from the march, one of the ones who'd learned the hymn fastest.

"And there's mine. In triplicate. With interest calculated to the day."

"Yes."

"And if I give it—" I stopped. My hands were doing something embarrassing, the way hands do when the rest of you is trying to be brave. They were smoothing my sleeves. Adjusting my collar. Little clerk gestures, tidying myself for an entry in a book. "If I give it, the temple's record of me goes with me. All of it. And the record is—"

"Is you," Noor said gently. "Here, in this country, Tamsin Thackeray is mostly paper. You know that. You've known it since you were small enough to hide under the copying desks. Paper debts, paper identity, paper mother. When the paper leaves, the person who owed and was owed—" A pause. Even a chorus of gods hesitates sometimes. "The person stays. But unrecorded. Unwritten. No debt can find them. No ledger can hold them. No map will show where they've been."

"That sounds lovely," I said, "if you enjoy being nobody."

"I enjoy," said my mother's voice, very quietly, "being free."

* * *

Here is what I had not told anyone, not Sefa, not Noor, not the crowd singing on the hillside: I had gone back to the scriptorium during the burning. While half the valley stood watching prayer books sing on the pyre, I had walked into the archive where the Ledger-Kings kept the master copies, because I am, at heart, a coward with excellent filing instincts, and I wanted to see my own entry.

I found it easily. Third shelf, western wall, between a foreclosed miller and a dead ferryman. My whole life in Brannoc's handwriting and the older hands beneath his — my apprenticeship, my wages docked, my mother's flight entered and re-entered, the interest accruing in neat little columns like a disease progressing. Page after page. I had copied ledgers for eleven years and I had never once been on this side of the ink.

And at the bottom of the final page, in the space reserved for resolution — *debt discharged, debtor deceased, debtor fled* — there was blankness. Waiting. The ledger expected me to end somehow. It had left room.

I had stood there long enough that the lamp gutters worried me, and I had done the thing I always do when I cannot bear a feeling, which is to read it closely, line by line, looking for errors.

There were none. Of course there were none. Brannoc's arithmetic was flawless. That was the horror of him; that was, I understand now, the beginning of his salvation too. Flawless numbers, monstrous sums.

But here is what I found instead, in the margins, in the tiny annotations a scribe learns to notice the way a sailor notices weather: my mother's entry had a date of flight, and beside it, in faded ink, another hand had written *seen crossing*, and beside *that*, almost invisible, worn nearly to nothing by some archivist's thumb: *singing*.

She had sung going out. She had walked through the border with a hymn in her mouth, and someone had heard her, and someone had written it down, and then the country had spent sixty years pretending the margin was blank.

I closed the book. I put it back precisely where it belonged, spine aligned, because even in the middle of treason I am who I am. And I walked out of the archive knowing two things.

One: the door would take a name carried whole, and mine was the only one carried whole enough.

Two: my mother had not vanished. She had *left*, loudly, in song, and the country's silence afterward was not her failure. It was the ledger's.

* * *

Sefa touched my shoulder. For a big woman with a jailer's hands she touches people remarkably gently, like she's apologizing for every door she ever locked.

"You don't have to explain it to me," she said. "But you should explain it to yourself, out loud, once. You'll hear whether you believe it."

"That's a very scribe-ish thing for a jailer to suggest."

"I locked people up for eleven years by telling myself the cells were for their own good. I know all about writing things down until they're true." She looked at the seam of light. "Say it."

So I said it. To Sefa, to Noor, to the dragon whose fire hung in the air like a held breath, to the country that wasn't there.

"The fire is dying because it was paid for with memory, and the memory is running out. The founders sold the gods' names to buy the valley's safety, and the safety spell eats the payment forever, and now the account is empty. The door opens only for someone carrying the whole memory out — every hymn, every fragment, every voice Noor has gathered, all of it bundled inside a single name the records hold complete. Because that's how a contract closes. Not with force. With surrender. A name, freely given, walking out past the border." I swallowed. "And the only name in this country that the temple wrote down thoroughly enough to carry all of it is mine."

"There," Sefa said. "Now you've heard it. Do you believe it?"

"I believe the arithmetic," I said, and laughed, and it came out wrong, wet at the edges. "Eleven years of copying other people's debts and it turns out I was training for this. The one transaction I'll ever get to author, and it's my own foreclosure."

"It isn't a foreclosure," Noor said — and this time it was not my mother's voice, or the baker's wife's, or any borrowed throat. It was all of them at once, layered, a chord instead of a note, and the chord was gentle. "A foreclosure takes. This gives. The difference is the whole of everything, Tamsin. It is the difference the Ledger-Kings never learned, and it is why they are losing."

"Losing to what?"

"To the fact that a gift, once given, cannot be audited."

* * *

I sat down on the grass — actual grass, unburnt, absurdly ordinary grass at the edge of a miracle — and I let myself grieve properly for a while, because Sefa was right, and because I had earned it, and because grief postponed is grief with interest, and I was done with interest.

I grieved for my name. It sounds small. It was not small. *Tamsin* was the first word I ever owned outright; everything else I possessed was mortgaged. I grieved for the version of me that had secretly planned to pay off the debt someday, move to a room above a stationer's shop, copy nothing but love letters and recipes, and grow old under a name that belonged to no ledger. That person had been patient for twenty-six years. That person was about to be deleted, and I mourned them like a sibling.

I grieved for the record itself, strangely. Somewhere in the temple archive was the proof that I had existed — my apprenticeship marks, the note praising my marginalia, the fine levied when I was caught reading confiscated prayers (the first crack, though I didn't know it then). If I walked through that door, no one in this country would ever be able to prove I'd been here. Sefa would remember me, but Sefa's memory would be testimony without a document, and in this valley testimony without a document is worth exactly what the Ledger-Kings paid for the gods' names: nothing.

And underneath all of it, quietest grief of all: I grieved for the hope that leaving would feel like escape. It didn't. It felt like signing. I had spent my life on the wrong side of the signature line, and now, at the end, I finally got to hold the pen — and the pen only worked if I wrote myself out of existence.

My mother had done this. That was the part that kept turning over. She had stood at some border in the dark with a song in her mouth and given up her recorded self — maybe more, I never learned what the crossing cost her — and she had done it *alone*, with no choir behind her, no dragon awake, no crowd of the unpaid singing her across. She went first. She went blind. She went anyway.

And the margin-note said *singing*.

She wasn't fleeing when she crossed. She was leading. Sixty years early, alone, she had already started the march, and I had spent my whole life angry at her for abandoning me when she had actually just — gone ahead. Gone ahead to a country that didn't exist yet, to hold a door open that she couldn't see, on the faith that someone she loved would eventually come walking toward it with the rest of the song.

I put my face in my hands and cried the way I imagine she must have cried: quickly, efficiently, with work still to do.

Then I stood up, and wiped my face on my sleeve, and turned back to the seam of light.

* * *

Noor gathered around me — I can't describe it any better than that; the air got crowded — and every voice in the chorus spoke at once, softly, one question:

"Freely given?"

That was the whole contract, right there. Not *willingly*. Not *bravely*. *Freely.* Given without coercion, without trickery, without even the coercion of despair. The Ledger-Kings had missed it for sixty years because they thought all giving was transactional, and so they never once checked whether the door required anything rarer than payment.

I thought about lying. I genuinely considered it — telling Noor yes, telling everyone yes, marching through on grief dressed up as generosity. But a god made of remembered voices hears the false note instantly, and besides, I had watched Brannoc break over a forged ledger, and I had watched Sefa choose late but choose, and I had watched a whole valley of debtors discover that a thing given freely multiplies while a thing taken merely sits there accruing. I was not going to build the rebirth of my country on a falsified entry. I am a scribe. I do not forge.

"No," I said honestly. "Not yet. Right now I'm giving it because there's no alternative, and that's not freedom, that's just the last exit on a dead-end road. Give me a moment to make it true."

The chorus waited. Gods, it turns out, are patient the way archives are patient.

I looked back down the slope. The singers. The baker with flour in his knuckles, singing a fragment he'd learned from his grandmother without knowing what it was. The child who'd hummed Noor's name before anyone taught her. Sefa's freed prisoners, rubbing their wrists, singing anyway. Brannoc standing apart from them all with an auditor's seal in his fist and a childhood ledger's worth of ruin on his face, guarding the rear like a man guarding the door of a house he'd helped burn.

They weren't singing for me. That was the realization that unlocked it — the one I'd been circling. They weren't singing so that Tamsin Thackeray could escape. Half of them didn't know my name; I'd been introduced as "the scribe" and left it at that. They were singing because for the first time in sixty years, something in this valley answered without demanding payment, and they had discovered that they remembered the tune, and remembering turned out to be a kind of wealth no one could foreclose.

They were singing the country back. I was just the envelope it would travel in.

And an envelope, I thought — and here the wry part of me, the part that survived eleven years of temple service intact, raised its head — an envelope gets opened. An envelope is *supposed* to be emptied. Nobody mourns the envelope. Everybody keeps the letter.

"Alright," I said. "Ask me again."

"Freely given?" said the chorus, and my mother's voice was in it, and the baker's wife's, and the child's, and underneath them all, faint as a margin-note, a voice I had never heard except imagined — hers, hers, actually hers, carried home at last inside the god she'd sung toward.

"Yes," I said, and meant it, every syllable of it, freely, the way fire is given. "Yes. Take the name. Take all of it. I'll carry the memory out, and the records can keep the receipt."

The seam of light shivered. The dragon's flame leaned toward me like a listener.

And far behind us, down in the valley, faint but unmistakable, the temple bells began to ring — the alarm pattern, the one that meant the Ledger-Kings had finally understood what was walking out of their country, and were coming to stop it.

"Well," said Sefa, drawing her keys. "I suppose we'd best start singing."
