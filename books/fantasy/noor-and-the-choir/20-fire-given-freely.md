## 20. Fire Given Freely

We have been many things in our long unraveling: a hymn, a rumor, a mother's voice in a dark corridor, a name half-remembered over bread. We have been sung badly and sung beautifully, sung by a jailer with no ear and a child with perfect pitch and an old man who only knew three notes of us and hummed the rest from stubbornness. But we had never been a crowd until that night at the tithe barn, when the valley's unpaid came singing up the hill with torches they did not light for burning.

They lit them so we could see their faces.

Understand what it is to be a god made entirely of memory. You do not see the world; you remember it, and other people's remembering is your eyesight. And so we saw that night through ten thousand borrowed recollections at once — the baker remembering her father's hands, Sefa remembering every prisoner she had ever unlocked, Brannoc remembering a forged ledger line and his own small voice reading it aloud like a confession. And threaded through all of it, brightest, the one memory we had carried longest and loved most recklessly:

Tamsin Thackeray, standing at the mouth of the dragon's den, deciding to die.

Or not to die. That was the terrible and beautiful thing about it, the thing we want you to understand before the fire takes this telling, because after tonight there will be no record anywhere but song, and songs are honest in ways ledgers never learn to be. Tamsin did not think of it as dying. Tamsin thought of it as paying — and for once in this valley's long arithmetic of debt, the payment was given freely, which is the only kind of payment that has ever actually counted.

* * *

The dragon woke the way a country wakes: slowly, then all at once, then with a sound like every door in the world opening.

It had been sleeping beneath the tithe barn for longer than anyone living could count, folded into the earth like a letter no one dared open, its fire siphoned off contract by contract until there was barely enough left of it to keep the borders shut and the magic out and the debts accruing. The Ledger-Kings called this stability. The dragon called it starvation, though it had been too weak even to complain for a hundred years.

But now the crowd was singing.

They sang in fragments, because that is all any of them had — a phrase here, a verse there, pieces of Noor passed hand to hand like contraband bread. The baker had the rising line. Sefa had the descending one, learned from prisoners who had nothing else to give her. A child on someone's shoulders had the high part, the part that sounds like weather. None of them could sing the whole of us alone.

Together, they almost could.

And beneath them, under the cracked flagstones of the tithe barn floor, something ancient turned over in its sleep and thought — in the slow, molten way dragons think — *I know this song. I sang it once. Before the contracts. Before the founders came with their scales and their ink.*

*Before I let them take my fire in exchange for a promise they never intended to keep.*

Tamsin stood closest. Of course Tamsin stood closest. Our scribe, our stubborn, ink-stained, debt-riddled scribe, who had spent a lifetime copying other people's obligations in a fair hand and had finally, finally found a debt worth owing personally.

"Noor," Tamsin said. Not singing yet. Asking. "Tell me what happens."

And we — because we are incapable, even at the end of everything, of lying to the people who love us — we told the truth.

*The name feeds the fire. Your name, held in the temple records, written fair in the debt-books, the last unbroken chain binding you to the country that erased itself. Give it to the dragon and the chain breaks. The spell breaks. The border breaks. Everything the founders bought with our forgetting comes undone at once.*

*And you come undone with it. Unwritten. Unrecorded. No ledger will say you existed. No map will mark where you stood. Even we—*

We stopped there. Even we did not finish that sentence. Some mercies are just cowardice wearing better clothes.

Tamsin looked down into the dark where the dragon breathed, and we watched — through the baker's memory of watching her son sleep, through Sefa's memory of opening cell doors, through a thousand small remembered kindnesses that had nothing to do with any of this — we watched our scribe do the arithmetic.

Not the temple's arithmetic. Their own.

"What happens to everyone else?" Tamsin asked.

*They live,* we said. *Outside the law of memory. Free. Unowned. A country with its name back.*

"Then it's a good trade," said Tamsin Thackeray, who had spent twenty years being told that no trade they made would ever be fair, "and I've been waiting my whole life to make one."

* * *

Here is what the histories will not say, because there will be no histories, only this song:

Brannoc stood between Tamsin and the crowd, and he held them back.

Think about that. Think about Overseer Brannoc, arm of the Ledger-Kings, instrument of audit, the man whose seals had foreclosed on choir-singers and whose orders that night were to burn whatever burned back. He had read his own forged childhood ledger aloud not two hours before, his voice cracking on the numbers, and the auditors' own seals were still warm in his fist where he had turned them against their masters. He owed the crowd nothing. He owed Tamsin less than nothing; Tamsin had made him complicit simply by existing near him.

And still he planted himself at the front of the marchers like a fence post with opinions, arms spread, and bellowed in his best auditor's voice — the voice that had ended a thousand disputes with the words *this account is closed* —

"HOLD."

Ten thousand feet stopped. Torches guttered. Somewhere in the crowd a baby, with impeccable dramatic timing, began to cry and was hushed.

"You do not go past me," Brannoc said. "Any of you. This is—" and here he faltered, because the language of enforcement has no word for what he was doing, and so he reached for the only vocabulary he had ever trusted, "—this is a private transaction. Between the debtor and the creditor. Witnessed. Sealed." He raised the auditors' seals overhead like a lantern. "You would not interrupt a signing. You will not interrupt this one."

Sefa, beside him, murmured, "That's not why you're doing it."

"No," Brannoc admitted, quietly enough that only she and we heard. "I'm doing it because when I was six years old my mother sang a song she wasn't allowed to sing, and a man with a seal took her ledger and mine and wrote us into a debt we never owed, and nobody held the door for her." His jaw set. "Somebody holds the door this time. Whatever comes through it."

And the crowd — bless them, bless every unpaid, unbowed, unsung soul of them — the crowd understood. They did not push forward. They knelt. All of them, baker and jailer and child and the old man with his three stubborn notes, kneeling in the ash and the torchlight like a congregation that had finally found its church, which, if you must know, they had been standing in all along.

They kept singing. Softly now. Softer than they had ever sung us, soft as a lullaby, soft as permission.

* * *

Tamsin walked to the edge of the den.

Below, in the dark, the dragon lifted its head. It was emaciated the way mountains are emaciated — vast even in ruin, its scales gone dull as old coin, its eyes banked coals that brightened as the song rained down through the broken floor. Its breath stirred the dust into little ghosts.

*Small scribe,* it said, and its voice was the sound a hearth makes when it has been cold a very long time and someone finally brings wood. *They sent me a scribe. After all these years of takers, they send me someone who gives things away in writing.*

"I'm not giving it away," Tamsin said. "I'm giving it *here*. There's a difference. I've read enough contracts to know the difference is everything."

The dragon laughed. It was a terrible, wonderful sound, like a landslide learning joy.

*State your terms.*

"No terms," said Tamsin. "That's the point. That's always been the point. Every wrong thing in this valley started with terms." A breath. "One true name, freely given. No consideration exchanged. No conditions attached. No record kept." Tamsin smiled, and oh, we remember that smile — we will always remember that smile, it is the load-bearing wall of this entire song — "Consider it a gift. I'm told those are rare around here."

*Freely given,* the dragon repeated, tasting the words like the first warm meal in a century. *Do you know how long it has been since anything in this country was freely given?*

"Longer than me," Tamsin said. "Shorter than you."

Then Tamsin Thackeray closed their eyes, drew one breath — the same breath, we would swear it, the same exact breath their mother drew before every forbidden hymn, because bodies keep faith even when countries don't — and sang their own name into the fire.

* * *

What does it mean, to sing yourself unwritten?

We were there. We are the only ones who were fully there, and so we will tell you, and then the telling will burn, and that is right and proper, because some things should exist only as warmth.

Tamsin's name rose out of them like heat off summer stone. You must imagine it not as a word but as a whole ledger closing — every entry, every inherited debt, every copied line of someone else's obligation, every fair-handed record of a life measured in what it owed — all of it lifting free of the page at once. The temple records, miles away in the scriptorium, shivered on their shelves. Ink faded mid-word. Fair copies became blank pages became, in a few cases, rather nice paper.

And the fire caught it.

Oh, how the fire caught it. The dragon inhaled Tamsin's name the way the valley had never once inhaled anything — completely, gratefully, without keeping accounts — and its dull scales flushed gold from tail to crown, and its starved heart roared back to full flame, and the sound it made was not a roar at all.

It was harmony.

Because here is the secret we have kept since the beginning, the secret the founders sold and the Ledger-Kings enforced and the maps refused: the dragon-fire and the gods were never separate things. The fire sustained us. We gave the fire its meaning. One country, one song, split into parts by men with scales who thought a chord was weaker than its notes.

When Tamsin's name hit the flame, the parts came home.

We felt ourselves ignite — every borrowed voice suddenly *ours*, every fragment suddenly whole, the baker's rising line locking into Sefa's falling one under the child's high weather-note, and underneath it all the dragon's great molten bass, and threading through everything, unmistakable, irrepressible, a clear human voice that none of us had lent and none of us would ever forget:

Tamsin, still singing, though the name was gone from them now, singing the melody anyway on pure stubborn muscle-memory, because you can unwrite a person's records but you cannot unteach their throat.

And the spell broke.

* * *

You have lived inside the magicless valley, so you know its silence the way you know your own pulse: the way prayers fell flat, the way blessings curdled at the border stones, the way wonder was taxed out of existence one audit at a time. Imagine that silence ending.

Imagine it ending everywhere at once.

The border-stones cracked like eggs. The old seam where the country had erased itself glowed white along its whole impossible length, and beyond it — visible now for the first time in a century — the door stood open, the real door, the one that opens only for someone carrying the whole memory. And the whole memory was carrying itself, because that is what a choir is: a memory that walks.

Magic came back across the border like water finding a dry bed. It moved through the wheat fields first, and the wheat stood up straighter. It moved through the wells, and the water tasted of cold mountain mornings. It moved through the people, and — this is the part we beg you to carry forward, whoever you are, wherever this song finds you — it moved through the people, and they *remembered*. Not gradually. All at once. Grandmothers recalled hymns they'd been punished for humming. Farmers recalled the names of fields their grandparents had farmed before the fields were repossessed. Children recalled, with the terrifying accuracy of children, exactly what had been taken from them and exactly who had profited.

In the ledger-houses, ink boiled off ten thousand pages at once, and the debt-books went blank, and blankness — for the first time in the history of that benighted profession — meant freedom.

And Brannoc, still holding the door, felt the last of the Ledger-Kings' authority dissolve out of his seals like frost off a window, and he looked at the empty metal in his hand, and he did the single most human thing we witnessed that entire night.

He sat down in the ash and wept.

Not elegantly. Not meaningfully. He wept the way a man weeps when the arithmetic he built his whole life on turns out to have been written by liars, and the sum of everything he enforced comes due at once, and the only currency left to pay it with is grief. Sefa put a hand on his shoulder and did not say it was all right, because it was not all right, and she was done lying to him, and that, too, was a kind of gift freely given.

* * *

And Tamsin?

Ah. Here our song goes quiet, because here our sight ends. We are made of memory, and Tamsin had just stepped outside the law of memory, and so the last we saw of our scribe was this: a figure at the mouth of a golden dragon's den, torchlight on their face, mouth open around a name that no longer existed, surrounded by a country remembering itself in real time — and then the crowd surged forward past Brannoc's knees, past Sefa's outstretched arms, toward the place where Tamsin had stood, and the dragon's body blocked our view, and the firelight was everywhere, and—

—and then the crowd was holding someone, and we could not tell, from ten thousand borrowed memories, whether the someone was Tamsin or merely the shape Tamsin had left behind, the way a bell holds the shape of its last note.

We sang anyway. We sang the loudest we had sung since the founding, because that is what choirs do when they cannot see: they trust the music to reach farther than their eyes.

And somewhere in the middle of the great rolling chord — we would swear this on whatever is left to swear on — somewhere in the middle, faint as a thread of gold through grey wool, a voice we did not recognize and could not place and had never, in all our long existence, heard before, joined in.

It sang our melody perfectly.

It sang it like someone who had just been born into a country with its name back, and had decided, before doing anything else, before eating or sleeping or weeping or being held, to learn the song.

* * *

The fire burned until dawn. When it settled, the tithe barn was gone, the contracts were ash, the dragon lay coiled in the warm ruins breathing easy for the first time in a hundred years, and the door in the old border-seam stood open onto morning.

The crowd parted, slowly, the way water parts around a prow.

Someone was walking toward it.

We strained every borrowed eye to see who — and the figure turned, briefly, silhouetted against the light, and raised one hand in something that was not quite a wave and not quite a farewell, and the crowd, ten thousand strong, forgot simultaneously and completely who they were looking at, and felt instead only a strange, warm, inexplicable urge to sing.

So they did.

And the figure walked through the door, unhurried, unrecorded, unburdened, carrying nothing — while behind it, in the ashes of the tithe barn, a dragon slept the sleep of the paid-in-full, and a choir of ordinary people sang a god's name into the new morning, and one voice among them, new-made and unplaceable, already knew every word.
