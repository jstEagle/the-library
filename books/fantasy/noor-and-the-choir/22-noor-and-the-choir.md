## 22. Noor and the Choir

The border of the reborn country is not a line anymore. That's the first thing you have to understand, if you're going to understand why I was standing on a hillside in late autumn with nothing in my pockets but a pebble I liked, watching a cart road that used to be a wall.

There is no gate. There is no seam of burned air where the dragon opened the old country like a letter. There's just a place where the grass changes color slightly — greener on one side, dustier on the other — and a signpost that some farmer put up because farmers will always put up a signpost, even when the thing it announces has stopped existing. *Welcome to Vell*, it says, in letters that were freshly painted over older letters that said something else entirely, probably *Trespassers Will Be Audited*.

I had been walking for three days. I was carrying nothing, which sounds like a hardship until you've spent your whole life carrying a debt, at which point carrying nothing feels like being carried.

"Are we doing this?" I asked the empty hillside.

The hillside did not answer, which was how I knew Noor was listening. The god only ever went quiet when it wanted me to say something myself. It had learned that trick from me, or I from it; by now I honestly couldn't tell which.

I stepped over the green line into the dusty half, and nothing happened. No repulsion, no spell snapping shut around my ribs like a ledger closing. The air on both sides of the old border tasted the same: woodsmoke and rain coming somewhere behind me. A country with its name back doesn't need to keep anyone in. That was the whole point. That was what we'd paid for.

I looked back once. Vell lay in its valley, gold and gray in the autumn light, the tithe barn's roof patched with new slate, the temple towers still standing but with their doors propped open, which they had never been in my lifetime. Somewhere down there, Sefa was arguing with a mason about whether the old debt-prison should become a school or a bathhouse, and winning, because Sefa always wins arguments she's having against her own guilt. Somewhere down there, Brannoc was teaching children arithmetic without telling them what it was for yet, and every one of them would grow up thinking numbers were just numbers, never realizing what a miracle that was.

And somewhere down there, faint as heat off a stone, a choir was singing.

I couldn't hear the words from this distance. I didn't need to. You stop needing the words after a while. That's the strange mercy of it — Noor doesn't live in the lyrics anymore. Noor lives in the fact of people singing, anywhere, about anything. A harvest song works. A lullaby works. Once, in the market at Lowmarsh, I heard a fishmonger keeping rhythm by humming while he gutted his catch, and the air above his stall shimmered the way it does when a god leans close, and he never noticed, and that was fine. Gods don't need to be noticed. They just need to be sung past, occasionally, like a neighbor you nod to.

I turned my back on Vell and walked.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being unwritten: it takes about four months to get used to.

For the first weeks, I kept reaching for myself the way you reach for a tooth that isn't there anymore. Every morning I'd wake in whatever barn or hedge I'd slept in and think, *Tamsin Thackeray, scribe, debtor of the temple scriptorium, debt of nine hundred marks inherited,* and then I'd wait for the familiar sick drop in my stomach, the arithmetic of my own worth, and it wouldn't come. There was no number where I used to be. The temple records had burned with everything else — well, not burned exactly; they'd been *sung out of existence*, which is different and worse and better — and so there was no page anywhere in the world that said who I was, which meant, under the old law, that I wasn't.

Under the new law, which mostly consisted of people shrugging and saying *well, you're obviously here*, I was whoever I said I was each morning. Some days that was exhausting. Most days it was the best thing that had ever happened to anybody.

The trouble came at borders — real ones, between actual countries, the kind with guards. Guards want papers. I had a pebble.

"You can't cross without documentation," said the first guard, at the ford crossing into Harrowmere, a country I had copied maps of for six years and never once believed I'd see.

"I'm from Vell," I said.

"Where?"

"Exactly," I said, and something in my chest did a small complicated flip, because there it was again — the vertigo and the freedom wearing the same coat. My country had been erased so thoroughly that even its resurrection hadn't reached the paperwork of its neighbors. Officially, no one had ever come from Vell, because officially there had never been a Vell to come from.

The guard squinted at me. Behind him, the queue grumbled. And then — I swear this happened — someone in the queue started humming. Just a traveler, a woman with a market basket, passing the time. But it was a hymn. An old one. One of the confiscated ones, one of the ones I had transcribed with shaking hands in the scriptorium while the ink answered back, and I would know its tune the way you know your own heartbeat.

The guard's face changed. Not dramatically. He didn't fall to his knees. He just got the look of a man hearing a song his mother sang, except his mother had never sung it, because no one's mother had sung it here — and yet he knew it, the way everyone everywhere seemed to know it now, because memory that gets carried out of a dying country doesn't stay politely inside the person carrying it. It leaks. It spreads. It becomes a tune you can't place, stuck in ten thousand heads across four borders, and none of those people know why they love it.

He waved me through. "Vell," he said, tasting the word. "Is that near the mountains?"

"It's wherever the singing is," I said, and walked into Harrowmere before he could decide whether that was philosophy or contraband.

That night, lying in a field with the pebble in my pocket, I heard the choir clearly for the first time since leaving the valley. Not in my head — I want to be precise about this, because people keep asking and I keep having to explain. Not in my head. In the *world*. In the wind through the barley, in a dog barking in triple time two farms over, in the woman's hymn still hanging in the air at the ford like smoke. Noor had never lived in a temple. Temples were just places people had agreed to point at. Now the pointing had stopped, and the god was everywhere the way weather is everywhere, and the only price was that nobody could own it, including me.

Especially me.

"Tamsin," said the barley, in my mother's voice.

"I know," I said. "You're there."

"We're everywhere. It's very nice. We've been to the sea. Have you been to the sea?"

"Not yet."

"You should go to the sea. There are voices there that even we haven't heard. Old ones. Salt-cured." A pause, and then, in a voice that was definitely a dead choirboy named Pell who had died eighty years before I was born and whose laugh I now knew better than my own: "Also there's a ship leaving from Corrow Bay in nine days that you should be on."

"Why should I—"

But the barley had gone back to being barley. Noor never explained itself. It had spent centuries being a chorus of fragments; it had earned the right to be cryptic in stereo.

* * *

I want to tell you about the inn at Corrow Bay, because it's where I finally understood what I'd become, and because the ale was good and I feel the story deserves at least one detail that isn't metaphysical.

The inn was called the Drowned Ledger, which made me laugh so hard on first sight that the stable boy asked if I needed to sit down. Inside, it was loud and warm and full of sailors, and in the corner a fiddler was playing, and a dozen voices had picked up the tune, and woven through the tune — the way thread picks up dye — was the hymn. Nobody was singing the hymn. Everybody was singing the hymn. It had gotten into their song the way Noor had gotten into mine: uninvited, unpayable, free.

I sat with my ale and listened, and an old sailor at the next table leaned over.

"You've got the look," he said.

"What look?"

"The look of somebody the songs are about." He nodded at the fiddler's corner. "Happens more these days. Ever since last spring. People walk in off the roads with that look, and the music bends toward them a little, like plants toward a window." He squinted at me. "You from the erased country? The one that came back?"

"Vell."

"Huh." He drank. "My grandmother used to sing a song about Vell. Thought she made it up. Whole family thought she was touched." He set down his mug and looked at me with an expression I couldn't name at first, and then could: it was the expression of a man meeting proof. "What was it like? Before?"

I thought about the scriptorium. The scratch of pens. The ledgers. The way I used to measure my days in marks of debt paid down, nine hundred becoming eight-fifty becoming eight hundred, a life spent bailing a boat that was nailed to the ocean floor. I thought about the prayer book that answered back. I thought about my mother walking into the border mist with a hymn in her mouth and her daughter's debt on her shoulders, and how long it took me to understand that those were the same act.

"Loud," I said finally. "It was very quiet, actually, and it felt loud. Like a room where someone is always counting."

"And now?"

"Now it's the opposite. Everyone sings, and somehow it's the quietest place I've ever been."

The sailor considered this. Then he raised his mug to me, and the fiddler finished the tune, and in the silence between one song and the next, the whole room — forty strangers, maybe fifty — hummed the same note. Just one note, held for a breath, the way congregations do when they've forgotten they're a congregation. The candles leaned. The ale in my mug shivered into rings.

"There it is again," the sailor said softly. "Did you feel that?"

"I felt it," I said. "It does that."

And then, underneath the room's held note, clear as a bell rung inside my own skull, a voice said — in *my* voice, pitched exactly the way I sound when I'm trying not to cry:

*"Carry it forward, Tamsin. That's all it ever was. Somebody carries it forward."*

My mother's words. Sung in my mouth. Because that was the last turn of the key, the thing I'd been circling for four months without letting myself say: the choir had started including me. Not as a listener. As a *voice*. Somewhere between the threshold and the ford, between giving my name away and picking up the pebble, I had stopped being the person Noor spoke *to* and become one of the people Noor spoke *with*. When the god wanted to say something tender, sometimes it borrowed me. When the god wanted to say something stubborn, it borrowed Sefa. When it wanted to say something about justice with the safety off, it borrowed Brannoc, who complained about this constantly and in increasingly theological terms.

A god made of remembered voices, and I was a voice it remembered. I had traded my recorded self for that, and I would make the trade again every morning for the rest of my life, and the trade got better every time, because that's the secret arithmetic Noor runs on: nothing freely given ever leaves you. It just stops belonging to you alone.

* * *

The ship left from Corrow Bay nine days later, as promised. I was on it, because when a chorus of the dead that includes your mother tells you to get on a boat, you get on the boat; arguing with Noor is like arguing with weather, except the weather holds grudges in beautiful harmony.

Before I boarded, I did two things.

First, I went back to the Drowned Ledger and taught the fiddler the whole hymn — all seven verses, the ones the Ledger-Kings burned, the ones that burned back. He learned fast. By the time the ship cleared the harbor, I could stand at the rail and hear my hymn drifting out of the town behind me, already changing, already growing a local verse about herring and fog, which Noor pronounced *an improvement* and my mother pronounced *sacrilege* and both of them meant affectionately.

Second, I took the pebble out of my pocket and looked at it properly for the first time in months. Gray-green, river-smoothed, ordinary. I'd picked it up off the tithe-barn floor the night the dragon woke, off the spot where the great body had lain for two hundred years being milked of its fire, and I'd kept it because I couldn't keep anything else and because some part of me understood, even then, that I would need one object to be the past so all the other objects could stop mattering.

I threw it into Corrow Bay. It made a very small sound. The water accepted it without ceremony, the way the new law accepts everybody.

"No grave?" Noor asked, in a voice like my own.

"It wasn't a grave. It was a bookmark." I watched the rings spread. "I'm done reading that part."

"Mm," said the god, in the voice of a baker from Lowmarsh, a jailer, a child, a dragon, my mother, me. "And what comes next?"

I looked out at the open water, at countries I'd only ever known as columns of figures in trade ledgers — grain exports, tariff schedules, populations reduced to sums. Countries full of people humming a hymn they couldn't place. Somewhere out there, other valleys might be sitting inside other erasures, other fires guttering under other tithe barns, other names sold off by other founders, waiting for somebody to walk out of them carrying the memory. Waiting for a scribe, maybe. Debt-bound, diligent, quietly suffocating, the perfect vessel precisely because no one watches the person copying.

"Well," I said, "you heard the choirboy. There are voices at the sea even you haven't heard."

"Our reach has limits," Noor admitted, with the modesty of a being that was currently everywhere. "We are remembered only where we were once known. Beyond that—" the voices braided together, dozens deep, and for a moment the whole harbor seemed to lean in— "*someone has to carry us.*"

The wind filled the sails. On the dock, the harbormaster was calling passengers aboard, and a woman with a market basket — the same woman, I'd have sworn it, though that was impossible, and I no longer believed in impossible — was humming as she handed her ticket over, and the hymn slipped into her humming like light into glass.

I went up the gangway carrying nothing, which is to say carrying everything, which is to say carrying the song.

Somewhere ahead, in a country that had never heard of Vell, someone was about to start singing a tune they didn't know, and stop halfway through, and wonder, and ask their grandmother about it, and begin.

I'd be there when they did. One of the voices, anyway.

We all would.
