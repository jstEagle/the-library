## 1. The Road That Isn't There

The pass did not exist.

That was the whole point of it. On no map drawn by the crown's surveyors, in no ledger of tolls, on no soldier's memory of the Thornfell range was there a gap between the twin shoulders of Mount Ceren and the Greytooth crags. The mountain was solid there. Every official record agreed on this, and so every patrol rode the low passes instead, and so Hazel Villalobos walked her smugglers through solid rock twice a season like a woman stepping through a wall that only she could see.

She had not found it herself, strictly speaking. She had asked a fox.

Three winters ago, caught above the snowline with a broken axle and a crown patrol two ridges behind, she had crouched in the scree and put her mouth close to a red vixen's ear and said, *I need a way through that isn't a way.* The vixen had looked at her with the flat gold patience of her kind, then trotted north along a contour Hazel would have sworn was cliff face, and stopped, and sat, and waited to be followed. What opened up when Hazel committed her weight to it was not a tunnel and not a trick of light. It was a real passage between the stones, narrow enough that the mules had to be unloaded and led blindfolded, wide enough at its heart for a wagon if you knew which three boulders to shift—and it had been there all along, or perhaps it had become there just then; Hazel had never been able to decide which answer frightened her more.

She never asked the mountain questions twice. That was the first rule of her gift, learned young and paid for younger: ask once, take what you're given, and tell no one you asked. A beast-speaker who let the wrong ears hear her speak was a witch by sundown. The crown paid silver for tongues like hers—paid better than any smuggling run—and the pyres they fed were not a rumor. She had watched one burn outside Millbrook when she was nine years old. The woman had screamed about the weather, about the birds, about how the crows had told her things, right up until the smoke took her voice, and after that Hazel had folded her gift small as a coin sewn into a hem and carried it that way for twenty years.

So the caravan behind her this morning knew her only as Villalobos, the guide who charged too much and earned it, a lean woman of thirty-one with wind-cracked hands and a habit of walking ten paces ahead of everyone as though she couldn't stand the smell of people. They didn't know why she paused at certain bends with her head cocked, listening to nothing. They didn't know that when she murmured *easy, easy* under her breath at a nervous mule, the mule understood her perfectly. They thought she read the mountain the way they read a ledger: lines and figures. Let them think it. Thinking it kept them alive, and alive was what she sold.

There were eleven of them today. Six wagons, half-loaded with salt pork and uncut garnets from the Kellin diggings, riding under the flag of no one because flags were for people who wanted to be found. Their captain was a broad, gray-stubbled man called Odo Ferran, who had once tried to cheat her out of a season's fee and now paid upfront without being asked, because the year he'd shorted her he'd taken the low road instead and lost two wagons and a brother to a rockslide that she would have seen coming in her sleep. Smugglers learned arithmetic quickly.

"Storm before dark," Odo said, squinting east where the sky over Greytooth had gone the color of pewter. "You'll get us through the narrows first?"

"I'll get us through." She didn't look back at him. Looking back invited conversation, and conversation invited questions, and questions were how guides ended up described in witch-hunter reports. *A woman, thirties, speaks softly to animals.* She kept her voice flat and her eyes forward. "Muzzle the dogs. All of them."

Odo grunted but passed the word down the line. He'd learned that too: when Villalobos said muzzle the dogs, you muzzled the dogs, because something in these high reaches made them stupid with fear, and a dog that bolted barking through the narrows could bring half the mountain down on their heads. What Odo didn't know was that the dogs weren't afraid of the mountain. They were afraid of what was waking underneath it. But that was a debt of knowledge Hazel hadn't yet decided how to carry, and she set it down again, the way she set everything down, and walked on.

* * *

The narrows came an hour past noon, exactly where the map said there was nothing.

Hazel felt it before she saw it—the particular hush, like a held breath, that meant the passage was open to her today. Some days it wasn't. She'd come up here alone in the off-season, once, testing it, and found only cliff, seamless as poured wax, and had stood there feeling like a fool talking to stone until a marmot whistled at her from a ledge and she'd understood: the road wasn't hers. It was lent. Everything out here was lent—the pass, the good weather, the stag's life she'd patched together with splint and moss two springs ago. You borrowed from the wild and the wild kept accounts.

"Single file," she said. "Hands on the wagons. Nobody speaks above a murmur until I say."

The walls closed in around them, grey granite streaked white with old birdlime, and overhead a strip of sky so thin you could cover it with two fingers. The mules went quiet, which mules never did, and Hazel took it as the compliment it was: even the beasts bought and branded could feel that this place was not entirely the crown's world. There were marks on the stone here, old ones, older than the kingdom—spirals cut hip-high, worn shallow, that the smugglers took for natural weathering because taking them for anything else was bad for business. Hazel ran her thumb across one each time she passed. It was warm. It was always warm, even in January, and she had never once let herself wonder aloud why.

They were halfway through, the last wagon still in the throat of the pass, when the dogs went mad against their muzzles.

Not fear-mad. Alert-mad. Hazel froze mid-stride and turned her head, slow, the way you turn when you already know what you'll see, and saw it: far below and east, along the valley floor where the legal road ran, a thread of dust. Riders. Eight, maybe nine, moving in the tight disciplined wedge that no smuggler ever bothered to learn and every witch-hunter drilled until it lived in his spine.

"Wagons stop." Her voice came out level, which cost her something. "Kill the lanterns even though it's day. Everyone down behind the lead mule."

Odo's head came up. "Patrols don't come up here. There's no road up here."

"There's no road anywhere near us," Hazel said, "and they're a mile off the legal road anyway. Look at their line. They're not patrolling. They're casting." Casting—a sweep pattern, quartering the slopes like hounds working a scent. And the scent, she understood with a cold drop in her stomach, might be theirs. The dogs had smelled it first. Whatever the hunters were following, the animals had known about it before the men did.

She climbed the fallen slab at the pass's mouth, keeping her body inside its shadow, and glassed the riders with the cracked spyglass she'd traded a garnet for two years back. Gray cloaks. Crown gray, not smuggler drab. And at the column's head, on a banner no bigger than a hand towel, a device she knew the way you know a scar: a black bell on a white field.

Witch-hunters. Not tax men, not border levies. The Office of the Bell had come into the Thornfell itself, and it was hunting something, and it was hunting in a pattern that would bring it, within the hour, to the base of the scree below the narrows, where eleven people and six wagons full of contraband were standing in a road that officially did not exist.

Her options arranged themselves with the bleak clarity of all her worst arithmetic. Fight: eleven smugglers against nine hunters, and even if they won, survivors talked, and talk reached the Office, and the Office asked why smugglers had been ready for them. Run: back down the narrows the way they'd come, into country she hadn't scouted, hauling wagons, slower than mounted men. Hide: sit tight in the pass and pray the hunters' cast swung wide.

Or—

She lowered the spyglass. Below and to the west, maybe four hundred yards down the slope, a stag was grazing at the treeline. She knew him. Of course she knew him. Two springs ago she'd found him in a willow-choked gully with a wolf-bite torn down his haunch to the bone, leg ruined, eyes already going dull the way hurt animals' eyes go dull when they've decided the story is over. She'd spent half a night on him—splints, moss packing, water dripped into his mouth from her cupped hands—knowing the whole time that a hunter finding her kneeling over a wounded stag with blood to her elbows was a hunter writing a report. She'd done it anyway, because he had looked at her, and she had never yet in her life been able to bear being looked at like that and do nothing.

Then she'd walked away and told herself the debt was settled. Kindness given freely cancels itself. That's what she'd believed. That's what she'd needed to believe, because the alternative—that every mercy left a thread, and the threads accumulated, and somewhere the wild was weaving them into a net around her—was a thought she couldn't afford.

The stag lifted his head.

He looked up the slope, directly at her, across four hundred yards of loose scree, and held her gaze for the length of three heartbeats. Then he turned, stepped out of the treeline onto the open hillside—deliberately, extravagantly visible, a full-grown red stag in autumn coat standing in plain sight of nine armed men—and began to move. West. Away from the narrows. Trotting at first, then bounding, antlers flashing, a display calculated to catch the eye of anyone scanning a hillside for movement.

Hazel watched the lead rider rein in. Watched the wedge break apart as the point men spurred toward the stag. Watched the whole cast swing west, away from her, chasing a deer that was not fleeing anything so much as leading.

He took them down into the alder bottoms, along the stream, up the far ridge—always just far enough ahead to keep the chase alive, never so far as to let them lose interest. Hazel tracked him through the spyglass until distance and dusk swallowed both stag and hunters together, and then she stood a long moment with the glass pressed to her eye seeing nothing, feeling the cold come up through her boots, doing the thing she always did when the wild reminded her of its ledger: refusing to look directly at the total.

"You knew that stag was there," Odo said behind her. Not accusing. Wondering.

"I saw him earlier." True. "Lucky he cut west." Also true, in the way that all her truths were true the way a loaded cart is balanced—accurate to the ounce, and hiding what it carries.

"Lucky." Odo spat, philosophically. "We've had a lot of your kind of luck on your roads, Villalobos. I've stopped asking after it."

Good. Keep not asking. She climbed down and gave the order to move, and the caravan groaned back to life in the failing light, and they came out the far side of the narrows as the first flakes of the storm came down, fat and slow, erasing the pass behind them as thoroughly as the mountain erased it every night of the world.

* * *

Camp that night was a hollow below the tree line, fires small and shielded, watches doubled. Hazel took second watch herself, as she always did—not because she trusted herself most, but because the hours between midnight and dawn were when the mountain spoke, and she was the only one in camp who could hear it, and someone had to listen even if listening was the most dangerous thing she did.

The storm thickened. Snow hissed against the fire-shields. And the animals around the camp went strange.

Not restless—she knew restless, restless was weather in their bones, restless was a wolf's voice two valleys off. This was different. The picketed horses stood with their heads hanging low and their ears swiveled backward, all four of them, all pointed at the same invisible place beneath the ground. The dogs wouldn't lie down. They sat in a row facing downslope, into the mountain's roots, silent, rigid, watching nothing with the total attention they usually spent on rabbits. One of the mules began, very quietly, to tremble.

Hazel crouched by the nearest dog, a rangy black bitch called Sorrel who tolerated her better than most, and put her hand flat on the animal's skull, and let the question move down her arm without words, the way she'd learned to do it—wordless, weightless, a touch shaped like a question.

What is it? What's down there?

Sorrel leaned into her hand. And what came back up through the contact was not an image, not a smell, not any of the ordinary currencies of animal speech. It was pressure. A vast, deep, patient pressure, like the feeling of standing on a frozen lake and hearing, far below the ice, something enormous turn over in its sleep. Fear, yes—but fear braided with something worse. Recognition. The animals weren't afraid of a sound. They were afraid of a presence, and the presence was old, and it was below, and it was paying attention to the surface of the world again for the first time in a long time.

Hazel took her hand off the dog's head slowly, as if from a hot stove.

She sat back on her heels in the snow and made herself breathe. Old gods. The word rose in her unbidden, from stories her mother had told before the grief took her mother apart—stories of the court beneath the Thornfell, of bargains struck in the famine years, of prayers that used to be answered before the crown's god came over the mountains with its book and its bell and taught the valley to forget. Her mother had told those stories in a whisper, looking at the door. Her father had gone into the mountain the winter everything went wrong and had not come back out, and Hazel had spent twenty years building a life on top of that silence like a house built on a filled-in well.

The animals know something is waking. The thought arrived with the gentleness of snowfall and the weight of an avalanche behind it. And I can hear what the animals know. Which means whatever is waking can hear me.

Every prayer in the valley, every desperate whisper aimed at the old names, was rising off the surface of the world like heat shimmer—and her gift made her visible to it, a lit window in a dark house. She had spent her whole life learning to be no one, and it had worked, it had worked beautifully, right up until the ground itself opened its eyes and started looking for someone to hear it.

Behind her, at the edge of the firelight, Sorrel stood up, shook the snow off her coat, and crossed the camp to Hazel's bedroll. She lay down against it, facing outward, toward the mountain. Guarding it. Or guarding Hazel. With the dogs tonight, Hazel couldn't have said there was a difference.

She wrapped her cloak tighter and looked east, where the storm had torn a seam in the clouds, and the peaks of the Thornfell stood black against a sky the color of a bruise going yellow. Somewhere out there, a stag was picking his way home through the snow, having spent an afternoon buying her another season of anonymity. Somewhere below her, under a thousand feet of stone, something had begun to stir that her mother had whispered about and her father had vanished into.

And somewhere in the world between those two darknesses, she understood, the debts were coming due. The kindnesses. The threads. The whole woven, accumulating, unpayable ledger of a woman who had tried to live invisibly in a world that was starting, very carefully, to wake up and look around.

The fox found her three days later. It sat at the edge of camp at dawn, in the open, where no fox sits, and it looked at her, and it spoke.

But that was the next chapter, and the next chapter did not care that Hazel Villalobos had plans.
