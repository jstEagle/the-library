## 2. What the Fox Said

The fox found her three days later. It sat at the edge of camp at dawn, in the open, where no fox sits, and it looked at her, and it spoke.

Hazel had been awake since the grey hour, as she always was, lying in her bedroll under the lean-to of cut pine with one ear tuned to the camp. Twelve smugglers breathing. The mules shifting. Old Sefa muttering in her sleep about a husband dead eleven years. The ordinary music of people who trusted her to get them through the mountains alive.

The fox broke the music.

She felt it before she saw it—that prickle along the back of her neck she'd learned to read as *something is looking at you*, the beast-speaker's oldest warning. She opened her eyes and it was sitting three paces from her boots, red as a coal banked for winter, its tail wrapped neat around its feet like something out of a lady's tapestry. Its eyes were amber and old and they did not blink the way fox eyes blink.

*You*, said the shape of its attention against her mind—the wordless pressure she knew, the language beneath language that every creature spoke if you listened right. *You healed me.*

"I remember," Hazel whispered. She kept her voice below the sleepers' breathing. "Two winters ago. Snare wire, left foreleg. You were very rude about it."

*I was dying,* the fox said, with dignity. *Dying things are permitted rudeness.*

That was normal. That was the whole of her gift, the thing she had spent thirty years folding small and hiding: animals spoke to her in pressures and pictures, needs and warnings, the simple grammar of hunger and fear. She could answer. They remembered her. That was all. That was supposed to be all.

Then the fox opened its mouth, and what came out was not a pressure or a picture or the wordless push of animal need.

What came out was words.

"They are waking," the fox said, in a voice like dry leaves moving over stone, a voice no fox throat should have been able to make. "Run."

The camp went very quiet around her—or rather Hazel went very quiet inside it, everything else continuing at its ordinary volume while some part of her stepped backward out of her own body to look at the impossible thing sitting in the frost. A fox. Speaking. In the common tongue. With a sentence that had structure and intent and—she heard it again in memory, replaying, unable to stop—*weight*. Words with weight behind them, like coins of real gold among counters of tin.

Animals did not speak. Not like this. Not ever, in thirty years of listening, not once.

"Who told you to say that?" Hazel asked. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. Underneath the steadiness her heart was going like a fist on a door.

The fox tilted its head. For a moment the amber eyes were only eyes again, animal and puzzled, as though whatever had spoken through it had set the instrument down. Then:

*Cold coming,* it said, in the old way, the safe way, the way of pictures and pressures. *Wrong cold. Not weather.* And it stood, shook itself once, and trotted off into the treeline without looking back, leaving prints in the frost that steamed faintly, as though the ground itself found them warm.

Hazel lay in her bedroll until the sun cleared the ridge, staring at the pine boughs overhead, and did not run anywhere at all.

Because that was the problem with the word *run*. Run where? She had spent her whole life learning every fold of the Thornfell, every road that wasn't there, every hollow where a woman could be nobody. If the waking thing was under the mountain—and she knew, in the place where knowing lived before thought, that it was—then running was a joke. The mountain was everywhere. You would have to run off the edge of the world.

And besides. She had a crew to deliver. She had a contract. She had a life built carefully, plank by plank, on the principle that nothing strange was happening and no one was looking.

She got up, fed the fire, and said nothing to anyone.

* * *

By midday she was counting wrongnesses.

It started with the birds. Hazel noticed birds the way other people noticed weather; they were the mountain's gossip, its early-warning system, and she had never known them to lie. But the choughs were leaving. Not trickling south the way they did when autumn bit—leaving *en masse*, wheeling up off the crags in ragged hundreds and streaming over the pass in one long river of wings, weeks ahead of any season. She watched them go with a flat feeling in her stomach and counted four separate flocks before noon.

Then the mules. Mules were honest creatures, too honest, and hers had spent the morning refusing to graze on the south-facing slope where they always grazed, crowding together instead with their ears pinned and their noses aimed at the mountain's heart. When she ran her hand down Dapple's neck she felt the tremor in him, fine as a plucked string.

"What's eating them?" Sefa asked, coming up beside her. Sefa was sixty and had smuggled salt and silk through these passes since before Hazel was born, and she trusted exactly two things: Hazel's road-sense and Hazel's weather-sense. It was the second one Hazel reached for now, because it was the lie she had ready-made.

"Deep snow coming early," Hazel said. "Something in the air. Animals feel it first."

Sefa squinted at the sky, which was clear and hard and blue as a blade. "Doesn't look like snow."

"It doesn't have to look like anything yet."

It was a good lie. It was even mostly true, which was what made her best lies good. Sefa nodded and went to double-check the lashings, and Hazel stood alone with the truth she couldn't say: *not weather*. The fox had said it plain. Wrong cold. Something under the stone, turning over in its sleep, and every living thing above it reading the change in the dark the way sailors read a swell before they see the wave.

In the afternoon a bear crossed their trail half a mile ahead, going downhill, fast, with two cubs clinging to her back—a thing Hazel had never seen in twenty years of mountain travel. Bears did not run. Bears did not put cubs on their backs like panicked deer. She watched the brown shape crash away through the scree and thought, with a calm that frightened her more than panic would have: *the animals know something I don't, and they are all leaving.*

At dusk the wolves sang. That in itself was ordinary—wolves sang most nights in the high country—but this song was wrong. It started low, almost subterranean, a sound she felt in her back teeth more than heard, and it rose and rose and then simply stopped, all of them at once, mid-note, as though someone had clapped a hand over the whole range's mouth.

Silence after that. A silence with texture. A held breath.

Brannick, the crew's hired muscle, laughed by the fire and said the wolves were fighting over a kill. Kess said the same. Marta said the birds were fleeing a hard winter and so should we all, ha, and passed the flask. They were good people, her smugglers, practical people, and they had a language ready for every strangeness: weather, wolves, luck, the mountain being the mountain. Hazel listened to them build their comfortable explanations brick by brick and understood that she could not hand them the true one, because the true one had come from a talking fox, and the penalty for a guide who claimed her beasts spoke words was a stake and a pyre, or worse, a quiet report to the witch-hunters and a cell in Corven's name.

So she said, "We make the north fork tomorrow, and I want us moving before light," and let them believe it was about weather.

Only when the fire had burned down and the crew slept did she allow herself, finally, to think the thought she'd been holding at arm's length all day.

*They are waking. Run.*

Waking. Which meant sleeping. Which meant something had slept, long enough and deep enough that its waking was news worth carrying up out of the stone in a stolen fox's mouth. And the Thornfell had always been full of the old stories—the chapel gods, the hearth-gods, the ones the crown had burned or buried or starved out three hundred years ago. Her grandmother had hummed songs to gods whose names weren't in any book. Half the valley still left bread on windowsills for reasons no one remembered.

Bread on windowsills. For gods no one remembered.

Hazel pulled her blanket to her chin and told herself she was being a fool, and fell asleep anyway, and the dream took her like water takes a dropped coin.

* * *

There was a door under the mountain.

She knew it the way you know things in dreams—not learned but remembered, the knowledge simply *there*, worn smooth like a stair tread. The door was older than doors. It was made of the mountain itself, of stone that had been asked, politely, long ago, to become a door, and it stood at the end of a passage that went down past all sense of down, past roots of mountains into the roots of roots, and it was carved all over with faces.

Not god-faces. Small faces. Human faces, dozens of them, hundreds, each one different, each one rendered with terrible tenderness—young faces, old faces, faces mid-laugh, faces asleep—and the longer Hazel looked the more she understood that these were not decorations. They were records. They were *kept*.

And behind the door, something breathed.

The breathing was vast and slow, the tide-breath of a sleeper, and underneath it, threaded through it like a hair caught in amber, there was another sound. Smaller. Closer. A voice, humming.

Hazel knew the humming.

She was thirty-four years old and she had not heard that humming since she was nineteen, but a body does not forget the sound its own blood makes. Tomas used to hum when he worked, when he thought no one was listening, a little three-note turn of melody he'd invented himself at seven years old and never abandoned, and their mother used to say he'd still be humming it at his own funeral, and—

"Tomas?" she said, in the dream, and the dream went taut as wire.

The humming stopped.

For a long moment there was nothing but the vast slow breathing of the sleeper behind the door, and then the voice came, and it was his voice, it was *her brother's voice*, thinned and stretched and sanded down fifteen years past use, but his, unmistakably his, the way a scarred hand is still a hand.

"...someone said my name," it said. Wondering. Frightened. "Someone up there said my name. It's been so long since anyone said my name."

Hazel tried to move toward the door and the dream would not let her; her feet had become part of the floor, her body part of the passage, and she understood dimly that this was not her dream at all, that she had been *pulled*, that somewhere a door had been knocked upon from both sides at once.

"Hazel," the voice said. "Hazel Villalobos. You're the one saying it. Aren't you. You're saying it right now."

"Yes," she said, or tried to say, and the word came out as frost.

"Don't—" The voice cracked, frayed, and for a moment it was not fifteen years older but seven years old, a boy's voice on a homestead porch at dusk. "Don't tell Father you heard me. He'll—he doesn't remember what he paid. They don't let him remember. Don't let them know you can hear me, Hazel, they'll want you too, they're hungry, they've been hungry so long—"

"Tomas, where are you? What happened to you?"

But the vast breathing was changing rhythm. Quickening. The carved faces on the door seemed to shift in the corner of her eye, mouths opening by fractions, and the voice inside was speaking faster now, tumbling, urgent, a man drowning in words:

"They're turning over. All of them. Fifteen years I've polished their thrones and they never stirred and now they stir and when they wake they'll reach for whoever they can hear, and I can hear you hearing me, sister, I can hear your gift like a bell through the stone, you have to stop, you have to be quiet, you have to be *no one*—"

"I'm done being no one," Hazel said, and didn't know until she said it that it was true.

The breathing behind the door stopped altogether.

In the silence that followed, every carved face on the ancient door turned, slowly, all at once, to look at her.

Then the dream folded shut like a book, and Hazel woke in the grey hour with frost on her blanket and her heart slamming and the taste of stone dust in her mouth, and above the camp, streaming south in the pre-dawn dark, went another river of birds, thousands of them, silent, urgent, gone.

She sat up. Around her the crew slept on. Somewhere east, over the shoulder of the world, the sky was the colour of an old bruise.

*They are waking. Run,* the fox had said. And in the night, her brother's voice, thin as thread through solid rock: *they'll want you too.*

Hazel looked north, toward the mountain's heart, toward the door she now knew was there, and understood that she had already made her choice, had made it in her sleep, had made it the moment she answered her brother's name with her own. Running was for people with something to lose who hadn't lost it yet.

She banked the fire, woke the crew, and pointed them down the north fork.

Three days later, in a village called Ashfordly, she would find a witch-hunter half-dead in the snow outside an empty chapel—and the ledger of her father's sins would begin, page by page, to open.

But that morning there was only the road, and the wrong silence where the wolves should have been singing, and a woman leading twelve smugglers and six mules through a pass that existed because she'd once asked a fox where it hid, wondering, with a calm that had nothing calm underneath it, how long a person could keep being no one when everyone—the dead, the gods, the sleeping stone itself—had begun, patiently, one by one, to learn her name.
