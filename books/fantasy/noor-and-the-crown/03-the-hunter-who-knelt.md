## 3. The Hunter Who Knelt

The warrant said *beast-tongue*, and the warrant was the only honest thing Elias Corven had carried into the Thornfell.

He'd read it eleven times on the road up from Casterhale, until the wax seal had gone soft under his thumb and the words had worn grooves in him. *Rumors of a speaker of beasts operating among the smuggler roads of the Thornfell passes. Locate. Confirm. Report to the Office of the Bell for disposition.* Disposition. The word did its work quietly, the way the worst words always did. Nobody had to write *burn* anymore. They just wrote *disposition*, and men like Elias understood.

He understood because he'd been one of the men who burned things for six years, and because his father had burned things for thirty before him, and because somewhere back along that line the Corvens had stopped being people who served the crown and started being the crown's teeth, and teeth don't get to ask what they're chewing.

His patrol was three men: himself, Brother Odo, and a conscript named Pell who was nineteen and still flinched at his own crossbow. Odo was a true believer, which made him easy to manage and impossible to trust. Pell was nothing at all yet—clay waiting to see which hands shaped him. Elias had been watching the boy the whole climb, the way you watch a candle in wind.

The Thornfell in late autumn was a country that had decided against visitors. The passes closed like fists. The pines stood black and silent, holding their snow like a debt. Elias had hunted heretics in the wine country and the river deltas and the salt flats south of Vail, and every one of those places had eventually told him its secrets, because people were the same everywhere—lonely, frightened, and full of talk. The Thornfell told him nothing. The Thornfell just watched him with a thousand green eyes and said nothing, and he had the growing, unprofessional suspicion that it was laughing.

"Signs," Odo said, for the fortieth time that morning. He said it the way other men said *amen*. "There'll be signs, Corven. There are always signs."

There were. Elias had been reading them for two days and hadn't liked a single one. Deer tracks that crossed the road and then crossed back, as if the animal had changed its mind. Crows sitting in a row on a dead branch like a jury in deliberation. And once, unmistakably, a fox sitting in the middle of the trail ahead of them—not fleeing, not scavenging, just *sitting*, looking at Elias with an expression he refused to name until he'd had brandy and distance. When Odo raised his crossbow, the fox had looked at Elias a moment longer, and walked away unhurried, and Odo's bolt had gone into a stump.

"Marked," Odo said darkly. "We're marked."

"We're cold," Elias said. "There's a village over the ridge. Ashford Hollow, on the survey maps. We'll take shelter and ask questions."

"Questions come after the burning."

"Burning comes after *evidence*, Brother. The Office didn't give me a rank so I'd waste wax on smoke." He said it flat, and it worked, because Odo was a believer and believers obey the hierarchy even when they hate the man in it. But Elias noted the look Odo gave him—the look that would eventually become a letter to Casterhale—and filed it away with all the other small debts he was accruing.

* * *

Ashford Hollow was twelve houses, a mill, a shrine that had once been a chapel, and a silence that Elias felt in his teeth before he heard it.

The silence was wrong. He'd heard village silence before—the held-breath quiet of a place where the hunters had come and the burning was done. That silence had a texture like ash. This was different. This was the silence of people who had decided, collectively and without discussion, to be elsewhere while he was among them. Doors closed as they entered the square. A woman pulling water from the well simply set down her bucket and walked away from it, mid-task, without hurrying. A boy of maybe eight stood in a doorway staring at Pell's crossbow with an expression Elias had never seen on a child before, and it took him a long moment to identify it, and when he did he wished he hadn't.

The boy wasn't afraid of them. The boy was *sorry for them*.

"Chapel's there," Odo said, pointing.

It had been a chapel to the Concordant faith once—you could tell by the footprint of it, the long nave, the bell-gable, now empty of its bell. The Concordant had pulled its priests out of the Thornfell a generation ago when the tithes stopped covering the road. The crown had pulled its soldiers out around the same time, when the passes stopped being worth garrisoning. Ashford Hollow had been left to God and the mountain, and God, by all official accounting, had taken the better offer.

But the chapel door stood open. And from inside it came the smell of bread.

Elias stood in the square and let that fact arrive the way you let cold water arrive, all at once, no negotiating. Woodsmoke and yeast and the brown crust smell of loaves done right, drifting out of an abandoned building in a village that had no baker, because the baker's house was the third one on the left and it had been empty since spring according to the survey rolls he'd memorized at Casterhale.

"Corven." Odo's voice had gone thin and bright. "Do you smell—"

"I smell it."

They went in together, Pell hanging back with the crossbow up and shaking, and the chapel interior stopped Elias two steps past the threshold the way a hand on the chest would have.

It was clean. Not maintained—*clean*, the way a grave is kept. The pews had been pushed to the walls. The floor was swept. And on the altar where a Concordant altar-stone should have been there was a flat rock from the river, worn smooth, and on the rock lay six loaves of bread, still warm, steam rising off them in the cold air of the nave like the building itself was breathing.

Beneath the rock, scratched deep into the altar wood in letters that had been cut and recut and cut again until they were more groove than wood, was a name.

Elias knew the old names. Every hunter did; it was half the training, learning what to listen for in the dark. The Concordant had nine saints and one God, all of them accounted for, all of them properly dead or properly distant. But before the Concordant there had been other names in these mountains, names the archives kept in the sealed annexes, names that were never burned because you can't burn a word out of the world, you can only make saying it cost something.

The name on the altar was *Hearthmother*.

A dead god. Dead two hundred years, dead in every record the crown kept, dead the way the Concordant meant dead—unfed, unprayed, unremembered. And here was her name cut into an altar by hands that had clearly been at it for years, and here was her offering, six loaves of bread, warm, in a cold chapel, in a village with no baker.

"Where did the bread come from?" Pell asked. His voice cracked on the last word. He was looking at the loaves the way a starving man looks at them, which was a mistake, and Elias saw him realize it was a mistake and go pale.

"From the villagers," Elias said. "Obviously."

"But who *baked*—"

"Pell." Elias didn't raise his voice. He'd learned that from a woman in the mountains, years ago, though he'd have cut out his tongue before admitting it. "Stand by the door."

* * *

They found the village headman in the mill, because the mill was the only building with a lock and the lock was the only defiance left to him. His name was Garrick Voss, and he was sixty if he was a day, with miller's flour worked so deep into his knuckles it looked like scar tissue, and he sat with his hands flat on the table and answered Elias's questions with the terrible calm of a man who had already done all his arithmetic.

"Who leaves the bread?" Elias asked.

"The chapel does."

"Who bakes it?"

"The chapel does."

"Brother Odo is going to put you to the question if you keep answering like that, and I will not be able to stop him. So I'll ask it differently. Six loaves, every morning, in a chapel that hasn't held a priest in thirty years. Someone carries them in. Someone lights no fire, because there's no fire-scar on the chapel hearth—I checked. Someone leaves them on the altar of a god that the Concordant killed two centuries ago. Who."

Garrick Voss looked at him for a long time. Outside, the millwheel turned and the water said its one word over and over.

"You ever been hungry, hunter?" he said at last. "Real hungry. Not soldier hungry. Famine hungry. The kind where you watch your neighbor's children get smaller and you do sums at night that you're ashamed of in the morning."

Elias said nothing. He had been eleven years old in the Vail famine year, and he had done those sums, and his father had been on the road burning hedge-witches while the sums got done.

"Twenty years back we had a bad one. Bad as the old stories. The Concordant priest was long gone, the crown was long gone, and the granary was empty, and there was nothing left to pray to that had ever answered. So we did what the old people said. We swept out the old chapel. We cut the old name. We left bread." He turned his hands over, palms up, an old man's shrug. "And in the morning there was more bread than we left. Always more. Never much. Just enough. Twenty years, hunter. Not one child buried of hunger since. You do the sums on that."

"Someone's feeding you," Elias said. "Someone with a bake-oven and a reason. Smugglers do it to buy silence. The Office has seen it before—"

"Maybe," Voss said, agreeable and immovable, a man talking to weather. "Maybe someone feeds us. You want to burn the chapel, burn it. We'll sweep it out again. You want to burn us, there's twelve houses and it's a long winter and we've been left before." He looked at Elias's warrant, hanging from his belt where warrants hang, and something in his face was almost gentle. "But I'll tell you what I told the last one. The bread was there the morning after we cut the name. Before anyone in this village knew we'd done it. Before *we* knew if we meant it. So whoever's baking, hunter—they weren't waiting on us. We were waiting on them."

* * *

The order was unambiguous. Elias read it again by lamplight in the mill loft they'd been given, Odo snoring below, Pell awake and pretending not to be.

*Signs of pre-Concordant veneration: burn the site. Identify the source of the anachristic feeding: report. Any person found to traffic with unlicensed powers: disposition.*

Burn the site. It was three words. He'd burned sites before—a hedge-circle in the wine country with nothing in it but candle wax and a frightened widow; a river shrine with offerings of fish; a whole farmhouse once, in his first year, with a family in it, though the report had said *empty* and his father had written it and Elias had signed beneath and that signature was the first lie he'd ever told and it had fit his hand like a glove fits a hand, which was the horror of it.

He sat with the lamp and the warrant and the memory of bread-smell, and he did the sums.

Here was what the sums said. Six loaves a day, twenty years. A village that should have lost a child a winter and hadn't. No fire, no priest, no payment, no power gathered to any human hand—Voss had answered forty questions and never once lied, Elias was good enough at this to know. Whatever was in that chapel, it wasn't a smuggler's trick and it wasn't a cult. It was a *hearth*. It was the oldest, smallest kind of god there was, the kind that had never asked for anything but a swept floor and a name, the kind the Concordant hadn't even bothered to burn because it was beneath the effort.

And his orders said burn it.

And there was a memory, the way there was always a memory, and this one came up on him now with its usual terrible timing.

Nine years ago. He'd been twenty-three, a new hunter on his first mountain posting, and he'd gone up the Thornfell passes alone against orders because he'd been young and the orders had been stupid, and the mountainside had come down on him the way mountainsides do—no malice, just gravity and old stone. He remembered the crack, and the white roar, and then being pinned under a slab the size of a door with his leg folded wrong and the cold coming up through the rock like a tide, and knowing, with the strange calm of the truly doomed, that nobody knew where he was.

And then a girl had come out of the trees.

He'd never learned her name. That was the thing that had kept him up some nights over the years—that he'd never gotten her name, and she'd never offered it, and he'd been too half-dead and too proud to ask. She'd been maybe sixteen, dark-haired, weathered, with a scarf up over her face and eyes that looked at him the way you look at something you've found and haven't decided about. She'd moved the slab—him, later, alone, he'd done the arithmetic on that too, and the slab had weighed more than she could have lifted, and he had never once let himself finish that thought—and she'd splinted his leg with a hunter's own competence, and fed him, and when he'd asked who she was she'd said, "Someone the mountain owes," and when he'd asked how to repay her she'd looked at him a long moment and said:

"Next time your work tells you to burn something that feeds people—don't."

And then she was gone into the trees, and he'd limped back down the mountain and reported a rockslide and no contact, and had never told a living soul, because what would he say? That a girl in the woods had moved a stone no girl could move? That was a confession, and confessions went to the Office of the Bell, and the Office of the Bell had a room below Casterhale where confessions went to be made useful.

He'd told himself for nine years that it was a coincidence. That she'd been a smuggler. That the slab had shifted on its own. That the words had been a guess, a lucky arrow.

He was thirty-two years old and lying in a mill loft in Ashford Hollow, and he was done telling himself that.

* * *

He burned the chapel at dawn, because not burning it at all was impossible—Odo would do it anyway, and worse, and would write it up with Elias's name left off, which was its own kind of disposition.

But he burned it *empty*, and empty was a thing a man could build.

He went in first, alone, before the light. He swept the floor one last time. He took the smooth river-stone from the altar and put it in his pack, against the warrant, where it sat against his hip like a hand. He cut the name from the altar wood with his knife—Hearthmother, two centuries dead, fed twenty years on six loaves a day—and he folded the shavings into his glove. What the Office didn't find, the Office couldn't disposition.

Then he stacked the pews and the broken altar wood and set the fire himself, and stood in the square with Odo and Pell and half the village watching from their doorways with that same terrible calm, and watched the chapel burn.

The bread had been gone. He'd checked. Six loaves, every morning for twenty years, and on the last morning the altar had been bare, and he had stood there in the cold with his heart going like a hammer and understood that he had been *answered*—that something in that chapel had looked at the hunter standing in its nave with a warrant and a knife and had understood the arithmetic, and had cleared its own altar, and had let him burn an empty room.

The smoke went up straight in the still air. An old woman in the crowd was crying without any noise at all, just the tears, and Elias made himself look at her, because he owed her that much and more.

"Report," Odo said beside him, satisfied, smelling of smoke and righteousness. "Site cleansed. No anachristic source located. The villagers are cowed. That's a clean day's work, Corven."

"A clean day's work," Elias agreed.

He wrote the report that evening in the mill loft, by lamplight, in the careful hunter's hand that had signed one lie before and was now signing its second. *Site of pre-Concordant veneration located and cleansed by fire. No source of the anachristic feeding identified; likely smuggler provisioning, now disrupted. Village compliant. Recommend seasonal re-inspection. No evidence of beast-tongue activity in this sector.*

Every word of it was false, and his hand never shook once, and that was the part he would think about later, in the years when he thought about it at all. Not the lie. The steadiness.

He signed it. He sealed it. And because the Office required a disposition recommendation and because he was, apparently, now a man who lied in ink, he added the line that would send him where he wanted to go and hoped to God he'd live to regret it:

*Recommend this hunter proceed deeper into the Thornfell passes to pursue the beast-tongue rumor to its source.*

Below the mill, in the square, in the ash of the chapel, the wind moved through the coals, and for a moment the smoke made a shape like a woman standing in a doorway, and then the wind changed and it was only smoke.

Elias banked the lamp and lay down and did not sleep, and in the dark he heard, very far off up the mountain, a sound he had no warrant for at all.

It sounded like a bell. But it was ringing from *under* the ground.
