## 4. Bread from an Empty Chapel

The village of Lowmere sat in the crook of the Thornfell's lower slopes like something the mountain had coughed up and forgotten to swallow. Forty roofs, a mill with a broken wheel, and a chapel of pale stone that had no business still standing.

Hazel came down to it alone, as she always did. She'd left the caravan camped two ridges over with strict instructions not to follow—Marta Voss didn't pay her for company, she paid her for roads, and Hazel had told her this particular errand was about finding a winter route through the eastern passes. Which was even true. Mostly.

The truth underneath was simpler and worse: the birds had been flying south for three weeks, and the fox had said *they are waking*, and Hazel had spent every night since dreaming of a door under the mountain with her brother's voice behind it. She could sit in a smuggler's camp pretending it was weather until the snow buried her, or she could go where the prayers were coming from and see for herself.

Lowmere was where they were coming from. She'd heard them on the wind for two days—a low, many-throated murmur, like bees under snow.

She approached from the treeline at dusk, hood up, hands in her pockets, a woman with nothing in her but cold and curiosity. The village dogs should have announced her. They didn't. They lay flat on their bellies in the doorways and watched her pass with wide amber eyes, and one old bitch whined once, softly, the way dogs whine at funerals.

*They know what I am,* Hazel thought, and her stomach clenched. *Or they know what's coming.*

The chapel stood at the village center, and it was wrong in a way that took her a moment to name. Then she had it: there was no smoke. Every other chimney in Lowmere bled thin gray threads into the blue dusk, but the chapel's flue stood empty, and its windows were dark, and its door hung open on hinges that hadn't been oiled in years.

An abandoned chapel. And yet.

She crossed the frozen square, boots crunching, and stopped in the doorway.

Inside, someone had lit candles. Dozens of them, cheap tallow stubs arranged on the flagstones in a pattern that wasn't the crown's sanctioned geometry—the neat triangle of the Church of the Ascendant—but a rough circle, older, worn into the stone itself by centuries of knees. The air smelled of tallow and bread.

Bread. Fresh bread, warm enough that its smell cut straight through the candle smoke.

On the altar—no, not the altar; the altar had been stripped bare when the crown deconsecrated the place, and everyone knew it—on the bare stone shelf where the altar used to be, there sat six loaves. Brown, cracked-crusted, real. Steam still rose off the nearest one.

Hazel stood in the doorway for a long time.

There was no baker in Lowmere who would leave bread here. There was no one in Lowmere at all—the square was empty, doors shut against the cold, and yet the loaves were warm, and the candles were lit, and the circle worn into the floor was glowing faintly, faintly, like a banked fire deciding whether to come back.

"They are waking," she said aloud, just to hear it in her own voice. "Run, the fox said. Well. I never was good at running."

She should have taken the bread and gone. That was the smart move: evidence, warmth, food, out. Instead she found herself stepping inside, kneeling at the edge of the circle, and saying, in the low voice she used for animals and never for people:

"I don't know your name. Nobody's spoken it in so long I don't think anyone remembers. But if you're there—if you're waking—I'm listening."

The candles all leaned at once, every flame bending toward her like grass toward sun, and then straightening.

Her skin prickled from wrist to shoulder. Somewhere far above, under tons of stone, something turned over in its sleep.

And that was when she heard the breathing.

It came from behind the chapel, ragged and wet and human, and it snapped her out of her skin faster than any god could have. She blew out the nearest candle and moved to the side window, low to the ground, hand going to the knife at her belt.

A man lay in the snow against the chapel's back wall. Big, broad-shouldered, dressed in the gray-and-silver of a crown witch-hunter, though the colors were dulled by frost and filth. His patrol cloak was torn at the shoulder. One boot was missing. He'd wrapped his bare foot in sacking, badly. Frost had gotten into his beard and turned half of it white, and his lips were the color of old bruises, and he was shivering in great slow waves, the kind that meant the body was past fighting and starting to shut down.

Hazel knew him.

Not well. Once, seven years ago, on a rockslide slope above the Kestrel road, she'd dug a young hunter out of the scree with her bare hands while his own squad stood at a safe distance shouting useless orders. She'd known what he was even then—one of theirs, the silver pin at his collar marking him as Corven, Elias, junior warrant, Third Company—and she'd done it anyway, because he was dying and she couldn't watch anything die. She'd vanished before he could properly see her face. She'd told herself for seven years that kindness was safe if you didn't stay to collect thanks.

He'd been hunting her kind ever since. She'd seen his name on warrants passed between smugglers, seen the descriptions: *beast-tongue, unknown identity, operating Thornfell region.* Elias Corven. The man whose file probably had her face sketched in charcoal by now, or close to it.

And his own people had left him here. In the snow. Missing a boot.

Hazel crouched in the shadow of the window and did arithmetic. If she walked away, the mountain finished him by morning, and one less witch-hunter in the world was arguably a gift to everything she loved. If she saved him, she saved the one man in the Thornfell with both the authority and the obligation to burn her.

The candles inside flickered. All of them, together, like a held breath.

"Oh, don't you start," she muttered—to the god, to the chapel, to whatever was listening. "This isn't a sign. This is me being an idiot."

But she was already moving.

* * *

Getting him inside was the worst quarter hour of her winter. He was dead weight plus armor, and the sacking on his foot had frozen to the skin beneath. She got him through the door, onto the flagstones near the candle circle, and set about him the way she'd set about hypothermic mules: blankets from her pack, hot stones from the chapel's own hearth—which, she discovered with a chill unrelated to cold, was full of burning wood nobody had put there—melted snow worked into his extremities, rough brandy from her flask forced between his teeth.

The hearth that burned by itself decided her, more than anything. Gods didn't light fires for witch-hunters. Unless they did. Unless the whole point of gods was that they didn't get to choose whom they warmed.

His eyes opened near midnight.

They were gray eyes, level and tired, and they found her face across the candlelight and went very still. She watched recognition move through him—not of her, not yet, but of what she might be. A woman alone, in a haunted chapel, tending a hunter. His hand crept toward the knife that wasn't at his belt anymore. She held it up, hilt first, and set it on the flagstones between them.

"You were searched before they left you," she said. "Whoever took your kit wanted you dead but wanted deniability more. Cold exposure. Tragic. Nothing on the report but weather."

His voice came out cracked. "You've done this before."

"I've dragged enough fools out of snowdrifts." She fed the fire another log. "Drink."

He drank. The brandy hit him and he shuddered, and some of the gray receded from his face. For a while neither of them spoke. The candles burned. The bread on the bare altar, untouched since evening, was somehow still warm.

"Corven," he said finally. "Elias Corven, Third Company. You have me at a disadvantage."

"Do I?" Hazel kept her voice flat. "Third Company came through the Kestrel valley seven years back. Rockslide. One of their own went off the road and a woman pulled him out and disappeared before anyone could ask her name." She watched his face do the work. "You wrote a report about it. I'm told you described her as 'a local woman, unremarkable.'"

Something shifted behind his eyes. Not fear exactly. Recognition arriving late, like thunder after lightning.

"That was you."

"That was me."

He tried to sit up and failed, and settled for propping himself on one elbow, and studied her the way he must have studied suspects a hundred times—except that now the studying went both ways, and he knew it.

"You're her," he said quietly. "The beast-tongue. The one the warrants describe." A pause. "I've been looking for you for two years."

"I know."

"And you knew that when you dragged me in here."

"I knew it when I saw you in the snow." She wrapped another blanket around his shoulders, briskly, the way you handle something you refuse to treat gently. "Believe me, I ran the numbers. You dying was better for me than you living. I did it anyway. I don't know why. Maybe because someone has to keep doing things that don't make sense, or this valley stops being worth saving."

The fire popped. Down in the worn circle, the candle flames leaned toward them both.

"Why did they leave you?" she asked. "Your own company?"

Corven was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. When he did, his voice was flat, report-voice, the voice of a man reading his own condemnation.

"There's a village called Ashford, east of here. Praying to a dead god. Getting bread from an empty chapel—yes, I know how that sounds, I've seen it myself now. My orders were to burn the chapel and take names." He looked at his ruined hands. "I burned the chapel. It was empty. I filed a report saying the congregation had dispersed voluntarily and the site showed no residual activity." A breath. "My sergeant read the report differently than I intended. Or he read it correctly, and my captain decided a hunter who hesitates is a liability. Either way, they split the patrol this morning. Told me to sweep the north ridge alone. No one's come looking."

"So they'll list you as lost to weather too."

"If they bother listing me at all." He laughed, short and without humor. "Seven years ago a rockslide nearly killed me and a stranger saved me. Today my own company did the rockslide's work and a stranger saved me again. There's a lesson in there about who my people actually are, and I've been slow learning it."

"Don't learn it too fast on my account."

"No." He met her eyes. "I imagine your account is complicated."

Hazel sat back on her heels. Outside, the wind had dropped, and in the silence she could hear it again—the murmur under the world, the bees-under-snow sound, closer here than anywhere she'd felt it. The candles breathed.

"Ask," she said. "Whatever you're circling. Ask it."

"The reports say the beast-tongue can talk to animals. That animals do favors for her. That a stag misled a crown patrol last autumn—" He stopped. "That was you too."

"The stag owed me. I'd splinted his leg after a snare." She shrugged. "Out here, kindness leaves debts. Apparently I've been leaving them all over the mountain without noticing."

"And the gods waking? The bread, the fires lighting themselves, the villages praying to names that haven't been spoken in a thousand years?" His voice dropped. "Is that you?"

"It's not me." And then, because he had told her the truth about Ashford, and because his own people had left him to freeze, and because she was so tired of carrying it alone: "But I think it knows I'm here. Whatever's waking. I think my gift is a lamp in a dark room, and the room is starting to notice the lamp."

He absorbed that. She could see him weighing it against everything the Order had taught him—that gifts were corruptions, that speakers were kindling, that the proper response to a lamp in the dark was to smash it.

"What happens to you," he said slowly, "if the Order finds you?"

"You know what happens. You've done it. Pyres are inefficient, though, so lately they use the bell-trucks. Ring the silence-bells until a speaker's gift tears itself apart trying to reach through." She smiled without warmth. "You'd know the procedure better than I would."

"I know the procedure." His jaw tightened. "I executed it twice before I understood what I was executing."

The confession sat between them, ugly and honest. Hazel found, to her surprise, that it bought him more than his life had.

"Then here's my price," she said. "For the blankets and the brandy and the foot, which is going to scar. Your silence. You never saw me. The beast-tongue of the Thornfell died tonight in a snowdrift, and Elias Corven, sole survivor of a misjudged sweep, crawled to shelter and found nothing but an empty chapel and his own fever dreams. Can you live with that report?"

"I've lived with worse ones lately." He paused. "But silence isn't free either, and I won't pretend otherwise. I'll give you yours. In exchange, I want information. Not about you. About this." He nodded at the candles, the bread, the self-lit hearth. "Because my Order's archives mention something I wasn't supposed to read, and I've spent two years telling myself I misread it."

Hazel went still. "What kind of thing?"

"A record." He pushed himself up against the wall, wincing, and his gray eyes fixed on hers with an intensity that had nothing to do with interrogation and everything to do with dread. "In the restricted folios. Old. Older than the crown. It's a ledger entry, actually—a debt recorded against a family name. Not a tax debt. The other kind." He swallowed. "A soul-debt. Recorded against the name Villalobos."

The word landed in her chest like a stone dropped down a well. She heard it fall and fall and never hit bottom.

"One child," Corven said quietly. "One soul, sold to 'the court beneath,' in exchange for a harvest. Dated the famine year. Signed by a Villalobos."

The candles guttered—all of them at once, a single indrawn breath—and the murmur under the world swelled, just slightly, like something turning toward the sound of its own name.

"My father," Hazel heard herself say. Her voice sounded very far away. "The famine year. My brother Tomas disappeared that winter. Everyone said the fever took him." Her hands had gone cold despite the fire. "Everyone said."

Corven reached out—carefully, the way you reach toward a wild thing—and closed his ruined hand over hers.

"I don't know what it means," he said. "But I know where the folio is kept. And I know that whatever's waking under this mountain has been waiting a long time for someone to come asking about a debt."

Hazel looked at the bread on the bare altar, still warm after a day and a night, baked by no one for everyone, and made the only decision left to her.

"Then we're going to need more than a ledger," she said. "We're going to need the deep roads. And there's only one person in the Thornfell who knows them."

Outside, the fox was sitting in the snow, watching the chapel windows, its breath steaming, patient as a debt.
