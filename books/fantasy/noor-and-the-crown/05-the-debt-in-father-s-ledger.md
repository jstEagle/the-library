## 5. The Debt in Father's Ledger

The homestead still stood, which was the first surprise.

Hazel had expected ruin—roof fallen in, walls shrugged down by frost heave, the mountain quietly reclaiming what her family had abandoned. But the house sat where it had always sat, at the crook of the river where the water went slow and dark before the falls, and though its windows were blind with grime and the fence had rotted to lace, the bones of it were sound. Someone had kept it standing. Or something had.

She stood at the edge of the dooryard for a long time, letting the wind off the high snowfields move through her coat and find nothing underneath worth warming. The fox sat beside her, tail curled over its feet, watching the house with an attention that made her uneasy. Animals didn't look at empty houses like that. Animals looked at houses that weren't empty.

"You could have told me where we were going," she said.

The fox's ear flicked. It said nothing. Since the night it had spoken—*They are waking. Run.*—it had gone back to being a fox, which was somehow worse. A talking fox was a miracle. A silent one that knew things was a debt collector.

Hazel crossed the yard. The gatepost leaned; she remembered leaning on it herself, age nine, watching her father come up from the fields with his shoulders bent under a sky that hadn't rained in eleven months. She remembered her mother's hands in the flour barrel, searching for flour that wasn't there. She remembered Tomas, seven years old, sitting on this exact stretch of porch step, whittling a stick into nothing because there wasn't food enough to justify the energy of play.

She had not been back in fourteen years. She had told herself it was practicality—the homestead lay too close to the crown road, too exposed for a woman who lived by not being seen. That had been true. It had also been a lie she'd fed until it grew fat enough to carry all her weight.

The door wasn't locked. It didn't even have a lock anymore; the latch had rusted through. She pushed it open and the smell of the past came out to meet her—cold ash, mouse droppings, the ghost of tallow smoke soaked so deep into the beams that fifteen winters hadn't burned it out. Her mother's loom stood against the far wall, threads still strung, mid-pattern, as if the weaver had only stepped out to draw water and would return any moment to finish the row of blue.

Hazel's chest did something complicated and painful. She let it, briefly, then shut the door behind her and got to work.

Because that was the other thing. She hadn't come back for grief. She'd come back because of what Elias Corven had said, half-frozen in the chapel doorway with his breath smoking and his eyes full of a shame he hadn't learned to hide yet: *the archives mention a soul-debt recorded against the Villalobos name.*

Soul-debt. Two words, spoken flatly, the way a clerk reads a charge sheet. He'd said them like they meant nothing, and then watched her face change, and understood that they meant everything.

She'd spent three days telling herself it was a clerical error. Villalobos was a common enough name in the valley—there were Villaloboses in Harrowmere, Villaloboses who kept goats above the tarn, half the valley probably had a grandmother named Villalobos somewhere back. A record-keeper's slip. A tax ledger misfiled. The crown wrote nonsense about souls all the time; that was what witch-hunts were *for*, paper fears stamped onto paper people.

But the fox had led her here anyway. And the fox had never once led her wrong.

* * *

She searched the way she searched everything: thoroughly, silently, without hope.

The main room gave up nothing but dust and the furniture of a life interrupted. In the loft, her old bed and Tomas's, the straw pallets long since eaten to skeletons by mice. Under Tomas's bed frame, scratched into the wood in a child's uneven hand: *T VILLALOBOS WAS HERE* and beneath it, after a gap, *AND ALSO THE BEAR WHICH I DID NOT SEE BUT HAZEL SAYS IS TRUE.*

Hazel put her hand flat over the letters for a moment. Then she kept moving, because if she stopped moving she would sit down on the cold floor of her brother's room and not get up again, and there was no version of this day that allowed for that.

Her parents' room was worse. Her mother's shawl still hung on its peg. Her father's boots stood by the bed, upright, waiting for feet. People who flee a place take their boots. People who are taken leave them.

It was in her father's room, in the end—not hidden in the walls or under the floorboards, which was where she'd looked first, second, and third—but in plain sight, which was the best hiding place in a poor house. His account book lay in the bottom of the chest at the foot of the bed, beneath winter cloaks that smelled of cedar and extinction. A farmer's ledger. She'd seen him hunched over it a hundred nights, lips moving as he counted seed and debt and the difference between them, which in the famine years had been the same thing counted twice.

She carried it to the main room, set it on the table, and opened it by the grey light of the window because she couldn't bring herself to light a fire in her mother's hearth. Not yet. Maybe not ever.

The early pages were ordinary. Rain tallies. Egg counts. What the miller owed and what the miller lied about owing. Her father's hand was small and neat, a schoolmaster's hand wasted on a farm, and reading it was like hearing his voice again—that patient, tired murmur, always explaining why the math wouldn't work this year either.

Then she reached the year of the famine, and the hand changed.

Not gradually. On one page it was the neat schoolmaster script, tallying the last of the barley, *enough for bread till Thawfeast if we eat thin*. She turned the page and the writing was different—larger, pressed hard enough into the paper to emboss the next leaf, the letters of a man writing fast or writing terrified or both.

*They came at dusk. Not men. I will write it plainly because no one will believe me and that is my protection.*

*They came out of the ground itself, from under the fell, and they wore shapes like people wear coats, badly. They asked what I would give for the valley. I thought they meant grain. I was a fool. I offered grain.*

*They said grain is nothing. Grain grows or it doesn't. They wanted something that does not grow back.*

*I said no. I swear before every god I knew the names of, I said no, and I walked away, and I got as far as the gate.*

*And then I heard Tomas laughing inside. He was laughing at nothing, the way children laugh when they are happy and don't know to hide it, and I stood at my own gate and understood what they were asking for, and I understood that the valley would be dead by midwinter, every soul in it, Harrowmere and the tarn villages and the miller and his lies, all of it, gone.*

*And I turned around.*

Hazel stopped reading. Her hands were shaking, and the page blurred, and she set the ledger down on the table with exaggerated care, the way you set down something that might go off.

Outside, the wind moved through the dead fence-lace. The fox had come up onto the porch—she could see its shape through the window glass, sitting at the top of the steps, facing the door, waiting. Keeping watch, maybe. Keeping witness.

She picked the ledger back up.

*The bargain was struck at the stone under the fell, the old meeting-stone, the one Grandfather said the first families swore on. They wrote it in the book so there would be a record, because even gods keep accounts, maybe especially gods. This is what was written and I copy it true:*

*One soul, freely given by the father of the house, in payment for the valley's harvests for seven years and seven. The court beneath shall hold the soul entire. No rescue. No ransom. No appeal. When the seven-and-seven are done, the debt renews unless the court is paid anew or the covenant is broken by consent of both parties.*

*And then they asked me to sign, and I signed, and my hand was steady, which is the thing I cannot forgive. My hand was steady.*

*His name is Tomas. He was seven. He liked the story about the bear better than any other story, even though he never saw it, because Hazel saw it and Hazel doesn't lie about animals.*

*God forgive me. If there are gods left to forgive me. I begin to suspect we ate them, all these years, and never noticed the taste.*

Hazel read it four times. The words didn't soften with repetition. If anything they sharpened, each pass cutting deeper, until by the fourth reading she could hear her father's voice saying it, that patient tired murmur, *I signed, and my hand was steady,* and she made a sound she hadn't made since she was nine years old and had to muffle it in her own sleeve because grief that loud carries across valleys and she had spent her whole life learning not to carry.

* * *

When she could see straight again, she kept turning pages, because her father had kept writing, and a man who writes down his damnation usually writes down what came after.

The next entries were short. Dated. The famine broke that same season—rains in a month that had never once known rain, barley coming up green in fields everyone had already given over to salt-grass. The miller's stores unspoiled. The wells sweet. Seven years and seven of plenty, exactly as written, and the valley called it a miracle and built a little shrine to a god whose name nobody remembered, and Hazel's mother stopped speaking in the spring.

Not all at once. First she stopped speaking about Tomas. Then she stopped speaking about the future—no plans, no *when the boy is grown*, none of the small forward-looking talk that holds a household together. Then she stopped speaking about the present. By the third year she spoke only of the past, and only of the years before the famine, and she wove the same blue pattern into cloth over and over, a pattern Hazel now recognized on the abandoned loom: interlocking circles, broken at intervals, like a chain with links missing.

Like a family with someone missing.

*My wife knows,* her father wrote, in the fifth year. *I never told her and she knows. Mothers know. She looks at me across the table and I can feel her counting our children and finding one short, and she has decided that I am the reason, and she is right, and I cannot defend myself without destroying the valley, so I let her be right. It is the least I owe her.*

*Some nights I go down to the stone and I ask for him back. They do not answer. Once—I think it was once, I was drinking then—they answered. They said: the account is open. Nothing more. The account is open. As if my son were a column of figures they were holding in trust.*

*Hazel asks where her brother goes at night. She says the animals tell her he walks. She is nine and she says it plainly, the way she says everything about animals, and I cannot bear it. She has the gift. Of course she has the gift; the mountain gives it to someone in every generation, and it gave it to the daughter of the man who sold a son. There is a joke in that somewhere, and the joke is on me, and it will go on being told long after I am dust.*

*If she ever reads this: Hazel. My girl. I did not choose you over him. I chose everyone over him, which is worse, and I have known it was worse every day since. Do not forgive me. Forgiveness is a debt and this family has had its fill of debts. Just remember the bear story. Remember that I told it to him last, the night before, because he asked, and I could not say no to him, and that is the whole of me, the beginning and the end: I could not say no.*

The last entry was undated, the handwriting gone loose and wandering, a man writing at the end of his strength:

*Seven-and-seven ends this winter. The account comes due. They will come to renew it, and I know what renewal costs, and I will not pay another child, and I will not pay myself because they do not want me, they want what I have, and what I have is already spent. So I am going down to the stone to break the covenant. They will tell me it cannot be broken except by consent of both parties. Then I will give them my consent, whatever form that takes, and if the price is that I join my son in the dark, then I will finally be somewhere I can apologize to him face to face.*

*Tell Hazel the foxes were always fond of her. Tell her it runs in the family.*

That was the last line. After it, blank pages. After the blank pages, the ledger simply ended, the way a voice ends.

Hazel sat in the cold kitchen of her childhood while the light failed, and the past detonated in her piece by piece, quiet explosion after quiet explosion, each one rearranging the landscape of everything she'd believed.

Her mother hadn't gone mad. Her mother had *known*—had done the arithmetic of a missing child and a miraculous harvest and arrived at the only answer that balanced, and had been forced to live inside that answer with a man she couldn't accuse and a truth she couldn't say aloud. The grief-madness the village whispered about had been the sanest thing in the valley.

Her father hadn't deserted them. He'd gone down to the meeting-stone to break a contract with gods, alone, armed with nothing but consent, and he had never come back up—and the contract, apparently, had held anyway, because the court beneath did not renegotiate with the dead.

And the gods—the old gods the valley mourned, the ones whose names wore smooth from disuse, the ones Elias had found a village praying to—the gods hadn't abandoned Thornfell at all. The valley's survival had never been grace. It had been a purchase. Every loaf baked in the fat years, every child fattened through the seven-and-seven, every full granary and mild winter—all of it bought and paid for, priced in her brother, and the whole valley had eaten off that price without once tasting it.

Just as her father wrote. *We ate them, all these years, and never noticed the taste.*

The fox was looking at her through the window. It had been looking at her for some time. When she raised her head, it rose too, and trotted down off the porch, and stopped at the edge of the dooryard, and looked back at her with an unmistakable imperative: *follow.*

"Not tonight," Hazel said aloud, to a fox, in an empty house, because her standards for conversation had collapsed entirely.

The fox did not move.

Hazel looked down at the ledger in her hands—at the copied terms, the seven-and-seven, the clause about renewal. *The debt renews unless the court is paid anew or the covenant is broken by consent of both parties.* Seven years and seven, counted from the famine year. She did the arithmetic the way her father had taught her, lips moving, and the answer landed in her stomach like a dropped stone.

The seven-and-seven had ended two winters ago.

Two winters ago, her father had gone down to break the covenant—and failed, or succeeded, or something in between—and since then the valley's luck had begun to turn. Thin harvests. Hard ice. Birds flying south weeks early. Animals restless. A fox saying *they are waking, run.*

The account hadn't renewed. The account had *come due*—and the court beneath, unpaid, unappeased, was opening its books again. And somewhere down in the dark, held entire these fifteen years, a boy who liked the bear story best was waiting to learn whether his family's debt would be collected in full.

Hazel closed the ledger. She took her father's boots from beside his bed, because leaving them there one more night was unbearable, and she put them in her pack, and she banked a small fire in her mother's hearth after all—just coals, just enough that the house wouldn't be wholly cold, in case anyone ever came home.

Then she stepped out into the blue dusk, and the fox turned south along the river path, toward the fell, toward the stone, and this time when it glanced back at her, its eyes caught the last light like coins at the bottom of a well.

"Where?" Hazel asked.

For the second time in her life, the fox spoke.

"To the one who keeps the accounts," it said. "Before someone else settles them."
