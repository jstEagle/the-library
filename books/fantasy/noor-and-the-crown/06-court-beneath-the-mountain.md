## 6. Court Beneath the Mountain

He does not remember his name.

He remembers that he had one. That is different. That is the whole of the difference, and he has had—how long? A thousand polishings? Ten thousand?—to learn the shape of that difference, to run his thumb along its edge the way he runs his cloth along the armrests of the thrones.

The thrones are cold. They are always cold. He polishes them anyway, because polishing is what kept things do. There were instructions once, carved into him the way water carves stone: *keep the seats ready, keep the lamps lit, keep the doors counted.* The gods sleep between answers, and when they wake they want their thrones gleaming, and so Tomas—the name arrives like a bird through a crack, startling him every time; Tomas, Tomas, where did that come from?—so Tomas keeps them ready.

There are nine thrones. Or there have always been nine thrones. He counts them each morning, though there is no morning here, only the long amber light that comes from nowhere and goes nowhere, light like honey held up against the sun by someone who has never seen the sun.

*One,* he thinks, standing before the first throne. It is made of river-stone, and it dreams of rain. When he touches it, his palms come away wet.

*Two.* Birch-wood gone silver with age. It smells, faintly, impossibly, of bread.

*Three.* Bone, or something like bone, white as a winter field. He does not linger at this one. The third throne sometimes notices him back.

*Four. Five. Six.* Iron, salt, ash. *Seven* is woven from rope, and once, in the time before counting, it whispered a knot into his fingers that took him a hundred years to untie. *Eight* is empty—not vacant, but *empty*, hollowed out like a gourd, and when he looks into it he sees a valley under snow, and smoke rising from chimneys, and—

And there is a girl on the road below the smoke, small and quick, walking a path that isn't there.

*Nine.*

He stops. He always stops at nine. The ninth throne is the oldest, older than the others, older maybe than the floor it stands on, which is not stone but compressed prayer, ten thousand years of *please* and *thank you* and *why have you forsaken us* pressed down until it became something you could stand on. The ninth throne is made of dark wood he cannot name, and it is warm.

It is the only warm thing in the court.

He polishes it last, always, because after the ninth throne there is nothing else to do, and the nothing else lasts a very long time.

* * *

Here is what he knows about himself, kept like coins in a shoe:

He had a sister. He knows this the way he knows the thrones—by touch, by habit, by the worn place where knowing has been rubbed smooth. Her name is gone. Names go first. Names are the softest parts of a soul and the court takes those earliest, tucks them away somewhere, spends them slowly like a miser spending a fortune.

He had hands smaller than these. These hands are wrong—they are the hands of a man grown, because the court let him grow, or made him grow, or simply did not notice that growing was a thing bodies insisted on doing. But underneath the man-hands he can feel the ghost of boy-hands, hands that held things. A wooden fox, maybe. Someone's sleeve. His sister's sleeve—he is nearly sure it was her sleeve, rough wool dyed some color he can no longer see.

He had a father who smelled of ledger-ink and lamp oil. This memory is dangerous and he keeps it buried deepest, because when he digs it up—and he does dig it up, he cannot stop digging it up—it comes with a sound attached. The sound of a door closing. Not the great door of the court. A kitchen door. A door closing on a boy sent outside on an errand, and the errand was the longest walk of his life, down through the frost-burned fields to a place where the ground opened like a mouth, and—

No. He puts the coin back in the shoe. Some memories are load-bearing. Remove them and everything above collapses.

He had a mother who sang. The song survives without the words, the way a riverbed survives without the river. Sometimes, when the ninth throne is warmest, he hums it, and the humming goes out into the amber dark, and nothing answers, and that is fine. That is fine. Nothing answering is the arrangement. Nothing answering is the deal.

* * *

The gods sleep between answers. He has watched them do it for what might be centuries.

They are not shapes, exactly. They are more like weather given opinions. The god of the river-stone throne pools along the ceiling when it sleeps, a slow grey drift, raining upward. The birch god stands in the corner like furniture, dreaming its bread-smell. The bone god does not sleep so much as *wait*, facing the wall, and Tomas has learned not to walk between the bone god and the wall, ever, for any reason.

When prayers come down—from above, from the world, from the valley he can almost remember—the gods stir. He feels it in the floor first, the compressed prayer shivering like a struck drumhead. Then the amber light thickens. Then the god rises, or pools, or unfolds, and turns its attention toward the surface, and for a moment—for one shining, terrible moment—the court is full of purpose.

A prayer answered. Bread appearing in an empty chapel. Rain arriving on schedule. A fever breaking at midnight.

Then the answer is spent, and the god settles back into its throne or its corner or its ceiling, and the purpose drains out of the room like water out of a cracked bowl, and Tomas picks up his cloth and begins again.

*Keep the seats ready.*

Once—he has told himself this story so many times it may be true—once, a god woke and looked directly at him. The birch god. It stood over him while he polished, close enough that he could smell the bread, and it said, in a voice like a forest deciding:

*You are still here.*

Not a question. An inventory. And then it lay back down inside its own dreaming, and Tomas sat on the cold floor and shook for a hundred years, because *you are still here* was the closest thing to being seen that he had known since the door closed.

He wants to be seen again. He hates that he wants it. Wanting, in this place, is a hole in the hull.

* * *

It happens on a day that is not a day.

He is at the ninth throne, cloth moving in slow circles, humming the wordless song, when the floor shivers—but wrong. Not the drumbeat of a single prayer. This is deeper, longer, a rolling groan like ice shifting on a lake, and the amber light flickers, actually flickers, the way candlelight does, the way real light does, and every god in the court stirs at once.

Tomas goes still. Kept things do not go still well; stillness invites attention. But he cannot move. Because through the shivering floor, through the compressed prayer, through ten thousand years of *please* pressed into stone, something is ringing.

A bell. No—a name.

His sister's name.

He doesn't know how he knows it's hers. He doesn't know the name itself; it won't resolve, it rings and rings and stays just past understanding, a bell heard through a mountain of stone. But he knows whose it is the way he knew the sleeve, the way he knew the song. It comes up through the floor and into his knees and his spine and his teeth, and it says, without words: *someone above remembers you. someone above is asking after you by name.*

The ninth throne grows warmer under his hand. Warmer than it has ever been. And from within the dark wood—within the god that owns the dark wood, the eldest, the one that never fully sleeps—comes a sound like a hinge turning after a long disuse.

Attention. Enormous, ancient attention, turning toward the surface.

Tomas should keep polishing. Tomas should be invisible, the way kept things survive by being invisible. Instead he stands there with his cloth hanging forgotten from his fist, and he listens to his sister's name ring through the stone, and he does the most dangerous thing a kept thing can do.

He begins, carefully, to want.

Wanting starts small. It starts as a question: *is she looking for me?* It becomes a hope: *she might be looking for me.* It becomes a plan, tiny and trembling, no bigger than a seed: *if she is looking, I could leave a mark. I could push something up through the floor. I could hum louder. I could be findable.*

He looks down at his wrong, grown hands. He flexes them. Somewhere far above, snow is falling on a valley he half-remembers, and a girl is walking a road that isn't there, and she is saying his name—or a name, his name—to anyone who will listen.

The eldest god's attention sweeps across the court like a lighthouse beam. It passes over Tomas. It stops.

Comes back.

*You rang,* the dark wood says, or seems to say, though Tomas's lips never moved. *Or something in you did.*

Tomas opens his mouth. Fifteen years of silence—has it been fifteen years? fifty?—and what comes out is not words. It is the song. The wordless one, the riverbed-song, his mother's melody, hummed straight up into the face of the eldest god like an offering, like a coin laid on a tongue.

The court goes utterly still. Even the bone god turns from its wall.

The amber light steadies. The attention withdraws, folding back into the ninth throne like a hand closing. But before it goes, it leaves something behind—one word, dropped into Tomas's chest like a stone into a well:

*Soon.*

And far above, muffled by miles of mountain, a bell keeps ringing. A name keeps ringing. Hazel. The name arrives whole, unbroken, blazing through the stone like dawn through a keyhole—*Hazel, Hazel, Hazel*—and Tomas stands in the shaking amber light with his cloth on the floor and his heart hammering like a trapped bird, and for the first time since the kitchen door closed, he knows exactly what he wants.

He wants to go home.

Above him, faint but unmistakable, the great door of the court begins—very slowly—to unlock.
