## 7. The Smuggler-Queen's Price

The salt caves opened onto the world three ways: a fisherman's crack behind a waterfall on the Greywash, a sinkhole in the high pasture that shepherds called the Devil's Keyhole and never grazed near, and a door in the cliff face at Tarn Hollow that was only a door when Vess Andrade wanted it to be.

Hazel came in through the waterfall, because the waterfall didn't ask questions.

She'd left the caravan two days south with Brannic grumbling about lost wages and her own name unspoken between them—*go, come back, don't tell me what for*, which was the closest thing to tenderness the crew allowed itself. She'd climbed alone through country gone strange. The birds had been flying south for three weeks now, weeks too early, and the fox's sentence still sat in her chest like a swallowed coal.

*They are waking. Run.*

She hadn't run. She'd done something worse. She'd started asking questions.

The passage behind the waterfall narrowed to a crawl, then widened into a gallery of white stone veined with rose and grey, lit by lamps of rendered tallow set in niches every twenty paces—burning in a cave where no one tended them, or so it seemed until you noticed the scorch marks of older lamps beneath, generations deep. The smugglers of Thornfell had been lighting these halls longer than the crown had existed.

Two figures detached from the shadows ahead. Not guards exactly. Salt-cave people were lean and quiet and dressed in grey wool that made them hard to see against stone, and they looked at Hazel the way shepherds look at weather coming over a ridge.

"Name," said the nearer one.

"Hazel."

"Just Hazel?"

"Just Hazel."

The other one snorted softly. "Everyone who comes down here is *just* someone. That's the whole business." He turned. "Walk. Don't touch the walls past the third gallery. And whatever you hear singing, it isn't singing."

Hazel walked.

The caves went down in long spirals, galleries opening off galleries like the rings of a felled tree. She passed storerooms stacked with casks of salted eel and bolts of untaxed linen, a chapel-sized chamber where someone had carved a shrine to no god Hazel recognized—a smooth hollow in the wall, worn deeper by centuries of hands touching it—and a corridor where the air tasted faintly of iron and old prayer. In the fourth gallery she heard the singing. It wasn't singing. It was the mountain breathing through its salt throat, a low harmonic that lived in the teeth more than the ears, and she understood why the man had warned her. A person could stand in that sound and start believing things.

Vess Andrade received visitors in a chamber shaped like an amphitheater, tiers of salt stone descending to a floor of black basalt, because Vess liked to talk down to people and saw no reason to pretend otherwise.

Hazel had heard descriptions all her life and none of them had prepared her. The smuggler-queen was small—smaller than Hazel—with a face like a folded map, all creases and hidden routes, and eyes the pale grey of the salt itself. Her hair was iron-white and cut brutally short, and her hands, resting on the arms of a chair carved from a single block of basalt, were the hands of someone who had personally done everything she now paid others to do.

"You're Villalobos's girl." Her voice was gravel washed smooth by use. "The guide. The one who finds roads that aren't there."

"I find roads that are there," Hazel said. "Other people just can't see them."

Something moved at the corner of Vess's mouth. Not quite a smile. An assessment. "I've bought that modesty from better liars than you. Sit."

There was no chair for Hazel. She sat on the steps anyway, three tiers down, which put her looking up and somehow still not beneath the woman's attention. Around the amphitheater, in the shadowed upper tiers, other shapes watched—Vess's captains, or her ghosts. Hard to say which.

"The fox found you," Vess said.

Hazel kept her face still. It cost her. "You know about that."

"I know about everything that moves in my mountain, girl. A red fox with a healed foreleg, going where foxes don't go, carrying messages like a paid courier. I know whose hand closed that leg wound, too. There's a stag in the north passes that walks toward witch-hunters instead of away from them, and wolves on the Greywash that have stopped fearing men entirely. You've been leaving debts all over Thornfell for years and calling it nothing." Vess leaned forward. "The wild keeps books, Hazel Villalobos. So do I. Yours just came due."

"I need the deep roads," Hazel said. "Under the Thornfell massif. All the way to the court."

The word dropped into the amphitheater like a stone into a well. Somewhere above, one of the watching shapes shifted. The mountain's breath-sound filled the silence that followed, that low false singing, and Hazel felt it against her molars and refused to flinch.

"No one goes to the court," Vess said.

"People used to."

"People used to *pay* it. There's a difference, and the difference is everyone who tried the second thing is dead, and everyone who did the first thing is—" Vess stopped. Her jaw worked once, like a door catching on a warped frame. "What's your business there?"

"My brother's soul is held there. Sold. By my father, fifteen years ago, in the famine year. I've seen the ledger."

For a long moment the smuggler-queen looked at her, and Hazel watched something happen behind those salt-grey eyes—recalculation, the rapid silent arithmetic of a woman who had survived thirty years outside the law by knowing what everything cost.

"A soul-debt against the Villalobos name," Vess said quietly. "I heard that phrase once, years ago, from a man who sold maps he shouldn't have owned. I thought it was a story drunk men told." She stood. She was even smaller standing, and somehow more enormous. "Come with me. There's something you should see before we talk price."

* * *

They went down.

Past the last lamp-niche, past the last storeroom, into corridors where the salt gave way to raw granite and the air grew cold enough that Hazel's breath showed. Vess carried a lantern herself, refusing help, and her short legs ate the distance with the tireless rhythm of a woman who had walked these depths since before Hazel was born.

"This whole range is honeycombed," Vess said as they walked. "Old mines, older shrines, oldest of all—the roads under. My grandmother's grandmother traded with the court. Salt for blessings. That was the old arrangement, before the crown decided the gods were competition. We'd leave offerings at the boundary stones and take nothing more than we brought. Then the crown sealed the deep roads, burned the boundary stones, put bells on their churches, and told everyone the gods were dead." Her voice flattened. "The crown lied. The gods only stopped answering. There's a difference, and I've spent my life learning how much difference matters."

The corridor ended at a ledge overlooking a vast dark space. Vess raised the lantern.

Below lay a cavern like the inside of a bell—a dome of black stone hung with thousands of small objects on cords of woven hair. Bones. Rings. Locks of hair. Baby teeth. Name-tokens, the little lead tags valley folk once tied to their children's wrists at blessing-day. They hung in their thousands, turning slowly in a draft Hazel couldn't feel, and each one chimed—not with sound, but with that tooth-deep harmonic, each token ringing its own private note.

"What is this place?"

"The pawnshop," Vess said. "Or the graveyard. Depends on the decade." She lifted the lantern higher, and its light caught one cord among thousands, thicker than the rest, leading down into the dark below the hanging tokens. "Every family in this valley has something on these strings. Debts to the court, from the famine years and before. The crown thinks the mountain is empty. The mountain is *collateral*."

Hazel looked at the hanging tokens and thought of her father's ledger, the careful entry in his cramped hand: *To the court beneath, one soul, in trust, against the winter.* She thought of her mother rocking in the dark saying a name over and over as if keeping it warm. Fifteen years of collateral. Fifteen years of interest accruing somewhere below in the dark.

"Why show me this?" Hazel asked. "You could have named your price in the amphitheater."

"Because I want you to understand what you're asking me to risk." Vess turned to face her fully, lantern light carving her face into planes and shadow. "The deep roads shift when the gods dream. Right now they're dreaming more than they have in a century—that's why the birds are flying early, why your fox talks, why half my routes have gone soft as wet bread. Guiding anyone to the court in a season like this is a coin flip with my life and my crews' lives on the table. I don't flip coins for sad stories. Everyone who comes down here has a sad story. Sad stories are our currency and they spend worthless."

"So name your real price."

Vess studied her for a long moment. Down in the cavern, the tokens turned and rang their soundless notes.

"There's a convoy," Vess said. "Crown tax train, coming up the King's Road through Thornfell in nine days. Forty soldiers, six wagons, carrying the winter levy out of the valley—grain the valley doesn't have, taken from mouths that needed it. Every year they take it. Every year I watch it go and I can't touch it, because forty soldiers is a fight I lose, and if I'm ever proven to be more than a rumor, the crown seals these caves and burns the hollows that shelter us." She stepped closer. "You're going to turn the mountain's beasts against that convoy. Wolves, first. Whatever follows after. You'll drive them off the road, scatter the wagons, spill the levy back into the snow where my people can gather it. And you'll do it openly, with your gift, so the beasts know who asked and remember."

The cold of the deep place settled into Hazel's shoulders. "That's not smuggling. That's war."

"That's arithmetic," Vess said. "Forty soldiers against one valley's winter. You've spent your whole life being no one, girl—I can smell it on you, the same way I smell it on every ghost I shelter. Being no one is safe. But your brother's soul sits in a court at the bottom of my mountain, and the only person alive who knows the way is me, and I am telling you my price is that convoy. Pay it, and I walk you down myself. Refuse, and I walk you out through the waterfall and you can spend the rest of your life being no one with a clear conscience." She smiled then, and it was the worst smile Hazel had ever seen, because it was kind. "I'm told the gods are waking. Maybe they'll take pity on you. Pity's cheaper than me."

* * *

Hazel didn't answer that night. Vess hadn't expected her to; she'd been given a cell in the third gallery, a straw pallet, a jug of melted snow, and time, which was the most expensive thing the smuggler-queen owned and the only thing she gave away free, because time always collected in the end.

Hazel sat on the pallet and bargained with herself.

It was astonishing, she thought, how many voices a person carried. One voice said: *forty soldiers, and some of them are boys from the valley itself, conscripted, freezing in crown wool for wages that barely feed their mothers.* Another voice said: *Tomas. Tomas polishing thrones in the dark for fifteen years while you learned fox-roads and called it survival.* Another voice, quieter and crueler than the rest, said: *you've already decided. You came down here knowing. This whole night is just you trying to make yourself believe you haven't.*

She thought about the stag in the north pass, walking toward witch-hunters because she had once pulled a barbed arrow from its shoulder and packed the wound with moss. She thought about the fox with the mended leg, running messages for a woman who had never asked it to. Kindness left debts. Vess was right about that. Every kindness she'd spent so carelessly had been sitting in the wild's ledger, waiting.

And now the wild would collect, and the collection would be blood.

She thought about her father, and understood him suddenly, completely, in a way that made her sick. He hadn't been a monster in the famine year. He'd been a man at a counter with a merchant across from him, naming a price, and the price had been his son, and he had paid it because the alternative was watching the whole valley starve including the son. That was what bargains were. They were never evil. They were arithmetic wearing mercy's clothes. Vess's price was arithmetic. Her father's bargain had been arithmetic. Somewhere below her feet, a court full of sleeping gods ran the longest ledger of all, and every entry in it had probably sounded reasonable at the time.

*How much of your soul will you spend to save his?*

The question arrived without a voice attached, and Hazel recognized it as her own, finally asked honestly.

She got up. She walked the galleries in the dark, past the tallow lamps burning for no one, past the shrine with the worn hollow, down to where the mountain breathed its false singing. She put her hand against the salt wall and let her gift open—really open, wide, for the first time in the deep places—and listened.

The mountain was full. That was the truth the birds had been fleeing. Under the stone, in dens and hollows and winter burrows, the beasts of Thornfell were awake when they should have slept, restless, listening to the same deep harmonic she was, waiting the way animals wait—for the world to decide what it was becoming. She felt foxes, dozens. She felt the northern pack, eleven wolves strong, hungry and disciplined. She felt things larger and stranger, moving in the high snowfields, that she had no names for. And threaded through all of it, thin as wire, she felt something else: attention. Something down in the dark noticing her noticing it.

*They are waking,* the fox had said. *Run.*

She didn't run. She reached along the wire of her gift, carefully, the way you reach toward a fire you intend to carry, and she touched the mind of the great grey wolf at the head of the northern pack—an old female, scarred muzzle, eyes like winter water—and she showed her a road. The King's Road. Nine days hence. Wagons heavy with grain, and men around them, and beyond the men, spilling into the snow where the pack's pups had gone hungry two winters running: food.

The wolf considered. Hazel felt her considering, felt the cold patience of a creature that had survived every winter the mountain had ever dealt, and felt—this was the part that undid her—the wolf's complete lack of malice. Wolves didn't hate. Wolves drove, and killed to eat, and the mountain finished what the wolves started. If Hazel aimed them at men, the deaths would be real, but they wouldn't be cruelty. They'd be weather. She would be the one choosing where the weather fell.

She withdrew gently. The wolf's attention lingered a moment, then released.

Hazel stood in the dark with her hand on the salt and understood that she had just crossed a line she couldn't uncross. She had been a hiding thing her whole life. Now she was a weapon that hadn't yet been swung, and both the mountain and the woman in the amphitheater knew it.

* * *

She found Vess at dawn—or what passed for dawn down here, the lamps trimmed brighter in the galleries, the smell of frying eel drifting from the mess hall. The smuggler-queen was on the amphitheater steps drinking something bitter from a clay cup, alone, which Hazel suspected was the rarest sight in the salt caves.

"Yes?" Vess said, without looking up.

"I'll pay your price. The convoy. But I have terms."

Now Vess looked up, and the salt-grey eyes glittered with something that might have been approval. "Terms. From the girl who came in through the waterfall."

"One: no killing that isn't necessary. Your people take the grain after the wolves scatter the wagons, and they take it fast, and they don't finish what the mountain starts unless they have to. Two: when we reach the court, my brother comes out, and whatever else happens down there, you don't bargain with anything in the dark using me as currency without my voice in it. Three—" Hazel paused. This was the term she'd decided on at the wall, hand against the salt, and it still frightened her more than the other two combined. "Three: you teach me the deep roads as we go. All of them. If you die, if anything happens to you, I have to be able to find my own way out. I'm done depending on other people's knowledge to survive."

Vess drank. Set down the cup. Stood, and for the second time Hazel noticed how small she was, and how completely it didn't matter.

"Cruel terms," Vess said. "The first one costs me discipline I'll have to enforce with my own people. The second one ties my hands in a place where loose hands keep you alive. The third one makes you dangerous to me forever." She extended her hand—scarred, blunt-fingered, steady. "Smuggler's oath on all three?"

"Do smugglers swear oaths?"

"We swear the only oaths that get kept. The ones with teeth." Vess waited.

Hazel took the hand. Vess's grip was dry and strong and final, and the mountain hummed its note through the stone beneath their feet, and somewhere far above, Hazel knew, the fox was running, and the birds were flying south, and the crown was doing arithmetic of its own.

"Witnessed," Vess said, and the watching shapes in the upper tiers murmured the word down through the tiers like a tide. "Nine days. We move in four—you'll need three to learn the first descent, and the road shifts slowest early in a waking. Eat. Sleep. Then come to the keyhole gallery and bring your gift with you, because starting today, girl, you stop hiding it from my people." She turned away, then paused. "One more thing. Since we're trading true prices."

Hazel waited.

"Fifteen years ago, in the famine year, I sold something to the court too." Vess's voice didn't change at all, which was how Hazel knew it cost her everything. "I've never said that sentence out loud. When we get down there—if there's anything of mine among the kept things—you'll stand beside me and you won't ask what it was." The salt-grey eyes came up and held Hazel's. "Because if I'm walking you to your brother, you're walking me to mine."

Down in the hanging dark of the pawnshop cavern, thousands of tokens turned on their hair-thin cords, ringing their soundless notes, and Hazel felt—distinctly, unmistakably—one of them chime louder than the rest, like a bell rung through stone, like a name spoken in a dream.

Like something below, hearing hers.
