## 8. First Prayer Answered

The report Elias Corven wrote in the chapel at Ashen Hollow was the first lie of his career, and it had been easy. That frightened him more than the burning had.

*Structure destroyed by lightning strike. No cultic activity found. Village dispersed of its own accord.* He'd signed it with his hunter's mark pressed in black wax, and the wax had still been warm when he rode out, and no one in the garrison at Thornfell Gate had looked at him twice.

A good man's first lie should cost something. His hadn't. It had felt like setting down a pack he'd forgotten he was carrying.

Three weeks later, he understood why.

* * *

The valley below Rooksbridge was called Merrow's Rest, and it was dying the way valley villages died—slowly, then all at once. The wheat had come up gray two seasons running. The millpond froze in high summer and steamed in winter. Half the houses stood empty, doors swinging, their owners gone downriver to the crown towns where there was bread that wasn't gray and work that wasn't prayer.

Elias rode into it on a wet Tuesday with a warrant in his saddlebag and orders to assess "anomalous devotional activity." Which was the Office's way of saying: someone is praying to the wrong gods, find out who, and prepare the pyre.

He found the village nearly empty and the remaining villagers nearly silent. They watched him from doorways with the flat look of people who had already lost everything worth taking. An old man mended a net that would never see water again. A woman beat dust from a rug no one would walk on. Nobody ran. Nobody lied to him. That was the worst part—their honesty, offered like the last coins of a beggar who knows you'll take them anyway.

"You're one of them," said the old man with the net, when Elias finally asked. Not hostile. Just tired. "One of the god-hunters."

"I'm an investigator."

"Ah." The old man smiled without any humor in it. "That's what they called them last time, too. Before my grandmother's time. Investigators." He went back to his net.

Elias should have pressed. Should have asked names, taken statements, marked the houses where the praying happened. He did none of it. He stabled his horse at the empty inn, paid for nothing because there was no one to pay, and sat by a cold hearth telling himself he was waiting for more information.

He was waiting for something else, and he knew it, and he let himself not know it.

* * *

The child's name was Wren.

He learned it the night she started dying, because her mother screamed it into the dark like a name could hold a soul in a body if only it were shouted loudly enough.

Elias had taken a room above the inn's taproom—not that anyone collected for it—and the screaming woke him near midnight. He came down the stairs with his hand on his knife out of habit, and stopped in the doorway, and forgot the knife entirely.

The taproom was lit by a single candle. A woman knelt on the floorboards beside a small bed dragged down from upstairs, and on the bed lay a girl of perhaps six, gray as the wheat, gray as the pond ice, her breath coming in a thin wet whistle that Elias recognized because he'd heard it before—in his sister, in the fever year, the winter the Office had taught him that prayer was theft and medicine was suspicion and both were crimes against the crown's order.

The mother wasn't looking at him. She was looking past him, past the walls, past everything, at some point in the air where maybe she thought a god might be standing.

"Please," she said. Not to Elias. To the air. "She's all I have left. Everyone else has gone downriver or into the ground. Please. I know you're waking. I've felt it under the floor. I heard the foxes singing in the fall and I know what that means, I know what my grandmother told me it means. I don't care what the crown says. I don't care if you take my voice for the payment. Take anything of mine. Just not her breath. Not her breath."

Elias stood in the doorway of a crime in progress. That was what the training said: unlicensed invocation, appeal to pre-Covenant powers, the old gods, the dead gods, the gods the crown had starved for three hundred years so that its own order would be the only thing left to kneel to. Every word out of that woman's mouth was a hanging offense. He had a warrant. He had a duty. He had a pyre to prepare.

He didn't move.

The candle flame leaned sideways, though there was no draft.

And the room warmed.

It was not dramatic. That was what undid him, afterward—it was not thunder, not light, not any of the theater the Office's histories described when they described the old days of wonders and why they had to be ended. It was simply warmth, spreading through the taproom like an oven door opened in the next room, like sun through south glass, like every good memory of hearth and home arriving at once and settling into the boards. The candle stood upright again. The whistle in the girl's chest softened. Softened again. Stopped—and then resumed, deeper, cleaner, the wet rattle gone as though someone had wrung the sickness out of her lungs like water from cloth.

The girl opened her eyes. She looked at her mother. She said, with the aggrieved clarity of a six-year-old: "I'm hungry."

Her mother made a sound Elias had never heard a human being make—a sound that was grief and joy and three years of held breath leaving the body all at once—and gathered the child up, and the two of them wept into each other's hair while the candle burned steady and the warmth slowly drained back out of the room like a tide going out, polite about it, unhurried, gone.

In the corner by the cold hearth, where no one had put anything, a loaf of bread sat on the stones. Still warm.

Elias looked at it for a long time.

Then he turned around, climbed the stairs, and sat on the edge of the inn bed until dawn, listening to the mother and daughter breathing in the room below, alive, alive, alive, and trying to find in himself the shape of the man who was supposed to burn this house down.

He couldn't find him. He looked everywhere. The man was gone, or had never quite existed, or had been hollow all along—a warrant walking around wearing a person's face.

* * *

The crown's answer came four days later, and it came marching.

Elias was in the village square buying eggs—he'd insisted on paying, over the mother's protests, because he needed the transaction to be ordinary, needed to stand in the morning light and watch a healed child chase a cat and believe the world had not changed—when the column came down the river road.

He counted forty before he stopped counting. Inquisitors in slate-gray, hoods back, faces bare and unashamed, which was new; the Office had always preferred to work quietly, in the dark, with warrants and whispers. This was different. This was display. At the head of the column rode a woman on a white mule, and even at distance Elias knew the silhouette, because everyone in the Office knew it: Mother Serane, chief inquisitor of the Thornfell March, who had once, the story went, burned a shrine so thoroughly that the ground refused to grow grass for a decade.

Behind the riders came wagons. Covered wagons, heavy-axled, drawn by ox teams, the kind used for ore.

Not for prisoners. For cargo.

Serane reined up in the square and looked at the village the way a merchant looks at a scale—checking the weight of it, deciding what it would bear. Her gaze crossed Elias, noted his hunter's badge, moved on. He was beneath her notice, or she was saving him for later. With Serane it was always later. Later was where she kept everything.

"People of Merrow's Rest," she said, and her voice carried the way voices carry when they've been trained on congregations, "the crown has heard your prayers."

Nobody spoke. The healed girl's mother went white and pulled Wren behind her skirt.

"The crown is merciful," Serane continued. "The crown does not punish the desperate. It corrects them." She dismounted, unhurried, and walked to the well at the square's center, and laid one gloved hand on the well-stone, and Elias saw the villagers flinch as one body, as though she'd touched each of them. "There are those among you who have appealed to dead things. You will tell me who. Not because I require confession—" she smiled, and it was the gentlest thing Elias had ever seen and it made his skin crawl off his bones, "—but because confession is the beginning of correction, and correction is love."

The old man with the net stepped forward. Elias didn't know when he'd arrived; he seemed to simply be there, standing between Serane and the mother and child, holding his useless net like a shepherd's crook.

"We'll tell you nothing," he said. "You're too late anyway. Whatever you've come to snuff out, it's already awake. You can't arrest the spring, woman."

"No," Serane agreed pleasantly. "But you can harvest it."

Something in the way she said *harvest* snagged in Elias's mind and would not let go. Harvest was not a word inquisitors used. Harvest was a word for crops, for ore, for whales. He looked at the covered wagons again, at their heavy axles, and thought of the Office archives he'd spent five years cataloguing—the sealed annexes, the ledgers written in a cipher only senior hunters could read, the requisitions for "reliquary materials" that no reliquary he'd ever seen had contained.

He thought of Ashen Hollow. Of the chapel he'd burned, and how the fire had burned strangely, greedily, the flames leaning toward the altar instead of away from it, drinking something.

He thought: *they aren't hunting the gods. They're collecting them.*

The realization arrived whole, the way ice breaks—no cracking, no warning, just the sudden fact of open water where solid ground had been. Three hundred years of witch-hunting, of pyres and purges and the Covenant that forbade the old names, and underneath all of it, all along, the crown had not been afraid of the gods returning.

It had been farming them. Starving the field so the harvest, when it came, would be theirs alone.

And now the field was waking on its own, ahead of schedule, in gray-wheat valleys and empty chapels and the prayers of a mother over a dying child—and the crown had sent wagons.

* * *

He packed that night. He was surprised how little there was to pack. Five years of service fit into one saddlebag: clothes, a whetstone, his mother's ring, a book of Office doctrine he decided at the last moment to leave on the inn table, open, like an apology to whoever read it next.

The warrant he almost left too. It sat on the table beside the book—black wax, silver seal, the words *By authority of the Crown, the bearer is licensed to investigate, detain, and execute*—and he looked at it the way you look at a scar, tracing the shape of the wound that made you.

Every road he'd ever walked had been built on that paper. Every door had opened. Every lie he'd told himself—that the burning was necessary, that order was worth the ash, that a man could carry fire and keep clean hands—had been possible only because the paper said he could.

If he left it, he was nobody. A deserter with a sword and a bad conscience, hunted by his own former brothers, worth nothing to anyone.

If he took it—

He took it.

Because a warrant, he realized, folding it into his breast pocket, was not loyalty to the crown. It was access. And somewhere in those sealed annexes, in those ciphered ledgers, in the wagons rolling toward the mountain with their heavy ore-axles, was the truth of what the crown had been doing for three hundred years—and the only people who could walk into an Office archive without being shot were men carrying warrants.

Let them hunt a deserter. Let them hang him twice over. But he would walk through their front door wearing their own seal, and he would read every page, and then he would decide whose side the fire was actually on.

Before he left, he stopped at the mother's door. She opened it before he knocked, Wren peeking around her hip, healthy and round-cheeked and furious about bedtime.

"They'll ask who prayed," Elias said quietly. "Give them me. Say a hunter was here, say he questioned you, say whatever puts your name last on her list."

"And what will it put on yours?"

"Nothing I haven't earned." He hesitated. Then: "The thing that answered you. Did it feel like a plague to you?"

The mother looked at him for a long moment, and something in her face was older than her years and gentler than her fear. "It felt," she said, "like being allowed."

He nodded once, and turned to go, and she caught his sleeve.

"Hunter. The foxes were singing in the fall. My grandmother said that meant the speaker was coming—the one who'd call the old ones home." Her grip tightened. "If you find her before they do—"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to. He'd read the same fragmentary reports she clearly had: rumors out of the deep Thornfell, a beast-tongue, a guide who walked roads that weren't there. The Office had a file. The file had a name half-legible in the margin: *Villalobos?*

"I'll find her," Elias said. "I think I already have."

He rode north that night, toward the mountains, toward the sealed archives he meant to rob and the hunter's office he meant to betray and the woman whose debt he'd been carrying since a rockslide in another life. Behind him, Merrow's Rest slept with its windows lit for the first time in years, and ahead of him the wagons' ruts cut fresh mud toward the peak where Mother Serane was already building something.

Duty broke in him cleanly, like ice under a boot—one moment solid, the next open water—and he found, riding hard into the dark with a stolen warrant over his heart, that he did not miss the footing at all.
