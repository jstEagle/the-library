## 9. Beasts of the Long Winter

The wolves came when Hazel called them, and that was the whole horror of it.

She had expected resistance. She had expected the old friction, the way wild things always made her pay for the asking—a snarl first, a test, a price. But the wolves of the high Thornfell came down through the snow-pines like water finding a low place, six of them, then nine, gray shapes moving with the particular silence of animals who have decided something. They circled her once at the tree line, breath steaming, yellow eyes patient.

*Well?* said the oldest, a bitch with a torn ear and frost in her ruff. *You called. We came. What do you want?*

Hazel had spent twenty-six years making sure no animal ever asked her that question so directly. She looked at Vess Andrade, standing ten feet away with her arms crossed and her breath smoking, watching Hazel the way a moneylender watches a signature dry.

"You see?" Vess said. "It's like opening a door."

"It's like begging," Hazel said.

"Everything is begging. The trick is knowing who owes you." Vess nodded at the wolves. "They owe you. I watched you pull a thorn from that big male's paw two winters back, up by the scree. Don't look surprised—my people watch everything on my roads. You've been spending kindness all over this mountain for years, girl, and now it's time to collect."

The torn-eared wolf sat down in the snow and waited. That was the worst part, Hazel thought. Not the teeth. The patience. As if they had always known what she was, and had simply been courteous enough to let her pretend.

"The convoy," Hazel said, because there was no way back through the sentence now. "Crown tax wagons, coming up the salt road from Ferrow's Crossing. Twelve soldiers, maybe fourteen. Two drivers. You're to drive them off the road—not kill them—and keep them scattered until the mountain does its work. Then you come home."

*And if the mountain kills them?* the wolf asked.

"They won't die," Hazel said. "Drive them. Only drive them."

The wolf looked at her for a long moment, and Hazel understood, with a lurch, that the animal was not agreeing. It was granting her the fiction she needed. Wolves knew exactly what happened to men scattered alone in a Thornfell winter. They had been doing it longer than crowns existed.

*We will drive them,* the wolf said. *You taught us mercy is a thing with edges. Come see.*

Then they turned as one and flowed uphill into the dark, and Hazel stood in the falling snow feeling the shape of what she'd just done settle onto her shoulders like a yoke.

Vess whistled softly. "There it is," she said. "First time calling something that big?"

"First time calling anything to hurt someone."

"Hurt's a strong word for scattering." Vess pulled her fox-fur hood up. "Come on. We'll watch from the ridge. If you're going to spend yourself, you should at least count the coin."

* * *

They watched from a granite shelf above the salt road, two hundred yards of white silence between them and the convoy's route, close enough to hear the wagon-creak and the mules complaining and the soldiers' boots crunching in the packed snow. Hazel had never watched a convoy she was robbing before. For eleven years she had guided smugglers past crown patrols, and the whole craft of it—the entire architecture of her life—had been built on one principle: *no one gets hurt.* Roads that didn't exist. Passes only a fox could find. She moved people around danger the way water moves around stone, and nobody bled, and that was how she'd told herself she was different from every other criminal between here and the capital.

Now she lay on cold rock with her chin on her forearms and waited for wolves she had personally invited to ruin fourteen strangers' lives.

"There," Vess said.

The convoy came around the shoulder of the mountain in a line of lantern-light: four wagons heavy with grain-tax and salt-tax and the tithes of villages that would go hungry paying them, flanked by soldiers in crown-gray with their halberds slung and their collars up. Young, most of them. Hazel could tell even at this distance—young men walk with their weight too far forward, like they haven't yet learned the mountain doesn't care about them.

One of them was singing. A marching song, tuneless, half-swallowed by wind. The sound carried up the ridge and broke against Hazel's ribs.

*Now,* she sent, down the thread that connected her to the pack, the thread that felt less like speech every time she used it and more like pulling a rope through her own chest. *Now, now, the left flank, the treeline, eyes and teeth but no blood—*

The wolves hit the convoy like weather.

That was the thing no one told you about wolves: they didn't attack like animals in a story, all snarling heroics. They attacked like a storm front. One moment the road was lanterns and singing; the next it was screaming mules and a lantern smashing in the snow and gray shapes everywhere at once, inside the column, between the men, gone again before a halberd could swing. The wolves did exactly what Hazel had asked. No blood. Teeth closing on sleeves and thighs and one unfortunate man's collar, hauling, herding, driving the soldiers off the road and apart, splitting fourteen into clusters of three and two and one, pushing them uphill into the trees, downhill toward the river-cut, anywhere but together.

It took ninety seconds. Hazel counted without meaning to. Ninety seconds, and a disciplined crown column ceased to exist, and the tax wagons stood empty and rocking on the salt road with their lanterns guttering, and Vess's crew came out of the rocks below like ants to strip them to the axles.

"Clean," Vess said beside her, satisfied. "Not a drop spilled. You're a marvel, Villalobos. My grandmother would have made you an offering."

Hazel wasn't listening. She was counting.

Three soldiers driven east into the pines—pines that went down to the river-cut, where a man could follow the ice shelf to Ferrow's Crossing if he kept his head. Fine. Four driven west, uphill—harder country, but they had the slope behind them and the garrison fort was only six miles as the crow flew. Fine. She tracked each cluster, ticking them off in her head, telling herself the story: *driven, not killed; scattered, not slaughtered; they'll be cold and humiliated and alive.*

Two she couldn't find.

"Where are the last two?" she asked.

Vess shrugged. "Does it matter?"

"Yes."

"Girl." Vess's voice gentled in a way that was worse than cruelty. "Fourteen men came to take bread out of hungry mouths. Some of them are going to have a bad night. That's the arithmetic. You don't get to do a thing like this and also get to not know the numbers."

"I'm not trying not to know the numbers. I'm trying to find the numbers." Hazel was already moving, sliding down the scree toward the road, and behind her she heard Vess mutter something that might have been a prayer to a dead god or a curse on sentimental women.

*Find them,* she sent to the pack. *The two you lost. Where?*

*We did not lose them,* the torn-eared wolf answered, from somewhere upslope. *We drove them where they drove themselves. Men run strange, when they are afraid. One ran downhill. One ran along the river.*

Hazel found the first one at the river-cut.

He had gone onto the ice shelf—she could see why, from above; it looked like a road, smooth and moonlit, leading away—but the shelf ended forty yards out at open water where the current kept the river breathing, and beyond the open water the far bank rose in a cliff of black stone with no handholds a freezing man could use. He was standing at the edge of the open water when she saw him, haloed in his own lantern light, and he was not moving. Just standing. Looking at the water. Looking at the cliff. Doing the arithmetic Vess had talked about, and arriving at the sum.

"Hey!" Hazel shouted, and slid down the bank. "Hey—soldier—the ice holds near the shore, come back the way you came—"

He turned. He was very young. Nineteen, maybe, cheeks already gray with cold, and he looked at her with the flat incomprehension of a man whose world has stopped making sense. "Wolves," he said. "There were wolves."

"There were. They're gone. Walk back along the shore, there's a game trail at the bend, it goes to the crossing—"

He took one step toward her voice. The ice spoke—a sound like a giant clearing its throat—and a crack ran out from under his boot across the moonlit shelf, quick as writing.

He didn't fall through. That was the detail Hazel would carry for the rest of her life, the detail that made it worse: he didn't fall through. He froze standing up, more or less, kneeling by the end, one hand still stretched toward the shore and her voice, and the cold finished him in minutes, because the wind off the open water was a knife and his clothes were soaked to the shoulder where a wolf had knocked him down at the road's edge, and wet wool in a Thornfell wind is a clock running fast. Hazel got within thirty feet of him. She shouted herself hoarse. She threw him a rope from the game trail, coiled, weighted, and it fell short and the wind took it sideways and he was past answering by then anyway, past hearing, kneeling in the posture of a man praying to the wrong god.

When she finally reached him—by going out on the shelf herself, flat on her belly, distributing her weight the way she'd seen river-people do in another life—he was a statue in a soldier's coat. His name, when she went through his pockets for something to tell his mother, was written on a folded leave-pass: *Corporal Ansel Rook, second battalion, granted leave to attend his sister's wedding, month of Deepfrost.*

He had been going home in three weeks.

* * *

Vess's crew burned the wagons at dawn and were gone before full light, twelve thousand pounds of crown grain and salt redistributed along roads that didn't exist, and the smuggler-queen counted her takings with bright, ruthless satisfaction and said nothing about the soldier until they were back in the salt caves and the fires were lit.

"He froze," Hazel said. She was sitting by the fire with Corporal Rook's leave-pass in her hands. She had carried it thirty miles to give it to Vess, though she couldn't have said why. Proof, maybe. A receipt. "I called the wolves. I told them to scatter them. I stood thirty feet away and I watched the mountain finish what I started, and I want you to say it plainly, because everyone in this cave keeps saying *scattered* and *a bad night* like words can launder it."

Vess crouched across the fire from her. Up close, in firelight, the smuggler-queen's face was older than her reputation—lined around the eyes, seamed by cave-dust and salt wind, the face of a woman who had done her own counting for years.

"Plainly, then," Vess said. "A boy died tonight because you pointed hunger at the crown and the crown pointed him at the mountain. Also plainly: fourteen soldiers came to take the harvest from villages eating bark-bread, and if your wolves hadn't stopped them, those villages would have buried more than one boy this winter—just slower, and poorer, and you'd never have had to watch. That's the trade. Nobody sells you a clean one."

"That's not a trade. That's a bill."

"Same thing, most days." Vess held out her hand. Hazel looked at it, then gave her the leave-pass. Vess read it by firelight, and something crossed her face—quick, quickly buried—that Hazel almost missed. "Ansel Rook," she read. "Sister's wedding." She folded it away in her coat, and Hazel knew she would never see it again, and knew also that Vess would keep it, somewhere, the way she seemed to keep everything that hurt. "You'll want to quit now. Say it."

"I want to quit every hour."

"And you won't. Because there's a court under this mountain holding your brother's soul, and the only map to it runs through me, and the price of the map is that you stop being a woman who hides and become a woman who acts. I told you that price in the salt market. You paid it tonight. First payment always hurts most." Vess stood, and her shadow climbed the cave wall, huge and wavering. "Here's what nobody tells you about power, beast-speaker. It isn't a gift. It's tuition. Every time you use it, you learn something true about the world, and the lessons cost blood—sometimes yours, sometimes a stranger's. Tonight's lesson: the mountain doesn't care about your intentions. Call a storm, own the drowning. Call wolves, own the freezing. There is no calling that comes with clean hands."

Hazel stared into the fire. Somewhere deeper in the caves, water dripped in slow rhythm, the mountain's own pulse. She thought about Tomas, fifteen years gone, a kept thing polishing thrones in the dark, and about how she had sworn on his name that she would cross any distance, pay any price. She had imagined prices like gold, like secrets, like years. She had not imagined a nineteen-year-old corporal kneeling on the ice with his hand stretched toward her voice.

"I'll keep the bargain," she said.

"I know," Vess said. "That's what frightens me about you."

* * *

She dreamed of the door under the mountain that night, in the bunk Vess's crew had given her, and in the dream the door was open a crack, and through the crack came not her brother's voice this time but a sound like a bell being struck very slowly, deep under stone, a tolling that she felt in her teeth more than heard.

And beneath the bell, faintly, a voice that was not her brother's either—older, vaster, amused and sorrowful at once:

*You have begun,* it said. *Good. Now they have begun too. Listen, little speaker. Something is climbing the mountain with a bell in its hands, and the bell is not meant to silence us.*

*It is meant to harvest us.*

Hazel woke with frost on her eyelashes and the dream tolling still in her skull, and outside the salt caves the wind had changed direction, and far off to the north, at the edge of hearing, at the edge of sense, something metallic rang once against the silence and did not stop.
