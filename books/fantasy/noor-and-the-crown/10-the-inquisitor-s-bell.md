## 10. The Inquisitor's Bell

The bell came up the mountain road on a wagon drawn by six white oxen, and the oxen were blindfolded.

Elias watched from the ridge above the switchbacks, half-hidden in the gray scrub, and understood the blindfolds before he understood anything else. You didn't blindfold draft animals to spare them. You did it because they could see something you didn't want them looking at. Because whatever sat under the canvas tarp in that wagon bed was the kind of thing that made honest beasts refuse.

He had been a witch-hunter for eleven years. He knew what the crown's processions looked like—the black lacquer, the silver trim, the banners of the Vigilant Eye snapping in the cold. This was not that. This was six wagons and forty soldiers moving without banners, without hymns, without the theatrical dread Serane's office usually wore like perfume. They came up the Thornfell road quiet as debt collectors, and that frightened him more than any procession ever had.

At the head of the column rode a woman on a gray mare, and even at this distance Elias knew her by her stillness. Mother Serane did not ride so much as allow the horse to carry her. Her hands stayed folded on the pommel. Her face, when the morning light caught it, was the face of a woman doing arithmetic.

*She knows,* Elias thought. *She already knows more than she's told me.*

Which meant his false report about the chapel—filed three weeks ago, neat as a lie could be—had not fooled her. It had merely been noted. Filed away in whatever ledger she kept behind those folded hands.

He pulled back from the ridge before the outriders crested it. His heart was doing something strange—not pounding, but knocking, slow and deliberate, like someone asking to be let in.

* * *

They made camp in the ruins of Fort Brannoc, the old crown garrison above the valley, abandoned since before Elias was born. He watched from the treeline as the soldiers unloaded the wagons with a care usually reserved for explosives or relics. The tarps came off the last wagon at dusk, and even across half a mile of cold air, Elias felt it.

The bell.

It was smaller than he expected—no cathedral monster, no great bronze throat to swallow a valley. It hung from a simple frame like a chapel bell in some poor parish, perhaps four feet tall, and its metal was wrong. Not bronze, not iron, not any alloy he'd seen poured in the crown foundries. It was dark, almost black, with a faint oily sheen, and where the firelight touched it, the surface seemed to move, very slowly, like something thinking.

And it hummed.

Not a sound. Elias realized after a long moment that he wasn't hearing it with his ears. He was feeling it in his back teeth, in the marrow of his forearms, in the old scar tissue along his ribs where the rockslide had nearly ended him years ago—a low, patient vibration, like a note held by a throat that never needed breath.

He knew that hum.

He had felt it once before, three weeks ago, standing in an empty chapel in a village that should have been starving, watching bread appear on an altar for people praying to a dead god. No—that wasn't right either. The chapel hadn't hummed. But the power in it, the thing that filled the bread and warmed the stones, had carried the same signature. The same deep frequency. Like recognizing a voice through a wall.

Elias crouched in the snowmelt brush and let the truth assemble itself piece by piece, the way he'd been trained to assemble evidence, and hated every joint of it as it locked together.

The bell was forged from god-stuff.

Not consecrated metal. Not blessed. *Harvested.* Somewhere, sometime, the crown had gotten hold of a piece of the divine—of the old gods themselves, or what remained of them beneath the Thornfell—and rendered it down, and cast it, and hung it in a frame, and brought it here.

And if the crown had one piece, it had more. If the crown had one bell, it had a forge. If the crown had a forge—

His orders, unrolled in his memory: *Find the beast-tongue. Silence the speakers. Prepare the valley for the Vigilance.*

Silence the speakers. He'd read that phrase a hundred times over eleven years and never once asked what it meant operationally. Now, feeling the bell's hum in his teeth, he understood. Speakers weren't to be killed first. They were to be *collected*. A speaker who could hear the gods could also be used to find them. To draw them. And a bell forged from god-stuff wouldn't just deafen—it would *pull*, the way a magnet pulls iron filings out of sand, dragging the waking power of the old gods up out of the mountain and into the crown's coffers.

The crown hadn't sent Serane to kill a plague of miracles.

The crown had sent her to reap one.

* * *

He got closer that night than he should have. That was the trouble with conscience; it made you reckless in exactly the ways training warned against.

The camp slept in watches. Serane kept no tent—he saw that much, and marveled at it. She sat beside the bell's frame on a folding chair, wrapped in a gray cloak, awake, her hands still folded, watching the dark metal the way a farmer watches a field two days from harvest. Two acolytes knelt behind her, heads bowed, and between them on a folding table lay documents. Elias worked his way down the slope and along the fort's collapsed wall until he was close enough—close enough to hear, close enough to see the wax seals, close enough to make out the crest pressed into the largest one.

Not the Vigilant Eye. Not the crown proper.

The seal of the Royal Treasury.

Documents about gods, sealed by accountants.

Serane spoke, softly, to the acolyte on her left. "Yield projections for the third ringing."

"Fourteen percent above the second, Mother. The resonance deepens as they wake. Each god that surfaces adds—" the young man paused, consulting a slate, "—roughly a tenth again to the draw."

"And the speakers?"

"Mapped. Eleven confirmed in the valley and foothills. The beast-tongue remains unlocated." A hesitation. "Though the animals continue to behave... anomalously. Southbound migrations reversing. Predators denning together. Whatever moves them is moving outward from the mountain's root."

Serane nodded slowly. "Then we ring early. Before the harvest ripens past our reach. Send to the capital tonight."

Elias lay flat against the frozen wall, breath shallow, and felt the last wall of his old life come down all at once, quietly, the way ice lets go of a branch.

Eleven years. Eleven years of burning chapels and filing reports and telling himself the Vigilance protected people from things that would eat them. And underneath it all, the whole time, the Office of the Vigilant Eye had been a harvesting operation. Every speaker silenced was a tap closed—or a tap identified. Every god hunted was inventory assessed. The crown didn't fear the old gods' return.

The crown had been *waiting* for it. For decades. Maybe centuries. The famine, the sold souls, the court beneath the mountain—all of it, perhaps, known. All of it, perhaps, *arranged*. A granary left to ripen.

And Hazel Villalobos—the woman who'd pulled him bleeding from a rockslide and asked nothing, the woman whose name sat in the archives attached to a soul-debt she didn't know she'd inherited—wasn't just a target.

She was a *yield*.

* * *

He went back down the mountain the way he'd come, faster than wisdom allowed, sliding twice on scree, arriving at the valley's edge with blood on his palms and the bell's hum still lodged in his bones like a splinter. The warrant in his breast pocket felt heavier than it ever had—a sheet of vellum, a seal, a license to do exactly what he was about to betray.

There was no plan anymore. There was only the next true thing.

The fox found him at the shrine stone below the tree line, the way it seemed to find everything now. It sat in the snow ten feet away, amber-eyed, tail curled over its feet, utterly unafraid of a man in hunter's black. Elias had watched this animal carry messages before—had learned, these past weeks, that the wild held a postal service older than any crown—and he knelt in the snow and spoke to it as though it were a colleague, because at this point it was the only institution he still trusted.

"I need Hazel Villalobos," he said. "Tell her this. Tell her exactly."

The fox tilted its head.

"The crown has a bell. Black metal, humming, forged from the same stuff as the power under the mountain—they've cut a piece of the gods out and hammered it into a weapon. It doesn't silence. It *harvests*. Serane means to ring it within days, maybe sooner, and when she does, it will drag the waking gods up out of the court and pour them into the treasury's coffers—and everyone bound to them, every soul the gods hold, goes into the scales with them." His voice cracked; he steadied it. "Including her brother. Tell her the debt her father signed isn't just a debt anymore. It's collateral. And the crown has come to collect."

The fox stood. For a moment it regarded him with an expression Elias would have sworn, in any other life, was pity.

Then it turned and ran north along the tree line, quick and low, a streak of rust against the snow, carrying his treason in its teeth.

Elias remained kneeling at the shrine stone long after the animal vanished. Above him, at Fort Brannoc, the first pale edge of dawn found the bell's frame, and the dark metal caught the light and moved, slowly, like something dreaming of ringing.

He put his hand in his pocket and closed it around his warrant. Eleven years ago, a clerk in the capital had pressed a hot seal into that vellum and told him he now carried the crown's justice in the wild places. He had believed it. He had built a life on it.

Tomorrow, or the day after, Serane would ring the bell. And everything Elias had failed to protect—the chapel village, the bread, the child healed by prayer, the whole waking valley—would be dragged up the mountain's throat and weighed and sold.

Unless someone broke it first.

He looked at his hands—hunter's hands, scarred, steady—and for the first time in eleven years, they were not trembling.

They were deciding.
