## 11. Down the Salt Roads

**Hazel**

The salt caves began where the world's skin ended.

Hazel had known that in the way she knew most things—through her feet, through the faint tremor of stone under boot leather. But knowing it from above and standing at its mouth were two different hungers entirely. The entrance wasn't a cave so much as a wound: a seam in the mountainside wide enough to swallow a wagon, breathing out air that tasted of old brine and something older still, something like the inside of a bell.

Vess Andrade stood at the threshold with a lantern held high, and the light did not go far. It fell into the dark and simply stopped, as if the dark had eaten it.

"Salt roads," Vess said, to no one in particular. "Every kingdom has one road it doesn't put on maps. Ours happens to go down."

Behind Hazel, Elias Corven shifted his weight. He'd been quiet since they'd left the treeline—quiet the way men are quiet when they've burned their last bridge and can hear it crackling behind them. His hunter's warrant sat against his chest under his coat, and Hazel had noticed him touching it every few minutes, like a man checking whether a wound was still bleeding.

"You don't have to come," Hazel said to him, low.

"I owe you my life twice over." He didn't look at her. "Third time's not a debt anymore. It's a habit."

The fox sat at Hazel's heel, ears forward, tail wrapped over its paws. It had led them here without being asked, trotting ahead on the smuggler trails until the trees gave out, then sitting down at this wound in the mountain and waiting. Animals didn't come into the salt roads. Hazel could feel that in the fox's reluctance—a tightness in its small body, a wish to be anywhere else.

"It won't follow me down," she said.

"No beast will." Vess spat, ceremonially, into the dark. "That's how you know you're going somewhere the gods still own."

* * *

**Elias**

The first day underground, Elias counted his sins instead of his steps.

It was an old habit from the seminary, before the hunters' office got hold of him: catalog the failings nightly, confess weekly, carry the ledger honestly. He'd stopped believing in the ledger years ago—the office taught you that a clean conscience was a luxury for people who never read their own orders—but the habit of counting remained, and down here in the salt dark it kept him sane.

Sin the first: he had burned a chapel that deserved better. Sin the second: he had lied in a crown report, which was treason with paperwork. Sin the third, largest, still warm: he had deserted, taken his warrant, and walked out of the hunters' garrison past men who would hang him if the mountain didn't kill them all first.

And yet.

He looked at Hazel ahead of him, moving through the dark with that uncanny sureness, one hand trailing the salt wall like she was reading it. She hadn't asked him about the chapel. She hadn't asked what he'd seen there, or why a witch-hunter's report had described a village praying to nothing and receiving bread anyway. She just walked, and let him keep his silences, and somehow that was worse than interrogation.

"The walls are wrong," he said finally, because silence was becoming unbearable.

"They're not wrong," Hazel said. "They're listening."

He touched the salt. It was warm. Underground, in winter, in a place no sun had ever reached, the walls were warm as a sleeping animal's flank.

"That should frighten you," Hazel said, without turning around.

"It does."

"Good. Frightened men check their footing." She glanced back, and in the lantern light her face was half shadow, half something harder than shadow. "It's the comfortable ones who walk off cliffs."

* * *

**Vess**

Vess Andrade had run these roads since she was nine years old, and she knew them the way other women knew their own hands.

Which was why the wrongness made her furious.

The salt roads obeyed rules. That was their whole religion: three turns left at the Weeping Column, count two hundred paces past the drowned stair, mark your passage with chalk and trust the chalk. Smugglers survived because the deep places were honest even when kings weren't. You paid attention, you marked your road, and the mountain let you pass.

Except now the chalk marks were migrating.

She found her own mark—an X scratched at knee height, hers, cut with her own knife two winters back—on the *opposite wall*. Then she found it again forty paces later, higher up, tilted, as if someone had picked up the tunnel and shaken it.

"Roads are shifting," she announced, and heard her crew murmur behind her. "Everyone rope up. Chalk every ten paces. If we get separated, you don't search—you sit, you sing, and you wait for me. The singing matters." She looked around at their faces, lantern-lit, nervous. "You sing because the things down here that move in the dark listen for breath. A man holding his breath sounds like prey. A man singing sounds like a guest."

"And what do we do," asked Doss, her second, a big gentle man with a voice like a landslide, "if we're not welcome?"

Vess smiled the smile she used on tax collectors and rival captains. "Then we sing louder."

But privately, walking point with her knife drawn and her heart doing arithmetic, she admitted what she'd been refusing to admit since the valley: the roads weren't shifting randomly. They were shifting *toward* something. Every wrong turn, every migrated chalk mark, bent them gently southward and down. Toward the court. As if the mountain wanted them to arrive.

Vess had spent thirty years taking things from powerful people by pretending to be less clever than she was. She recognized the technique instantly.

She hated being on the receiving end of it.

* * *

**Hazel**

On the third day, the dreams started.

They came through the salt, up through her boots, into her sleep wherever sleep could find her—in the hollows between shifts, in the brief black hours when the party camped in a chamber wide as a cathedral and let the lanterns burn low. The dreams were not images. They were *directions*. A pull behind her sternum, southward and down, patient as water wearing stone.

And threaded through the pull, faint as a voice through a closed door, something that sounded almost like humming.

She woke once with her hand pressed flat against the salt floor and her lips moving, shaping words she hadn't chosen. Elias was watching her from across the dead fire, warrant in hand—not threatening, just holding it, the way a man holds a rail in rough water.

"What was I saying?" she asked.

"I couldn't tell." His voice was careful. "It sounded like a prayer. Or a list."

"A list of what?"

"I don't know." He paused. "Names, maybe. It sounded like names."

Hazel sat up slowly. Somewhere below them, behind miles of stone, her brother was polishing thrones for gods who slept between answers, and she had begun to suspect that the road down wasn't leading her to him so much as *tuning* her to him, the way you tune a string to its neighbor until both hum together.

"We're close enough now," she said, "that I should tell you both what I'm carrying."

* * *

**Vess**

They gathered in the chamber Vess privately called the Manger—a hollow of white salt shaped, disturbingly, like a cradle the size of a hill—and Hazel told them everything. The ledger. Her father. The famine year. Tomas sold to the court beneath for a harvest that never came, and the valley's prosperity built on a child's soul like a house built on a grave.

Vess listened with her arms crossed and her face doing nothing at all, which was how she listened when the thing being said was too large for her face to hold.

When Hazel finished, the silence rang.

"So," Vess said at last. "Your father sold your brother to buy the valley bread. And the crown's been ringing bells to steal the gods' power while the gods themselves wake up starving. And you want to walk into their court and rob them." She nodded slowly. "Good. Clean plan. I've robbed worse houses."

"Hazel," Elias said quietly, "you don't have to perform for us."

"I'm not performing." But her voice cracked on it, just slightly, and Vess saw it and decided—deliberately, the way she decided everything—to give something back.

"Fine. Fair's fair." She unslung her pack, took out the bottle of plum brandy she saved for funerals and betrayals, and poured four measures into tin cups. "Here's mine, then. Since we're telling truths down a hole in the world."

She drank, and told them.

"My daughter's name was Lira."

The words came out strange, like coins from a pocket that shouldn't hold them.

"She was born in the salt roads, actually. Not far from here. And one winter—I don't remember which winter, that's the whole cursed point—one winter she was gone. Not dead. Dead I could work with; dead has a body, dead has a grave, dead lets you grieve forward. No. She was *taken*, and what was left—" Vess's hand closed around the empty cup until the tin creaked. "What was left was a girl who grew up healthy and strong and could not remember why her mother cried whenever she looked at her. Because the gods took her *name*, understand? Not her life. Her name. The part of her that connected everything else. She sits in my camp right now, eating my food, calling me 'the captain,' and she cannot remember why she wakes some mornings with her face wet."

The chamber was very quiet.

"I told you I wanted the gods dead," Vess said. "That was true. But it wasn't the *reason*. The reason is I'm going down there to find whatever's left of my daughter's name, and I'm going to take it back if I have to pry it out of a god's teeth." She refilled her cup with a steady hand. "I forgot her myself, you know. That's the worst of it. There was a bargain—I know there was a bargain, I can feel the shape of it like a missing tooth—but I made it and I *forgot making it*, and my daughter paid the interest." She looked up, and for once there was no captain's smile anywhere on her face. "So no, Hazel Villalobos. I'm not guiding you for gold or grudge. I'm guiding you because you're the only soul in the Thornfell who talks to things older than kings, and I need someone who can make those things *listen*."

* * *

**Elias**

Elias turned his cup in his hands and understood that he was the only one at the fire who hadn't bled yet.

Hazel had lost a brother to the court. Vess had lost a daughter's name. Even the fox, sitting at the chamber's edge, refusing to come closer to the deep salt, had its debts—it had repaid Hazel's kindness with warnings, and warnings were all it could give.

And him? He had a warrant and a conscience and a talent for lying to his superiors. He had watched a dying child heal under a mother's prayer and marched inquisitors' colors through the valley afterward. He had burned an empty chapel and called it duty.

"Well," he said, when the silence had gone on long enough, "I suppose it's my turn."

He told them about the rockslide—how a girl with mud on her face had pulled him from under a ton of mountain fifteen years ago, before either of them knew what the other would become. He told them about the chapel, the bread, the orders, the lie. He told them about Mother Serane's bell, and the metal that hummed like the inside of this mountain, and what he suspected the crown had really been doing all these years while it preached against the old gods.

When he finished, Hazel was looking at him with an expression he couldn't name.

"You lied to save a village," she said. "And you're ashamed of it."

"I'm ashamed of how easy it was."

"That's not shame," Hazel said. "That's grief. They're neighbors, but they're not the same thing."

Down here, in the Manger, in the warm salt dark, Elias felt something shift in his chest that he would spend the rest of his life trying to describe. It was not absolution. It was lighter than that and heavier: it was being *counted*. Three damaged people and a fox who wouldn't cross the line, sitting in a hole in the world, each holding a piece of the same broken thing.

* * *

**Hazel**

Vess stood, and when Vess Andrade stood, rooms rearranged themselves around her.

"Enough truth," she said. "Truth without oath is just gossip. Down here, gossip gets you killed." She drew her knife—not the ceremonial one, the working one, the one with thirty years of salt roads in its edge—and laid it flat on the stone between them. "Smuggler's oaths. Old form. Older than the crown, probably older than the gods, though don't tell them I said so."

She explained it simply: three vows, spoken over the blade, sealed in blood or salt—your choice, and everyone chose salt, because blood invited things down here.

"First vow: no one leaves the road alone. Second vow: no one carries a secret that endangers the rest. Third vow—" and here Vess paused, and her voice went quieter, "—third vow is the one that costs. *What one gains, all gain. What one loses, all lose.* Shared purse, shared wounds, shared graves. Most crews swear the first two and skip the third." She looked at Hazel. "Most crews aren't going where we're going."

Hazel thought of her whole life spent keeping secrets—her gift folded up small, her name hidden, her kindness practiced in the dark so no one would see. The idea of vowing to be *known* was more frightening than the court beneath the mountain.

She laid her palm on the flat of the blade.

"I swear it," she said. "All three."

Elias swore next, his voice steady, his hand not quite. Then Doss and the six others of Vess's crew, one by one, palms on steel, salt scattered from a shared pouch, the words passing mouth to mouth like a flame down a line of candles.

When it was done, Vess retrieved her knife and grinned—the real grin, rare as a blue moon.

"There. Now you're not passengers. You're crew." She jerked her chin toward the southern passage, where the air moved with that faint bell-hum Hazel had been hearing in her sleep. "And crew moves out. The road's bending hard to port, and if my chalk is honest, we reach the outer door by tomorrow's dark."

They packed in silence, efficient, bound. Hazel took point with the fox's memory in her mind and the hum in her chest, and behind her came a disgraced witch-hunter, a smuggler-queen, and seven souls who had sworn to share each other's graves.

The passage narrowed. The warmth increased. And somewhere below, so faint it might have been her own pulse, Hazel heard it clearly for the first time:

Humming. A tune she knew.

A lullaby their mother had sung to two children, long ago, in a house that no longer stood.

Tomas was singing to himself in the dark, and the mountain was carrying his voice up the salt roads like a thread thrown down a well—and Hazel followed it, hand on the warm stone, into the throat of the world.
