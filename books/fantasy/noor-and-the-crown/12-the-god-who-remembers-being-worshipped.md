## 12. The God Who Remembers Being Worshipped

The salt roads went down the way grief goes down—not all at once, but in shelves.

Hazel felt each one. The staircases of white stone that Vess's crew had cut a century ago and then half-forgotten, so that every landing was a small argument with the mountain: here a wall that had been a floor, there a doorway that opened onto solid salt, polished smooth by hands that no longer existed. The air tasted of old tears. That wasn't poetry. Salt was tears; everyone knew it; and this deep, Hazel could taste how much of it had been wept out of people who came down these stairs carrying things they never brought back up.

"Roads are wrong," Vess said, for the third time in an hour.

She said it without alarm, which was worse than if she'd screamed it. Vess Andrade did not raise her voice for anything short of betrayal. She stood at the head of the column with her lantern held high, and her face—brown, seamed, younger than her hands—had gone still in the way it went still when she was doing arithmetic she didn't like.

"What do you mean, wrong?" Elias asked. He had his hunter's warrant out again, turning it over in his fingers like a coin he was deciding whether to spend. "Wrong how?"

"The pitch." Vess licked her thumb and pressed it to the wall, then to her tongue. "Salt remembers pressure. This shelf was cut under two hundred feet of stone. We're under six hundred, maybe more. Either the mountain is growing—" she shrugged, letting them finish the thought themselves, "—or the road is moving."

"Roads don't move," said one of her crew, a broad-shouldered woman called Ferro who had not spoken since they'd entered the caves.

"These do," Vess said. "When the gods dream, the deep roads rearrange themselves around whatever the dream is about. My mother told me. I thought she was drunk." A pause. "She might have been. She was usually right anyway."

Hazel kept one hand on the wall as they walked. It was habit from the passes above—always touch the stone, always let it know you're there—but down here the habit had teeth. Every time her palm met the salt, something on the other side of it seemed to lean back. Not hostile. Curious. Like pressing your hand against the flank of an enormous animal in the dark and feeling it turn its head toward the warmth.

The fox rode in her pack. It had refused to stay above. When they'd made camp at the cave mouth, it had simply walked into her bag and curled up against the bread, and when she'd tried to shoo it out, it had looked at her with those black-button eyes and said nothing, which from a fox was a kind of argument. Since then it had been silent, ears flat, and Hazel had learned in twenty years of listening to beasts that silence meant *I am afraid and I trust you to be afraid where I can see you.*

They stopped to eat on a landing where the ceiling opened into a hollow full of salt crystals as long as her forearm. They caught the lantern light and threw it back in slow colors, and for a moment the whole chamber looked like the inside of some vast sleeping throat lined with frozen fire.

Elias sat apart, as he had since the desertion. He ate mechanically, watching the dark behind them. "How far?" he asked.

Vess didn't answer him directly. She answered Hazel, which was its own message. "Two more shelves. Then the outer door."

"You've seen it?"

"No one's seen it and come back to sell the story." Vess tore her bread with her teeth. "But my grandmother drew maps. Maps of roads she claimed she'd walked in dreams. There's a door on every one of them, and beside the door, the same mark." She spat into her palm and pressed it flat against the crystal wall, and where her hand lifted, the salt had darkened—a spiral, tight as a snail shell, drawn in shadow beneath the surface. "My grandmother said the mark meant *knock politely.*"

"And if you don't knock politely?" Ferro asked.

"My grandmother also died alone in a salt cave with her boots on the wrong feet," Vess said. "So. Knock politely."

Hazel laughed before she could stop herself—one short, cracked sound—and Vess looked at her with something almost like approval. It was the first time Hazel had laughed in three days, and it hurt going down, like swallowing a stone.

Because she knew what waited behind the polite knock. Tomas. Fifteen years of polishing thrones in the dark, if the ledger told true. Fifteen years of being a kept thing while their father traded him away for wheat that never grew, and their mother went mad with a grief she couldn't name because the thing she grieved had been stolen so cleanly even the shape of the loss had been taken from her.

*You'll get him back,* Hazel told herself, the way you tell yourself the ice will hold.

The ice, in her experience, rarely cared what you told yourself.

* * *

They came to the last shelf near what Hazel's body insisted was midnight, though time below the mountain was a rumor. The stairs ended at a corridor so wide and so smooth that their footsteps didn't echo—they were simply absorbed, swallowed into walls of salt gone translucent with age, so that walking through it was like walking inside a candle flame.

And at the end of the corridor: smoke.

It hung in the air ahead of them, a column of grey no taller than a child, and it was not drifting. Smoke drifts. This held its shape the way a held breath holds its shape, and it was thin—thin the way a starving dog is thin, all outline and no substance, flickering at the edges as though it couldn't quite afford to keep existing and was doing it anyway out of stubbornness.

Everyone stopped. Even Vess. Especially Vess.

Hazel's gift woke in her chest like a struck bell.

She had spent her whole life hearing animals—their hungers, their fears, their small bright wants—and she had learned to translate them into human sense: a wolf's warning became a coldness on the neck, a horse's trust became warmth in the palms. But this was not an animal. This was older than animals, and when it noticed her noticing it, what poured into her was not hunger or fear or want.

It was *loneliness.* Ten thousand years of it, compressed into a single instant, the way a star collapses.

Hazel went to her knees. She didn't decide to. Her legs simply understood, before the rest of her did, what she was standing in front of.

"Hazel—" Elias had his knife out. Of course he did. He was a witch-hunter; his whole life was knives pointed at things like this.

"Don't," she said. Her voice came out scraped. "Elias. Put it away. Please."

He hesitated. Then, slowly, he sheathed it—not because he trusted the thing, she saw, but because he trusted her. That was new. That was terrifying. That was a debt she hadn't known she was taking on.

The smoke drifted closer. Close enough now that she could see it wasn't formless after all. There was a suggestion of a hearth in it—a low arch, a bed of coals that glowed and guttered and glowed again, too weak to warm anything. Around the arch, shapes moved in the smoke like shadows cast by a fire that had gone out years ago: the curve of a kettle. A stool. Small hands, maybe. Or paws. It kept trying to remember what it had been shaped by, and the memory kept slipping.

"It's a hearth-god," Hazel whispered. "A little one. A house god."

"I know what it is," Vess said quietly. "Every farmhouse in the valley used to have one. Before the crown's calendar, before the bell-towers. You fed it the first spoon of the pot and it kept your fire honest." Her voice had gone strange. "My mother fed ours every night of her life. I forgot. I actually *forgot* we ever did that."

"That's what they took," Elias said slowly. "Not the temples. The temples were just buildings. They took the—" he gestured, helpless, "—the first spoon of the pot."

The hearth-god turned toward Hazel. It had no eyes, but she felt seen anyway, seen all the way through, the way you feel seen by a dog who has known you since you were small.

And it spoke.

Not in words, exactly. In warmth—or the memory of warmth—shaped against her gift until her mind translated it whether she wanted it or not:

*You hear me.*

"Yes," Hazel said. Her mouth was dry as the road they'd walked. "Yes, I hear you."

*Nobody hears me.* The coals in its chest guttered. *For a long time, everybody heard me. Ten thousand years. First spoon of the pot. First word of the prayer. Little god, little god, keep the fire, keep the child, keep the winter out. I was small and I was loved and I was everywhere.* The smoke trembled. *Then they stopped. Not all at once. That's the cruel part. If they had stopped all at once, I would have died like a proper god, and death is only a door. But they stopped slowly. One farm at a time. One family deciding the crown's god was more respectable. And I got smaller, and I got hungry, and I could not tell anymore—*

It stopped. The silence it left was enormous.

*Could not tell what?* Hazel asked, inside herself, gently, the way she'd ask a wounded hawk.

*I cannot tell if they loved me,* the hearth-god said, *or if they only used me. Is a prayer love? Is feeding the fire love? Or is it payment? I have ten thousand years of prayers in me and I do not know a single one that was given freely. I only know they stopped. And now I am waking, and the others are waking, and we are coming up out of the dark, and we do not know if we are coming home or coming to collect a debt.*

Behind Hazel, Vess made a small sound. Hazel didn't look. Looking would have been mercy, and Vess would rather die than take mercy in front of her crew.

"They're waking angry," Elias said. It wasn't a question.

*Waking hungry,* the god corrected. *Anger needs strength. We have none. Only appetite, and the memory of being loved, and no way to tell the difference between the memory and the lie.* The smoke gathered itself, pulled inward, denser now, and Hazel felt the temperature of the conversation change. *But you. You hear me truly. Not the way the priests heard us—through bells and books and fear. You hear me the way the fox hears you. So I will make you an offer, beast-speaker, daughter-of-the-valley, carrier-of-a-name-that-rings-through-my-stone.*

Hazel's heart slammed. *Tomas.* It knew about Tomas. Of course it did. Everything down here knew everything down here.

*The outer door of the court stands behind me,* the god said. *I am the last servant of the threshold. I open it for whom I choose. I will open it for you—if you give me one true prayer.*

"A prayer," Hazel repeated. Her voice sounded very small in the huge white corridor.

*A true one. Not a bargain dressed as worship—I have heard ten thousand years of bargains, I know their smell. Not a plea for a favor. Those are transactions, and transactions are what emptied me. A true prayer. One. Given freely, wanting nothing back. Give me that, and I will know whether mortals are capable of it, and if you are capable of it, then perhaps my brothers and sisters can be woken gently instead of hungrily. Perhaps the valley survives what is coming. Perhaps—* the coals flared, briefly, desperately, *—perhaps I was loved after all, and it was worth it.*

The silence stretched.

"Well," Vess said flatly, into it. "That's a trap."

"It's not," Hazel said.

"It absolutely is. Nothing asks for something for free. I've run smuggler roads for thirty years; the free thing is always the most expensive thing on the menu."

"She's right that it costs," Elias said quietly. "But she's not right that it's a trap." He was looking at the hearth-god with an expression Hazel had never seen on him—hunger of a different kind, the hunger of a man who had spent his life burning prayers and was only now, standing in front of one of the prayed-to, understanding what he'd been burning. "It's asking for the one thing the crown taught us never to give. Something for nothing. Grace, my grandmother would have called it." His jaw worked. "I don't know if I remember how."

Hazel knelt there on the cold salt floor and tried to think of a prayer.

It should have been easy. She was drowning in wants. She wanted Tomas back whole. She wanted Vess's daughter found. She wanted the inquisitors' bell broken and Mother Serane at the bottom of a very deep hole. She wanted the winter to end, wanted her own fear to end, wanted to stop being invisible, wanted—

And there it was. Every single one of those was a transaction. *Give me, give me, give me.* She could feel the god tasting them as they rose in her, patient, sorrowful, already knowing. Ten thousand years of *give me.* The valley's whole history was *give me*—and look what the valley had done when the harvest failed. Look what her father had done. Traded a boy's soul for bread, because even their desperation had been a negotiation.

*One true prayer,* the god said. *Take your time. I have had a great deal of practice waiting.*

Hazel closed her eyes.

And what came up out of her—not chosen, not crafted, rising the way groundwater rises—was not for Tomas at all.

*"I'm sorry."*

Her own voice, aloud, cracking in the white corridor. She hadn't meant to speak it out loud. It kept going anyway.

*"I'm sorry I hid. My whole life, I hid—from the hunters, from the crews, from myself. I had a gift and I treated it like a disease. I heard the foxes and the stags and the crows and I pretended I was deaf, because listening felt like danger, and I told myself that was survival, and maybe it was, but—" her breath hitched, "—but there were nights, whole winters, when the mountains were talking to me and I put my hands over my ears. And you were in there. All of you. Waking, or almost waking, or calling, and I heard you, and I chose not to answer. Not once. Not ever. I chose silence every single day of my life because silence was safe."*

The words kept coming, faster now, ugly and true.

*"And I'm sorry. Not because it serves me to say it. Not because you'll open a door if I say it. I'm sorry because you were lonely down here and I was lonely up there and we were both hiding from the same people and I never once thought to hide with you instead of from you. I'm sorry I made you a secret. You were never a secret. You were a neighbor. And neighbors deserve better than ten thousand years of a girl pretending her house is empty."*

She ran out. She knelt in the salt dust with her fists against the floor and her breath ragged, and she felt stripped—more stripped than she had ever felt in front of any soldier or smuggler or god, because there was nothing in the prayer she wanted back. No door. No brother. Just the apology, sitting between her and the hearth-god like a bowl of water set out for a stray.

The silence went on so long she began to think she'd failed.

Then the coals in the little god's chest caught.

Not guttered—*caught*, flared from grey to orange to gold, and the smoke thickened, filled out, remembered itself. For one moment the arch of a hearth stood complete in the corridor: black iron, warm stone, a kettle beginning to sing. The smell of woodsmoke and baking bread rolled down ten thousand years and broke over Hazel like a wave, and it was her mother's kitchen, it was every kitchen, it was the first spoon of the pot offered to something small and unseen and beloved.

*Freely,* the god said, wondering. *Given freely. Wanting nothing.* The kettle sang. *Ten thousand years, and I had begun to believe it was impossible—that everything mortal was barter, that love was just a trade we agreed to call holy. And you kneel in the dirt and apologize to a dying ember for ignoring it.* The warmth washed over Hazel's face, and it was indistinguishable from weeping. *Little sister. You were never pretending your house was empty. You were keeping the door locked so well that you forgot there was anyone knocking.*

"I know," Hazel whispered.

*The door is yours.* Behind the hearth-god, where there had been only blank white salt, a seam of darkness opened—tall, narrow, ancient, edged with the same tight spiral Vess's grandmother had drawn. Cold air breathed out of it, smelling of stone and old gold and something else, something vast and sleeping and stirring. *Go and find your brother. But hear me before you go, beast-speaker, because what waits beyond my threshold is older and hungrier than I, and they have forgotten even the memory of the first spoon of the pot. Whatever they offer you—and they will offer you everything—remember what you just learned. Remember what you gave me. It is the only coin that spends down there.*

The hearth-god began to fade. Not dying—going home, or going to wherever a threshold god goes when its door is finally, after ten thousand years, opened for someone worthy. As it thinned, it leaned close, and its last warmth brushed Hazel's cheek like a hand.

*Tell them,* it said, *that someone said sorry. Tell them it counted.*

Then it was smoke, and then it was less than smoke, and then it was a faint smell of woodsmoke in a corridor of salt, and the dark door stood open, breathing cold.

Nobody moved for a moment.

Then Vess stepped forward, past Hazel, and stood looking into the dark with her lantern raised, and her face was wet and furious about being wet. "Right," she said, rough. "Knock politely. Apparently that works." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, hard, once. "Crew. Weapons out. Oaths bound. We're going in."

Elias offered Hazel his hand and pulled her to her feet. Her knees ached. Her cheeks were stiff with dried tears she had no memory of crying.

"You gave it nothing," he said quietly. "And it gave you everything."

"I know." Hazel looked at the open door—at the dark beyond it, where somewhere a boy who didn't remember her face was polishing thrones. "Now I just have to find something to say to the ones who aren't sorry."

From her pack, silent all this way, the fox finally spoke—one word, soft as ash falling:

*Sister.*

And the door breathed cold, and waited.
