## 13. Thrones of Kept Things

The door the hearth-god opened was not a door. It was a wound in the salt, tall as a cathedral arch and smooth as the inside of a shell, and it breathed.

Hazel felt the breath on her face—warm, sweet, faintly rotten, like fruit left too long in a cellar—and understood that she was smelling time itself, the air of a place where years pooled instead of passing.

"Stay close," Vess said behind her. Her voice had gone flat and hard, the voice she used for pricing things that couldn't be priced. "Roads in here don't forgive wandering."

Elias said nothing at all. He had stopped being a witch-hunter somewhere on the salt roads and hadn't yet become anything else, and he walked like a man carrying a name he no longer trusted himself to wear.

Hazel went through first, because it was her brother on the other side of the door, and because if she let herself think about what she was walking into, her legs would stop working.

* * *

The court beneath the mountain did not look like a palace. It looked like a harvest.

That was the first thing she saw, and the last thing she would ever unsee: a hall so vast its ceiling was a rumor, and filling it, row upon row upon row, thrones of black stone rising out of the dark like teeth. Hundreds of them. Thousands. And on every throne sat a child.

Not children. Kept souls. She could see the difference now that she was close enough to look—the hearth-god had warned her, in its way, that seeing would cost her something—but the difference was small and terrible. Each figure had the shape of a child, the stillness of a statue, and a faint inner light like a coal banked under ash. Some of the coals were bright. Some were nearly out. The oldest thrones, far back in the dark, held shapes that had burned down to outlines, to suggestions, to names worn smooth as river stones.

They weren't suffering. That was the worst of it. Their faces were calm. A few were smiling, faintly, the way children smile in their sleep. Whatever the court took from them, it had taken gently, the way a field takes rain.

"The valley," Hazel said. Her voice came out strange, scraped thin. "Every good year. Every harvest that came when it shouldn't have."

"Paid for," Elias said quietly. He had seen ledgers like this before, in the crown's archives, written in a hand that predated the kingdom. "Line by line. Child by child."

Vess walked the rows without touching anything, reading thrones the way she read cargo manifests, and Hazel watched her stop at one, and stand there, and not move for a long time. When she finally turned away her face was composed into something worse than grief: arithmetic. She was counting how many bargains deep this went, and whose signatures were on them, and hers was somewhere in the dark, signed by a woman who no longer remembered why she grieved.

Hazel didn't ask. There wasn't time, and there wasn't mercy enough in her yet to survive the answer.

She walked the aisle between the thrones, calling his name—not aloud, at first, because some animal part of her feared waking all of them—but the hall heard anyway. The coals flickered. Heads turned, slow as weathervanes, following her with eyes that saw nothing and missed nothing.

"Tomas," she said aloud at last, and the word rang through the stone like a bell rung underwater, and every throne in the hall shivered.

And three rows up, on a throne of black stone worn glossy at the armrests where small hands had gripped them for fifteen years, a boy lifted his head.

He was fifteen years older than the brother she'd lost. He had their father's jaw and their mother's brow and a face that had finished growing without anyone to measure it against a doorframe. His coal was dim—dimmer than most, dimmer than it should have been after only fifteen years, as if he had spent those years burning himself down on purpose, hoarding whatever he could, refusing to be consumed evenly.

He looked straight through her.

"I'm here," Hazel said. "Tomas. It's Hazel."

Nothing moved in his face. But his hands moved—one finger, then another, curling against the armrest, gripping the stone the way he must have gripped it ten thousand times before, and she understood suddenly that the polishing she'd dreamed of, the kept-thing work of the court, was done by hands like these, and that the gloss on the armrest was his own doing, his own small rebellion, wearing his prison smoother rather than letting it wear him.

"You don't know my face," she whispered.

"No," he said.

His voice was rust and riverbed. Fifteen years of disuse, and still, underneath it, her brother's voice, the one that had sung off-key in the barn and argued with their mother about chores.

"But you know—" she started.

"My voice," he said. "I remember your voice." He frowned, a child's frown on a man's face, reaching for something through ash. "You used to talk to the foxes behind the woodpile. Father said stop wasting breath on beasts. You said beasts listen better than people." The frown deepened. "I don't know why I know that. I don't know your face. I don't know my own face. I know your voice like I know the sound of the door of our house, except I don't know the house anymore either."

"That's enough," Hazel said, and her voice cracked clean through. "That's more than enough."

* * *

They came then.

She had felt them stirring since the moment she entered—vast attention shifting in the dark above the thrones, the ceiling-rumor moving—and now they descended, or assembled, or simply became present, the way cold becomes present in a room. The old gods of the Thornfell did not take shapes she could hold onto. They were pressure and vastness and a smell like snow about to fall, and where they gathered, the light of the kept souls bent toward them like grass toward sun.

The eldest spoke without a mouth, directly into the marrow of her, and its voice was patient the way mountains are patient.

*A speaker. Come below at last.*

"I've come for my brother."

*We know what you've come for. We have always known. Your father knew. His father's neighbor knew. Every soul in the valley who ate bread in a famine year knew, and forgot conveniently, and slept well.* The vastness shifted, and there was something in it that might have been amusement, or might have been contempt, and might have been neither, because gods did not feel things the way people felt things; they felt things the way weather feels things. *You are angry. Speakers usually arrive angry.*

"I'm not angry." And she found, examining herself, that it was true, which frightened her more than anger would have. "I'm sick. This whole hall makes me sick. You sit on children like they're coin."

*They are not coin.* For the first time the eldest god's voice lost its patience, just a hairline fracture of it. *Coin is spent and gone. These are kept. Nothing here is wasted. Nothing here is harmed. They wait, warm and whole, until the debts they secure are honored. Show us another creditor in any kingdom above who can say the same.*

"And if the debt is never honored?"

Silence, long as a winter.

Then, gently, horribly reasonably: *Then they wait. We are patient. Patience is the oldest thing we have.*

* * *

It was Elias who named the trap, later, but Hazel saw it open in front of her in real time, and the awful thing—the thing she would carry out of this hall whether or not she carried her brother—was how fair it looked.

*One soul may leave,* the eldest god said. *This is the law of the court, older than the mountain, older than us, and even we do not break it. Any soul may leave, freely given passage, if another soul of equal weight remains.*

"Equal weight," Hazel repeated.

*Weight is not size. Weight is not age. Weight is what a soul is owed, and owes, and holds, and has survived. We will weigh fairly. We have never weighed unfairly. Ask the hall.*

And the hall answered—a thousand faint voices, calm as sleepers murmuring—*it is fair, it is fair, it is fair*—and that chorus was the most horrifying sound Hazel had ever heard, because they meant it. They had been kept so long, so gently, that they had come to love the fairness of their cage.

"There isn't another soul here of equal weight to Tomas," she said. "Whatever he weighs, I'm not leaving someone else's child in his place."

*No,* the god agreed. *Which is why the trade is offered to you, speaker, and not demanded. Bring a soul of equal weight from above. Or—*

It paused. And in the pause Hazel felt the trap's true teeth, the ones hidden under the fair ones.

*—or remain yourself. If your weight suffices. Yours may. Speakers weigh heavy. You have spent a lifetime unseen, unheard, unbought, and unbought souls are rare as star-metal down here. We would keep you well. We would keep you warmly. Your brother walks free into the snow tonight, and the valley keeps its prosperity, and no other child is taken, and everyone is satisfied, and it is fair.*

Everyone is satisfied. It was dressed as mercy. It was dressed as love. It was shaped exactly like the bargain their father had made fifteen years ago, and their father's bargain had built a graveyard of thrones.

Behind her, Vess made a sound low in her throat—not words, just weight. Elias's hand found her shoulder, steadying, and stayed there, and she realized distantly that he was also holding himself upright.

Hazel looked at Tomas.

Her brother was watching her with his dim coal-bright eyes, and there was something new in his face, something the court had not put there. He had heard the offer. He understood it, maybe better than she did—he had lived inside the arithmetic of it for fifteen years.

"Don't," he said.

"Tomas—"

"Don't." Louder now, rust flaking off the word. "I know what I am in here. I'm the payment. Payments don't get traded twice. That's not—" He stopped, groping, a man rebuilding language one plank at a time. "That's not how it works. If you stay, you're not saving me. You're just becoming the next thing they polish."

"I can carry it," Hazel said. "I've carried everything else."

"Not this." And then, quieter, with a certainty that had no business living in a kept thing: "Someone's coming. I don't know how I know. Something's coming, and it's going to change what everything here weighs. Wait for it. Whatever it costs—wait."

Above them, far above, through a mile of stone and snow, something began to ring.

Not a church bell. Not any bell Hazel knew. It came down through the mountain like a needle through cloth, and every coal in the hall guttered at once, and the eldest god's vast attention snapped upward, away from her, toward the peak, toward the sound—

—and for one heartbeat, the court beneath the mountain was afraid.

Hazel stood in the middle of a thousand thrones, her brother's hand almost in reach, and understood that the bell Mother Serane carried was ringing, and that whatever it meant—for gods, for souls, for the fair and terrible arithmetic of this place—it had already begun.
