## 14. What Vess Buried

The road under the mountain was made of salt and memory, and Vess Andrade hated every step of it.

She had walked the salt roads for thirty years. She knew them the way other women knew their own hands—every fork, every collapse, every stretch where the ceiling wept brine and the dark tasted of old iron. But these were not those roads. These were deeper, older, roads that had not existed until someone needed them to, and they moved when the gods dreamed. Twice since they'd left the last waystation the passage ahead had simply been a wall, and twice Hazel Villalobos had put her palm flat against the stone and asked it—asked it, like a woman asking a neighbor for a cup of flour—and the wall had remembered it used to be a door.

Vess did not ask walls for favors. She bribed them, or she broke them, or she went around. That was how you survived the Thornfell: you never let anything above you or below you think it owned you.

And yet here she was, three days down into the dark, following a beast-speaker and a deserter witch-hunter toward a court of sleeping gods, because something down there had her daughter's name.

Not her daughter. Her daughter's *name*. There was a difference, and the difference had kept Vess alive through winters that should have eaten her whole. You could lose a child and go on, if you could remember why it hurt. Grief with a name attached was a wound you could bind. Grief without one was just weather.

That was what the gods had taken from her, eleven years ago. Not Marisol. Just the reason she mourned.

She'd woken in her own bed in the salt caves with salt crusted on her lips and an empty crib at the foot of it, and no memory of ever having filled it. Her crew had found her sitting up, calm as a ledger, saying, "I've lost something, but I can't recall what," and half of them had wept on her behalf without knowing who they wept for. She'd torn her own quarters apart looking for evidence of a child. She'd found none. The gods were thorough thieves. They didn't take the thing itself—they took your claim to it, so that even your grief became deniable, unprovable, a madness with no corpus.

Except she'd kept one thing. One thing the theft hadn't reached, because she'd hidden it from herself before she'd hidden it from anyone else. A single small shoe, salt-stained, wrapped in oilcloth and buried in the ballast of her oldest ship, the *Drowned Prayer*, under six hundred pounds of smuggled tin. She'd buried it so well that for eleven years she'd walked past it without knowing what she'd buried. Then Hazel's fox had come down the mountain with Elias Corven's warning about the bell, and Vess had dreamed—for the first time in eleven years—of a shoe.

She woke with salt on her lips and her own name in her mouth, and she knew.

*Marisol.* The name fit like a key turning in a lock rusted shut. It hurt the way a limb hurts when blood comes back into it. She lay in the dark of the waystation and said it over and over, testing it, and each time she said it the room got colder and more real, because now there was someone missing from it.

Now she was walking toward the court beneath the mountain with that name burning a hole through her chest, and she had not told Hazel, and she had not told Corven, and she would not, because a name spoken aloud is a debt acknowledged, and Vess Andrade paid her debts on her own schedule or not at all.

* * *

The outer court announced itself by smell.

Hazel smelled it first—the beast-speaker's nose was better than any hound's—and she stopped so suddenly that Corven nearly walked into her back. "Smoke," she said. "Hearth-smoke. Down here."

"Gods-smoke," Vess said, and spat, an old habit that had outlived its reasons.

The passage opened the way a throat opens, widening by degrees, and then they were standing at the edge of a cavern that should not have fit inside a mountain. The ceiling was a night sky with no stars in it, just a black so complete it pressed down like water. And across the floor—salt floor, white as bone—burned a line of small fires, hundreds of them, thousands, each one burning without fuel, each one the size of a hearth in a poor man's house.

The first god sat among its fires like an old woman among cats.

It was small. That was the obscenity of it. Vess had expected vastness, terror, something with too many eyes and a voice like an avalanche. Instead there was a hunched shape of grey smoke and ember-light, no bigger than a stove, and when it turned toward them she saw that it was starving. Its edges frayed and regrew. It looked at them the way a beggar looks at a rich man's table—not with hunger exactly, but with the terrible politeness of someone who has learned that hunger gets you nothing.

"You came down," it said. Its voice was banked coals, a fire settling. "Nobody comes down. They only pray up." Its attention slid past Corven as if he weren't there, snagged briefly on Hazel with something like recognition, and settled on Vess with a long, long stillness.

"You," it said. "You smell of salt and of a bargain gone unpaid."

"I don't make bargains I don't pay," Vess said.

"No," the god agreed gently. "You make bargains you forget. That is worse. For you."

Hazel stepped forward then, and Vess watched her do the thing she'd watched her do with wolves and stags and once, memorably, a bear with an arrowhead lodged in its shoulder: she made herself small and open at the same time, hands loose, voice low. "We need the outer door," she said. "There's a soul inside that doesn't belong to you."

"There is nothing inside that does not belong to us," the god said, without malice. "That is what a court is. Everything brought before it belongs to it. That is also what a debt is." And then, to Vess, in that coal-settling voice: "Salt-queen. You want your name back. I can taste it on you like iron in water. Come. Let me show you what you buried."

"I haven't buried anything," Vess said, and heard her own voice crack on the lie, and hated herself for the crack, and hated the god for hearing it.

"It will cost you nothing but the truth," the god said. "I know you distrust that price. Truth has ruined better smugglers than you. But you are already carrying it. I am only offering to set it down where you can see it."

Corven's hand had gone to his knife. Hazel caught his wrist without looking. Vess stood very still, doing the arithmetic she always did: what does this cost, what does it buy, who else is bidding.

Eleven years of arithmetic collapsed into one number.

"Show me," she said.

* * *

The god led them along the edge of the great cavern, past its little fires, and the fires leaned toward Hazel as she passed the way flowers lean at sun. Vess noted that and filed it away; the beast-speaker was becoming something, and somethings were dangerous, but dangerous could be spent.

At the far side of the cavern stood the outer door of the court. It was not grand. That was the second obscenity. It was a door like a farmhouse door, wood gone silver with age, iron hinges green with verdigris, the kind of door you'd find on a shepherd's hut in the high valley. Scratched into its lintel, in a hand Vess recognized with a lurch she refused to name, were tally marks. Hundreds of them. Thousands.

"The court keeps accounts," the god said. "Every soul sold to it is a mark on this door. Do you see yours?"

Vess looked. She told herself she was looking for Hazel's brother's mark, for Corven's sake, for strategy.

She found it in the third row from the bottom, small and deep, cut hard enough to splinter the wood around it. Beside it, in the same furious hand, a word:

**MARI**

The world went white and silent.

She was aware of Hazel saying her name. She was aware of Corven catching her elbow. She was aware that she was on her knees on the salt floor and that her hands were flat against the door as if she could push through it, as if the door owed her passage, as if doors answered anger. The tally mark swam in front of her. **MARI.** Not Marisol. Mari. The short name, the home name, the name she'd used when the girl skinned her knee, when she wouldn't come in from the rain, when—

When Vess had stood in a famine year with empty holds and a crew of forty mouths and a valley dying around her, and something in the deep dark had offered her a harvest, and she had—

"No," she said aloud. "No. I didn't sell her. I would never—"

"You did not sell her," the god said, and its gentleness was the cruelest thing Vess had ever heard. "You pawned her. There is a difference, and the difference is that pawns can be redeemed. You traded her name and her keeping for one winter's grain for your crew, and the term was that you would return within a year and a day to pay the balance. And you did not come. Because we took the memory of the bargain with the girl—it was part of the price, that you should not suffer—and so you did not know you had a debt, and so you did not pay it, and so she waits."

"How long?" Vess heard herself ask. Her voice sounded like someone else's. Like a stranger reading her ledger aloud.

"Eleven years," the god said. "She is not dead. Nothing here dies. She is kept. She polishes thrones, sometimes. Mostly she sits by the door and waits for her mother, because that is the last instruction she was given, and kept things hold their instructions the way the dead hold their graves."

Vess had commanded people for thirty years. She had sent men into avalanches and women into crown garrisons and boys onto frozen passes, and she had learned to carry every one of those weights without bending, because bending was death and straightness was survival. She had built herself out of straight lines.

She bent.

What came out of her was not crying. Crying was clean. This was eleven years of grief arriving all at once with its name restored, and underneath it something hotter and older, and when she finally spoke again her voice had burned all the way down to the metal.

"You took her memory from me so I wouldn't suffer," she said. "Kind of you. Considerate. And in doing that, you guaranteed I'd never come back. You designed the theft so the debt would never be paid. You didn't want my grain. You wanted a kept thing, and you built a machine to make sure I'd deliver one and never redeem it."

The god was quiet for a long moment. Fires do not flinch, but this one dimmed, fractionally, like a man lowering his eyes.

"That is true," it said at last. "We were hungry. Gods starve slowly, but completely. We told ourselves the mercy was real. Perhaps it was. Mercy and appetite wear the same coat down here."

"And her? In there. Is she—" Vess couldn't finish it. Couldn't ask if her daughter still had a self, after eleven years of polishing thrones in the dark.

"She remembers being loved," the god said. "Kept things keep what they held dearest. It is how we feed, a little, without taking. We warm ourselves at the fires they carry." A pause. "Yours carries a shoe."

Vess closed her eyes. The oilcloth. The ballast. The one thing she'd hidden so well she'd hidden it from herself—and her daughter, eleven years old and alone in the dark, holding a single salt-stained shoe, waiting at a farmhouse door for a mother who had forgotten she existed.

Something in Vess Andrade finished breaking, and what stood up in its place was not grief.

Grief was for afterward. This was before.

* * *

She got to her feet. She felt Corven watching her with the careful face of a man who had seen executions and knew the posture of someone deciding one. She felt Hazel watching her with the other thing, the animal thing, the thing that read fear and rage in creatures and knew the difference between the two.

"We're going in," Vess said. "We're getting my daughter, and the beast-speaker's brother, and whatever else in that court still has a mother somewhere above ground who misses it. And then—"

"Then we leave," Hazel said. "In and out. That's the plan. Rob the court and run. That's what you're good at."

"Yes," Vess said. "That's what I'm good at." She looked at the farmhouse door with its thousand tallies, at the word **MARI** cut into the wood by her own hand eleven years ago—she remembered cutting it now, remembered the fury and the shame of the famine year, remembered promising herself she'd be back before the girl forgot her face. "But here's what I've learned about courts, beast-speaker, and I've robbed three of them, the human kind. You don't burn down a court and leave the ledgers standing. Ledgers rebuild. Debts regrow. As long as this place exists—as long as there's a door with tallies on it—some starving father in some famine year is going to stand right where I stood, and some god is going to offer him mercy with the same coat on, and another child ends up polishing thrones in the dark waiting for a mother who can't remember why she's sad."

"So what are you saying?"

Vess put her hand flat on the door, over the tallies, over her daughter's name.

"I'm saying we don't rob this place," she said. "We end it. Every kept soul goes free or goes to rest—whatever freedom means for things that have been kept this long—but the court itself, the door, the ledgers, the *appetite*, ends. I don't care what it costs. I've already paid the worst price there is and didn't even know I was paying it. There's nothing left in me for this place to take."

The hearth-god had gone very still among its little fires. "If the court falls," it said quietly, "everything kept inside falls with it. Some of those souls are eleven years kept. Some are a hundred. Some are older than your kingdom. Unmade, not freed. Do you understand what you are asking?"

"I understand arithmetic," Vess said. "I've run cargo through avalanches. Sometimes you jettison the load so the ship lives. Except this time the load *is* the ship, isn't it? That's the trick of it. That's the beautiful, rotten trick. The court can't fall without taking everyone in it, which is why nobody's ever ended one. Everyone who gets this far has someone inside they love." She smiled, and knew it was a bad smile, the smile her crew called the ledger-smile, the one that preceded hard decisions. "Well. I have someone inside too. And I am telling you, god, mother to whatever you are: if the choice is my daughter kept forever in the dark, or my daughter unmade and *done*, out of this place, out of this hunger, out of this waiting—if those are the only two doors—then I will burn the ledgers myself and thank you for the match."

"*Vess,*" Hazel said, sharp, alarmed. "There's a third door. There's always a third door. That's what smuggling *is*—"

"Third doors are for people who haven't checked the whole map," Vess said. "I've been walking this mountain for thirty years. There is no third door under it. There's the door you enter and the door you pay for."

Behind them, faint as a bell rung through miles of stone, the mountain shuddered. Dust sifted from the ceiling. Far above, muffled by a mile of rock, something began to toll—a slow, heavy, rhythmic sound that the salt floor carried up through their boots like a pulse.

Corven's head snapped upward. His face had gone the color of the salt. "The bell," he said. "Serane's reached the peak. She's ringing it."

The hearth-god made a sound Vess had never heard a god make. It took her a moment to recognize it, because she'd only ever heard it from sailors whose ship was going down under them.

It was fear.

"The bell doesn't silence," the god whispered. "The bell *harvests*. And a court mid-harvest convulses. The tallies—" It turned its fading face to the farmhouse door, to the thousand marks, to **MARI**, and the little fires around it guttered. "The kept souls are starting to unravel. You may not have to choose between freeing them and ending them, salt-queen. The crown may end them for you. Unless you get through that door before the convulsion reaches the throne room."

Vess looked at the door. At her daughter's name. At the tally marks counting down everything the valley had ever sold to survive.

She set her shoulder against the wood.

"Then we stop choosing," she said, "and start moving."
