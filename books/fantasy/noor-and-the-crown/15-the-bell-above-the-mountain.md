## 15. The Bell Above the Mountain

Elias Corven had climbed a great many mountains in his years as a witch-hunter, and he had learned that the crown always sent you up the worst side. The north face of Thornfell was no exception—a wall of scree and black ice where the wind came off the high snowfields like something with teeth, and every handhold was a gamble you made with numb fingers.

He climbed it anyway, because the bell was going to be rung at the top, and he needed to see it with his own eyes before he believed it.

Mother Serane's column had taken the pilgrim road on the south side, the easy way, the way that wound gently through pine and then bare granite to the summit plateau where, according to every map Elias had ever stolen or memorized, there was nothing at all. No shrine. No watchtower. No reason for a man to stand in the wind and freeze.

But Serane hadn't come for a place. She'd come for a height.

Elias had deserted two days ago. He'd walked out of the inquisitors' camp at dawn with his warrant papers folded against his heart and his hunter's badge left on his bedroll like an apology he didn't know how to write. By rights, the men he'd served beside for eleven years should have shot him for it. Instead they'd let him go—Corven, the soft one, the one who filed false reports about empty chapels—and maybe some part of them had wanted to desert too, and couldn't.

So now he was a man without a rank climbing a mountain alone in the dead of winter, following the sound of a bell that hadn't been rung yet.

The bell itself traveled in a wagon drawn by eight oxen and guarded by forty soldiers, which was absurd, because a bell was not a thing that needed guarding. Unless what it carried inside its bronze was worth more than forty men. Unless the metal itself was the treasure.

Elias had heard it humming from half a mile away when the wagon passed him on the valley road three days ago. Not ringing—humming. A low, resonant tone that lived in the chest more than the ears, that made his fillings ache and the frost on the rocks shiver into patterns too regular to be natural. He'd stood behind a boulder and felt that hum pass through him like a hand through water, and he had recognized it, because he had felt its cousin once before: in the salt caves, deep below, where the roads shifted when the gods dreamed.

The bell was made of god-stuff. The crown had been mining the divine for years, smelting it, casting it, and now they were carrying it up the mountain to ring it over the waking court like a fisherman casting a net over a shoal of silver fish.

They weren't coming to silence the gods.

They were coming to harvest them.

* * *

He reached the summit plateau near dusk, cold past feeling, and lay flat behind a ridge of wind-carved stone to look at what the crown had built.

It was not a chapel. It was not an altar. It was a machine.

Scaffolding of black iron rose thirty feet into the grey air, guyed with chains, and hanging from its apex by a yoke of the same god-metal was the bell itself—larger than any church bell Elias had ever seen, its surface worked all over with script he couldn't read but instinctively understood was *prayer*. Thousands upon thousands of prayers, hammered into the bronze in letters small as lice. The collected devotion of a kingdom, cast into an instrument.

Soldiers ringed the scaffold. Inquisitors in their grey-and-gold moved among them with censers, and at the center of it all stood Mother Serane herself, a tall woman in white wool with a face like a closed door, looking up at her bell the way a farmer looks at a field before the reaping.

Elias had met her once, years ago, at his induction. She had asked him one question: *"Do you believe the old gods are dead?"* He had said yes, because it was the correct answer, and she had smiled at him the way you smile at a dog that has performed a trick correctly.

Now he watched her raise her hand, and the whole plateau went still—even the wind seemed to hold its breath—and she brought it down.

The striker swung. The bell rang.

And the world *tore*.

There was no other word for it. The sound wasn't sound; it was a pressure, a great invisible hand closing around the mountain and squeezing. Elias felt it in his bones, in his teeth, in the backs of his eyes. Snow leapt off the slopes in white sheets. Birds burst from the pines far below in shrieking clouds. And from beneath the mountain—from the court under the stone—came a sound in answer, a groan so deep and vast that Elias felt it in the soles of his boots before he heard it with his ears, the sound of something immense turning over in its sleep and finding itself *held*.

Because that was what the bell did. He saw it within moments, saw it as clearly as he'd ever seen anything.

The bell did not silence the gods. It *drew* them.

Where the sound touched the mountain, the stone began to glow—not with fire but with a pale gold light rising through the rock like sap through wood, veins of it spreading outward from the summit, down the slopes, reaching, searching, *pulling*. And wherever those veins of light found the deep places—the caves, the salt roads, the buried halls of the sleeping court—they drank. The light thickened. It brightened. It flowed back up the mountain in slow luminous rivers, converging on the bell, sinking into the bronze, and the script hammered across its surface began to move, the prayers crawling like insects, drinking, filling.

Serane stood beneath it with her arms spread, her face turned up into the falling light, and she was smiling.

"Gods," Elias whispered, and the wind took the word. "You're not killing them. You're *milking* them."

Every prayer ever prayed to the old gods had gone somewhere. The crown had known that for longer than anyone guessed. They hadn't burned the chapels out of piety—they'd burned them to clear the field, to make sure that when the gods woke, the only vessel tuned to receive their power would be the crown's own. Every witch-hunter, every pyre, every village emptied of its shrines had been husbandry. Pruning. Keeping the herd small and frightened so that the harvest, when it came, would be clean.

Eleven years of his life. Eleven years of burning and breaking and filing reports, and he had been a farmhand all along, tending the crown's crop of fear.

* * *

The ringing went on. Not continuously—Serane was too careful for that—but in measured peals, each one followed by a silence in which the light kept moving, kept flowing, and Elias could feel through the stone beneath him that the mountain was convulsing.

He felt it because the mountain told him. That was the horror of it: after everything, after the warrant and the badge and the years, the wild had never stopped talking to him. He wasn't a beast-speaker—he'd tested clean at his induction, or been judged clean, which amounted to the same thing—but the land spoke anyway, in pressures and pulses, in the ache of old wounds. And right now the whole mass of Thornfell was aching like a beaten animal.

Something down there was being pulled apart.

He thought of Hazel Villalobos descending the salt roads with Vess Andrade's crew, heading straight down into the convulsing dark. He thought of the fox, who had carried his warning to her and come back with frost in its whiskers and a look in its black eyes that Elias could only describe as *reproach*—as if the fox had expected better of the world than bells.

He thought of the kept souls. Children sold across generations, held beneath the thrones. If the court was being dragged apart—if its power was being siphoned up through the stone into bronze—then whatever held those souls together was being stretched thin as well.

He had seen unraveling once. A hedge-witch in the Weald, three winters ago, whose power the crown had drained through a similar device, smaller, cruder. She hadn't screamed. She had simply come apart, quietly, thread by thread, sitting in her chair with her hands in her lap, becoming less and less until there was nothing left but clothes and a smell of smoke. He had filed the report. *Subject deceased. Cause: divine contamination.* He had been proud of the clean handwriting.

Elias pressed his forehead against the frozen stone and made himself breathe.

Then he looked at the warrant papers against his heart.

A hunter's warrant was a strange document. It wasn't orders—it was *authority*, distilled. It said, in the crown's own hand, that the bearer spoke with the crown's voice, acted with the crown's power, and was owed obedience by every soldier, magistrate, and gate-warden in the realm. Men had killed for lesser papers. Men had built careers out of the fear they inspired.

And it was written on vellum cured in the old way, with rites older than the crown itself, because the first warrants had been made to bind things that were not men. Elias had known this distantly, the way you know the floorboards of your house are load-bearing. He had never thought about what else a warrant might bind, or unbind.

The bell above him drank another peal of golden light. Below, the mountain groaned. Somewhere in the dark under all that stone, souls were fraying.

"All right," Elias said to the warrant, to the mountain, to the boy he had been at his induction who answered *yes* because it was safe. "All right."

He got up off the ridge and started climbing toward the scaffold, and this time he didn't hide.

* * *

The sentries saw him coming, of course. Forty soldiers don't miss a lone figure walking upright out of the wind with his hood back and his hands visible. What they didn't expect was what he held up: the warrant, high, catching the last gold light of the bell's after-ring.

"Hunter Corven," a sergeant called, squinting. "We were told you'd deserted."

"I did." Elias kept walking. His legs were shaking—from the climb, from the cold, from the thing coiling in his chest that felt almost like laughter. "And then I read my orders again. Warrant-holder's privilege. I'm here on crown authority to inspect the instrument before full harvest. Mother Serane's own protocol, section nine. You can verify with her clerk."

It was a lie stitched together from half-remembered regulations, and it should not have worked. But the bell had been ringing for hours, and the soldiers' eyes had a glazed, bruised look, and nobody wanted to be the man who questioned a hunter's warrant during a sacred operation. Two inquisitors moved to flank him, censers swinging, and Elias showed them the warrant too, and something in the vellum made them step back—the same instinct, maybe, that made animals yield to a larger predator.

"You'll need to clear it with Mother Serane," one said.

"Yes," Elias agreed pleasantly. "That's exactly what I intend to do."

He walked through the ring of soldiers toward the scaffold, toward the bell, toward the woman in white with her arms spread beneath the falling light, and with every step he could feel the warrant warming against his chest like a second heartbeat, like something waking up along with everything else.

Below his feet, the mountain convulsed again, harder, and this time the groan came with a sound he knew from the salt caves—the sound of stone shifting where stone should not shift, of roads rearranging themselves around a dream gone wrong. Somewhere down there, Hazel was running out of time.

Somewhere down there, children were unraveling.

Mother Serane turned as he approached, and her closed-door face opened just enough to show surprise, and then, curiously, pleasure.

"Corven," she said. "The deserter. How convenient. I was going to have you dragged back in chains." Her eyes flicked to the warrant in his raised hand, and her smile sharpened. "Instead you've delivered yourself to the harvest. Do you know what we're doing here? Truly?"

"I'm starting to," Elias said.

"We are ending an age of superstition," Serane said, gazing up at her bell, at the crawling prayers, at the rivers of gold pouring out of the living rock. "Not with fire—with ownership. The gods wake, and we will be holding the leash. Every miracle in this valley will flow through the crown, priced and taxed and licensed. There will be no more free bread from empty chapels, Corven. No more gifts." She looked at him then, really looked, and her voice dropped to something almost tender. "You always hated that, didn't you? The gifts. Things given for nothing. It's why you were never any good at the work."

Elias thought of a girl with fox-bright eyes pulling him out of a rockslide and asking for nothing at all.

"No," he said. "I loved it. That was always my problem."

His hand slipped inside his coat, to the warrant, to the vellum that bound authority older than the crown, and beneath his boots the mountain shuddered, and the bell swung back for its next peal, and Elias Corven—who had spent eleven years learning exactly how to burn a thing down cleanly—took a breath, and moved.
