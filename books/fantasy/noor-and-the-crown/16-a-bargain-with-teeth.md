## 16. A Bargain With Teeth

The court was dying around them, and it was beautiful.

Hazel felt it the way you feel a fever breaking—heat and shudder and something letting go. The thrones of kept things were unraveling. Children who had been polished into ornaments for fifteen years, thirty, fifty, flickered at their edges like candles in wind. The salt roads above groaned. Somewhere far overhead, a bell was ringing, and every toll of it pulled another thread out of the world.

"Keep moving," Vess said behind her, voice raw. "Don't look at them."

Hazel looked anyway. She couldn't not.

* * *

They had come into the throne room through a door that had opened for her prayer—the small hearth-god's door, the one bought with forgiveness—and what waited inside was worse than any horror she'd imagined and stranger too. Not a dungeon. A court. A vast hall of black stone veined with gold, lit by no fire she could name, lined with thrones that had once been magnificent and now slumped like tired old men. And on the steps of each throne, a kept soul.

Tomas had been third from the end.

She'd found him kneeling with a cloth in his hands, polishing stone that didn't need polishing, and when he'd looked up at her his face had been her father's face, her mother's face, a face she knew without knowing why—until he'd spoken and her whole body had recognized him before her mind caught up.

"You're the bell," he'd said. "I remember a bell. Your voice is like the bell."

"That's me," she'd said, kneeling, taking his cold hands. "That's my voice. I'm Hazel."

And something had moved behind his eyes—not recognition exactly, but the shape where recognition used to live.

Then the ceiling had cracked, and the eldest god had woken all the way.

* * *

It did not look like anything. That was the first thing Hazel understood about it. The other gods she'd met below had worn shapes—smoke, stone, the suggestion of antlers—but the eldest one simply *was*, the way a mountain is, the way winter is. It filled the throne room without displacing anything. When it spoke, she heard it in the marrow of her teeth.

*Little speaker.*

"I'm not your speaker," Hazel said. Her hand was still wrapped around Tomas's wrist. She could feel his pulse, slow as sap. "I'm nobody's."

*You prayed to us.* The voice was not unkind. That was the worst part. It was patient and warm and old the way rot is old. *You asked us to wake. You are the first mortal in three hundred years to stand in this hall and speak our names without flinching. Do you know what that is worth?*

"It's worth nothing," Vess snarled from the doorway. She had her knife out, which was absurd, which was a candle held against an avalanche, but Hazel loved her for it. "You took my daughter's *name*. Whatever she's offering, don't take it."

*I know what I took,* the god said, and there was something in its voice then that Hazel would spend years trying to describe—a weariness so deep it had curdled into hunger. *We have taken much. We have been taken from. The bell above us rings, and we are being poured out of the world like wine from a jug. You feel it, little speaker. Even you feel it.*

She did. The bell's toll came through the stone now in visible waves, and each one made the kept souls flicker harder. One of them—a girl near the far wall, maybe ten years dead of the world—came apart entirely, dissolving into motes of gold light that drifted upward toward the ringing. Toward the crown. Toward coffers.

Tomas made a sound. Small. Animal. His fingers tightened on hers.

"The bell," Hazel said. "Elias went after the bell."

*Your hunter climbs,* the god said. *He may reach it. He may not. Either way, we are ending, and ending fast, and everything kept in this hall ends with us—unless.*

"There it is," Vess said flatly. "Unless."

*Unless,* the god agreed, and the warmth in its voice thickened into something almost tender, and Hazel understood with sick clarity that tenderness was this thing's native weapon. It had been worshipped for ten thousand years. It knew exactly how love sounded.

*Here is what I offer, freely, as mercy:*

The hall changed. Not physically—nothing moved—but Hazel suddenly saw it as the god saw it, and the seeing was the trap.

She saw Tomas whole. Not half-memory, not servant—whole, standing upright, blinking in real sunlight, snow in his hair, laughing at some joke she hadn't told yet. She saw Vess's daughter with her name restored, running down a hill toward a woman who remembered why she grieved and grieved anyway because grief was just love with nowhere to go. She saw the valley green in a summer that came early, granaries full, lambs fat, the famine broken forever. She saw the long winter end.

*All of it,* the god said. *Every thread. Tomas walks free. The woman's child wakes with her name in her mouth. The snow stops by spring's first moon. Ask nothing more of your hunter, risk nothing more of your smuggler. All you must do is stay.*

"Stay," Hazel repeated.

*Be our speaker. Our voice in the world above. The covenant requires a keeper beneath the mountain—one who remembers us willingly, so that we may be remembered at all. Your brother filled that seat poorly; he was sold, not chosen, and a kept thing cannot speak for the free. But you—* the warmth gathered, focused, became almost intimate, a hand cupping her cheek, *—you chose to come here. You chose every step. Chosen things burn brighter, little speaker. Chosen things last.*

The hall showed her more. It was generous that way.

It showed her the homestead restored, her mother's madness lifted, her father coming home down the road that wasn't there. It showed her Elias pardoned, Vess crowned in something better than salt caves, the fox trotting beside her through winters that never again starved anyone. It showed her herself—visible, finally, gloriously visible—standing at the mouth of a cave with light pouring off her like water, while a valley full of people looked at her and *saw* her and needed her.

Hazel had spent her whole life being no one. Being no one had kept her alive. But she knew, standing in that dying golden light, exactly how much of her had been starving right alongside the gods.

That was the teeth in it. That was the whole architecture of the trap. It wasn't a cage offered to a prisoner. It was a feast offered to a starving woman, and every dish was true.

"You're doing the thing," Vess said quietly, from the doorway. "The fair-trade thing. Same as the ledger. Same as my bargain, whatever it was, the one I can't remember making." Her knife-hand shook. "Fair bargains are how they farm us, Hazel. Don't sign."

*She is not signing,* the god said gently. *She is choosing. There is a difference, and it is the only difference that has ever mattered to us.*

Hazel looked down at Tomas.

He was flickering. The bell tolled again, somewhere impossibly far above, and his edges went translucent, and for one terrible heartbeat she could see the throne room wall through his chest. He was coming apart faster than the others. Fifteen years of being polished had worn him thin.

If she refused, he unraveled here, now, in front of her, and no amount of climbing or bell-breaking would stitch him back. If she accepted, he walked into sunlight. Into laughter. Into a life.

It was the fairest bargain ever struck. One woman beneath the mountain, in exchange for everyone she loved walking free. Her father had paid less for less. Kings had paid more for less.

*Well, little speaker?*

Hazel opened her mouth—

—and Tomas spoke first.

"No."

His voice was rust and disuse, barely a whisper, but it was a word, a whole word, his second sentence in fifteen years, and it hit the hall like a stone dropped into a well. The golden visions shivered.

*Tomas—* the god began, and Hazel realized with a jolt that it was using his name the way a handler uses a leash.

"Don't." His grip on her hand tightened until it hurt. His eyes were fixed on the middle distance, on the vision of himself in sunlight, and tears were cutting tracks down his thin face, and he was shaking his head slowly, stubbornly, like a man arguing with a beautiful lie. "Not her. Not—" His jaw worked. Words were clearly costing him something enormous, each one hauled up from fifteen years underwater. "Not her. Me. Seat was mine. Debt was mine. Father's bargain—my name on it. Not hers. Never hers."

"Tomas, I can do this—"

"*No.*" Louder. Cracked. Alive. "You don't get to disappear for me. You spent your whole life—hiding, small, nobody—" He turned and looked at her then, really looked, and behind the ruin of his eyes something was burning that the court had never managed to polish away. "I remember your voice. Only thing I kept. You think I kept it so it could be buried under a mountain?"

The bell tolled. The hall convulsed. Gold dust rained from the ceiling like snow falling upward in reverse, and the eldest god's presence pressed down on them both, patient still, terribly patient, waiting to see which of them would break first.

Vess was saying something from the doorway—about the bell, about Elias, about time running out—but Hazel barely heard it. She was looking at her brother, who had been a kept thing for half his life and was, in this moment, the freest person in the room.

And the eldest god leaned close, and its voice dropped to something only she could hear, soft as snowfall, sweet as the answer to every prayer she'd never dared say:

*Choose, little speaker. Before the bell chooses for you.*
