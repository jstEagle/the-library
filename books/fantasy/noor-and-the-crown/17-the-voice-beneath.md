## 17. The Voice Beneath

The eldest god's offer hung in the air of the collapsing court like a held breath.

*Stay,* it said. *Speak for us. Be our voice in the world above, and all debts are paid.*

Hazel stood in the throne room of kept things, and the throne room was dying around her. The walls of the court had been stone once—she could see the ghost of that stone beneath what they had become—but now they rippled like the surface of a frozen lake in thaw, and through the ripples she could feel the bell above the mountain dragging at everything, hauling the gods' waking power up into the crown's coffers strand by strand, like fishermen hauling a net full of living fish.

The kept souls were unraveling. She could see them now clearly, the way she had not let herself see them on the way in: children, generations of them, hung along the walls of the throne room like lanterns. Some glowed steady. Some flickered. One near the far wall had gone out entirely while Hazel watched, and the darkness where it had been did not close over—it just stayed empty, a hole shaped like a person.

Tomas stood three paces from her. He was more himself than he had been an hour ago—the hollow look was gone from his eyes, replaced by something rawer and more frightened and more alive—but he was still thin in a way that had nothing to do with his body. Fifteen years of being polished and shelved and spoken *about* instead of *to*. He looked at her the way a man looks at a door he'd forgotten existed.

And the eldest god—Vessel-of-Every-Hearth, the small starving thing of hearth-smoke that had opened the outer door for her prayer of forgiveness, grown vast now as it drank its own returning siblings—curled around the both of them and made its offer again, gently, the way you'd offer bread to someone you suspected hadn't eaten in days.

*Stay. Speak. Be remembered by us, and remember us, and no one else need be kept. Your brother walks free. The smuggler's daughter wakes with her name whole. The winter ends early. All of it, for one voice.*

One voice.

Hazel had spent her whole life learning how to be quiet.

She thought about that with terrible clarity, there in the shaking heart of a god-court, because clarity was all she had left. She thought about the fox road, the pass that existed only because she'd asked, and how she'd never told a single soul how she found it. She thought about healing the stag's broken leg alone in the dark with her hands cupped over the wound, whispering to it so no one would hear. She thought about twenty years of guiding smugglers down roads no soldier could find and taking credit for nothing, being paid in coin and silence, being *no one*, because being no one was the only way a beast-speaker survived the crown's hunts.

Being no one had kept her alive. It had also meant that when her father sold her brother, no one came looking. No one asked questions. No one noticed that the Villalobos family had lost a son, because the Villalobos family had already learned to be invisible too, grief folded up small and hidden under floorboards next to ledgers.

And here, now, in the last hour of everything, a god was offering her the exact opposite of invisibility. A purpose. A name. To be the voice beneath the mountain—to be *heard*, every prayer in the valley passing through her, every soul above knowing, without knowing why, that someone below was listening. To matter. To be the light in the deep place. To be, if she was honest, the thing she had pretended she didn't want since she was six years old and first understood that her gift was a death sentence: to be *seen*, and loved for it, and safe.

The court would fall. That was the price of refusing. She understood that much. The bell above was harvesting the gods whether Hazel stayed or went, and a god-court without gods was just a tomb with excellent acoustics. If she refused, Tomas might still walk out—but Vess's daughter might not. The winter would come down on the valley like a lid. Everything the eldest god promised would curdle into everything it threatened.

It wasn't even a hard bargain. That was the horror of it. It was dressed as mercy because it *was* mercy, mostly, except for the part where it was a cage.

"Say yes," whispered one of the kept souls nearest her—a girl of maybe ten, glowing faintly gold, her voice dry as old paper. "Please. We've been here so long. Say yes and we can go."

Another soul, a boy older, said nothing, but his light leaned toward her like a plant toward a window.

Hazel closed her eyes.

She felt the court settle around her shoulders like a mantle. She felt how easy it would be. She felt the hearth-smoke god waiting, patient as only something ten thousand years old can be patient, and underneath its patience she felt its hunger—not for her soul, not exactly, but for the thing her soul could do. To be remembered willingly. To have a voice that chose to speak rather than a kept thing forced to serve. Every bargain this court had ever struck had been theft wearing fairness's clothes; she would be the first gift. And gods, she knew now, treasured their gifts above all things.

She opened her mouth.

"No."

The word didn't come from her.

It came from Tomas.

His voice cracked in the middle of it, rusted from fifteen years of disuse, and he swayed on his feet like a man who has just discovered standing is a skill you can lose. But he said it again, louder, steadier:

"No. Hazel. *No.*"

"Tomas—" She turned to him. "It saves everyone. You, Vess's girl, the valley—"

"It saves everyone *but you*." He took a step toward her, and another, and his legs shook but held. "That's the trade. That's always the trade. One stays. Father stayed in the ledger. I stayed in the court. And now you want to stay in the *walls*." His hand came up, trembling, and touched her face—her actual face, which he had not seen since he was nine years old—and his fingers were cold as the mountain itself. "I don't remember your face, Hazel. They took that. But I remember your voice. Do you know what I remember? I remember you singing to the goats when you thought I was asleep. I remember you talking to the fox under the porch like it was a person. I remember you *hiding*, every day, so well that when they came for me I thought—I thought maybe you'd hidden so well even God couldn't find you, and that's why it was me."

"I was a child," Hazel said. Her voice broke on it. "I couldn't have—"

"I know." His thumb brushed her cheekbone, awkward, unpracticed, the touch of someone relearning hands. "I know. I'm not blaming you. I'm telling you what fifteen years in a throne room teaches you." He looked past her, up at the vast slow coiling of the hearth-smoke god, and his chin came up, and for a moment he looked so much like their father that Hazel's breath caught. "This court runs on fair bargains. That's its whole architecture. Fairness. Equivalence. A soul for a harvest, a voice for a winter. It cannot imagine anything else. So it offers you the fairest bargain it has ever offered anyone, because that's the only weapon it knows." He turned back to her. "Don't pick up its weapon. Don't inherit Father's debt. It was mine to pay. It was *always* mine to pay, and I've paid it, and you don't get to take my place out of love, because that's just the ledger again with kinder handwriting."

The eldest god stirred. Its smoke curled tighter, and its voice, when it came, was still gentle, still patient, and underneath the gentleness something vast shifted its weight.

*The bargain stands or falls together, kept-one. If she refuses, the terms revert. The court takes back what it offered.*

"You'll take nothing," Tomas said, "because there'll be no court to take it," and Hazel heard it then, heard what he was saying, and her stomach dropped.

"Tomas, no—if the court falls before the covenant—"

"The souls unravel," he said simply. "I know. I've watched it happen to others, when bargains soured. I know exactly what I'm asking you to risk." He smiled, and it was a terrible smile, brave and cracked and nine years old and thirty-some years old all at once. "But I'd rather end than be saved by putting you in these walls. That's the first thing I've wanted in fifteen years that the court didn't put in me. Did you feel that? *Wanting* something they didn't give me. That's what freedom is, Hazel. It's wanting the wrong thing, on purpose."

Hazel stood very still.

Around her the court convulsed again—above, distant and enormous, the bell rang, and a whole shelf of kept souls guttered like candles in wind, and somewhere a mother-shaped absence screamed without a mouth—and inside all that ruin Hazel felt the two halves of herself pull against each other with a force that should have torn her in half.

Take the bargain. Save them all. Spend yourself; you've spent yourself your whole life, what's one more expenditure, at least this one buys something visible—

Refuse the bargain. Honor your brother. Watch the souls unravel and live with it, the way you've lived with everything, quietly, invisibly, forever—

And underneath both voices, a third one, small and fox-quick, saying: *there's a third road. There's always a third road. You've spent your whole life finding roads that aren't there.*

Her whole life, she had hidden her gift because hiding was survival. But hiding had a twin sister, and its name was *choosing*. She had never once chosen her gift openly. She had never walked up to a god and said: here is what I am, take it or leave it, on my terms.

Every bargain in this room had been struck by someone desperate offering something taken. What had never once been tried, in ten thousand years of this court's careful fair thefts, was someone offering freely, on her own terms, and walking away if the terms weren't met.

That was Chapter Eighteen's thought, though she didn't know it yet. Right now she only knew that Tomas was right, and that being right cost everything, and that she loved him for it with a ferocity that felt like the mountain itself.

"Hazel," the eldest god said, and its patience was fraying now, threads of smoke pulling taut, "*the bell rings. Decide.*"

"No," Hazel said.

It was her own voice this time, and it came out level and clear and carried, the way her voice carried to beasts across whole valleys, and every kept soul on the walls turned toward it like flowers toward sun.

"I won't stay. I won't be kept. And I won't accept your bargain—any version of it where someone stays behind these walls. Not me. Not him. Not the smuggler's daughter. Not one single child on these walls." She stepped forward, into the god's smoke, until she could feel its heat like a hearth on her face, and she made her voice gentle, because you catch more flies with honey and you reach more gods with tenderness. "You're starving. I know. I've seen starving things all my life, and I know what starvation makes you do—it makes you take, and call it fair. But listen to me, old one. Listen the way the stag listened, the way the fox listens. There is a thing you want more than a voice. You told me so yourself, at your outer door. You want to be remembered *willingly*. And a kept speaker is not willing. A kept speaker is just a prettier kind of prisoner. If you take me, you'll have a voice that hates you, and in a hundred years you won't be able to tell the difference between my prayers and the crown's bell."

The god went very still. All of it. Every curl of smoke in the vast chamber stopped moving at once, and the silence was so complete Hazel could hear the souls' lights humming.

"*What do you offer,*" it said at last, and its voice had changed—smaller, hungrier, younger. The voice of the hearth-thing she had met at the door, before the waking had swollen it. The voice of something that remembered being loved and could not remember how.

"That," Hazel said, "is the real negotiation. And we're going to have it properly. Not as a hostage's trade. As a covenant. Terms on both sides, sealed by choice, breakable by either party—which means, old one, that for the first time in your existence, someone will stay *because they could leave*."

"And if I refuse?" the god asked. "If I hold you to the old terms?"

"Then the court dies with the bell ringing," Tomas said, "and you die unfed and unremembered, and the crown wins, and you will have traded your one chance at being loved for one last fair bargain. Your choice." He grinned, fierce and exhausted. "You're a god of the hearth. You know better than anyone: you can't keep a fire. You can only keep it fed, and hope it chooses to stay."

The silence stretched.

Then, high above, through miles of stone and snow, the bell rang again—and this time it rang wrong. Not the clean harvesting toll of before but a stuttering, cracking sound, metal striking metal badly, and through the court's rippling walls Hazel felt a shockwave of fury and panic that was not the god's. It came from above. From the peak. From Elias, and Vess, and whatever they were doing with the warrant and the wolves and the long fall of the mountain's throat.

The eldest god's smoke shivered.

"*They are breaking it,*" it whispered, wonderingly. "*A mortal is breaking our bell.*"

"Not your bell," Hazel corrected softly. "Their bell. That's the difference, and it's the whole difference, and if you learn it, you might live through the winter after all."

The god turned its attention back to her, and it was smaller now—or truer, perhaps, the bloat of desperation burned away—and what looked out of the hearth-smoke at her was ancient and tired and, for the first time in ten thousand years, listening.

"*Speak your terms, beast-speaker,*" it said. "*We are listening.*"

Beside her, Tomas exhaled—a long, shaking breath, fifteen years held and finally released—and reached down without looking and found her hand, and gripped it, cold fingers around warm ones, brother and sister in the heart of a falling god-court, as above them the bell began, note by note, to come apart.

* * *

The negotiation lasted eleven minutes.

Hazel would remember that number afterward, the absurdity of it—eleven minutes to unwind ten thousand years of kept children and fair bargains and famine-ledger arithmetic. Eleven minutes, most of which she spent not bargaining at all but *listening*, because that was her gift, really, distilled to its essence: she listened, and things told her the truth.

The god wanted, in order: to be named again by living mouths. To be fed—not with souls, it clarified quickly, with *attention*, with the small daily currency of a hearth-prayer, a thanks before sleep, a curse when the porridge burned. To have a door that worked both ways. And, buried deepest, admitted last, in a voice so small Hazel almost missed it: to be forgiven. For the kept children. For the famines it had ended at prices no child should pay. For becoming, slowly, over millennia, the thing its worshippers had feared.

Hazel gave it back its own test, from the outer door. "Tell me one true confession," she said, "and I'll believe you're capable of change."

And the god of every hearth, in front of its whole unraveling court, confessed.

When it finished, the walls of the throne room had gone quiet—not dead-quiet, but held-breath quiet, hundreds of kept souls straining toward a conversation that sounded, suddenly, like the first honest words ever spoken in this place.

"Terms," Hazel said, and her voice didn't shake, which surprised her. "The kept souls go free. All of them. Not traded—released. The debt against the Villalobos name is annulled, root and branch, father to son. The court dissolves. In exchange, I carry your memory into the world above—by choice, reviewable yearly, renounceable any solstice. I speak for you. I teach others to listen. And you come up *small*, old one. Hearth-sized. You live in kitchens and doorways and the space between travelers on a cold road. No thrones. No courts. No more bargains with teeth. Ever."

The god considered her for a long moment.

"*Those are hard terms,*" it said. "*For us.*"

"They're impossible terms for a court," Hazel agreed. "Good thing we're ending the court."

And the eldest god of the Thornfell, keeper of the deep places, eater of fair bargains, laughed—a sound like the first crack of ice on a spring river, terrifying and joyous at once—and said:

"*Then let it end. Seal it, speaker. Seal it before the bell finishes falling, seal it while there is still a we to be sealed—*"

Hazel reached for the covenant the way she reached for a fox's mind: openly, gently, with her whole self offered and her hand left free.

And the court beneath the mountain began, at last, to fall open.
