## 18. What the Gods Actually Want

The eldest god's offer hung in the air like smoke that refused to rise.

*Stay. Speak for us. Be our voice in the world above, and your brother walks free.*

Hazel stood in the convulsing throne room with the bell's harvest dragging at the ceiling like a tide going out the wrong way, and she felt the pull of it in her chest—the same pull that had kept her invisible her whole life, only inverted. All those years of being no one. Here was a court offering to make her *the* someone. The voice beneath the mountain. The name every prayer would wear.

It would mean something.

That was the trap. She could see the teeth now, and the terrible thing was that the teeth were honest. The eldest god—Vaelun, hearth-father, first of the woken—was not lying to her. It had never learned how. Lies were a mortal invention, and the gods had been asleep too long to pick up new tricks.

"You're starving," Hazel said.

Vaelun's shape flickered—a hearth-fire guttering, a harvest moon behind cloud, a woman's face made of river-stone and patience. Around them the court groaned. Somewhere above, Mother Serane's bell rang on, and each peal pulled another thread of divine substance up through the stone, and the kept souls lining the walls flickered like candles in wind.

"We are," Vaelun said. "Ten thousand years of prayers fed us. Then the valley grew rich enough to forget us, and we slept, and hunger became the shape of our sleep." Its voice was not sound so much as warmth against cold skin. "Your father gave us a soul. The smuggler-queen gave us a name. Mortals have always paid us in pieces of themselves, because they believed that was the price."

"And it wasn't?"

"It was never the coin we asked for." The god's fire dimmed. "We asked to be remembered. They heard *worshipped* and built altars. They heard *fed* and gave us their children. We were too proud—or too asleep—to correct them. And now we are dying of a meal we never ordered."

Hazel thought of Tomas. Fifteen years polishing thrones, half memory, half servant, wanting so carefully he barely let himself want at all. She thought of her father's ledger, the neat hand writing *paid in full* beside his son's name. She thought of the fox saying *they are waking, run*, and how she had run from everything true about herself until running was all she knew.

"You don't want a hostage," she said slowly. "You've had hostages for generations. Look what they got you."

The court shuddered. A section of wall near the door dissolved into salt-white dust, and one of the kept souls—a girl who might have been sold before Hazel was born—faded a shade more transparent. Time was not on anyone's side here. The bell above was eating the gods alive, and when the gods finished being eaten, everything they held would go with them.

"No," Vaelun said. "Hostages are what fear makes. We have been feared for ten thousand years and it has bought us a throne room full of fading children and a kingdom that sends bells to kill us." For the first time, something moved through its voice that Hazel recognized from her own throat: exhaustion. "What we want is smaller than anything mortals have ever offered us. We want to be carried willingly. To be spoken of by choice. To be *remembered*, not kept."

"Remembered," Hazel said. "Like a story."

"Like a debt of love," the god said, "that no one is forced to pay."

Hazel looked at Tomas. He stood near the dais where the eldest god had set him free to stand—actually free, actually standing—and he was watching her with eyes that were coming back to themselves by degrees, like frost leaving a window. He didn't know her face yet. But he knew her voice. When she'd spoken to him an hour ago, something in him had turned toward her the way plants turn toward light, without understanding why.

She thought about what she was about to do, and her whole body resisted it. Every instinct honed across twenty years of hiding said: *don't be seen, don't be known, don't put your name where power can find it.* Becoming the gods' speaker meant becoming visible to the crown forever. It meant witch-hunters and inquisitors and Serane's bell pointed at her heart until one of them succeeded.

But she had spent her whole life surviving by being no one, and look where it had gotten everyone she loved. Her father had paid in a son because he couldn't imagine another way. Vess had paid in a daughter's name. The valley had paid in silence for three generations, letting the old gods starve rather than risk the crown's attention.

Every tragedy in this mountain had been purchased with the same currency: duty without love. Bargains made out of terror. Payments extracted instead of given.

"I'll make you a third offer," Hazel said. "Not a hostage. Not a trade. A covenant."

Vaelun went very still. Even the convulsing court seemed to lean in.

"A covenant isn't a bargain," Hazel said. "A bargain is two parties holding knives, each waiting for the other to flinch. I've watched my whole life how bargains work—smugglers, hunters, kings, gods. Someone always pays more than they agreed to." She stepped closer to the eldest god, close enough to feel its heat, or whatever heat meant for a thing made of hearth-smoke and memory. "So no knives. Here's what I give you freely: I will carry you into the world above. Your names, your stories, your ten thousand years—I'll speak them, teach them, plant them in living mouths so you exist in memory again. Not because I owe you. Because I choose to. Every day, chosen again. That's the only kind of remembering worth anything, isn't it? You said it yourself."

The god's fire trembled.

"And here's what I ask—not demand, *ask*: release every kept soul in this court. All of them. Tomas, Vess's daughter, the children sold before either of them was born. Let the court fall. Let this place end. You don't need a vault under a mountain. You need people above it who say your names at their tables because they want to."

"That is not a small thing you ask," Vaelun said. "The court is our body. Ending it—"

"Ending it is the point." Hazel's voice cracked and she let it crack. "You can't be remembered while you're hoarding. Everything you keep, you lose. Everything you give away—" She thought of the stag she'd healed on the pass, misleading soldiers out of pure animal gratitude. She thought of Elias kneeling in the snow outside an empty chapel, choosing mercy over orders. "Everything you give away comes back wearing different faces. I've seen it. Kindness leaves debts in the wild, and the wild pays them back."

The eldest god was silent for a long moment. Above them the bell rang, and the ceiling wept threads of light, and the kept souls flickered, and Hazel understood that if she was wrong—if the gods' hunger was deeper than their longing—then she had just offered herself to a thing that would take the offer and the souls and everything else besides.

But she looked at Vaelun's face—that patient river-stone face, that hearth that remembered ten thousand years of prayers and couldn't tell love from use anymore—and she saw it there: the wanting underneath the hunger. The thing it had actually been praying for, all this time, with no one left to pray to.

"You understand," Vaelun said quietly, "that a covenant binds both ways. If you carry us and the world refuses to listen—if they burn your chapels and ring their bells—we starve anyway, and you will feel us starving inside you. There is no protection in what you offer. Only company."

"Good," Hazel said. "I'm done with arrangements that protect me and cost other people. That's the family business. I'm closing it down."

Tomas made a sound. Small, rough, like a hinge unused for years. He took a step toward her, then another, and when he spoke his voice was still learning its own weight—but it was his, fully his, the second full sentence of his new life:

"She means it. She always meant it. Even when she was little and hid in the barn talking to the goats, she meant every word." His eyes found hers, and this time—this time—he saw her. "You came for me."

"Of course I came for you," Hazel said. "You're my brother. That's not a duty. That's just true."

Something broke in the court then, but it was not the ceiling and it was not the walls. It was older than architecture. Vaelun's fire rose—not hungrily, not grasping, but the way a fire rises when someone finally opens a window after a long winter—and the eldest god said, in a voice that shook the salt roads for miles in every direction:

*Covenant accepted.*

* * *

It happened fast after that, the way avalanches happen fast after the long quiet.

The eldest god turned from Hazel and faced the walls of kept souls, and it did not unlock them. There were no locks. There had never been locks—only the weight of the bargain, the gravity of debt, holding each child in place like stones at the bottom of a pond. Vaelun spoke the annulment aloud, in the old tongue and then, deliberately, in the common speech of the valley, so that every soul in the court would understand exactly what was happening and why:

"The debts are void. Not forgiven—*void*. What was taken was never owed. Go home."

And the walls began to come apart like snow in spring.

Hazel had imagined rescue as a violent thing—doors broken, chains snapped, souls torn loose screaming. Instead the kept souls simply... loosened. The girl near the door became solid, became warm, became a child blinking in sudden light. An old man who had been sold sixty years ago sat up from his niche like a man waking from a nap, looked at his own hands, and started to laugh—a cracked, disbelieving laugh that spread through the dissolving court like fire through dry grass.

They were going home. All of them. Into bodies that had waited, or into whatever waited next—the gods were not precise about it, and Hazel suspected even the gods weren't sure. But nothing here was collateral anymore. Nothing here was payment. The ledger was ash.

Tomas gripped her hand. His grip was strong. Fifteen years of polishing thrones had not weakened him; it had only hollowed him, and the hollow was filling now, memory by memory, as the court released its hold. She watched him remember their mother's lullabies. Watched him remember rain. Watched him remember her—really remember her—and his face did something complicated and wet and young.

"I was eight," he said. "I remember the day Father took me up the mountain. He told me it was an errand. He cried the whole way and wouldn't say why." His voice steadied. "He knew. He knew what he was doing and he hated himself for it until the day he walked off into the Thornfell and didn't come back."

"He's dead?" Hazel asked. She had wondered for fifteen years.

"I think he tried to be," Tomas said. "I think part of him is still walking. Some debts don't end when they're paid; they end when they're named." He squeezed her hand. "Name yours out loud someday, Hazel. It helps."

Above them, muffled by a thousand feet of stone, the bell rang on. The court was falling, yes—but it was falling *because* the covenant had unmade its purpose, not because the bell had won, and Hazel felt the difference in her bones. Still, the bell was harvesting what remained, and somewhere up there Elias was climbing toward it with a hunter's warrant and no army, and Vess was somewhere below with her daughter's name coming back to her like blood returning to a numb limb.

"We have to move," Hazel said. "The souls need a way out, and my friends need help, and the bell needs breaking."

"The souls will find their way," Vaelun said. The eldest god was growing brighter and thinner at once, spending itself like a candle spending wax. "The covenant carries them. But you—you should know what you've taken into yourself before you climb back into the daylight."

"What do you mean?"

"You are our speaker now. By choice—which means by *invitation*, which means we may settle into you as embers settle into a hearth. You will feel us. All of us. Ten thousand years of prayers, Hazel Villalobos. Ten thousand years of farmers asking for rain and mothers asking for mercy and children asking for things children shouldn't have to ask for." The god's voice gentled. "No one person was ever meant to hold all of it. That is why speakers used to be many—whole villages, whole valleys, everyone carrying a piece. Your plan to teach others to listen is not kindness. It is necessity. Do you accept that weight anyway?"

Hazel thought about refusing. About negotiating the terms down, carrying only one god, only the gentle ones, only the hearth-spirits and not the storm-fathers.

She thought about her brother, who had carried a debt alone for fifteen years because no one thought to share it.

"Yes," she said. "All of it. And I'll share it as fast as I can teach people to carry."

Vaelun smiled. It was a strange sight—a hearth-fire smiling—and then the eldest god leaned forward and pressed its burning forehead to Hazel's, and the world went white and warm and impossibly, unbearably *full*.

* * *

She came back to herself on her knees on the salt road, with the court dissolving around her like sugar in water, and the gods settling into her chest one by one.

It did not hurt. That surprised her most. She had braced for pain, for possession, for the cold grip of ten thousand years of otherness. Instead it felt like coming home to a house she'd never lived in and finding every lamp already lit. Prayers moved through her like weather. A farmer dead four hundred years asking for rain. A bride asking for courage. A child, generations gone, asking please please please for the fever to stop—and Hazel felt the old, unanswered ache of it and wept without deciding to, because now she was the one who could answer, or at least the one who could *carry* the question, and carrying was its own kind of answering.

"Hazel." Tomas crouched beside her, solid, real, breathing fog into the cold air. "Talk to me. What's it like?"

"Loud," she managed. "Loud and kind. Like a market full of people who all love you." She laughed, wet and shaky. "I need to sit down for a year."

"You can sit down when we're above ground." He hauled her up, and his arm around her shoulders was the arm of a grown man who had been eight years old yesterday, and she leaned into it and let him hold some of her weight. First lesson of the covenant, she thought. Received gladly.

Around them the court was ending beautifully. Walls of salt and shadow folded down into drifts. The thrones crumbled into soil—actual dark, rich soil, smelling of spring. And through the collapsing halls, the freed souls were walking toward the light of the upper world, hundreds of them, thousands, generations of the valley's stolen children going home at last. Some ran. Some wandered, dazed. One very old woman stopped beside Hazel, studied her with eyes like winter stars, and said:

"You're the one who bargained them free."

"I didn't bargain," Hazel said. "That was the whole trick."

The old woman smiled, patted her arm like a grandmother, and walked on into the light.

Vess found them at the great outer door—the door from Hazel's dreams, the door under the mountain, standing open for the first time in living memory. The smuggler-queen came through it with a girl of maybe twelve clutching her hand, a girl whose face was Vess's face younger, and Vess's expression was one Hazel had no name for: grief and joy fused into a single unbearable alloy.

"She remembers," Vess said hoarsely. "Everything. She remembers the bargain I made and forgot. She remembers *me forgetting her*." Her jaw worked. "And she forgave me before I finished apologizing. Who does that? Who teaches a child to forgive that?"

"Someone better than any of us," Hazel said. "Take her home, Vess. Raise her loud. Make sure the whole valley knows her name."

Vess stared at her. "The bell—"

"Is being handled. I hope. By a disgraced witch-hunter with a warrant and a grudge and, if the gods I'm currently hosting have any influence at all, decent footing on the ice." Hazel met the smuggler-queen's eyes. "Go. Your crew's waiting at the salt gates. This part of the mountain is done with us."

Vess hesitated—one heartbeat, two—then pulled Hazel into a fierce, brief embrace that smelled of salt and gunpowder and twenty years of hard roads. "Speaker," she said against Hazel's ear. "If the crown comes for you, the salt caves open for you. That's not a bargain. That's a covenant of my own."

Then she was gone, up the road with her daughter's hand in hers, and Hazel watched them go until the light took them.

Tomas touched her shoulder. "Elias," he said. "The bell. Can you feel it?"

She closed her eyes and reached outward through the new senses crowding her chest—and yes. She could feel it. The bell's harvest as a cold draft pulling at the embers in her ribs. And beneath it, fainter, climbing: one stubborn human heartbeat moving up through the storm toward the peak, with a warrant in his fist and a lie already told in his heart, the good kind of lie, the kind that saves villages.

"He's almost there," Hazel whispered. "And so is she. Serane." The name tasted like iron. "The gods remember her line, Tomas. Her order was founded to serve them, once, before it turned to owning them. There's a thread between her and this mountain older than the crown itself."

"What does that mean?"

Hazel opened her eyes. Far above, through the open door and the thinning storm, she could see the peak of the mountain black against the churning sky—and on it, tiny as a needle, a figure raising something that caught the lightning.

"It means," Hazel said, starting up the road with her brother beside her and ten thousand years of prayers singing in her blood, "that the bell is about to meet the one thing it was never forged to silence."

"A speaker?"

"Worse." Hazel smiled, and the smile had embers in it. "A volunteer."
