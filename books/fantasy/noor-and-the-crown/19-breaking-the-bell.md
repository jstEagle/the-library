## 19. Breaking the Bell

Elias climbed.

That was the whole of it, in the end. All the theology, all the doctrine, all the ten thousand pages of the *Malleus Ferae* he had memorized as a boy—every one of them reduced, on the mountainside, to a man putting one boot above the other in snow that came to his thigh and did not care what he believed.

The peak of Thornfell was a day's climb from the salt caves' mouth. Mother Serane had made it in two days with forty inquisitors and a sledge drawn by mules too dumb to fear what the mountain was doing beneath them. Elias made it alone, in half a day, because he knew the paths no soldier could find—and because Hazel had told him where they were, and because the fox ran ahead of him the whole way like a small orange flame that refused to go out.

*Left at the split stone,* she'd said, pressing the warrant into his hand before he went. Not her hand—the fox's mouth, actually, delivering a folded scrap of paper while Vess watched with an expression that suggested she found the entire arrangement undignified. *The bell is forged from god-stuff, which means it can be unmade by god-stuff. Your warrant has been drinking from the court beneath since the day they issued it. You've been carrying a piece of the mountain this whole time, hunter.*

He hadn't known that. He should have. The warrant had always hummed against his chest in a way the other hunters' never seemed to. He'd told himself it was authority. It had been kinship.

Now he climbed toward the sound.

You could hear the bell from three miles off. That was the horror of it—it wasn't loud, exactly. It was *deep*, a tone that lived in the bones rather than the ears, and every time it rang, the snow around Elias shivered sideways and the wind stopped for a heartbeat as if the mountain were holding its breath. Between the peaks, far below, he could see the court's convulsions written on the land: ridgelines flexing, a frozen waterfall cracking and re-freezing in shapes no water had ever made, and once—a thing he would not be able to unsee—a whole hillside exhaling a plume of steam that smelled, even at this distance, of incense and old copper.

The crown wasn't killing the gods. The crown was *harvesting* them. Every ring of the bell dragged something up out of the deep places and into the bronze, and Serane stood at the summit ringing it with the patience of a woman milking a cow.

Elias had served the crown for nineteen years. He had burned chapels and broken shrines and once—he thought of the village, the empty chapel, the bread that had appeared on the altar anyway—once he had lied in a report to save a handful of people who prayed to a dead god who turned out not to be dead at all, only starving.

He had thought that lie was the worst thing he would ever do.

It turned out to be the first honest thing.

* * *

Vess Andrade did not climb. Vess Andrade did things properly, which meant she did them from below, with other people's hands and a plan and a flask of something warming.

She stood at the tree line with thirty smugglers and a view of the inquisitors' camp on the shoulder of the mountain—forty soldiers, supply sledges, picket lines, and a ring of consecrated iron stakes driven into the snow around the bell's scaffold, because Serane was thorough even in blasphemy. Especially in blasphemy.

"You understand," said Marisol, her lieutenant, "that we are about to attack the crown's army."

"We're about to annoy it," Vess corrected. "There's a difference, and the difference is currently climbing the mountain with a piece of paper in his pocket."

"And if he fails?"

"Then we die annoying it." She took a pull from the flask and passed it down the line. "Which is, historically, how most of us planned to go anyway. Better this than the tax gallows."

But her hands weren't steady, and it wasn't the cold. Somewhere under her feet, in the dark of the salt roads, Hazel Villalobos was bargaining with gods for the soul of Vess's daughter. Vess had put a name to that grief yesterday—her daughter's name, *Lucía*, spoken aloud for the first time in nine years—and now the name burned in her chest like a coal she could not set down. If Hazel failed below, none of this mattered. If Hazel succeeded—

If Hazel succeeded, then everything Vess had done to survive the forgetting would have been worth it, or nothing would have been, and either way there was a battle to fight in the meantime, and battles were simpler than hope.

"Beasts," she said, and raised her voice so it carried down the line. "You've all seen what Hazel can do. I can't do what she does. But I spent nine years learning the salt roads, and the beasts of this mountain know me, because I fed them through three hard winters when the crown's trappers took everything else. They owe me. And today—" she felt it come up through her boots, a tremor, the mountain flexing like a sleeper turning over, "—today the mountain is awake enough to remember its debts."

She knelt and pressed both bare hands into the snow. It was a smuggler's trick, older than the crown, older probably than the gods: you didn't *command* beasts, you *invited* them, and the invitation was written in blood and salt and patience. Hazel had shown her the shape of it weeks ago, laughing—actually laughing—at how close the smuggler's version was to the beast-speaker's.

"Wolves of the high passes," she said quietly, to the snow, to whatever listened. "Cats of the scree. Bears in their winter dens, if you can spare the waking. Ravens, crows, the little fierce things, the weasels and the wolverines. Come up. Come hungry. There are men on our mountain with iron stakes and a bell that steals, and we would take it kindly if you reminded them whose home this is."

For a long moment, nothing.

Then the tree line behind her moved.

Not wind. Not weather. *Moved*—a brown-gray ripple running uphill through the pines like current through water, and the sound that came with it was not a roar but a murmur, hundreds of throats, a low conversational growl rolling up the slope like a tide deciding to come in. Wolves broke from the trees in loose formation, flanking wide. A snow cat flowed along a ridge above them like smoke given opinions. And overhead, rising out of the valleys in spiraling columns, the birds came—hundreds, thousands, a black weather of wings blotting out the pale morning sun.

Marisol made a noise that was not quite a prayer.

"No," Vess said, standing, wiping her hands. "Prayers are what got us into this. Give the order. *Everyone moves on my mark.*"

* * *

At the summit, Mother Serane rang the bell, and Elias watched her do it.

He had come up over the last rise on his belly through the snow, and now he lay flat behind a shelf of granite and looked at the thing he had spent his life serving without ever seeing.

Serane was not what the stories said. The stories said monster; the woman was worse, because she was *careful*. She stood beside the great bronze bell on its timber scaffold with her sleeves tied back and her gray braid pinned up like a general's, and each time she swung the striker she did it with measured force—not rage, but *rationing*. Beside her, four acolytes tended a row of long bronze vessels, and each time the bell rang, the vessels filled a little further with a light that had no business existing: gold-white, slow-moving, thick as honey, humming the same note as the bell itself.

God-stuff. Harvested. Cooled. Ready for transport to the capital, where it would be poured into... what? Crown for a king? Scepter? Walls? Elias thought of the palace's famous Undying Flame, the one that never went out, the one every schoolchild learned to be proud of.

It had never gone out because it had never been allowed to.

"Brother Corven."

He hadn't heard her move. He never would have; that was the point of her. Serane stood six feet away, having crossed snow without sound, looking down at him with an expression of mild, almost maternal disappointment.

"You were reported dead at the village," she said. "Burned with the chapel. A tidy report. I signed it myself." She tilted her head. "And yet here you are, crawling toward my harvest like a penitent. Tell me, Elias—did you come to confess?"

"I came to return something." He got to his feet slowly, hands open. Snow slid off his shoulders. "My warrant."

"That scrap?" Her eyes flicked to his chest, where the warrant hung, and for the first time something moved behind her composure. "Do you know what a warrant is, Brother Corven? It is a *license to hear*. The crown permits its hunters to perceive the divine so that they may suppress it. Every warrant in the kingdom is tuned to the same frequency as the old power. Why do you think we issue them at all? We cannot hunt what we cannot hear." She smiled, and it was the smile of someone explaining arithmetic to a child. "Your warrant isn't evidence against us. It's proof of method."

"I know," Elias said. "Hazel figured it out. She's smarter than the whole archive." He lifted the warrant over his head. "But here's what your method didn't account for. You built your licenses out of the same stuff you're stealing. Which means every warrant in the kingdom is a piece of the court beneath the mountain. And pieces of a thing"—he held it up between them, and it was humming now, audibly, a thin bright note rising to meet the bell's deep one—"want to go home."

"Elias," Serane said, and her voice changed, became genuinely gentle, genuinely sad, and that was the most frightening thing yet. "I read your file. I know about the rockslide. I know about the girl who dug you out. I know you've been soft since before you took your vows. Put the warrant down. Kneel. I will even let you keep your lie about the chapel—you'll need it, where you're going."

"The chapel wasn't a lie," Elias said. "That's the whole problem with me. Nothing I ever did for the crown was true, and everything I did against it was."

He threw the warrant at the bell.

Not at Serane. At the bronze. And in the last instant of its flight, the warrant stopped being a document. Nineteen years of issued authority, stamped seals, consecrated vellum and hammered god-metal—all of it remembered what it was, unfolded like a struck bell itself, a sheet of light and sound, and it hit the great bronze throat of Serane's machine with a note that was the exact inverse of the harvest-tone.

The bell cracked.

Not broke—*cracked*, a seam splitting from lip to crown with a sound like a glacier calving, and the light in the acolytes' vessels surged and sloshed and began, horribly, to *boil*. The hum became a scream became a feedback howl, and every inquisitor on the summit clapped their hands to their ears, and the mountain beneath them bucked like a horse feeling a wasp.

Serane did not scream. She lunged—for the striker, for the cracked bell, to ring it one more time, one more pull of the harvest—and Elias hit her at the waist and they went down together into the snow, and above them the bell tolled one final time, raggedly, all by itself, dragging a last enormous gulp of light up out of the mountain's throat.

Far below, Elias felt more than heard the court convulse.

"Hold on," he whispered, to no one, to everyone—to Hazel somewhere in the dark, to Tomas, to a girl named Lucía he'd never met. "Hold on. We've got the top."

* * *

Vess gave the mark, and the mountain answered.

It was not a battle, afterward, in any of the songs. The songs got it wrong because songs need swords, and there were almost no swords. What it actually was: wolves hitting the picket lines from three sides at once and finding, to their visible surprise, that the consecrated iron stakes meant nothing to them anymore—the mountain had woken too thoroughly for old wards to bite—and the inquisitors' formation simply *unmaking*, forty men becoming eighty fleeing legs. The snow cats took the mules first, not to kill but to scatter, and the supply sledges went sideways into a ravine with all of Serane's cooled and bottled harvest, the vessels shattering on the rocks and the god-light soaking into the snowfield like spilled dawn, and where it soaked, things *grew*—green shoots in midwinter, absurd, impossible, a field of grass blooming in the blood-warm snow while men ran screaming past it.

The birds did the rest. You cannot fight a weather of wings. The ravens went for eyes, the crows for the signal flags, and the little fierce things—the weasels, the wolverines, the furious weasel-hearted multitude—went everywhere at once, into boots, into tents, into the inquisitors' trousers, with a methodology that Vess later described, with genuine professional pride, as "the finest chaos I have ever commissioned."

Vess herself walked through the middle of it untouched, because every beast on the mountain could smell Hazel's covenant on her, and she climbed the scaffold steps to where the cracked bell hung dying, still keening its broken note, still trying to drink.

She put her palm flat against the bronze.

It was warm. It was warm like a living thing, and underneath the greed of it she could feel the *hunger*—the same hunger she had spent nine years mistaking for her own, the hollow where a name used to be. This thing had eaten names. It had eaten her daughter's name out of the world and poured it into a vessel and shipped it toward a king's treasury.

"Oh," Vess said softly. "You're just a bigger version of what was done to me."

She drew her knife—the salt-road knife, the one she'd cut oaths with—and drove it into the crack, and leaned her weight on it, and the bell split the rest of the way with a groan like a ship going down.

Half of it fell outward, down the mountainside, booming off the rocks in diminishing, dying notes. Half of it fell inward, toward the summit cairn—and Serane caught it.

That was the thing the songs also got wrong. She didn't fall. She *caught* it—a fragment of the bell as big as a shield, cradling it like a child, like a relic, like the last piece of a faith—and she stood up holding it while the whole summit shook, and she looked at Vess across the wrecked scaffold with an expression of absolute, unbroken certainty.

"You cannot end it," Serane said. Blood ran from her ear—one eardrum gone, burst by the crack-note—but her voice was level. "The gods are power. Power belongs to whoever holds it. That is the entire history of the world, smuggler. I am not its villain. I am its *clerk*."

"Yeah," Vess said. "I've met clerks." She nodded uphill, toward the cairn, toward the old mine-adit beyond it, the black throat in the mountain's shoulder. "Problem with being a clerk is you spend your life around ledgers, and you start thinking everything's an entry. Even yourself."

Serane frowned. "What—"

The ground opened.

Not dramatically. Politely. The way the court beneath had begun doing everything now that Hazel was speaking to it—as if the mountain had finally been asked, after ten thousand years, whether it wanted anything, and had discovered that it did. The snow beneath Mother Serane's feet parted along a seam that had existed since before the crown, before the kingdom, before the word *crown*, and she dropped—still clutching her fragment, still clutching her ledger-entry, still certain—into the dark of the mountain's throat, and the seam closed over her with a sound like a book shutting.

Vess stood alone on the wrecked scaffold, breathing hard, listening to the silence where the bell had been.

Below her, the inquisitors were running. Above her, the birds were wheeling down out of the sky, settling, folding their wings, going quiet the way animals do when a thing is finished. And somewhere beneath her feet—far beneath, deeper than the salt roads, deeper than the maps—she felt a door open, and felt her daughter's name come back to her across that distance like warmth through a wall.

*Lucía.*

She said it out loud, to the snow, to the sky, to whatever was listening up there on the peak where a disgraced hunter was limping out of the wreckage with a shattered warrant-chain hanging around his neck like a broken collar.

"It's done," Elias said hoarsely, coming up the slope. "Is it done? Down there—is she—"

"She's bargaining," Vess said. "With gods." And then, because the mountain chose that moment to shiver again—not in pain this time, but in something that felt, unmistakably, like *exhale*—she grabbed his arm and pointed at the summit cairn, where the snow was beginning to glow from below, gold-white, honey-thick, the color of a hearth seen through a doorway.

"But hurry," she said. "Whatever she's offering them—I don't think they're waiting for an answer."
