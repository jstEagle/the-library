## 1. Charms for Small Coin

The morning fog in Vhalen smelled of coal smoke and yesterday's fish, and August Eriksen loved it the way a man loves a scar—because it was his, and because it had never once tried to kill him before breakfast.

He set up his stall in Copperlane Market the way he did every morning: awning first, then the folding table with the wobbly left leg he'd long ago stopped trying to fix, then the trays of charms laid out in careful rows. Knot-charms for colicky babies. Braided-hair charms against headaches. Little tin bells on red string that were supposed to keep nightmares out of a sleeper's room and, as far as August knew, actually worked—not through any magic of his, but because a bell is a comforting sound, and comfort does half the work of any cure.

None of it was magic. That was the point.

Fifteen years since the Wardens had burned the last mage in Vhalen's public square, fifteen years since the edicts went up on every gate—*no gift spoken, no gift taught, no gift kept*—and August had survived all of them by being small. Not invisible; invisible was suspicious. Small. Harmless. The charm-seller who haggled cheerfully, overcharged only slightly, and always, *always* had a boring explanation for everything.

"Morning, Master Eriksen!" called Toma the cooper's boy, hauling a barrow of staves past the stall.

"Morning, Toma. Tell your mother the knot-charm I gave her for your father's back wants retying—knots loosen, that's just knots."

"It worked, though! Da slept the whole night through!"

"Knots," said August modestly, "are underrated."

Toma grinned and rattled off toward the timber yards, and August allowed himself one small, private smile before the first customer of the day arrived: a pigeon, landing on the corner of his table with the entitled air of minor nobility.

"You're early," August said under his breath.

The pigeon cocked its head. To anyone watching—and Copperlane was full of watchers—it was a man idly regarding a bird while he arranged his stock. But August felt the familiar tickle at the back of his skull, the sensation he'd spent half a lifetime learning to keep no bigger than a candle flame: the shape of another mind, small and sharp and interested.

*Grain,* said the pigeon. *The baker's boy sweeps at midmorning. The gulls come. The gulls take.*

"I know." August broke off a crust from the roll he'd bought at dawn and scattered it along the table's edge, away from his merchandise. "But here's my offer. You and yours keep to the eaves by the grain stalls. No fouling the flour, no mobbing the delivery carts, and when the gulls get ideas, you remind them whose market this is. In exchange, I sweep my own crumbs to the gutter every evening, and old Marta's scraps go where they've always gone."

*The gulls are many.*

"The gulls are stupid. You're many too, if you call your cousins."

The pigeon considered this with the gravity of a magistrate, then ate the crust, which was its way of signing. It lifted off in a clap of grey wings, and August felt the little flame of its attention gutter out, leaving him alone in his own head again—the way he preferred it, mostly, though "mostly" had been doing a great deal of work lately.

That was the whole of his gift, the thing he'd hidden since he was seven years old and had asked a stray dog why it was crying and been heartbroken to understand the answer. He could speak to beasts. Not command them—he'd never had the knack for command, thank every merciful saint, because a boy who could *command* would have been found out inside a season. He could only talk. Bargain. Listen. Make friends, if you wanted to be sentimental about it, with creatures nobody else bothered to know.

It was astonishing how much of a city ran on creatures nobody else bothered to know.

* * *

By midday the market was in full roar, and August had sold eleven charms, argued down two prices, and argued *up* one price for an old woman who insisted on paying more because her late husband had bought his wedding-ring knot from August's father, back when there had still been a father and back when a knot was only ever a knot.

The rats came at noon, as they always did, working the shadowed seam between the fishmongers' row and the tannery carts. August crouched behind his stall pretending to sort inventory.

"Report," he murmured.

The lead rat—a big scarred female he privately thought of as the Duchess—emerged from beneath the cheese-wright's cart and sat up on her haunches.

*The new traps at the granary,* she said. *Iron jaws. Three of ours taken since moonset.*

August's chest tightened. He kept his voice level, his eyes on his inventory like a man muttering to himself about ribbon stock. "Where?"

*North wall, by the drain. And a man sets them. Grey coat. Watches them too long.*

Grey coat. August's hands went very still among the ribbons. Ash Wardens wore grey coats with ash-white cuffs, and they watched things too long as a profession. But Wardens didn't set rat traps. Wardens didn't do anything so humble.

"Probably the granary master hired a trapper," he told the Duchess, and made himself believe sixty percent of it. "Here's the bargain, then. Stay clear of the north wall drain until I can look at it myself. Take your people around by the tannery run-off—yes, it smells, I'm sorry, but smell beats iron. And if any of you see a grey coat near my stall, you come tell me before you do anything brave. Bravery is for creatures with nine lives. You have one."

*One and a half,* said the Duchess, with dignity, and took her people away through the shadows.

August straightened up, smiled at a customer, sold a headache-braid, and did not let his smile slip until she was gone.

A man setting traps who watches them too long. He turned it over in his mind all afternoon, the way you turn a stone to see what's underneath. Probably nothing. Probably a trapper. Vhalen had rats the way it had rain; somebody had to earn a living at it.

But fifteen years of being small had taught him one thing above all others: the feeling of being *looked at*. Beasts felt it too—that was how prey animals knew to run. And all afternoon, the pigeons on the awning beam kept shifting their weight, kept turning their heads, kept looking at the same spot across the square where a man in a grey coat stood beside the fountain, not buying anything, not selling anything, just standing with the patience of furniture.

*He's been there two hours,* reported a sparrow from the bakery awning, in the terse, breathless way sparrows had. *Two hours. He doesn't drink. He doesn't eat. His eyes move like a hawk's.*

"Thank you," August said quietly, and gave the sparrow half a walnut, and began thinking very hard about closing early.

* * *

He might have gotten away clean, if it weren't for the cat.

She was a young tortoiseshell who'd taken to following him for three weeks now, sitting at the edge of his stall with the fixed conviction of her kind that charm-sellers were secretly in the business of fish. August had never learned her name—cats introduced themselves when they pleased, and she hadn't pleased yet—but he'd been slipping her sardine ends, and this afternoon, bored and bold, she hopped up onto his display table directly in front of a customer.

"Mistress," August said, mortified, "that's not—"

*You smell worried,* the cat observed, tail flicking. *Worried smells like warm milk gone off. Why?*

"*Later,*" August said—which was a mistake, because he said it in the speaking-way, the real way, the way that wasn't shaped for human ears at all, and the customer heard it.

Not the words. Nobody heard the words. But the customer—a thin man in a grey coat with ash-white cuffs, who had materialized at the stall without ever seeming to cross the square—heard the *shape* of it. Heard a man address a cat and the cat pause, and tilt its head, and *answer*, in whatever language lives between a question and a silence.

The Warden's eyes were pale as wood ash. They went from the cat, to August, to the cat again.

"Lovely animal," the Warden said pleasantly. "Does she talk much?"

"All cats talk," August said, with the easy grin he'd practiced in front of a cracked mirror for fifteen years. "Mostly complaints. She's cross with me about the sardines—I've been rationing her. Bad for business, letting a cat think she owns the place." He scooped the tortoiseshell up, and she permitted it with the weary tolerance of the deeply fed. "Can I interest you in a knot-charm, sir? Good against rheumatism. Or bad dreams. Or, if you're the practical sort, I've got boot-laces."

The Warden looked at him for a long moment. Long enough for August to count four of his own heartbeats and notice, distantly, that the pigeons had all gone silent on their beams, and the Duchess's people had melted out of sight beneath the stalls, and the whole hidden population of Copperlane Market was holding its small collective breath.

Then the Warden smiled, bought a boot-lace he plainly didn't need, paid exact coin, and walked away toward the fountain, unhurried as furniture.

August stood there holding a cat, smiling, while cold sweat dried between his shoulder blades.

*That one knows things,* said the tortoiseshell. *His smell has layers. Under the coat-smell and the city-smell there's a burnt smell. Old burnt. Like a fire that thinks about starting again.*

"Yes," August said faintly. "Thank you. Yes."

He closed the stall an hour early. He walked home the long way, through streets where the lamplighters were just beginning their rounds, with the cat trotting at his heel and every rat and rook and stray dog along the route falling in, unbidden, into a loose and ragged honor guard that he didn't ask for and couldn't help noticing. Fifteen years of smallness, and one glance from one pair of ash-pale eyes, and suddenly the whole overlooked city was at his shoulders like a tide coming in.

At his door, on the third-floor landing of the crooked house on Bellows Lane, he found the note that had been slipped under it sometime that afternoon. Sefa's handwriting—quick, slanted, ink-smudged, unmistakable:

*Don't forget tonight. Restricted stacks, after close. I'm finally showing you the thing I promised. Wear something nice. —S.*

P.S. If you tell me the charm for nerves doesn't work, I'll remind you that you sold it to me and I've slept fine ever since.

August read it twice. He should have felt afraid—there was a Warden in Copperlane with a burnt smell under his skin, and a man setting traps who watched them too long, and every instinct August owned was telling him to stay home, bolt the door, be small, be harmless, survive.

Instead he caught himself smiling at the note like an idiot, and the cat looked up at him with deep feline disappointment.

*You smell happy now,* she said accusingly. *Happy and worried. Pick one.*

"I'm working on it," August told her, and went inside to find something nice to wear.
