## 2. The Archivist's Date

The Restricted Stacks of the Vhalen Grand Archive smelled of dust, lamp oil, and secrets that had been sitting too long in one place. August Eriksen had been in a great many places he wasn't supposed to be over fifteen years—rooftops, cellars, once memorably the private aviary of a magistrate who kept talking ravens—but nowhere had he ever felt quite so much like an intruder as here, where every book on every shelf seemed to be watching him with the patience of something that had outlived everyone who'd tried to read it.

"You're standing like a man at his own trial," Sefa said, not looking up from her desk. "Relax. The books can't arrest you."

"They're judging me anyway. I can feel it."

"That's the dust settling on your shoulders. You've been fidgeting for ten minutes." She finally looked up, and her smile did what it always did to him—arrived slowly, like she was deciding whether he'd earned it yet, and then landed all at once. She had ink on her left cheekbone and a smudge of it on her chin, and her hair was escaping its braid in the particular way it did when she'd been working since dawn. August had spent four months learning the exact taxonomy of her dishevelment. This was the *deep-in-a-catalogue* variety. His favorite.

"I brought dinner," he said, holding up the wrapped bundle. "From Marisol's. The good bread, not the day-old bread I usually pretend is a romantic gesture."

"Flattery," Sefa said, rising. "You'll ruin me for other suitors."

"There are no other suitors."

"There are *always* other suitors. There's a junior cartographer who leaves me pressed flowers with notes about river deltas. It's honestly more effort than you put in."

"The flowers die. My charms don't." He set the bundle down between two ledgers. "Mostly. The one on your door latch has held for three months now."

"Which is either very good craftsmanship or very suspicious, given that you claim to be a simple charm-seller."

"I am a simple charm-seller."

"Mm." She poured tea from the pot permanently warming on the archive's little stove, and handed him a cup, and for a while they simply ate—bread and spiced lamb and pickled turnips that Sefa claimed were terrible and finished entirely—and talked the way they always talked, which was to say: her needling him, him pretending to mind, both of them circling the thing they weren't saying.

Four months. He had sold her a luck charm in the market on a rainy morning, mostly because she'd haggled with such cheerful ruthlessness that he'd fallen a little in love before she'd even paid. Since then: walks along the canal wall, arguments about whether pigeons could be trusted (they couldn't; he knew this professionally), and a slow accumulation of evenings like this one, stolen in the margins of her workday because the Wardens watched the streets after dark and courting in Vhalen meant courting in daylight, in public, in the small unwatched spaces.

He had never told her. That was the arithmetic of his life—the gift balanced against everything else, and everything else always winning. A man who spoke to beasts did not get to also have an archivist with ink on her face and a laugh like a door swinging open. He'd done the sums a hundred times. They never came out differently.

"So," Sefa said, wiping her hands, "I promised you a surprise."

"You promised me a surprise or you threatened me with one? Because with you it's genuinely hard to tell."

"Surprise." She stood, took a lamp off her desk, and beckoned. "Come on. And touch nothing unless I tell you to."

They walked deep into the stacks, past the geography folios and the tax ledgers and the shelf of municipal complaints going back two hundred years (Sefa had once read him excerpts for entertainment; Vhalen's ancestors had apparently feuded endlessly about goats), until they reached a section where the air changed. Cooler. Quieter. The lamps here burned behind glass, and the shelves were iron instead of wood.

"The Sealed Collection," August said. His voice came out lower than he intended. "Sefa. You need clearance for this."

"I *have* clearance. I'm the youngest senior archivist in the history of the institution, thank you, and I earned it by being extremely good at my job and moderately good at flattering the head archivist's terrible poetry." She stopped before a case at the aisle's end—a black iron box built into the shelving itself, locked with three separate mechanisms. "And I'm showing you this because you make me laugh, and because I've decided you're trustworthy, and because if I can't show the man I'm—" she caught herself, ears going pink, "—*courting*, then who can I show?"

August's heart performed a maneuver he chose not to examine. "*Courting.*"

"Don't make it weird."

"It's already weird. You said it first."

"I will lock you in here with the tax ledgers."

She worked the locks—one key from around her neck, one combination she turned without looking, one that required pressing a hidden stud she found by memory alone—and the iron box swung open on a book.

It sat on a stand inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age. Even before she unwrapped it, August felt something. Not the dusty watchfulness of the shelves behind them—something warmer than that. Older. Like standing near a hearth in a house everyone said had burned down years ago.

"Sefa," he said carefully. "What is it?"

"A prophecy book." She said it lightly, but her fingers were careful on the cloth. "One of the pre-Consolidation texts. The Wardens burned most of them during the Purification—prophecy was classed as 'unlicensed foresight,' which is the most bureaucratic crime I've ever heard of. This one survived because someone hid it in a shipping manifest collection, of all places. It was misfiled for ninety years. I found it last month." Her eyes were bright. "Do you know how rare this is? Do you know what I would have given, five years ago, just to *touch* one?"

"And now you keep it in a box and show it to charm-sellers."

"Now I keep it in a box and show it to *you*," she corrected. "Because you looked at my goat-feud ledger readings the way other people look at fireworks, and I thought—you'll understand. What this is."

She lifted the book free. It was smaller than he expected, bound in leather so dark it was nearly black, cracked along the spine, with a clasp of dull metal that didn't shine even in lamplight. The cover bore no title. Just a single stamped mark: a circle with a line through it, worn almost smooth.

"Every page is dated," Sefa said, turning it toward the lamp. "That's the strange part. Most prophecy is vague—eclipses, comets, *a king shall rise when the owl drinks wine*, that sort of nonsense. This one is specific. Dates. Names, sometimes. And the dates run forward past its writing." She glanced up at him, and there was something in her face now beyond the archivist's delight—something almost shy. "The last dated entry is tomorrow."

August laughed, because the alternative was something else. "Tomorrow."

"Tomorrow. Which means either some medieval fraudster had a very long game, or—" She shrugged, and her thumb moved to the clasp. "Well. May I? It's supposed to be sealed shut, but the mechanism's degraded. I've been dying to know if—"

"Sefa."

"Just a peek. One page. Consider it dessert."

He should have said no. Later—in the days that followed, which had very few later-days left in them—he would return to this moment again and again, turning it over like a coin, looking for the side where he'd said *put it back, Sefa, seal it up, some things stay closed*. But she was smiling at him with the lamp gilding the ink on her cheekbone, and she had shown him the one secret she wasn't supposed to have, and he was twenty-nine years old and tired of doing sums.

"One page," he said.

The clasp gave with a sound like a sigh.

The book opened itself.

Not dramatically—not a rush of wind, not a flare of light. It simply fell open, past wherever her thumb had been, to a page near the end, as though the binding had been waiting ninety years to relax. And there, on paper the color of old cream, in ink that was not old at all—ink that gleamed, wet-dark, still shining—

Two names.

**AUGUST ERIKSEN.**
**SEFA VALO.**

Beneath them, a date. Tomorrow's date.

And beneath *that*, a line of text in a hand too fine and too certain to be anything but deliberate:

*The ember wakes when the reader reads aloud.*

"Sefa," August said. His voice sounded very far away. "Why does it know my name?"

Her face had gone white around the ink smudge. "I never told it your name. I never told it anything. It's been sealed since before either of us was born." She reached toward the page and stopped, her hand hovering, afraid to touch. "August. The ink is *wet*."

He looked closer, and she was right—the letters had a fresh-laid sheen, as if written moments ago, as if someone crouched just out of sight with a pen still moving. And the names weren't printed, exactly. They glowed. Faintly, warmly, the way coals glow under ash—faintly enough that he might have imagined it, except Sefa saw it too, and made a small sound he'd never heard from her before, something between a laugh and a wound.

"This is a trick," she said. "This is—someone got into the collection, someone's playing a joke, the junior cartographer, I'll kill him—"

"Has anyone else touched this book?"

"No. No one. I haven't even catalogued it properly, I was going to, I was going to write it up next week, I—"

The glow brightened.

Just slightly. Just enough. As if the page had heard them arguing and found it interesting.

August became aware, all at once, of a warmth against his chest. He thought at first it was the lamp. Then he thought it was his own heartbeat, hammering. Then he understood, with the cold certainty of a man who has spent fifteen years keeping himself very small, very quiet, very *unremarkable*—who has whispered to rats in alleys and bargained with pigeons on rooftops and never once, not ever, let anyone see—that the warmth was coming from *him*. From whatever it was inside him that he fed scraps and kept leashed. It had woken up. It was leaning toward the book the way a dog leans toward a door it knows is about to open.

*Sefa,* he almost said. *There's something I should tell you. About me.*

But the lamp guttered—all the lamps guttered, down the whole aisle, a long shivering breath through the stacks—and somewhere far above them, muffled by stone and shelf and ninety years of silence, a bell began to ring.

Not the archive bell. The district bell. The one that rang only for one reason.

Ash Wardens.

Sefa's hand found his in the dark, gripping hard. "That's not possible," she whispered. "They don't come to the archive. They've never—they have no reason—"

The page glowed on, patient and warm, in the dark like a coal refusing to die.

*The ember wakes when the reader reads aloud.*

August realized, with a lurch, that he had read it aloud. Under his breath, without meaning to—the way he read everything, the way he'd been reading all evening, murmuring titles and dates and goat-feud grievances to himself in the quiet. The words had left his mouth. The words had been *heard*.

"Sefa," he said, "we need to go. Now."

"What did you do?" she breathed.

"Nothing," he said, which was the last lie he would ever manage to tell her. "I promise you, I didn't do anything."

Down the aisle, at the far end of the stacks, iron doors began to close—one, then another, then another, a slow rolling thunder of the building sealing itself shut, and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of boots. Many boots. Coming down.

* * *

They ran.

Sefa knew the archive the way he knew the alleys—every service stair, every mislabeled door, every gap where the building's ambitions hadn't matched its construction. She dragged him through a doorway marked MUNICIPAL COMPLAINTS, 1780–1802, down a corridor that smelled of mice and forgotten petitions, and shoved at a panel that swung open onto a delivery passage.

"They'll have sealed the main entrances," she panted. "But the loading dock—the night cart comes at the half bell—if we can reach it—"

Boots above them. Voices, clipped and cold, calling positions. August's chest-warmth was burning now, not painfully, but insistently, like a hand tugging his sleeve, and he had a horrible suspicion he knew what it was reaching toward.

"Why us?" Sefa whispered as they crouched in the dark of the passage, waiting for a patrol to pass. Her hand was shaking in his. "August, why our names? Out of everyone in this city, why—"

"I don't know."

"You're lying to me."

He looked at her—ink-smudged, terrified, sharp-tongued even now, even here—and the truth rose in his throat like a tide. *I speak to beasts. I have since I was a boy. I am the thing your Wardens burn people for, and I think that book has known it longer than I have.*

Somewhere below them—or above them, or possibly everywhere at once—he heard it. Not with his ears. With the older sense, the hidden one, the one he'd spent a lifetime starving quiet.

Rats. Hundreds of them, in the walls and under the floors, and every one of them suddenly, urgently awake.

And moving toward him.

"*August,*" Sefa whispered. "The rats. Why are all the rats—"

The passage door at the end of the corridor burst open, and the light that came through it was not lamplight.

It was fire—small, contained, curling in midair like a living thing—and standing in it, silhouetted, was a fox. A fox made of ember and shadow, with eyes like struck flint, and when it spoke, its voice was the crackle of a burning page:

*"You read aloud."*

August's whole hidden life ended in that moment, quietly, the way a coal goes out—except nothing about it was quiet, and nothing, he suspected, was ever going to go out again.

Behind them, the boots came down the stairs.
