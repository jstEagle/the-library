## 4. The War Beneath the Cobblestones

Sefa had spent eleven years in the restricted stacks of the Vhalen Archive, and in all that time she had learned exactly three things about forbidden books: they were heavier than they looked, they lied more often than people did, and the Archive's cataloging system had been designed by a man who clearly hated his successors.

The prophecy book was all three at once.

She had smuggled it home wrapped in a shawl of unremarkable gray wool, tucked under her arm like bread, past two Wardens who were too busy arguing about shift rotations to notice an archivist with the posture of someone carrying contraband. Now it sat on her kitchen table between a cold cup of chicory and a plate of crumbs she kept meaning to sweep away, and it glared at her.

Books weren't supposed to glare. But this one had shown her August's name and hers glowing on a page dated tomorrow, and then it had snapped shut like a trap, and Sefa knew—professionally, intimately—that it was waiting for her to come back.

"Fine," she said to it. "I'm coming back. You don't have to be smug about it."

She washed her hands first. Not superstition—well, not only superstition. Old texts degraded under oily fingers, and whatever else this thing was, it was old. The binding was leather gone dark as chestnut, stitched with thread that might once have been silver. No title on the spine. No title anywhere. The Archive's registry listed it as *Miscellaneous Folio 114*, acquired "circumstances unclear," which in archivist's language meant *we found it somewhere we shouldn't have been and decided not to ask questions*.

Sefa opened it to the page that had burned their names into the air, and made herself look at what surrounded them.

The page was dated tomorrow. That alone should have been impossible; prophecies were supposed to be vague, all mist and metaphor, so their authors could squirm out of being wrong. This wasn't vague. It read like a ledger.

*On the last night of the Ember Moon, the archivist opens the door. The beast-speaker carries the spark. The sacrifice is given willingly, and the sleepers keep their long bed beneath the stones.*

Her name appeared twice. Once as *the archivist who opens the door*—fine, flattering even, she'd always suspected she'd amount to a good footnote—and once, further down, in smaller letters, as *the sacrifice*.

She read it four times. The words didn't change. She turned the page sideways, held it to the lamp, checked for palimpsest, for forgery, for the sly hand of some medieval prankster having fun at future generations' expense. Nothing. The ink sat in the vellum the way roots sit in soil.

"The sacrifice is given willingly," she said aloud, because saying things aloud made them smaller. "Willingly. Well. How considerate of it to ask."

The book said nothing, but she had the distinct feeling it was pleased with her tone.

* * *

By the third hour she had stopped reading pages and started reading *around* them—the marginalia, the water stains, the places where a binder's stitch pulled wrong. Forbidden books told on themselves if you knew where to look, and Sefa knew where to look the way other people knew how to whistle.

The marginalia came in three hands. The oldest was cramped and furious, a scholar writing in the margins the way a drowning man claws at water:

*They did not end the war. Ask where the ash went.*

Underneath, in a different ink, decades later:

*The Wardens' account says nine hundred died in the Burning Quarter. The Quarter's dead were counted at two hundred and six. Where are the other seven hundred?*

And beneath that, in a hand so careful it looked printed:

*Beneath. All of them. Beneath.*

Sefa set the book down and pressed both palms flat against the table, because the room had developed a slow tilt, like a boat taking on water very politely.

Seven hundred people didn't vanish. Seven hundred people got *filed*. And Vhalen—she thought of Vhalen now with new eyes, with archivist's eyes—Vhalen was a city built on its own foundations, level after level, the way you build when you don't want anyone digging. The Undercrofts. The Sealed Galleries. The whole stratum of the city that appeared on no map and answered to no census, officially "unstable ground," officially "don't go down there."

Officially, fifteen years ago, the mage war ended in one glorious night of fire, and the Ash Wardens saved everyone, and magic died, and peace began.

Unofficially—

Unofficially, the war hadn't ended. It had been *interred*. You didn't end a war by killing every mage in it. You ended it by putting the survivors somewhere out of sight and building a city on top of them, and then you wrote a nice clean history with a nice round number of casualties, and you let the arithmetic do the rest.

"And the ember," she whispered. "The ember is the key."

It was there in the text, once she let herself see it. Pages of it, in that third careful hand—a chronicle of the burial, written by someone who had apparently helped arrange it and spent the rest of their life being sorry. The buried mages slept under wards laid into the city's foundations, wards that needed feeding. And the food the wards wanted, the one spark that could either keep the sleepers dreaming forever or wake them all at once, was called—in the old tongue, in a translation she checked three times against her reference shelf—*the ember*.

A living spark. A person, or a power, or something in between. The last living thread of the war.

August could talk to rats.

August had been talking to rats his entire life, quietly, in market stalls and back alleys, keeping his gift folded up small enough to fit inside a harmless charm-seller's smile. Fifteen years. He was twenty-six, or near enough. The math arranged itself without her permission, ugly and neat: he'd been a child when the war was buried. A child born in the last season of it. A child who grew up carrying exactly the kind of gift the Wardens had supposedly stamped out—and survived anyway, unnoticed, unreported, *unburned*.

Because he was small. Because he hid. Because the city had taught him that the only safe magic was invisible magic.

Or—her stomach turned over slowly, like a page being turned—because something under the cobblestones had been holding him gently in place. A ward doesn't just feed on a spark. A ward *keeps* its spark. Keeps it close, keeps it hidden, keeps it alive the way you keep a coal banked in ash: covered, protected, ready.

He wasn't hiding from the Wardens. He'd been hiding *for* them, all these years, without ever knowing whose hands were cupped around his life.

"Oh, August," she said, to the empty kitchen, and her voice cracked in the middle of his name.

* * *

She worked until the lamp burned low and the chicory went from cold to arctic. She mapped it, because mapping was what she did when the world broke: the burial sites cross-referenced against the sealed galleries, the ward-lines traced through the city's oldest foundations, and everywhere the lines converged, the same phrase recurred in the careful hand, worn down by repetition like a stair worn by feet:

*So long as the sacrifice sleeps uneaten, the sleepers sleep.*

Uneaten. Unspent. The sacrifice was meant to be consumed—given willingly, the book insisted, given *willingly*—and as long as no one gave it, the wards held. Fifteen years the wards had held. Fifteen years the sacrifice had walked around Vhalen selling charms for small coin and flirting with an archivist who was, according to the book, going to open the door.

She was the door. She was also the meal. The book had cast her in both roles and hadn't seen fit to explain whether opening the door and being eaten were the same act, or merely adjacent ones, and Sefa found that this sloppiness offended her almost as much as the content.

"You're a badly edited manuscript," she informed the book. "Whoever wrote you should be ashamed."

But the guilt arrived anyway, right on schedule, the way it always did, settling into her chest with the familiarity of an old cat finding its spot. Because here was the shape of it, plain as a catalog entry: August had trusted her. Months of courting, months of her showing off restricted stacks like a peacock displaying feathers, and the one time she'd wanted to impress him she'd handed him a book that had been waiting a century to say his name. She'd opened the door. The book had known she would—it had *dated* it—and she'd walked in on schedule, punctual as a delivery.

And tomorrow, the page said, the sacrifice would be given willingly. Tomorrow. Her name, his name, one date, and underneath it all seven hundred sleepers whose dreams depended on someone dying neatly on time.

Unless.

Sefa stood up. Her knees protested; she ignored them, the way she ignored most things that weren't immediately useful. She went to the window and looked out at Vhalen at night, at the lamplighters making their rounds along streets that sat, every single one of them, on top of somebody. Seven hundred somebodies. Buried by men who then had the gall to call it peace.

The guilt was still there. She let it stay—guilt was honest, guilt was data—but she felt it change texture as she stood there, the way wet clay changes when you work it. Guilt said: *you did this.* Resolve said: *then you can undo it.* They were made of the same material. It was only a question of what you fired it into.

"I am not a footnote," she said to the city, to the book, to whoever was listening down there beneath the stones. "And I am certainly not dinner."

Behind her, the prophecy book lay open on the table, patient as a held breath.

And as she watched—she would swear this later, to August, to Noor, to anyone who asked, though no one ever quite believed her—the ink on tomorrow's page shifted. Just slightly. Just enough. A new line surfacing like something rising through dark water, in handwriting that was not any of the three hands she knew:

*The archivist has read ahead. Adjustments will be made.*

Sefa closed the book, put the shawl back over it, and reached for her coat.

If the book could edit itself, then it could be edited back. Every text had an author, and every author had a weakness, and she had eleven years of restricted stacks behind her and absolutely nothing left to lose that the book hadn't already claimed.

Tomorrow, the page said, the sacrifice would be given willingly.

Well. The page had never met her mother, and it clearly didn't know how stubborn archivists got when someone tried to write their ending for them.

She blew out the lamp, tucked the book under her arm, and went out into the night to find August—because whatever came next, they would face it together, and no century-old manuscript was going to schedule otherwise.
