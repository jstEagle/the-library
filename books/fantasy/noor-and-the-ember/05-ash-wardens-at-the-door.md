## 5. Ash Wardens at the Door

The trouble with hiding a gift for fifteen years, August reflected as he swept pigeon feathers off his doorstep, was that you got very good at pretending it wasn't there—and then it started sulking.

The charm-shop smelled of beeswax and dried lavender, the way it always did before opening. He lit the lamps with a taper like an honest man, though he could have asked the moths to do it. He arranged his wares on the counter with the care of a man arranging alibis: luck-knots braided from ordinary twine, "warding" bells that were just bells, love-charms that worked only because Sefa had once told him confidence was half of everything and he'd taken the advice personally.

The real magic in the shop lived behind a loose floorboard: three copper coins that rats had returned to him, one at a time, over the course of a winter, each polished to a shine no rat could manage. Payment, they'd insisted. For services rendered. He still didn't know what services.

"You're up early," said the sparrow on his windowsill, who had appointed herself his conscience some years ago and took the role seriously.

"I'm always up early."

"You're *worried* early. Different thing." She hopped along the sill. "The pigeons won't roost by the archive quarter anymore. They say the stones taste wrong there. Burnt."

"The stones can't taste anything."

"Yours could," she said, and flew off before he could argue.

He stood in the doorway a moment longer than necessary, looking down the street toward the distant grey bulk of the Archive, where Sefa was presumably already cataloguing something dangerous with that look she got—half delight, half appetite. Two days since the book. Two days since their names had glowed off a page dated tomorrow, and neither of them had slept properly, and both of them had pretended otherwise in two letters apiece.

*Tomorrow* was today.

August told himself the tightness in his chest was just the cold.

* * *

It was not the cold.

He knew it first as a silence—not the absence of sound but the presence of held breath. The market noise outside thinned, then stopped, the way water stops when something large moves beneath it. His rats felt it before he did; he felt them feel it, a ripple of small panic running through the walls like a shiver through fur.

Then came the boots. Ash Wardens did not march quietly. They wanted you to hear them coming. It was, Odile had once explained over tea, a form of mercy—you got exactly long enough to regret your choices.

There were four of them when they filled his doorway, grey-cloaked, ash-pale sigils stitched at their collars, and between them, unhurried, walked Warden-Captain Vess, whom August had seen twice in fifteen years and remembered both times the way you remember a scar.

"Master Eriksen," Vess said pleasantly. "Lovely shop. So many little charms. None of them work, of course."

"They work beautifully," August said. "Mrs. Halloran swears her luck-knot cured her rheumatism."

"Mrs. Halloran's rheumatism was cured by spring arriving." Vess stepped inside without invitation, gloved fingers trailing across the counter, and August's whole body went still in the old way, the practiced way—the way of a man making himself small enough to be furniture. "We sealed the Archive district last night. Curious thing: a restricted text went missing from its case. Curiouser thing: the case wasn't broken. It was *opened*. By someone with archive access."

"Sefa," August thought, and hated how loudly his heart said the name.

"We're interviewing everyone connected to the archive staff," Vess continued. "Friends. Family. *Suitors.*" The word landed like a coin dropped in a quiet room. "You've been courting an archivist, Master Eriksen. Months now, by all accounts. How romantic. Tell me—did she ever show you anything unusual?"

"No," August said. "She showed me a first-edition atlas of the river deltas. Very unusual, if you like maps."

Vess smiled. It was a professional smile, worn smooth from use. "May we look around?"

"You may look at my shop from the doorway," August said, "like everyone else."

Something flickered behind Vess's eyes—amusement, maybe, or appetite. And then, from the back room, where August absolutely should not have left it, came a soft metallic *chime*.

The warding bell. The one that was supposed to be just a bell. The one Noor's brand had been quietly resonating against for two days, singing a note only things made of ash could hear.

Vess turned her head toward the sound with the slow precision of a hawk.

And Odile stepped out of the crowd in the doorway.

She was seventy-one years old, four foot eleven, and moved through the world like weather. She'd taught him which herbs masked beast-speech, which lies kept Wardens bored, which silences were safest. She put herself squarely in the frame of the door, hands on hips, and said in a voice that carried to the far end of the market:

"Captain Vess. Still bullying shopkeepers? I thought the Guild pension would have mellowed you."

"Odile Marchetti." Vess's smile sharpened into something genuine, which was worse. "Former hedge-witch, current nuisance. We have questions about you too, actually. Old ones. From before the city learned its manners."

"You'll want tea, then. Questions go better with tea." Odile glanced back at August—one heartbeat, one look, and in it was fifteen years of instruction compressed to a single command: *back door, now, don't argue*—and then she turned and walked into the shop as if she owned it, because in every way that mattered, she had helped build it.

August did not move. Every part of him refused. His feet were traitors to his survival.

"*Go,*" Odile said, without turning around, pouring tea that shook only slightly. "I burned your kettle last week anyway. You're better off elsewhere."

The Wardens closed around her like ash closing over a coal.

August ran.

* * *

He ran through the tannery lane, over the wall he'd climbed since he was nine, down into the laundry yards where the steam hid even a Warden's sight. Behind him, faintly, he heard the shop door break, and Mrs. Halloran shouting something indignant about luck-knots, and then nothing—nothing was worse.

Noor found him in the culvert beneath the dye-works, sitting in the dark with his knees drawn up, doing the arithmetic of cowardice. *If I keep running, they stop following me. If I keep running, Odile is the only name they take.*

The fox spirit sat down opposite him, tail curled around her paws, embers drifting off her coat like slow snow in reverse. In the dark she was less fire than ember-glow, a warmth with edges.

"They took her," August said. His voice sounded like someone else's. "They took her because of me."

"They took her because she chose," Noor said. "There is a difference. You will need to learn it quickly."

"Don't philosophize at me. Don't—" He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "Fifteen years. Fifteen years of being small, of being *nothing*, of selling fake charms and thanking the Wardens for their service, and it bought me nothing. It bought her a cell."

"It bought you today," Noor said quietly. "That is what being small buys. Days. Not safety. Days."

He lowered his hands. Her ember-light caught the wet in his eyes and turned it briefly gold.

"There's more," she said, and hesitated—which, from a creature made of prophecy, was alarming. "The brand I gave you. On your wrist. I told you it marked you as the ember-bearer. I did not tell you everything it does."

"I'm listening."

"Ash listens to ash," Noor said. "The Wardens' sigils are woven from the same war-fire I am. Your brand sings to them, August. Faintly, constantly, wherever you are. That bell in your shop—it wasn't me they heard. It was *you*, through the bell. They didn't come for the book. They came because for two days you have been a lit candle walking through their dark."

August looked down at his wrist. The mark was invisible in the culvert dark—or rather, it was visible only as a faint warmth, a pulse under the skin like a second heartbeat. He thought of every street he'd walked since the archive. Every corner he'd lingered on. Every place he'd gone thinking he was choosing freely.

"So I can't hide," he said. "Ever again. Anywhere."

"You can't hide," Noor agreed. "But here is the other thing, the thing I *should* have told you first: the brand points both ways. What the Wardens use to find you, you can use to find what they guard. Cells. Vaults. Prisons. The buried places." Her ember-eyes fixed on him. "Your mentor is alive. I can feel her through your fear of losing her. She is beneath the Wardenhouse, third level, and she is telling them nothing, and she is buying time the only way she knows how—by being infuriating."

A laugh broke out of him, cracked and involuntary, half sob. Of course she was. Odile had once talked three Ash Wardens into reorganizing their own filing system as a distraction.

"She's alive," he repeated, holding the words like live coals.

"For now. But August—" Noor rose, and her light grew, filling the culvert with a low orange glow. "Every hour you run alone, they hold her tighter. Every hour you hide, the prophecy page turns closer to tomorrow's date. And there is one more name on that book besides yours."

The culvert went very quiet.

"Sefa," August said.

"She opened the book with you. She read the names aloud beside you. As far as the old words are concerned, she is already bound to this—as sacrifice, as key, as whatever the page decides she is." Noor's voice gentled, which somehow made it heavier. "You can run. The Wardens will chase the brand, and Sefa will be a footnote—an archivist questioned and released, watched, perhaps forgotten. Perhaps. Or you can go to her, warn her, bind her fate to yours openly—and if the Wardens follow the brand to her door, then everything that happens to her after tonight happens because you knocked."

"That's not a choice," August said. "That's a knife with two handles."

"All true choices are," said Noor. "I would know. I was one."

August sat in the dark a long moment. Somewhere above, the city went about its morning—carts, gulls, the market reopening around a broken shop door as markets do, hungry and indifferent. He thought about fifteen years of smallness, all those days purchased at the price of never mattering, and how the bill had come due anyway, paid in someone else's blood.

Odile's blood. Possibly Sefa's.

His wrist pulsed warm, like a hand waiting to be held.

"Which way to Sefa?" he asked, standing.

Noor's tail flicked—satisfaction, or pity, or both. "Through the undercity. And August—walk slowly. A lit candle that hurries draws more moths than one that strolls."

"That's not how candles work."

"Neither is prophecy," said the fox, "and yet here we are."

She poured herself into the dark ahead of him, a ribbon of ember-light threading down into the bones of Vhalen, and August Eriksen—who had spent fifteen years learning to be small—followed the fire, making himself, step by step, impossible to ignore.
