## 6. Bargains with Rats

The undercity of Vhalen smelled like a promise nobody had ever intended to keep.

August had been down here three days, and he'd catalogued the smells the way he used to catalogue customers: old rain and older stone, tallow from the shrine-candles that guttered in alcoves no priest had tended since the war, and underneath it all, the sweet-rot breath of the river that the city had built itself on top of and then pretended to forget. The river remembered. Rivers always did. That was why they flooded.

He sat on an overturned cistern lid with his boots off, because his boots had developed opinions about his feet around hour thirty, and he addressed the assembly before him.

It was not a dignified assembly. It was, in fairness, mostly rats.

"Let me summarize," August said. "I have no coin. I have no shop. I have a brand on my ribs that itches like a moral lesson, a fire-fox who eats my rations and complains about the seasoning, and a talent I've spent fifteen years pretending was a party trick. What I *do* have—" he spread his hands, "—is ears. Thousands of them. All over this city."

A rat the size of a loaf of bread sat up on its haunches at the front of the crowd. She had a torn ear and the bearing of a dowager duchess who had been through some things. August had named her Duchess within the first hour, mostly because she'd bitten him when he tried to name her something cuter.

*You talk too much,* said Duchess.

"He does," Noor agreed. She lay draped across a dry pipe like a fox-shaped puddle of embers, tail flicking sparks into the dark. "In three languages. None of them interesting."

"You don't even speak two of those languages."

"I speak *fire*, dear boy. Everything else is dialect."

August ignored her, which was a skill he'd been refining since childhood. "Here's the bargain. Not the one I'm famous for—no charms, no trinkets. This is the real one. You tell me what moves through the dark. Where the Wardens go, where their wagons stop, where the doors open that shouldn't. In exchange, I keep my end of every small promise I've ever made you. Bread crusts from the bakery on Corvel Lane—the baker owes me, don't ask why. Warm places to sleep above the steam grates. And no more poison in the granaries. I'll talk to the grain merchants myself."

*We could just take the bread,* said a young rat near the back, all bravado and half a tail.

"You could," August agreed pleasantly. "And then the traps come back, and the dogs, and the boys with sticks. Or you work with me, and Vhalen becomes a city where rats eat well." He shrugged. "I've survived fifteen years by being small. Turns out there are a lot of us down here. Small things. Overlooked things. Nobody watches the things nobody watches. That's not a weakness. That's the whole trick."

Silence, except for the drip of water and Noor's low crackling amusement.

Then Duchess stood, and the crowd went still the way a congregation goes still.

*Done,* she said. *But we want the stories.*

"The... stories?"

*When you learn things. When your plan works or fails. We want to hear it. All of it.* Her whiskers twitched, which August had learned was the rat equivalent of a raised eyebrow. *You humans hoard your stories like dragons hoard gold. We are owed our share.*

August blinked. Then, unexpectedly, he laughed—a real laugh, rusty from disuse, echoing off wet stone. "Deal. Every story. You'll regret it. My life is mostly paperwork."

*So is ours,* said Duchess. *Yours just has better furniture.*

And so began the strangest week of August Eriksen's life, in which he discovered that Vhalen's sewers contained the most efficient intelligence network in the city, staffed entirely by creatures everyone pretended didn't exist.

---

The pigeons were harder.

Not because they were stupid—they weren't; pigeons simply operated on a philosophy of radical self-interest punctuated by sudden collective panic, which, August reflected, described half the merchants he'd known. But the pigeon flocks answered to the Coopmother, an ancient gray bird who ruled the rooftops of the Ash Quarter from the belfry of a deconsecrated chapel, and she drove a hard bargain.

*You want eyes above,* she said, when he finally climbed to her roost, wheezing, Noor laughing silently at him the whole way up. *Everyone wants eyes above. Do you know what we see? Everything. Do you know what anyone gives us? Crumbs. Crumbs!* She puffed herself to twice her size. *I want respect. I want a statue.*

"A statue."

*A modest one.*

"Noor, am I negotiating with a pigeon for a monument?"

"Yes," said Noor, delighted. "This is the best day of my long, strange life."

He gave her the statue—or rather, he promised that once this was over, if any of them were still alive, he would commission a small stone pigeon for the chapel roof, inscribed with words of his choosing. The Coopmother dictated the inscription twice. It was, August thought, actually rather moving, though he suspected the stonemason would refuse the commission on grounds of length.

By the fourth day, the network breathed.

Rats along the sewer lines reported Warden patrol rotations by counting boot-steps and smelling ash-oil. Pigeons tracked lanterns across the rooftops. A colony of bats in the old aqueduct—bats who wanted nothing but quiet and were promised it in writing, which August found charmingly bureaucratic—mapped the sealed doors beneath the archive district. Even the cats got involved, through a one-eyed tomcat called General who communicated exclusively in insults and, somehow, better intelligence than anyone else.

And through it all, thread by thread, a picture assembled itself in August's head.

---

On the sixth night, Duchess came to him fast, whiskers flat, breathing hard in the way rats breathe hard, which is to say barely at all.

*They have your old woman,* she said.

August went cold from the feet up. "Odile."

*The deep cells. Under the Warden citadel, where the old war-tunnels meet the foundations. Two hundred steps below the street.* Duchess's voice dropped, went strange. *There are others down there, little king. Old ones. Asleep. The Wardens feed them through slots in the stone, like animals. Like us.*

Noor had gone very still on the pipe. Stillness, August had learned, was the most frightening thing a creature made of fire could do.

"How many?" he asked.

*I stopped counting at forty,* said Duchess. *My people do not go past the third gate anymore. The dark down there is wrong. It has weight.*

August sat with that. Around him, the undercity dripped and breathed, and dozens of small eyes watched him—not waiting for orders, exactly. Waiting to see what kind of man he was when the news was bad.

"They're keeping her alive," he said finally. "Wardens don't feed prisoners they intend to kill. They're keeping her alive because she knows something. Because—" and the thought arrived whole, terrible, obvious, "—because she knows *me*. They're not hunting me to destroy the ember. They never were."

*Then what?* asked Duchess.

"They want to claim it." His voice came out flat and strange. "You don't bury forty mages under a city and keep them fed for fifteen years unless you're saving them for something. And you don't hunt the last spark unless you need it to light them again. The Wardens aren't putting out the fire. They're *banking* it."

Noor made a sound he'd never heard from her—low, cracked, almost human grief. "That's why I was bait," she whispered. "That's why they let me burn."

August looked at her, and at Duchess, and at the shadows beyond, where a hundred small lives hung on his next word. Three days ago he'd have run. Fifteen years of running said run now, run alone, run small.

Instead he said: "Get me every rat who can count. Get me the General and his cats. Get me the bats' map of the third gate. If Odile fed anyone down there—if she was kind to anyone—it's time they repaid it."

*And what will you give us in return, little king?* Duchess asked, but gently now.

"The story," August said. "All of it. The true one, start to finish—and a city afterward where none of us have to be small to survive."

The sewers of Vhalen, which had heard every secret the city ever flushed away, went quiet as a held breath.

Then Duchess turned to her people and began, in the high chittering language of rats, to give orders.

Above them, faint through sixty feet of stone, the bells of the Ash Citadel rang the midnight hour—and beneath the ringing, so faint August might have imagined it, something far below shifted in its sleep, and turned toward the sound of his voice.
