## 7. The Page That Writes Back

Sefa had catalogued eleven thousand books in her nine years at the Vhalen Archive, and she could count on one hand the ones that frightened her. There was the ledger of the Salt Guild, which recorded debts in a hand that grew shakier as it approached its own final entry, as though the clerk had known something was coming for him. There was the anatomical atlas with marginalia that argued back. And there was *The Ember and the Ash*, which she had carried up from the restricted stacks two nights ago like a sleeping snake, and which now sat on her kitchen table doing absolutely nothing, which was somehow worse.

Books were supposed to be finished. That was the whole covenant of the thing: someone wrote, someone read, and between them hung a story preserved like a moth in amber. A book that kept going after its last page was not a book anymore. It was a mouth.

She had told August she would study it. She had kissed him on the cheek at the door of his shop—his shop with its scorch mark shaped suspiciously like a fox, its shelves of harmless charms, its owner who looked at her like she was the first warm thing he'd seen in fifteen years—and she had said, "I'll have answers by tomorrow," and he had believed her, because August believed people the way other men believed in weather: completely, and without any evidence it deserved.

That was the problem. That was the whole terrible problem, sitting on her kitchen table with its cover of grey vellum.

* * *

The candle guttered at midnight, and Sefa noticed the ink.

Not new ink—not yet. She noticed the *absence* of it, which was an archivist's trick, the way a weaver notices a dropped stitch by the hole it leaves rather than the thread. She had been copying the prophecy's final pages into her own notebook, translating the old Vhaleni script line by line, and she had reached the passage about the sacrifice—the one with her name glowing in it like a brand, *Sefa Ilvane, who gives what is asked*—and she had stopped copying there. Deliberately. She'd written instead: *and here the text becomes unclear,* which was a lie so bald it should have embarrassed the notebook.

But when she flipped back through her transcription to check a word, she found the gap had moved. The unclear passage was three lines longer than it had been an hour ago. And the lines weren't in her handwriting.

They were in the book's.

She sat very still. The kettle on the stove ticked as it cooled. Somewhere below, a rat in the walls went about its small commerce, and Sefa thought absurdly of August, who spoke to rats, who had probably already negotiated this one into some kind of tenancy agreement.

Then she made herself do what archivists do, which is look.

The new lines described a night. Rain beginning before dawn. A man standing in a doorway deciding whether to knock. A woman at a table with a book open before her, reading words she has not yet decided are true. The ink was still wet at the edges—not glistening, exactly, but *settling*, the way fresh ink settles into paper, and Sefa had handled enough forgeries to know wet ink when the paper itself was dry.

The page was describing tonight. Her tonight. The rain that hadn't started yet. August's knock, which hadn't come yet.

Her name glowed faintly at the bottom of the passage, patient as a banked coal.

* * *

By the second hour she had run every test the Archive trained its archivists to run, and invented four more.

She closed the book and weighted it with the iron paperknife. The ink continued. She turned the book upside down; the new lines obligingly rearranged themselves to read right-side up, which felt less like defiance and more like courtesy, and courtesy from a book was somehow more frightening than malice. She burned a corner of the page over the candle—a small, controlled singe, purely experimental—and watched the charred edge smooth itself out within a minute, the text flowing back across the wound like water closing over a stone.

Every attempt to alter it fed it. She learned this the hard way, empirically, the way she'd learned everything worth knowing. When she wrote in the margin—*this is not true, I refuse*—the next paragraph bloomed with an account of her writing in the margin, refusing, the refusal itself rendered in loving detail, her stubbornness folded into the prophecy like an ingredient. When she hid the book under the floorboard where she kept her mother's letters, the ink simply... waited. She could feel it waiting. When she took it out again, three paragraphs had accumulated, describing the hiding, the waiting, the taking-out, each one ending with her name.

It wasn't predicting her. That was the horror she finally named, alone at her table with the candle down to a thumb's width. It wasn't *predicting*. Prediction implied a future that existed independently, a river you might dam or divert. This was something else. This was a book that wrote the future *by writing it*, and every act of resistance was just more material, richer material, plot it could use. She was not fighting the story. She was *collaborating* with it, involuntarily, the way a drowning man collaborates with the sea.

And the ending never changed. She checked. Eleven times she traced the final passage, the sacrifice, the ember rising, the city waking, and eleven times the words around it shifted and swarmed and rewrote themselves—the details of how she'd get there, who would stand beside her, whether it rained—but the ending itself sat fixed as a keystone. *Sefa Ilvane, who gives what is asked.* Not *who refuses*. Not *who runs*. Who gives.

The book was very confident about her generosity.

There was a bitter joke in that, and she found it, because finding the joke was how she kept her hands steady. Fifteen years the Wardens had hunted magic out of Vhalen, and the one piece of magic they'd missed was a book that had spent a century practicing her signature.

* * *

The knock came at half past two, and her heart did the stupid, hopeful thing hearts do, entirely without regard for prophecy.

August stood in the rain—he'd knocked even though she'd told him the door was unlatched, because August knocked, August always knocked, as if permission were a thing you asked for even when it was given—and he was soaked and trying not to show how afraid he was, and Noor sat on his shoulder like a scarf made of embers, regarding Sefa with eyes like struck flint.

"You felt it too," August said. "The book. It's—" He glanced past her into the room, the way people glance at a sick relative. "Noor says it's still awake."

"It's writing," Sefa said. "New passages. Tonight. Us." She stepped aside to let him in, and hated herself a little for how much steadier the room felt with him in it. "I've been testing it all night. It can't be altered. It can't be starved. It can't be burned. Every time I push against it, it just—absorbs the pushing. Writes me pushing."

"Then we don't push," August said. He said it the way he said everything, plainly, like a man laying charms on a market stall: no flourish, no promise of more than the goods delivered. "We go around it. You said yourself the old scripts have gaps, mistranslations. Prophecies rot like anything else. We find the rotten part and we—"

"Sefa," Noor said, and her voice was the sound a fire makes when it finds a draft, "ask her what the last page says."

"I know what the last page says," August began.

"Ask her if it's changed."

The silence stretched. Sefa watched August look at her, and she watched him arrive at the answer before anyone said it, because he was not a clever man in the way archivists were clever but he was clever about people, about her specifically, about the particular set of her shoulders when she was keeping something. Fifteen years of hiding a gift had made him a connoisseur of concealment. He recognized it the way she recognized wet ink.

"The ending hasn't changed," Sefa said carefully. "But I've found things in the margins. Variants. Old translations disagree about the sacrifice—it may not mean what we think it means. Give me until tomorrow night. I'll cross-reference the restricted commentaries, and—"

"You're lying to me," August said, wonderingly, like a man discovering rain falls upward.

"I'm protecting you."

"That's my line." He almost smiled. It was the worst thing he could have done. "Fifteen years, Sefa. Fifteen years I've been telling people 'it's nothing, it's a trick, it's a charm for small coin.' I know exactly what protection sounds like when it's wearing a lie's coat. It sounds like *give me until tomorrow.*"

Noor made a low sound, ash settling. "Tell him," she said. "Or I will. And I am not gentle. I am a fox."

So Sefa told him. All of it—the moving gap, the self-healing page, the eleven endings, the way her refusals became fuel. She told him the worst part last, the part she'd been carrying since midnight like a stone behind her ribs: that the book didn't just describe her surrender, it seemed to *require witnesses*, required the ember present, required August standing in the crypt watching, and that every hour she spent near him was an hour the ink used to write him deeper into the scene.

"If I go away from you," she said, "the passages thin out. I tested that too. When you left the archive district, whole paragraphs went sparse. Sketchy. Like a drawing missing its shading." She made herself meet his eyes. "You are the shading, August. I'm the line work. If I take myself out of the picture—"

"No," he said.

"—then maybe the book loses its ending. Maybe a prophecy needs its sacrifice to *agree*, needs her close, needs the whole cast assembled. Odile taught you bargains. Well, this is a bargain: my absence for your survival. I go to the Wardens myself, tonight, before dawn, and give them what they actually want—which is me, not you—and the book can write whatever it likes about a woman who isn't there to live it."

"No," August said again, louder, and Noor's ears flattened, and somewhere in the walls the rat went silent, listening.

"You don't get to refuse on my behalf. That's the entire lesson of the last three days. Nobody gets written into anybody else's story without their consent—I won't do it to you, and you don't do it to me." Her voice cracked on the last word and she despised it and pressed on. "You survived fifteen years by being small. Let me survive this by being *gone*."

"It doesn't work," August said quietly. "Bargains where one person pays and the other walks away. I've made those. They always come back around. Always with interest." He reached out and touched the book, flat-palmed, careful, the way he touched animals. Under his hand, Sefa saw it happen: new ink blooming, eager, describing *a man touching a book while a woman watches, deciding.*

"See?" she whispered. "Even this feeds it."

"Then we stop feeding it the easy meals." He looked up at her, rain still dripping from his hair, utterly exhausted and utterly immovable. "Tomorrow night. Together. We find the rotten part of the prophecy, or we find the thing it's afraid of, because every book has one—even this one. Even a mouth." His jaw set. "And Sefa—if you slip out that door before dawn to hand yourself to the Wardens, I want you to know I will spend every coin I have, speak every name I know, and burn down every quiet arrangement I've built in this city to come find you. I'm telling you this now so you can factor it into your plan."

Behind him, Noor yawned, showing teeth like candle flames, and said, "He means it. I've seen him bargain with pigeons. He is capable of *anything*."

Sefa laughed despite everything—a short, wrecked sound—and then, because the book was watching and she wanted, just once, to give it a passage it hadn't predicted, she crossed the room and kissed August Eriksen thoroughly enough to make the ink stutter.

When she pulled back, both of them looked down at the page.

The new lines had stopped mid-sentence. The ink hung there, unfinished, waiting—as if, for the first time in a hundred years, the book did not know what happened next.

And then, slowly, in the space beneath the broken sentence, a single word began to surface, pale as a scar:

*Afraid.*
