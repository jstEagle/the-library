## 8. Noor's True Name

The undercity smelled of wet stone and old promises, and Noor had not spoken in two days.

August noticed it the way you notice a clock stopping—not the silence itself but everything that had been built on top of it. For six days she had been his shadow and his fire, trotting at his heel through the sewers with embers flickering along her spine like a cat deciding whether to be annoyed. She talked constantly. She complained about the damp. She had opinions about rats—*unflattering* ones—and about Sefa, which she delivered with the sly precision of an aunt who has decided she approves of someone and will never admit it.

Now she lay curled on a dry ledge above the drainage channel, tail wrapped over her nose, and said nothing at all.

"You could just tell me," August said, for the fourth time that morning.

Noor's ear twitched. That was all.

He went back to work. Work, these days, meant listening. He sat cross-legged on the cold flagstones with his palms flat against them, the way Odile had once told him was *theatrical nonsense, August, the gift doesn't care about your posture*, and let himself hear the city beneath the city. Rats came first—they always did—and then the pale blind fish of the cisterns, the bats in the bell-tower foundations, the feral cats who had appointed themselves wardens of the grain cellars. His network. His ragged, whiskered, entirely illegal parliament.

*Rats say,* he murmured to himself, sorting the morning's reports, *Wardens moved out of the Ashgate barracks before dawn. Forty of them. Heading down.*

Down. Not across, not up into the merchant quarter where a fugitive charm-seller might plausibly run. Down, toward the old crypt district, where the streets had names nobody used anymore because the people who'd named them were ash.

His stomach turned over slowly, like something rolling in its sleep.

"Sefa goes to the archive at midday," he said aloud, mostly to hear a human voice. "Odile's still in the Warden cells—I felt her yesterday, through the mice in the wall. She's alive. She's angry. She's going to be insufferable when we get her out." He paused. "And forty Wardens are walking into the crypt district, which is where the book says everything ends."

Noor's tail uncurled one inch.

"There it is," August said softly. "There's my fox."

She lifted her head. Her eyes were banked coals, and for the first time since he'd known her, they looked tired. Not fox-tired. Old-tired. The kind of tired that takes a century to earn.

"Ask me," she said.

"Ask you what?"

"Ask me why I found you in the archive. Ask me why I marked you. Ask me what I am." Her voice was ember-crackle, but quieter than usual, the way a fire sounds just before it settles into ash. "You've been too polite to ask for six days, and I have been too much of a coward to offer, and we are running out of both."

August stayed where he was, hands on the stone. "What are you, Noor?"

"I'm bait."

The word dropped into the dark like a coin into a well. He waited for the splash. It didn't come—or rather, it kept coming, because she went on, and each sentence fell further.

"Not a messenger. Not a guide. Not the prophecy's loyal hound, whatever the book implies." A thin curl of smoke rose from her shoulders and unraveled. "Fifteen years ago there was a mage named Ysolde Marran. She held the eastern gate at the last battle—the one your history books don't mention because your history books were written by the people who won and then paved over the losing. She burned. Not died. *Burned.* There is a difference, and the difference is me."

The torches along the channel guttered, though there was no draft.

"The Wardens didn't kill us, August. They couldn't. So they buried us instead—alive, sleeping, under the crypts—and they needed one thing left burning above ground. One spark the sleepers would feel through the stone and rise toward. Hope, shaped like a fox." Her laugh was a small, terrible sound. "They made me out of the burning so that I would want to find them. And I did want to. For fifteen years I walked this city looking for the door to my own grave, and I thought it was longing. I thought it was grief. It was engineering."

August's mouth was dry. "The brand."

"The brand is a leash with a bell on it. It lets them track me, yes—but more than that, it lets them *aim* me. Every place I lead you, every person I draw close, is a place they've already chosen." Her eyes met his, and he saw her flinch from what she found in his face. "I led you to the archive. I didn't know I was doing it. The brand pulled; I walked. You read the book because I was there, and I was there because they wanted the book read, and now forty Wardens are marching on the crypts because I have finally, finally done what I was made for."

"And Sefa," August said. His voice came out very level, which surprised him. "Her name's in the book as the sacrifice. If everyone converges on the crypt tonight—"

"Then the page writes itself true." Noor stood, and the embers along her spine flared, and for a moment August saw through the fox-shape to something taller and winged with light, a woman made of the moment after a pyre. Then it was gone, and she was only a small red animal on a ledge, looking ashamed. "I'm sorry. I have been sorry since the night I marked you. Sorry is such a small word for what I owe you. But I wanted you to hear it from me before you heard it from them."

* * *

He didn't forgive her. He wanted to be clear about that with himself, pacing the flooded lower gallery while the fish watched him with their milky patience. Forgiveness required, he felt, at least a day and possibly a good meal, and he'd had neither.

But he also didn't leave. That was the thing about August Eriksen: fifteen years of being small had taught him exactly one lesson worth learning, which was that the overlooked things in this city were never what people said they were. Rats weren't vermin. Pigeons weren't flying garbage. Charm-sellers weren't frauds. And a fox made out of a murdered woman's burning wasn't a trap—she was a prisoner who'd been made to carry the key to her own cage and call it hope.

"You could have kept lying," he told her when he came back.

"Yes."

"Why didn't you?"

Noor considered this with the gravity she usually reserved for insulting bats. "Because you talk to rats," she said at last, "and mean it. Because you spent half your coin last week buying bread for a pigeon with a broken wing, and then lied to the pigeon about the bread being for you, so it wouldn't feel like charity." Her ears flattened. "I have been used by clever people for a hundred years, August. I wanted, just once, to be trusted by a kind one. Even if trusting me is stupid."

"It is stupid," he agreed. "We're doing it anyway."

Then the rats screamed.

Not the ordinary alarm-chatter he'd learned to sort into *cat*, *trap*, and *Warden patrol*. This was the other sound, the one he'd heard only once before, the night the archive sealed—a rising shriek passed mouth to mouth through the walls of the undercity, dozens of voices braided into one word his gift translated without asking permission:

*Fire. Fire below. Fire where nothing should burn.*

He pressed his palms to the stone and listened harder, and the picture assembled itself in fragments: Wardens at the crypt district's upper gates, forty strong, carrying lanterns that burned white and cold. Wardens descending. And in the deepest vault, behind a door that had not opened in fifteen years, something answering the white fire with warmth—the slow, vast, many-hearted warmth of sleepers beginning to stir.

"They're not coming to stop the waking," August breathed. "They're herding it. They want the sleepers to rise *toward* something. Toward—" He stopped. Looked at Noor. "Toward the ember. Toward you."

"Toward whoever holds me," Noor said. "Which is why they'll want you there too. The brand, August. Wherever you are, I'm drawn. Wherever I'm drawn, they follow. You are the hand on the leash whether you hold it or not."

"Then we break the leash."

"With what?"

He was already moving, splashing up the gallery stairs, shouting to the dark in the old way, the forbidden way, the way that had gotten three charm-sellers burned before he was born. *Wake up. Wake up. All of you—cats in the granaries, bats in the towers, rats in the walls—wake up and listen.* The undercity stirred around him like a great animal turning over, and for the first time in his life he didn't flinch from the size of his own voice.

"Odile's in the Warden cells by Ashgate," he called back to Noor. "Sefa will go to the crypt tonight whether we warn her or not—she's been reading that book, and the book wants her there. So we don't choose between them. We take the whole board."

Noor bounded up beside him, embers streaming. "You've met my former owners, have you? They're very thorough."

"So am I," August said. "I sell charms for small coin. Nobody's ever accused me of thinking big, but nobody's ever accused me of quitting, either."

* * *

They surfaced near the tanneries at dusk, in the reeking yard behind Odile's shuttered shop, and that was where August found the note.

It was pinned to the door with a knife—a Warden-issue knife, black-hilted—and written in a hand he knew better than his own, because he'd spent fifteen years forging it badly on practice charms. Odile's handwriting, sharp and slanted and infuriatingly neat even now:

*They've offered me a trade, boy. My freedom for your fox. I'm telling you plainly so you'll do something foolish and noble instead of something sensible. Come to the crypt. Bring the girl. Bring the whole wretched city if you can manage it.*

*—O.*

P.S. *Your wards on the back door were sentimental and full of gaps. We'll fix that when I'm home.*

August read it twice. Then he laughed—a cracked, exhausted sound—and Noor pressed her warm flank against his leg.

"She's alive," he said. "She's free enough to write insults. And she's telling us it's a trap."

"Are you going anyway?"

"Sefa's already halfway there. I can feel it—the sparrows saw her leave the archive an hour ago, heading east, walking fast." He folded the note into his pocket, next to the stub of pencil and the dried apple slice he kept for bribing magpies. "So yes. We're going. All of us."

Above the rooftops, the first stars were coming out, and beneath the cobblestones, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, something buried began to sing.

Noor's ears went flat. "August," she said quietly. "That song. I know that song."

"Whose is it?"

"Mine," she said. "It's mine. It's the shape they burned me from. Which means whatever's waking down there isn't just the sleepers." Her eyes reflected the distant glow rising through the sewer grates, gold and growing. "They're waking *me*, too. And I don't know which of us they intend to keep."
