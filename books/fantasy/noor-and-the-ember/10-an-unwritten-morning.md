## 10. An Unwritten Morning

Dawn came to Vhalen the way it always did—gray, grudging, smelling of bread and river mud—but this morning the pigeons were singing.

Not cooing. Singing. Badly, in three-part harmony that no one had asked for and no one could stop, but singing all the same, perched along the gutters of the Ash Quarter like a choir that had discovered religion overnight.

"That's your fault," Sefa said.

They were sitting on the steps of August's shop, which was not burned down, which still had its crooked sign reading *CHARMS FOR SMALL COIN — NO REFUNDS*, and which had somehow survived a night of prophecy, insurrection, and fire-fox because the universe, apparently, had a sense of humor after everything.

"Technically it's Noor's fault," August said. "I just gave her the gift."

"You gave a fox spirit the ability to speak with every animal in the city, and you're surprised there's a choir."

"I'm not *surprised*. I'm experiencing consequences."

Sefa laughed—that sharp, unguarded laugh he'd spent months earning—and leaned her head against his shoulder. Her hand found his and turned it over, examining his palm like she was checking for damage.

The brand was gone. The mark Noor had pressed into him in the archive stairwell, the ember-shaped sigil that had let the Wardens track him across half the city—it had faded sometime in the crypt, when he'd spoken every beast-name he knew and the animals of Vhalen had risen like a tide with teeth. In its place: nothing. Just an ordinary hand. Ink stains from cheap charm-work. A callus on the middle finger where he gripped his carving knife.

An ordinary hand belonging, as of roughly four hours ago, to an ordinary man.

He kept turning that fact over, the way you'd probe a missing tooth.

* * *

The crypt beneath the Wardens' citadel had emptied by moonrise. The buried mages—the ones the city had sealed under its own foundations fifteen years ago, the war's survivors, sleeping in stone like seeds nobody meant to water—had climbed into the gray light blinking and furious and, when it came down to it, very tired.

August had expected rage. He'd braced for fire.

What he got instead was a committee.

"You have to understand," said a woman named Ilse Vane, who had apparently been the war's foremost battle-mage and now mostly complained about her joints, "we were promised a reckoning."

"There was a reckoning," August said carefully. "Last night. There were fires. Several buildings are gone."

"Yes, and then what? We wake up, we burn the citadel, we take the city back, and then what? We spend the next fifty years being hunted by whatever replaces the Wardens?" She rubbed her hip. "I'm sixty-one years old and I slept through two governments. I'd like to sleep somewhere with a sea view."

And so it went. The mages argued until sunrise—some wanting vengeance, some wanting nothing but to be left alone, most, when the shouting wore itself out, wanting simply to go somewhere the word *mage* wasn't a death sentence. The Wardens, leaderless since their commander had been dragged out of the crypt by a very determined badger (August hadn't even known badgers could be determined in that specific way; the badger had taught him), had no strength left to fight and too much shame to pretend otherwise.

The treaty, if you could call it that, was negotiated over stolen Warden rations: the mages would go east, beyond the Salt Marches, taking their war with them. The Wardens would disband—or rather, rebrand, because bureaucracies are immortal—and the law against small magics would quietly rot off the books, unenforced, unmentioned, the way inconvenient laws do.

Noor had stood at the edge of it all, flickering, listening, saying almost nothing.

She'd been saying almost nothing ever since.

* * *

August found her at midmorning on the roof of the old tannery, where the city's chimney smoke rose in columns and the gulls wheeled overhead screaming insults. She sat at the roof's edge with her tails wrapped around her feet—all nine of them now, blazing softly, brighter than they'd ever been, as if the gift settling into her had fed her something she'd been starving for.

"You're sulking," August said, sitting down beside her. "You're a legendary spirit of fire and grief, and you're sulking on a tannery roof."

"I am *contemplating*," Noor said. "There is a difference. Sulking is for children and cats."

"And foxes."

"Foxes are above such things." Her ears twitched. Below them, the pigeon choir attempted a key change and failed spectacularly. "Your birds are terrible. I want you to know I take full responsibility and feel no remorse whatsoever."

"They've been asking me to teach them rounds. Me. As if I still—" He stopped. The sentence had a hole in it where his gift used to be.

Noor looked at him sideways, firelight catching in eyes the color of banked coals. "You regret it."

It wasn't a question, so he didn't answer it right away. He watched the smoke rise instead, and thought about how to say a true thing honestly.

"When I was eight years old," he said finally, "a sparrow fell out of its nest onto our step. Broken wing. And I held it, and I told it—I don't know what I told it, some nonsense about being brave—and it understood me. It looked at me like I was the whole world. And I thought, *this is the best thing about me.* Not the only thing. But the best thing." He flexed his ordinary fingers. "And last night I handed it away like a coat I was tired of wearing."

"You didn't hand it away," Noor said. "You put it where it could do the most good. There is a difference. Regret is for children and cats."

"Ha."

"But." She shifted, and her voice did something he'd never heard it do—went quiet, went careful. "You should know what you actually gave me. Not the trick of talking to rats. That was never the gift. The gift was the *listening*. Fifteen years you listened to the smallest voices in this city and decided they mattered. That's why the beasts came for you in the crypt. Not because you called them. Because you were the first human in living memory who bothered to ask."

August found his throat had gone tight. "That sounds like something you rehearsed."

"I rehearsed it eleven times. You weren't supposed to notice."

"It was very good."

"I know." She stood, stretched, and her flames rose with her, taller than he'd ever seen them, gold shot through with white. "The mages leave at dusk. Ilse wants you to come see them off. She says—" Noor paused, savoring it— "she says you have a talent for making stubborn people listen, and that someone should use it for something other than selling love charms to teenagers."

"Tell her my rates are non-negotiable."

"She also says you should eat something. Everyone says that. Is everyone in this city your grandmother?"

"Effectively, yes."

Noor leapt from the roof edge—and instead of falling, she caught the air, and the air caught her, and for one long moment August saw what she was becoming: not a fox shaped like fire but a fire shaped like freedom, wings of ember unfolding where the tails had been, her true form arriving by degrees like dawn. Then she was gone over the rooftops, and the gulls scattered, and somewhere below a rat began, tentatively, to sing.

* * *

The book burned at noon.

They did it in the shop's back room, over the little iron stove August used to heat glue, because grand gestures were for people with more resources. Sefa carried the prophecy book herself—refused to let anyone else touch it, refused even Odile's offer of gloves. The thing had stopped writing the moment the final page tore free; it lay inert now, heavy as a paving stone, its remaining pages full of futures that had quietly become lies.

"You're sure?" August asked, though he already knew.

"The book gave us each other," Sefa said, "on a schedule, with a sacrifice attached. I'd like to see what we do without the supervision." She set it in the stove. "Also it tried to write my death twice. So. Personal reasons."

The pages caught slowly, reluctantly, ink flaring green before the flame took proper hold. For a moment the fire made shapes—a tower, a fox, two figures holding hands—and then it was just fire, and then it was ash, and Sefa stirred it with a poker until there was nothing left but gray dust and a smell like old rain.

Odile watched from the doorway, arms crossed, one eye still bruised from the cell they'd kept her in. "Fifteen years I taught you to hide," she said. "Best lesson I ever gave you was the one I failed to give: that hiding isn't the same as living." She sniffed. "You'll both come to dinner Sunday. Non-negotiable. I've decided to be insufferable about family now that I have any."

"Odile—"

"Dinner. Sunday. Bring bread, your charms are terrible and we all know it."

She left. Sefa slid down the wall to sit on the floor amid the ash-smell and the afternoon light, and patted the boards beside her until August sat too.

"So," she said. "Ordinary man."

"Completely ordinary. Devastatingly so. I tried to talk to a cat this morning out of habit and it just stared at me like I was rude."

"Welcome to everyone else's life." She took his hand again—the ordinary one—and laced her fingers through. "For what it's worth, I liked you before the book. Months before. When you were just the charm-seller who overpaid for candles and talked to pigeons in the market like a madman. The prophecy was an interruption. You were the point."

"Sefa Veyn, archivist, romantic."

"Don't tell anyone. I have a reputation for rigor." She rested her head on his shoulder. Outside, the choir had found its key change at last, and the sound drifted through the window, ragged and joyful and entirely unscheduled. "No book wrote this. No page predicted it. We just... did it. Isn't that strange?"

"It's the strangest thing I've ever done," August agreed. "And I once negotiated a peace treaty between two rival rat gangs over a disputed dumpling."

"I'm going to hear that story someday."

"Over Sunday dinner. Odile will corroborate."

They sat a while longer, in the warm ash-light, in a city learning to breathe. Tomorrow there would be forms to file with the new Wardens, and goodbyes to say to mages bound for the coast, and a shop to reopen, and a hundred small ordinary problems that no prophecy had ever bothered to predict. But tomorrow could wait.

Then Sefa sat up suddenly, frowning at the stove.

"August."

"What?"

"The ash. Look at the ash."

In the cold grate, among the gray powder, a single line of fresh black ink had appeared—unburned, impossible, curling across the brickwork in a neat archival hand:

*Chapter Eleven.*

August and Sefa looked at each other.

"...I thought we burned it," Sefa whispered.

"We did," August said. "So who's writing?"
