## 1. The Weight of Small Debts

The Ledgerhouse kept its accounts the way a miser kept his teeth: close, counted, and never shown to strangers. Ada Galloway had learned this in her first week, and she had learned it the way she learned everything—quietly, from the edges of rooms.

Winter came to Grayhollow early that year. By the time the frost fair banners went up along the Merchant's Span, the city's gutters had gone white and the canals wore skins of ice thin enough to crack under a gull's weight. Inside the Ledgerhouse, the cold was held at bay by old bargains—heat bought and paid for, entered in a column somewhere—and by the sheer density of paper. Ten thousand ledgers made their own weather. Ada worked among them like a fish among stones.

Her desk sat in the third tier of the Counting Hall, beneath a window so grimed with age that daylight arrived pre-filtered, gray as tallow. She shared the tier with eleven other auditors, none of whom knew her name. This was not an accident. She had spent four years making sure of it.

She was, officially, *Ada G.*—the initial standing for nothing, or for anything, depending on which clerk you asked. Her file at the door-registry listed her trade as *auditor, second class*, her origin as *south districts*, her references as *on file*. The references were forged. The origin was invented. The name itself was a coat she'd cut for herself out of cheap cloth: common enough to pass unnoticed, plain enough to be forgotten five minutes after any conversation ended. When people asked where she'd trained, she said *a small house upriver*, and when they asked which one, she looked at them with such mild, apologetic blankness that they stopped asking. Invisibility, she had discovered, was mostly a matter of being aggressively unremarkable. It was a discipline. She practiced it daily, the way other women practiced needlework.

It had kept her alive for four years. She reminded herself of that now, dipping her pen, opening the winter accounts.

* * *

The audit was routine, which meant it was tedious, which meant it was safe. Every autumn the guild called in its small debts—the three-copper favors, the borrowed umbrellas of luck, the half-hours of memory pawned by drunks who'd regret it sober—and every winter Ada reconciled them against the master ledger before the solstice closing. It was work that required patience, a steady hand, and no curiosity whatsoever. She had all three in abundance.

Or she had believed she did.

She began with the eastern district rolls. A widow had pawned her grief for bread money—a standard transaction, grief being one of the more liquid commodities, easily bottled, easily resold to mourners who wanted to feel things properly at funerals. The widow had repaid in full. Ada noted it, closed the entry, moved on. A ferryman had borrowed six months of his own youth to court a woman too young for him; the youth had been returned wrinkled and resentful, but returned. Noted. Closed. A cooper's apprentice had sold his shadow for a season and bought it back at a markup he'd weep about later, if he ever noticed. Noted. Closed.

Ada worked the way water works—without hurry, without stopping, wearing down whatever resisted her. Around her, the Counting Hall murmured with the sounds of the guild's quiet industry: the scratch of pens, the soft thump of ledger-spines, the occasional cough of a clerk who had inhaled too much dust off documents older than the kingdom. Somewhere below, in the vaults, the guild's stranger inventory slept in labeled drawers. Bottled griefs. Borrowed years. Names that opened doors. Ada had seen the vault exactly once, on her hiring day, and had made a point of never looking curious about it again.

Curiosity, in the Ledgerhouse, was a debt. Everything here was.

She reached the western rolls near midday, working through entries written in the cramped hand of Master Quillon, who dotted his i's like knife-points and never used a straight line where a crooked one would do. Quillon handled the collateral accounts—the serious ones, the ones where what was traded couldn't be weighed on any scale. Ada read his entries with the particular care she reserved for dangerous things: quickly, and without letting her face change.

Most of them were ordinary. A voice pawned against a gambling debt. A first kiss sold by a girl who wanted, the margin note said, *to stop remembering it*. A man's talent for whistling, purchased by a client whose reasons were nobody's business but the client's and the guild's.

Then she turned the page, and her hands stopped being hers.

The entry was short. That was the first wrong thing. Collateral accounts ran long—conditions, clauses, contingencies, the guild's whole architecture of entrapment rendered in careful script. This one was four lines. Quillon's hand, but hurried, the knife-point dots slightly drunk.

*Received into holding: one soul, entire, unencumbered.*
*Collateral against: debt of honor, House of Vess to this Guild, sum undisclosed.*
*Consideration given: three favors, nature unspecified, to be named by debtor.*
*Status: active.*

Ada read it twice. Three times. The words didn't rearrange themselves into sense. They stayed exactly as impossible as they were.

A soul, entire. Not a shard, not a season of it, not the grief-shaped sliver the guild usually dealt in. An entire soul, taken into holding as collateral—which meant someone had defaulted, which meant somewhere in the guild's possession was a person walking around hollow, breathing and eating and laughing at jokes without anything home behind their eyes. And the consideration—three favors. Not coin. Not years. Favors. As if a soul were worth what you might ask a neighbor: watch my shop, feed my cat, hold my place in line.

Her pen was still moving. She noticed this distantly, the way you notice your own heartbeat when it goes strange. She had written something in the audit column—*verified*, probably, or *in order*—because her hands were trained to keep working even when the rest of her had left the room. Four years of discipline, doing its job while she stood inside herself and stared.

*House of Vess.*

She knew that name. Of course she knew that name. She had spent four years not knowing it, which is a full-time occupation, and she was very good at her work.

Vess. Hedge-witch. Teacher. The woman whose door Ada had slipped out of on a night much like this one, frost furring the window-glass, with a stolen grimoire under her coat and her heart going like a trapped bird. The woman who had taught her letters in three dead scripts and the grammar of bargains, and who had wanted, in the end, to teach her something else entirely—something Ada had run rather than learn.

Ada had told herself the story many times since, in the dark, in the voiceless way one tells oneself things: *I didn't steal from her. I escaped her. There's a difference.* The difference was thinner than she liked. But it had held. It had held for four years, this side of a sheet of paper, and now here was Vess's name in the guild's own books, attached to a soul sold for the price of a neighborly kindness.

She became aware, gradually, that the Counting Hall had continued around her—that clerks still scratched, that dust still settled, that Master Quillon himself was crossing the far end of the tier with a stack of folios under his arm, nodding to no one, seeing no one. Seeing no one was the guild's collective genius. She had hidden inside it for four years.

And now her hands were shaking over a single entry, and shaking hands, in the Ledgerhouse, were a kind of signature.

She pressed her palms flat against the desk until the tremor died. Breathe. Audit. The entry was marked *active*, which meant the debt stood, which meant the soul was still in holding—not yet consumed, not yet filed away into whatever drawer held the guild's unreturnable stock. Active meant recoverable. Active meant there was still a person, somewhere, missing the middle of themselves.

Active meant it wasn't too late.

The thought arrived uninvited and unwelcome, because thoughts like that were how people got caught. Ada had built her whole life on the principle that other people's catastrophes were weather—you could note them in a column, you could feel briefly sorry, and then you could go indoors. She had watched the guild ruin hundreds of lives from behind her gray window, and she had written *verified* beside each ruin, because the alternative was unemployment, and unemployment was exposure, and exposure was death. Or something worse. The guild had ways of collecting from the exposed.

But the entry would not behave like weather. It sat on the page like a splinter, working deeper every time she looked at it. *Three favors.* Who took three favors for a soul? Either the debtor had been desperate past reason, or the guild had been greedy past honesty, or—

Or both. Or the third thing, the thing she was afraid to think: that Vess had known exactly what she was trading, and had considered it fair.

Ada pulled the entry toward her and copied it out in her own hand, letter for letter, onto a slip of scrap paper no larger than a playing card. This was against procedure. Procedure said audits stayed in the hall, in the book, under seal. Procedure said auditors did not carry fragments of the guild's business out into the world like loose coins. She folded the slip anyway, small, smaller, until it disappeared into her sleeve, into the seam she'd sewn there years ago for exactly this kind of sin, though she'd never once used it.

Four years of never using it. Four years of being a woman named Ada G., south districts, references on file, opinions none, history none, danger none.

The bell above the Counting Hall rang the hour—three of the clock, the short dark afternoon already leaning toward evening. Around her, auditors stretched and rose and gathered their coats, and the great machine of the guild exhaled toward supper. Ada closed the winter accounts, squared their corners, blotted her columns, and performed the small ritual of appearing to have done nothing interesting whatsoever.

In the stairwell, descending through the smell of ink and cold stone, she let herself think it plainly, once, the way you let yourself touch a burn to confirm it's real:

*A child. It would be a child. Souls like that—entire, unencumbered—they come from children. Adults have already spent theirs in pieces.*

She didn't know why she was sure. She only knew that certainty had arrived whole, the way certain knowledge does, fully formed and terrible, and that it had brought with it a memory she'd been keeping in a locked room for four years: Vess's voice, low and urgent, on the night she ran—*You think disappearing is safety, girl? Nothing is free. Not even hiding. Someone always pays the price of your survival, and someday the bill comes due, and I pray to every bored god it isn't me that pays it.*

Ada had thought those words were a threat. Standing in the stairwell with a stolen fragment of ledger against her wrist, she understood, with a lurch like a floor giving way, that they had been a warning.

Outside, the frost fair was lighting its first lanterns along the canal. Music, thin and bright, drifted across the ice. Somewhere in the crowd, a vendor was selling roasted chestnuts, and children were running between the stalls with their breath steaming, spending the last gold of the afternoon.

Ada pulled her hood up and walked toward the sound, because walking toward the light was what invisible people did, and because she needed, very badly, to be surrounded by ordinary human noise before the shaking started again.

Behind her, in the third-tier window, the gray light failed by degrees. On her desk, precisely squared, the winter accounts waited for the solstice closing—all in order, all verified, all except one entry that she had copied and carried away like a coal cupped in bare hands.

She would tell herself, later, that she went back the next night only to look. That was true, in the way that the first lie in any ruin is technically true.

What she found at the back door of the Ledgerhouse, on the night of the frost fair, was not in any ledger at all.
