## 2. A Child at the Door

The frost fair came to Grayhollow the way debt came to a poor household: announced for weeks, dreaded in advance, and somehow worse than promised.

From the Ledgerhouse's third-floor window, where I spent my evenings reconciling accounts no one else could read, I could see the market square strung with lanterns made of blown glass and frozen breath. The ice-sellers had carved their stalls into shapes of swans and wolves. Children ran between the legs of adults with candied apples held high like torches. Somewhere a fiddler was playing badly enough that everyone would remember it fondly by spring.

I did not go down. I never went down.

That was the arrangement I had made with the world, or rather the arrangement I had stolen from it: I would be useful, quiet, and unremarkable, and in exchange no one would ever look at me long enough to see what I was. Four years in the Ledgerhouse and I had perfected the art of being furniture. I signed my work as *A. Galloway* because a ledger needs a name, but I had chosen one so plain it might as well have been *A. Table*. The guild master called me "the auditor" the way you'd call a broom "the broom." It was the safest thing anyone had ever given me.

So when the knock came at the back door — three small knocks, spaced like a heartbeat skipping — I almost didn't answer it.

Almost.

The back door of the Ledgerhouse opened onto Tallow Lane, which on frost-fair night should have been empty, everyone having gone toward the lights and the fiddler and the bad sweet wine. The knock came again. Small knuckles. I knew this because I pressed my eye to the peephole we were all forbidden to use, and there she was.

A girl. Six years old, maybe seven. Barefoot on the frozen cobbles, in a shift too thin for the season, her hair cropped short and unevenly, as if someone had cut it in a hurry or in grief. She wasn't shivering. That was the first wrong thing. The second was that she was looking directly up at the peephole, as if she knew exactly where my eye was.

The third wrong thing was what happened when I opened the door — because of course I opened it. I want to say it was kindness. It was partly kindness. But it was also four years of careful invisibility curdling suddenly into something reckless, and I have learned since that those two things live closer together in me than I'd like.

She looked up at me and said:

*Nen vetha sollum. Nen vetha, ada.*

I did not move. The cold poured past her into the hall and I let it, because moving would mean admitting I had heard it, and hearing it meant admitting I understood it, and understanding it meant I was not who I had spent four years pretending to be.

It was not any language of the nine kingdoms. It was not High Tharic or Low Tharic, not the trade-tongue, not the salt-moor dialects, not the river cant the bargemen used. It was older than all of those. It was the language Mistress Vess had muttered over her mortar when she thought I was asleep — the language of the grimoire's margins, the one she'd told me once, drunk on plum brandy, that no living tongue spoke anymore, because everyone who spoke it had gone somewhere else.

Gone under, she'd said. Gone down.

The girl said it again, patiently, the way you repeat yourself to someone slow: *Nen vetha sollum.*

And here is the part I have never told anyone until now, set down in ink where at least the ink can be burned: I half-understood her. Not fully. The words arrived in my head like fish seen through dark water — shapes, movement, meaning just below the surface. *Nen vetha* was something like *open* or *unfasten*, but pointed inward, at a person, not a door. *Sollum* was a name-shape. And *ada* —

My name. Not A. Galloway. Not the auditor. *Ada.* My true name, the one I had buried under four years of meticulous anonymity, spoken in a dead language by a barefoot child on the coldest night of the year.

"Where is your mother?" I asked, in the trade-tongue, because asking normal questions in a normal voice felt like grabbing a rail over a drop.

The girl tilted her head. Then she answered — not in the old tongue this time but in something worse: fragments of the trade-tongue stitched together wrong, like a dress sewn from scraps of other dresses. "Mother. Is. Under." She touched her own chest, then the ground. "Under. Where the ledgers go."

I looked at her feet. Blue with cold, filthy, and — I bent down despite myself — unmarked. No chilblains. No frost-nip. As though the cold had been asked politely not to touch her.

Every instinct I had honed since fleeing Vess's cottage screamed at me to close the door. Send her to the parish house. Forget her face. Invisibility was not a luxury; it was load-bearing. The whole architecture of my survival rested on the fact that nobody in the Ledgerhouse knew I could read the pre-kingdom scripts, that nobody knew I'd been raised by a hedge-witch whose grimoire I still dreamed about, that nobody knew my name was worth anything to anyone.

But the girl reached out and took the hem of my sleeve. Just held it. Two fingers, light as a bird landing.

And I stood there in the doorway of the only safe place I had ever built, holding the door open against every rule I lived by, and said the stupidest sentence of my life:

"You'd better come in before someone sees you."

* * *

Getting her upstairs without being seen was easier than it should have been, because on frost-fair night the Ledgerhouse emptied itself. Even the door-wardens drew lots for who got to attend the fair, and the losers drank mulled wine in the gatehouse and pretended not to sulk. I took the servant stair with the girl's hand in mine — her palm was warm, which was the fourth wrong thing — and installed her in the records room on the third floor, the one where I worked, the one nobody entered after dusk because they believed it was haunted.

It was haunted, actually. But the ghost kept different hours than us, and I judged they wouldn't overlap.

I sat her in my chair and wrapped her in my spare cloak and put bread and honey in front of her, and she ate with the focused gravity of someone performing a ritual rather than a meal. Up close I could see details that rearranged my dread into something more specific. There was a mark on her collarbone — a small spiral, inked or scarred, I couldn't tell, turning inward like a fingerprint. And when she swallowed, the candlelight did something wrong around her throat, a refraction, as if she were slightly more *there* than the air around her, or slightly less.

"What's your name?" I asked.

She looked at me with an expression I recognized from mirrors: the look of someone checking whether a question is a trap.

"Sollum," she said finally. Which was not a name. Which was the word for *name*, in a language that shouldn't exist, spoken by a child who seemed to be missing hers the way a book is missing its title page — everything else intact, binding sound, pages crisp, and yet something essential gone, leaving a shape-shaped absence.

"I'm going to ask you some questions," I said. "You don't have to answer."

She nodded solemnly, as if we had signed terms.

"Where did you walk from?"

"Under," she said, touching the floor again. Then, correcting herself with visible effort, dragging words up from somewhere deep: "Up. From under. To up." She frowned at her own mouth, dissatisfied with it.

"How long?"

She held up her hands, all ten fingers, then folded them closed, then opened them again. Either fifty days or infinity. With her, I suspected both answers were true.

"Do you know what the Ledgerhouse is?"

At that, something moved behind her eyes — not fear exactly. Recognition. She pointed at the shelves around us, at the thousands of ledgers in their thousands of spines, and said, in the old tongue, one word:

*Vetha.*

Unfastened. Opened. Or — and this was the reading that made my hands go cold on the table — *owed*.

Because here is what I had spent four years auditing and never once letting myself think about too hard: the Ledgerhouse does not only trade in bottled griefs and borrowed years. There are entries in the deep ledgers, the ones written in scripts most clerks can't parse, that record transactions in a currency older than coin. The guild master calls them "legacy instruments." Legacy. As if age laundered them.

Souls. Collateral. Names sold outright, or pledged against debts, or — in the oldest entries — taken as payment from people too desperate or too ignorant to know what they were signing.

I had told myself, for four years, that auditing those entries was neutral work. That recording a sin accurately was not committing it. That the woman who fled a hedge-witch's cottage rather than become what her mistress wanted could still keep her hands clean by keeping them busy.

The girl ate her bread and watched me with patient black eyes, and I sat across from her doing arithmetic I didn't want to do: barefoot, no frostbite, a soul-mark on her collarbone, speaking the language of the court beneath the mountain, arriving at the back door of the very guild that dealt in souls — on the one night of the year when the whole city was looking elsewhere.

Children don't find the Ledgerhouse. The Ledgerhouse finds children. Which meant either she had been delivered, or she had escaped, and I genuinely could not decide which possibility frightened me more.

* * *

They found us at the ninth bell.

Not because we were hidden badly — I had killed the candles and we sat in the dark like conspirators — but because the girl began to hum. A tune with no kingdom in it, a tune like water finding its level, and the door-warden on the stairs heard it through three locked doors and came up to investigate, and I had perhaps four seconds to decide whether to lie.

I lied. Badly. "Found her at the back door. Frost fair strays. I was going to take her to the parish in the morning."

The warden, whose name was Brannock and whose suspicion was proportional to his wine intake, squinted at me, at the small shape bundled in my cloak, at the crumbs on the desk. "Guild property isn't a nursery, Galloway."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't."

But he'd already turned to go fetch the guild master, because a stray child in the Ledgerhouse after dark was above his pay grade, and I stood in the dark listening to my own pulse and thinking, with terrible clarity: *you had one job, Ada. Be furniture. Be the broom.*

The guild master arrived within the quarter hour, which told me something too. Master Ondrell was not a man who hurried for strays. He was a narrow, silvering man with the particular stillness of someone who has spent decades learning how much force a situation actually requires, and he entered the records room the way he entered everything: as if taking inventory.

He looked at the girl for a long time.

She looked back. And then she did the thing that decided everything, though none of us knew it yet. She spoke to him — not in trade-tongue, not in the old language, but in *his* voice. His exact voice, pitch and grain and the slight wet click at the end of his sentences, saying words I couldn't follow but that made Ondrell's hand go to his collar as if something had tightened there.

"What did she say?" I asked.

Ondrell didn't answer me. He was watching the child the way a man watches a scale settle. When he finally spoke, his voice was perfectly level, which is how I knew he was rattled.

"She said," he told the room, "that she knows what I filed away last spring. Under seal. In the vault."

Brannock, by the door, went pale. I kept my face still and my heart hammering and thought: *she reads people the way I read ledgers. She opens them.*

Ondrell straightened his cuffs. Whatever had flickered through him was packed away with a clerk's efficiency. "Interesting," he said. "Assessment, then. Brannock — the cellar room. Third key. She stays tonight; the parish won't take strays on festival nights anyway, and the guild has protocols for... unusual acquisitions."

Acquisitions. He said it the way he said everything, mildly, and it landed on me like a slap anyway.

"She's a lost child," I said. "Not an acquisition."

He turned to me then, and gave me the look he gave incorrect entries — not anger, just correction. "Everything that comes through that door is an acquisition, Galloway. That is what the House *is*. You of all people should appreciate the distinction between sentiment and inventory." A pause, thin as a knife. "Unless you'd like your handling of this stray reviewed?"

There it was. The hook under the bait. Review my handling — pull my thread — discover that A. Galloway had no history before four years ago, no family, no apprenticeship papers, nothing but immaculate work and a name chosen for its dullness.

"No," I said. "Of course not."

"Good." He gestured, and Brannock took the girl's arm. She didn't struggle. She looked back at me once, and said, in the old tongue, quietly, the way you remind someone of a promise:

*Nen vetha, ada.*

Open, Ada. Unfasten.

Then the door closed behind her, and I stood alone in the dark records room surrounded by ten thousand ledgers, every one of them a door somebody had fastened shut, and I understood — with the sick, settling certainty of a figure that finally balances — that I was going to open one of them.

I told myself I would sleep on it. That discretion was survival. That one child in a cellar was a tragedy, but a dead auditor was a statistic, and the smart move, the move four years of careful anonymity demanded, was to walk her existence out of my mind by morning.

Instead I sat down at my desk, lit one candle, and pulled the winter audit ledger toward me. Because there was one thing I hadn't checked, one entry I'd skimmed past weeks ago and flagged only as *anomalous script, defer review* — and I remembered now, with the girl's voice in my ears, why the date on it had snagged my eye even then.

It was dated fifty days ago. The same span her ten fingers had shown me.

Entry 4471. A sale recorded in the pre-kingdom script. Collateral received. Destination: *under*.

I turned to it, and my hands were shaking before I'd read a single line.
