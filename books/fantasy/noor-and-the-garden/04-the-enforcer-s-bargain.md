## 4. The Enforcer's Bargain

Corvin Dain had spent eleven years learning to read a room the way other men read ledgers: line by line, entry by entry, looking for the figure that didn't add up.

The reading room of the Ledgerhouse at half past midnight added up wrong.

He'd come down for the same reason he came down most nights now that they'd stripped his badge and his rounds and left him nothing but a desk and a title with no duties attached—because sleep was a debt he couldn't collect, and because the archives were the only place in the guild where the silence didn't feel like punishment. He kept to the far carrel, the one with the broken lamp, and he copied out old collection reports in his own hand, over and over, the way a man might copy scripture if scripture had ever once forgiven him. It was penance disguised as diligence. He was very good at disguises. It had been his trade.

Which is why he noticed her.

The woman at the third carrel had been there since the frost fair crowds thinned, and she was doing everything wrong in ways that were almost right. She sat like a clerk—spine straight, quill moving, eyes down. She had the right shawl, the right ink-stained fingers, the right air of a junior auditor buried in winter accounts and resentful about it. But she'd turned her chair four degrees too far from the door. A clerk who wanted to be seen never did that. And every time the stair creaked—and the stairs in this building creaked on a schedule you could set a watch by—her quill stopped. Not slowed. Stopped. Dead still, mid-stroke, until the sound passed.

Clerks flinched at interruptions. Thieves froze.

Corvin had collected from thieves for a decade. He knew the difference the way he knew the weight of a locked strongbox by its sound.

He also knew her face, though she clearly believed he didn't. Ada Galloway. Hedge-witch's runaway, three years in the House, promoted twice for being invisible and accurate, which in this guild were the same virtue. He'd filed her intake assessment himself, back when filing things was still his job. Quiet girl. Careful girl. The kind of careful that comes from having been hunted once and deciding never again.

And tonight, careful Ada Galloway was not auditing winter accounts.

Winter accounts lived in the blue-bound folios, second shelf, west wall. She had them in front of her, yes—a screen of respectability—but beneath them, angled just enough to catch lamplight, lay a ledger bound in grey calfskin with a brass clasp gone green at the hinge. Corvin knew that binding. Grey calfskin meant the Debt Registry, sub-vault, restricted access, senior signature required. He knew it better than he wanted to. Three years ago he'd carried a ledger exactly like it up a mountain road, and what he'd carried back down had cost him everything he'd been.

She turned a page. Her hand wasn't steady.

Corvin should have reported her. That was the arithmetic of survival in this place: you saw something, you said something, and the House remembered who'd helped it keep its books balanced. He had no badge anymore, but he still had ears, and ears could be re-earned. One word to Master Quillon—one word, *the Galloway woman is in the restricted registry*—and he'd be back inside the warm circle of the guild's trust by morning.

He thought about it for the length of one full page turn.

Then he thought about a cellar door, and a child behind it, and the sound a small body makes when it stops trying to speak because no one answers.

He got up, took his copied pages, and walked past her carrel without looking at her. As he passed, he set a slip of paper on the edge of her desk, weighted under an inkwell, and kept walking.

On the slip, in his flat enforcer's hand: **You're holding your page upside down. West alcove. Ten minutes. Come alone or don't come.**

* * *

She came, of course. He'd known she would. People who run toward danger dressed as paperwork always come; it's the sensible ones who stay away.

The west alcove was a dead pocket behind the map cabinets, built for storing charts nobody consulted anymore, lit by one guttering lamp that the cleaning staff had never gotten around to replacing because the alcove was officially empty. Corvin stood with his back to the shelves and watched her come around the corner of the cabinets with her chin up and her hands loose at her sides—the posture of someone who has decided that if this goes badly, she'll go down swinging, and who has probably never actually swung at anything in her life.

"You held your registry page upside down," she said. No preamble. Good. Preamble was for people with time.

"I did." He let that sit. "For about ten seconds, while I decided whether to report you."

"And then?"

"Then I read the rest of it." He nodded at the grey ledger under her arm, and something flickered across her face—not guilt exactly. Guilt would have been easier. This was closer to grief wearing guilt's coat. "Entry forty-one," he said quietly. "Collateral clause. Sub-vault seal. You went looking for something specific, and you found it."

Her jaw tightened. "Say what you mean, enforcer."

"I'm not an enforcer anymore. They took the badge in spring. Kept the memories, though. Those don't file off." He pushed off the shelf and stepped into the lamplight, letting her get a full look at him—the grey at his temples that had arrived early, the scar through his left eyebrow that apprentices whispered about, the general weathered fact of him. "My name is Corvin Dain. Eleven years' service. And three years ago I rode to a village called Harrowmere and collected a debt owed to this House by a man named Tam Wicker, and the collateral on that debt was not coin, and not goods."

The lamplight buzzed. Somewhere above them, the building settled, timbers ticking like a cooling engine.

"The collateral," Corvin said, "was a little girl's soul. Her father signed for it. I witnessed the signature. I carried the receipt home in an iron box so it wouldn't whisper to me on the road." His voice stayed level. He'd practiced level for years; it was load-bearing now. "I've had three years to tell myself the man knew what he was signing. Three years to tell myself the House only does what the contracts say. And then last night I walked past the cellar stairs and heard a child speaking a language that predates every contract this guild has ever drawn, and I stopped being able to do the arithmetic."

Ada hadn't moved. But her grip on the ledger had changed—from carrying it to guarding it.

"You heard her too," Corvin said.

"Half of it." The words came out of her like something pried loose. "Maybe less than half. Enough to know it isn't madness, and it isn't mimicry. It's old. Older than the charter. Older than the kingdoms the charter predates." She looked down at the ledger as if it might bite. "The entry says her soul was taken as security against her father's default. And her father defaulted in autumn. So the House holds it now. Holds *her*."

"Holds the shard of her," Corvin corrected, and hated how fluently the terminology came. "The body walks around. The name, the fire, whatever you want to call the part that makes a person a person instead of a shape—that's filed downstairs in the sub-vault, in a lead case, in the dark, accruing interest." He laughed, short and joyless. "That's the part that used to keep me up at night, back when I still slept. We charge interest on souls, Mistress Galloway. Compounded quarterly."

"Don't call me mistress."

"No?" He tilted his head. "Everyone here calls everyone mistress or master. It's how we pretend the ledger doesn't apply to us."

Something moved behind her eyes—recognition, maybe, of a fellow practitioner of pretending. Then she shut it away, quick as a clerk closing a drawer.

"Why am I here, Corvin Dain? You could have had me flogged out of the House before dawn. You could have bought your way back into favor with one sentence. Instead you wrote me a note in your own hand, which means if I talk, there's evidence against you too." She was smarter than her file suggested. He'd have to remember that. "So either you're stupid, or you want something."

"I want to un-collect a debt," Corvin said. "And I can't do it alone, and neither can you."

"That's not wanting something. That's wanting two somethings."

"It's wanting the same something twice, which is worse." He rubbed the bridge of his nose. Tired. He was always tired now; disgrace sat on the chest like wet wool. "Listen to me, because I'll say this once and then we'll both go back to being strangers. You think the hard part will be getting into the sub-vault. It won't be. I know the sub-vault. I could draw you the locks in my sleep, and some nights I do. The hard part is that even if you steal the shard—even if you carry it out of this building past every ward and watcher—the debt doesn't die. Collateral returns to the debtor, not the thief. The moment the House notices the case is empty, they don't hunt the shard. They hunt the *claim*. And the claim is written in the Registry in ink that doesn't fade, under seals that don't break, and the only thing in the world that voids a sealed claim—" he watched her face as he said it, watched her arrive ahead of him, because of course she had, she'd been living in the charter for three years— "is proof the original sale was fraudulent. Bad faith. A signature obtained under false pretense. Contested debt. And contested debt gets argued in front of the court that honors such things, which is not any court with a roof on it."

Silence. The lamp guttered and steadied.

"You know all this," she said slowly, "the way a man knows a route he's walked."

"I walked it." He turned half away, because some things were easier said to shelves. "Harrowmere. Tam Wicker owed the House three favors—he'd borrowed luck, real luck, the bottled kind, when his crops failed and his wife was dying. Luck like that has to be paid back with interest, and interest like that eats farms. By the time I got there he had nothing left but the thing the brokers had told him was worthless anyway." Corvin's voice did not change, which took work. "They'd told him a child's soul-shard was a trinket. A pretty nothing. That the girl wouldn't miss it, wouldn't even feel it, that it was like selling a baby tooth. I stood in his kitchen and watched him sign, and I *knew*—I knew in my gut, the way you know weather—that the broker had lied to him. That 'worthless' was a sales pitch. But knowing isn't proof, and my job wasn't truth. My job was the receipt."

"What happened to the girl?"

"She stopped talking. Within a season. Just—stopped. Words went out of her like water out of a cracked bowl. Her father drank himself dead within the year, which the village considered justice and I considered bookkeeping." He finally looked back at her. "Then the mother died too, and the girl went to an aunt, and the aunt sold her onward, and somewhere in those transactions the House acquired custody of the body as well as the shard—which is how a barefoot child ended up locked in our cellar during a frost fair, assessed like damaged goods. And I let it happen. Again. Standing in another kitchen, watching another signature, doing my job."

Ada was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had lost its edge and gained something sharper underneath.

"You said the broker lied. Can you prove it?"

"I can prove the broker's standard practice. I can prove that the same language appears in six other Harrowmere-era contracts—'of negligible value to the bearer, freely relinquished'—in the same hand, with the same phrasing, which no honest broker uses because honest brokers describe assets, they don't pre-anesthetize the seller." He shrugged. "Whether that constitutes fraud in front of the court below the mountain depends entirely on who argues it, and how well, and in what tongue."

"In what tongue," Ada repeated, softly, and Corvin watched her arrive at the next door down the corridor of her own thoughts: the old language, the child's language, the scripts she apparently half-read and fully hid. He didn't ask. Asking was pressure, and pressure made people lie, and he needed her not to lie to him more than he needed to know her secrets.

"So," she said instead. "Your proposition. Say it plainly."

"My proposition is that we are two people the House has already taught to betray, which makes us the only two people in this building qualified to attempt something that requires betraying it." He held up a hand before she could bristle. "Not tonight. Tonight we go back to our desks and our carrels and our respectable insomnia, and we touch nothing, and we change nothing, and we become the two most boring employees in the Ledgerhouse. Because the first rule of stealing from auditors is that auditors notice motion. We move slow. We move boring. You keep your registry findings memorized, not written—I know you haven't written anything down, you're too good for that, which is exactly why I'm standing here. And in a few days, when the frost fair traffic gives us cover, we start pulling threads."

"Threads toward what?"

"Toward proof. Toward the loophole. Toward whatever it takes to make that claim contestable and that child whole." He paused. "And toward the part I can't do and you can't do and maybe nobody alive can do, which is the old scripts. The court below the mountain doesn't read Common. If we ever stand in front of it, we stand in front of it in *its* language, or not at all."

Something crossed her face then—fast, quickly buried, but not fast enough. Fear, yes, but underneath the fear, a kind of grim recognition, like a woman hearing her own name called from a house she'd burned down long ago.

"You know something about old scripts," Corvin said. Not a question.

"I know someone who did," Ada said. "Knew. Past tense." She adjusted the grey ledger under her arm, and her voice flattened into clerk-neutral. "That's my business. Yours is locks and routes and how not to get caught. Agreed?"

"Agreed." He almost smiled. Almost. "One more term. Mine. Non-negotiable."

She waited.

"When this is done—however it ends, vault or gallows—we settle accounts between us honestly. Every favor logged, every price named up front. No debts between us that either of us discovers later, in the dark, with interest attached." He extended his hand. "I've spent eleven years watching what undisclosed balances do to people. I'd rather hang clean."

Ada looked at his hand the way she'd looked at the registry—as though it might contain a clause in small print. Then she took it. Her grip was stronger than her hands suggested, and colder, and it did not linger.

"For the record," she said, releasing him, "I still think this is a trap. I think you're cleverer than you look and kinder than you admit, and I think either one of those could be the hook."

"It is a trap," Corvin agreed. "Just not mine. The House builds traps around anyone who starts caring about collateral. We're both standing in one already. The only question is whether we spring it together or apart." He stepped back into shadow, done with lamplight. "Go to bed, Mistress Galloway. Audit something harmless tomorrow. Smile at Master Quillon. Be boring."

"And the day after?"

"The day after," Corvin said, "we find out whether the ghost in your grimoire teaches as well as she used to."

He heard her breath stop—actually stop, one full second of arrested air—and heard the ledger clasp click shut under her suddenly tight fingers.

"You know about Vess," she whispered.

"I know about everyone in this House," Corvin said. "It used to be my job. Now it's going to be our weapon. Dawn's in five hours. Go."

She went. He listened to her footsteps fade up the archive stairs—quick, controlled, the walk of a woman already rewriting tomorrow in her head—and then Corvin Dain stood alone in the dark alcove among the forgotten maps, and allowed himself, for exactly the length of one breath, to feel hope.

Then he filed it away where he filed everything, and went back to his copying, and waited for morning.
