## 5. Rules of the House

The charter lived on the third floor, in a room nobody visited because everybody already knew what it said.

That was the beauty of it. The Ledgerhouse's founding document sat behind glass older than the guild itself, a scroll of vellum gone the color of weak tea, and its authority rested not on being read but on being *believed*. Clerks swore oaths on copies of copies. Enforcers carried memorized excerpts like knives. In eleven years, Ada had never once seen anyone open the case.

Which was exactly why she opened it.

She had told herself she was doing nothing unusual. The case required two keys and a witnessed entry in the access ledger; she had both keys—junior auditors were trusted with one, and the second hung on Master Hollis's belt from dawn bell to dusk bell, which meant she'd had to take hers at night, which meant she was already committing a crime, fine, yes, but a small one, a tidy one, the kind of crime that left no splinters. She signed the access book with her work-name—*A. Marsh, Third Auditor*—in her careful, characterless hand, and she noted the hour as regulation required, and she did not let herself think about how easily rules could be followed all the way into wrongdoing. That was, after all, the first thing the Ledgerhouse had ever taught her: legality is a corridor. It does not care where you are walking.

The reading room smelled of dust and lamp oil and, faintly, of the cellar below—the cold mineral breath of stone that had learned to keep things. Ada tried not to think about the cellar either. She failed, the way she failed at most things lately.

She unrolled the charter with both hands, weighting the ends with the brass rods provided for the purpose, and began at the beginning, because Vess had taught her—no. Because someone had once taught her that you read a contract from the front if you want to be deceived politely, and from the back if you want to know where the bodies are buried. She started at the back.

*Article the Last. On Dissolution.* Boilerplate. Guild property reverts to the Crown in the event of insolvency, excepting items of a soul-nature, which revert to their origin. Her eyes snagged on that phrase—*items of a soul-nature*—and she made herself keep moving. Somewhere under her feet, three floors down, a barefoot girl was sleeping in a cellar among bottled griefs and borrowed years, classified, provisionally, as an item pending assessment.

*Article the Second-to-Last. On Precedence.* Debts outrank debts by seniority of registration. A debt registered earlier may not be voided by a later claim. Fine. Expected. She copied the clause reference anyway, because thoroughness was her armor and she put it on even when no one was watching. Especially then.

Then, wedged between an article on interest accrual and one on the sanctity of collateral, she found it.

*Article the Ninth-and-Twentieth. On Contested Collateral.*

She read it twice. Three times. Her lips moved on the fourth pass, which was a habit she thought she'd killed years ago, and she pressed them flat and read it a fifth time, silently, word by word, like counting coins:

*A debt registered with this House may be contested before any tribunal of record within one year and one day of registration, or thereafter upon petition, should the collateral named therein be shown capable of declaring its own name. A name spoken freely by the thing itself shall constitute proof of personhood, and proof of personhood voids the classification of the collateral as property, and the debt secured thereby shall stand contested until adjudicated.*

There it was. There it *was.*

Ada sat very still in the empty reading room while something enormous and fragile unfolded in her chest, the way a paper flower unfolds in water—fast, irreversible, and doomed to go soggy if you handled it wrong.

Because she knew what this clause was. Everyone in the trade knew what this clause was, the way everyone knows about the loose brick in their own wall. It was the guild's conscience, filed away. Centuries ago, when the Ledgerhouse was young and hungry, it had bought and sold things that turned out to be people—household gods, river spirits, the occasional unlucky ancestor—and the resulting scandals had nearly drowned the guild in blood and litigation. So the founders wrote Article Twenty-Nine as a pressure valve: if the merchandise could stand up and say *I am*, then it wasn't merchandise, and the guild would rather lose a debt than a war. It was not mercy. It was risk management. But it was written into the founding law of the House, above amendment, above the guild master's signature, above everything except the Crown itself.

And the child in the cellar—if she could speak her name, she could contest the debt. Not escape it. Contest it. Drag it into a tribunal of record, force an adjudication, put the whole rotten sale on a table under lamps where honest accounting might do what honesty does.

Ada was already halfway down the stairs before she remembered she hadn't locked the case.

* * *

The cellar took some getting used to, which was a lie; it didn't take getting used to at all, you just stopped flinching eventually. The lamps along the wall burned low and blue—guild lamps, fed on something other than oil—and they made the shelves look like the ribs of a drowned ship. Bottled griefs murmured in their racks. Borrowed years ticked faintly, each cask a different tempo, so the whole room kept several kinds of time at once. And in the far corner, past the shelf of names-that-open-doors (each vial humming a syllable when you walked by, like insects in a wall), there was a cot, a blanket, and a small barefoot girl sitting cross-legged, drawing spirals in the dust with one finger.

They still hadn't given her boots. Ada had raised it twice and been told assessment came first, amenities after, as if the child were a shipment awaiting inspection. The frost fair had come and gone. Four days now.

"Hello," Ada said, and set down the bread.

The girl looked up. Her eyes were the unsettling part—not the language, not the spirals, but the eyes, which had the particular patience of something that had been waiting a very long time and intended to go on waiting without complaint. She looked at the bread. Then at Ada. Then she said something in that language that predated kingdoms, the one that lived in the marrow of old stones and the grammar of rivers, and though Ada understood perhaps one word in ten, she caught the shape of it: a question. Not *who are you*. Something more like *what do you want*.

"I found something," Ada said. "In the charter. I need to tell you, and I need you to understand me, and I only half understand you, so I'm going to do this badly."

She knelt, which put them at eye level, which was a trick she'd learned from Vess—no. From no one. From observation. She held up one finger.

"A debt," she said slowly. "Was made. With you. As the—" she searched, gesturing at the shelves, at the bottles, at the whole apparatus of the House around them— "*collateral*. Do you know that word?"

The girl tilted her head. Then she reached out, took Ada's hand, and turned it palm-up, and with one dusty finger she drew in Ada's palm: a spiral, tight and precise, the same spiral she drew everywhere, over and over, on floors and tabletops and once, memorably, in the condensation on a window. Ada had spent four days telling herself the spirals meant nothing, were just a child's fidgeting, the way some children draw houses or horses.

But drawn in her palm, slow and deliberate, the spiral felt less like a doodle and more like a definition. Like the girl was saying: *this. This is the word I have instead of your words.*

"You were sold," Ada said quietly. "To something under a mountain. Your—" she touched her own sternum, hesitated, touched the girl's. "This. Whatever this is. It was signed away. Three favors. Someone traded you for three favors, and the paper is upstairs, and I've read it, and it's real."

The girl did not cry. Ada had expected crying, had braced for it, and the absence of it was worse. The girl simply watched her with those patient eyes, and then she spoke—a short run of that old language, soft, almost conversational—and pointed at her own chest, and then shook her head.

No. *No*, unmistakably, in any tongue.

"What do you mean, no?"

The girl pointed at her chest again. Shook her head again. And then, with terrible clarity, she opened her mouth and made a sound—not a word, not the old language, just a sound, a thin exhale shaped like the beginning of something, a door opening onto a room with nothing in it—and closed her mouth again.

Ada felt it land in her stomach like a dropped coin.

She tried anyway. Of course she tried. "Say your name," she whispered. "Just—whatever it is. However it sounds. Say it, and I can take it to a tribunal, and everything changes. Can you do that? Can you try?"

The girl tried. Ada watched her try—watched her gather herself, watched her mouth move through shapes that produced air and nothing else, watched her press her hand flat against her own chest as if holding a door shut against a draft, and produce, again, that hollow non-sound. Like a bell with the clapper removed. Like a word whose middle had been cut out.

Because that was the transaction, wasn't it. That was the whole elegant horror of it. You couldn't sell a soul outright—every law, mortal and otherwise, forbade it, and souls were notoriously bad collateral, prone to escaping. So the guild's drafters had done something cleverer. They hadn't taken the child's soul. They'd taken her *name*—the true one, the deep one, the one that is the handle by which a self is lifted—and filed it away beneath a mountain as security on a debt. And a name, once filed elsewhere, cannot be spoken by its owner. It isn't censorship. It's *custody*. The child wasn't mute. She was *lien-ed*. Every attempt to speak herself came up against the fact that the essential word of her was being held in a vault three days' ride away, accruing interest.

The loophole required the collateral to declare its own name.

The collateral could not declare its own name because the name was the collateral.

Ada sat back on her heels in the blue lamplight and laughed—one short, ugly bark of a laugh that echoed off the grief-bottles and came back sounding like someone else.

"It's airtight," she said. "It's completely airtight. Whoever drafted your paperwork deserves to burn."

The girl reached out and, very gently, closed Ada's open hand around the piece of bread.

* * *

She went back up. She always went back up; that was the discipline of it, the choreography of a double life—you surface, you breathe, you file your reports, you smile at the right clerks, and then you descend again. She logged her visit as a routine welfare check, per protocol 12-c, in her characterless hand. She ate dinner in the common hall and tasted none of it. She lay in her narrow bed staring at the ceiling beams and ran the problem the way she ran audits: line by line, refusing to skip ahead to despair.

The clause needed a name, spoken freely by the collateral. Blocked. Unless—

Unless the name could be *restored* first. The article didn't say the name had to be spoken *now*, in the tribunal's presence. It said *capable of declaring its own name*. Capability. Present capacity. If the name were returned—even briefly, even partially—the child would be a person in the eyes of the founding law, and the debt would stand contested, and then it would be adjudicated, and adjudication meant scrutiny, and scrutiny was the one thing the guild master could not survive. He'd signed the sale himself. She'd seen his mark on the instrument, that fat confident loop of ink. He would burn the archives before he let a tribunal read that page.

Which meant the fight wasn't legal at all, really. The law was just the door. Getting the name back meant going to where the name was kept. Which meant going *under the mountain*, into the court that held it, and taking it—or trading for it, or arguing for it, or whatever you did with beings who filed souls like documents. And it meant doing it outside every protection the House offered, because the House was the seller. She would be robbing her own employer to enforce her employer's own founding law, which was either the most lawful thing she'd ever contemplate or the least, and she genuinely could not tell which.

And she couldn't read the old scripts. That was the wall she kept hitting, the one the audit had shown her weeks ago: the original instruments of sale, the pre-kingdom contracts, the court's own filing conventions—all of it written in scripts that predated the common alphabet, the kind of writing Vess had read the way other people breathed. She had the loophole. She had the will. She had, God help her, the guilt, which burned hotter and cleaner than either.

What she didn't have was the language. Or an ally in the world. Or a plan that didn't end with her name added to somebody's ledger.

She got up, lit her candle, and pulled the grimoire out from under the floorboard where it had slept for eleven years—the runaway's inheritance, the one thing she'd taken when she fled, heavy as a sin, bound in leather gone soft as skin. Mistress Vess's book. Full of scripts she could recognize and not read, marginalia in a hand she'd know anywhere, and somewhere in its pages, if the stories were true, a bound ghost who had once been the sharpest tongue in the hedge-witch's cottage.

Ada had run from that woman to become invisible. To be *A. Marsh, Third Auditor*, a woman with no past and no debts and no one to lose. And now the road forward ran straight back through everything she'd buried: the old tongues, the old teacher, the old failure.

She set her palm flat on the cover. The leather was cool. Then, faintly, like a pulse under a wrist, it was not.

"Well," Ada said to the empty room, to the sleeping House, to the girl three floors down drawing spirals in the dark. "I suppose I'm going to need you after all."

Under her hand, very softly, the grimoire turned its own page.
