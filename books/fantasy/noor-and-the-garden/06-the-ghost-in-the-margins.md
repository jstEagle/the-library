## 6. The Ghost in the Margins

The grimoire had been under her bed for three years.

Not hidden—Ada had told herself it wasn't hidden, merely stored, the way you store a knife you no longer intend to use but cannot quite bring yourself to sell. It sat wrapped in oilcloth beneath a loose floorboard, beneath the narrow cot in her rented room above a chandler's shop on Candlewick Row, and for three years she had walked around it the way you walk around a grave.

Tonight she knelt on the cold boards and pried the floorboard up with her thumbnail, because her hands were still shaking too badly to manage anything blunter.

She told herself it was necessity. The charter's loophole was real—she'd read it four times by candlelight in the guild archive, her finger tracing the archaic script while the archivist snored two desks away: *a debt may be contested where the collateral can speak its own name.* A clean rule. An old rule. The kind of rule that predated the Ledgerhouse itself, back when contracts were sworn aloud rather than signed in blood and ink, and the old scripts were full of such rules because the people who wrote them understood that names were not decorations. They were load-bearing walls.

But the child could not speak her name. Her name was what had been sold. Which meant the contest would have to be argued some other way—in the old tongue, in the old forms, in language precise enough that even a court of accountants could not wriggle out from under it. And the only person Ada had ever known who read those scripts fluently was the woman she had run from at nineteen, leaving a note written in code and a debt of gratitude she had never once acknowledged aloud.

Mistress Vess was dead. Ada had learned of it eleven months ago, from a funeral notice in a trade gazette, three lines long, no survivors listed. She had read it standing in a bread queue and felt nothing she could name, which was itself a kind of naming.

She unwrapped the oilcloth.

The grimoire was smaller than memory made it. A squat volume bound in grey-green leather gone soft as skin at the corners, its spine cracked in three places from decades of being thumbed open to the same pages. Vess had never been precious about it. Other hedge-witches kept their books chained and warded; Vess left hers on the kitchen table between the salt crock and the onion basket, because she said a book that lived like furniture never lied to you about what it was.

Ada opened it to the first page and the smell rose up out of it—tallow smoke, dried yarrow, the particular sourness of iron-gall ink aging into rust—and her throat closed so suddenly she had to sit back on her heels and breathe through it.

*You are being ridiculous,* she told herself. *You are a grown woman with a forged identity and a guild salary. You are kneeling on your floor crying over a cookbook.*

Because that was what it looked like, mostly. Remedies. Cures for colic and cow-rot and the sweating sickness. Instructions for finding lost sheep. Vess had been a hedge-witch of the unglamorous kind, the kind who got paid in eggs and gossip, and the grimoire reflected it: page after page of practical craft, annotated in a cramped left-slanting hand that Ada could have reproduced blindfolded, having spent six years copying it.

But the margins—

That was why she'd really come. Not the remedies. The margins.

Vess had written in her margins the way other people muttered: constantly, irritably, in a mixture of common script and the older characters underneath it, the pre-kingdom glyphs that the Ledgerhouse's founding charters were drafted in and that maybe forty living scholars could parse. Ada had spent her girlhood absorbing them without knowing she was doing it, the way you absorb a house's creaks. She could read perhaps a third of what was there. Enough to be dangerous. Enough to know that the third she couldn't read contained exactly the grammar she needed—the formal language of contestation, of challenge, of *naming against naming*.

And enough to know something else, something she had noticed at sixteen and filed away and never mentioned to anyone, including herself:

The margins answered each other.

It was subtle. A note in Vess's hand about the proper dosage of willowbark, and beside it, in ink of a slightly different brown, in a hand that was almost Vess's hand but pressed lighter, as if the writer were tired or far away—a correction. A second opinion. Sometimes agreement. Once, memorably, an argument spanning three pages about whether grief could be bottled whole or had to be decanted, which Ada had assumed was Vess arguing with herself, because who else would it be?

Now, holding the book with the floorboard gaping beneath her, Ada let herself know who else it was.

Ghosts bound to books were rare but not legendary. It was old craft, pre-kingdom craft, the kind of thing the guild had spent two centuries pricing out of existence because a bound ghost couldn't be taxed, tracked, or traded. You didn't bind a ghost on purpose. You wrote yourself so thoroughly into a book—your habits, your arguments, your corrections—that when you died, the book noticed. And books are terrible gossips. They keep everything.

Vess had written herself into this book for forty years. Of course she was still in it.

"Of course," Ada said aloud, and her voice cracked in the middle of the word, and she gave up on dignity entirely.

* * *

She prepared the summons the way Vess had taught her to prepare everything: cheaply, precisely, without flourish.

No circle chalked on the floor—Vess had called circles "theatrical fencing." No candles beyond the one already burning. What the old scripts required was simpler and worse: attention. Undivided, honest attention, offered like payment. You did not command a ghost in the old forms. You *invoiced* one. You stated what you wanted, what you brought to the exchange, and you let the dead decide if the terms were fair.

Ada sat cross-legged on the floorboards with the grimoire open across her knees, turned to the page where Vess had recorded the rite itself—not as a spell but as a recipe, wedged between a cure for hives and a note about a neighbor's disputed fence line, because Vess had considered summoning the dead roughly as remarkable as mending a hinge.

*To speak with the keeper of this book,* the margin said, in the old glyphs with a common-script translation beneath, *state your need plainly. State what you offer. Do not flatter. Do not apologize. The dead have heard both too often.*

Ada took a breath that hurt on the way in.

"Mistress Vess," she said. "I need the grammar of contestation. The full forms. I need to argue a debt before a court that thinks in ledgers, and I need to do it in the old tongue, and I can't—I'm not good enough alone."

Her voice sounded very small in the rented room. Downstairs the chandler's boy dropped something and swore.

"What I offer—" She stopped. This was the part she'd rehearsed and it collapsed anyway, because every offering she'd drafted felt like bribery and every honest one felt like exposure. In the end she said the true thing, which was the only currency the old forms respected:

"I offer the truth, which is that I ran away from you and never sent word, and I have regretted it in a way I refused to examine, and I am examining it now because I need you, and needing you is the most expensive thing I own."

Silence.

The candle flame leaned, though there was no draft. The room's warmth pooled strangely, gathering toward the open book the way water gathers toward a drain.

Then the margins moved.

Not the words themselves—they stayed fixed—but the space *around* them, the white of the page darkening faintly, like breath fogging glass, and in that fogged space new letters surfaced. Slowly. Laboriously. As though the hand writing them had to reach a very long way.

*Three years,* the margin wrote. *Eleven months dead. And she comes at midnight to discuss grammar.*

Ada's eyes stung. "I came as soon as I—"

*Do not apologize,* the margin reminded her, in Vess's exact cadence, the dry clipped rhythm Ada could hear as clearly as if the woman stood behind her. *I taught you better.*

"You didn't teach me at all," Ada said, and something in her chest unclenched and bled all at once. "That was the problem. You taught me letters and dosages and how to gut a chicken, and you never once taught me what any of it was *for*, or who you were, or why a woman who could raise ghosts chose to spend her life curing cow-rot in a village that didn't like her. You taught me everything except the parts that mattered, and then you acted wounded when I left to find them."

The fog on the page stilled. For a long moment nothing wrote itself, and Ada thought—with a lurch—that she had insulted the ghost into silence, that pride survived death in this woman the way ivy survives winter, and that she had just spent her one summons on an argument.

Then, slowly:

*Fair,* the margin wrote. *Accurate. Insolent.* A pause, and then, in smaller letters, almost shy: *I missed insolence. The dead are terribly polite.*

A laugh broke out of Ada, wet and startled, and she pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes until the room stopped swimming.

"I need you to teach me," she said again, steadier now. "All of it. The contestation forms, the court protocols, the—whatever else the old scripts hold that I don't know I need. There's a child. Eight years old, maybe. Barefoot. Mute, except she isn't mute, she speaks a language that predates kingdoms, and the Ledgerhouse sold her soul to the court under the mountain to cover a debt, and I found the entry myself, in my own neat little auditor's hand, because I audit their accounts, Vess. I keep their books. I ran from your kitchen to hide inside the largest ledger in the world, and the first thing I found in it was a child priced at three favors."

The page fogged and cleared, fogged and cleared, like breathing.

*A soul,* the margin wrote. *Collateral.* And beneath it, in strokes gone suddenly hard and deep enough to score the paper: *I know that court. I dealt with its collectors twice, in my fifties, over a boundary dispute they tried to convert into a soul-debt. They are honest the way frost is honest. They will honor the letter of any agreement and the spirit of none.*

"So I need the letter," Ada said. "Perfectly. Better than theirs."

Another pause. When the writing came, it was slower, more deliberate—the ghost thinking, actually weighing, and Ada realized with a chill that whatever happened next was not sentiment. It was negotiation. Vess had died and become exactly what she'd always been: a party to a contract.

*I will teach you,* the margin wrote. *The grammar. The forms. The history the guild burned. Everything in these margins and everything I never wrote down because I didn't trust paper.*

Ada's heart lifted—and then dropped, because she had been raised by this woman, and no contract ever arrived without its final clause.

"The price," she said flatly. "Name it."

The fog gathered, dispersed, gathered again. When the answer came, it came in the old glyphs first, formal and binding, and only afterward translated into common script, as though the ghost wanted the obligation recorded in the serious language before softening it for her student:

*My price is this: I will name it later. When I know what the work requires. When I know what you have become. Until then, the debt stands open, unpriced, and you accept it freely or not at all.*

"That's not a price," Ada said. "That's a blank check."

*Yes,* the margin agreed, with something that in life would have been a raised eyebrow. *It is also the only kind of contract I ever trusted. A named price can be paid and forgotten. An open one keeps both parties honest—or at least attentive. You of all people should appreciate the accounting.*

Ada stared at the page. Every instinct she'd honed in three years at the Ledgerhouse screamed at her: never sign what isn't priced. Never owe what can't be calculated. Anonymity was safety; open debts were hooks; and this was a hook baited with the one voice she'd spent three years not hearing.

But the child was locked in a cellar below the guildhall, drawing spiraling maps of an underground court in bread crumbs and candle soot, and somewhere beneath the mountain her name sat in a filing system, patient as frost.

And there was this, too, which Ada made herself look at directly for the first time: the fear wasn't really about the blank check. The fear was that accepting help from Vess meant admitting the running had failed. That three years of meticulous invisibility had bought her nothing but a better view of other people's wounds. That the woman she'd fled had been right about something, and Ada would rather sign away her future than say so.

She put her palm flat on the page.

"I accept," she said. "Freely. Witnessed by the book, by the candle, and by whatever's left of my honesty. Teach me."

The warmth in the room shifted—settled, like a house at night when someone finally lies down. On the page, beneath the formal clause, one last line surfaced, small and slanting and unmistakably, achingly Vess:

*Then we begin tomorrow, at dawn, because you always were useless before coffee. Bring the child's drawings. And Ada—*

The pause stretched so long Ada thought the ghost had withdrawn.

*—you left to find what mattered. You found it barefoot in our doorway. I am not surprised. I am, however, going to be insufferable about it.*

Ada laughed again, and this time it didn't hurt, much.

* * *

She slept two hours, fully dressed, the grimoire on the pillow beside her head like a cat.

She woke before dawn to find the margins already busy. New annotations had surfaced overnight in the fog-brown ink—corrections to her own earlier reading, arrows pointing out glyphs she'd misparsed for years, and along the bottom of the title page, in fresh and impatient script:

*Up. Kettle on. Page 114. We have a great deal of ground to cover and, I remind you, I am dead, which makes mornings the only time I have.*

Ada lit the stove, put the kettle on, and turned to page 114.

At the top of the margin, in the old tongue she was only beginning to truly see, waited her first lesson—and beneath it, a single line in common script that stopped her cold:

*Lesson one: in the old forms, a name given freely outweighs a name taken by force. Remember that. It will cost you everything, and it will be the only thing that works.*

Outside, the frost fair's distant music had begun. Somewhere across the city, a barefoot girl was waking in a cellar, speaking the language of before.

Ada copied the lesson into her notebook, in her own hand, and began.
