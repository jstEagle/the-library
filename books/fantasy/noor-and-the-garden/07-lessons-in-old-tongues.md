## 7. Lessons in Old Tongues

The grimoire smelled of tallow and iron when it was open.

Ada had learned that on the first night. Closed, the book was just a book—cracked leather, swollen spine, the kind of object a pawnbroker would wave away without opening. Open, it breathed. The candle flames in her rented room leaned toward it like men leaning over a well.

"You're holding your mouth wrong," Vess said.

The ghost sat—or occupied the shape of sitting—in the chair by the window, where no draft could reach her because she was the draft. She had been dead eleven years and had lost none of her talent for making Ada feel like a first-year apprentice with ink-stained fingers and a head full of porridge.

"I'm holding my mouth *exactly* as you told me."

"And you're doing it like a woman reciting a grocery list. This is not a language you say. It's a language you *mean*. There's a difference, and the difference is everything." Vess's translucent hand cut the air, sharp as a paper knife. "Again. From the top. The word for *debt*."

Ada set down her pen, flexed her cramped fingers, and said it: "*Vesh-karai.*"

The candle nearest the window guttered. Somewhere below, in the street, a dog began to howl and then thought better of it.

"Better," Vess admitted, which from Vess was nearly a parade. "Now the word for *name*."

"*Sera-ith.*"

"Slower on the second syllable. You're swallowing the *i*. The *i* is the whole point. A name without its *i* is just a label, and labels are what the Ledgerhouse trades in. Names are what the old courts held sacred. Say it again and mean it."

Ada said it again. And this time—she couldn't have explained how—she did mean it. She meant the name she'd buried under three false ones. She meant the name locked in a cellar two streets away, sold for three favors and a forged signature. The word came out of her like something pulled by the roots, and the candles all stood up straight at once, and the frost on the window glass bloomed into a pattern of spirals that lasted four full seconds before melting.

Vess looked at the spirals. Then at Ada. For a moment the ghost's bitterness slipped, and underneath it was something older and more tired and almost tender.

"Good," she said quietly. "You always were better at meaning than at obeying. That's why you ran."

"That's why I ran," Ada agreed, "because you made obedience a survival skill."

"I made you a survivor. Obedience was the tuition." Vess folded her hands, an oddly solid gesture for someone made of cold air. "We'll break before dawn. You need sleep, and I need to stop pretending I can't see the girl watching us from the stairs."

* * *

The girl watched from the stairs every night.

She did it the way small creatures do everything: completely, without apology, as if watching were her work and she intended to be paid in full. She'd appear at the third hour, bare feet silent on the boards, and settle herself on the fourth step with her knees drawn up, and she would watch Ada's mouth the way a locksmith watches hands.

Tonight, though, tonight she didn't stop at the stairs.

Ada heard nothing—of course she heard nothing; the child moved like weather—but suddenly there was a warmth against her knee, and then small fingers closing around her wrist, and the girl was standing beside her chair, looking up with those eyes that were too dark and too still, like wells that had decided to be patient.

"What is it?" Ada asked. Then, feeling foolish, in the old tongue: "*Sereth?*" — *what do you want?*

The girl's face changed. Not a smile, exactly. More like a door opening somewhere behind her eyes. She tugged Ada's wrist downward until Ada's hand hovered over the open grimoire, over the line of script Ada had been butchering for two hours—the passage about contested debts, the loophole Vess had found, the clause that might save them all or might be a beautiful lie written by a dead woman.

Then the girl touched the page.

Not pointed. *Touched.* Two fingertips, resting on the letters the way you'd rest fingers on a sleeping animal's flank.

And the letters moved.

They didn't crawl or swim, nothing so vulgar. They simply *settled*, the way a house settles, each character shifting a hair's breadth into a truer position, and Ada felt the correction travel up through the girl's fingers into her own wrist, into her throat, and she understood—not in words, never in words—what sound those letters wanted to make. What they had *always* wanted to make. Her mouth shaped it before her mind could interfere:

"*Sera-ith vesh-karai orenn.*"

*The name is the debt's own key.*

The candles flared white. Frost raced across the window in ferning branches. In the corner, Vess made a sound Ada had never heard her make in life or death—a short, involuntary note, like a struck bell.

"Say that again," Vess whispered.

But the girl had already let go of Ada's wrist and stepped back into shadow, and the letters on the page had gone still and ordinary, and whatever current had run through the room drained away through the floorboards like water through sand.

"She corrected me," Ada said stupidly. "By touch. She—Vess, she can't speak, and she corrected me by *touch*."

"Pre-kingdom speakers," Vess said slowly, "didn't only use their mouths. The tongue was the least of it. Sound, breath, intention, contact—it was a whole-body language. That's why the courts beneath the mountain could hear it through stone. That's why—" She stopped. Her gaze went to the stairs, to the small shape already retreating upward into the dark. "That's why a soul sold in that language doesn't stay quiet. It keeps trying to speak itself. Through whatever vessel will carry it."

"Me?"

"You. Tonight. Congratulations." Vess's mouth twisted. "Do you understand what happened just now? You've been studying the language for six nights. Six. I studied for nine years before I could make frost bloom. And she handed it to you in one touch like a woman passing bread across a table."

"Why would she do that?"

"Because," Vess said, and for once there was no cruelty in it, only a bleak and ancient arithmetic, "you are the first person in her entire ruined life who has tried to learn *her* language instead of teaching her ours."

* * *

The child dreamed in colors.

This was how Ada learned it, in the nights that followed: not from words, since the girl had none, but from the way the dreams leaked. She slept in the narrow bed Ada had given her—the first bed she'd ever been given, judging by how carefully, how suspiciously, she'd approached it—and her dreams came off her like heat off a stone.

The first time it happened, Ada woke at the hour before dawn with the absolute conviction that she was falling upward through warm water, and that far above her something enormous and kind was waiting with its hands open, and that its hands were also a door, and that doors could love you. The feeling faded in maybe ten seconds. It left her weeping anyway, quietly, into the pillow, for reasons she could not have testified to under oath.

The second time, she woke tasting copper and hearing ledger-pages turning, thousands of them, a wind made of accounting, and she knew whose dream it was. She got up, crossed the cold room, and stood over the small sleeping figure until the taste faded.

The girl dreamed of the court beneath the mountain. Of course she did. Her soul lived there. Half of her, the truest half, filed away in some drawer in that beautiful bureaucratic dark, and every night the severed half reached for the kept half, and the reaching was the falling-upward, and the door-that-loved-you was—

Was the thing they were going to take back.

Ada stood there in the dark and made herself a promise with the old language running under it, wordless, structural, the way a foundation runs under a house: *I will learn this tongue until it lives in my teeth. I will walk into that drawer myself. I will carry you out.*

On the bed, the girl turned over in her sleep, and her small hand uncurled toward Ada across the blanket—an offering, or an answer, or simply the reflex of a creature that had finally stopped bracing for the hand that took things and started reaching for the ones that gave.

* * *

By the ninth night, they had a system.

Vess drilled. Ada stumbled. The girl corrected.

It worked like this: Vess would give Ada a phrase—the formulas of contestation, the grammar of witness, the precise cadences a debt-dispute required in the old courts, because the old courts, Vess insisted, had been lawyers before they'd been anything else, and their magic was procedure sanctified—and Ada would attempt it, and get it wrong in some way too deep in the mouth for her own ears to catch. And then the girl would rise from her step, cross the room without a sound, and lay her fingers on Ada's jaw, or her throat, or—once, memorably, flat on her sternum, which had produced a phrase so resonant that the neighbor's shutters rattled—and adjust her.

Touch by touch, the language installed itself.

"Tongue forward. No—*forward*, not up. You're shaping it like a Ledgerhouse clerk shapes his vowels, all efficiency and no reverence." Vess drifted around the room as she lectured, passing through the table edge without noticing, a habit that still unsettled Ada after nine days. "The old speakers believed a word spoken carelessly was a small theft—from the listener, from the world. Every utterance was a transaction. You are learning to *pay* when you speak. Do you understand? Nothing free. Nothing cheap. Everything exact."

"It sounds exhausting."

"It sounds like honesty." Vess paused by the window, looking out at the frost fair's distant glow, the lanterns strung along the canal like a necklace someone had dropped. "Your guild learned the same truth and built a business on hiding it. Words are transactions. Debts are real. Collateral has weight. They just stripped the reverence out and kept the receipts." Her voice curdled. "I taught in their academy for twenty years, Ada. I know exactly what they kept and what they threw away."

Ada bent over the page. "The formula for witness. Again."

"*Orenn sera-ith, karai vennath—*"

Wrong. She heard it wrong even as she finished it, a flatness in the middle syllables, a hollowness, and before she could try again, warmth touched her throat.

The girl stood beside her. Both hands now, cupping the sides of Ada's neck, thumbs resting just under her jaw, and through that touch came the correction—not instructions, nothing so crude, but the *shape* of the right sound, delivered directly, the way you'd guide someone's hand on a bow. Ada felt her own throat reshape itself around the phrase, felt the reverence arrive where the efficiency had been, and spoke:

"*Orenn sera-ith, karai vennath.*"

*I bear witness: the name is owed.*

Every candle in the room burned perfectly still. Not flickering. Still. As if the flames were listening.

"There," Vess breathed. "There it is. Nine years, and she gives it to you in nine days." The ghost laughed, a dry rustle. "Mind you don't waste it. That formula, spoken truly, binds the speaker. Whatever you testify to in that court, you will carry afterward. Truth spoken in the old tongue doesn't leave the speaker. It *stays*."

"Stays how?"

"How does anything stay?" Vess shrugged. "Ask your scars."

The girl released Ada's throat and stepped back—and then, unexpectedly, she did something new. She reached up and touched her own throat, mirroring, and her mouth moved around the same phrase, silent, practicing. Watching herself in Ada the way children watch themselves in mirrors.

And then, still silent, she smiled.

It was the first one. Eleven days, a stolen soul, a cellar, a flight planned across a salt moor—and this was the first time Ada had seen the girl's face do anything but endure. It was a crooked, lopsided, entirely ordinary child's smile, and it undid Ada more thoroughly than any spell had.

"Well," Vess said, from somewhere very far away, or perhaps very close. "Look at that."

* * *

They fell into it the way people fall into anything good: accidentally, and then deliberately, and then helplessly.

Nights became lessons. Lessons became rituals. The girl arrived at the third hour whether summoned or not, carrying a stub of candle she'd taken to hoarding like treasure, and she'd light it from Ada's flame with great ceremony, so that each session technically had two candles, hers and Ada's, burning side by side. She arranged the grimoire. She positioned the chair. She had opinions about the chair.

Vess pretended to disapprove of all of it and adjusted her lectures around the girl's presence so smoothly that Ada suspected the ghost had been lonely for eleven years and would have chewed glass rather than admit it.

"The subjunctive of restitution," Vess announced one night, pacing. "The form used when a debtor wishes to return something voluntarily rather than be compelled. Listen, because this is the heart of your entire ridiculous plan: the old courts honored voluntary return above enforced collection. Always. A soul returned freely outweighs a soul seized lawfully. It's in the root grammar. *Karai* means debt, yes, but *karai-oren*—debt returned by choice—that word carries honor in it. Honor, Ada. Your guild's founders took one look at that concept and ran screaming."

"So the court beneath the mountain might prefer we return the girl's soul willingly over the guild's claim of lawful purchase."

"The court might *rejoice*. Quietly. With paperwork." Vess's grin was terrible. "Bureaucracies love nothing more than a legitimate reason to refuse another bureaucracy."

While Vess talked, the girl sat cross-legged on the floor with Ada's spare quill, drawing. Spirals, always spirals, but lately the spirals had begun to resolve—into corridors, into arches, into a great descending stair rendered in confident strokes. Maps of the way down. She was mapping their route from memory, from the half of her that lived below, and every night the map grew longer, more detailed, closer to the door.

Tonight she finished a section, considered it, and then—without looking up—held the page out sideways toward Ada.

An invitation.

Ada took it. The paper was warm. On it, beneath the drawn stair, the girl had written—not drawn, *written*—three characters in the old script, small and careful, the ink still wet.

"What is it?" Ada asked.

Vess leaned in, and her translucence actually sharpened with attention. "It's a greeting," she said at last, and her voice had gone strange. "A formal one. The kind you'd offer a guest you intended to feed and shelter. In the old custom, you'd write it for someone you were claiming as—" She stopped.

"As what?" Ada said. "Claiming as what?"

"Kin," Vess said. "It says kin."

The candle flames leaned together, the girl's and Ada's, until they burned as one.

Ada looked down at the child, who was watching her with that bottomless patience, that well-water calm, waiting to see what the strange large woman who was learning her language would do with a word like that.

What Ada did was this: she took the quill, and beneath the three characters, in the same careful script, she wrote the reply-greeting Vess had drilled into her two nights before—the one offered to kin who had suffered loss, the one that promised shelter without conditions. Her hand shook. The characters came out slightly crooked.

The girl read them. Read them twice. Then she reached up and pressed her palm flat over Ada's heart, briefly, gravely, the way you'd seal a document—

—and outside, three streets away, a bell began to ring. Not the frost fair bells. Not the hour.

The Ledgerhouse's alarm.

Once, twice, and then a third ring, long and low, the tone reserved for exactly one circumstance, a tone every guild member had drilled into them until it lived in the spine:

*A breach. The archives have been opened without authorization.*

In the corner, Vess went very still, and said, in a voice gone thin as paper:

"They've found the missing key."
