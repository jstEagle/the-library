## 8. The Frost Fair

The fair came to Grayhollow on the longest night of the year, when the river froze hard enough to hold market stalls and the whole town pretended, for one evening, that the Ledgerhouse did not exist.

Ada had not been to the frost fair since she was nine years old. She remembered it wrong, of course—the way memory always lies about joy. In her recollection the lanterns had been gold. Now she saw they were cheap tallow in tin cups, guttering orange, and the ice beneath them was gray as old bone. But the sound was right: fiddles and haggling and children shrieking down the slide of packed snow by the millrace, and somewhere under all of it the low groan of the river shifting its weight.

She pulled her hood lower and counted exits. Old habit. The fairground had four: the bridge road, the mill path, the gap in the fishmonger's fence, and the frozen river itself if you were desperate or drunk. She marked them the way other people said grace.

Beside her, Corvin moved through the crowd like a man walking through a room he'd once been thrown out of. He wore no guild colors—hadn't for two years—but his shoulders still carried the shape of them, that particular set of a man who had spent a decade making strangers flinch. People stepped out of his way without knowing why, then turned to look at him and found nothing remarkable, which unsettled them more.

The child walked between them with her hand in Ada's pocket instead of Ada's hand, because the girl did not like to be held. She was wearing one of Ada's spare cloaks cut down and pinned, and her feet were wrapped in rags over wool, because boots cost money they could not yet account for.

She was looking at everything. That was the thing about Noor—though Ada didn't know her name yet, wouldn't for months—and it was the thing that made Ada's chest hurt in a way she refused to examine: the child looked at everything as if it might be taken away mid-glance. The lanterns. The sugar-dusted chestnuts. A dog asleep under a stall. She catalogued each one with those dark, unblinking eyes, filing them somewhere behind her silence.

"Stay close," Ada murmured, in the old tongue, because in Grayhollow only three people living understood it, and two of them were hers.

The child answered without moving her lips—a breath shaped into meaning, the way Vess had taught Ada to hear it. *Close is a word. Staying is a promise. Promises are debts.*

Even now, even here, the girl thought in ledgers. Whatever court had held her soul had taught her arithmetic before it taught her anything else.

*Not tonight,* Ada signed back, clumsily, fingers stiff in the cold. *Tonight nothing is owed.*

The child considered this the way she considered everything—with total, unnerving seriousness—and then reached up and took Ada's actual hand.

Ada's heart did something she would later refuse to record.

Corvin's POV

Corvin had a list. He kept it in his head, where no auditor could seize it, and he worked it the way he'd once worked collection routes: efficient, unsentimental, done.

Dried meat. Tallow candles. A needle and waxed thread. Salt—he took extra salt, because salt moor crossings ate a body's reserves and he'd carried men off that ground before, or what was left of them. A second knife small enough for a child's belt. And papers.

The papers were the hard part, and the papers were why he was here at all.

He found Ferren Wicks at the far end of the fair, past the mulled-wine fires, in a stall that sold "antiquities" which were mostly broken things with stories attached. Ferren was a forger the way some men were drinkers—not well, but constantly. He'd been expelled from the Ledgerhouse's scriptorium eleven years ago for selling blank charter pages, and he had never forgiven the guild, which made him useful, and never stopped being frightened of it, which made him dangerous.

Corvin set a coin on the counter and let Ferren see his face.

Ferren went pale above his scarf. "You're dead," he whispered. "Everyone says you're dead."

"Everyone says a lot of things." Corvin slid a folded sheet across: names, dates, a route south through the salt moor, descriptions vague enough to survive scrutiny. Travel passes for three. "I need these finished by midnight. Clean hands, Ferren. If there's so much as a smudge—"

"There won't be." Ferren was already reaching for his pen, because fear made him fast. Then he paused. "Three? You traveling with—" His eyes flicked past Corvin's shoulder, to where Ada stood at the edge of the firelight with the girl, and Ferren's face did something complicated. "That's the cellar child. They're saying the guild master's put a price on—"

"I know what they're saying."

"They're saying forty crowns, Corvin. Forty crowns and a pardon for whoever brings her in. A *pardon*. Men have killed their brothers for less than a pardon."

Corvin looked at him until Ferren looked away.

"Midnight," Corvin said, and left the coin.

He should have felt afraid. He noted the absence of fear the way an accountant notes a missing entry, and moved on.

The truth was, he had been doing arithmetic of his own these past weeks, in the cellar lamplight while the girl drew her spiraling maps and Ada bent over stolen ledger pages. Corvin had collected the debt that sold her soul. He had stood in a doorway very much like this one, in a house very much like any house, and taken a child as collateral because the charter authorized it and the signature was valid and he had been very good, for eleven years, at not looking at what he carried.

Forty crowns was what the guild thought she was worth.

Corvin knew better. He knew exactly what she was worth, because he had seen the entry in the original debt-book, in the margin, in the collector's hand—his own hand—and beneath it a line he had written himself and never understood until recently:

*Balance outstanding.*

He crossed the fair toward the cobbler's stall, where a pair of red boots hung from a nail, dyed the color of winter berries, too bright for anything practical.

He bought them without explanation, because some debts you pay forward, quietly, and never file.

The Child's POV

(Here the account must be given as she gave it, which is to say: as sensation, as color, as weight.)

Red.

The man-who-smells-of-rain-and-old-leather knelt in the trampled snow and held out two red things, and the whole fair went quiet inside her the way the deep places were quiet, and she understood that this was a door being offered.

Boots. She knew boots. Boots were what feet wore when feet belonged to someone.

She put her foot out. Her toes were cold; her toes were always cold; cold was the floor of the world and she had learned to stand on it. The boot went on and it was warm—warm because it had been sitting near the wine-fires, warm because someone had thought ahead, and thinking-ahead was a thing mothers did, and nurses, and no one else, ever, in her remembering.

The second boot. Laces. The man tied them in a strange flat knot, a soldier's knot, quick and ugly and strong.

She looked at his face. His face was doing the thing faces did when they were trying to be stone and failing at the edges.

In the old tongue—the true tongue, the tongue under all tongues, the one the mountain-court had tried to burn out of her and failed—she said: *Why.*

And the man said, in plain Grayhollow words, gruff, almost annoyed, as if the answer were obvious and he resented having to say it aloud:

"Because your feet are cold."

It was not, strictly speaking, an answer. It was the most honest sentence anyone had spoken to her since before the debt, and she filed it in the place where she kept things that must never be lost: the smell of bread rising, a green door, a woman's voice singing low, the red of the boots.

Then the lights of the fair flickered—all of them at once, every lantern on the ice bending sideways as if a wind had come through that touched flame but not skin—and the child's blood went thin and cold, because she knew that wind. She had felt it through the cellar floor. It was the wind that came before collectors.

Someone was reading her name.

Ada's POV

Ada felt it a half-second after the child did: a prickle along the jaw, the sensation of being *read*—of someone turning her over like a page. She had spent six years building herself into a person no spell could grip. No name in any register. No debts on file. A woman made entirely of margins.

But the pull wasn't aimed at her.

It was aimed at the girl.

Ada's eyes swept the crowd and found him in the shadow between the wine-fire and the antiquities stall: a narrow man in a gray coat, unremarkable except for his hands, which were held loosely at his sides in the guild spotter's stance—fingers spread slightly, as if feeling for a draft. A name-tracker. Second circle, by the silver pin at his collar, half-hidden. He wasn't looking at the child. He was looking at the space around her, the way you look at the space around a candle to find the flame.

*Forty crowns,* Ada thought wildly. *A pardon.* Ferren had told Corvin; Corvin had told her; and now the whole fair was worth forty crowns and everyone in it was a stranger with a price.

The spotter began to move toward them—not hurrying, working the crowd, closing at the pace of a man who already knew how the night would end.

Ada did the math. Bridge road: thirty paces, crowded, slow. Mill path: exposed. River: suicide. Fence gap: behind the spotter.

Behind the spotter.

"Corvin," she breathed, and Corvin was already turning, because two years of disgrace had not dulled whatever animal sense he ran on. He saw the gray coat. His face closed like a ledger.

"Fish fence," he said. "Now. Walk, don't run."

They walked. The child's new red boots left prints in the snow—bright, unmistakable, terrible—and Ada wanted to scream at the beauty of them. Behind them the spotter drifted closer, unhurried, certain, and Ada understood with sick clarity that he wasn't following the boots. He was following the *name*, and a name could not be wiped from snow.

Then the fair itself intervened, the way crowds sometimes do, gracelessly and perfectly: the sledding hill emptied its annual cargo of shrieking children directly across the lane, a tumbling avalanche of scarves and mittens and one overturned cider cart, and in the chaos Corvin lifted the girl onto his shoulders as if he'd done it a hundred times—which he hadn't, and which broke something open in his face that Ada wisely did not look at—and they cut through the fishmonger's fence gap into the dark of the alley beyond.

They ran. Three streets. Four. The child's breath fogged against Corvin's neck, and Ada ran beside them with her hood back and her heart slamming, and nowhere in any of it did she feel afraid for herself, and that frightened her more than the spotter had.

They stopped in the lee of the tannery wall, where the smell covered everything and no one lingered. Silence, except breath.

"He'll report by morning," Corvin said finally. "By dawn the guild master knows we were at the fair, and by tomorrow night there won't be spotters. There'll be collectors." He set the girl down. She immediately checked that both boots were still on her feet, patting them like small animals. "We leave tonight. Not tomorrow. Tonight."

"The papers aren't ready," Ada said. "Ferren needs until midnight—"

"Then we collect them at midnight and we're on the moor by one." Corvin was already retying the child's laces with those quick ugly knots, not looking up. "Ada. Listen to me. When they come, they won't come for the girl first." He glanced up, and in the dark his eyes were steady and awful. "They'll come for me. I'm the loose end. I'm the enforcer who went soft. Everything after that is paperwork."

The child reached down and put one small red-booted foot on top of his boot. Just that. Just weight.

Corvin went very still.

Above the tannery roofs, the last lanterns of the fair burned on, gold and ordinary, and somewhere out in that light a man in a gray coat was writing their description in a notebook, and the ink was already drying, and the longest night of the year had hours yet to spend.

Ada looked north, toward the black line of the salt moor, and heard Vess's voice in her memory, dry as old vellum: *Every escape has a toll, dear. The only question is who pays.*

At midnight, they would go and get the forged papers from a frightened man in a stall full of broken things.

At one, they would be fugitives.

But for ninety more minutes, standing in the tannery stink with a child in red boots between them, Ada allowed herself—briefly, secretly, against every rule she owned—to call it what it was.

Not a team. Not a scheme. Not a debt arrangement.

A family, running.
