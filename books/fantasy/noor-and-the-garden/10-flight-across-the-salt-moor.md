## 10. Flight Across the Salt Moor

**Ada**

The salt moor began where the road gave up.

One mile past the last tollgate, the packed earth simply ended, and white crust stretched to the horizon like a page no one had bothered to write on. Ada had seen it from the Ledgerhouse's high windows a hundred times—a pale smear between the guild's territory and the mountains—and had never once thought of it as a place. Places were for people with names to lose.

Now she was losing hers by the hour, and the moor was the only way across.

"Keep off the black patches," Corvin said behind her. He walked with the flat, economical stride of a man who had crossed worse ground in worse company. "Crust over brine pits. Looks solid, isn't. Step on one and you'll go down to the shoulder before you stop sinking."

"And how do I tell black from white in the dark?"

"You don't. You walk where I walk."

It was not tenderness. It was logistics. Ada had learned the difference years ago under Mistress Vess, who had loved her the way a carpenter loves a good joint—functionally, without sentiment—and she found, to her surprise, that Corvin's version hurt less. Vess's love had always come with an invoice attached.

The child rode on Corvin's back, her arms looped around his neck, her bare feet—still bare; the red boots from the frost fair were wrapped in sacking inside his coat, saved for reasons he hadn't explained—tucked against his ribs for warmth. She hadn't spoken since the stacks. Since Ada had burned her own ledger page and made them all into smoke.

Three days of silence now. Three days of walking through the nights because the hounds ran cooler after dark, sleeping in shifts in the hollows, eating hard bread and salt-preserved plums that tasted like punishment. The child ate what she was given and watched everything with those eyes that seemed to hold more weather than a child's face should.

Ada checked the sky out of habit. No lantern-glow from the east. Yet.

"They'll have our scent by now," she said.

"They don't need scent." Corvin shifted the child higher on his back. "They track names. That's what they're bred for. A name leaves a trail like spilled honey—everyone you've ever been called, every place you've said it aloud. The hounds follow the sweetest string back to the mouth."

"Then we're easy to find. We've been calling each other *Corvin* and *Ada* for three days."

"We have." He didn't break stride. "That's why we're still alive."

She waited for him to explain. He didn't. He was like that—information dispensed at the exact rate required, never a drop more, as if words cost him something at the source.

The child stirred against his neck. And then, without lifting her head, without opening her eyes, she spoke.

"*Sarn veth issa koreth,*" she said softly, in that language older than kingdoms, older than the guild, older than the salt itself. "*Issa koreth, maren.*"

Ada stopped walking.

Because she understood it. Not with her ears—with something deeper, some cellar of the mind where Vess's lessons had been poured and sealed. The words meant: *the small debt is paid, the small debt is paid, mother.* And they were not the child's words. They came out in another voice entirely, a woman's voice, dry and precise, and Ada knew that voice. She had spent six years trying to forget it.

"That's—" Her throat closed. "Those are Vess's words. From the night I left. She said that to me at the door. *The small debt is paid.*"

Corvin had gone very still. "Say again?"

"The child." Ada turned. In the starlight the girl's face was slack, dreaming even though her eyes were open. "She's speaking in fragments. Of things she couldn't possibly know. Vess died two hundred miles from here, and this girl just repeated her goodbye to me word for word, in a dead language, in Vess's own voice."

The child blinked slowly, like a cat surfacing. Then she looked at Ada with perfect, terrible clarity and said, in trade-common this time, in a voice that was only her own:

"You owe her a price. You promised."

* * *

**Corvin**

He had collected debts for eleven years. He knew what people looked like when they realized the bill had come due: the flinch, the arithmetic behind the eyes, the sudden interest in escape routes. He'd put that look on a thousand faces and slept fine afterward, right up until the night he hadn't.

He did not sleep fine now.

They made camp at dawn in a brine hollow, downwind of nothing, because there was nothing to be downwind of. The salt stretched white in every direction, cracked into polygons like the floor of some abandoned courtroom. Corvin built no fire. Fire was a signature. Instead he rationed out dried fish and let the child chew hers in small, methodical bites, watching the horizon over her head.

"She spoke your mistress's words," Ada said, low, beside him. "What does that mean? For the hounds. For us."

"It means she's waking up." He kept his voice as flat as the moor. "A soul that's been sold gets... thinned. Poured thin, like broth stretched with water. Most of them stay thin forever. But she's close to her own name now—the court holds it, and names pull. The nearer we get, the more of her comes back. And when a soul comes back, it comes back *hungry*. It reaches for whatever's near to clothe itself in."

"Near being—"

"People. Memories. Me. You." He finally looked at her. In the grey light her face was all sharp angles and sleeplessness, and he thought, with the detached professionalism he applied to everything now, that she looked like a ledger balancing badly. "When she speaks in my voice, you'll know it. When she speaks yours, don't answer questions she asks. Not yet. A soul gathering itself will take answers and keep them."

"That's horrifying."

"Yes."

But it was also, Corvin thought, the most hope he'd heard spoken aloud in eleven years, and he hated that he recognized it as such.

The child finished her fish and wiped her hands on her knees with grave thoroughness. Then she looked up at him—at him specifically, not past him as children usually did—and said, in a man's voice, gravel-deep, a voice Corvin had last heard coming out of his own mouth on the worst night of his life:

"*Take the boots out. She'll want to see them.*"

His hands went cold.

Because he had said that. Last night, or the night before—he couldn't remember which—to himself, silently, in the dark watch while the girl slept against his back. He had taken the red boots from their sacking and held them and thought exactly that, exactly those words, and told no one.

Ada was staring at him. "Whose voice was that?"

"Mine," he said. "From inside my own head."

The child tilted her head, considering him the way she considered everything: as if he were a document she was reading. Then she reached into his coat herself, pulled out the sacking, unwrapped the red boots, and set them side by side in front of her knees in the salt.

She looked at them for a long time.

Then she put them on. They fit. Of course they fit; he'd known her size the way he knew everything about her, from the file, from the collection, from the night he'd carried a soulless child out of a burning farmhouse and told himself the paperwork was in order.

"They're too big," Ada said quietly.

"No," Corvin said. "They're right."

The child stood up in her red boots, took three steps across the salt, and turned around. Testing them. Or testing him. Then she pointed—not at the horizon, not at the mountains, but at a patch of ground fifty yards off that looked identical to every other patch of ground.

"There," she said, in the old tongue first, then in common. "Water. Under the black. Sweet water, under the bitter."

Corvin went and checked, because a man who has betrayed everyone eventually learns to check everything. He cut through the crust with his knife, dug two feet down, and hit seep—a lens of fresh water sitting atop the brine table, cold and clean and impossible to find unless you already knew.

He crouched there a long moment, hand in the hole, and did not say thank you, because the child was watching him and he suspected she could hear the unsaid thing anyway.

* * *

**The Child**

The world was full of voices that were not voices.

Before, there had been only the quiet—the big quiet, the quiet like a room with the furniture taken out. She had lived in the quiet so long she had forgotten it was not the natural state of things. She had forgotten there was anything to forget.

Now the quiet was filling up. It filled from the edges inward, like water finding the low places, and what filled it was other people.

The tall one—Corvin, but the name sat on him like a borrowed coat—was mostly a gray color inside. Gray and gray and gray, and underneath the gray, a door. Behind the door, a fire. She could smell the fire sometimes when he carried her. Burning wood. Burning paper. A voice saying *sign here* while something behind his eyes screamed in a language he didn't have anymore. She didn't understand the screaming. She only knew it was his, and that he kept it locked, and that locks were heavy, and that carrying her plus the lock was why his shoulders hurt.

The other one—Ada, whose name fit better but was still not quite hers—was mostly edges. Sharp, careful edges, drawn tight like a page folded and folded and folded until it was small enough to hide. Inside the folds there was a garden. The child had seen it twice now, when the woman slept: a garden with a stone wall and a fig tree and someone kneeling in the dirt with dirt under her nails, laughing. The laughing person wore Ada's face but younger, and the garden was not any garden that existed anymore, and the child understood that this was what a hidden name looked like from the inside. Not a word. A place.

And beneath everything, far away, under the mountain, her own name was calling.

It didn't call in words. Names didn't, really—it had taken her three days of listening to other people's insides to learn that. A name called the way home calls: as warmth, as weight, as the shape of a door your hand remembers. Every hour it got louder. Every hour more of her came back to hear it. She was filling up with herself, and the overflow came out of her mouth in other people's voices, and she could not yet tell which parts were hers.

That frightened her less than it should have. Perhaps because she had been nobody for so long that being partly everybody felt like wealth.

In the middle of the day, hiding in a hollow from a light on the eastern horizon that moved the way hunters move, she lay between the two of them and listened to them not-sleeping, and she said, in the voice of a woman neither of them had ever met—a voice that belonged to Corvin's door, behind the fire—

"*You signed for her. You can carry her.*"

Corvin's breathing stopped for four counts. Then resumed, slower.

And later, in the voice of a young girl with dirt under her nails, she said to Ada: "*The wall needs mending before winter. You always said you'd come back to mend it.*"

Ada made a sound the child had no color for yet. It was small and wet and it happened once and then was folded away with all the other edges.

The child filed both sounds away carefully. She was learning to be careful with people. They broke easier than ledgers.

* * *

**Ada**

On the fourth night the hounds found their trail.

Not the hounds themselves—the trail. Corvin woke them an hour before moonrise with a hand on each of their shoulders, no shake, just pressure, and said one word: "String."

Ada understood immediately, and the understanding was nausea. "They've picked up our names. We've been speaking them."

"We've been *living* them. Doesn't matter. What matters is the string runs tautest where the name is spoken most. So we stop speaking." He was already moving, pulling the sacking from the gear. "New rule. From here to the mountain, we don't say our names, we don't say each other's, we don't write them, we don't think them loud. The hounds read loud thoughts like signposts. You think your name, they hear it."

"How do you know all this?"

"Because I trained them." He said it without inflection, which somehow made it worse. "Eleven years. I wrote the handling manual. Chapter three: the quarry's best defense is silence, and no quarry ever manages it, because people would rather die than stop being themselves."

The child was already standing, already dressed, already pointing west-northwest toward a darkness on the dark horizon. "There," she said. "The bitter pools. Hounds hate the bitter. Their noses are names, and the salt burns the names off."

So they ran.

Not ran—moved, fast and low, across crust that rang faintly underfoot like porcelain, toward a region where the salt had pooled into shallow brine lakes that reflected stars with ugly fidelity. Ada's lungs burned. Her legs burned. Somewhere around the second mile her body stopped sending complaints and started sending invoices instead, itemized, payable on delivery, and she paid them because the alternative was stopping, and stopping was dying.

Behind them, faint but gaining, came the sound.

She had expected baying. This was worse: a kind of chiming, high and sweet, like tiny bells, like a music box wound by something patient. The hounds' voices. Namesong, Corvin had called it once, in a briefing she'd overheard in another life. Each hound sang the name it tracked, and the song grew sweeter as the distance closed, until the quarry heard its own name sung so beautifully it wanted to walk toward the singing and lie down in it.

Ada heard her own false name begin, thin and lovely, somewhere behind them. *Ada. Ada. Ada,* sang the bells, and it was her name, the safe one, the invented one, the wall she'd built her whole life behind, and it sounded like rest, it sounded like forgiveness, it sounded like a warm room where no one had ever known her—

"Don't listen," Corvin gasped. "It lies. It sings the name you *wish*—"

"I know what it does!" But knowing didn't help. Her feet slowed. Her body wanted to turn. Six years of invisibility were being sung back to her as a lullaby, and some traitor part of her soul leaned toward it like a plant toward light.

Small hands grabbed hers.

The child had fallen back to run beside her, red boots flashing, and gripped Ada's fingers with a strength no child should have, and spoke—not in a stolen voice this time, but in the old tongue, sharp and imperative, and Ada understood every word because Vess had drilled her until the syllables lived in her teeth:

"*Your name is not the song. Your name is the singer. Run.*"

*Your name is not the song. Your name is the singer.*

Ada ran.

They crashed through the first brine pool together, the three of them, salt water exploding up their shins, freezing, stinging, and the chiming behind them faltered—flickered—rose again, angrier. The second pool. The third. Ada lost count. The world reduced itself to splash and burn and breath and the child's hand in hers, and Corvin's shape ahead breaking crust deliberately, choosing footing for all of them, and the bells falling further and further back, souring, dissolving into ordinary dog-sounds and then into nothing but wind.

They came out the far side of the bitter country an hour before dawn, soaked to the thigh, crusted white, half-frozen, and collapsed in the lee of a leaning salt pillar like three rag dolls dropped by the same careless hand.

For a long time nobody spoke. Speaking was dangerous. Breathing was expensive.

Then the child, lying on her back looking at the fading stars, said—in her own voice, her real one, the one that was starting to exist again, thin but unmistakably hers:

"When we get my name back. Will I still hear you?"

Ada turned her head. Salt crust crackled. "Hear us?"

"In the quiet. The way I do now." The girl's eyes were enormous and clear. "I don't want to lose it. It's the first thing anyone ever gave me."

Ada's chest did something complicated. "Some things you get to keep," she said. "Even after they're paid for."

The child considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then she rolled onto her side, tucked herself against Ada's ribs the way she'd tucked against Corvin's back, and closed her eyes.

Within minutes she was asleep. Within ten, she murmured something in her dreams—a fragment, soft, in a voice Ada almost recognized and couldn't place, a voice that made Corvin, keeping watch twenty feet away, go rigid as a struck post.

Ada looked at him. He shook his head once: *not now.*

But she had caught enough of the words to translate them, and the translation sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone all through the cold morning watch:

*The bridge is ahead. The bridge is ahead, and the keeper knows your face.*

* * *

**Corvin**

Dawn came up the color of old bone, and with it, far to the east, a smudge of dust that was not weather.

Corvin counted riders. Lost count. Counted again. Somewhere north of thirty, moving in the loose spread of a professional sweep, and ahead of them—not behind, *ahead*, cutting the angle—rode the thing he'd feared since the stacks: a spotter with a compass-needle eye and a warrant with all three of their descriptions on it.

Thirty riders they might outrun to the mountain. Thirty riders plus a spotter, plus the toll-keeper's bridge that was the only crossing over the gorge for forty miles in any direction—they would not outrun that.

He looked at Ada, asleep at last, salt-crusted and hollow-eyed, with the child curled against her like a comma against a line of text. He looked at the mountains, still two days' hard walking away, blue and indifferent on the horizon.

He thought about chapter three of his own handling manual. *The quarry's best defense is silence.*

He thought about the other thing he'd written, in the margin, in his last week before the fall, in ink he'd later be beaten for: *a debt is not discharged until the debtor chooses to pay it.*

Well. He was choosing.

Corvin settled his coat over the two sleepers, checked the horizon one more time, and began, silently, in the privacy of his own skull, to rehearse what he would say at the bridge—and what he would be willing to give the keeper, if giving was what it took.

The child opened her eyes without moving otherwise, and watched him do it, and said nothing at all.
