## 12. Vess's Price

The ghost had been avoiding her for three days.

Ada knew it the way she knew an account was cooked—by the shape of the absence. Vess's presence in the grimoire had a texture, a pressure against the margins, like a thumb pressed to the far side of a page. For three days that pressure had thinned each time Ada opened the book, until by Thursday night it was nothing but cold ink and a faint smell of camphor, as if the ghost had folded herself into some deep crease of the binding and refused to come out.

"She knows what I'm going to ask," Ada said to the child.

Noor—the name was still provisional, a placeholder, a ledger entry awaiting its true figure—sat cross-legged on the floor of the safehouse attic with a stub of charcoal and a sheet of butcher's paper, drawing spirals. She'd drawn spirals for six days straight. Corvin said they were maps. Corvin said everything was maps now: the moor, the fair, the guildhouse, all of it reduced in the girl's hands to coiling lines that Ada was beginning, with Vess's lessons half-finished and her head full of pre-kingdom declensions, to almost read.

The child looked up. Her eyes were the gray of old pewter, and they did not blink as often as other eyes did. She pointed at the grimoire on the table, then at Ada, then pressed her small palm flat against her own sternum.

"Yes," Ada said. "It's about me."

The girl went back to her spirals.

Ada had promised herself she would not beg. She had fled this woman once already, at nineteen, with nothing but a stolen cloak and the conviction that survival meant never owing anyone anything—and here she was, six years later, owing everyone: Corvin his wary alliance, the child her half-learned tongue, the Ledgerhouse a burned page that could never be unburned. Begging Vess would just be one more entry in a column that never balanced.

But the mountain court did not conduct business in Common Tongue. The Auditor's clerks kept their ledgers in the old script, the pre-kingdom hand that Vess alone could teach at speed, and Ada's pronunciation was still wrong in ways that mattered. She'd learned that much from Noor's touch—small fingers pressing her throat, adjusting the angle of her jaw, correcting sounds that no living teacher could hear properly anymore because no living throat made them naturally. The dead language lived most truly in a child whose voice had been sold out from under her.

"Vess," Ada said to the open book. "I know you're there. You're always there. That's rather the point of you."

Nothing. The candle guttered. Somewhere below, Corvin was re-waxing forged seals with the patience of a man who had once destroyed lives with less care than he now gave to counterfeiting them.

"I'm not going to apologize again," Ada said. "I've apologized. You called my apologies 'interest payments on a debt of cowardice,' which was very funny and also very you, and I've paid them. So." She pulled the chair around backward and sat astride it, arms folded on the backrest, the way she used to sit across from Vess's worktable when she was fifteen and pretending not to listen to lessons about contract law and the ethics of extraction. "You can sulk in the margins or you can finish teaching me. One of those gets your unfinished business carried where it needs to go. The other gets you bound in a book in an attic forever. Choose."

The candle flame stood up straight.

Then the ink moved.

It came up off the page like smoke deciding to be a woman: first the long hands, then the severe collar, then the face Ada had spent six years not dreaming about—sharp-boned, disapproving, mouth set in the particular line it took when Vess was about to say something devastating and wanted you to have time to brace.

"You mistake sulking for accounting," the ghost said. Her voice was dry leaves and chalk dust. "I have been reviewing my assets."

"You don't have assets. You're a marginalia."

"I have *leverage*, which is the same thing wearing better clothes." Vess drifted to the window, though the window showed nothing she could want—salt moor, gray sky, the petrified forest a black seam on the horizon. Ghosts, Ada had learned, looked at horizons anyway. Habit outlived the body that formed it. "You want the old script. All of it. Not the traveler's phrases I've been feeding you—not enough to order wine or insult a customs clerk, but the whole grammar, the formal registers, the legal formulae the mountain court will actually accept in a contested hearing. Do you know why you need the legal formulae?"

"Because the court runs on procedure."

"Because the court runs on *procedure*," Vess agreed, "and procedure is a language, and languages are how the powerful make cruelty look like arithmetic. You cannot contest a soul-debt in their house with a merchant's vocabulary. You need the oath-forms. The invocation of witness. The formula of free and unencumbered consent—which, incidentally, is the only sentence in that entire tongue that has ever been used honestly, and even then only twice." She turned. "That is what you are asking me for. Sixty years of study, compressed into whatever nights we have left before the guild's hounds find your scent on the moor. And you think I'll give it for nothing, because you're doing a good deed."

Ada held her gaze. Ghosts' eyes didn't quite focus; they focused slightly past you, at the version of you they remembered, or the version they feared. "Name your price."

"There it is." Something moved through the translucent face—pride, or grief wearing pride's coat. "You've grown a spine since you ran. It was the running that grew it, I expect. Nothing teaches posture like flight."

"*Price.*"

Vess was quiet for a long moment. Outside, wind came off the salt flat and worried the loose shingle. Below them, faintly, Noor hummed—a tuneless, spiraling sound, the melody of a language with no word for goodbye.

"My confession," Vess said at last. "There is a thing I never said. In life. Not to anyone. Not under torture, and I was threatened with it twice, and not on my deathbed, which I had three weeks to prepare and used to dictate shipping instructions instead." She lifted one long hand and looked at it as if checking it for ink stains. "A confession is not a secret. A secret is something you keep. A confession is something that *wants out*—it presses on you from inside, it changes how you hold your shoulders, it leaks into your handwriting if you're careless. Mine has been leaking for forty years. You may have seen it."

"In the ledgers?"

"In the ledgers. In the way I taught you. In the fact that I taught you at all." The ghost's outline flickered, steadied. "Hedge-witches do not take apprentices, Ada. We especially do not take apprentices who arrive on our doorstep with literacy in three scripts and a face like a locked drawer. Taking you was a risk so stupid that I have spent six years as a ghost trying to decide whether it was stupidity or something else. It was something else. It was guilt. And guilt requires a source."

Ada's mouth had gone dry. "What did you do?"

"That is precisely the question you will not get answered here." Vess's voice sharpened to its classroom edge. "This is the price, and hear all of it before you agree, because I will not repeat terms and I do not negotiate with former apprentices. When you stand before the Auditor—in their hall, under their law, in the old tongue—you will speak my confession for me. Word for word, as I teach it to you, in the form the court requires. You will deliver it as your own testimony, given freely, and you will let it stand in the record under your name."

The room tilted slightly. Ada gripped the chair back.

"Under *my* name."

"You haven't got one worth the ink, more's the pity, but yes. Under whatever name you present yourself under. Which means—" Vess raised a finger before Ada could speak, "—that you must present yourself under a real one. The court does not accept anonymous testimony. Anonymous testimony is how the Ledgerhouse operates, and the mountain court, whatever else it is, is honest about its cruelties. They will require a name, freely spoken, fully owned, accountable forever. And in that same breath, in that same standing-before-them, you will confess my crime as yours."

"That's—" Ada stood up. Sat down. Stood again. "That's two prices. You said a price."

"It is one price with two faces, like every honest transaction." Vess drifted closer, and the cold came with her, the particular grave-cold that smelled of camphor and chalk. "Think, girl. Use the mind I ruined you for ordinary work with. What happens when you speak a confession in that hall? You become accountable. Accountable people have names. Named people can be found, judged, punished—or forgiven, which is worse, because forgiveness creates obligation. You cannot carry my confession without surrendering your invisibility. The two are one act. That is the elegance of it. That is why I waited until now, when you understand exactly what invisibility has cost you." She gestured, one long finger, toward the floor—toward the child drawing spirals below. "Toward her."

Ada's heart was going hard. "Your crime. Is it connected to her? To the sale?"

The ghost was silent long enough that the candle burned down a visible quarter inch.

"You will learn it when you speak it," Vess said finally. "Not before. If I tell you now, you will rehearse it, soften it, edit it for mercy—and confessions edited for mercy are worthless. It must come out of you raw, in a language you barely hold, in front of a court that files souls like correspondence. That is the price. That is the whole price. Take it or leave it, and if you leave it, take your traveler's phrases down the mountain and watch a clerk void your contest on a technicality while a child spends eternity as collateral."

* * *

Ada walked.

She didn't decide to walk; her legs simply removed her from the attic, down the stairs, through the safehouse's low rooms, out the back door into the gray afternoon, along the drainage ditch toward the edge of the salt flat. Cold air. The moor stretched white and cracked to the horizon, and beyond it, a day's hard travel away, the petrified forest stood like the ribs of something the world had failed to finish eating. Beneath it, the door. Beneath the door, the court. Beneath the court, a soul in a filing cabinet, waiting for someone to argue paperwork on its behalf.

Survival has always had a price, she thought. She'd known that at nineteen. That was the whole revelation of her flight—that safety wasn't free, it was purchased, and the currency was other people. Vess's secrets. Corvin's career. The child's name. Every comfortable thing Ada had ever owned had been bought with someone else's coin, and she had built her invisibility on top of that pile like a house on landfill, telling herself the ground was solid.

The guild master sold souls for three favors. The enforcer collected them and lost his own name doing it. The mistress hoarded a confession for forty years until it killed her twice. Everyone pays. The only choice available to any of them was *what*, and *knowingly*, and *in whose place*.

She turned back.

Corvin was in the kitchen, sealing wax cooling in little red pools beside him. He glanced up, read her face in the single glance of a man trained to assess threats, and said nothing, which was his version of asking.

"Noor," Ada said instead. "Where is she?"

"Pantry. She likes it in there. Small rooms, she says—" he hesitated over the translation, as he always did, careful with the fragments the girl managed, "—she says small rooms hold their shape."

Ada found her sitting on the flour sack among the jars, knees up, drawing a spiral on the floorboards in spilled flour. The spiral had a door in it. The door had a keyhole shaped like a mouth.

Ada crouched down to her level. Up close the child smelled of woodsmoke and cold, and her pewter eyes held the particular patience of someone who had already waited longer than childhood should allow.

"I'm going to tell you a thing," Ada said, "and you won't understand all of it, and that's fine. I'm going to go to a place under the mountain and say some words that aren't mine. Bad words. Old words. Words a woman I ran away from has been carrying since before you were born. And when I say them, everyone in that room will know who I am. Really know. Forever." She swallowed. "And I'm telling you because you're the reason, and because someday you'll have prices of your own to name, and I want you to know that naming them out loud is allowed. Even when it terrifies you. *Especially* then."

The child considered her for a long moment. Then she reached out, took Ada's hand, and pressed it—gently, deliberately—against her own small chest, over the hollow place where a soul goes missing.

Then she pointed at Ada's chest.

Then she smiled. It was the first smile Ada had seen on her, and it was crooked, and it was ancient, and it undid her completely.

"Yeah," Ada said, voice cracking. "Yeah. Exactly that."

* * *

Upstairs, the grimoire lay open where she'd left it, and Vess stood at the window with her back turned, translucent hands folded, looking for all the world like a woman waiting for a student who was late.

"I accept," Ada said.

The ghost did not turn around. "Say it properly. In the old tongue. Contracts in that language bind deeper—it's why the court uses it, and why I taught it to you badly on purpose until tonight."

So Ada closed her eyes and reached for the words Vess had drilled into her over six nights of candlelight and chalk: the formula of agreement, the syllables Noor had corrected by touch, throat and jaw and breath arranged just so. She spoke them slowly, feeling each one land somewhere below language, in the place where promises become weight.

When she finished, the room was colder. The candle flame had gone blue at its base.

Vess turned. And for the first time since the summoning—since the death, maybe, since forty years before the death—her face was unguarded. It looked, Ada thought, like a locked drawer finally opened: not treasure inside, exactly. Just space. Room that had been pressed shut for decades, breathing again.

"Good," Vess said quietly. "Now sit down. We begin with the invocation of witness, and you will get it wrong eleven times, and I will enjoy every one."

Ada sat. She dipped the pen. The ghost began to speak in the language older than kingdoms, and Ada wrote it down word by word, transcribing a confession she was forbidden to read ahead of time, her own hand becoming the vessel for a crime she wouldn't understand until she stood in a hall beneath a mountain and gave it, freely, under a name she hadn't yet chosen to say aloud.

They worked until the candle died. They worked by moonlight after that, because ghosts see fine in the dark and students must learn to.

Near dawn, footsteps on the stairs—Corvin, gray-faced, moving fast in the way that meant bad news delivered at a controlled pace. He stopped in the doorway. Behind him, Noor stood in the stairwell, barefoot still, one hand gripping the banister, the other pointing east through the window. Toward the petrified forest. Toward the black seam of roots on the horizon.

"She's ready," Corvin said. "She says the door is listening."
