## 13. The Door Beneath the Root

The world has too many edges.

That is the first thing the girl knows when they leave the salt moor behind and climb into the hills where the trees turned to stone before her grandmother's grandmother was a rumor. The moor was flat and honest—it hurt everywhere at once, evenly, like a blanket made of cold. But this place is all corners. Stone branches. Stone leaves that have not fallen in ten thousand years and never will. Her feet, inside Corvin's red boots, keep expecting bark to be soft.

It is not soft. Nothing here is soft. But something here is *hers*.

She walks ahead of them without deciding to. Her legs know the way the way a river knows downhill.

Behind her: Ada, whose name is a held breath, whose pockets are full of paper that whispers. Behind her: Corvin, who smells of iron and of a door he once closed on her, though he does not know she remembers that. She remembers everything now. That is what the hollow in her chest has become—a room where other people's memories live, shelved and waiting, and lately the shelves have been filling with hers again too, coming home one splinter at a time.

She does not tell them she remembers Corvin's boots from before. Some things are kinder as secrets.

"The maps say the entrance is near the old quarry," Ada says, for the fourth time since dawn. She has a leather roll of Vess's drawings, and she keeps unrolling it and frowning at it as if the drawings might apologize. "But there's no quarry marked past this ridge."

The girl stops. Turns. Looks at Ada with the patience of someone who has waited three hundred years under a mountain and can wait out one more frown.

*Not a door you find,* she thinks at her, hard, the way she used to think before she had words, before the words were taken and given back wrong. *A door that finds.*

Ada blinks. Lately, when the girl pushes thoughts at her, Ada's face does a small thing—a flicker, like a candle hearing its own name. "You know where it is," Ada says. Not a question anymore. She is learning.

The girl nods and points with her whole arm, the way pointing is done properly, and walks on.

* * *

The petrified forest begins without ceremony. One step there are pines, black and ordinary, dusted with frost; the next step the trees are stone, gray-white and gleaming, every needle preserved mid-shiver. It is like walking into a held breath. The girl puts her palm against a trunk as she passes and feels nothing, which is itself a kind of feeling—the absence of sap, of pulse. These trees died so completely they became permanent. She understands them. There was a time when she was becoming permanent too, filed and shelved and done.

But she got out. Most of her got out.

The part of her still down there pulls. She follows the pull.

Corvin's hand comes to rest on his knife the way it always does when the land goes quiet. "No birds," he says.

"No roots either." Ada crouches, brushes snow from the ground between two stone trunks. Underneath: bare stone, seamless, no soil, no moss. "Nothing grows here. Nothing *grew* here, maybe ever. This forest didn't turn to stone. It was poured."

"Vess's notes say the court grew the forest as a lock," Corvin says. "A forest of keys, actually. Every tree a key that doesn't fit any door we'll ever see."

"He would say that," the girl thinks, and almost laughs, because Vess—who lives now in a grimoire and in Ada's mouth—had told them exactly this in her dry rasp: *the mountain is vain, child, it builds locks it alone can open, and vanity always leaves a back door.* The girl is the back door. She has known this since the cellar, since the bread and the spiraling maps, since Ada looked at her drawings and went pale as paper.

She leads them deeper. The stone trees thicken. Their branches knit overhead until the sky is only a memory of gray light, and their roots—here is the thing, here is the thing she has been walking toward since the frost fair—here their roots do not go down.

They go *in*.

The girl stops at the edge of it and lets herself feel the terror properly, because Vess taught her that too: *name the fear or it names you.* The roots of the petrified forest do not disappear into the earth. They braid together above the ground into walls, arches, vaulted corridors—a palace of root grown upward instead of down, hollowed, ribbed like the inside of a chest. And at the heart of it, where all the braids converge, is a shape her eyes keep sliding off of.

A door.

It is not carved into the roots. It is *grown*, the way a pearl is grown around a wound. Dark wood that is also gray stone that is also, when she tilts her head, something warm and living underneath, like flesh remembering it was wood. No handle. No hinges she can see. Only a seam, thin as a hair, running floor to lintel.

And across the seam, in letters older than kingdoms, older than the language she speaks in her sleep:

*a name.*

Her name. The stolen one. Written where the door opens, because down below, in the beautiful bureaucratic dark, they file everything, and the label of a thing must be kept with the thing, and the door is the thing's last label before the shelf.

"You see it," Ada breathes beside her. "Don't you. You've seen it this whole time."

The girl nods.

Corvin has gone very still, the stillness of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like. "Vess said it opens to the collateral's echo. That the door tests—" He stops. Looks at the girl. Looks at the name written in the seam. "It wants her to say it. But her name is what they *took*. If she could say it, we wouldn't need the door."

The girl shakes her head slowly. Then she kneels in the frost and begins to draw, because words are still new to her and some things are better drawn. She draws the door. She draws the seam. She draws, above the door, a mouth—not a person's mouth, just an opening, an echo shaped like listening.

Then she draws the same name twice: once whole, once broken into pieces, the pieces scattered.

Ada watches her hands. Ada is good at watching hands; the girl has noticed this about her. "The name on the door isn't the whole name," Ada says slowly. "It's... the echo of it. What's left of it up here, in the world. They took the soul of the name but an echo stayed behind, the way a sound stays in a room after—" Her voice does the crack-thing it does when she talks about rooms. "After someone leaves it."

*Yes,* the girl thinks, fierce, glad. *Yes, yes.*

"And an echo," Corvin says, and the girl hears him arrive at it a half-step before he says it, hears the dread land in him, "an echo answers if you call to it. Even a stolen name echoes. Even a sold name."

The girl stands. Brushes frost from her knees. Looks at the door.

Here is the truth she has not told them, the truth she carries like a stone in the red boot: she is afraid. Not of the door. Of what happens after. Because she has been down there, most of her, for longer than she can count, and the part of her that walked up the mountain and knocked on the Ledgerhouse's back door with bare frozen feet—that part is an echo too. A splinter. When the door opens and the name answers, the splinter will feel the whole of itself pulling, the way a river's edge feels the current. And she does not know which part of her will walk through that door first: the girl who likes bread and red boots and Ada's careful hands, or the filed and labeled thing the court has been keeping warm for three hundred years.

*Name the fear or it names you.*

She is afraid of meeting herself.

She takes Ada's hand anyway. Ada's fingers are cold and ink-stained and they close around hers immediately, no hesitation, and that helps. That helps more than bread helped, more than the boots helped. Corvin steps up on her other side, and his shadow falls over her like a roof.

"Whenever you're ready," Ada says softly.

The girl is not ready. She will never be ready. Ready is a word for people who are whole.

She steps forward, lifts her face to the seam and the name written along it, and does the thing Vess drilled into her across six nights of candlelight: she finds the echo of her name in the hollow of her chest—the splinter of it, the shard that came away with her—and she calls to it the way you call to your own reflection.

She does not use her throat. Her throat holds only borrowed words, everyone's memories, fragments of Ada's childhood winters and Corvin's mother's lullaby. Instead she uses the place beneath the words, the old place, the pre-kingdom place, and what comes out of her is not sound.

It is recognition.

The name on the door stirs.

The girl feels it move the way you feel a sleeping animal wake under your hand—a shiver traveling the length of the seam, letter by letter, each character turning toward her like a face toward a lamp. The gray wood warms. The stone grain softens. Somewhere far below, deep in the beautiful bureaucratic dark, a clerk three hundred years dead looks up from a ledger and frowns at an entry that has begun, impossibly, to glow.

The seam widens. A hairline. A thread. A crack of amber light that smells—she wasn't expecting this, nobody warned her—smells like her mother's kitchen, like yeast and woodsmoke, like a life so old it has become a country.

The door opens inward, the way doors open for people expected home.

And the girl, standing on the threshold with her hand in Ada's hand and Corvin's shadow on her shoulders, feels the pull begin—the current, the long reach of the whole self she left below, tugging at her splinter-heart like the tide tugging at a footprint.

Beyond the door: stairs. Cut from living root, spiraling down into amber light, going further than eyes can follow.

Corvin exhales. "That's a lot of stairs."

Ada squeezes her fingers. "Together," she says, which is not a plan, exactly, but is somehow better than one.

The girl looks back once—at the petrified forest, at the held breath of the stone trees, at the sky, small and gray and free above the braided roots.

Then she takes the first step down, toward the largest part of herself, and the door begins, gently, patiently, like a bookkeeper closing a ledger at the end of a very long day—

—to shut behind them.
