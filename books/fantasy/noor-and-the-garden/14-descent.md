## 14. Descent

The door closed behind us with a sound like a ledger snapping shut.

I felt it in my teeth. Behind us: the petrified forest, gray and lightless, the frost fair a rumor of warmth somewhere far above. Ahead of us: stairs that had not existed until Noor touched them, spiraling down into dark that was not empty but *expectant*.

Noor went first. She always would now; the door had opened to her stolen name's echo, and whatever waited below knew her shape better than it knew ours. She walked barefoot on stone cold enough to crack skin—Corvin had bought her red boots at the frost fair and she wore them slung around her neck like a talisman she wasn't ready to trust—and she did not shiver. The dark didn't frighten her. It recognized her.

"Stay close," I said, though to whom, I couldn't have said. Corvin walked behind me with his lantern held low, the way enforcers are trained: light the ground, not your own face. Vess rode against my ribs inside the grimoire, silent as a shut book, which is what she had been since we crossed the salt moor. Her price sat in me like a swallowed stone. A confession never spoken in life, carried into the mountain court. I still didn't know its words. I suspected I wouldn't until the moment came to speak them.

The stairs went down for a long time.

That sounds like nothing. It was not nothing. Stairs in the ordinary world obey arithmetic—you count them, they end. These stairs counted *us*. Somewhere past the hundredth step, I stopped hearing our footsteps as four separate rhythms and started hearing them as entries being made. *One, descending. Two, descending.* The mountain was keeping books on us already.

"Noor," I said softly, "what do you see?"

She turned. In the lantern light her face was calm in a way no child's face should be, the calm of someone reading a page everyone else finds illegible. She pointed down, then touched her own chest, then mine, then—after a hesitation—Corvin's.

"The passage tests," I said. Vess had told me this much before going quiet, back when she still spoke: *The door beneath the root is only the mouth. The throat remembers every debt you carried into it.* I had thought that was poetry. Vess did not waste words on poetry.

"I know," Corvin said. His voice was flat, professional. He'd been an enforcer for twenty years; he knew what a toll road looked like even when the toll was invisible. "Everyone walks their own ledger down here. Fine. Mine's short."

He said it like a man who believed it. I noticed he also moved closer to Noor without seeming to decide to.

* * *

The first test was mine, because the mountain apparently worked alphabetically or by cruelty or by some order only auditors understand.

The stairs opened into a chamber no wider than a cellar, and the walls were pages. Not paper—stone grown into the texture of paper, grain and all, blank until you looked at it directly. And when I looked, the nearest wall filled itself with writing in a hand I knew better than my own.

My own hand.

Every entry I had ever hidden. The favors I'd taken from Mistress Vess and never repaid—the winter she fed me when I'd run from my father's house, the years of teaching paid for out of her own stores while I told myself hedge-witches owed their apprentices nothing but instruction. The night I left. I had always told myself I fled the grimoire's binding, the slow erosion of self that comes of serving a woman who trades in names. That was true. It was also true that I took three vials of bottled grief from her stores when I went, because grief sells well in cities, and I had never once admitted that to anyone, including myself. There it was, in my own handwriting, dated, itemized, *unpaid*.

"That's not—" I started, and the wall wrote my protest down too.

"Ada." Corvin's voice, careful. He had seen it. He was looking at the wall the way you look at a wound on someone else's body—with sympathy and with the urgent wish not to be next.

"It's not real," I said. "It's a trick of the passage."

"It's accurate," said the wall, in my voice.

Vess stirred against my ribs. Not words—just a pressure, the ghostly equivalent of a hand on the shoulder. *Read it,* the pressure said. *You cannot contest a debt you refuse to look at.*

So I read it. All of it. The small debts were worse than the large ones, the way splinters are worse than breaks: the apology I owed the miller's daughter whose name I'd used to pass through a checkpoint; the letter I'd never sent to my father, not because he didn't deserve one but because sending it would mean admitting I still wanted his answer; the promise I'd made to myself at seventeen, kneeling in Vess's garden, that I would come back someday and burn the grimoire rather than let it bind another girl. I had not come back. I had built a life of meticulous anonymity instead, and called the building survival.

The wall waited. The passage waited. Noor waited, watching me with those eyes that understood languages older than kingdoms, including, apparently, the language of a woman lying to herself for fifteen years.

"I owe," I said aloud, and my voice cracked on the second word, "more than I can pay. I know."

The writing faded. Not forgiven—filed. The mountain had entered it into its books, and somewhere below, I understood, there was a court that would want to know what I intended to do about it.

We went down.

* * *

Corvin's chamber came two turns later, and it was larger than mine, which surprised me until I thought about it. Men who say their ledgers are short usually mean the opposite.

His walls showed debt in a different script—not handwriting but marks, tallies, the notation of enforcement. Every collection he'd ever made. Every soul, every borrowed year, every name pried out of a debtor's mouth with the patient machinery of guild procedure. He stood in front of them with his jaw set and his lantern raised, and I watched him do the thing he must have done his whole career: convert feeling into inventory.

"Collateral recovered per contract," he read aloud, flat. "Per contract. Per contract." A litany. A shield.

Then the wall changed.

It showed him a single entry, and I saw him stop breathing. I couldn't read the script—it was enforcement cipher, guild-internal—but I could read Corvin. I watched twenty years of discipline fail him in the space of a heartbeat. His lantern hand shook. The tally-marks on the wall seemed to lean toward him like creditors.

"What is it?" I asked.

"Nothing." His voice came out sanded smooth. "An old file."

Noor stepped forward.

She didn't ask permission. Children don't; they haven't learned that some rooms are locked. She walked to the wall, put her small palm flat against the entry that had broken him, and stood there. And the wall—this wall of stone and judgment, this instrument of the court beneath the mountain—went soft. The cipher blurred. What came through instead, faint as heat off a stone, was sensation, the way Noor's mind rendered everything: the taste of rain on a bridge, a name spoken once and never again, the specific loneliness of a man who has spent so long collecting other people's souls that he forgot to keep receipts on his own.

I understood then, standing in the dark with a mute child's hand on a wall, what Corvin had lost collecting Noor's debt. Not money. Not favor. His sworn name—the enforcer's oath-name, the thing that let him act in the guild's authority. He'd surrendered it as surety when the original sale went through, and the sale had gone bad, and the guild had simply kept both. He had spent three years walking around as a legal nullity, a man who could not swear to anything because he had already been sworn away. And he had followed us down here anyway, owing the child a debt he could never discharge in any currency the guild recognized.

"You could have told me," I said.

"Telling costs," he said. "Everything down here costs. I'm saving up."

Noor took his hand. Just that. She tugged, gently, the way you tug someone past a shop window, and Corvin—who had dragged debtors out of cellars and stood unmoved through screaming—let himself be tugged. The wall dimmed behind them. Filed, not forgiven. But filed.

We went down.

* * *

The third chamber was Noor's, and none of us wanted to enter it, and all of us did, because leaving her alone in it was unthinkable.

Her walls did not show writing. They showed *absence*. A child-shaped hole in the world, the exact size and color of a soul that had been sold. Around the hole, everything she was without it: the dream-language, the spiral maps, the way she spoke in fragments of other people's memories because her own had been repossessed. The debt against her was the largest in the room by far—a whole self, itemized as collateral on a guild debt of three favors.

Three favors. I had seen the ledger line myself, back in the stacks, and my hands had shaken then too. A soul for three favors. The arithmetic of it was obscene in a way that made obscenity look polite.

But here is what I learned in that chamber, and I have never been able to unlearn it: Noor looked at the hole in her own wall the way a carpenter looks at a joint that needs setting. Not with horror. With *plans*. She traced the hole's edges with one finger, then turned to me and made a quick series of gestures—hand to heart, hands opening, hands pressing together like a book closing—that I had learned over the moor meant *taken, returned, sealed.*

"Not yet," I said. "But soon. We're almost there."

She nodded, satisfied, and walked out of her own absence like a girl walking out of a fitting-room, leaving the garment hanging.

Corvin and I followed. Neither of us spoke for a long time. Some things you can't discuss without cheapening them, and a child auditing the theft of herself is one of them.

* * *

The fourth chamber had no walls at all.

The stairs simply ended, and we stood on a landing above a space so vast the lantern couldn't find its floor. Far below—so far the distance felt theological—lights moved. Not torchlight. Lamplight over desks. Rows and rows of them, receding into dark, and between them figures that might have been clerks or might have been something older wearing clerks like coats.

And rising up toward us out of that vastness came a sound I knew in my bones: the sound of the Ledgerhouse at closing time. Pages turning. Entries being made. Quills, thousands of quills, whispering across ten thousand records.

"It's the same," I whispered. "It's the same place."

"No," said Vess.

She hadn't spoken since the salt moor, and her voice came out of the grimoire like smoke from a doused fire. I clutched the book through my coat.

"It looks the same," she said. "That's the trap, girl. You'll walk in there thinking you know the rules, and the rules will know you better. Below, they don't hide what they take behind courtesy and contracts. They just take it, and they write it down honestly, and honest accounting is crueler than any fraud ever invented." A pause, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped into something almost gentle, which from Vess was more frightening than rage. "And you're carrying my confession into that place. Remember whose debt opens hardest down there. It won't be yours."

"I know," I said. "It's yours."

"Then keep it close. And Ada—" the smoke-voice wavered, and for one moment the bitter ghost sounded like the woman who had fed a runaway girl through a winter, "—when it asks you to pay, and it will, remember that you chose this. Choosing is the only currency they respect."

Below us, the lamplights shifted. As if the desks had heard. As if somewhere in that enormous dark, an auditor had looked up from a page that bore our four names, and made a note.

The stairs resumed on the far side of the landing—wider now, cut for processions, worn smooth by feet that had descended for centuries. Noor stepped onto them without hesitation. Of course she did. This was the road her soul had been carried down, and some part of her remembered it the way a riverbed remembers water.

Corvin checked his lantern, checked his knife, checked me—the enforcer's triple audit—and nodded once.

"Last chance to turn around," he said. It was courtesy. Neither of us believed in it anymore.

"Noor," I said, and she turned, red boots swinging against her chest, eyes catching the far lamplight like a cat's. "When we get down there—when they offer me the trade—don't let me hesitate too long."

She reached up and pressed her palm flat over my heart, right where the grimoire lay, right where Vess's silence lived, right where my own name had hidden for fifteen years. Then she pointed down.

The message needed no translation. *Your name is the key. Spend it.*

We descended together, four debtors and one confession, into the honest dark.
