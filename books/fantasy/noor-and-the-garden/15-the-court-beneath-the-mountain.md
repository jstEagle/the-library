## 15. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The door behind them sealed itself with a sound like a ledger closing.

Ada felt it in her teeth. Not a slam, not a boom—something quieter and worse: the soft, final *thock* of a page turned by someone who would not be turning back.

They stood in a corridor that should have been impossible. It was carved from the same petrified wood as the forest above, but here the grain ran in columns so straight they might have been ruled onto the stone by hand, and the walls gave off a faint warmth, like paper fresh from a press. Light came from nowhere Ada could identify—no torches, no lamps, no veins of glowing mineral. The air simply *was* luminous, the way a well-kept archive is luminous at noon, all its dust suspended in order.

Noor walked ahead of them.

Not walked—*proceeded*. The child moved down the corridor the way water moves downhill, without hesitation, without looking back to see if Ada and Corvin followed. Her bare feet made no sound on the stone. She had refused the red boots for the descent; she'd carried them slung around her neck by their laces instead, like a talisman she wasn't ready to wear. Now, as the corridor widened, she stopped, lifted one foot, and looked at it as if noticing it belonged to her.

Then she put the boots on.

Ada's chest did something complicated. Corvin said nothing, but she heard him exhale through his nose—the enforcer's version of weeping.

"Stay close," Corvin murmured. "And don't sign anything."

"I know how contracts work," Ada whispered back.

"You know how *our* contracts work." His eyes tracked the corridor ahead, cataloguing exits out of habit even though there were none. "This place wrote the originals. Everything we do downstairs is a copy of a copy. Remember that when it starts feeling familiar. Familiar is how they get you."

But that was exactly the problem, Ada thought. It already felt familiar. That was the horror of it.

* * *

The corridor opened into the court.

Ada had imagined many things beneath the mountain: a cavern dripping with dark water, a throne of roots, something with too many eyes enthroned in the dark. What she had not imagined was a *reading room*.

It was vast—a hall the size of the Ledgerhouse's great floor, vaulted like a cathedral, but every surface was shelving. Shelves rose from the floor and continued up into the vaulting until they vanished into light, and every shelf was filled—not with books, but with *souls*. She understood this immediately, the way you understand a thing you've spent your whole life preparing to understand. Each soul was filed as a slim volume bound in whatever it had been in life: leather, linen, bark, wool, salt-stiffened hide. Some shelves held thousands of identical spines, uniform as hymnals. Others held single volumes set apart on stands, lit individually, as if under permanent consideration.

And it was quiet. That was the worst part. A court, Ada had thought, would roar. This place hummed. Pages turning somewhere far above. The occasional soft chime of a bell marking some filing completed, some account balanced. Somewhere, faintly, a voice reading aloud—not pleading, not screaming, just *reading*, in the flat cadence of a clerk reciting terms.

Souls filed like documents. Justice administered as accounting.

"Oh," Ada said. Just that. Her voice came out very small in all that ordered light.

Corvin had gone rigid beside her. He was staring up at the shelves with an expression she'd only seen once before, the night he'd told her what happened to collectors who failed to bring in collateral. "I've been here before," he said quietly. "In my head. Every night since I collected that debt. I told myself I was remembering it wrong. Making it worse than it was." He swallowed. "I wasn't."

Noor tugged Ada's sleeve. When Ada knelt, the girl pointed—at a shelf near the base of the far wall, low, unremarkable, holding perhaps two hundred volumes—and then pressed her palm flat against her own sternum.

"There," Ada breathed. "You're *there*."

A bell chimed somewhere high above. Then another. Then a third, closer, and out of the shelving glided a figure that Ada's professional eye assessed before her fear could finish arriving: robes the grey of unsullied vellum, hands gloved in white kid, a face smooth and ageless as an unwritten page. It stopped precisely three paces away—an auditor's distance, close enough to read, far enough to avoid smudging.

"You are expected," it said. Its voice was neither male nor female, warm nor cold. It sounded like a form being read aloud. "Unregistered arrivals are irregular. Irregularities require documentation. Please accompany me to Intake."

"And if we don't?" Corvin asked.

The Auditor tilted its head with the mild curiosity of someone consulting a reference. "Then you will be documented as refusing. Refusal is also a record. All records persist." A pause, precise as a semicolon. "Nothing here is lost. That is the promise of this place. And the price."

Ada looked at Noor. Noor looked back at her, and in the child's strange clarity there was no fear—only the patient certainty of someone returning to a building where she had once been left.

"Intake," Ada said. "We'll come to Intake."

* * *

Intake was a desk.

That was the joke of it, Ada thought, half-hysterical, as they were seated on benches before a counter of black stone worn glossy by centuries of elbows. The desk stretched away in both directions farther than the eye could follow, and along its whole length sat Auditors, dozens of them, each attending to some petitioner or other: grieving widows, ancient kings still wearing crowns gone green, farmers, children, things that had never been alive at all. Each petitioner received the same treatment. Each was handed a slate. Each was asked questions in that form-voice, and answered, and their answers were written down, and their writing-down was carried away by lesser clerks—small pale figures like folded letters—to be shelved.

Justice, administered as accounting.

An Auditor placed three slates before them, and chalk, and a card written in a script Ada recognized with a lurch: pre-kingdom glyphs, the old tongue Vess had drilled into her by candlelight while Noor corrected her pronunciation with small cold fingers. The card read:

**STATE YOUR NAME AS IT IS HELD, NOT AS IT IS SPOKEN.**

Ada's blood went cold. Beside her, Corvin had gone the color of old tallow. He had no name to state—that was the point of him now; he'd paid his sworn name at the bridge, and whatever the toll-keeper had done with it, it was no longer his to give. He picked up his chalk anyway, and after a long moment wrote, in careful block letters:

*I HAVE NONE. THIS IS MY ANSWER.*

The Auditor across from him read it, considered, and made a note. Whatever the note was, Corvin didn't ask, and Ada loved him a little for not asking.

Her own slate waited. Ada looked at the blank surface and felt the whole architecture of her life rise up in her throat: ten years of meticulous anonymity, the false names layered like coats, the borrowed handwriting, the way she'd taught herself to walk through rooms without displacing air. She had built her survival out of not-being-written-down. And here, at the root of everything, was a desk asking her to be written.

She wrote: *ADA GALLOWAY.* Then, because the card had said *as it is held*, she added, smaller: *and other names, withheld.*

The Auditor read both lines. Something that was almost amusement moved across its smooth face—or perhaps it was only the light changing.

"Withheld names are noted," it said. "Withholding is itself a holding. You may keep what you keep. For now." It stamped her slate with a seal that appeared from nowhere and vanished the same way, and the stamp's sound was the same soft *thock* as the door closing behind them. "Your business?"

"The child," Ada said. "Noor. Her soul was sold to this court as collateral on a guild debt. We're here to contest the sale."

For the first time, the Auditor went still in a way that felt less like a pause and more like attention. Around them, along the endless counter, other Auditors had paused too—she couldn't say how she knew, but the whole hall had shifted by a degree, the way a room shifts when a stranger says something true.

"A contested sale requires grounds," the Auditor said. "Grounds must be stated in the original language of contract." Its gaze moved to Noor, then back to Ada. "You speak it imperfectly."

"I'm learning," Ada said. "From a ghost."

Something flickered then—deep in the shelving, far above, a volume on a high stand seemed to lean, very slightly, toward the sound of Vess's name. Ada felt the grimoire warm against her ribs where it hung in its sling, and inside it, faint as breath on glass, she felt the old woman paying attention.

"Noted," said the Auditor. "Contested claims are heard in the Hall of Reckoning. Reckoning is scheduled by availability. Availability is currently—" it consulted nothing at all, and yet consulted it thoroughly, "—immediate. You will be advised of the requirements. Failure to meet requirements voids the claim. Void claims return the contested asset to the shelf." It rose. "Follow."

* * *

They walked between the shelves, and Ada made herself look.

She was an accountant of impossible things; she knew what avoidance cost, compounded over years. So she looked. She saw griefs bound in grey silk, shelved by intensity. She saw borrowed years in bundles tied with red thread, each thread knotted with the count. She saw a shelf of names that opened doors, each one humming faintly, straining toward its lock. And she saw souls—hundreds, thousands—each spine lettered in the script of whoever had sold them, each filed with the same terrible tenderness a good archivist gives anything entrusted to their care.

Because that was the thing. That was the moral vertigo rising in her like floodwater. Nothing here was cruel. Nothing here was sloppy. The volumes were dusted. The light was calibrated. Somewhere a clerk was mending a damaged binding with stitches fine as hair. The court beneath the mountain was beautiful, and orderly, and honest in a way no living institution had ever managed to be: it kept exactly what it said it kept, charged exactly what it said it charged, and lost nothing, ever.

And the Ledgerhouse—

The Ledgerhouse traded in the same goods. Bottled griefs, borrowed years, names that open doors. Souls, when the price was right, when the signature was forged cleanly enough. Ada had audited those entries for ten years. She had smoothed the ink of them. She had filed the winter accounts and felt *safe*, because the numbers balanced, because balancing was its own kind of virtue, wasn't it, wasn't that what she'd told herself every night in her narrow bed above the counting floor?

The difference, she thought, walking these luminous aisles, was a single line in the charter. The Ledgerhouse buried its prices in footnotes. This place printed them on the door. The Ledgerhouse called it trade. This place called it custody. The Ledgerhouse lied about what it was.

This place never had.

"We could work here," she heard herself whisper, appalled.

"Don't," Corvin said.

"I mean it. That's what frightens me. If I'd been born under this mountain instead of above it—" She stopped. Noor had paused at a shelf and was touching one spine among hundreds, gently, the way you touch a sleeping animal. The volume was small, bound in something pale. The lettering on it was in the old tongue, and Ada could read enough of it now to make her stomach drop.

It was a name. Not Noor's. Another child's, sold long ago, never claimed.

Noor patted the spine twice, the way you'd reassure someone through a door, and walked on.

Ada stood in the aisle of the beautiful, honest dark and understood, finally, the shape of what she had come to do. Not to burn this place down—it could not be burned; it was older than fire's jurisdiction. But somewhere in it was a forged signature with the Ledgerhouse's mark, and somewhere in herself was a woman who had spent ten years hiding inside other people's accounting. To contest the debt, she would have to stand in a hall of perfect records and argue against the guild that had sheltered her invisibility—using her own imperfect, human, half-learned voice.

The grimoire warmed against her ribs again. Inside it, unmistakably now, Vess's presence gathered like a storm deciding whether to break.

"Well?" Ada murmured, so low only the dead could hear. "Any advice?"

The answer came as pressure against her back, cold and familiar, like a hand between her shoulder blades pushing her forward.

*Forward,* the dead mistress said. *And don't embarrass me.*

Ahead, the shelves parted onto a hall of white stone, and a bell began to ring the hour of reckoning.
