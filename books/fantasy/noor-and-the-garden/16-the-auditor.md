## 16. The Auditor

They gave us a waiting room.

That was the first thing I understood about the court beneath the mountain: it was not a dungeon, not a lair, not any of the places stories promise you when you dig too deep. It was an office. The chamber where we sat had been grown, not built—pale stone like the inside of a shell, walls ribbed and warm to the touch—and it was furnished the way a guild waiting room is furnished, which is to say with furniture designed to make you feel that your business is small.

A bench of polished rootwood. A shelf of ledgers behind glass, their spines numbered in a script I now knew well enough to read and wished I didn't. A bell on a stand, small and silver, with a card beside it in careful pre-kingdom characters:

*Ring once. Ring twice only if you are certain.*

Noor sat between me and Corvin, her red boots—scuffed now, salt-stained from the moor—swinging above the floor because they were still half a size too big. She did not fidget. She looked at the shelf of ledgers the way other children look at caged birds, and I caught myself wondering, for the hundredth time since the descent, what it was like to be a girl whose soul lived in one of those spines. Whether she could feel it down here. Whether it hummed to her through the stone, the way a dog knows its own whistle from a mile off.

Corvin sat very still, hands flat on his knees, the posture of a man who has spent years being summoned into rooms and has learned every way a room can turn against you. His face was gray. The passage down had taken something out of him—not blood, not breath, but something adjacent to both. Every unpaid debt we carried had been made visible in that tunnel; I had seen his laid out like instruments on a table, and I had seen him flinch from them, and I had seen Noor take his hand without a word and walk him past them step by step. He hadn't thanked her. He hadn't spoken at all since the door beneath the roots sealed itself behind us with a sound like a book closing.

I rang the bell once.

The sound it made was not a sound so much as a filing. Somewhere far below us—or perhaps directly beside us; distance worked strangely here—I felt something open, consider, and close again. Then the wall opposite the bench simply became a doorway, the way a page becomes a page when you stop looking at the paper and start reading it.

And the Auditor came through.

I had expected a monster. Everyone expects a monster; it's how we keep from expecting an accountant. What stood in the doorway instead was a figure of indeterminate height wearing robes the color of wet slate, and where a face should have been there was a surface like the cover of a ledger—smooth, dark, faintly ruled with lines—and upon that surface, letters surfaced and sank as it regarded us, forming and dissolving words in no alphabet I knew, though I felt each one land somewhere behind my sternum.

It carried no weapon. It carried a pen.

"Ada Galloway," it said, and my whole body went cold, because I had not given it that name. "Also recorded as: the woman who burned her page. Also as: apprentice to Vess of the Hedgerow, unregistered. Also as: *unnamed*, per the Ledgerhouse rolls, entry struck." The letters on its face rippled. "You have presented this court with several versions of yourself. We will need to reconcile them."

"I go by Ada," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "The rest is old paperwork."

"All paperwork is old," said the Auditor. "That is what makes it powerful."

Behind me, Corvin shifted his weight—a small sound, but in that room it might as well have been a shout. The Auditor's blank face turned toward him, considered him, and returned to me with the unhurried attention of a creature that had all the time that had ever been minted.

"You contested a debt," it said. "You will have your hearing. That is the law here, and the law here is older than the law up there, and unlike yours, ours has never once been amended for anyone's convenience. But before the hearing, there is a matter of procedure." It raised the pen. Not threateningly. The way a clerk raises a pen. Which is worse. "You brought collateral into our court."

"Noor is not collateral," I said. "She's a child."

"She is both," said the Auditor, without malice, without anything at all. "Most things are. The child's soul-shard sits in our archive, filed under the debt of the Ledgerhouse of [a name I will not repeat], lot 4,417, entered in the year the frost fair last fell on a new moon. You wish it returned. The debt will be examined. If it is found fraudulent—as your ghost believes, and ghosts are excellent witnesses, having nothing left to lie for—the shard reverts to its origin." A pause. Letters swam across its face and drowned. "But fraud must be proven, and proof requires standing, and standing requires a party who can be held to account for the outcome."

"And there it is," Corvin said quietly.

"There it is," the Auditor agreed.

* * *

They let us wait again after that—of course they did; waiting is the oldest instrument of institutional power, older than the sword, cheaper than the whip—and in the waiting, Noor climbed onto the bench beside me and leaned her head against my arm, and I felt her do the thing she had begun doing on the moor: reaching, gently, into the weather of other people's feelings and naming them back to us in fragments of borrowed memory.

She pressed two fingers to my wrist, right over the pulse, and spoke in the voice of my mother reading aloud by candlelight, a voice I had not heard in twenty years and had not known I still carried:

*"Read it again,"* Noor said, in my mother's cadence, *"the part about the fox."*

Then she blinked, and the voice was gone, and she was just a tired girl in too-big boots, watching me with eyes the color of deep water.

"That's what he'll ask for, isn't it," Corvin said. He wasn't looking at either of us. He was looking at the shelf of ledgers behind the glass. "Not the child. Not the soul. A name. Freely given. Because everything else can be argued with, and a freely given name can't. It's the one currency that doesn't bounce."

"You know this place," I said.

"I know guilds," he said. "This is a guild. It just doesn't pretend otherwise." He rubbed his jaw, and I saw the toll-keeper's bridge in the motion—the memory he'd paid without hesitating, the one he'd never told me the contents of, though I had pieced together enough: whatever he lost collecting Noor's debt three winters ago, it had included his own sworn name, the enforcer's oath-name that had once made him untouchable and now made him nobody. He had walked into this mountain already half-unmade. Perhaps that was why the descent had cost him so much. There was less of him left to spend.

"The Auditor will offer you a trade," he went on. "It's what Auditors do. They don't want your soul—they don't even particularly want your name. They want the *transaction*. A clean one. Witnessed, consensual, irrevocable. Upstairs, the Ledgerhouse takes things. Down here, they get you to give them. And then it's done forever, and everyone's books balance, and no one ever has to feel anything about it again." His mouth twisted. "I signed one of those once. Three winters ago. I told myself it was just paperwork."

"It was a child's soul," I said.

"Yes," he said. "And it was just paperwork. Both things were true. That's the trick of it." He finally turned and looked at me, and his eyes were the flattest I'd ever seen them, which from Corvin meant they were holding something enormous down by main force. "So before you're in that room with the pen in front of you, Ada—you decide now, here, what you're worth. Because if you try to decide it in there, in front of *that*, you'll decide wrong. Everyone does."

Noor tugged my sleeve. When I looked down, she held up her hands, palms out, and shaped slow shapes in the air—the old language, the pre-kingdom tongue that Vess had drilled into me by candlelight while the girl corrected my pronunciation by touch. She was signing a word I knew. Vess had made me write it fifty times, chant it until my mouth ached, because, she said, it was the hinge-word of all contracts, the word that meant both *gift* and *debt* depending on which side of the mouth you said it from.

*Given,* Noor signed. *Given. Given.*

Then she folded her small fingers closed, one by one, and showed me the empty palm.

*Gone.*

* * *

The hearing chamber was round, and it was full of desks.

That sounds absurd. I know it sounds absurd. But I have stood in the great hall of the Ledgerhouse with its marble scales and its gilded charter, and I would take the mountain's desks over the guild's theater any day, because at least the mountain was honest: row upon row of pale stone desks rising in tiers like an amphitheater, and at every desk a figure in slate robes, and before every figure a ledger, and every ledger open, and every pen moving. The sound of the room was the sound of writing. Thousands of pens, whispering. It was the most terrible thing I had ever heard, and I include the night I fled my mistress's house with my name sewn into my hem like contraband.

At the center of the floor stood a single pedestal. On the pedestal lay a single document, and I knew it before I could read it—I knew it by the weight of it, the way you know a person's footsteps on a stair. The original sale. Lot 4,417. Three favors owed by the Ledgerhouse to the court, secured against the soul of a girl whose village had burned, whose tongue had gone silent, whose name had been the only thing of value anyone had ever bothered to appraise.

The Auditor stood beside the pedestal. Its face-letters had settled into something almost like patience.

"Before proceedings begin," it said, and every pen in the amphitheater paused, "the court offers the visiting party an expedient."

Every eyeless face turned toward me. I felt the attention of the room like water pressure.

"The examination of the debt will take time. Days, perhaps weeks by your reckoning. Evidence will be weighed. Signatures will be traced to their sources. Your ghost will testify, and testimony is slow, and during that time the child remains filed—present, accounted for, neither harmed nor free. There is another path." The Auditor lifted the pen. "The court will release lot 4,417 immediately, today, in exchange for consideration of equal weight, freely tendered. You possess such consideration. You have carried it hidden for eleven years, through seven towns and two false identities, sewn into your hems and buried under aliases and burned out of every record you could reach." The letters on its face formed my name—my true name, the one I had not spoken aloud since I was nineteen years old, the one not even Corvin knew—and then, deliberately, let it dissolve. "Your name, Ada Galloway. Given, not taken. In this court, a freely given name is total. It means we may call on you. It means you are answerable, forever, to every consequence of every act performed under it. It means you can never again be invisible, not here, not above, not anywhere the ledgers reach." The pen hovered. "We do not deceive our counterparties. Most find the terms acceptable until they understand them. Do you understand them?"

I understood them.

God help me, I understood them better than the Auditor knew.

I thought of the winter accounts, chapter one of my life in that house: the comfort of anonymity, the meticulous nothing I had built myself into, the relief of being no one. I had fled Vess because Vess wanted to use me—my literacy, my ear for dead languages, my talent for being underestimated—and invisibility had seemed like safety. Eleven years of safety. Eleven years of eating lunch alone, of leaving rooms without goodbyes, of watching injustices scroll past the guild's counters and telling myself that a woman with no name could not be blamed for them, because blame needs an address.

And then a barefoot child had arrived at the back door speaking the oldest language in the world, and my invisibility had turned out to be exactly what it always was: not armor. Just a smaller and smaller room.

If I gave them the name, I would be accountable. Forever. Every debt I'd dodged, every silence I'd chosen, every kindness I'd withheld because kindness leaves traces—they would all come due at once, and keep coming due, eternally, in a court whose entire architecture was consequence. That was the trade. Not my comfort for the child's freedom. My *future*—every unwritten year of it, mortgaged to beings who measured time in centuries of filings—for one small girl's wholeness.

Anyone sensible would refuse. Anyone sensible would say: we'll do it the long way, the lawful way, the way with witnesses and delays and a ghost's testimony, and maybe we win and maybe we don't, but at least I walk out of here still able to disappear.

I opened my mouth to say yes anyway—

—and stopped.

Not from cowardice. I need whoever reads this someday, in whatever ledger it ends up in, to understand that it was not cowardice. It was arithmetic. Vess's voice, sharp and bitter and true, spoke in my memory: *Nothing in this world is free, girl, and the things offered free are priced highest.* The Auditor wanted a transaction. Clean, witnessed, irrevocable. And a transaction freely entered cannot later be contested—which meant that if I traded my name for Noor's soul *now*, before the fraud was proven, I would be laundering the guild's crime myself. Converting stolen goods into purchased goods. Signing, with my own accountable, eternal, unhidden name, a document that made the original sale legitimate retroactively. The court would balance its books. The guild would escape scrutiny. And somewhere in the archives, lot 4,417 would be marked *resolved by private treaty*, and no one would ever examine how the shard came to be filed in the first place.

The trade was real. The freedom it bought would be real. But it would also be the exact thing the Ledgerhouse had done to Noor, performed again, by me, with better manners.

"They taught me this," I said aloud, to the Auditor, to the thousand listening pens. "Upstairs. The guild taught me that every entry has two sides, and you check both, and you never accept a settlement that closes the question you were actually asking." My heart was slamming against my ribs like something trying to get out—or in. "You're offering me my heart's desire, and it's genuine, and I'm refusing it. Not because I don't mean to pay. Because paying *this way* would make me your accomplice instead of your adversary. And I didn't cross the salt moor to become the Ledgerhouse with a conscience."

The silence that followed lasted four pens' strokes. I counted.

Then, on the Auditor's smooth dark face, for the first time, the letters did something I had not seen them do. They arranged themselves into a shape that was not a word.

It took me a moment to recognize it, because I had only ever seen it inked on paper, in a dead language, in a dead woman's grimoire.

It was a smile.

"How refreshing," the Auditor said. "Proceed with the contest, then, Ada Galloway. Full examination. All evidence. Ghosts welcome." The pen descended and touched the pedestal, and the original sale document shivered like a living thing. "Be advised: the court will now require your name regardless—not as payment, but as registration. One cannot litigate anonymously. You will be answerable for the outcome of these proceedings, whatever it is, forever." The smile-letters widened by a single stroke. "You have, in effect, just volunteered for the very thing you refused to sell. We find this *deeply* satisfactory. Step forward, claimant. The duel of ledgers begins."

Behind me, Noor slipped her small hand into mine, and Corvin exhaled like a man stepping off a ledge, and somewhere in the tiers above, a thousand pens resumed their whispering—all of them, I realized, already writing about me.

Under my true name.
