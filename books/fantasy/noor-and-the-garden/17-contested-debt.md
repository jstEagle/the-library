## 17. Contested Debt

The chamber where the court heard disputes was not a courtroom. It was a counting room.

That was the first thing Ada understood, standing on the cold marble with Noor's small hand gripping two of her fingers: the court beneath the mountain did not pretend to be anything other than what it was. Benches instead of a bar. Scales instead of a judge's chair. And everywhere, everywhere, ledgers—stacked in towers, shelved into the living rock, chained open on lecterns like prisoners made to watch their own sentences read aloud.

The Auditor sat at the far end behind a desk of black stone, and it had no face worth describing. Where a face should have been there was a page, blank and faintly luminous, and when the Auditor spoke, words surfaced on that page the way ink blooms through wet paper.

"Contestant," the page said. "You have one hour of the court's attention. The court bills by the minute."

"Then I'll be quick," Ada said, and her voice only shook a little.

Corvin stood at her left shoulder, arms crossed, wearing the expression he wore for everything now—a flat, unreadable patience, as if he had already calculated every way this could go wrong and found them all acceptable. Noor stood at her right, barefoot still; the red boots waited in Ada's satchel for a girl who could walk in a world that knew her name. Above them, at the top of the stair they'd descended, Corvin had wedged the door shut with an iron bar and a prayer neither of them believed in. Somewhere above, the guild's collectors were finding the trail. Somewhere above, hounds were sniffing along the salt moor for the scent of three names walking together.

Ada unrolled the ledger page she had stolen from the Ledgerhouse itself—the original record of sale—and laid it on the presentation table with hands that wanted to tremble and were not permitted to.

*Item: one soul, whole and undivided, held in trust against the debt of the House of Ledgers to the Court Beneath. Collateral acquired from the bearer's surviving kin. Value assessed at three favors, compounded.*

"The debt," she said, "was never valid."

The blank page tilted, considering. Around the chamber, the clerks—thin grey figures whose fingers ended in nibs instead of nails—paused their writing. In this place, Ada realized, silence was not respect. Silence was an auditor checking arithmetic.

"On what grounds?" the page asked.

"On the grounds of signature." She turned the ledger toward them. "Look at who signed for the child. The entry says *surviving kin*. But the child had no kin. Her mother died owing nothing—her account is clean; I've seen it. Her father was never entered in any ledger in any kingdom, which means he was never born into debt, which means he could not sell what he did not hold. So who signed?"

She let the question hang. Then she set down the second document, the one Vess had spent three nights teaching her to read—not the words themselves so much as the weight of them, the grammar of obligation older than kingdoms.

"This is the hand that signed. And this—" she produced a rubbing she'd taken from the guild charter, pressed against the master's own seal-ring, "—is the hand that signs everything else in the Ledgerhouse. Every contract. Every foreclosure. Every soul sold for three favors."

The nib-fingered clerks had gone very still.

"They are the same hand," Ada said. "The guild master signed as kin to a child who had none. He forged a family to forge a sale. And under your own law—" here was the part Vess had drilled into her until her voice went hoarse, the clause carved into the court's founding stone before any kingdom had a name, "—a debt contracted by false identity is not a debt. It is theft wearing a debt's clothing. And you do not collect theft. You return it."

For a long moment the only sound was the scratch of one clerk, somewhere high in the stacks, who had not stopped working and would apparently never stop working, world without end.

Then the page-face rippled. "Verification requires testimony from the contracting parties. The seller is deceased."

"The seller," Ada said, "is available."

* * *

Vess came up through her like cold water rising in a well.

Ada had learned the shape of it over the weeks of lessons—the first pressure behind the sternum, the taste of candle smoke though no candle burned, the strange doubling of her own heartbeat as something else moved into rhythm beside it. She planted her feet, gripped the table's edge, and let her mistress in.

When she opened her mouth, the voice that emerged was not hers. It was dry and precise and carried the particular authority of a woman who had spent forty years being right in rooms full of people who wished she weren't.

"I am Vess of the Hedgerow," the ghost said through Ada's lips. "Dead eleven years. Bound, at my own request, to my grimoire, because I did not trust anyone living to finish my work. I witnessed the original transaction."

The clerks wrote. Oh, how they wrote.

"I was present when the guild master came to the child's mother's deathbed," Vess continued. "I was present because the mother was my student once, before the guild bought out her debts and called it mercy. The woman was dying and delirious and could not hold a pen. So the guild master took her hand—her dead-weight hand—and moved it across the page himself, and then he signed beneath it in his own script and recorded both marks as consenting parties. I said nothing." The voice did not waver, but Ada felt, from somewhere deep inside the possession, a grief like a stone dropping through dark water. "I said nothing because intervening would have cost me my charter, and I told myself the child was better off in the guild's keeping than starving. That was the lie I lived on for eleven years. I am here now to spend it."

"You testify," the Auditor's page said, "that the signature of the deceased was applied post-mortem or ante-mortem-incapable."

"I testify that the signature of the deceased was written by another hand entirely. I will match it stroke by stroke if your clerks require. I taught calligraphy to half the hedge-witches of the northern moors; I know a forged line when I see one, and I know the man who made it."

"And the second signature? Your own attendance does not invalidate the sale."

"No," Vess agreed, and here the voice sharpened to a point. "But the second signature was mine—or rather, it was placed where mine should have been. The old law requires a witness of standing to countersign any sale of an undivided soul. The guild master countersigned in the space reserved for the witness, in his own hand, and then altered the register afterward to record the witness as *one Maren Duskwater, hedge-witch, deceased*, a woman who never existed. I have checked. There is no Maren Duskwater in any registry, living or dead, above ground or below. Your own clerks can confirm it. Ask them."

The clerks confirmed it. Ada watched it happen in real time—one of the grey figures ascending the ledger-towers, pulling volumes, running a finger down columns, and then, after a long interval, making a small mark in the margin of its own body, somewhere near the wrist, that Ada somehow understood meant *confirmed*.

The page-face brightened. "Two fraudulent signatures constitutes structural fraud. Structural fraud voids the instrument. However." The word *however* landed in the chamber like a dropped coin. "Voiding the instrument does not automatically release the collateral. The soul has been held for eleven years. Eleven years of custody accrues fees. Storage. Preservation. Interest, compounded seasonally. The court cannot release an asset while any balance remains outstanding against it."

Ada felt Corvin shift beside her. "How much?" he asked, in the flat voice of a man who had spent his life asking that question and hated every answer.

The clerks conferred. One of them descended from the stacks carrying a slate, and on the slate was written a number, and the number had more digits than Ada's entire childhood house had had objects in it.

"That's not a debt," Corvin said. "That's a sentence."

"It's an accounting," the page corrected mildly. "There is a difference. Debts can be forgiven. Accountings must be resolved."

* * *

It was Noor who broke the arithmetic.

Later, Ada would try to reconstruct exactly how it happened, and she never quite could. But in the moment, what she saw was this: the child let go of her fingers, walked forward past the presentation table, past the clerks, past the scales, all the way to the Auditor's desk of black stone, and stood looking up at the blank luminous page with the absolute unblinking directness of someone who has not yet learned that some things are not looked at.

And the page began to write on itself.

Not the Auditor's words. Something else—characters surfacing in the old pre-kingdom script, the spiraling letters Noor drew in cellar dust, the language that was less a language than a memory of what language was before it learned to lie. The clerks stopped writing. Even the eternal one in the stacks paused, nib lifted, and for the first time in what might have been centuries, the counting room was completely silent.

Noor could not speak the words. Her voice had been sold; that was the whole wound of her. But the page could speak them for her, and what it said, letter by blooming letter, was this:

*I am not collateral. I am the creditor.*

The translation arrived in Ada's mind the way Vess's lessons always had—by sensation, by pressure, by meaning arriving whole. She heard herself gasp. Beside her, Corvin said, very quietly, "What did it say," and Ada answered because someone had to:

"She's saying the court owes *her*. Eleven years of custody—that isn't a fee owed by us. Custody of a person without consent is a liability. The court has been holding a free soul in bondage and booking it as an asset. Under the old law—the law this place was founded on, the law older than the guild, older than the kingdoms—"

"—an asset held unlawfully," Vess finished through her mouth, dry and triumphant, "inverts. The holder becomes the debtor. The court doesn't owe her release. The court owes her *eleven years*."

The blank page stared at the child. The child stared back.

And then, with a sound like a mountain deciding something, the Auditor rose from behind the black desk—an actual motion, a body unfolding that had perhaps not unfolded in an age—and inclined what served it for a head.

"The court acknowledges the inversion," it said, and every clerk in the chamber wrote the acknowledgment simultaneously, a sound like rain beginning. "The instrument is void. The custody is unlawful. The balance owed runs to the collateral." The page-face turned toward Ada, and though it had no eyes, she felt it see her, wholly, the way a ledger sees a number it has finally understood. "One matter remains. The soul-shard itself. Records indicate it was removed from general storage during the recent archive disturbance and moved to the deep vault. Retrieval requires—"

The ceiling came down.

Not all of it. A section of it, the north-east corner, where the stair they'd climbed from the surface burst inward in a gout of torchlight and smoke, and through the smoke came men in the grey-and-silver livery of the Ledgerhouse, and behind the men came the thing the guild master had saved for last resorts: a collector made of collected things, a hound-shaped mass of seized assets, repossessed years and foreclosed names packed tight into a shape that breathed.

The lead enforcer's voice rang off the marble. "By warrant of the Guild Master of the Ledgerhouse: seize the contested asset, arrest the thieves, and burn whatever refuses to be seized!"

The clerks scattered like startled birds. The Auditor did not move. And Ada, snatching Noor up onto her hip, saw Corvin already turning toward the breach with the grim calm of a man doing arithmetic of his own—counting bodies, counting seconds, counting the door.

"The vault," the Auditor's page said calmly, amid the smoke, "is below."

* * *

They ran.

Down behind the black desk, down stairs that had not been stairs until the Auditor spoke them into being, Corvin at the rear throwing a borrowed year behind him like a flare—it detonated into a wall of remembered winter that bought them ten seconds and cost him something Ada didn't ask about. Vess raged inside her skull, wanting to stay and watch the archives burn the liars' signatures to ash, and Ada told her, aloud, in her own voice at last, "You'll get your confession delivered. That's the deal. Stay with me."

The vault door was iron, and the Auditor reached it first, and opened it with a word.

Inside, on a shelf of black stone, between a jar of bottled griefs and a coffer of borrowed years, sat a shard of light the size of a sparrow's egg. It pulsed. It had a rhythm. Ada knew that rhythm; she'd felt it against her palm every night for weeks, the small stubborn beat of a girl asleep in a cellar who had refused, all this time, to stop being a person.

Noor reached for it with both hands.

And the whole mountain shuddered, dust sifting from the vault ceiling, and from somewhere far above came the sound of fire meeting paper—the guild's collectors burning the archives, burning the evidence, burning eleven years of proof—and Ada understood with terrible clarity that the verdict meant nothing now unless the proof of it walked out of this mountain alive, in their hands, in their mouths, in a child's restored voice.

"Hold the door," she said to Corvin.

He was already turning.
