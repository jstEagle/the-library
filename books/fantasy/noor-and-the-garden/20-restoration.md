## 20. Restoration

They came out of the mountain at dawn, and the light did a thing Ada had never seen it do.

It caught the child first. Noor—though she was not yet Noor, not wholly; she was still the girl with the hollow where her name had been—stood at the mouth of the sealed door with the sunrise pouring over her like water over a stone, and for one moment she seemed almost translucent, almost finished, as if the world were checking her against some original and finding her nearly complete.

Nearly.

The hollow was still there. Ada could feel it the way you feel a missing tooth: not pain, exactly, but an attention. A place where the tongue keeps going.

Corvin leaned against a petrified trunk behind them, gray-faced, his left hand wrapped in a strip of his own shirt where the collectors' fire had kissed him on the way out. He hadn't spoken since the door closed. He kept looking at his hands as if expecting to find ledger lines written across them.

"Sit down," Ada said to the girl. "Please."

The ground here was salt moor at its edge, white-crusted earth giving way to honest soil, and already the petrified forest thinned behind them into ordinary dead trees, then living ones, bare-branched and waiting for spring. Ada had chosen this spot without choosing it. Her feet had simply stopped walking when the light came through the branches at the right angle, and she understood, in the way Vess had taught her to understand things—that old, unkind, precise way—that this was where it had always been meant to happen. Every restoration has a place. The books said so. Vess had said so, in the last lesson, the one that wasn't a lesson at all but a bequest.

*You'll need clean ground,* Vess had said, her voice like paper turning. *Not holy. Clean. There's a difference, girl, and the difference is everything. Holy ground belongs to something. Clean ground belongs to no one. You're going to give her a name that belongs to herself, so you build it on land that owes nothing to anybody.*

Ada knelt. She took off her coat and spread it on the frost-stiff grass, because the child had spent three days underground and her knees were blue.

"Here."

The girl sat. She looked up at Ada with those eyes that were too old and too young at once—the eyes of someone who had been filed away in a cabinet beneath a mountain and remembered everything about being a person except the part that made her one.

Ada's hands were shaking.

She had imagined this moment a hundred times during the descent, during the trial, during the terrible bright chaos of the burning archive room when Corvin held the door and the soul-shard—a thing like a seed of warm glass, like a coal that dreamed—had been pressed into her palms by the court's own clerk, who wept as he handed it over, because even accountants weep when a debt comes home. She had imagined her voice steady. She had imagined Vess's words coming out of her mouth clean and certain, the old language flowing through her like water through a channel cut long ago.

Instead her hands shook, and the shard sat in its wrapping of Corvin's shirt-cloth, and the morning waited.

"You don't have to do it perfectly," Corvin said quietly. He didn't look up from his hands. "Nobody does anything perfectly. I've read enough ledgers to know. It's the corrections column that tells you who people really are."

"That's the most sentimental thing you've ever said."

"Don't tell anyone. I have a reputation."

A sound came out of the girl—not quite a laugh, not yet. Laughter needed a self to laugh with. But it was the shape of one, the outline, and Ada felt her chest crack a little along a seam she'd been holding shut since the cellar, since the frost fair, since the winter accounts and the entry that read *one soul, sold for three favors*, since before all of it, back to a green door and a woman teaching her scripts and a night when Ada Galloway had decided that the safest thing a person could be was unreadable.

She unwrapped the shard.

It was warmer than it should have been. It pulsed very slightly, the way a sleeping bird breathes, and when the light hit it, colors moved inside it that had no names in any guild inventory. This was what they had taken. This small warm breathing thing. Three favors. That was the price. Somewhere in the Ledgerhouse there was a page with a signature on it, and the signature had been fraudulent, and Ada had proven it before a court older than kingdoms, and the proof had cost her everything she'd built her life out of.

Her name. Given freely, as the Auditor required. *Ada Galloway*, spoken aloud in that vast filing-hall, witnessed and entered and made permanent. Total accountability, forever. She could feel the weight of it now, actually feel it, like a coat of lead she would wear the rest of her life. Every debt she owed was now traceable. Every wrong she'd done was now addressable. Anyone, anywhere, who knew how to read such things could find her.

And the strange thing—the thing she kept turning over like a coin she didn't trust—was that it didn't feel like loss. It felt like setting down something heavy she'd forgotten she was carrying.

"Vess," she said softly. "If you're still in there. I could use the words."

Silence. The grimoire lay in her satchel where it had lain since the mountain, and the margins stayed empty. Vess had gone quiet after the trial—spent, Ada thought, or satisfied, or both. A ghost's unfinished business, carried and delivered. Maybe that was all a ghost was: a sentence that hadn't been finished yet. Maybe Vess was finally, wherever ghosts go, finished.

But the words were still in Ada. Weeks of candlelit lessons, the child's fingers correcting her tongue-position by touch, Vess's voice drilling her mercilessly—*again, again, your mouth is lazy, again*—the old language lived in her now the way a scar lives in skin. She didn't need the ghost. She needed only to trust what had been put into her.

That was the last lesson, she realized. That was the one Vess never said aloud.

Ada knelt opposite the girl and took her hands. They were small and cold and they gripped back with surprising strength, and in that grip Ada felt the language rise—not summoned, just *arriving*, the way dawn arrives—and she began.

She spoke the opening words of restoration in the tongue older than any kingdom, the tongue the child had babbled at the guild's back door on the night of the frost fair, the tongue that predated names themselves and was therefore the only language in which a stolen name could be honestly returned. Her voice sounded strange to her own ears: deeper, slower, each word landing like a stone set into an arch.

The shard warmed in her cupped hands.

She spoke the second part, the naming-back, and here Vess's teachings were exact: *you do not give the name. You never give a name. Giving creates debt, and this child will owe no one ever again. You return it. Returning settles accounts.* The distinction mattered more than anything else in the whole rite, Vess had said, gripping Ada's wrist hard enough to bruise a ghost's chill into living flesh. *Get it wrong and you've just rebuilt the cage with kinder bars.*

So Ada did not give. She opened her hands and spoke the name—not the true name, which belonged to the girl alone and which even the court's archives had kept under seal—but the *shape* of it, the echo of it, the door-shaped absence rendered in sound, and she said: *This was yours. It was taken wrongly. It was never ours. Take it back.*

The shard rose.

It lifted out of her palms like a spark off a fire, hung in the cold morning air between them, and turned slowly, showing colors, showing—Ada caught her breath—showing images, tiny and fast: a garden, a woman's hands in dirt, a door painted green, a hearth, a dog, rain on a window, a lullaby in a language the girl had never been taught because the lullaby had been sold along with everything else—

Her mother's memories. Fragments of a life before the sale. The court had kept the collateral intact, honest in its horrible way, and now all of it came home at once.

The shard sank into the girl's chest like a stone into still water, without a ripple, and the girl gasped.

For a long moment nothing happened. The morning went on being morning. A crow argued with another crow somewhere in the bare trees. Corvin had stopped pretending not to watch; he was leaning forward, his burned hand forgotten, his whole scarred attention fixed on the child.

Then the girl blinked, and her eyes changed.

Not dramatically. Not with light or fire or any of the theater the ballads would have added. She simply blinked, and behind her eyes something *sat down*—something that had been standing in a doorway for years, waiting—and the looking-out became a looking-from. Presence arrived in her the way an owner arrives in a house that has been kept by strangers: cautiously at first, touching the walls, and then all at once, completely, home.

She opened her mouth.

"Noor," she said.

Just that. One word, in the common tongue, in a clear young voice with a slight rasp in it from disuse, and the word was her own name, spoken by herself, chosen from herself, claimed. Not returned after all, Ada realized with a jolt—in the end the girl had refused even the echo Ada offered and had reached past it, past the archive, past the sale, down to whatever original place a name is kept before anyone writes it in any book, and had taken it herself. Free and clear. Owing nothing.

Noor.

The name meant light. Ada knew that much of the old language, and she suspected the girl had known it too, and had reached for exactly that word, in exactly that language, as if to say: *this is what was mine all along, and no one knew, because no one thought to look for a lamp in a ledger.*

"Noor," the girl said again, tasting it, and then she laughed—the real laugh now, full and unguarded and shocking in its ordinariness, a child's laugh on a cold morning—and Corvin made a sound that he would deny to his grave, somewhere between a cough and something else entirely, and turned his face away toward the trees.

Ada found she was crying. She let it happen. Accountability, she was learning, worked in both directions: having a name again meant having a face again, and having a face meant tears could fall in front of witnesses, and that was fine. That was, in fact, the entire point.

* * *

They walked out of the petrified forest together as the sun climbed, and the world did the other thing Ada had never seen it do: it rearranged itself around a person instead of around her absence.

Before, in the Ledgerhouse years, doors had opened away from her. Clerks forgot she'd been in the room. Guards looked through her. Even the guild master, who signed off on her audits every season, would have struggled to describe her beyond "small, quiet, correct." Invisibility had been her craft and her armor and her home, and she had been so good at it that she'd half-believed she wasn't real.

Now innkeepers noticed her. At the crossroads tavern that evening, the keeper met her eyes and asked her name, and Ada—who had braced for the question for two days, who had rehearsed deflections—heard herself answer plainly: "Ada Galloway." And the keeper wrote it in his register, in his terrible handwriting, in a book that would sit on a shelf in a public room where anyone could read it, and Ada watched her own name sitting there in a stranger's book and felt—exposed, yes, terrified, yes—but also *entered*. Recorded. Real.

Noor watched this happen from the corner table with open fascination, eating stew with the concentration of someone making up for lost years.

"It frightens you," Noor said. Her voice was still raspy, still new, but she used it constantly now, the way children use a new pair of boots—stamping around in it, testing it. "Being seen."

"Constantly."

"But you did it anyway."

"I did it anyway."

"Why?"

Ada considered the question honestly, because the girl had earned honesty and would accept nothing less; the old language had a way of making liars physically uncomfortable, and Noor slipped into it whenever she smelled one.

"Because you were worth more than my hiding," Ada said. "That's the whole arithmetic. I spent six years building a number so small no one could find it, and then I met you, and the sum came out wrong. You were worth infinity and I was offering zero. The math embarrassed me."

Noor considered this with her spoon halfway to her mouth. Then she nodded, once, with the gravity of a magistrate, and went back to her stew.

Corvin, at the next table, snorted into his ale.

Later, by the fire, Noor sat between them—she had appointed herself between them, a border-state with a strong foreign policy—and studied Ada for a long time in the firelight. Ada had learned to wait when the girl got this look. Some process was running underneath, something older than childhood, and interrupting it was unwise.

"What?" Ada finally asked.

"You gave up your name," Noor said. "To the Auditor. Freely given. Forever accountable."

"Yes."

"So everyone can find you now. Everything you owe. Everything you've done."

"Yes."

Noor nodded slowly. Then she frowned, and the frown deepened, and she said something in the old language—a short phrase, questioning—and answered herself in the same tongue, and frowned harder, and finally looked up with an expression of genuine distress.

"That's wrong," she said. "That's *unbalanced*. You paid a name and got mine back, but a name freely given isn't a payment—it's a gift, and gifts create debts—" she stopped, hearing herself, hearing the guild's grammar come out of her own new mouth, and grimaced. "No. That's their math. The mountain's math. The Ledgerhouse math." She kicked at the hearthstone. "Everything I know about names, I learned from the people who stole mine. How do I know what's true?"

Ada's heart broke a little, and mended a little, in the same instant.

"Come here," she said. "Firelight. Good posture. We're going to do a lesson."

"A lesson? I'm not the one who needed lessons."

"You needed different lessons. And so did I." Ada sat cross-legged and gestured the girl down opposite her, and Corvin, pretending to doze, arranged himself into maximum listening position. "Vess taught me the old rules. Return, don't give. Settle, don't owe. But there's a third rule, and she only hinted at it, and I think you already know it better than she did, because you did it in the mountain without being taught."

"Which is?"

"When someone gives you something freely," Ada said, "you don't repay it. Repayment turns a gift into a transaction, and transactions are how cages get built. But you also don't just take it and walk away. That turns the giver into a tool. What you do—" she took a breath, because she was inventing this as she said it, or perhaps remembering it from somewhere older than Vess, older than the guilds, from whatever human machinery ran underneath all the accounting—"*is you pass it on. To someone who can't pay you back. Someone who'll have to pass it on too.*"

The fire popped. Noor stared at her.

"Like the boots," Corvin said suddenly, from his fake sleep, eyes still closed.

"Like the boots," Ada agreed. He had never explained the red boots. He never would. That was rather the point of him.

Noor was quiet for a long time. Then she said, in the old language, a single word, and held out her hand.

Ada took it.

What happened next happened quickly and gently, the way snowfall happens. Noor spoke a name into the space between them—not Ada's name, not the one the Auditor held, not the one in the innkeeper's register. A *new* name. A small one, a private one, a name in the old tongue that meant something like *the one who reads in the dark* or *the one who opens doors from the inside*—Ada couldn't translate it fully, and understood she wasn't meant to. It settled over her like a shawl, like a second skin, weightless and warm, and it was given with no expectation, no entry in any ledger, no witness but a dozing enforcer and a hearth fire.

Freely given. Creating, by every law Ada had ever been taught, an infinite unpayable debt.

And Ada felt it land, and felt the old panic rise—*I owe, I owe, how will I ever*—and then felt Noor's small hand squeeze hers, and heard the girl say, in the common tongue, grinning:

"Pass it on."

* * *

They left the tavern at first light, because Corvin said guild spotters worked taverns at first light, and because Ada had copied evidence into her coat lining and intended to use it, and because some debts are settled not with payment but with action.

At the crossroads they paused. The road forked: north to the city, to the Ledgerhouse, to testimony and reckoning; east along the moor edge, to anywhere at all.

"Well," Corvin said. "This is where I'd make a dramatic speech, if I were the type."

"You're not the type."

"No. Thank the gods." He shifted his pack, wincing at the burn on his hand. "I'll see you in the city, eventually. Someone has to survive being a witness twice." He crouched, abruptly, to Noor's level—an awkward maneuver, all elbows, clearly unrehearsed. "Keep the boots resoled," he said gruffly. "Red wears out faster than black. Everyone knows that. It's why they charge more."

Noor threw her arms around his neck. He endured it with the expression of a man being audited by affection, and then, after a moment, with an expression that wasn't that at all.

Ada watched them, and touched the new name resting against her skin like a coal that didn't burn, and thought about gardens.

When Noor released him and took Ada's hand, Corvin straightened, squared his pack, and turned north ahead of them, walking backward a few steps.

"One more thing," he called. "The guild master keeps a private ledger. Red binding. Third shelf of his office safe, behind the charter copies. If you're going to testify, testify with *everything*." He grinned, sharp and sudden. "Consider it passed on."

He turned and was gone up the north road, a gray figure shrinking into gray morning, and Ada stood in the fork with a child's hand in hers and a stolen name of her own choosing warming her throat, and looked down at Noor, who was looking up at her with the particular brightness of someone about to propose something enormous.

"He said pass it on," Noor said. "So. When we finish with the guild master—" her grip tightened, her chin lifted, and the morning light caught her eyes, and in them Ada saw the future arriving, patient and unstoppable as spring— "*can we plant a garden?*"
