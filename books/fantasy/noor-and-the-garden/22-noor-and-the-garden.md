## 22. Noor and the Garden

The first spring after the mountain, Noor planted beans over the sealed door.

Not flowers. Not a memorial. Beans, because they came up fast, and because she wanted to watch something happen. She knelt in the thaw-soft earth with her sleeves pushed up and pressed each seed into the soil like she was filing it—thumb to the first knuckle, spaced evenly, a row straight enough to satisfy any auditor. Ada watched from the cottage step with a cup of tea going cold in her hands and thought: *she learned that from me, and I learned it from a guild that deserved neither of us.*

"You're staring," Noor said, without looking up.

"I'm supervising."

"You're worrying." The girl patted down the last seed. "It's different. I can tell by your breathing."

Six years old now, or passing for it—the body had settled into its years the way a ledger settles after an audit, everything finally where it belonged. Her hair had grown back in dark and thick. She wore red boots two sizes too large because she refused, with the full stubbornness of her restored soul, to give them up before she grew into them. Corvin's boots. Bought at a frost fair without explanation, the way he did everything that mattered.

"They'll fit eventually," Ada said.

"That's what you said last year."

"And I was right."

"I was right faster," Noor said, and grinned, and went back to her rows.

* * *

The garden spread the way gardens do—by accident, then by intention. Beans became peas, peas became a bed of onions, onions became a trellis for climbing roses that Vess would have mocked and secretly loved. By midsummer the whole slope above the petrified forest's edge was green and humming, and beneath it, three fathoms down behind a door grown shut with root and stone, the court kept its silence.

Ada checked the seal once a season. That was all. A hand flat against the warm stone of the doorframe, a listening, a nod. The court honored its verdicts; that was the one thing you could say for a place that ran on accounting. The debt was void. The child was free. And somewhere below, in the archives that smelled of dust and cold iron, a disgraced enforcer filed documents in perpetuity, proof against his own erasure.

The first letter had come at midwinter, a year after the fire in the stacks. It arrived without cover or signature—a single sheet of guild paper, ruled and dated, stamped with the court's seal instead of the Ledgerhouse's. *Document 4471-C: Inventory of Sealed Archive, West Annex. Filed by C. of the Undercourt. Condition: complete.* Nothing else. No greeting, no name, no word of how he was, whether the work was cruel, whether the court's service weighed on him the way debts weigh.

Just proof. Filed each season since, punctual as frost.

Ada had cried over the first one. She'd admit that much, privately, to no one. The second she read twice and put away. By the fourth she had started keeping them properly—in a box, in order, the way Corvin would have wanted—and by the second year she understood what they were. Not letters. Receipts. *I endure. I am accounted for. Do not grieve me until the filings stop.*

She wrote back exactly once, early on, a long letter that said everything and therefore nothing, and it came back unopened with a court stamp: *Correspondence not permitted under current terms.* So she stopped writing and started doing what he could actually receive. Each season, when his document arrived, she sent back nothing—but she added a line to a different book entirely.

* * *

The new ledger lived on the shelf above the hearth, bound in undyed linen, its pages unruled. Ada had made it herself the winter after the trial, sitting up nights while Noor slept, and the first entry was still the truest thing she'd ever written:

*Gifts given, never repaid.*

Not debts. She had spent her whole life inside the grammar of debt—owed, owing, collateral, interest, the price of survival—and she was done with it. This book recorded only what flowed one way and was never meant to flow back.

*To Noor: bread, honeyed, the night we met. Repayment: none required.*
*To Noor: pronunciation corrected by touch, forty-one times, without complaint from either party.*
*To Noor: a name, freely given, and hers forever.*
*From Noor: a name for me, chosen freely, which I will not write here because some gifts are too large for the page.*
*To Corvin: my silence about the cellar key, which he never asked for but accepted anyway.*
*To Corvin: red boots, delivered through a frost-fair stranger who owed me a favor. He will never know. Good.*
*From Vess: every lesson, priced in advance and paid in full, except the confession—which was never a price at all, only a burden looking for shoulders.*

That last entry had taken Ada three tries. She carried Vess's confession to the grave the autumn after the reckoning—spoke it aloud over the mistress's headstone in the old tongue, the pre-kingdom words rolling out of her mouth rough and sure while crows listened from the fence posts. *I taught you to hide so well that you hid from me. Forgive me the hiding. I was afraid of what you'd become, and more afraid of what I'd made you.* The wind had moved through the churchyard grass in a long slow ripple, and Ada had chosen to believe it was an answer, because believing things was also a gift you gave yourself.

The ledger filled slowly. That was the point of it. Debts accumulated; gifts were rationed out like candlelight, precious because they were finite. Some entries were small—*to the toll-keeper's bridge, a memory of rain on the moor, left as thanks rather than toll*—and some were enormous. But every one of them was written in the same ink, and none of them expected anything back.

Noor knew about the book. Ada had shown it to her on the girl's last birthday, expecting questions, maybe mockery. Instead Noor had studied it for a long time and said, "You should add one more," and dictated, in her strange clear voice: *"From Noor: the garden. Given to the ground, never to be dug up."*

So it was entered. In Ada's best hand.

* * *

Autumn came gold and brief. The beans were long finished; the roses held their last few blooms like coins clutched in a fist. On a bright cold morning, Noor stood at the garden gate arguing with the post rider about whether a document constituted mail.

"It's not a letter," the rider said, holding it out of reach with the weary patience of a man who had done this four times a year for six years. "It's a filing. Court-stamped. I just deliver it."

"She means is there a message in it," Ada said, coming down the path. "There isn't. There never is. Thank you, Tam."

"Ma'am." He touched his cap, glanced at Noor, and rode on, leaving the girl scowling at the paper in Ada's hands.

*Document 8890-B: Quarterly Verification, Sealed Archive. Filed by C. of the Undercourt. Condition: complete. Note appended per filer's discretion:* and here, for the first time in six years, a single line beyond the formula, in a hand Ada knew better than her own—

*The boots fit, I'm told. Tell her to stop growing.*

Noor snatched the page before Ada could react, read it, and went very still. Then she laughed—not the light laugh she gave strangers, but the deep one, the real one, the one that meant something had landed in the place where her soul had been stitched back together.

"He counted them," she said. "He's been counting. Six years of filings and he finally—" She pressed the paper flat against her chest. "He remembers the fair. He remembers buying them."

"He remembers everything," Ada said. "That's his punishment and his profession."

"Can I keep it?"

"It's addressed to the household." Ada paused. "Keep it."

Noor ran for the house, boots slapping, to press the document into the box with its eighty-seven siblings, and Ada stood alone in the garden with the wind moving through the dead beanstalks and felt the season turn in her chest the way it turned in the air.

Eighty-eight filings. He was still there. Still enduring, still complete, still filing proof of himself quarter by quarter like a man laying stones across a river he couldn't yet cross. Someday, perhaps, the terms would change. Someday a document would arrive with more than a note appended—or a door would open in the roots, or the court would release him as it released all debts it deemed paid. She didn't know when. She had stopped needing to know. Hope, she had learned, was not a balance to be struck but a seed to be planted and tended without guarantee.

Like beans. Like a garden over a sealed door.

* * *

Winter came early that year, and hard. The frost fair returned to the valley on the anniversary week, as it always did, and Ada took Noor the way she always took her—early, before the crowds, when the lanterns were still being lit and the ice on the pond sang in the cold.

They walked the stalls hand in hand. Noor haggled ferociously over a knife sharpener's wares and won, which worried Ada slightly and delighted her more. They drank spiced cider from clay cups and watched a puppeteer perform the Trial of Ledgers, badly, with a dragon made of socks, and Noor heckled the puppet-guild-master with such accuracy that the puppeteer broke character to shake her hand.

At the bootmaker's stall, Noor stopped.

Her current boots—Corvin's originals, finally outgrown this past summer despite her best efforts—hung laced together over her shoulder, retired with honors. She looked at the rows of new leather, the small sizes, the reds, and then up at Ada with an expression of terrible gravity.

"I want to buy some," she said. "Red ones. For someone else. Without explaining why."

Ada's throat tightened. "Who?"

"A boy at the mill. His mother can't afford boots and he goes barefoot past our gate every morning and pretends not to care." Noor was already digging in her pocket for the coin purse she'd earned haggling. "And someday, when he asks why, I'll say nothing. And he'll spend years wondering. And then maybe he'll do it for somebody else." She counted out the coins with the precision of a born accountant. "That's how it works. You told me. Gifts move forward, not back."

"I don't think I ever said that exactly."

"You said it in everything else." She took the boots from the startled bootmaker, red as embers, sized for a boy of eight. "Write it in the book tonight. *From Noor: red boots, given forward.*"

That night, by candlelight, Ada opened the linen-bound ledger and wrote it exactly so. Then she sat for a while with the pen idle, listening to the wind off the moor and the small sounds of Noor sleeping in the next room, and she added one final entry of her own, the kind that wasn't a gift or a debt but simply a fact, set down because facts deserve recording:

*Year seven. The child is whole. The enforcer endures. The ghost is at rest. The garden grows over the door, and the door stays shut, and the world has not ended, which I am told is unusual for stories like mine.*

*I was invisible once, and called it safety. I am visible now, and accountable, and known by a name I did not choose and have come to love. If survival has a price, then let this be the payment: that I stay. That I am seen. That I answer, every morning, to someone who needs me and whom I need, and that neither of us keeps score.*

*Except, of course, in this book. Where the score is only ever: given, and given, and given.*

She blotted the page. Down the hall, Noor murmured in her sleep—a fragment of dream-language, the old tongue rising soft as breath, words older than kingdoms that Ada no longer needed lessons to understand:

*Planted. Growing. Enough.*

Ada closed the ledger, banked the fire, and went to stand a moment at the window, where the snow was beginning to fall on the garden—on the bean rows waiting under white, on the rose trellis, on the warm stone seam far beneath the soil where a door slept behind roots. Spring would come. Seeds would rise. And somewhere below the frost line, a man who had traded his freedom for a child's soul would file his quarterly proof, and a woman who had traded her name for that same child would read it by firelight, and between those two fixed points—filed and received, given and received—the years would keep turning like pages.

Outside, the snow thickened. Inside, the ledger waited for spring, and for whatever the next season's document would bring—and for the first time in her careful, hidden, costly life, Ada Galloway found she was not afraid of what might be written next.
