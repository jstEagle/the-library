## 1. What the Bone Remembers

The bone told Noor it wanted to be a wren.

She'd known from the moment she pulled it from the tinker's cart—a knuckle of river-polished femur, dense and pale as moonlight on snow. Most carvers saw only material. Noor saw inclination. Bone had preferences the way wood had grain, and if you fought them, the piece came out dead. If you listened—

Well. Listening was the whole problem, wasn't it.

She turned the half-formed wren under her lamp, the single candle guttering in the draft that crept through her cellar workshop beneath Tam's chandlery. Wax smell above, tallow and honey, masking the finer scent of marrow dust and linseed oil down here. Her knives lay ranked on oiled leather: three gouges, two gravers, the hooked little burin Grandmother Ishikawa had left her, its handle worn to the shape of another woman's grip.

Outside, Thornfell was closing up for the night. She could hear it through the floorboards—the smuggler's warren settling into itself like an old dog. Cart wheels on wet cobbles. The tavern on Rope Row hitting the loud hour. Someone's baby crying three tenements over, then not. Rain starting, soft as an apology.

Noor set the burin against the wren's wing and cut.

And the bone opened.

It always happened at the first true cut, past the surface, into the living architecture of the thing. Not sound exactly. More like pressure behind the ears before a storm, then a voice arriving whole, the way you sometimes wake already knowing what someone said to you in a dream.

*Cold,* said the bone. *That's the thing nobody tells you. It isn't the dying that hurts. It's how long the cold takes.*

Noor kept carving. Steady hands, steady breath. This was the discipline, the one Grandmother had drilled into her before her voice went quiet for good: *You are the door, girl, not the room. You open. You do not fall through.* Twelve years old, sitting on a cold step, learning to hold a knife while the dead spoke through her hands.

*She'll want to know I didn't suffer. Lie to her about that part. No—don't lie. Just... don't tell her everything.*

The voice belonged to a man. Old, by the texture of it—old the way rope is old, frayed but still holding weight. Aldous Wren, she'd have bet her last coin. The widow Merrin had come to Tam's shop three days ago with this bone wrapped in flannel, paid too much, and asked for "something small he can rest in. Something with wings." She'd said his name like it still had edges.

Aldous Wren. Died in his sleep, they said, which meant he hadn't, quite.

Noor carved the wing feathers one by one, and with each stroke the man's last hours unspooled—not as memory, exactly, but as residue. Bone remembered what flesh let go. The ache in his hips. The taste of the fever-tea his wife had brewed, chamomile gone bitter. The window he'd asked to be left open, because he wanted to hear the rain on Thornfell's roofs one more time, and Merrin had sat beside him with her mending and pretended not to watch him listen.

*Good woman,* the bone murmured, and there was such plain love in it that Noor's throat tightened. *Better than I deserved. Tell her—*

He stopped. Dead men always stopped at the telling part, as if the words that mattered most were packed too deep even for bone to reach. Or as if some things could only be carried up, never down.

Then the voice shifted. Firmed. The way a dream sharpens just before waking into something else entirely.

*Riders,* Aldous Wren said, and now he wasn't talking to his wife anymore, or to Noor, or to anyone in particular—he was talking to the dark, to whatever might be listening, because a man who has nothing left to lose spends his last coins freely. *Riders in the high passes. I saw them from the ridge before my legs gave out. Crown livery. Grey cloaks, grey horses, moving quiet as ash falling. They're not tax men. Tax men travel loud and drink louder. These ones travel like hunters.*

Noor's burin stopped mid-stroke.

*They stopped at the shrine on Coldspur and burned it. Didn't take anything. Didn't ask questions. Just burned it and stood watching until the stones went white. I've seen soldiers my whole life and I've never seen men stand and watch a fire like they enjoyed the quiet of it.*

The candle flame leaned. Somewhere above, Tam's footsteps crossed the shop floor—the particular drag of his left heel that meant he was carrying the good beeswax down to count. Normal sounds. Safe sounds. But Noor sat very still, the way you do when a floorboard creaks somewhere it shouldn't.

*There's talk in the low country,* the dead man went on, faster now, urgency bleeding through the bone like water through chalk. *An edict coming. Something about gifts. About people who hear what shouldn't be heard. They're calling it cleansing. Cleansing.* A dry sound that might have been laughter. *I'm going to die tonight and I find I mind less about that than I should, because at least I won't be here when they come asking names.*

The voice thinned. Frayed. Went out like a lamp pinched between fingers.

Noor sat alone with the wren and the rain and the ringing silence where a dead man's fear had been.

She looked down at her hands. Steady. Of course they were steady; they were always steady; that was the curse of a well-made door. Inside, though, something had begun to move, slow and cold, the way floodwater moves under ice.

Crown riders in the passes. Burned shrines. An edict about gifts.

About people like her.

* * *

She finished the wren. It took her another hour, and she made herself do it properly—every feather barbed, the beak bright, the little bird caught mid-turn as if startled off a branch. That was the covenant of her craft, unwritten but absolute: grief got her best work, always. Grief and terror used the same hands, apparently.

The bone had gone quiet after the warning. They usually did, once they'd given up what they carried. She held the finished piece to the light and saw nothing in it now but bone—a small white bird with empty eyes, beautiful and mute as any stone.

This was the loneliness of it, the part no one guessed. People imagined hearing the dead must be like a market, all noise and bargaining. It wasn't. It was this: a candlelit cellar, a finished carving, and silence. The dead spoke once, mostly, and then they were gone wherever the gone things go, and Noor remained—with warm hands and cold knowledge and no one, no one at all, she could tell.

Tam knew, of course. Tam knew everything that moved through Thornfell, legal or otherwise. But knowing and understanding were different animals, and Tam, kind as he was, crossed himself with a chandler's casual piety whenever her gift came up, the way you'd touch iron against a bad omen.

Grandmother would have understood. Grandmother was twelve years dead, and Noor had heard her last words through the bones of her own hand—the hand that had held hers at the end—and what Grandmother had said was strange and unfinished and Noor had spent twelve years turning it over like a stone with something underneath:

*The Hollow is hungry, little door. And someone is locking the pantry.*

She'd never learned what it meant. There was no one left to ask.

Noor wrapped the wren in clean linen, blew out the candle, and climbed the stairs into the chandlery's back room, where Tam sat counting wax by lamplight, his reading spectacles pushed up into grey hair.

"Finished?" he asked, without looking up.

"Finished." She set the bundle on the counter. "For Widow Merrin. Aldous Wren."

Tam's scales clinked as he weighed nothing in particular. "Good man. Bad lungs. He owed me eleven years of candles and paid every debt, which makes him nearly unique in Thornfell." He glanced up then, and his eyes narrowed—not at her face, but at whatever was written around it. "What did you hear?"

Noor hesitated. Lying to Tam was like lying to a wall; it stood there and waited.

"He talked about his wife," she said. "Mostly."

"And?"

"And riders." She heard her own voice go flat and careful. "In the high passes. Crown livery. They burned the shrine on Coldspur."

The scales stopped. In the silence, the rain filled the whole world, drumming on the roof of the chandlery, on the warrens, on the passes where grey-cloaked men watched fires burn down to white stone.

Tam took off his spectacles very slowly. "Burned shrines," he repeated. "That's not collection. That's declaration."

"I know."

"Noor." He said her name the way he said *fire* near the wax stores. "If there's an edict coming—if they're hunting gifted folk—" He didn't finish. He rubbed his jaw and looked at her the way he'd looked at her when she was nine years old and he'd found her in his cellar, an orphan with stolen bread and a bone knife, listening to a dead rat's last confused hour. He'd fed her anyway. He'd hidden her ever since. "You need to think about leaving Thornfell for a while."

"I live here."

"You *hide* here. There's a difference, and I've been generous enough not to say it aloud for fifteen years." He put the spectacles back on, and his voice gentled. "Take the wren to Merrin tomorrow. Early. Then come back and we'll talk about the north road."

Noor picked up the bundle. Through the linen, the little bone bird pressed cool against her palm, and she thought of Aldous Wren spending his final coins of speech on a warning no one had asked him for, because the dying can afford honesty.

"They're coming for us, aren't they," she said. Not really a question.

Tam didn't answer. Above them, faint under the rain, a bell began to ring in the temple quarter—one strike, then another, then many, urgent and overlapping, bells that had no business ringing at midnight.

Tam rose so fast his stool went over. "That's the alarm," he said. "That's the *raid* bell."

Down in the cellar, in the dark, the wren sat silent in its wrappings, holding a dead man's warning in its hollow chest—and outside, torchlight was already blooming orange against the rain-slicked windows of Rope Row, moving toward the chandlery, street by street, like fire finding its way along a fuse.
