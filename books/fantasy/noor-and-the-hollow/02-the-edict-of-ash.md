## 2. The Edict of Ash

The proclamation came to Thornfell the way rot comes to a house—quietly, through the walls, and then all at once, everywhere.

Noor heard it before she saw it. The dead in the bone had gone restless all morning, a low murmur like wind through a keyhole, and when she finally climbed the stair from her cellar workroom to the street above, she understood why. A crowd had gathered at the well square, pressed shoulder to shoulder around a rider in crown livery who stood on the fountain's rim and read aloud from a scroll sealed with black wax.

"—and let it be known that those who traffic with the unquiet dead, who hear their whispers and carry their words among the living, are vessels of corruption. Such persons shall surrender themselves to the offices of cleansing, where they will be purified by fire and prayer—"

*Purified.* Noor had heard enough euphemisms in Thornfell to fill a graveyard. Purified meant burned. Cleansed meant ash.

She pulled her hood lower and slipped along the edge of the crowd, listening with her ears instead of her gift. It was safer that way. Her ears only caught what living mouths said, and living mouths were saying very little. That was the worst of it—the silence. In Thornfell, silence was never empty. It was the sound of people deciding not to look at each other.

The smugglers of the warrens had always been a loose family: thieves, runners, forgers, fence-menders, bound together less by loyalty than by mutual usefulness. Noor's carvings had been part of that economy for six years. A widow wanted a word from her drowned husband; a runner wanted his dead mother's blessing before a dangerous job; old Marta paid in bread for messages from a son hanged for theft. Nobody asked how the bones spoke. Nobody wanted to think too hard about it. The gift had been useful, therefore invisible, therefore safe.

By nightfall, invisible was over.

* * *

Tam found her in the cellar, as he always did, letting himself down the ladder without knocking because knocking, he said, was for people with something to hide.

"Word's out," he said. He was a broad man gone soft at the edges, with hands like knotted rope and a face that had stopped pretending to be kind around the time Noor met him. "Yara's pulled every carving order off the board. Every one."

Noor set down her knife. On the bench before her lay half-finished a knucklebone for the widow Sera, whose husband had died in the pass last winter. She'd been hearing his voice for three days now, patient, faint, saying the same thing over and over like a man tapping on glass: *the riders, tell her about the riders.*

"I don't understand," Noor said. "They've never cared before. Half the crown's own tax collectors buy love-charms from hedge-witches."

"They didn't care." Tam lowered himself onto the stool across from her, and the stool creaked under him the way it always did, and somehow that small familiar sound made everything worse. "Something's changed up-country. There's a new office. An Inquisition, they're calling it. Witch-hunters with ledgers and seals and a mandate straight from the throne. They're not burning hedge-witches, girl. Hedge-witches are noise. They want *listeners*. People who can actually hear."

"Why?"

"That," Tam said, "is the question nobody's answering. But I'll tell you what I know. Yara says the edict names a price. Silver for every gifted soul turned in. And she says—" he paused, and Tam did not pause often; pausing was a luxury for honest men— "she says some of her own crew have started looking at each other sideways."

Noor looked down at the half-carved bone. The dead man inside it murmured his warning about the riders, patient as rain.

"Sera's still waiting on this," she said.

"Sera can wait until spring."

"She's already waited since winter."

"Noor." He leaned forward, and his voice dropped into the register he used when he was being gentle, which was worse than when he was being harsh. "You finish that carving, you hand it to a grieving woman in an open market, and someone marks her. Someone follows her home. Someone asks who made the bone. You understand? It's not your neck anymore. It's hers."

He was right. She knew he was right, and knowing it felt like swallowing a stone.

"I'll finish it anyway," she said quietly. "I'll leave it at her door. No market. No faces."

Tam sighed the sigh of a man who had argued with her for six years and won perhaps four times. "Leave the city, Noor. There are passes east. Yara knows routes even the crown doesn't."

"And go where?"

"Away."

"There is no away," she said. "Not for me. The dead are everywhere, Tam. I'd just be a listener in a quieter room."

He left without another word, which was its own kind of answer.

* * *

For three days, Thornfell held its breath.

Noor worked by candlelight and delivered nothing. She sat on her hands, literally, pressing them flat against her thighs while the bones on her shelves whispered and tapped and sometimes, in the small hours, wept. She had never realized how much of her life was conversation until it was taken from her. Six years of voices, and now silence, and the silence had teeth.

On the third night, the screaming started two streets over.

She knew the sound of a midnight raid the way sailors know weather. Boots, many boots, moving in rhythm. Then the splintering of a door that had been locked for forty years. Then Tam's voice—not screaming, Tam didn't scream—but raised, for the first time she could remember, in something close to anger.

She was at the window before she knew she'd moved.

Across the lane, torchlight spilled from the doorway of Tam's chandler shop, the one above his rooms, the one that smelled of tallow and pipe smoke and where he'd taught her, years ago, to hold a blade steady by carving soap. Four men in crown livery dragged him into the street. A fifth carried a box—Noor's stomach dropped, because she knew that box, knew the carvings Tam had bought from her over the years and kept like letters from home—and set it down in the mud and kicked it open for the torchlight to see.

A woman stepped forward. Tall, grey-cloaked, unhurried. Even at this distance Noor could see the ledger under her arm, bound in black leather, and the way she consulted it like a priest consulting scripture. She lifted one of the carvings from the box, turned it in the light, and Noor saw her lips move—reading it, or reading *through* it, the way a hunter reads tracks.

Then the woman looked up, slowly, and swept her gaze across the dark windows of the lane.

Noor dropped below the sill so fast she cracked her knee on the floorboards. She stayed there, pressed against cold wood, heart hammering, while outside the woman's voice rang out clear and carrying:

"This man trafficked with the dead. His punishment is purification. And to any others in this quarter who share his corruption—" a pause, deliberate, savoring— "we will find you. The ledger remembers."

Boots receded. A cart rattled away south, toward the citadel road. And then the lane was quiet again, that terrible Thornfell quiet, the sound of forty households each deciding, in the same instant, not to open their doors.

Noor knelt on the floor and shook.

Not from fear—or not only fear. From the arithmetic of it. *They took Tam because of the carvings. The carvings were mine. My hands put him in that cart.* Every bone she'd ever sold was a signature. Every widow, every runner, every grieving client was a thread leading back to her, and the woman with the ledger was pulling them one by one.

She should have stopped months ago. She should have seen it coming. The dead in the passes had been whispering warnings for weeks—*riders, riders, tell them about the riders*—and she'd filed it away as weather-talk, the usual grumbling of the long-dead about the living. She hadn't listened. She, of all people, hadn't *listened*.

And now Tam was in a cart heading for a place called cleansing, and she was kneeling in the dark, alive, hidden, guilty.

Guilt, she discovered, sounded exactly like the dead. A murmur behind the wall of the world, patient, repeating itself: *your fault, your fault, your fault.*

* * *

She packed by feel and moonlight. One knife. Two days of bread. The good coat with the hidden seams. And then she stood before her shelves, before the hundred small bones waiting on their rows of cloth, each one holding somebody's somebody, and she could not leave them.

So she did the last thing a sensible fugitive would do.

She opened the window, and she listened.

The voices rose around her like water finding a breach—the drowned husband in the pass, old Marta's hanged son, a child's voice she'd never traced, dozens more, all of them talking at once now, urgent, overlapping, and beneath them all, underneath everything, a single thread she'd been hearing for weeks without understanding:

*Riders in the passes. Riders closing the passes. The gates between are closing. Closing. Closing.*

Noor went very still.

The dead weren't warning her about witch-hunters. The dead were warning her about something else entirely—something older, vaster, that made the edict look like a spark thrown from a much larger fire. The passes east, the ones Tam said Yara knew, the ones even the crown didn't—

The crown was sealing them. All of them.

She blew out her candle, slung her pack, and climbed through the window into the sleeping warrens, toward the one person in Thornfell ruthless enough, and desperate enough, to get her out.

Toward Yara.
