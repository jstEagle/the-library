## 3. Grandmother's Knucklebone

The pass above Thornfell had no name on any map, which was the only reason it still existed.

Noor climbed it in the dark, one hand on the wet stone, the other closed around the knucklebone she'd pried from the floorboards of Tam's workshop before the smoke finished rising. It was her grandmother's—had been, forty years ago, when the old woman pressed it into a child's palm and said *keep this where your ears can't reach it*. Noor had never understood that instruction until this week. Now she carried it like an ember she couldn't put down and couldn't drop.

Behind her, Thornfell burned in the small, efficient way the crown burned things: not the warrens themselves, not yet—the crown wanted the warrens intact, its smuggling economy bleeding—but Tam's workshop, and the two houses flanking it, and the notice board where the Edict of Ash hung in three languages and none of them kind.

*All who hear the dead shall present themselves for cleansing.*

Cleansing. She'd carved the word into a scrap of pine just to see what it felt like under her knife, and the wood had told her nothing, because wood never does. Wood is honest that way. Bone remembers; wood only waits.

She climbed until the fires were a smudge below her and the wind was the only voice left, and then she made her mistake. She stopped moving.

That's all it takes, in the high passes at night. Stop moving, and the cold files down your thoughts until they're thin as wire, and thin wire snaps. She found herself thinking of Tam's hands—cracked, ink-stained, always warm from the stove he wasn't supposed to have—and of the sound the door had made when the riders kicked it in, and of how she'd run instead of staying, because staying meant dying and dying meant—

Meant what? she asked herself bitterly. You hear the dead every day. What are you afraid of?

The answer came from behind her.

"You were afraid of me."

Noor spun, knife out, back to the cliff face. The path behind her was empty. Of course it was empty. It was also not empty, the way a room is not empty after someone has just left it, the air still holding their shape.

"Put the blade away, child. You'll cut yourself, and I'll feel obliged to comment."

The voice was dry as old paper and familiar as her own heartbeat, and Noor's knees nearly went. "Grandmother."

Sachiko Ishikawa stood at the bend of the path where the moonlight pooled, and the moonlight went through her. That was the detail that undid Noor—not the transparency, not the faint silver fraying at the edges of her like cloth worn too long, but the way the light passed through and kept going, unbothered, as if she were a rumor the world had half agreed to believe.

"You're dead," Noor said. "You've been dead since I was nine."

"Yes." Her grandmother looked down at her own hands, flexing them, testing them the way Noor tested bone. "It's less restful than advertised."

* * *

They walked. Or Noor walked, and Sachiko drifted alongside in a way that made Noor's eyes ache if she tried to track it directly. The pass narrowed, walls rising on either side, and the wind dropped away as if the mountain itself were leaning close to listen.

"Why now?" Noor kept her voice low anyway. Habit. "I've carried your bone for twenty years. I've heard a hundred strangers through it and never once heard you. Why come now?"

"Because you finally stopped," her grandmother said. "Stopped running, stopped carving, stopped filling every hour with other people's dead so you'd never have to sit with your own. Grief is a door, Noor. You've been nailing it shut for two decades. Tonight you opened it a crack, and I am nothing if not punctual."

"I'm not grieving."

"Noor."

"I'm *angry*." The word came out ragged. "Tam is in a crown cell or he's dead, and either way it's my fault, because they traced the carvings. My work. My marks. Every keepsake I ever sold was a signature, and I signed them all, because I was proud of them, because I wanted—" Her voice cracked. "I wanted to be good at something. Even if it had to be secret."

Sachiko was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke again, the dryness had gone out of her voice, and what remained was older and sadder.

"That pride was mine first. I gave it to your mother, who refused it, and your mother gave it to you by refusing it, which is how these things work. Listen to me now, because the wind up here carries well and we haven't much time." She stopped drifting and turned, and though her eyes were the same grey as memory, they held something Noor had never seen in them while she lived: urgency. "Do you know why you hear the dead?"

"Because I'm cursed."

"Because you're *employed*." A ghost of the old sharp humor. "The Ishikawa line are covenant-keepers. We have been, since before there was a crown, since before there was a Thornfell. There is a gate in the deep places of this country—a gate between the living realm and the Hollow, the land of the dead—and there is a covenant binding the two together, so that the dead may speak and the living may hear, and neither realm forgets the other exists. For a thousand years, keepers of our blood tended that covenant. Not many. One in a generation, sometimes. The gift skips and thins, like a river going underground. But it always surfaces."

Noor's hand had gone to the knucklebone without her deciding to. "The carvings. When I carve bone and listen—I'm not doing a trick. I'm doing the covenant. Small. Local. But the same thing."

"The same thing," Sachiko agreed. "A covenant-keeper working through a single bone is a lamp. Working at the gate, you would be a lighthouse. And here is what no one told you, because I died before I could tell your mother, and your mother died before she could tell you: the Hollow is starving."

The word landed strangely. Starving was for people. For winters. For smuggler children in bad years.

"What do you mean, starving?"

"I mean the dead are fed by remembrance. That is the whole architecture of it, child—the covenant doesn't just let voices through. It lets *substance* through. Memory, grief, love, even hatred—anything the living pour toward their dead becomes bread in the Hollow. And the crown has spent thirty years building a machine to stop it." Sachiko's form flickered, silver edges fraying harder, and she steadied herself with visible effort. "Every witch-hunt before this one was fear or greed or politics. This one is different. Serane Voss isn't cleansing the gifted. She's *harvesting* them. Everyone the crown takes—everyone burned or walled away in their 'quiet houses'—is severed from the living side of the covenant. Each one cuts a channel. Each channel starves a region of the Hollow. They are farming silence, Noor, and feeding it to something."

"To what?"

"I don't know. The dead don't know. That's the worst of it—we can see the fields going grey but not whose hand holds the scythe." She reached out, and though her fingers couldn't touch, Noor felt a cold like high altitude settle over her hand where the ghost's hand hovered. "But I know this: the Hollow has perhaps a season left before the last channels close. And when the covenant dies entirely, it won't only be the dead who suffer. The realms are bound for a reason. Cut the thread, and both sides unravel."

* * *

They sheltered before dawn in a smuggler's cache Noor knew from her running days—a hollow beneath an overhang, dry, invisible from the path. Noor built no fire. Fire draws eyes. She sat with her knees drawn up and the knucklebone in her palm and tried to make the arithmetic work.

Tam. Yara's crew, if any of them were still free. The widow Maren and her daughters. Every gifted soul in Thornfell who'd ever slid a carving across her table and whispered *thank you* like a confession. All of them prey, and all of them prey *because the crown needed prey*, systematically, deliberately, to starve a land Noor hadn't known existed until tonight.

"They'll catch me eventually," she said. "If I stay hidden. Voss traces carvings like a hound traces blood. I've seen her ledger-work in the notices—they post descriptions of the pieces. My pieces. She knows my style better than my clients do."

"So don't hide," Sachiko said.

Noor laughed, short and ugly. "And do what? March into Thornfell and surrender for cleansing?"

"Go to the gate."

The words dropped into the hollow of the cache and rang there.

"It's real, then. The gate. It's reachable."

"It's reachable," her grandmother said, "by someone willing to pay for the road. Nothing about this will be cheap, Noor. The covenant is broken—not severed, broken, cracked at the keystone—and reforging it requires a keeper. Living blood, keeper's gift, standing at the stone. And the price—" she paused, and for the first time the ghost looked away, out at the greying sky, "—the price is written in the old tongue and I remember it and I will tell you, but not tonight. Tonight you should sleep, and eat, and hate me a little for what's coming. That's traditional."

"Grandmother." Noor turned the knucklebone over in her fingers. In the pre-dawn grey it looked ordinary, yellowed, chipped at one edge. "This bone. All those years you told me to keep it where my ears couldn't reach it. Why?"

Sachiko smiled, and it was the smile from Noor's ninth winter, the one right before she pressed the bone into a child's hand.

"Because it's mine, and I knew I'd need to find you through it someday. A keeper leaves a key where her heir will carry it." She began to fade with the strengthening light, edges dissolving into the grey morning like sugar in water, and her last words came from everywhere and nowhere, the way the dead speak when they stop pretending to be people. "South and down, past the drowned chapel. Three days' walk if the riders don't find you. And Noor—when you reach the gate, you'll understand why I never told you. Some inheritances are kinder as surprises."

Then she was gone, and the cache was just a cache, and Noor sat alone with a dead woman's knucklebone warming in her fist like a live coal.

She didn't sleep. She took out her knife, found a splinter of the pine she'd carried from Thornfell, and began—without deciding to, the way breathing happens—to carve. And for the first time in her life, the wood spoke back.

It said: *hurry.*
