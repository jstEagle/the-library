## 4. The Inquisitor's Ledger

The ledger had a smell Serane Voss trusted more than any incense: tallow, iron, and old paper. She opened it on the table between them and smoothed the page flat with two fingers, the way she would smooth a fevered brow.

"Name," she said.

"You know my name." Tam sat chained to the chair, one eye swollen shut from the ride down from Thornfell, and still he managed to look at her the way a man looks at weather he has decided not to fear. "You wrote it yourself when you took me."

"I write many names. I like to hear them spoken by their owners first." She dipped her pen. "Tam. No family name?"

"Smugglers don't get family names. We get what our mothers called us when we were angry."

"Then your mother was angry often."

He laughed—a real laugh, cracked and warm, and it cost Voss something to keep her pen steady. Not because the laughter moved her. Because it reminded her of Lira.

She wrote: *Subject cooperative. Subject jovial. Joviality in the gifted is a known symptom.* The ledger did not care about his warmth. The ledger cared about patterns, and patterns were how you saved people before they burned.

"Tell me about the carvings," she said.

"What carvings?"

"Bone tokens. Small ones—knuckle-sized, mostly. Sold through your network to grieving families in Thornfell and the passes. Carved so finely that folk say they can *hear* things when they hold them." She looked up. "I have eleven of them in an evidence box. I have held every one. I heard nothing. Which tells me the gift is not in the bone at all. It's in the hands that made them."

Tam's good eye narrowed, just slightly. There it was—the flicker. She had learned to read guilt the way other clerics read scripture.

"Carvings are just carvings," he said. "Folk like pretty things. Grief makes them hear what they want."

"Grief does," Voss agreed. "That's precisely the danger." She turned the ledger toward him, though she knew he couldn't read the cramped script, and tapped a line. "A widow in Lowmark held one of these tokens and spoke to her dead husband for six hours. Do you know what she said at the end? She said she didn't want to live anymore. Said the living world had gone grey and thin, that her husband's voice was the only true thing left. She stopped eating. Her neighbors found her at the bottom of the cistern with the token clenched in her fist."

"That's grief," Tam said quietly. "Grief does that with or without bone."

"With or without," Voss said. "But the bone *invites* it. It props the door open. And what comes through a propped-open door is rarely the person you buried." She closed the ledger gently. "I am not cruel, Tam. I want you to understand that before anything else happens here. I have watched what the dead do to the living who listen too long. I grew up watching it. Everything I do—the edict, the cleansing, all of it—is mercy performed early enough to matter."

"Is that what you told your sister?"

The room went very still. Voss did not move. She had spent twenty years making sure that name could not move her, and yet some part of her registered, with cold professional interest, that Tam had done his own reading of patterns—that somewhere between arrest and this chair, the smuggler had learned enough about Inquisitor Serane Voss to find the one seam in her armor.

"You've been talking to my soldiers," she said.

"Soldiers talk. They talk even more when a man shares his bread ration." He leaned forward as far as the chains allowed. "You had a sister who heard the dead. You turned her in yourself. Sixteen years old, they say. Is that the mercy too?"

Voss stood, unhurried, and crossed to the window. Below, the garrison yard of Thornfell's little fort lay in grey morning light, riders saddling, torches being doused. She let the silence stretch until it became hers rather than his.

"She heard the dead," Voss said at last, to the glass. "From the age of nine. Our mother thought it was a blessing. Our priest thought it was a trial. I thought—I was twelve, and I thought it was wonderful. My sister knew what Grandfather said before he died. Knew where he'd hidden the ring Mother lost. She told us things no child should carry, voices of people fifty years drowned, and she smiled while she did it, because they praised her for it, and praise is a kind of leash." She turned back. "By fifteen she wasn't sleeping. By sixteen she was speaking to someone named Ellery who had never existed in any parish record, and Ellery wanted things. Small things at first. A candle. A lock of hair. Then larger. My sister walked into the millpond on Ellery's instruction, and she would have gone under if I hadn't been there. I pulled her out. She fought me. She fought me *for him*, Tam. For a voice."

"So you gave her to the pyre instead of the pond."

"I gave her to the fire," Voss said, "because fire cleanses and water only hides. And because the alternative was watching her drown slowly for another ten years, hollowed out voice by voice, until there was nothing left to save." She returned to the table and sat, composing herself with the ease of long practice. "You think I burned her out of hatred. Every gifted soul thinks that, right up until the moment the voices turn. Hatred would be simpler. Hatred doesn't require you to love someone first."

"And if you're wrong?" Tam asked. "If the dead aren't wolves at the door? If they're just—people, finished with their lives, wanting to be heard once more?"

"Then I have damned myself," Voss said simply. "And I will answer for it. But I will not gamble other people's children on my doubt. Doubt is a luxury for those without a ledger full of drowned widows."

She reopened the book to a fresh page. "Now. The carver. Someone in your network has been moving these tokens for years, and the work is distinctive—a maker's mark, three lines crossing at the wrist of whatever figure is carved. Who taught them? Where do they work?"

"They're just trinkets—"

"The widow in Lowmark. The ferryman at Coldbridge who steered his boat into the weir with a token around his neck. The child in Harrow's Bend who wouldn't stop screaming until her mother burned the thing in the stove—and then screamed anyway, saying the voice followed her home." Voss laid each case on the table like cards. "Three deaths, one near-drowning, one child broken. That is what your trinkets have bought. So you will give me the carver, or you will give me the network, and either way I will follow the mark to its source. The only question is how much of Thornfell burns with it."

Something shifted in Tam's face—not fear. Calculation, and beneath it, a stubbornness she recognized from every interrogation that had ever failed her. He would not give up the girl. She could see that already. Fine. She hadn't expected him to. People protected their gifted ones the way farmers sheltered sick cattle—out of habit and affection, straight into the slaughterhouse.

"Take your time," she said, rising. "Think about which mercy you'd rather I perform."

* * *

In her quarters she spread the eleven confiscated tokens across the desk in a row, each in its evidence sleeve, and worked by lamplight the way she had worked since she was a novice: methodically, without hope, without despair.

The maker's mark was consistent. Three lines crossing at the wrist—she'd confirmed it on all eleven, under magnification, including the newest token, seized only four days ago from a courier in the lower warrens. Four days. The carver was still working. Still selling. Still propping doors open in a town full of grieving people while the crown's patience ran out and the hardliners in the capital whispered about burning whole districts to be sure.

That was the thing none of them understood, she thought, turning a small carved wren in her fingers. The soft approach—the ledgers, the interrogations, the careful cleansing—was the *kind* version. Every week the carver stayed free, the political ground shifted further toward men like Chancellor Dreven, who had proposed, in open council, sealing Thornfell's gates and letting the "contamination" starve. Voss had argued against it. Had won, barely. But her victory bought time, not safety, and time was a currency the carver was spending recklessly.

She set the wren down and picked up the oldest token—a knucklebone, worn smooth by a decade of handling, carved with a woman's face whose eyes had been left deliberately blank. Whoever made it had understood that eyes were dangerous. Under the magnifier, the three-line mark crossed the figure's wrist, fine as spiderwork.

Voss had traced supply chains for fifteen years. Smugglers, coin-lenders, relic-priests. This one was better than most—no single buyer, no fixed market day, tokens passed hand to hand like secrets. But chains had weak links, and weak links talked, and she now had the network's spine sitting in her cells sharing bread rations with her soldiers.

She opened the ledger again and wrote:

*Mark confirmed on 11 specimens. Carver active within 4 days. Source likely Thornfell warrens or adjacent passes. Recommend expanded sweep of low-town before month's end.*

Her pen hovered. Then she added, in smaller script, the line she would deny writing if anyone ever read it:

*Note: specimens show no residual corruption. No verified harm traceable to objects themselves. All documented harms correlate with pre-existing grief instability.*

She stared at the note for a long moment. Then she did not strike it through. A good inquisitor recorded everything, even the observations that undermined her. Especially those. Lira had taught her that, in a way—her sister had never lied about what the voices said, even when the truth was unbearable. Honesty with the record was the last discipline she had kept from their childhood, and she clung to it the way a drowning woman clings to a plank that may, in fact, be dragging her under.

There was a knock. Sergeant Ilray, cap in hand, snowmelt dripping off his cloak.

"Inquisitor. The prisoner's asking for you."

"At this hour?"

"He says—" Ilray hesitated. "He says if you bring your ledger, he'll give you the carver. Says he's decided which mercy he can live with. Only he wants to dictate the terms, and he'll only talk to you."

Voss felt the old alertness rise in her chest, hunter's blood, clean and certain. She took up the ledger, then paused at the shelf where she kept the evidence box, and after a moment lifted out the newest token—the wren, four days old, wings half-spread as if caught mid-flight.

She carried both into the corridor, lamplight swinging, and did not ask herself why.

Below, in the cells, Tam was waiting with the look of a man who had finally chosen whom to betray, and behind his swollen eye, behind the smuggler's grin, Voss saw something that made her slow her step: pity.

He pitied *her*. As if she were the one in the cage.

"Ledger's ready," she said, keeping her voice level. "Talk."

Tam leaned close, chains clinking, and smiled with bloodied teeth. "Her name's Noor Ishikawa," he said. "And before you go hunting her, inquisitor—you should know the dead have already been asking after *you*."
