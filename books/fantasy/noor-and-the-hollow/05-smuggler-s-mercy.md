## 5. Smuggler's Mercy

The Saltwife's Rest sat where the warrens gave up pretending to be houses and became instead a throat of tunnels leading down to the flooded docks. Noor had been there twice in her life, both times to deliver carvings wrapped in oilcloth and both times without looking anyone directly in the eye. You didn't look at Yara Venn. Looking was a kind of asking, and asking was a kind of debt, and debts were the only currency the smuggler queen respected.

Tonight she walked in with her hood back and her chin up, because she was done surviving by being small.

The tavern was built inside the ribs of a beached river-barge, its hull silvered with salt-rot, and the lamplight came through gaps in the planking in long yellow blades. Smoke hung thick enough to chew. Every conversation stopped when Noor crossed the threshold—not all at once, but in a ripple, like wind moving over barley, until the only sound was the drip of water somewhere below and the creak of the old ship settling into the mud.

Yara Venn sat at the far end on a chair that might once have been a throne, if thrones were made from a captain's seat pried out of a wrecked galley. She was smaller than her reputation—wiry, grey-streaked, with a face like driftwood and hands that never stopped moving, turning a copper coin over her knuckles the way other people tapped their fingers. Her eyes found Noor before Noor had taken three steps.

"The bone-carver," Yara said. Her voice carried without effort. "Come to spend coin you don't have."

"I've come to make an offer."

"Offers." The coin stopped. "Sit, then. Not there." A flick of two fingers dismissed the stool nearest her. "There. Where I can see your hands. I'm told they're worth more than most men's tongues."

Noor sat. The chair was cold. Everything down here was cold; the damp climbed up through the floorboards and settled into the bones like it was paying rent.

"You know what's happening above," Noor said. "The edict. Riders in every pass. They took Tam Vessari three nights ago—you'll have heard that too, since half your crew drank with him."

"I heard." Yara's face did nothing at all. "I also heard he kept his mouth shut under questioning, which is more than most do. It bought him nothing. Voss doesn't trade in silence."

"They're not just hunting us." Noor leaned forward. "They're starving something. Something old. And whatever they're feeding with it—the hunt itself, the witch-fires, the whole rotten machine—it runs on what the dead used to give freely. I have to reach the Hollow-gate. The one past Greywater, in the teeth of the old pass."

The coin resumed its slow orbit across Yara's knuckles.

"And why," Yara said, "would I spend good people and better boats getting one girl to a gate that the crown has been fortifying for a month? What's my return?"

"Coin I can pay in one way only."

She set her right hand on the table, palm down, and drew the knife from her belt.

* * *

The knife was Tam's. He'd given it to her when she was fourteen, saying a carver should own steel older than her fear. Now she used it to open her own hand.

She'd thought about this for two days, walking the long way around Thornfell, listening to her grandmother's ghost argue with her about it the entire time.

*"You cannot carve what is still part of you,"* Grandmother Sae had said, pacing beside her along the aqueduct wall in the grey hour before dawn, visible only as a shimmer like heat off summer stone. *"The gift lives in the marrow, child. Cut into the living bone and you cut into the covenant itself."*

"That's rather the point," Noor had said.

*"It is madness."*

"It's also the only thing I have that Yara can't buy anywhere else. Coin she has. Favors she has. But no one else alive can put a voice in her ear. And grief"—she'd looked at her grandmother then, properly looked—"grief is the one currency everyone respects."

Sae had gone quiet at that, the way the dead go quiet when a living person says something too true.

Now, in the lamplight of the Saltwife's Rest, with forty smugglers watching and not one of them breathing, Noor pressed the point of the knife against the back of her own hand, over the thin ridge of metacarpal below the middle finger, and pushed.

The pain was white and total. She made herself breathe through it the way Tam had taught her to breathe through long carving sessions—four counts in, four counts out—and she did not scream, because screaming would have cost more than blood. The blade scraped. Bone has a sound when it's cut alive, a high thin singing that only she could hear, and underneath the singing she heard something else: the chorus. All the voices she carried, hers and inherited, crying out at once—not in pain, exactly, but in recognition. As though the bone knew what it was being asked to become.

Blood pooled black in the lamplight. She carved anyway.

It took eleven minutes. She counted them by breaths. When it was done she held up a sliver of her own metacarpal, white as river pearl, already cooling, already beginning the slow work of becoming separate from her. It hurt worse than the cutting, this separation—a tugging behind her eyes, as though a thread had been drawn out of her chest and left a hole shaped like itself.

Then she began to carve.

Small work, fine work, the kind she did best: lines no wider than a hair, spirals within spirals, the old Ishikawa marks that predated any crown or kingdom. She carved the shape of a boy—she'd asked Yara nothing, but the whole tavern knew the story, how Yara's son Denny had drowned in the flooded workings two winters back, how the queen of the smugglers had stood knee-deep in black water for a day and a night and come out with empty hands and a face that never mentioned it again.

She carved his laugh first. Then his voice. Then the last thing he ever said, which she could not know yet—but the bone knew, the way bone always knows, and her knife found the shape of it without her understanding what she was making.

When she finished, the sliver lay warm in her bleeding palm, and the tavern was so quiet she could hear the river muttering under the dock pilings.

Yara Venn had not moved. The coin was still frozen between her second and third knuckle.

"What is it," Yara said. Not a question. A demand wearing a question's clothes.

"A key," Noor said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "One use. Hold it, speak to it, and for the space of ten breaths you'll hear your son answer. Once. After that it's only bone."

Silence. Long enough that the lamp flames leaned and recovered twice.

"You're lying," Yara said finally, softly. "Or you're mad. Or—" and here her voice did something Noor hadn't expected, something that cracked down the middle like river ice in spring, "—or you're telling the truth, and I will have to live with what he says to me."

"Yes," Noor said simply. "That's the price. Mine and yours both."

Yara stood. She crossed the distance between them in four steps and took the sliver from Noor's palm with a gentleness that no one in Thornfell would have believed possible, cradling it the way you hold a sleeping child you're afraid of waking.

"When?" she said.

"Now, if you want. Here, if you must. Somewhere private, if you're wise."

Yara looked at the sliver. Looked at Noor's ruined, bandaged hand. Looked at the forty faces of her crew, who had seen their queen hold a piece of a stranger's skeleton like it was made of glass.

"Private," she said hoarsely. "And then we sail on the morning tide."

* * *

They went down to the flooded chapel beneath the docks—a sunken nave from the old religion, its bell tower drowned, its bell still hanging below the waterline where divers sometimes swore they heard it toll. Yara lit one candle. One. The light barely reached the walls.

She held the sliver in both hands and stared at it for a long time.

"He was nineteen," Yara said, to the candle or to Noor or to no one. "He wanted out of the life. Said he'd take a boat north, honest cargo, honest coin. I told him the honest world eats people like us. So he stayed. And two weeks later the workings flooded, and I—" Her jaw worked. "I pulled bodies out of that water for six hours. Mine was the last face he saw, and I was screaming at him. Telling him he should have gone north. That he'd killed himself by listening to me."

Noor said nothing. There was nothing to say that the bone wouldn't say better.

"Ten breaths," Yara said. "That's all?"

"Ten. Make them count."

Yara lifted the sliver to her lips like a woman taking communion, and spoke into it.

"Denny."

For a moment—two heartbeats, three—nothing. The candle flame bent. The drowned bell far below them gave a single note, soft as a sigh, though no water moved.

Then the sliver warmed, and a young man's voice came out of it, clear and amused and unbearably alive:

*"Mum. Mum, stop crying, you'll flood the chapel."*

Yara made a sound Noor had never heard a human being make—a sound with no name, joy and grief crushed into the same instant.

*"You weren't wrong,"* the voice said, and now it was softer. *"About the north. About all of it. I stayed because I chose you, Mum. Not because you told me to. Nobody chooses wrong by loving somebody."* A pause, and then, lighter, almost laughing: *"And tell the bone-girl her hand's going to ache in the rain forever, and it's worth it. Tell her the gate opens inward. Everyone tries to push."*

The candle guttered. Ten breaths, spent.

Yara stood in the dark holding a piece of ordinary bone, and Noor watched the smuggler queen of Thornfell come apart and reassemble herself in the space of a single exhalation—grief folded away, purpose locked in, the face rebuilt plank by plank like a ship after a storm.

"The gate opens inward," Yara repeated. Her voice was steady again. Almost. "Everyone pushes. My son just handed you the one thing no scout, no spy, no crown map has."

She turned to Noor, and in the candlelight her eyes were wet and terrible and absolutely certain.

"Morning tide. Full crew. We go through Greywater fast and quiet, and if Voss's riders want to argue about it, they can argue with thirty crossbows." She paused at the chapel door, silhouetted against the lamplight above. "Bone-girl. One more thing."

"Yes?"

"Denny said 'tell her.' Which means he could see you. Standing here. In this room." Yara's mouth curved, not quite a smile. "Whatever you are, girl, the dead aren't afraid of you. Remember that, when it gets dark."

* * *

They sailed at first light, in a flat-bottomed barge called the *Kindly Stranger*, running the grey river toward Greywater with sails down and poles working, hugging the mist along the banks. Noor sat amidships with her bandaged hand cradled against her chest, watching Thornfell shrink behind her, and felt the hole in her chest where the bone had been—a hollow with edges, aching in time with her heartbeat.

Grandmother Sae sat opposite her, translucent in the morning fog, and for once said nothing at all. Just watched her granddaughter with an expression Noor couldn't read, and let the river make its arguments.

It was near midday, with Greywater's cliffs rising ahead like the jaw of some enormous beast, that Noor felt it: a wrongness in the water, a stillness where there should have been current. The mist ahead thickened, coiled, took on shapes.

And out of that mist, standing on the surface of the river as though it were cobblestone, came riders. Six of them, in ash-grey cloaks, and at their head a woman with a ledger chained to her belt, whose eyes found Noor across the water as though the mist were glass.

Inquisitor Serane Voss smiled.

"Hold the poles," Yara breathed, reaching for her horn. "Company's come to call."
