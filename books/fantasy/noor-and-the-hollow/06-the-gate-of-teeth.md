## 6. The Gate of Teeth

The mountain had teeth, and it was chewing.

Noor felt it before she saw it—a grinding in the bone she carried, a vibration like a struck tuning fork that wouldn't die. The pass narrowed to a throat of black stone, and at the end of it stood the gate: two pillars of fused vertebrae, some ancient leviathan's spine planted upright in the rock, and between them a shimmer like heat-haze though the air was cold enough to crack skin.

"That's it?" said Brannick, Yara's second, a slab of a man with frost in his beard. "Looks like a door made of graveyard."

"It is," Noor said. "That's rather the point."

Behind them, down the switchbacks they'd climbed since midnight, torchlight bloomed. Not one torch. Dozens. A serpent of fire winding up the pass, and beneath the wind she could hear the riders' voices—harsh, disciplined, calling to one another in the clipped cadence of soldiers who expected to find what they hunted.

Yara spat over the cliff edge. "How far?"

"An hour," Noor said. "Maybe less. They have horses on the lower stretches."

"They'll leave the horses when the path turns to stairs." Yara's voice was flat as slate. She was not a woman who wasted breath on hope or despair; she spent both, hoarded them, and paid out only certainty. "Question is whether we're through it before they reach us. Tam—"

Tam was already moving. He'd been moving since the first torch appeared, silent as a man walking through his own house in the dark, which in a sense he was: these passes had been his for forty years, since before Noor was born, since before her grandmother's ghost had begun whispering covenant-secrets into her ear. He wedged himself against a boulder the size of a cottage and put his shoulder to it.

"Brannick. Sela. Here." His voice didn't rise. It never needed to. "We bring the scree down across the stair-cut. Buys you twenty minutes, maybe thirty."

"Buys *you* a grave," Yara said.

"Everyone's bought one of those, love. Question's just the delivery date." He grinned at her, gap-toothed, unbothered, and Noor's chest went tight because she knew that grin. She'd heard its echo in the bones of every smuggler Tam had ever buried. Men who smiled like that had already made their peace with the ledger.

"No," Noor said. "There has to be another way. We can hold the gate itself—the dead will—"

"The dead are starving, girl." Tam's eyes were kind and terrible. "You told me so yourself. Your grandmother told me so, back when your mother was still in braids. They've got nothing left to give you but their voices, and voices don't stop crossbow bolts." He turned back to the boulder. "Go. Carve your way through that door. Make it right. That's your work. This—" he set his boots, rolled his shoulders like an old man preparing to lift a grandchild, "—this is mine."

Noor's grandmother stood beside him.

She was more solid now than she'd been three days ago, when she'd found Noor in the hidden pass—more solid every hour, feeding on proximity to the gate, on the thin gruel of power that leaked through it. But even solid, she was grey as ash, and where her hand rested on Tam's shoulder, it passed through cloth and flesh alike, touching nothing, holding nothing.

"He won't hear me," she said quietly. "None of them ever did. Only our line hears, and only through the bone." Her voice caught. "Tell him, Noor. Tell him I'm here. Tell him the pass he named after my mother—I remember when he cut those stairs. Tell him he was a good man to an old woman nobody else would carry water for."

Noor's throat closed. She crossed the frozen ground and took Tam's arm.

"Tam."

He looked at her, patient, already half-turned back to the stone.

"My grandmother is standing next to you. Right now. She says—" Noor's voice cracked and she hated it, hated the weakness, pushed through anyway. "She says she remembers when you cut the Widow's Stair. She says you were good to her when no one else would carry her water. She's been watching you for forty years, Tam. She wanted you to know."

Something moved through the old man's face—not surprise, exactly. Recognition. Like a man hearing a song he'd forgotten he loved.

"Well," he said softly. "Well, then." He pressed his palm flat against the boulder, the way a man touches a doorframe he's leaving forever. "Tell her the water was the least of it."

Then he shoved, and Brannick and Sela shoved with him, and the mountain began to come apart.

* * *

They ran.

The last hundred yards to the gate were a scramble over ice-slick stone, Yara's crew moving with the desperate economy of people who had done this sort of thing before—Sela hauling the rope-ladder from its cache behind a fallen pillar, Brannick counting heads twice with his lips moving, the twins, Ferro and Ash, dragging the sledge of supplies Noor hadn't asked for and couldn't refuse. Bone-carving tools. Salt. A lantern burning oil that smelled of something older than oil.

Noor reached the gate and put her hands against the left pillar.

The vertebrae were warm. Warmer than the air by far—blood-warm, almost body-warm—and under her palms she felt the slow arrhythmic pulse she'd felt once before, in her grandmother's knucklebone, the night it had first spoken to her. The Hollow's heartbeat, leaking through a wound in the world.

And through the bone, faint as a voice through a wall, the dead.

Not words yet. Not yet. Just pressure. Presence. Thousands upon thousands of them pressed up against the far side of the shimmer like faces against frosted glass, and the hunger coming off them was so vast and so patient that Noor's knees nearly buckled. They weren't angry. That was the worst of it. They were *waiting*. They had been waiting for generations, polite as churchgoers, while the crown siphoned the covenant dry to fuel its witch-hunt, and now here at last was a keeper of the line with her hands on the door.

*Open*, they didn't say. They didn't need to say it.

"Noor." Yara's hand closed on her shoulder, hard. "Riders. Look."

Noor looked.

The torch-serpent had reached the base of the Widow's Stair, and even as she watched, the leading torches stopped. Spread out. Began to climb on foot, exactly as Yara had predicted—but faster, much faster, because these weren't conscripts. These were Voss's own. She could see the white sashes, the silver chain-of-eyes at the lead rider's throat catching torchlight. And ahead of them all, a figure in grey who climbed without slowing, without speaking, whose posture even at this distance radiated the terrible patience of a woman who had burned her own sister and slept well after.

Voss.

"She can't be stopped by rock," Noor heard herself say. "Can she? People like her—they don't stop."

"Rock stops everyone," Yara said. "It's just a question of how loud." She turned and bellowed down the pass, and her voice carried the way it always carried, like a blade laid flat across a table. "TAM! WHEN YOU'RE READY!"

Below, small in the torchlight, Tam raised one hand. Then both. Then he threw his whole weight sideways against the keystone boulder, and Brannick and Sela threw theirs, and the mountain answered.

It started as a sound Noor felt in her teeth—a deep subterranean groan, the sound of forty years of a man's knowledge of stone being cashed out all at once. Then the stair-cut folded. The whole switchback section, a hundred feet of engineered passage, slumped inward like a sandcastle kicked, and the scree above it let go, tons and tons of it, a white avalanche roaring down into the dark, taking torches and screaming men and the stair itself into the gorge below.

The boom rolled up the pass and broke against the gate like surf.

Silence after. Real silence, the kind mountains make.

Then, thin on the wind, a single voice. Not screaming. Singing—an old smuggling song, the one about the queen with the crooked crown, sung slightly off-key by a man with gravel in his throat, growing fainter and fainter as the dust rose, until it wasn't there anymore.

Noor screamed. She didn't decide to; her body did it for her, a raw tearing sound that echoed off the pillars, and Yara had to hold her upright, both hands clamped on her arms like shackles, because her legs had simply resigned.

"He's not gone," her grandmother said. Her voice was shaking. "Look. *Look*, child."

Noor looked, because looking away was impossible anyway.

Above the settling dust, translucent as breath on glass, a figure stood on the ruin of the stair. Old. Gap-toothed. Grinning. He tipped an imaginary hat to her, and beside him—solid now, solid as grief—her grandmother took his arm, and the two of them stood together on the broken stone like old friends meeting at a market, and then they walked. Not toward the gate. Toward *her*, through the air itself, unhurried, and as they came the air behind them filled with others—smugglers Tam had buried, miners, the widow's husband from three nights ago, dozens, scores, a procession assembling out of the dark of the pass like a congregation answering a bell.

And each of them, as they arrived, spoke. Not aloud. Into the bone. Into *her*.

*Tam's here.* / *He held the stair.* / *We heard the song.* / *Keeper. Keeper, we're so hungry.* / *Open the door, child. Open the door.*

Her chorus. Growing with every death the hunt left behind it, fed on sacrifice and fury, and Noor understood suddenly, with a clarity that felt like a blade going in, that this was how the covenant had always been reforged—that it ran on exactly this, on the living giving everything and the dead carrying it forward, and the crown had starved it not out of ignorance but out of greed, harvesting the deaths themselves.

"Fury's fuel," her grandmother said gently. "But it burns fast. Grief burns longer. Use both."

Noor wiped her face with the back of her wrist. Turned to the gate.

"Yara. Everyone behind the pillars. Now."

* * *

Carving the gate open was nothing like carving a keepsake.

A keepsake took hours and a steady hand. This took everything. She knelt between the pillars with her tools scattered around her and worked the living bone of the leviathan's spine the way a surgeon works a wound, cutting channels, opening veins of light, and the whole time the dead poured their voices through her hands until her skull felt like a bell being rung by a hundred clappers. Behind her, Yara's crew formed a ragged line facing down the pass, crossbows ready, watching the dust for movement. Above the scree-fall, torches were regrouping. Climbing. Finding another way, because Voss's people always found another way.

"There!" Sela shouted. "Grey coat, left flank—they're roping down the gorge wall—"

"Hold," Yara said. "Hold until the girl says."

The bone split along the channel Noor had carved, and light came out—not firelight, not moonlight, but the grey-gold of remembered mornings, the color of things seen once and kept forever. The shimmer between the pillars tore like wet paper.

Wind roared through. It smelled of rain on old stone, of bread, of woodsmoke, of every good smell attached to every memory the dead had carried with them, and it hit Noor's face like a hand.

Through the tear, Thornfell stood in grey echo—the warrens, the rooftops, the harbor, all of it rendered in ash and silver, crowded to bursting with the waiting dead.

And down the pass, clear across the ruined distance, a single figure in grey crested the scree and raised one hand, and every crossbow in Voss's line leveled as one.

"GO," Yara roared, shoving Noor toward the light. "ALL OF YOU, THROUGH—WE HOLD HERE—"

"I'm not leaving you—"

"You paid for passage, girl. Passage means arrival." Yara's grin was Tam's grin, gap-toothed and unbothered, and Noor understood that she'd learned it from him, decades ago, standing somewhere very like this. "Go make it right. We'll keep the door at your back."

Noor stepped through the Gate of Teeth, into the land of the dead—and the last thing she heard from the living world was the first bolt loosing, and Yara laughing.
