## 7. The Hollow

The first thing Noor noticed was the color—or the absence of it.

Thornfell stretched out below her in every direction she knew by heart: the crooked stairways of the warrens, the salt-stained docks, the leaning rooftops where she'd carved a hundred keepsakes for a hundred grieving families. All of it was here. None of it was right. The whole city had been dipped in ash and left to dry. Grey stone, grey water, grey sky that wasn't sky at all but something like the memory of one.

"Grandmother?" Her voice came out flat, swallowed. Sound didn't echo here. It just stopped, as if the air were too tired to carry it.

No answer. The old woman's ghost had walked her through the gate and then, at the threshold, had thinned like smoke in wind. *Follow the river,* she'd said. *The warden waits where the water forgets to move.* Then she'd frayed apart into the grey, and Noor had crossed over alone.

The river was there, running through the heart of dead Thornfell, except it didn't run. It hung—motionless, silver-grey, a ribbon of frozen light. Noor crouched beside it and saw no reflection. Where her face should have been there was only a smudge, a place where the world declined to look back.

She stood. She kept walking.

The streets were not empty. That was the worst of it. She'd expected emptiness—a dead land, vacant, silent. Instead the Hollow was crowded the way a famine village is crowded: everyone present, no one moving with any purpose, all of them waiting for something that wasn't coming.

They came to her slowly at first. A fisherman standing at a dock that led nowhere, his nets rotted to lace in his hands. An old man on a stoop, staring at a door that opened onto nothing. Children—children made her chest seize up—sitting in rows along a gutter, playing a game with pebbles that never left their fingers.

Then the fisherman turned his head, and his hollow eyes found her, and he *inhaled*.

Not breath. Warmth. She felt it leave her like heat off a stove—the living heat of her, the animal warmth of blood and pulse—and the dead man leaned toward it the way a starving man leans toward bread.

"No," Noor said, stepping back. "I'm not—I don't have anything to give."

He followed anyway. And then they all did.

By the time she reached the market square—grey awnings, grey fruit rotting eternally without ever finishing rotting—there were dozens of them trailing her. Dozens became hundreds. They didn't speak. They didn't beg. That would have been easier. They simply drifted after her, close enough to feel, and every step she took she could feel herself growing colder, dimmer, like a candle burning down in a room full of moths.

One of them touched her sleeve. A woman, young once, her face worn smooth as sea glass. Where her fingers brushed the wool, the fabric went grey too—not dirty, just *less*, as if color itself were a substance that could be eaten.

"Stop," Noor whispered, and her voice cracked. "Please. I'm trying to help you."

The woman tilted her head. And for a moment—one flickering moment—her eyes focused, and Noor heard a voice, thin as thread pulled through cloth:

*You smell like the other side of sleep.*

Then the focus was gone, and the hunger came back, and Noor understood with a lurching horror that this was what starvation did to souls. Not torment. Not fire. Just erosion. The dead here weren't suffering; they were *fading*, worn down to appetite and nothing else, because whatever fed them had been cut off.

She thought of her grandmother's words on the mountain: *The Hollow is starving, child. It has been starving for years.*

She thought of the edict nailed to the customs house door. *All who hear the dead must surrender for cleansing.*

And somewhere beneath the fear, the first hot coal of anger caught light.

* * *

The warden waited where the water forgot to move, exactly as promised—at the river's edge past the drowned quarter, where the frozen current pooled into a wide grey lake. The building there was the old tide-temple, the one from before the crown, before even Tam's smuggling kings: a low dome of black stone furred with salt. In the living world it was a ruin children dared each other to enter.

Here, its doors stood open, and light—real light, warm, gold, the only warm thing Noor had seen since crossing—poured out of them like honey.

The dead wouldn't approach it. Her crowd of followers halted at some invisible line twenty paces out, milling and pressing against each other like cattle at a fence, staring at the golden doorway with expressions Noor couldn't read and didn't want to.

She walked the last stretch alone.

Inside, the temple was intact. Candles burned in iron sconces though nothing fed them. And in the center of the floor, seated on a bench worn smooth by centuries of sitting, was the warden.

It looked like an old woman, mostly. Tall, broad-shouldered, skin the texture of birch bark, dressed in robes that might have been rich once and were now the grey of everything else. But her eyes were wrong—too deep, too still, like wells you could drop a stone into and never hear it land. And when she moved, the candle flames all bent toward her, gently, like flowers toward sun.

"You're alive," the warden said. Her voice was gravel rolled in velvet. "How long has it been since a living soul walked in here?"

"I'm told I'm the first in a long time." Noor kept her hands visible, her bone-knife sheathed. "My grandmother sent me. Ishikawa."

Something moved behind those well-deep eyes. "Ayame's girl." The warden rose, and she was taller than any woman had a right to be. "She reached the gate, then. Good. She was always stubborn enough to manage it." She studied Noor the way a jeweler studies a stone. "You have her ears. The true gift. How much do you know about what you carry, keeper?"

"Not enough. That's why I'm here." Noor's jaw tightened. "I know the Hollow is starving. I know my family kept a covenant between the living and the dead. I know the crown is hunting everyone like me. What I don't know is how those things connect. So tell me. All of it. I've earned that much by walking through your front door."

The warden laughed—a dry sound, like pages turning. "Earned. Yes. Sit, then, Ayame's girl. And I will tell you how your kingdom murders its own dead."

* * *

"The covenant is a channel," the warden said, pacing now, candles bowing as she passed. "You understand channels? Water finds a path, and the path makes the water stronger, and the stronger water cuts the path deeper. Your ancestors built such a channel between the realms. Grief flows down it—memory, love, the warmth the living give off when they remember their dead. That flow is food. It is *everything* here. Every soul in this land drinks from it."

"And the Ishikawa line tended the channel," Noor said slowly. "We heard the dead so the dead could be heard back. The carving—it isn't magic. It's maintenance."

"It is a valve. You open it, grief flows both ways, both lands are fed. For nine hundred years, that was the bargain." The warden stopped pacing. "Then your crown learned something uncomfortable. They learned that the flow can be dammed."

Cold walked up Noor's spine. "Dammed."

"Your king's grandfather built the first siphon. A clever thing—bone and law and blood, woven together, hidden under the palace. It doesn't stop the flow. It *redirects* it. All that warmth, all that grief pouring across the boundary—and instead of feeding the dead, it pools in one place, where the living can draw from it." She spread her bark-textured hands. "Power. Long life. The strength behind the throne that no army can see. Your kings have been drinking the Hollow for three generations."

Noor sat very still. Around her, the candle flames trembled.

"But a siphon needs tending," the warden went on, softer now, which was somehow worse. "The channel narrows if unused. The valve rusts shut. And so the crown needed to make sure that no one outside the palace ever opened the way again. Every hearth-tender. Every bone-carver. Every gifted soul who might, in kindness, let a widow hear her husband's voice one last time." Her eyes fixed on Noor. "Every Ishikawa."

"The hunt," Noor whispered. "The Witch-Hunt. It's not fear. It's not piety."

"It is harvest maintenance. They call it cleansing. What they mean is: they are cutting every last root that feeds this land, so that the only channel left runs through their siphon, and all the power of death belongs to the crown alone." The warden's voice dropped to almost nothing. "Look outside, keeper. Look at what your king calls mercy."

Noor looked.

Through the temple's high window, the grey city lay under its grey sky, and she saw it clearly now for what it was: not a haunting. A siege. Hundreds of thousands of souls, fading by inches, while their descendants' grief was piped away to fill a king's veins. The dead hadn't abandoned the living. The living—*the ones in power*—had locked the dead out and thrown away the key, and called the lock holiness.

The coal of anger in her chest went from ember to furnace.

"They seized Tam," she said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "My mentor. He carved nothing, heard nothing—he just sheltered people who did. And Voss—" Inquisitor Serane Voss, with her ledger and her soft terrible certainty. "She burned her own sister for hearing voices. She thinks she's saving people."

"She thinks she is draining a wound," the warden said. "That is what makes her useful. Fanatics make the best plumbers." She returned to her bench and sat, folding her long hands. "There is a way to end it. You already suspect what it costs. The covenant-stone lies beneath the palace, at the siphon's heart, and it can be reforged—but reforging demands payment from the one who holds the key. From you."

"My lifespan. My grandmother told me. Years of my life, poured into the stone."

"That is the price the stone asks." The warden's eyes were unreadable wells. "But hear me, Ayame's girl, because your grandmother never learned this, and it may matter more: the stone does not care *which* bond you pay with. Lifespan is one coin. There are others. Whatever binds you to the other side—whatever lets you hear—that too is currency. The stone will take what you offer most truly."

Noor's hand went, without thinking, to the little pouch of carving bones at her belt. Every voice she loved lived in that connection. Her grandmother. Tam, if the worst happened. Every ghost who had ever steadied her through the dark.

A trade: some years of her life, or the gift itself. The stone would decide by what she offered most honestly—and what she offered honestly depended on what she believed she could bear to lose.

"I need to see the stone," she said.

"You need to reach it," the warden corrected. "Under the palace. Through the siphon's roots, which are guarded by men who believe they serve heaven." She paused, and the candlelight guttered, and for one instant Noor saw the warden's true shape underneath the shape of an old woman—something vast and patient and terribly sad. "And keeper—one more thing. Before you decide what to pay, you should know that the crown's hounds have found the gate you came through. A rider crossed behind you. She carries a ledger full of names, and yours is written largest."

Noor's stomach dropped. "Voss. She came *through*?"

"She is in the Hollow now," the warden said. "Walking your grey streets. And the starving dead have begun to follow her."
