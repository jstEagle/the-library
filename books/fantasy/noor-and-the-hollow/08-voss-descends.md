## 8. Voss Descends

The gate had teeth, and Serane Voss walked into its mouth.

She had watched the smugglers' fire from the ridge above the pass—watched the girl Ishikawa and her ragged escort fight through a doorway that should not exist, a seam in the rock that wept grey light like an open wound. She had watched Tam the carver die holding the narrow ground against her own riders, an old man with a carving knife against six soldiers, and she had recorded his death in her ledger with a steady hand: *Obstructor. Unrepentant. Deceased.*

She had felt nothing. That was what she told herself, anyway, as she dismounted and ordered her sergeant to hold the pass. That was the discipline her whole life was built on: feel nothing, write everything, burn what must be burned.

Then she stepped through the gate alone, because no one else would, and the world went quiet in a way no living world is ever quiet.

* * *

The Hollow was Thornfell's echo, drained of color. The same crooked streets she had ridden through a hundred times, but rendered in ash and fog—the same leaning houses, the same rain-slick cobbles, except the rain fell upward here, slow silver beads rising from the stones to vanish into a sky the color of old pewter. No wind. No birdsong. No smell at all, which unsettled her more than anything; a world without smell was a world without rot, and Voss had built her faith on the smell of smoke.

Her lantern did nothing here. The light came from nowhere and everywhere, flat and sourceless, casting no shadows—which meant, she realized with a small cold shock, that *she* cast none either.

"Unclean," she said aloud, to hear a voice, any voice, even her own. "This place is unclean."

The word came back to her flattened, thinned, as if the Hollow itself were chewing it.

She had come for proof. That was the truth she carried down into the dead like a torch: the crown's doctrine held that the gifted were vessels of contamination, that the Hollow leaked its corruption into the living world through anyone who could hear across the boundary, and that cleansing—the pyre, the salt, the sanctified knife—was mercy. Her whole career rested on the axiom. Her sister had been the first proof she ever collected, twenty-two years ago, a girl of nineteen who heard voices in the well and smiled at empty rooms, and Voss had reported her, signed the writ, stood at the burning because running away would have meant doubt, and doubt was the one sin the Order punished worse than heresy.

*Mercy,* she had written in the margin of that first ledger, in her careful clerk's hand. *I gave her mercy.*

She walked the echoing streets toward the sound of weeping.

* * *

They came out of the doorways as she passed—not attacking, never attacking, just *looking*. Grey figures with grey faces, translucent as breath on glass, and every one of them thin. Not ghost-thin. Starving-thin. Cheeks hollowed past the bone, hands like bundles of kindling, eyes that fixed on her with a hunger so vast and so patient it made her skin crawl—and then, worse than the hunger, made her want to weep, because she recognized it. She had seen that exact look in the quarantine rows during the river fever, when she was a novice and the Order fed the sick last.

A child stood in the mouth of an alley, holding out its hands. Not begging. Just holding them out, palms up, the way children do when they want to be picked up.

Voss kept walking. She wrote as she went, because writing was her spine: *Subject observes ambient necrotic miasma. Subjects exhibit predation behavior toward living visitors.* The words looked wrong even as her pen made them. Predation. The child had not moved. None of them had touched her. They simply gathered, at a distance, the way the starving gather outside a lit window.

At the market square—the Hollow's copy of Thornfell's market square, stalls bleached to bone-white—a figure waited by the dry fountain, and this one was not starving.

He was tall, robed in grey that moved like water, and his face was the face of every face: familiar and unplaceable at once. The warden. She knew it the way you know a storm by its pressure.

"Inquisitor," he said. His voice was neither loud nor soft. It simply arrived inside her ears, already heard. "You've come far to count corpses."

"I've come to document contamination." She lifted her ledger like a shield. "The crown requires proof that the dead corrupt the living."

"The crown requires power," the warden said, "and has been taking it. Every soul your Order burns, the covenant carries here half-made, torn. Every rite your priests perform over their pyres siphons what should flow between the realms. Your hunt isn't cleansing anything, Serane Voss. It's a tap. And the Hollow is dying of thirst."

"Lies are the native language of this place."

"Is that why you're shaking?"

She looked down. Her hand was steady. Her hand was always steady. But the ledger trembled slightly, and she hated him for making her look.

"The dead are unclean," she said. "That is doctrine. Doctrine does not require my belief. Only my obedience."

"No," the warden said, almost gently, and gentleness from that face was obscene. "It requires your deafness. There's a difference. Go home, inquisitor. Or go deeper. One of those will change you."

He was gone between one blink and the next, and she stood alone in the bone-white square, hating him, hating the pity in his voice, hating most of all that some sealed room inside her had opened a crack and let in cold air.

She went deeper.

* * *

She told herself it was to find the girl. Ishikawa would be heading for the covenant-stone; the warden's talk of taps and siphons was exactly the sort of lie a fugitive witch would be fed. Find the girl, take her testimony under sanction, close the account. That was the mission. That was the ladder she repeated, rung by rung, as the streets narrowed and the grey deepened.

The house appeared without her choosing it. A cottage on a lane that existed in no city she'd served—except it did exist, once, in a valley two hundred leagues south, with a well behind it and a rowan tree, and she had not thought of it in years and now she could not stop walking toward it.

The door stood open.

Inside, the echo of a kitchen. A table scrubbed pale. Two bowls set out, though no one had eaten here in twenty-two years. And by the hearth, turning slowly as if warming hands at a fire that wasn't there, a young woman with dark hair and a well-water smile.

Wren.

Not the way Wren had looked at the end—gaunt, wild-eyed, screaming that the voices in the well were *people*, Mama, they're people, they're lonely—but the way she'd looked before, at fourteen, laughing, flour on her nose, teaching little Serane to knead bread badly.

"You came all this way," Wren said, and turned.

Voss's pen snapped in her fingers.

Every discipline she owned rose up at once: the ledger, the doctrine, the memory of the pyre, the careful sentence she had written at nineteen—*subject displayed terminal contamination, cleansing administered with compassion.* She reached for them the way a drowning woman reaches for rope, and found that in this place the rope was ash. It crumbled. Here there was no writing over anything. Here everything she had ever written was written on her face where the dead could read it.

"You signed it," Wren said. No accusation. Worse: no surprise. "I saw. From the smoke. I saw you standing there with your book, being brave about it."

"I gave you mercy." Her voice came out in pieces. "The voices were corrupting you. You were hearing—"

"People." Wren stepped closer, and up close she was both ages at once, the girl and the burned thing, flickering. "Lonely people. Like me. There was a drowned boy in the well who just wanted someone to know his name. I told you his name. You told the priest I was raving."

"He was dead. Dead things don't have names."

"They have nothing *else*," Wren said, and laughed, and the laugh broke something in Voss that twenty-two years of ledgers had mortared over. "Oh, Serane. You built such a big beautiful machine so you'd never have to hear me again. And now you've walked into the one country where the machine doesn't work."

"I am an inquisitor of the Crown of—" 

"I know what you are." Wren took her sister's rigid, trembling hands in her own weightless ones, and Voss felt it—not warmth, not cold, but *attention*, total and undevouring, the opposite of everything the doctrine claimed. "You came looking for proof the dead are unclean. So look at me. Look. Am I unclean?"

And Voss looked. At her sister's face, hungry-thin like all the others but lit from somewhere, patient, waiting—not to consume her, not to drag her down into corruption, just waiting to be *seen*, the way the child in the alley had waited with its open hands.

"I don't—" Voss began, and could not finish, because the honest ending was *I don't know*, and she had not said those three words since she was nine years old.

"There she is," Wren whispered. "There's my sister. Under all that ink."

"It isn't mercy," Voss said fiercely, to Wren, to herself, to the pewter sky. "Whatever this is. Whatever you think you see cracking. It is not mercy. Mercy is a doctrine term. I am conducting an investigation."

"Of course you are," Wren said, smiling the flour-on-her-nose smile. "Investigate, then. Follow the girl to the stone. Watch what she does. And then write *that* in your ledger, if your hand can hold the pen."

From somewhere deeper in the grey, faint as a heartbeat, came a sound Voss had never heard in this land of silence: singing. Many voices, thin and starving and rising—the dead, gathering around something warm. Around someone alive, walking toward the covenant-stone with a chorus at her heels.

Noor Ishikawa, going to reforge the covenant.

Voss closed her broken ledger. She left her sister standing in the kitchen of a house that had burned down decades ago, and she walked toward the singing—toward the girl she had hunted across two realms—telling herself, with every step, that this was still an investigation.

Behind her, unhurried, silent as smoke, Wren followed.
