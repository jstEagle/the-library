## 10. Silence, and the Living

Noor woke to birdsong.

That was the first wrongness. Thornfell's warrens did not sing with birdsong; they sang with dripping eaves and the mutter of the smuggler's tide and—since she was four years old—the whisper of the dead threaded through everything like rot through wet wood.

She lay on a cot in Yara's safehouse above the salt-sheds, and the world was quiet. Not the quiet of absence. The quiet of a room where no one was speaking. She had never known such a quiet in her life. It should have been a mercy. It felt like being buried alive in clean air.

She raised her hands. Both whole. The left one bore only a pale seam where she had carved away the bone of her palm to pay Yara's passage—a scar like a fish's mouth, already closing. Her fingers flexed. They were just fingers now. Tools for holding things. Nothing more.

"Ah," said her grandmother's voice—or rather, it didn't say anything at all, because her grandmother was gone, gone into the open Hollow with all the others, free to speak wherever they pleased, which meant no longer here, pressed against Noor's ear like a secret. That had been the price. She had known it when she set her hand on the covenant-stone. Every voice she loved, traded for every voice there ever was.

She had made the trade anyway. She would make it again. But knowing a thing and waking inside its consequences were different animals, and the second one bit harder.

Someone knocked. Then, without waiting, came in—Yara, carrying bread and a pot of honey and an expression Noor had never seen on the smuggler queen's face: uncertainty.

"You've been asleep two days," Yara said. "The city's gone mad. Good mad." She set the food down and stood there wringing her hands like a washerwoman, which was so unlike her that Noor sat up. "They're saying the dead speak in the streets. To anyone. Everyone. Old Marta at the shrine heard her drowned husband ask after the cat. The cat, Noor. And the crown's criers—" Yara laughed, a short ugly sound. "The criers read the Edict of Ash this morning and three hundred people laughed at them. Out loud. In the square."

"And Voss?"

Yara's face went strange. "You'd best come see."

* * *

Thornfell's great square had been built for executions. Noor knew its stones the way she knew her own teeth—the scaffold's shadow, the post where Tam had been held before the raid, the drain that ran red once a season. Now the square was full again, but nothing burned. Instead there was a tribunal.

Not the crown's kind. This one had been assembled in a single day out of sheer human stubbornness: guildmasters and harbor-wives, a retired judge who owed Yara money, two priests of the Quiet Faith who had spent their careers insisting the dead were at rest and now looked like men whose entire theology had been overturned by breakfast. And at the center, standing in the dock as though she had chosen it herself—which, Noor learned later, she had—stood Inquisitor Serane Voss.

She looked smaller out of her grey coat. Her hands were bound, but loosely; she could have slipped them if she wished, and everyone present understood she did not wish. She was speaking, and the square was silent enough that even mortal ears caught every word.

"—I signed forty-one orders of cleansing," Voss said. Her voice carried flat and clear, a blade laid on a table. "I believed I was saving souls from corruption. I believed the dead were a sickness. I was taught this by the crown, and I taught it to myself, because the alternative was to grieve my sister properly, and I could not bear it." A pause. "Then I went into the Hollow, and my sister spoke to me, and she forgave me, and forgiveness was worse than any judgment. Because it meant I had never needed to burn anyone at all."

The judge asked something. Voss answered. Noor did not stay for the details. She had heard enough testimony in her life through bone and ash to know how a confession ended: not with absolution, but with paperwork. Voss would hang or she wouldn't. The edict was dead either way, killed by ten thousand grandmothers and drowned husbands and dead sons suddenly, inconveniently, eloquently alive in the ears of the living.

What struck Noor, standing at the square's edge in the crowd, was that she couldn't hear any of it. The dead were everywhere—she could see them now, in a way, in the way people tilted their heads mid-conversation with empty air, in the way a widow by the fountain wept and smiled at once—but to Noor it was all silence. She was a deaf woman in a hall of music. She turned and walked home before the feeling could become something she'd have to name.

* * *

Her workshop smelled of dust and old tallow. Someone—Tam's ghost, someone told her later, though she hadn't heard him—had kept the fire banked. His tools hung where he'd left them. His mug still sat by the vice, a skin of mold across the tea.

She sat down at the bench and picked up a knife.

Carving had always been prayer and livelihood both. Her hands remembered the work even if the listening was gone: the way to find the grain, the way to let the shape come up out of the material instead of forcing it. She took a knucklebone from the jar—not hers, one of Tam's spares, bought years ago from a butcher who asked no questions—and began to carve.

She carved Tam. Not his face, exactly; faces were hard in bone. She carved what he'd been: a man leaning on a doorframe, laughing at some joke Noor had half-heard, one hand holding a lantern against the dark of the pass. He had died holding that pass so she could reach the gate. His ghost had joined her chorus for one bright terrible hour before the reforging scattered them all into freedom. She had not gotten to say goodbye. She suspected he'd have hated a goodbye anyway. He'd have wanted a joke and a drink and a job done well.

So she carved the laugh into the bone. It took most of the day. When she finished, she held it to her ear out of habit, an old reflex older than thought—

Nothing. Clean, total, mortal nothing. Just the sound of her own blood, and the fire ticking, and far off, the city learning to live with its ghosts.

She wrapped the carving in cloth and went out into the evening.

* * *

Tam's grave was on the hillside above the salt-sheds, under a rowan tree Yara had planted before his body was even cold. The smugglers had marked it with a lantern-hook, because Tam had loved lanterns, and because the living of Thornfell were already building new customs around their newly talkative dead. All down the hillside, little lights burned. People came and sat with their dead now, openly, the way you'd sit with an aging parent. An old man nearby was arguing cheerfully with the air about a card game from 40 years ago. A girl sat cross-legged before a flat stone, taking dictation from someone invisible, writing fast, tongue between her teeth.

Noor hung her carving on the lantern-hook and lit the lamp beneath it.

"I can't hear you," she said. "You know that. You're probably telling me right now that I'm an idiot for talking to a grave, and I can't hear a word of it." Her throat tightened. "Good. That's the point. That was the whole point."

She sat down in the rowan's roots and stayed while the light failed. Somewhere below, Thornfell was lighting its windows, and in the spaces between the windows, the dead were speaking—freely, to anyone, to everyone. The covenant she had shattered had been a wall with one small door, and she had knocked the whole wall down, and now the wind blew through and it was noisy and messy and unjust in entirely new ways, and it was better. It was so much better.

Her inheritance was gone. Her inheritance was everywhere.

When full dark came, she stood, brushed off her skirt, and paused with her hand on the lantern-hook one last time.

"Watch the city for me," she said to the silence. "You always were better at trouble than I was."

And then—because the silence answered nothing, and because she was mortal-eared now, and because she had a lifetime ahead of learning what that meant—she walked down the hill toward the lights, alone, and free, and listening with nothing but her own two ears to the enormous, ordinary noise of the living.
