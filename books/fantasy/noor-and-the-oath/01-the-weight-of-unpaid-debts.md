## 1. The Weight of Unpaid Debts

The Gilded Ledger kept its strangest accounts in the cellar, where the damp made ink bloom like frost and the candles burned with a smell of tallow and old secrets. Tamsin Ashgrove had spent eleven years among those ledgers, and in that time had learned that every impossible thing a person could want — a year of borrowed luck, a name returned from the dead, the last honest word of a hanged man — could be found written down somewhere, in someone's careful hand, with interest accruing.

They worked by lamplight tonight because the storm outside had swallowed the moon. The cellar of the guildhouse smelled of wet stone, beeswax, and the particular sourness of vellum that had been wept on. Tamsin sat with their sleeves rolled to the elbow, quill scratching, auditing the quarter's accounts: three jars of bottled thunder sold to a miller in Vessany; one memory of a first kiss, purchased twice by the same lonely widow; a debt of seven years' silence owed by a mother to her son, currently in arrears.

Gods, but people were hungry. That was the whole of the trade, if you were honest about it. The Gilded Ledger didn't sell miracles. It sold the space between what people had and what they couldn't stop wanting.

Tamsin did not believe in gods anymore, which was an awkward position for someone bound by oath to one. Eleven years ago they had sworn themselves — young, desperate, half-starved in a winter that ate two of their siblings — and the god had answered. A voice like a bell rung underwater. *You are mine, and I am yours, and your debts are my debts.* For six years the voice had been there, warm as a hearthstone at their back. Then, five years ago, mid-sentence, it stopped. Not faded. Stopped. Like a door closing in an empty house.

Tamsin had waited a long time for the door to open again. They had stopped waiting somewhere around the third year, and told no one, and kept the oath anyway, because an oath was the only thing they'd ever owned outright. Now they audited accounts and balanced columns and pretended the silence in their chest was just quiet.

The ledger before them was the guild's oldest — *Registry of Unresolvable Debts*, the spine read, in faded gilt. Most entries were nonsense or grief. But near the bottom of the current page, in ink so fresh it still shone, someone had written:

*One (1) map, provenance unknown. Value: incalculable. Holder: pending.*

Tamsin frowned. They had entered nothing into this book. No one had. The Registry hadn't been opened in nine years — the dust on its clasp had been unbroken when Tamsin unlocked it an hour ago.

"Pending," they said aloud, to the candle. "Pending what?"

The candle guttered. Not from any draft Tamsin could feel.

Then the floor above them exploded into noise.

Boots — many boots, fast, wrong. The kind of running that wasn't fleeing. Tamsin snuffed the lamp on instinct and stood in the dark, hand finding the short knife they kept balanced beneath the account shelf, listening. Above: the front door bursting, glass going everywhere, Master Corvane's voice pitched high with fear — "Gentlemen, please, the guild is neutral, the guild has always been—"

The voice cut off in a way Tamsin would spend years not describing.

Tamsin should have gone out the cellar's water-door. It was right there. Every instinct trained into them by eleven years of surviving said *run now, count later*. Instead they stood frozen as footsteps crossed the ceiling overhead, deliberate as a funeral procession, and something in their chest — the silent place, the closed door — went cold and attentive for the first time in five years.

Not a word. Just pressure. Like a hand pressed flat against the inside of a wall.

Then someone came down the cellar stairs.

He fell more than walked — a courier by his satchel and the mud of three kingdoms on him, a young man gone gray as ash, blood sheeting dark down his side and pooling on the flagstones with a sound like slow rain. He saw Tamsin in the dark and didn't scream, which Tamsin would remember. He simply crossed the room, grabbed their wrist with fingers already cold, and pressed something into their hands.

A map. Rolled tight, wrapped in oilcloth, soaked through with blood that was still warm.

"The stone—" he said. His breath whistled. "The stone still sings."

"I don't know what you're talking about," Tamsin said, which was true, and also the last lie they would tell for a long time.

"You're the holder." His eyes were fixed on theirs with terrible certainty, the way dying men see things that aren't there yet. "It wrote you in. I felt it write you in. Keep it from the hollow ones. Whatever they offer you — whatever they *show* you — it's a lie with the shape of mercy. Promise me. Swear it."

"I don't swear things anymore."

"You do." And here he smiled, blood on his teeth, almost gentle. "You've never stopped. That's why it chose you."

Above them, a door slammed. Voices — flat, unhurried, wrong, like speech played backward through a wall. The courier's grip tightened once, convulsively.

"They're here," he whispered. "Go. *Go.*"

Tamsin went — not out of courage, but because staying meant dying beside a stranger whose name they'd never learn, and survival was the one prayer Tamsin had ever kept faithfully. They took the water-door into the canal-dark, boots in hand, the map clutched against their chest like a second heartbeat, and heard behind them the courier begin, absurdly, to sing. A hymn, old and cracked, the kind sung before oaths were sworn. He sang until the singing stopped.

* * *

Tamsin crouched in the reeds along the canal bank and watched the Gilded Ledger burn.

Fire climbed the guildhouse like ivy, fast and greedy, gilding every window. The storm had passed and the night was clear and cold, and the flames threw light far across the water, so that Tamsin could see everything: the Hollow Order's riders in their colorless grey, faces masked in blank porcelain, standing in a ring around the blaze without hurry, without triumph. Watching it burn the way a clerk watches a column balance. One of them held something up to the firelight — a ledger, Tamsin realized. The Registry. They'd taken it from the ashes, or pulled it free before the burning, and now they turned its pages slowly, reading by the light of everything it recorded.

Looking for an entry. *Holder: pending.*

Tamsin's hands shook around the oilcloth bundle. Inside, glimpsed once by lightning-flash before they'd wrapped it away again: parchment covered in lines no cartographer would draw — coastlines that moved, roads labeled in a language that hurt to read, and at the center, circled again and again in different inks by different hands across what must have been centuries, a mark like a bell.

Beneath it, in a script older than the guild, older maybe than the kingdom: *where the stone still sings.*

Five years of silence, and now this. A dead stranger's blood soaking through to Tamsin's skin. A god pressing its palm against a closed door. An order of faceless clerks burning down the only home Tamsin had, hunting a thing Tamsin wanted no part of — gods, wars, relics, none of it. Tamsin had wanted a quiet life of columns and candlelight and small dishonesties, and they had been very good at wanting it.

But the map was warm against their chest. And the cold place inside them was not cold anymore; it was listening.

*I want no part of this,* Tamsin thought, fiercely, at the burning house, at the porcelain masks, at the silent heaven above them all. *Find someone else. I'm a bookkeeper. I keep accounts. I don't pay them.*

The pressure against the inside of their chest shifted — and for the first time in five years, faint as a bell rung underwater, half-drowned, almost gone, Tamsin heard:

*Your debts are my debts.*

And then silence again. But it was enough. Because Tamsin understood, suddenly and completely, standing in the reeds while their life burned, what the courier had known: the entry had been made. *Holder: pending* had become *holder: found*, and there was no auditing your way out of that. Survival had just become inheritance, and inheritance, in Tamsin's long experience, always came due.

Down the canal, one of the porcelain masks turned. Slowly. Precisely. Toward the reeds.

Tamsin ran.
