## 2. A Company of Borrowed Names

The map smelled of blood and myrrh.

Tamsin Hartwell had carried it for three days now, folded against their ribs where the courier's dying hands had pressed it, and still the smell clung — sweet rot and iron, like a chapel after a bad winter. They walked north along the Ashen Road with a hood up and a name that wasn't theirs, because names were debts too, and theirs had been burned along with the Gilded Ledger's front room.

Behind them, smoke still rose from Vellum Row. Ahead, the map promised drowned country: marshes, a river that ran backward at certain hours, and somewhere past all of it, a cathedral the water had swallowed whole.

*The stone still sings,* the courier had whispered. *Find it before they unmake the finding.*

Tamsin did not want to find anything. Tamsin wanted a bath, a ledger, and the small honest pleasure of arithmetic. But survival had become inheritance, and inheritance could not be refused — only spent.

The first debt on the road was owed to them.

Sefa Ilyan sat in the stocks outside the tollhouse at Marrow Ford, wrists locked in oak, selling insults to passersby with the fluency of a woman who had nothing left to lose but her voice. She'd been a guild debtor once — Tamsin remembered her file. Eleven crowns borrowed against a fishing boat, then a bad season, then a worse one, then flight. The Gilded Ledger's ledgers never forgot. Neither, apparently, did the tollhouse magistrate.

"You're the one who bought back Brannick's boat," Sefa said as Tamsin paused, studying her the way one studies an entry marked *doubtful.* "I heard. Paid his arrears out of your own purse so the guild wouldn't take his fingers."

"The guild takes fingers. I prefer signatures."

"Then sign me out of this." She rattled the stock's chain. "You need someone who knows the marshes north of here, and you need someone who hates the Hollow Order more than she fears drowning, and I am both for the price of eleven crowns and an apology."

"I don't apologize."

"Eleven crowns, then. And I'll pretend the apology was implied."

Tamsin paid the magistrate in clipped coin and watched Sefa work her wrists free before he'd even finished pocketing it. She was lean and sun-browned, with a deserter's walk — weight always on the balls of her feet, always ready to run — and when she fell into step beside them, she didn't ask about the map or the smoke on the horizon. She simply said, "Whoever's hunting you is two days behind and gaining," and Tamsin understood that trust, like credit, sometimes had to be extended before it was earned.

* * *

*From the private accounts of Sefa Ilyan, kept in a stolen notebook, in a hand that improved as the days went on:*

*Day one. The mercenary pays debts like other people breathe — constantly, without thinking, without joy. I have seen them buy a beggar's silence with a silver bit and buy a dead man's grave-marker with three. I asked why. They said, "Everything is a ledger. I'm just moving numbers to where they do less harm."*

*I think that's a lie. I think it's grief wearing arithmetic as a coat.*

*They talk to nobody when they think I'm asleep. Not praying, exactly. More like leaving messages for someone who never answers. I know what that is. I did it for two years after I ran from the Order's service. You keep talking to the god who left so you can pretend you're the one who walked away.*

*Day two. We bought passage on a flatboat with a man who charges by the lie. Good rates. He lies beautifully.*

* * *

The cartographer called himself Ovid Quill, which was not his name, and claimed to have surveyed the Drowned Coast, which he had not — but he lied with such architecture, such buttresses and vaulted ceilings of detail, that his falsehoods were more reliable than most men's truths. He met them at the ferry crossing with a leather case of charts and a smile like a signed contract.

"You want the Cathedral of Saint Verren," he said, unrolling a chart of the marsh coast across the flatboat's deck. "Submerged forty years ago when the river changed its mind. Everyone says there's nothing down there but eels and regret. Everyone is wrong, because everyone repeats the same surveyor's error — myself included, until last spring, when I discovered the tide gate beneath the bell tower runs dry for ninety minutes at each new moon."

"How would you know that?" Tamsin asked. "If you've never been."

Ovid's smile widened. "Because I invented being there, and invention requires rigor. A good lie must be load-bearing." He tapped the chart. "This one will hold your weight. Next new moon is in four days. The Hollow Order's hunters reach this crossing in two. I suggest we make the marshes uncomfortable for pursuit."

He was right, which was the maddening thing about liars of his caliber. They poled into the reed-country at dusk, where the water ran black and mirror-still and the midges sang hymns to no god in particular, and Sefa broke every trail behind them with cut reeds and false camps, and for two days the hunters' lanterns glimmered far behind like patient yellow eyes.

On the third night, Tamsin's god flickered.

It happened at the fire, while Sefa sharpened her knife and Ovid annotated his beautiful lies by candle stub. It began as warmth behind the sternum — a sensation Tamsin hadn't felt in six years, since the day every sworn voice in the world went silent mid-sentence, mid-prayer, mid-oath. Then came pressure, faint as a fingertip through cloth. Then — not words, never words, but something *shaped* like words:

*Turn. South bank. Now.*

Tamsin was on their feet before thought caught up, knife drawn, staring south across the black water. Nothing moved. But the reeds on the far bank stood wrong, parted in a line, the way a field parts around something walking through it carefully.

"What is it?" Sefa whispered, already up.

"They're not behind us anymore." Tamsin's voice came out strange, rusted with disuse. "My god just spoke."

"That's impossible," Ovid said. "All the gods are—"

"Silent. Yes. And yet." Tamsin flexed their hand, feeling the ghost-warmth fade like a coal covered in ash. Six years of nothing, and now a whisper — thin, strained, like a voice calling up from the bottom of a well. *Why now? Why me?* But gods answered questions the way rivers answered thirst: on their own schedule, if ever.

Across the water, the parted reeds closed. Somewhere in the dark, a hunter's whistle sounded — three notes, low, almost gentle. The Hollow Order's courtesy call. *Come out. Be unmade kindly.*

"We move tonight," Tamsin said. "In the dark. Through the fen, off any path Ovid hasn't invented."

"All my paths are inventions," Ovid protested, rolling his charts with wounded dignity. "That's what makes them safe. No one else knows they exist."

* * *

*From the private accounts of Sefa Ilyan:*

*We crossed the fen by starlight, thigh-deep in cold water, the mercenary leading with a compass and a certainty I couldn't explain. Twice they stopped us for no reason I could see, and twice, minutes later, we heard the Order's hounds on the bank we'd have taken.*

*Their god is flickering back to life. I saw it happen at the ford — they staggered like a woman struck, and when they straightened, their eyes had gone bright as gilded lettering. Whatever the stone is, whatever the Order stole, some thread of it runs through Tamsin Hartwell. They hate that. You can see them hating it, the way you hate a debt you didn't sign for.*

*But here is what I know, and I'll write it plainly because no one may ever read this: when the hounds were closest and Ovid's maps failed us and I thought we'd die in the reeds unnamed, Tamsin turned around in the black water and took my arm and Ovid's pack and pulled us both onto the shingle, and said, "No one gets written off. Not on my ledger."*

*I have served a knight's oath and a god's silence and a debtor's shame, and none of them ever made me feel the way that sentence did.*

*Whatever they choose when we reach the stone — restore the god, break the oath, burn it all — I think I'll follow them anyway. That's the dangerous kind of faith. The kind that doesn't come from heaven.*

* * *

They reached the coast on the eve of the new moon, salt wind stripping the marsh smells away, and there it was: the sea withdrawn to a brown ribbon, and beyond it, rising from wet sand and old bones of boats, the broken crown of a cathedral tower — black stone furred with weed, its bell chamber open to the sky like a mouth mid-sermon.

And on the tideline, planted upright in the sand in a perfect row, seven figures in grey, faces hidden, waiting. Not hunting. Waiting. As if they'd known the hour of arrival to the minute.

The warmth behind Tamsin's sternum flared — and this time it wasn't a whisper. It was a warning, sharp as a cracked bell:

*Not all of them are Order. One of yours has already sworn.*

Tamsin looked at Sefa. Looked at Ovid, whose beautiful face had gone carefully, professionally blank.

"One of ours," Tamsin repeated quietly, "has already sworn to *whom?*"

Neither of them answered. Down the tideline, the seven grey figures began, soundlessly, to walk.
