## 3. Where the Stone Still Sings

The sea had swallowed the cathedral whole and then, in some fit of ancient mercy, given it back.

It rose from the grey water like a ribcage picked clean — spires snapped at odd angles, windows blown out so that only their stone tracery remained, lacework of saints and seraphs with their faces worn to suggestion. The tide had retreated for the first time in living memory; Sefa said the fishermen spoke of it as an omen. Tamsin thought it was more likely the map's doing. Bloodstained things pulled at the world around them.

They came down the exposed seabed at low tide, boots sinking into sand that had never known sun. It was cold in a way that got into the joints and stayed there. Overhead, gulls wheeled and screamed, but not one of them would land on the cathedral's broken roof. Tamsin noticed that. Tamsin noticed everything now. Three years of silence from a god who had once spoken daily had made a listener out of them, the way hunger makes a thief.

"Tell me again why we're walking into a drowned church," muttered Brannic, the deserter knight, his armor wrapped in oilcloth to keep the salt off. He walked like a man carrying a house on his shoulders, which was how he did everything.

"Because the alternative is the Hollow Order catching us on open ground," Tamsin said.

"And because I want to see it," said Wren, the cartographer, whose lies were beautiful but whose maps were honest. She held the bloodstained original now, reverent as scripture. "Do you understand what this is? Every cartographer who ever lived guessed at this place. Half of them died guessing."

"That's supposed to comfort me?"

"No," Wren said cheerfully. "It's supposed to make you feel chosen."

The entrance was where the map promised: beneath the fallen bell tower, a stair spiraling down into dry dark. Dry — that was the miracle of it. Some old working still held, some covenant between the builders and whatever power they'd served, keeping the ocean out of the vault below with nothing more than a word that no one alive remembered speaking. Tamsin felt it as they descended, a pressure against the skin like walking through a curtain of warm water. Their god's silence pressed back against it, vast and sullen, and for one vertiginous moment Tamsin thought they heard — not a voice, not yet — but something turning over in its sleep.

The stair ended in a hall that took the breath away.

It was not grand. That was what undid them. It was *small* — a round chamber the size of a village well-house, its walls plain granite, unadorned. And in the center, floating a hand's breadth above a basin of black glass, hung the Oathstone.

It was not beautiful either. It looked like a river rock, grey-brown, fist-sized, utterly ordinary. But sound came off it. Not music — sound. A thousand thousand voices braided together into a hum that Tamsin felt in their teeth, in the scar tissue over their ribs, in the oath-scar on their palm that had been cold for three years and now burned like a brand fresh-cut. They knew those voices. Every sworn soul in the world had poured a thread of themselves into this stone, and every god had drawn their voice up through it like water through a root.

"It's *loud*," Sefa whispered. She had gone white. Her debtor's brand, the guild's inked chain across her collarbone, was glowing faintly gold. "Tamsin. Why can I hear my mother?"

"You don't," Tamsin said gently. "You hear what you swore to her. It's all still in there. Everything anyone ever promised." They stepped closer, and the hum resolved, unbearably, into fragments: *I will come home by harvest. I will protect her. I will be better. I swear, I swear, I swear.* "This is where the gods' voices lived. And someone cut them out of it."

"The Order," Brannic said flatly.

"Or someone the Order loved enough to damn themselves for."

That was when the lights came on along the walls — cold white flames in iron sconces, kindling without being lit — and the Hollow Order stepped out of the shadows they had been patient inside of for longer than any of them had been alive.

There were nine of them. Grey-robed, faceless behind masks of smooth bone, and at their center a woman with no mask at all: silver-haired, weathered, with eyes like the tide pool Tamsin had nearly drowned in as a child. She carried no weapon. She didn't need one. The air around her was empty of sound, a pocket of true silence, and Tamsin's skin crawled to look at it.

"Tamsin Ashgrove," she said, and her voice was kind, which was worse than cruelty. "Keeper of the Gilded Ledger. Sworn servant of Vael, the Quiet God, who has not answered you in three years and eleven months. You counted. Don't be ashamed. We counted too."

"How do you know—"

"Because we are the ones who silenced him." She said it plainly, without apology or pride, the way one reports a harvest or a death. "All of them. Every god that ever ate a promise."

* * *

They did not fight, at first. That was the trap of it. The woman — she gave her name as Mother Cassia, though Tamsin doubted it was the one she'd been born with — sat them down on the vault's stone benches as if they were guests, and told them the truth, and the truth was a blade slid slowly between the ribs.

"The gods did not abandon you," she said. "Understand that. Whatever bitterness you carry, carry it accurately. They were stolen from. As were you." She gestured at the humming stone. "Do you know what an oath is, mercenary? It is a hook in the soul. When you swore to Vael, he reached into your chest and set his line, and every morning after, he reeled. Your devotion fed him. Your guilt fed him. Your terror of breaking faith fed him. Ten thousand years, and every god fat on the promises of people who were never free to refuse."

Sefa was crying silently. Tamsin could not look at her.

"So we took the stone," Cassia went on. "We stole the gods' voices and we buried them here, and the cost—" her composure cracked, just once, just at the edge — "the cost was everything. Nine hundred of us went into the deep places where the stone was kept. Four walked out. The gods' voices do not leave quietly. And humanity woke the next morning free for the first time since before writing. Free to promise and break promise. Free to mean a vow because they chose it, not because a hook was in them." She leaned forward. "We are not your enemy, Keeper. We are the ones who freed you, and we have guarded your freedom for three years while you mourned a leash."

"Vael wasn't a leash," Tamsin said. Their voice came out rougher than intended.

"Weren't you listening? He *ate*. You were food that loved the mouth." Cassia spread her hands. "But I am not here to argue you into agreement. I am here to offer. Join us. You know ledgers; you know debts; you know the weight of a promise kept past all reason. Help us shatter the stone, and the silence becomes permanent — no god will ever reel in another soul. Or walk away, and live, and spend the rest of your life knowing what sleeps under the sea."

"And if I restore him?" Tamsin asked. "If I put the voices back?"

Cassia smiled, and it was the saddest thing in the room. "Then he will forgive you. That is the horror of them, child. They always forgive. Forgiveness is how the hook goes deeper."

And in that moment — Tamsin would deny it later, would hate themselves for it — the silence broke. Not with thunder. With warmth. A presence filled the vault like sunrise filling a window, and a voice three years absent poured directly into the hollow place behind Tamsin's sternum:

*Tamsin. My faithful one. I never left you. I called and called.*

It sounded exactly as it always had. That was the unbearable part. It sounded like home.

*Come back to me, and all of it is forgiven. The doubt. The wandering. All of it.*

Tamsin's hand shook. The oath-scar blazed.

* * *

It was Sefa who moved first, and Sefa who betrayed them.

Later — in the drowning dark, in the long aftermath — Tamsin would replay it a thousand times and find the seam every time: the way Sefa had gone quiet during Cassia's speech, the way her hand had drifted to her glowing brand, the way her mother's voice had been in the stone and Tamsin had told her *you can't hear her* and Sefa had believed otherwise.

She lunged for the Oathstone with a debtor's desperation, both hands out, whispering *Mama, Mama, I'll bring you back, I'll give them back their mouths, everyone comes home, everyone comes home* — and the Hollow Order moved to let her, because a mortal hand touching the stone would crack its warding, and that was all they needed.

"No!" Tamsin was moving before thought caught up, slamming into Sefa, driving her sideways into the black-glass basin. The stone spun wildly overhead. "Sefa — listen to me — that's not her! That's the *hook talking*—"

"She's IN THERE, Tamsin!"

"I KNOW." And the words tore out of Tamsin bloody: "I know. Mine's in there too. Do you think I don't hear him? Do you think I don't *ache*?" They gripped her face, forced her eyes up. "But we don't get them back by handing the world to the people who cut them out. Look at them. LOOK at them waiting for us to do their work."

The vault chose that moment to die.

Brannic had drawn steel against the Order — of course he had, he was a deserter, desertion was the one vow he'd ever kept — and the bone-masked nine had answered with silence made weapon, a wave of absolute nothing that sheared the sconces off the walls. The old working holding back the sea flickered. Somewhere far above, with a groan like a dying animal, the cathedral began to come down.

Water found the cracks. It came in black and fast and ancient, and the Oathstone screamed — a thousand thousand voices screaming at once, every broken promise in the world at the moment of its breaking — and the ceiling came down in slabs.

In the chaos, Cassia appeared at Tamsin's shoulder, calm amid the collapse, and pressed something into their hand: a hammerhead of grey metal, cold as the deep. "Shatter it," she said. "One blow. End the hooks forever. Be the last mercy of the old world, Keeper."

And Vael's voice, rising through the roar of the sea: *Don't. Tamsin. My faithful one. DON'T—*

Both of them offering. Both of them certain. Both of them wanting Tamsin's hand.

Wren was shouting something about the east stair. Brannic was bleeding. Sefa was sobbing in the shallows of the flooding floor. The stone spun above the black glass, singing, singing, singing —

And Tamsin closed their fist around the hammer, and turned — not toward the stone, not toward Cassia, but toward the dark water pouring through the breach, where something vast and drowned and older than the Order was beginning, very slowly, to wake.

"Oh," Tamsin breathed, as the first of the vault's ancient wards flared gold against the flood. "There's a third option."

The sea came in, and the last thing Cassia said was a name Tamsin hadn't heard since childhood — the name of the god before Vael, the one the ledgers said had died — and it was laughing.
