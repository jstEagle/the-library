## 4. What We Swear to Each Other

The vault was still settling when I woke.

Dust drifted down through the broken dome of the drowned cathedral like snow that had forgotten which way was up. Water lapped at the shattered flagstones, black and patient, carrying the smell of old incense and older stone. Somewhere behind me, Sefa was coughing — a wet, ragged sound that meant cracked ribs and meant alive — and the cartographer, Ilse, was swearing in three languages she'd invented herself, because real ones weren't sufficient for what had just happened to her maps.

I lay on my back and took inventory, the way you do after a battle: two fingers broken on the left hand, a gash along my scalp leaking warmth into my collar, ribs that complained when I breathed but let me breathe anyway. My oath-mark burned on the inside of my wrist, the brand of the Gilded Ledger's founding god, seared into me the day I signed my name into his service. It hadn't burned like this since the silence began.

Because he was there. Standing at the edge of the flooded nave, half-dissolved already, his outline flickering like a candle flame deciding whether to exist. The god who had gone quiet on me eleven years ago. The god whose voice I had crossed a country to find.

*Tamsin,* he said, and the word went through me like a bell through water. *You held the stone. You know now what it can do. Give it back to me, and every oath will be sacred again. Every promise kept. Every debt honored by divine law. No one will ever break faith with you again.*

The Oathstone sat in the shallows where it had fallen, no bigger than a fist, singing. That was the only word for it. Not sound exactly — more like the memory of sound, the hum a vow makes in the chest the moment before you speak it aloud. When I looked at it, I could feel every promise I'd ever made light up like lamps in windows.

And I could feel what the Order had felt, standing over it with their hammers and their certainty. Because the stone didn't just anchor oaths. It *enforced* them. It reached into the marrow of anyone who swore and held them there, forever, whether the promise was kind or cruel, freely given or wrung out of desperation. The gods' voices had been built on it. So had the Order's cages.

*No one will ever break faith with you again,* the god said again, gentler now, the way he'd spoken to me when I was young and hungry and grateful for any voice at all. *Isn't that what you've wanted?*

It was. That was the ugly truth of it. Eleven years of silence, of praying into a well that never echoed back, of keeping ledgers for a guild that traded in impossible things because impossible things were the only ones that still paid. I had wanted him back the way you want a wound to close.

But wounds close wrong if you force them.

"No," I said.

My voice came out ruined, smoke-scraped from the collapse, but it carried across the water. Sefa stopped coughing. Ilse stopped swearing. Even the dust seemed to listen.

*Think,* said the god, and there was something almost human in it now — fear, maybe, or its cousin. *Without me, an oath is only breath. Only words. People lie, Tamsin. People abandon each other. You have seen it.*

"I've seen it," I said, and pushed myself upright against a fallen pillar, and looked him in whatever passed for his face. "I've also seen people keep promises no god ever asked them to keep. Sefa carried me through the flood channel with three cracked ribs and no oath binding her to do it. Ilse redrew the map from memory while the Order was burning our trail, because she *chose* to. You were silent for eleven years and we survived you. That's the whole sermon."

*You would make every vow a mortal thing? Fragile? Breakable?*

"Yes." I knelt in the shallows and lifted the Oathstone. It was warm as a living hand. "Breakable is the point. A promise you can't break isn't a promise. It's a chain. And chains are the Order's whole religion — they just want to smash the chain-maker instead of freeing the chained. I'm done choosing between your cage and their hammer."

The god was quiet for a long moment. Around us, the cathedral groaned, shedding centuries.

*What will you do?*

"What should have been done from the start," I said. "Split it."

I didn't know how I knew. Maybe the stone told me; maybe eleven years of bookkeeping had taught me the shape of debts and how they divide. But when I pressed my broken fingers against its surface, it opened to me like a ledger opening to its owner, and I understood that power hoarded rots, and power shared compounds.

"Sefa," I said. "Ilse. Come here. Both of you."

They came — Sefa limping, Ilse clutching her ruined satchel like a child. Behind them, at the far end of the nave, the last of the Hollow Order's hunters stood swaying in the wreckage, hammer still in hand, watching. Veyra, the one who had offered me a place among them an hour ago, before the ceiling came down between us. Her face was ash-streaked and lost.

"You'll unmake everything," she said hoarsely. "Every sacred bond. Every certainty."

"Every certainty was already a lie," I told her. "Here's the truth instead: you'll have to trust each other. It's harder. Do it anyway."

Then I broke the stone.

Not with a hammer. With a word — the first honest one I'd spoken in years, the one I'd been carrying since a dying courier pressed a bloodstained map into my hands and made his death my inheritance. *"I swear this to you: no one holds another person's voice again."*

The Oathstone sang once, high and clear, a note like dawn coming through water. Then it came apart — not destroyed, but divided, pouring itself outward in threads of light that sought out every living soul in that ruined place. I felt mine settle under my sternum, small and warm, a coal instead of a bonfire. Enough to feel my own vows. Not enough to hold anyone else's throat.

Sefa gasped. Ilse laughed, a startled, wet-eyed laugh. And Veyra dropped her hammer, and it rang on the stones like a funeral bell, and she put both hands over her mouth because whatever she felt rising in her own chest, it wasn't nothing anymore. It was hers.

The god faded as the light spread. He didn't fight it. At the last, he was barely taller than the water's reflection of him, and his voice, when it came, was almost small enough to be a man's.

*I forgot,* he whispered, *that faith was never supposed to flow one way. Thank you for the reminder. Keep the books well, Tamsin Ashgrove.*

And then there was only the cathedral, and the water, and the four of us breathing.

* * *

We rebuilt the Gilded Ledger over the following spring, in the same narrow shopfront the Order had burned, because Ilse argued that premises with history appreciated in value and Sefa argued that she was tired of moving.

The new ledgers were different. Every contract we wrote now began with the same line, inked in my own hand: *This vow is yours alone to keep or break, and either choice has a price.* We traded in impossible things still — second chances, forgiven debts, apologies delivered across decades — but we stopped trading in bindings. No more oath-collars. No more enforced faith. If someone wanted a guarantee, we sold them a witness instead: a person who would remember what was promised and hold them to it with nothing but memory and stubbornness.

It turned out people paid surprisingly well for that.

Sefa runs collections now, which amuses her endlessly — the deserter knight, chasing down honest debts with a smile instead of a sword. Ilse is drawing a new map of the world, one that marks not treasure or vaults but every place a promise was kept so thoroughly it changed the landscape. There are more of those than you'd think.

My wrist still bears the old brand, though it's faded to a pale ghost of itself. Some nights I catch myself waiting for a voice to answer when I pray, out of habit. Nothing does. But underneath my sternum, my small coal of the stone glows steady, and when I swear something — to a client, to a friend, to myself — I feel it take hold the way roots take hold in soil. Shallow enough to pull up. Deep enough to grow.

Survival was never free. I knew that going in; the dying courier taught it to me with his last breath. But standing in the rebuilt shop on the morning we hung the new sign, listening to Sefa argue with a customer about the fair interest rate on a borrowed future, I understood the other half of the lesson at last:

Whatever survival costs, it costs less when you split the bill.

The bell above the door chimed. A stranger stepped in out of the rain, hood dripping, and set something wrapped in oilcloth on my counter — something that hummed faintly, the way a vow hums before it's spoken.

"You're the ones who freed the voices," she said. "I need to hire you. Someone stole mine — and I intend to get it back."
