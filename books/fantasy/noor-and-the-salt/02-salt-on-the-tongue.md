## 2. Salt on the Tongue

The traveler died on a Tuesday, which meant nothing in Salt Valley because no one kept calendars anymore, but it meant something to me because Tuesdays were when I ferried the wool merchants across the Brine and their coin bought my silence for another week.

He'd come up from the south road three days earlier, coughing blood into a rag he thought I hadn't noticed. A pilgrim of some kind—rope belt, dusty sandals, the particular hollow-eyed patience of a man who had walked a long way to die somewhere specific. He asked me to take him to the far bank, to the old shrine stones that nobody tended, and I did what I always did: I poled him across, took his coin, and told myself his business was his business.

That was the rule of the valley. Everyone's business was their own. The valley made sure of it. Twenty years I'd lived inside its quiet like a stone at the bottom of a well, and the quiet had never once let anything through—not prayer, not magic, not memory. That was why we came here, those few of us who survived. Not sanctuary exactly. Oblivion. There's a difference, and I'd had twenty years to learn it.

The traveler died at dusk with his hands folded over a wooden token, whispering something into the space between them. I heard only the shape of it—a word repeated, urgent, wet with blood—and then the shape went out of him and the valley closed over the sound like water over a dropped coin.

I buried him the next morning where he'd asked: near the shrine stones, facing east, so he could watch whatever sunrise he'd walked all this way for. I said no words over the grave. Words were the one thing I never gave away for free.

* * *

Three days later, the grave was white.

Not dusted with salt. Made of it. The mound of earth had hardened into a crust of pure white crystal, fine-grained and glittering, as if the ground itself had wept and the tears had set. It caught the morning light and threw it back in little blades, and from thirty feet away, poling my flat boat along the near shore, I saw it and felt my stomach drop through the bottom of the world.

Because I knew that white. Every swordspeaker knew that white.

Salt was our element—the one substance that drank magic and held it dead. We spoke our words into steel, yes, but we sealed them with salt. We salted our thresholds, our wounds, our dead. And when one of us died badly, wrongly, with truth still locked behind their teeth, the order's rite was to bury them in a shroud of brine-crystal so nothing could call them back or speak through them.

A grave crusted in salt meant one of two things. Either someone had performed the old rite—which no one alive in this valley knew—or the earth had done it on its own.

And the earth didn't know how.

I tied off the boat and stood over the grave for a long time, not touching it. My hands wanted to touch it. That was the problem with twenty years of silence: your hands forget they're not allowed to know things, and they reach anyway.

The crust was warm. I felt it before I touched it, a faint radiance like a hearthstone hours after the fire dies. When I finally pressed my palm flat against it, the warmth pulsed once—slow, deliberate, patient as a heartbeat—and beneath my feet, deep under the valley floor, something answered.

Not a sound. A pressure. Like the moment before thunder, stretched thin and held.

I pulled my hand back. The place where I'd touched was darker than the rest, damp, and when I rubbed my fingers together they came away slick and bitter.

Salt on my tongue. Salt in the wound of an old scar I'd almost stopped feeling.

"Who answered you?" I said to the grave. Quietly. In the flat trade-tongue, the safe tongue, the tongue that carried no power anywhere in the world.

The grave said nothing. But the warmth pulsed again, and this time I would have sworn—I would have sworn on the oath itself—that it pulsed in rhythm with my question. As if something below had heard. As if something below was pleased.

* * *

I told myself it was a trick of weather. Brine fogs came off the marshes sometimes; salt crusts formed on low ground after dry summers; dead men's graves did strange things in a land as old as this one. I told myself these things while I ferried the wool merchants, while I mended my nets, while I ate my supper alone in the ferry house with the door barred out of habit rather than need.

Then the boundary stones began to weep.

There are eleven of them, ringed around the valley at roughly a day's walk apart—gray standing pillars older than any village, older than any name anyone used. The valley folk called them the Grandmothers and left offerings of bread and buttons at their bases, the way people do with things too old to understand. Children dared each other to touch them. Nothing ever happened. Nothing ever happened at the Grandmothers, same as nothing ever happened anywhere in Salt Valley. That was the whole point of the place.

Old Sefa the dyer came to the ferry house at dawn, wringing her hands, saying the Grandmother by her fields was crying. I went expecting a cracked spring or a bird's nest fouling the stone. What I found was the stone sweating.

Brine ran down its face in slow beads, gathering in the carved hollows of its base, pooling there, glittering. The grass around it had gone white for a full arm's length in every direction. And the stone itself—when I laid my hand against it, careful, testing—it was warm. Hearthstone warm. Heartbeat warm.

The same pulse. The same slow, patient rhythm.

I checked four more of the Grandmothers that day, walking hard, telling Sefa I was going to look at my traps. All four wept. All four were warm. And around each one, the white ring of dead grass had grown since morning. I measured it with my stride. By evening, the ring around the southernmost stone had widened by three full steps.

Something was leaking. Something was seeping up through the bones of the valley like blood through a bandage, and the bandage was failing.

* * *

Here is what I should have done: gone to the elders, raised the alarm, organized. Here is what I actually did: waited until full dark, took my pole and my lantern and my father's knife, and went alone to the oldest Grandmother—the one on the western rise, the one the valley folk wouldn't even leave bread for because the path there was "unlucky."

Unlucky. Twenty years in this valley and I'd never once wondered why the paths to half the boundary stones were unluckier than the others. I'd never wondered why the Grandmothers stood in a perfect ring, evenly spaced, like points on a diagram. I'd never wondered why the valley's founders—if there had ever been founders—had built nothing, planted nothing, within a hundred paces of any of them.

You don't wonder about the walls of your prison when you don't know you're in one.

The western path climbed through gorse and old lava rock, and the higher I went, the stronger the smell grew—brine and something else underneath it, mineral and ancient, like the air inside a cave that has been closed for centuries. My lantern flame leaned toward the summit as if drawn. That should have frightened me more than it did. Instead I felt the old hunger waking in my chest, the thing I'd starved for twenty years, the thing with teeth: the swordspeaker's instinct for power, for source, for the current beneath the world.

It smelled the leak too. God help me, part of me was glad.

The stone stood at the top of the rise, taller than the others, black-veined, streaming. Its face was slick with running brine, and the moonlight turned the runoff silver, so the whole pillar seemed to bleed light instead of salt. I circled it once, twice, looking for the crack, the fracture, the wound. There wasn't one. The stone was whole. Whatever was weeping out of it was coming from deeper than damage—from underneath, from the foundations, from whatever the stone was planted in.

So I dug.

I dug at the base with my knife and then with my hands, hauling away twenty years of accumulated soil and gorse root, and the deeper I went, the warmer the ground became, until the earth itself was blood-warm and my fingers found not rock but worked stone—dressed blocks, fitted tight, laid in a curve. A foundation. No. A casing. The Grandmother wasn't standing on the hill.

She was plugged into it.

I cleared a space wide enough to lie down in and got my shoulder against the first block, and it shifted easier than it should have, grinding aside with a sound like a held breath released, and cold air breathed up out of the dark beneath it—cold and clean and utterly without salt, which in this valley was the strangest thing of all.

I lowered the lantern.

Steps. Narrow, steep, descending into the hill's heart, cut with a precision no valley mason possessed. And on either side of the topmost step, worn nearly smooth by age but still legible, still unmistakable, a sigil had been carved into the living rock:

A sword, vertical. A wave, horizontal. The blade driven down through the crest of the water, pinning it.

The mark of the Order of the Speaking Blade. My order. My masters' mark, the one burned into my forearm at fourteen, the one I kept hidden under a leather bracer every waking hour of every day for twenty years.

The valley folk called the Grandmothers grandmothers because they had no other name for them. But my order had another name for standing stones set in a ring, cased in dressed rock, sealed with our own sigil.

We called them wards.

* * *

I sat down on the edge of that hole in the world and I didn't move for a long time. The lantern guttered. The brine ran down the Grandmother's face above me, drip, drip, drip, keeping its slow time.

Think, Vesper. Think the way they taught you, before there was no one left to teach.

The valley was magic-dead. Everyone knew that; it was the reason we'd come. Prayers went unanswered here. Spells curdled and died. The old powers couldn't find you inside it. I had believed—for twenty years I had believed—that the valley's deadness was natural, a quirk of geology, a gift of blind luck. Sanctuary by accident.

But accidents don't wear sigils. Quirks of geology don't get founded in dressed stone by hands that cut stairs with swordspeaker precision. The deadness wasn't the valley's nature.

It was the valley's function.

Someone had built this. Someone had built a machine of silence the size of a country and set it humming, and the hum had swallowed every prayer and every miracle and every whisper of power for longer than the valley folk could remember. And now the machine was leaking. Now prayers were being answered at the edges, graves were crusting white, the Grandmothers were weeping brine, and the seal my own order had set—

My own order. The words turned over and showed me a different belly. Because if the valley was a ward, then it wasn't keeping magic out.

Wards keep things in just as well as they keep things out.

What had my masters sealed inside? What needed a prison the size of a valley, wrapped in silence, drowned in salt? What kind of thing do you bury so deep, so thoroughly, that you also bury the knowledge of the burial—erase the roads, unname the country beyond, scatter the survivors to the winds with an oath on their tongues?

An oath of silence. I'd taken it at fourteen, kneeling on cold stone, and I'd understood it as humility. Speak no truths of the art. Carry no tales of the order. Let the world believe you dead. I'd carried it like a penance, like grief, like the last gift my murdered masters had given me.

But oaths are locks, and locks have doors, and doors have reasons.

Drip. Drip. Drip.

Far below me, down the stairs I hadn't yet descended, the darkness breathed again—that same slow pressure I'd felt at the traveler's grave, the pre-thunder hum, except now it had a direction. It rose out of the hole and curled around my ankles like a tide coming in. Warm. Patient. Pleased.

And in the back of my skull, in the place where my scars ached when storms approached, a sensation I hadn't felt in twenty years and had hoped never to feel again: the feeling of being read. Of something turning its attention over me like a page, slowly, savoring.

It knew what I was. Whatever slept beneath my order's seal had felt a swordspeaker's hand on the stone, and it had woken a little, the way a prisoner wakes at the scrape of a key.

I backed away from the hole. I reset the block as best I could, packed soil over it with shaking hands, and stood, and looked west—past the weeping Grandmother, past the white ring of dead grass, toward the valley's edge and the dark line of hills beyond.

Somewhere out there, a dying pilgrim's whispered prayer had been answered. Somewhere out there, or somewhere down there, something had heard. And the first thing it had done with its borrowed voice was answer a stranger's last wish—kindly, gently, generously.

That was the part that scared me most. Not rage. Not malice. Kindness. Things that answer prayers are never kind by accident. They are kind the way fishermen are kind to bait.

I walked home in the dark without the lantern lit, and I did not sleep, and in the gray hour before dawn, when I finally lay down still clothed, a knock came at my door.

Maren the healer stood on my step, sixteen years old, gray-eyed, gray-faced, holding her hands out in front of her like a woman showing she carries no weapon. Her palms were crusted white with salt.

"I prayed for a fevered child last night," she said, and her voice shook. "Ferryman. My prayer was answered."

Behind her, far off across the sleeping valley, the Grandmothers wept in their ring, and the thing beneath them kept its slow, patient time.
