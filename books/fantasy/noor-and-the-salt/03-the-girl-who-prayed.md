## 3. The Girl Who Prayed

Maren had been a healer for six years and a liar for three of them.

The lying started small. A fever broke an hour after she whispered over it—she told the mother it was the willow bark. A cow's infected hoof drained clean the morning after she'd pressed her palm to it and asked, silently, please—she told the farmer it was the poultice. She got very good at the poultices. She learned which herbs did what so her lies would have somewhere to stand.

Because in Salt Valley, things didn't work that way. Everyone knew it the way they knew the salt wind came from the west and the ferryman never talked about his hands. The valley was quiet. Whatever moved the world out there—the answering power, the listening dark, whatever name you wanted to give it—did not reach past the boundary stones. Grandmother Sela used to say it plainly, spitting into the fire: *Prayers don't work here, girl. That's not a curse. That's a mercy.*

Maren had believed her right up until the night old Tam Vetch lay dying of the lung-rot and she knelt beside his cot with nothing left to give him—not willow, not poppy, not even a lie—and she prayed anyway. Not to anyone in particular. There wasn't anyone in particular to pray to in Salt Valley. She just put her forehead against his gray hand and said, *don't*, the way you'd say it to a river rising, to a storm coming off the flats. *Don't.*

And Tam Vetch opened his eyes at dawn and asked for broth.

That was eleven days ago. Since then, four more prayers had worked, each one worse than the last, because each one worked more completely, more *specifically*, as though something on the other side of the silence were leaning closer to hear her better. The last one had frightened her out of her skin: little Odile Brant, two years old, choking on a river pebble, blue as a mussel shell, and Maren had screamed *out* —one word, no thought behind it, just terror with a shape—and the pebble had come up out of the child's throat like a fish leaping, arcing through the air, and landed in the water bucket across the room.

Nobody else had been looking. That was the only reason Maren still had a practice.

She sat now on the low wall above the ferry landing, knees drawn up, watching the last light go copper on the brine flats, and turned the third thing over in her mind for the hundredth time. The first thing was the prayers working. The second thing was the boundary stones weeping—she'd seen it herself two days past, clear beads of brine crawling down the standing stone by the mill road like the rock was crying, and old Sela had touched the wet trail, tasted it, and gone white as the salt itself without saying a word.

The third thing was the hymn.

It had started the same night Tam Vetch lived. She'd woken at some black hour with a melody in her head, complete, patient, waiting—as if it had always been there and had only just decided to be noticed. Six notes, then a rest, then five more. She could hum it perfectly. She could not stop humming it. And threaded through the melody were words in no language she knew, syllables that fit her mouth strangely, like shoes made for someone else's feet—but when she mouthed them, silently, in the dark, they felt less like words than like *edges*. Like the rim of something sharp.

No one had taught it to her. She'd never heard it anywhere. It hadn't come from her—that was the part she kept circling. Things came from Maren Hale the way water came from stones: slowly, and only what soaked in from outside. This had arrived whole. Something had put it there, or opened a door in her and let it drift through.

There was exactly one person in Salt Valley who might know what it was, and he was poling his ferry toward the landing below her, silhouetted against the dying light.

* * *

Everyone called him Coldharbour, though nobody seemed to know if that was a surname or just what happened to a man after twenty years alone on the water. He was the valley's ferryman, its sometime carpenter, its reluctant undertaker's assistant, and its great unsolved question. He kept to himself the way a wound keeps its bandage. He was competent at everything and warm about nothing. Children dared each other to ask him questions; adults simply didn't.

Maren had patched his forearm once, three years back—a gash from a slipped adze, deep but clean. What she remembered wasn't the cut. It was the scars underneath, when she'd peeled back the sleeve to work. Rows of them. Deliberate ones. Thin parallel lines crossing older, thicker marks, all of them healed long ago and badly, the kind of scarring that comes from wounds made in patterns. She'd seen ritual scarring once, on a pilgrim passing through years ago, before the roads went quiet. Pilgrims used to cross the valley on the old brine road, back when there were pilgrims.

She'd asked him, gently, how he'd gotten them. He'd looked at her with eyes like frozen harbor water and said, "A life I don't have anymore," and pulled his sleeve down, and that was the end of the conversation and nearly the end of his willingness to be treated by her at all.

Now she watched him bring the ferry in—smooth, economical strokes, the pole moving like it was part of his arm—and waited until he'd tied off and shouldered his satchel before she slid off the wall and stood in his path.

He stopped. Of course he stopped; he was too controlled to do anything as honest as startle. Up close, in the last light, she could see new lines around his eyes that hadn't been there three years ago. Twenty years is twenty years, even for a man built like a mooring post.

"Healer," he said. Not unkindly. Not kindly either. "Someone hurt?"

"No." Her rehearsed opening collapsed on the spot. She'd planned to be casual. Casual died instantly under that flat gray gaze. "I—" She made herself breathe. "Can I talk to you? Not as patients. Just talk."

Something flickered behind his eyes. Wariness, maybe. Or recognition—he was a man who understood being sought out for the wrong reasons.

"The landing's public," he said. "Talk."

"Not here."

"Then nowhere."

"Coldharbour." She stepped closer, lowered her voice though the nearest house was fifty yards off and empty. "The boundary stones are weeping brine. Old Sela tasted it and hasn't spoken since. Tam Vetch recovered from lung-rot in one night. Odile Brant coughed a stone across a room. And I have a song in my head that nobody taught me, and I think—I think it came the same night Tam lived, and I think it's why the stones are crying, and I don't know who else to—" Her voice cracked and she hated it. "Please."

For a long moment he said nothing. The tide sucked softly at the pilings. Somewhere inland a dog barked twice and gave up.

Then Coldharbour glanced once down the empty landing, once at the darkening water, and said, quietly, "Not here either. Walk with me. Take the shore path. If anyone asks, you're gathering marsh herbs and I'm checking eel traps."

"You have eel traps?"

"I do now."

* * *

They walked north along the shingle with the flats going silver-dark on their left and the first stars pricking out over the hills. He set a pace that was almost rude, and Maren realized after a quarter mile that it wasn't rudeness—it was habit. The man walked like someone checking sightlines. She filed that away with the scars and the frozen eyes and the way his left hand never quite relaxed, even carrying a satchel.

She told him everything while the light died. The prayers, in order, each one more precise than the last. The weeping stones. And finally, haltingly, humming where words failed, the hymn.

She expected him to laugh, or dismiss it, or tell her grief and loneliness played tricks. She had prepared rebuttals for all three.

What she did not expect was for Coldharbour to stop walking so abruptly that she overshot him by three paces before she noticed.

"Again," he said. His voice had changed. All the flatness had gone out of it, and what replaced it was something she'd never heard from him and couldn't name—except that it sounded like a man hearing a dead relative's footsteps.

She hummed it. Six notes, rest, five more. Then, because he'd gone so still, she tried the words too, the strange edge-syllables, mouthing them half aloud the way she did in the dark.

His hand shot out and closed around her wrist. Not hard—but absolute, the grip of someone who had done this before, many times, to people mid-word.

"Don't finish it," he said. "Ever. Not aloud. Not alone. Not finished."

"It's just a song—"

"It isn't." He released her wrist slowly, finger by finger, like untying a knot he didn't trust. When he spoke again his voice was level again, but level the way ice is level. "Where did you hear it?"

"Nowhere. That's what I'm telling you. It woke me up. It was just—there. Complete."

"When?"

"The night Tam Vetch lived. Eleven days ago."

He turned away from her and looked out at the dark water for a long time. The tide breathed. When he faced her again, he looked ten years older, or perhaps ten years younger, stripped of the ferryman's dullness down to whatever was underneath.

"Maren." He said her name like he was testing its weight. "Do you know what I was, before I came here?"

"Ferryman."

"Before that."

She shook her head.

"There was an order," he said. "You won't have heard of it. We made sure of that. We were—" A pause, long enough that she thought he'd stop. "We were people whose words did things. Real things. Not prayer. Not asking. Speaking. There was a liturgy—a speaking-liturgy—that every member learned, and it began with a hymn. Six notes, rest, five more." His jaw tightened. "It was forbidden to teach it to anyone outside the order. Forbidden to write it down. Forbidden, near the end, to sing it at all."

The cold that went through Maren then had nothing to do with the sea wind.

"That's impossible," she whispered. "I've never met anyone from—there's no order here, there's nothing here, the valley doesn't—"

"I know what the valley does." He was already walking again, faster, and she had to hurry to keep up. "That's rather the point. Listen to me. Four questions. Answer honestly, because I'll know if you don't. Has the hymn changed since it came to you? Grown longer, added verses?"

"No. Same six-and-five. Always."

"Does it feel like it wants something? Pulls at you? Tries to make you sing it?"

She thought of the nights she'd caught herself humming it while boiling bandages, while walking the mill road, while standing over sleeping patients—always standing over someone, she realized now, always near the sick, the hurt, the dying. Her stomach turned. "It doesn't pull. It waits. But I hum it most when I'm... healing. Working."

"And the prayers." He stopped again and faced her fully, and in the starlight his face was all planes and shadow. "When they're answered. Does it feel like a god? Warm, vast, loving?"

"No." The answer came out of her before she'd decided it. "It feels like..." She searched for it, the thing she'd never admitted even to herself. "Like a person. Close. Leaning in. Like someone reading over my shoulder who's very pleased I finally wrote something down."

Coldharbour closed his eyes.

For a moment—one breath, two—he looked like a man standing in a burning building he'd thought was safely demolished for twenty years. When he opened his eyes, he was the ferryman again, controlled, unreadable, but Maren had already seen behind the mask and couldn't unsee it.

"Come with me," he said. "Tomorrow. Before dawn. There's a place I need to check, and after that there are things you need to understand, and I need to decide how much of it you can survive knowing."

"Survive knowing?" She planted her feet in the shingle. "Coldharbour, I've spent eleven days thinking I'm losing my mind, or cursed, or—something wore my voice to save a child today, something *answered me*, and you're standing there talking about tomorrow like—"

"Yes." He cut through her rising voice with terrible calm. "Something answered you. That's the part that matters. In the valley where nothing answers. Which means the valley is failing, which means everything my dead masters bled for is coming apart, and you—" he pointed one scarred finger at her, not accusing, more like marking a spot on a map, "—you are either the crack it's coming through, or the door it's using, or the bell it's ringing. I don't know which yet. So yes. Tomorrow. Before dawn. Because tonight I have to dig up a grave I swore I'd never open, and you are going to go home, bar your door, and under no circumstances sing."

Maren stared at him. The flats hissed. Far off, the boundary stone by the mill road stood black against the last gray light, and she would have sworn—even then, even doubting everything—that she saw it gleam wetly, like something weeping in the dark.

"One more question," she said quietly. "Your scars. The patterned ones. They're from the order, aren't you? You're one of them. The last one."

He didn't answer. He picked up his satchel, settled it on his shoulder, and walked away down the shingle with the tide sighing after him, and the only sound he made was the sound of a man trying to be nothing at all.

But as he reached the shadow of the sea-wall, Maren heard it—so faint she might have imagined it, except that she had stopped trusting imagination eleven days ago.

He was humming.

Six notes. A rest. Five more.

And then, very deliberately, silence.
