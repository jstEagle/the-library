## 5. A Church of Answers

The road to Salt Valley was a road the Church had never bothered to pave, and Brother Cassian understood why before he'd walked a mile of it. Nothing grew here that wasn't stubborn. The air tasted like a coin held too long in the mouth. Even his mule, a beast with no imagination whatsoever, kept stopping to look back the way they'd come, as if the country behind them were calling it home.

Cassian did not look back. He had spent eleven days looking back—back at the seminary where the Archivist-General had pressed a sealed letter into his hands, back at the burned chapel in Marrow Ford where a dying woman's prayer had knit her flesh whole while three hundred witnesses wept, back at every miracle that had bloomed across the provinces like mold after rain. He was done looking back. Everything worth seeing was ahead, in a valley so salt-scoured that maps marked it with a small white smudge and the words *barren; unremarkable*.

Unremarkable. That was what made it interesting.

He crested the last ridge at dusk and stopped, and for a long moment he simply stood there and let the valley look at him.

It lay below like a cupped hand: grey water, grey reed-beds, a scatter of low stone houses around a harbor the color of pewter. Smoke rose from chimneys in thin, obedient lines. Somewhere down there a bell rang the evening hour, unhurried, and the sound crossed the water and died without echo. No echo. He noticed that. In the mountains behind him every shout came back doubled; here, the valley swallowed sound the way sand swallowed water.

And along the shore, at intervals he could just make out in the failing light, stood stones. Tall ones. Dozens of them, ringing the valley like teeth.

Cassian took out his journal, wet the pencil stub with his tongue, and wrote: *Boundary markers, uncounted, uniform placement. Not decorative.*

Then he wrote, smaller: *Not natural.*

He had been a cartographer's son before he'd been anything else, and old habits of attention were the one inheritance he'd never managed to squander. His father used to say that a map was a confession—that every border told you what someone feared. And this valley was ringed with fear, laid down in stone by people who had wanted very badly for something to stay inside.

Or outside. He turned the thought over as he led the mule down the switchbacks. A ring of wards could hold a thing in. It could also hold a thing out. The Church's doctrine said the world after the Silence was a house from which God had departed, leaving only cold hearths and unanswered prayers—and now, suddenly, prayers were being answered again, first in trickles and then in floods, and the Church had declared it the Return and built a new liturgy around it inside a single season. The Answered Prayer. People needed it the way drowning men need rope. Cassian had watched them need it. He had needed it himself, once, more than he would ever write down.

But his father's voice was still in his head, patient and infuriating: *A border tells you what someone fears.* And the answered prayers clustered here like flies on a wound, and the wound was ringed with stones, and nobody in the Church hierarchy seemed to find that strange except him.

He intended to be strange about it thoroughly.

* * *

The ferryman found him at dawn.

Cassian had slept badly in the ruined pilgrim hospice above the shore—a building older than any church record, its lintel carved with symbols the Church's iconoclasts had missed or ignored—and he was standing at the water's edge watching the light come up grey and flat as hammered tin when the boat came across. A single figure at the oars, moving with an economy that looked almost lazy until you noticed how fast the boat was actually closing the distance.

The figure shipped oars, let the boat drift the last few yards, and regarded him.

"Brother." The voice was level, neither warm nor cold. "You'll want to state your business before I take you across. It's the custom."

"Your custom, or the valley's?"

Something flickered in the ferryman's face—not quite surprise. Recalculation. "Both," they said. "They're the same thing here."

Cassian smiled the way he'd been taught to smile in seminary debate classes: gently, and with total attention. "My business is prayer," he said. "I've come to pray."

"Prayer's free everywhere else in the world."

"So it is." He let the smile widen a fraction. "But answers are not. And lately, Brother Ferryman—or Sister; forgive me, the hood hides everything—I'm told your valley has been generous with answers."

The hood tilted. Under it, shadow. "Who told you that?"

"A woman in Marrow Ford whose hand grew back." He said it mildly, watching. "She'd never been to the valley. But the hymn she prayed with, she said, she'd learned from a peddler who traded through the salt country. Miracles leave trails, like anything else. You follow them far enough upstream, you find the source."

"And if there is no source? If it's just—" the ferryman gestured at the flat grey morning, "—weather?"

"Then I'll have wasted a journey, and the Church will have wasted a modest stipend, and I'll go home and be quietly reassigned to counting candle stubs." He spread his hands, a man of harmless piety. "May I cross?"

A long pause. Long enough that Cassian counted his own heartbeats against it—seven, eight, nine—and noted that the ferryman's eyes had gone, briefly, not to him but past him, up the slope to the boundary stones. Reading them. Or reading something in them.

"The fare is two coppers," the ferryman said finally. "And one rule. Whatever you preach in the square, you preach it plain. No fire-and-damnation. The valley doesn't care for it."

"The valley doesn't care for much, from what I can see."

"No," the ferryman said. "It doesn't." And put out a hand to help him into the boat with a grip that was brief, dry, and strong as a dock-rope.

Cassian sat in the stern and watched the water slide by. It was very clear and very dead. No fish rose. No weed waved. Once, looking down through the glass-grey surface, he thought he saw shapes below—rectilinear, regular, like walls—but the light shifted and it was only stone, only depth, only nothing.

His father again, from somewhere twenty years drowned: *When the map shows nothing, Cassian, that's not emptiness. That's erasure. Ask who held the pen.*

He asked himself who held the pen, and the answer arrived with the unpleasant clarity of things long suspected and newly admitted: the swordspeakers had held the pen. The order the Church had broken forty years ago, the order whose grandmasters had screamed heresy as they burned, the order whose surviving members had been hunted province by province until the lists went quiet. The Church called those decades the Purification. Cassian had grown up singing about it.

And now miracles were pouring out of the last place the swordspeakers had ever been seen to gather, and the Church had sent exactly one brother with a sealed letter and a stipend for candles, because the Archivist-General did not want to know what was upstream. The Archivist-General wanted plausible deniability with a collar on.

Fine. Cassian had never minded working alone. Alone, you could be honest.

* * *

The village received him the way villages receive weather: with watchful, practical courtesy. They gave him bread and salt fish—they gave everyone bread and salt fish, he learned within a day; it was simply what the valley had—and they let him preach in the square on the second evening because the ferryman had vouched for him, and because, he suspected, they were curious. Starved, even. Twenty years of a godless silence, and then prayers starting to work like wells refilling, and no one to tell them what it meant. Of course they wanted a man with a collar to stand up and explain their own lives to them. That hunger was the oldest instrument in the Church's case, and Cassian played it without apology, because the alternative was letting them starve on rumor.

He preached plainly, as instructed. He preached the Return: that the long silence was ending, that the gods—or God; the valley folk didn't distinguish and he didn't press—were listening again, that every answered prayer was a lamp lit in a dark house. He watched their faces while he spoke. He was good at watching faces. He saw hope, which he expected. He saw relief, which he expected.

What he did not expect, on the third night, was fear.

It came from the edges of the crowd, from people who didn't come forward when he invited them to. An old net-mender who crossed herself with the wrong hand, some pre-Church gesture, and looked ashamed of it. A boy of maybe fifteen who asked him, very quietly, whether the answering voice ever got anything wrong. Whether it ever lied.

"Why would it lie?" Cassian asked him.

"I don't know," the boy said. "But my gran says the old stories all agree on one thing. Anything that answers you too easily wants something." And he slipped away into the dark before Cassian could ask what stories, from whom, exactly.

Cassian stood in the emptying square with the salt wind pulling at his habit and felt, for the first time since Marrow Ford, the specific cold weight of a question he couldn't put down. He had spent eleven days cataloguing miracles, and every one of them had been *kind*. Healing, finding, mending, sparing. A god returning with nothing but gifts. And yet the boy's gran was right, in the way that grandmothers are right about everything that matters: generosity is a strategy. Every gift is a hook with the barb filed smooth.

He went back to the hospice, lit his candle stub, and unlocked the document box.

* * *

The list was old paper, soft as cloth at the folds. Forty names, in the Archivist-General's cramped hand, each with a province and a date of confirmation. Confirmed dead, most of them. Executed in the Purification's last years, or dead of age and hiding, or simply vanished into the general forgetting of the world. Thirty-nine names, and beside thirty-nine of them, the same notation in fading red ink: *closed*.

One name remained open.

*COLDHARBOUR, VESPER. Last apprentice of the Ninth Circle. Status unknown. Last sighted: Salt Valley, year of the Sealing.*

Cassian read it four times. He had known, of course—he had known since Marrow Ford, since the hymn-trails all bent toward the salt country like iron filings toward a lodestone. Knowing a thing in the abstract and seeing it in ink were different disciplines. Here it was. The last swordspeaker alive, or the last one the Church knew of, living in the one place on earth where prayers had started to be answered, working—as his informants' descriptions converged—*as a ferryman*.

A ferryman. He almost laughed. The Church had spent forty years teaching that swordspeakers were blasphemers, that their spoken art was theft from heaven's treasury, that their order had been rightly burned. And the final member of that order had chosen the humblest office a waterside community could offer: carrying people across. From one side to the other. Which was, if you thought about it even briefly, what thresholds were *for*, and what wardens were for, and what—

He stopped.

He set the list down carefully, the way you set down something that might be listening.

*A ring of stones. A sealed valley. A swordspeaker keeping the crossing.* His father's voice, patient as erosion: *Ask who held the pen, and ask what the border was drawn around.*

Every account of the Purification agreed on one detail the Church did not emphasize in its liturgies: in the final years, the swordspeakers had stopped fighting and started *building*. Records spoke of great works undertaken in secret—wards, enclosures, consecrations of ground—in the same seasons the executions peaked. The Church had always interpreted this as desperation. Fortification. Rats walling themselves into a hole.

But rats don't build. Builders build. And builders build *around* something.

Cassian sat with the candle burning down between his fingers and assembled it, piece by piece, the way his father had taught him to assemble a coastline from scattered soundings: the answered prayers clustering at the valley's edge like birds before a storm; the boundary stones weeping brine, according to the peddler's gossip; the dead-clear water; the boy's grandmother and her old stories about voices that answer too easily; and at the center of it, holding the crossing, the last person alive who might know what the stones were for.

Two hypotheses, and only two. Either the valley was a wellspring—the place the returning god touched earth first, the source he'd been sent to confirm. Or the valley was a cage, and the thing answering prayers all across the provinces was leaking out through cracks in a lock that the swordspeakers had built with their dying strength, and the Church was preparing to throw open the door and call it salvation.

He blew out the candle. In the dark, the hospice smelled of salt and old stone, and beneath that, faintly, of something else—brackish, organic, like the inside of a shell. He decided it was the building settling. He decided several things that night, lying awake, and none of them held.

* * *

In the morning he did what the Church trained him to do and what his conscience permitted: he began, methodically, to hunt.

Not with soldiers—he had none, and the Archivist-General had been explicit that the valley was to be approached softly, confirmed, reported. Softly. So he hunted the way he'd been taught in the archives: by asking small questions and writing down large silences. He asked the baker how long her family had lived in the valley, and she said always, and her face closed like a door. He asked the harbormaster about the boundary stones, and the man said marker stones, old ones, and changed the subject to tides with the fluency of long practice. He asked children, who lied least: they told him the stones were named, each one, though no adult would say the names aloud, and that you weren't supposed to count them, and that once a fisherman had counted them and left the valley the next spring and no one spoke of him after.

He visited the parish records, such as they were—forty years of births and deaths in a careful clerk's hand, and before that, a gap. A clean gap, forty years wide, as if the valley had begun existing recently. Erasure. Someone had held the pen.

And on the fifth day, walking the shore at low tide with his journal, he found the grave.

It was set apart from the village plots, up on the shingle above the wrack line, a single mound with a slab at its head. Fresh. Days old, no more. And on the slab, laid in a perfect unbroken line along the stone's upper edge, was salt. White, coarse, deliberate—not spilled, *placed*, the way you'd place flowers. It glittered in the flat light like a seam of frost.

Cassian crouched and did not touch it. His heart was behaving badly.

Because he knew this rite. Every archivist knew it, though the Church had expunged it from the living liturgy: the swordspeakers' burial custom, recorded in the interrogation transcripts of the Purification under the heading *Superstitious Practices Observed Among the Condemned*. Salt on the grave, they'd said, so the dead could speak. Salt on the threshold, so the truth couldn't cross uninvited. Salt, always salt—the substance of covenant, of preservation, of the word made mineral. The condemned had gone to the fire whispering about salt, and the inquisitors had written it down as madness.

Here, on a fresh grave in a godless valley, someone had performed the forbidden rite. Recently. Openly enough to leave it undisturbed.

And around the grave, pressed into the shingle by feet that had knelt there—more than one pair of knees, recent, the prints still crisp—someone had prayed. He could not prove that last part. He didn't need to. Prayers were being answered all through this valley; that was why he'd come. Someone had knelt at a swordspeaker's grave-marker and prayed, and something had answered, and the answer had left salt like a signature.

*So the dead could speak,* the transcript said.

Cassian stood for a long time in the wind with his journal open and unwritten in. Then he wrote one line, in the small tight hand he saved for conclusions he disliked:

*The lock is turning. Find the warden.*

* * *

He found the ferryman that evening at the crossing, mending a net with quick, blind hands, and sat down on the shingle nearby without invitation, the way you sit near a dog you haven't decided about.

"They say you've been here twenty years," he said.

"They say a lot of things."

"They say you came the year the valley sealed itself. The year the last roads out were closed." He watched the hands work. "That's a strange coincidence, Brother Coldharbour."

The net went still. Just for a breath—one missed stitch, one silence in the rhythm—and then it resumed, steady, unhurried, a professional's hands doing a professional's work. But Cassian had spent his life reading faces and hands and the pauses between words, and he had his confirmation, small and complete, folded away like the list in his box.

"You have me confused with someone," the ferryman said.

"I have a list," Cassian said pleasantly, "of thirty-nine dead people. I've spent five days in your valley learning about stones and graves and salt, and I have not once heard you raise your voice. Do you know what that tells me? Only someone who has taken a vow of silence spends twenty years practicing it." He rose, brushed the shingle from his habit, and smiled his gentle seminar smile. "I'm not your enemy, ferryman. I genuinely am not. I've read the same interrogations you'd have memorized at fourteen, and I noticed the same thing in them you must have: the masters weren't screaming blasphemies when they burned. They were screaming *warnings*. Nobody in the Church ever asked warnings about what."

The oars hung motionless. Across the water, the boundary stones stood black against the last light, a ring of teeth around a held breath.

"So here is my offer, and I'll make it only once," Cassian said. "Tell me what the stones are for, and I will write my report myself, and I promise you—I swear it on my order and my name—that the Church's soldiers never see this valley. Tell me nothing, and I write the truth as I best understand it, and men with less patience than I have come to ask in louder voices. You've carried people across this water for twenty years, Coldharbour. Carry yourself across this one."

For a moment—just a moment—the ferryman looked at him, and Cassian saw the eyes properly for the first time: not a servant's eyes, not a hermit's eyes. *Warden's* eyes. Old, and tired, and measuring him the way one measures a rising tide.

"I don't know what you're talking about," Vesper Coldharbour said. "Fare's two coppers. Same as anyone."

And turned back to the net.

Cassian stood a moment longer in the cooling dark. Then he paid the two coppers into the bottom of the empty boat—payment for a crossing not yet taken, a debt established, a thread attached—and walked back up the shore toward the lights of the village, already composing the first lines of his report in his head.

Behind him, unheard, the ferryman sat unmoving in the dark, and out beyond the last of the boundary stones, far out in the dead clear water, something that wore a god's patience turned its attention, gently and with enormous interest, toward the man from the Church—who had, without knowing it, just told it everything it needed to know about the last lock on the door.
