## 6. Oathbound

The ferry rope was fraying again, and Vesper let it.

There was a discipline in small neglects. A swordspeaker's hands wanted work—wanted the hum of a blade, the syllables that lived in the wrist—and if you gave them nothing, they went looking. So Vesper gave them rope, and tar, and the slow rot of a ferry landing that no one important ever used. Fraying rope was honest. It asked for patience and nothing else.

Maren sat on the far bench of the ferry with her knees drawn up, watching the water. She had been watching the water for an hour. The Salt Valley's lake didn't ripple like other lakes; it lay flat as poured pewter, and things that fell into it sank without ceremony. Vesper had told her once that the water was just water. That had been a lie of convenience, and they both knew it now.

"You're doing it again," Maren said.

"Doing what."

"Listening to the silence like it owes you money."

Vesper hauled the rope a hand's width and stopped. "It does."

* * *

Fourteen years old, kneeling on wet stone in the crypt beneath the Hall of Voices, and Master Ilenne's hand heavy on their crown like a yoke.

"The words are not yours," Ilenne said. "They were never yours. You carry them the way a sheath carries a blade—for someone else's use, until the day you are not needed. Say the oath."

Vesper had already learned eleven of the true words by then. They sat behind their teeth like swallowed coals, warm and terrible. Eleven words that could stop a heart at forty paces, could unmake a lock, could call rain out of a clear sky. Eleven words, and the oath was designed to make sure there would never be twelve.

"I renounce my voice," fourteen-year-old Vesper recited, palms flat on the stone. "I keep what I am given and speak none of what I know. My tongue is the order's. My silence is the world's shield. If I break this, may my sword forget me, and may I forget my sword."

"And mean it," Ilenne said.

That was the cruelty of it. The oath wasn't ink or ritual—it was meaning. You had to mean it, all the way down, or it wouldn't take. And so Vesper had knelt there and meant it, meant it with everything in them, because Ilenne was the only person who had ever looked at them like they were worth something, and because the masters knew best, and because fourteen is an age built for believing.

The oath took hold like cold water filling the chest. And somewhere behind their teeth, the eleven words dimmed—not gone, but wrapped, the way a blade is wrapped in oilcloth. Still there. Still sharp. Just not for them.

"Do you understand why?" Ilenne asked afterward, and for one moment her voice was not iron. For one moment she sounded tired, and old, and afraid.

"No," Vesper said honestly.

"Good," she said. "If you understood, you'd be too dangerous to keep alive." Then she smiled, and ruffled their hair, and the moment passed, and Vesper spent twenty years telling themselves they'd imagined the fear.

* * *

"They're weeping again," Maren said. "The boundary stones. Third time this week."

"I know."

"Do you? Because you go look at them every morning and come back and say nothing, and I've started to think the saying-nothing isn't a habit anymore, Vesper. I think it's a wound."

Vesper poled the ferry off the landing. The water took them without a sound, which was wrong; water should argue. The valley slid past—sheep, terraced barley, the chapel with its new crack running through the bell tower like a vein. Everything green and gray and salt-stained and ordinary. Twenty years of ordinary. It had been the whole point.

"My order," Vesper said, because Maren had earned fragments, if never the whole, "had a rule. We spoke power, and so we swore not to speak. Not humility. Architecture. Every word we didn't say was a brick."

"A wall against what?"

"That's the question, isn't it." The pole bit silt. "I spent twenty years thinking the answer was: against us. Against what we'd become if we kept talking. Arrogance. Fire. The usual reasons orders give for burying themselves."

"And now?"

"Now I've read a dead man's testament in a flooded crypt, and I think the bricks weren't holding something in the valley." Vesper looked at the flat water. "I think they were holding something under it."

Maren was quiet for a long moment. Then, in a voice carefully casual, the way people ask about things that frighten them:

"The hymn. The one I keep hearing. The one nobody taught me." She turned. "You recognized it. I saw your face when I sang it—you looked like someone had walked over your grave. What is it?"

"It's liturgy," Vesper said. "Mine. Ours. The Order of the Speaking Sword."

"Teach me the rest."

"No."

* * *

The night the order died, Vesper had been in the root cellars, which was where they always put the youngest during drills gone wrong, and the screaming came down through the floorboards in a language Vesper had been sworn never to understand and understood perfectly anyway.

Not screams of pain. Screams of *speech*. Masters dying with true words in their mouths, forbidden to finish them, each one cut off mid-syllable like a door slammed on a sentence. Master Corvane shouting the first three words of a warding that needed nine. Sister Amet crying out two syllables of a name and then nothing, nothing, nothing, and Vesper pressing both hands over their own mouth in the dark because the oath sat in their chest like a fist and even grief couldn't pry it open.

That was the lesson of that night, the one Vesper carried out of the burning Hall of Voices and across three hundred miles to the Salt Valley: the oath did not protect you from dying. It only guaranteed you died unfinished. Every master had gone out mid-word. The enemy hadn't silenced them. Their own vow had done the silencing, and the enemy had merely collected the silence.

Twenty years later, in the flooded dark under the wardstone, Ilenne's testament had said: *We did not seal the valley to hide from the Church. We sealed it to cage the thing that answers prayers. The oath was the lock's first tooth. Do not teach the hymn entire. Do not sing the binding. The words are the bars.*

And Vesper had surfaced from that black water knowing that the oath they'd meant at fourteen, all the way down, had been a bar in a cage—and that the caged thing was now answering prayers through cracks in the masonry, and that Maren walked around with pieces of the bars in her mouth without knowing what they held shut.

* * *

"You have to tell me why not," Maren said. She had followed Vesper up from the landing, up the goat track, to the lean-to where Vesper mended nets and slept badly. Evening came down the valley like slow honey. "You can't just say 'no' and expect me to carry that. Things are happening to me, Vesper. My prayers get answered. Your stones are bleeding. There's a hymn in my head that has no business being there, and the one person who might know what it means won't tell me. So either you trust me or you're using me the way everyone else uses me—as a vessel for something they won't explain."

The word *vessel* landed between them and neither picked it up.

"If I teach you," Vesper said slowly, "I break an oath I made at fourteen. An oath that has kept me alive, kept this valley asleep, kept—" they gestured at the whole green quiet of it, "—all of this, unbothered, for twenty years."

"Your oath also kept a lie alive."

"Yes," Vesper said. "Both things are true. That's what an oath is. A truth you pay for with another truth."

Maren stood in the doorway, backlit, stubborn, young in a way that hurt to look at. "Then teach me one line. One. Not the binding—whatever the binding is, I don't want it. Teach me enough that I know what's living in my mouth. You know what it's like to have words inside you that you're not allowed to understand. You've had twenty years of it. Are you going to hand me the same life?"

And there it was. Grief and temptation, sitting at the same table. Because Vesper remembered being fourteen and starving for meaning, and remembered Ilenne giving them the oath instead of the answer, and remembered how that hunger had curdled into a lifetime of listening to silence. To refuse Maren was to become Ilenne. To accept was to pull the first bar out of the cage with their own hands.

Somewhere below, faint across the water, the chapel bell cracked clean through and fell silent mid-ring.

Maren flinched. Vesper didn't. Vesper was looking past her, down the valley, to where the evening light caught the boundary stones—and saw, for the first time, that they were not weeping brine anymore.

They were bleeding light.

"Get your bag," Vesper said quietly. "Whatever I decide about the hymn, we're out of time to decide it."
