## 7. The Door in the Water

**Vesper**

The wardstone wept for three days before I understood what it was telling me.

Brine ran down its face in slow lines, like a statue crying, and pooled at its base in a crust of white. Every morning the pool was wider. Every morning the salt crept another handspan toward the grass, and every morning the grass died a little more, curling brown as if scalded. The valley was leaking. Not water. *Meaning.* Whatever my masters had poured into this seal, it was thinning, and thinning fast.

I told no one. I told Maren only that she should keep her prayers behind her teeth, and she had looked at me with that flat, stubborn healer's stare and said, "You don't get to decide what I do with my own voice," which was fair, and true, and made me want to put my fist through a wall.

So instead I did what I always do when the world goes wrong. I went to work.

I poled the ferry across the Greywash eleven times that day, back and forth, back and forth, carrying farmers and goats and one nervous bride with her whole dowry stacked around her like a fortress. The passengers talked about the rain of fish two villages over, about Brother Cassian's name arriving on a peddler's tongue like a fever, about miracles. Nobody talked about the wardstone. Nobody knew it existed. That was the point of it.

On the twelfth crossing, empty, dusk coming down purple over the reeds, I let the pole rest and looked at the water.

The Greywash runs through the center of the valley, wide and slow and so saturated with dissolved salt that nothing lives in it—no fish, no weed, not even the black beetles that thrive in worse places. The old folk called it the Spine of the World and forbade swimming in it, and children obeyed, because even at six you could feel something wrong when you dangled your feet over the side. A hush. A listening quality.

I had crossed it ten thousand times. I had never once asked myself why a valley with a perfectly good river running through it needed a ferryman who never aged, never left, never drowned.

My master's testament sat in the oilcloth satchel under my seat, where it had sat since the crypt, since I'd read it by candlelight with my hands shaking. *The valley is a door,* he had written. *We built the house around the lock.* And beneath that, in a different ink, a single line I had read forty times and still could not make sit still in my chest:

*If the seal weakens, the way out is through the water. It opens to a true name spoken aloud. Any true name will do—but it must be freely given, and it must be your own.*

Any true name. Freely given.

I am Vesper Coldharbour. I have been Vesper Coldharbour for twenty years, and before that I was something else, something with a different name, a child's name, an order-name, and I have not said it aloud since the night the order burned. Not because I couldn't. Because the oath sits in my throat like a swallowed hook, and every time I reach for that name the barb turns, and I remember what my masters' faces looked like when they made me swear: not stern. *Terrified.* Four adults, veterans of a hundred battles against things out of the deep dark, terrified of a fourteen-year-old's mouth.

They hadn't been afraid I would speak the Unnamed's name. They'd been afraid I'd speak *mine*—because a swordspeaker's true name is a key, and keys don't care which lock they open.

I poled the ferry to the middle of the Greywash and stopped.

Below me, visible only because the sun had dropped low enough to lay its light flat along the surface, there was a shape in the riverbed. Geometric. Deliberate. A ring of pale stone, maybe thirty feet across, and set into the ring—doors. Actual doors, standing upright on the river floor, seven of them, their frames carved with letters in a script I knew better than my own handwriting.

Swordspeak. The liturgy-script. The same script on the hymn fragment Maren carried, the same script on the wardstone, the same script scarred into my own forearms in the shape of oaths.

Seven doors at the bottom of a dead river, in a valley everyone believed was simply a valley.

I stood very still on my own ferry, in the last light, and felt the full weight of the irony settle onto my shoulders like a yoke. There was a door out. There had always been a door out. My masters had built the cage with an exit in it—because they were not monsters, because some part of them had wanted their students to survive, to leave, to live—and the exit required exactly one thing from me.

A word.

One word, freely given, and I could walk out of the Salt Valley tomorrow. One word, and twenty years of exile ended. One word—

—and the oath turned its barb in my throat, and I tasted blood, faint and coppery, though I hadn't bitten anything.

I poled back to shore without speaking at all.

* * *

**Maren**

He wouldn't tell me what he'd found. He just showed up at my door after dark, soaked to the knees, smelling of brine and old stone, and said, "Bring your kit. And don't bring your prayers."

Which was such an absurd instruction that I almost laughed. Then I saw his face, and didn't.

We walked upstream along the Greywash for an hour, past the last of the farmsteads, into country where the reeds grew taller than either of us and the silence got thicker with every step. Vesper moved like a man walking to his own execution—steady, deliberate, eyes forward, jaw set. Twice I opened my mouth to ask questions and twice the look he gave me closed it again.

At last we came to a place where the river widened into a still black pool, and Vesper stopped, and pointed down.

"Look," he said. "Where the light falls."

The moon was rising. Its light lay along the water in a long silver road, and at the bottom of the pool, beneath ten feet of dead clear brine, I saw them. Doors. Seven pale doors standing in a ring on the riverbed, carved all over with writing that made the hair on my arms stand up—not because it was frightening, but because I *knew* it. The way you know a song you've heard in dreams. The way I knew the hymn fragment I'd carried since childhood, the one nobody taught me, the one I hummed when I was frightened until the night Vesper went white as bone and told me what it was.

"Forbidden liturgy," he'd said. "Binding-verse. You've been humming the bars of a cage your whole life."

Now I stood over seven more bars of it, sunk in the heart of the valley, and felt something in me go quiet and cold.

"What are they?" I whispered.

"Doors." His voice was flat. "Ways out. My order built this valley, Maren. Built it around something they wanted locked away forever, and then sealed themselves inside with it, and then—" his jaw worked "—then made sure their students could never leave either. In case we were compromised. In case the thing learned our names through us." He crouched at the water's edge. "But they weren't complete monsters. They left a door. One door that opens from the inside."

"To who?"

"To anyone who speaks a true name. Their own true name. Freely given." He said it carefully, like a man handling broken glass. "That's the mechanism. Names have power—that's the first law of swordspeak, the first thing they teach you, the reason the order existed at all. A true name, spoken with a free heart, is the most honest thing a person can say. The doors were warded to recognize honesty. Nothing else gets them open."

"And you can't."

It wasn't a question. I'd known since the crypt, since he came back from the flooded dark with his master's testament and a look like a man who'd found out his whole life was a room with painted walls. But I said it anyway, because someone had to say things out loud in this valley or they rotted.

"I can't." He laughed, short and bitter, a sound I'd never heard from him before. "Twenty years I thought the oath protected me. Keep silent, stay safe, stay hidden. And now it turns out the silence isn't armor. It's the fourth wall of the cell." He looked up at me, and in the moonlight his face was older than I'd ever seen it, older than it had any right to be. "There's a door out of prison, Maren. And the lock only opens for the one word I swore never to say."

* * *

**Maren**

We tried anyway. Of course we tried. I think some part of both of us believed the oath was a habit, a scar, a story Vesper told himself—and stories can be argued with.

Vesper knelt at the water's edge and bowed his head, and I watched a grown man—a man who had pulled three drowning children out of the millrace two winters ago without hesitation, a man who faced down a bull once with nothing but a pole and pure contempt—struggle visibly, physically, to say his own name.

His lips moved. Nothing came. His lips moved again. A sound started in his chest and died somewhere below his collarbone. Sweat broke out on his forehead in the cold night air. On the third attempt he made a noise like a man taking a knife between the ribs, and clutched his throat, and bent double, coughing, and when he straightened up there was blood on his lip.

"It's not in my head," he rasped. "It's not fear. It's *in* me. They put it in me when I was fourteen. The oath is a working, a binding laid into the speaker's own voice." He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand and stared at the red smear like it belonged to someone else. "I could cut it out, maybe. If I still had the art. Cut the binding out of my own throat with a wordspear and take the damage." He flexed his right hand, and I saw the old scars on his forearm shift in the moonlight—the oath-scars, the ones he'd always told the village were rope burns. "But the art's been dead in me for twenty years. The valley killed it. That was the design. No magic inside the seal, not theirs, not mine, not *its*."

"So the valley took your sword," I said slowly, working it through, "and the oath took your voice, and the door needs your voice, and—"

"—and I'm the most thoroughly disarmed man in the world," Vesper finished. "Yes. My masters were thorough."

We sat on the bank for a while after that. The reeds hissed. Somewhere far off, a nightbird called, and the sound fell into the water and vanished.

"Say mine," I said.

He turned his head sharply. "What?"

"My name. Maren Ostrey, daughter of Sela, born under the east eaves in the wet spring." I said it plainly, the way you'd say it to a census-taker. "It's my true name. I'm giving it freely—I'm telling you right now, out loud, that I give it. Try the door."

"Maren—"

"You said any true name. Your master wrote it. *Any true name will do.*" I was on my feet now, and my voice cracked across the pool, too loud, too desperate, and I didn't care. "You've spent twenty years keeping everyone safe by saying nothing. Fine. Let me say something. Let *me* be useful. Open the door, Vesper. Even if you can't walk through it, prove it's real. Prove there's an out. Because if there isn't—if this whole valley is just a pretty tomb with farms painted on it—then I need to know that too."

For a long moment he just looked at me. Then something moved through his face—something I couldn't read, something between grief and wonder—and he nodded once.

He waded out into the shallows until the water reached his waist, until he stood directly above the nearest door, and he spoke my name.

Not shouted. Spoken—clear and level and carrying, the way he must once have spoken commands to battle-lines, the way a swordspeaker speaks, with the whole truth of himself behind the words. "Maren Ostrey. Daughter of Sela. She gives it freely, and I speak it truly."

The pool changed.

I don't know how else to describe it. The water didn't move, exactly—it *clarified*, all at once, as if ten feet of brine had become ten feet of glass. The seven doors leapt into view, every carved letter sharp and bright, and the letters began to glow, a soft gold like lamplight through parchment, spreading outward from the door beneath Vesper in a slow ripple. The ring of stone shivered. Sand lifted off the riverbed in delicate spirals. For one heartbeat—one held, singing heartbeat—the whole pool was a lamp, and I saw that the doors weren't separate at all but seven leaves of one great mechanism, and beyond their frames, through gaps that hadn't been there a moment ago, there was *darkness*. Moving darkness. Air that smelled, impossibly, from the bottom of a dead river, of rain on far-off hills.

Then the light guttered.

It went out like a breath blown across a candle, and the brine clouded back over the doors, and the pool was just a pool again, black and dead and silent. Vesper stood waist-deep in the middle of it, shoulders heaving, staring down at the water.

"It answered," he said hoarsely. "It recognized you. The door works." He turned, wading back toward shore, and his face when he reached the shallows was the strangest mixture I had ever seen on a human being—joy and despair in equal measure, each canceling the other out into something raw. "It works, Maren. Anyone can leave. Anyone except me."

* * *

**Vesper**

She didn't understand yet, and I loved her a little for not understanding, and I hated myself a lot for what I was going to have to explain.

"The name has to be yours," I said. "Freely given means given by its owner. You can't gift someone else's name to a door any more than you can spend their coin. Your name opened it because it was *true*—because every word of it was yours, honestly meant. Mine—" I touched my throat, where the barb of the oath sat coiled around the syllables like a snake around a sparrow. "Mine belongs to the oath. I gave it away when I was fourteen. Gave it to the order, to the silence, to the seal. It isn't mine to speak anymore. That's what 'freely given' cuts both ways, Maren. I gave my name freely once already. To a lock. This one."

We walked back downstream in the dark, and she was quiet for a long time, and I let her be quiet, because I could feel her thinking and her thinking was better than mine tonight.

It was nearly the village lights when she stopped walking.

"Then un-give it," she said.

I turned. Her face was pale in the starlight, set, absolutely certain, and I felt a cold finger trace down my spine, because I had seen that exact expression before. On my masters. On the last night. The same terrible clarity.

"There's a hymn," she said. "The forbidden one. The one I've been humming my whole life without knowing what it was. You told me it was binding-verse—cage-song. Well." She lifted her chin. "Cages have locks, Vesper. And locks have keys. And if the order bound your name into an oath with words, then the words that bound it can loose it. That's how their own magic works—isn't it? Everything they built opens to a true word. Including, maybe, the thing they built inside *you*."

"That hymn is a binding incantation for the Unnamed itself," I said. "You start reciting the whole of it and you're not picking a lock, you're ringing a dinner bell for the thing in the cellar."

"Then we don't recite it." Her eyes were bright and fierce and frightened. "We *study* it. You know the liturgy. You were trained in it. I have the fragments. Between us we might find the verses that bind a speaker's voice—the oath-verses—and learn how they come apart." She stepped closer. "You said your masters were thorough. Be thorougher."

"*More* thorough," I said automatically, and she almost smiled, and I almost smiled, and then neither of us did, because at that moment the wind shifted and came down the valley from the north—from the boundary stones, from the weeping wards, from the world outside—and it carried a smell that did not belong in the Salt Valley.

Woodsmoke. Horses. Incense.

And underneath it, faint but unmistakable, carried on air that should have been too magic-dead to carry anything at all: a human voice, pitched to carry farther, preaching.

I had heard that kind of voice before, twenty years ago, from the other side of a burning gate. A voice that believed so hard it became a weapon.

Maren heard it too. I watched her hear it—watched the fierceness drain out of her face and something more dangerous replace it, something hungry and wondering, the look of a woman hearing a hymn she half-remembers.

"Someone's at the edge of the valley," she whispered.

"Not someone." I turned toward the north, toward the voice, and my hand went to my hip out of twenty years of habit and found only the ferry-pole I wasn't carrying. "A church."

Far off across the dark fields, small and clear, the voice rose toward the stars, promising answers to every question, mercy for every wound, a god who listens.

And in my throat, around the one word that could open every door in the world, the old oath stirred like something waking up.
