## 8. Cassian's Sermon

The truce was written on a strip of vellum no wider than Cassian's thumb, and it cost him three days of negotiation with a ferryman who would not look at him and a village elder whose hands shook when she signed.

*One man. No soldiers past the boundary stones. Seven days.*

He had agreed to all of it. He had even left his escort at the ridge where the brine road began its descent into the valley, watching him go with the particular pity soldiers reserve for a brother walking into somewhere they'd rather not follow. Brother Ossian had pressed the reliquary into his hands at the last moment and said, "If the voice stops answering you down there, come home."

Cassian had smiled and said nothing, because the voice had not stopped answering since the valley appeared on the horizon like a held breath.

Now he stood at the edge of Salt Hollow's market square at dawn, and the first thing he understood—before the whitewashed houses, before the salt-crusted well, before the forty-odd faces already gathering with the wary curiosity of people who had heard a stranger was among them—was that this place *hurt* to stand in.

Not physically. Physically it was pleasant enough: cool morning air, gulls crying over the flats, the smell of fish and brine and woodsmoke. But beneath it all was a silence so total it pressed against the eardrums like deep water. Cassian had spent eleven years in the Church of the Answered Prayer. He had learned to hear the god the way a sailor learns to feel wind on his neck—a constant, faint pressure at the edge of perception, everywhere, always, *listening*. In the great basilica at Corvane it was a roar. In the smallest fishing chapel it was a whisper.

Here it was nothing. A hole in the world shaped exactly like a god.

And yet the miracles had come from here. The reports were unambiguous: prayers answered inside the valley's bounds, salt blooming on graves, wardstones weeping brine, a healer-girl whose touch had mended a broken arm in front of six witnesses. The god's voice did not reach here—and yet here was where it spoke loudest.

Cassian had turned that contradiction over for two hundred miles of road, and he still had no answer, only a suspicion that sat in his chest like a swallowed stone: that the silence here was not an absence but a *containment*, and that whatever answered prayers beyond these hills was, within them, something else entirely.

He set those thoughts aside. Doubt was a tool, not a dwelling. He had a sermon to give.

* * *

By midmorning the square was full—or as full as a village of four hundred could make it. They stood in the loose, shuffling way of people who had come to stare and told themselves it was only curiosity. Farmers with salt-bleached knuckles. Fisherwomen still smelling of the night's catch. Children perched on the well's rim until their mothers pulled them down. And near the back, apart from everyone, a tall figure in a gray ferryman's coat who stood with the stillness of a man pretending very hard to be furniture.

Vesper Coldharbour. The last name uncrossed on the list.

Cassian let himself look at the ferryman for exactly as long as it took to memorize the face—longer than courtesy allowed, shorter than accusation—and then looked away, because the first rule of hunting was that you did not flush the bird before the net was laid.

He climbed onto the mounting block beside the well, and the crowd quieted the way crowds do when a man in vestments stands above them, even a stranger, even one they distrust. Habit is older than suspicion.

"Good people of Salt Hollow," he began, and pitched his voice low, almost conversational, the way you speak to one person rather than many. "I have walked a long way to be lied to by you, and I'd rather not begin our acquaintance that way. So let me tell you my lie first, and then you can tell me yours."

That got them. Heads came up. A woman near the front folded her arms.

"My lie," Cassian said, "is that the Church sent me here to investigate rumors. That is what my superiors believe they have done. But I am not here to investigate. I am here because I prayed, once, for something that mattered more to me than my own life, and heaven said nothing. For nineteen years, heaven said nothing." He paused, letting the silence do its work; he watched it land on faces, watched the woman with folded arms loosen slightly. "And then, three years ago, the silence broke. Somewhere in the world, someone prayed and was answered, and the answering spread like fire through dry grass, and now there is not a chapel from here to Corvane where the prayers don't rise and the answers don't fall like rain. And every report, every witness, every miracle traces back—" he opened his hand toward the hills, toward the boundary stones, toward all of it— "*here.* To this valley. To you."

Murmurs. Not hostile. Hungry. He had counted on hungry.

"So I will ask you plainly, and I ask you to be honest, because honesty is the only currency I carry and the only thing I can offer in trade." He stepped down off the block—not down *to* them, that was the trick, down among them, shoulder to shoulder with the fisherwomen and the farmers. "How many of you have prayed in the last season?"

A rustle. Nearly everyone, or nearly everyone willing to admit it.

"How many of you were answered?"

Silence. Then, from somewhere in the middle of the crowd, a voice—the elder, Marta, the one with shaking hands. "Some." A beat. "A few."

"A few," Cassian repeated gently. "In a valley where no prayer has been answered in living memory. Where your grandparents taught you that the gods went silent before the founders' grandfathers were born. Where you built your whole life around the fact that heaven does not listen." He turned slowly, letting his gaze travel the circle. "And now it listens. Now, when a child breaks her arm, a healer touches it and it mends. Now, when a dying traveler whispers a wish on the ferry, salt blooms white on his grave like frost. You know these things. You've seen them. Some of you caused them."

He found the girl then—he'd known her by description, and she was easy to pick out anyway, standing near the chapel steps with her healer's satchel and her chin up in the way of someone daring the world to say otherwise. Maren. Young. Watchful. And afraid, under the defiance, in the specific way of a person who has received a gift and begun to suspect the giver.

He did not point at her. Not yet. The net first.

"I know what some of you are thinking," he continued. "You're thinking: *this priest comes with a church, and churches take.* They take tithes, they take obedience, they take your children for their seminaries and your dead for their graveyards. You're thinking the Answered Prayer is just the old hierarchies wearing new light." He shook his head, and made sure they saw him shake it. "I'm not here to take anything. I'm here because a god who answers is worth more than every doctrine ever written, and I don't care what name it prays under. I care that it *listens.*"

He let that hang. Then, softer:

"But I have to tell you what I've learned in eleven years of listening back. The voice that answers—it knows things. It knew my daughter's fever before I spoke it. It knew the words I meant to pray before I formed them. It answers the prayer you *should* have prayed, the one underneath, the one you're ashamed of." His voice dropped. "It has never once answered the prayer I actually asked. Have any of you noticed that? Have any of you gotten exactly what you asked for? Or have you gotten what you *needed*, as decided by something that knows you better than you know yourself?"

The crowd had gone very still. Somewhere a gull screamed and was ignored.

"That is either perfect mercy," Cassian said, "or perfect surveillance. And I have spent three years unable to tell the difference. I came here because this is where the voice is loudest, and I thought—if anywhere, here I would finally hear it clearly enough to know."

He stopped. Let the silence stretch. Forty seconds. Sixty. He had learned patience from the god itself.

Nothing answered. Of course nothing answered. The valley was a hole in the world's ear.

But they didn't know that. And in their silence, in the place where the god's voice should have risen to confirm him, they felt only their own hearts beating, and doubt moving through them like cold water through a net.

"Strange," Cassian said quietly. "Here of all places, it's quiet. Isn't that strange? The one place the miracles come from, and the voice won't speak to me." He turned, slow, deliberate, and now he did raise his hand, and now he pointed—not at Maren, not yet, but at the gray-coated figure at the back of the square, motionless as a mooring post. "Unless it isn't quiet. Unless it's just quiet for *me*."

* * *

He gave them the afternoon. That was part of the craft—you did not spring the trap while the iron was still warm from the forge. He walked the market, bought nothing, paid for everything anyway, pressing coins into reluctant palms. He blessed a fisherman's new boat and the man crossed himself out of habit and then stood looking at his own hand as if it belonged to someone else. He sat with Marta the elder on her step and listened to her talk about her dead husband for an hour without once mentioning the Church, and when she finally asked, "What happens to us? If we believe?" he answered honestly: "Nothing happens to you. That's the whole point. Believing costs nothing. It's the *not* believing that starts to cost, once you've heard it answer."

By evening the square filled again, and this time they came on purpose. Word had moved through the village the way word moves through small places—faster than walking, carried in glances and half-heard sentences. They came to hear more. Some came to argue. All came.

And Vesper Coldharbour came too, and stood in the same spot, and this time did not pretend to be furniture. This time the ferryman watched him with an expression Cassian recognized from the list-work, from the long years of crossing out names: the look of a person doing arithmetic about exits.

Cassian took the mounting block again, and this time he did not open with lies or confessions. He opened with the hymn.

"The old books say," he began, "that before the silence, there was an order of warriors who fought with voices instead of steel. Swordspeakers. You'll have heard the stories—every child in the borderlands has. They could kill with a word and heal with another, and their liturgy was forbidden even to their own novices, because a spoken truth in the right cadence doesn't persuade, it *compels*. The Church burned their cloisters a generation ago. We are told they were heretics. We are told their art died with them." He paused. "We are also told, good people, that heaven was silent until three years ago. And we know that isn't quite true anymore, don't we."

He saw Maren in the second row, gone pale. He saw her hand drift to the satchel at her hip, and he thought: *she carries it. She carries fragments of it. The reports said a hymn no one taught her.*

"Here is what troubles me," Cassian said, and his voice was gentle now, almost sorrowful, the sorrow genuine because it was also strategy and the best lies are load-bearing truths. "The miracles in this valley began with a dying man's prayer on a ferry. And the man who ferried him—" he turned, and the crowd turned with him, all eyes swinging to the gray coat— "is the last swordspeaker alive. Vesper Coldharbour. Sworn to an oath of silence twenty years ago, in the ruins of an order that the histories say was destroyed for its crimes."

He let the name sit in the air. He watched it work. He watched a fisherman who had nodded along all day take half a step away from the ferryman's corner of the square. He watched Marta's hands start their shaking again. He watched loyalty curdle into fear in real time, in forty faces, fear moving through the crowd like dye through water, and he hated himself with a clean and familiar hatred, and kept pouring.

"I am not accusing anyone," he said. "I am under truce, and I keep my truces. I am asking a question. If the last swordspeaker lives quietly among you, bound by an oath of silence—and if prayers suddenly begin to be answered in a valley where the gods have been deaf for centuries—then I ask you: what was the oath *for*? What was so dangerous to say that an entire order of miracle-workers swore themselves mute? And what has changed, after twenty years, to make the silence crack?"

Silence. The real kind, the valley's kind, pressing on all their ears.

Then Vesper Coldharbour moved. Just slightly. Turned from the wall where they'd been leaning and looked at Cassian across the heads of the crowd, and Cassian felt the look like a blade laid flat against his throat—not cutting, but measuring. The ferryman's face was calm. Their eyes were not. There was something in them that Cassian had seen only once before, years ago, in the eyes of a condemned heretic who had laughed on the pyre: the absolute composure of someone who knows something true and terrible and is deciding, minute by minute, whether the world is ready to hear it.

"There's your answer, priest," Coldharbour said, and their voice carried in the hush, low and even. "Ask your god. Oh—" the ghost of something that was almost a smile— "that's right. It doesn't speak here."

And the crowd laughed—nervously, but it laughed, and a few of them glanced at Cassian with something like apology, because the ferryman had ferried their grandmothers and mended their boats and never asked a coin from the desperate, and twenty years of small kindnesses do not dissolve in one evening.

But Cassian had done this work too long to be discouraged by laughter. Laughter was the sound a crowd made while it decided.

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "It doesn't speak here. Which means that everything you've felt these past months—the answered prayers, the mended bones, the salt on the graves—came from somewhere else. Something else. And that something has been very careful, hasn't it? Very selective. It heals a broken arm in public where six can witness. It blooms salt on a stranger's grave where the ferryman alone can see. It gives a hymn to a healer-girl in pieces, a fragment at a time, like a hand feeding crumbs through a fence." He let his gaze rest on Maren, briefly, kindly. "It is training you, good people. The way you'd train anything you intended to use."

The laughter died. The dye spread further.

"And so tomorrow night," Cassian said, raising his voice to fill the square one final time, "I will hold vigil here, in this square, under the terms of my truce—no soldiers, no force, only prayer. I invite every soul in Salt Hollow to come and pray with me, openly, to whatever power has been listening. Come and ask it your questions. Come and test it against the god of the Answered Prayer, which has never once hidden from me. And if it will not show itself—if it stays behind its fence, feeding crumbs through the wire—then ask yourselves why. Ask yourselves who taught it to hide. Ask yourselves what, in this valley, has spent twenty years practicing silence."

He stepped down. The crowd parted for him without meaning to, the way water parts, and he walked through the middle of them toward the chapel Marta had grudgingly offered, and as he passed the gray-coated ferryman he slowed—only slightly, only a heartbeat—and said, under the noise of the dispersing crowd, pitched for one pair of ears alone:

"Nineteen names crossed off my list, Coldharbour. Yours is the last. I didn't cross them out myself, you understand. Someone else did. Someone who wanted the last one left alive." He met the ferryman's eyes. "I've wondered for three years why. Tomorrow night, maybe one of us finds out."

He walked on. Behind him he heard the square emptying, the murmurs starting, the small poisoned conversations of neighbors turning on neighbors—*did you see, did you hear, how long has she been praying, what's in that satchel, what's he been hiding all these years*—

And beneath it all, faint as a pulse, the hole in the world where a god should be, silent as a sealed door.

Cassian smiled at no one, and went in to prepare his vigil.
