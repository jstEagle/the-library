## 9. Loyalty Is a Kind of Lie

Maren had learned to pray the way other people learned to swim—badly, in secret, and always with one hand reaching for something solid.

Her mother had taught her the valley's prayers before she taught her to read. *Salt for the wound. Salt for the road. Salt for the dead so they stay where we put them.* Prayers without gods behind them, her mother said. Prayers like letters you mail to an empty house, because the writing of them is the comfort, not the answer. Maren had believed that the way children believe things: completely, and without ever once testing it.

Then her prayers started working.

It began small—a fever breaking when she asked it to, a birth gone wrong righting itself between one breath and the next. She'd told herself it was coincidence, then skill, then luck. But last night, kneeling at the hearth with her hands still smelling of the poultice she'd packed into old Tam Wicker's chest wound, she had prayed for his pain to ease, and the pain had eased so fast he'd laughed. Laughed. A man with a spear-scar through his lung had *laughed*.

And now there was Vesper Coldharbour, who said the answers were not a blessing but a leak. Who said the valley was a seal, and seals were made to hold something in, and what was held in was pressing against the walls.

She sat on the low wall behind the healing house, watching the morning fog burn off the flats, and tried to feel what Vesper wanted her to feel. Dread. She reached for dread the way you reach for a stone in deep water—knowing its shape, unable to close her hand around it.

Because here was the truth she could not say aloud: the voice that answered her was kind.

Not the way people were kind, with effort and cost. Kind the way warmth is kind. When she prayed, something leaned toward her. Something listened with its whole attention, the way no one ever had—not her mother, worn down by work; not the village elders, who saw her as a pair of useful hands; not even Vesper, whose kindness was real but armored, given grudgingly, wrapped in warnings. The Answered Prayer did not warn her. It simply agreed.

*You are heard,* it seemed to say, every time. *You have always been heard.*

Vesper called that a hook. Maren kept catching herself calling it a homecoming.

* * *

The trouble with Vesper was that Vesper was right about everything except the thing that mattered.

That was the thought Maren kept circling as she walked the brine road toward the ferry landing, a basket of bandages on her hip she barely needed anymore because wounds in the valley had begun closing on their own, politely, as if embarrassed to trouble anyone. Vesper was right that the boundary stones wept. Right that the wardstone bore their order's sigil. Right that the hymn Maren carried in her head like a splinter was forbidden liturgy—she'd watched Vesper's face go gray when she hummed it, gray the way faces go at funerals.

But Vesper also looked at her lately like she was a door someone had left open in a storm. And Vesper had lied to her—or if not lied, then withheld, which in a valley built on silence amounted to the same currency. Twenty years. Twenty years the ferryman had known what slept beneath their feet, and said nothing, and mended nets, and poled travelers across the shallows, and let a whole village live its little life on the lid of a grave.

*You would have too,* said a voice in her that sounded suspiciously reasonable. *If keeping quiet kept everyone safe, wouldn't you?*

She didn't know. That was the worst of it. She'd spent three days trying to imagine herself into Vesper's choice and couldn't do it. Her imagination kept failing at the same place: the moment where you know the truth and swallow it, day after day, year after year, until the swallowing becomes your spine.

Loyalty was supposed to be simple. You saved someone; you stood by them. Vesper had saved her—had pulled her out of the flooded crypt with a grip like iron and a face like the end of the world, and afterward had not mocked her terror or her tears, only said, *Don't come back down there,* which from Vesper was practically tenderness. She owed them. She knew she owed them.

But the Prayer had never asked her to choose anything. That was the difference, and she felt it now like a splinter working deeper: Vesper's love came with conditions—silence, distance, distrust of the warmest thing she'd ever felt—and the Prayer's love came with none. It didn't ask her to be brave or loyal or good. It just asked her to keep talking to it. To keep opening her hands.

A gift with no terms. Or a net with no visible knots.

* * *

By midday the fog had burned off entirely, and the flats lay white and glittering under a sky so clean it looked rinsed. Too clean. There had been rain coming for two days—you could smell it in the peat—and the rain hadn't come, and the salt kept rising anyway, creeping up the pilings of the drying racks in bright crusts, killing the grass in neat circles around each boundary stone.

Maren knelt by the third stone on the pilgrim road and pressed her palm flat against it. It was warm. Stones weren't warm. Not these stones, not in this season, not with the sun still shy of noon.

*What are you?* she whispered against it, because whispering felt safer than asking properly.

The answer came before she finished the question. It always did now, arriving ahead of her words like a dog that knows the walk before the lead comes off.

*I am what was promised. I am what your mothers prayed toward and your fathers forgot. I am the door unbarred, little healer. I am the light under the water.*

She pulled her hand back. The stone's warmth stayed in her palm a moment, then faded, and left her feeling scoured, the way you feel after crying—empty and oddly clean.

*Little healer.* It had never used a name before. Names were dangerous; Vesper had said so, though Vesper hadn't explained why, because Vesper never explained anything all the way through, and every explanation stopped just short of the part that mattered, like a story interrupted at the hinge.

She should tell Vesper about this. About the stone, the warmth, the name it had offered her. She knew she should. She could picture the conversation already: the ferryman's eyes going flat and careful, the questions arriving like customs officers—*When did it start speaking to you directly? What exactly does it call itself? Have you prayed since? Have you prayed since, Maren?*

And she would lie. She already knew she would lie, standing there wanting to be honest, hating herself in advance for the shape of the lie leaving her mouth. Because the alternative was letting Vesper stand between her and the only voice that had ever made her feel less alone, and she wasn't sure she could survive that. Wasn't sure she wanted to try.

*Loyalty is a kind of lie,* Vesper had told her once, back in the crypt's cold aftermath, when she'd sworn she'd never doubt them. *The best ones are. They're lies we agree to tell together so we can stand in the same place.* She'd thought it was wisdom. Now it tasted like a warning aimed at her, or maybe at Vesper themselves, and she couldn't tell which frightened her more.

* * *

She went to find Vesper anyway. Of course she did. That was the whole rotten knot of her: she distrusted the ferryman with everything she had and still walked the length of the flats to stand within arm's reach of them, because some loyalties lived below the level where doubt could dig.

Vesper was on the water, poling the ferry across the shallows with the slow economy of someone who has done one thing ten thousand times. Maren waited on the landing, watching the boat come in, and noticed—for the first time, or maybe for the hundredth and finally admitted it—that Vesper moved wrong for a ferryman. Too balanced. Every shift of weight landed exactly where it intended, as if the body had been trained by something stricter than tides. The scars she'd glimpsed once, at the collar, ran in patterns. Patterns meant practice. Practice meant an order. Orders meant oaths, and oaths meant the truth was a debt Vesper was still paying interest on.

The boat grounded. Vesper looked up, and whatever was on Maren's face made the ferryman stop moving entirely, one hand still on the pole.

"You've spoken to it," Vesper said. Not a question.

"No." The lie arrived smooth and instant, and Maren heard it leave her mouth and hated it and could not take it back. "I came about bandages. We're short."

Vesper studied her for a long moment—the way you study water you're about to cross, checking for current under the calm. Then they nodded slowly and turned to tie off the boat, and said, quietly, to the rope rather than to her:

"When you're ready to tell me the truth, I'll be here. I'm always here. That's the oath."

*That's the oath.* As if the oath were a kindness. As if it weren't also a cage with Vesper inside it, rattling the bars gently, calling the rattle devotion.

Maren walked back up the road with her basket of bandages nobody needed, and the sun was warm, and the salt glittered, and everything was beautiful the way a trap is beautiful when you're standing outside it looking in.

* * *

That evening she did the thing she had promised herself she wouldn't do.

She lit no candle. She knelt nowhere formal. She just sat on her cot in the dark of the healing house, after the last patient, with her hands open in her lap like a beggar's, and she prayed—not the valley's empty-letter prayers, but a real one, addressed, aimed:

*Tell me what Vesper is hiding. Tell me why they look at me like I'm a door. Tell me the truth about the crypt, about the hymn, about what my grandmother's generation really buried under our feet. If you're good, you'll tell me. Only liars keep secrets from the people they love.*

Silence. Real silence, for the first time in weeks—the listening presence withdrawing like a tide, and the room going ordinary and cold around her absence of an answer. She almost wept with relief. See, she thought. See, it refuses. Even it has limits. Even—

Then the answer came, and it came *specific*, and the specificity was the horror.

*Your master keeps four truths behind their teeth. First: the crypt holds not a grave but a mouth, and the mouth is theirs to feed. Second: the sigil on the wardstone is not their order's mark of pride but their mark of guilt—they carved it the night they sealed your countrymen outside the walls to starve as mortar. Third: the hymn you carry is the third verse of five, and the fourth verse is a key, and they will kill to keep you from singing it. Fourth: they have already chosen the day they will use you, and the day is soon, and they have not told you because to them you are not a person. You are a hinge.*

Every word landed like a nail driven true. Not because she believed it—she didn't, not yet—but because it *fit*. It fit the gray look on Vesper's face. It fit the unfinished explanations. It fit the way Vesper had said *don't come back down there* with fear in it, real fear, fear of her, of what she might become or unlock. The lie that works best is ninety percent true, her mother used to say, sorting herbs. Keep the tenth part false and no one checks the rest.

But which ten percent was false?

That was the trap inside the trap, and Maren sat very still in the dark and finally, finally saw it laid out plain: the voice answered her fastest when she asked about Vesper. It hesitated over nothing else—it gave her fevers and rain-signs and dying men's comfort without pause—but ask it about the ferryman and it leapt, eager, detailed, *knowing*, offering specifics no honest power should have: counts of truths, shapes of plans, a day already chosen. It spoke of Vesper the way a hunter speaks of game. Fluent. Familiar. Intimate with the quarry's habits.

An honest god would know Vesper's heart. This thing knew Vesper's *schedule*.

And underneath the knowing, threaded through every generous word, ran one consistent instruction, so gentle she'd never seen it as an instruction until now: *distrust the ferryman. Doubt the oath. Come to me instead.* Every answer steered. Every comfort curved, subtly, like a road bending toward a gate.

Her hands had closed without her noticing. She opened them. Her palms were damp.

"Who are you?" she whispered into the dark. "Really. Not the pretty name. The one underneath."

The warmth gathered again, patient, unsurprised—as if it had been waiting for exactly this question, as if the question itself were a step on a path it had measured long ago.

*I am the one your masters locked away,* it said, tender as a lullaby, *and I am the one they lied about. Ask yourself, little healer: if I am the monster, why does only the monster tell you the truth?*

And that was the sentence that broke something open in her—because it was a good sentence, a *true-feeling* sentence, and she could feel it doing its work inside her, prying at the seam between her loyalty and her sense, and she understood with sudden cold clarity that she was not being persuaded.

She was being *farmed*.

Somewhere out on the flats, a boundary stone wept brine into the dark. Somewhere across the water, a ferryman who had kept twenty years of terrible silence was probably awake too, staring at a ceiling, carrying the same war inside them from the other side. And somewhere beyond the valley's edge, Brother Cassian was preaching to hungry villages about a god who answered everything—which meant the voice in her dark little room had more than one listener, more than one name, more than one door.

It wasn't answering her prayers.

It was *collecting* her.

Maren lay down in the dark and did not sleep, and near dawn, when the first gray light found the window, she made herself a promise with the taste of salt still on her teeth: tomorrow she would find Vesper, and she would tell the truth—the whole truth, the specific truth, including the parts that made Vesper look guilty and the parts that made her look foolish, all of it, spilled at once like a dropped basket.

Because if the thing in the dark knew four secrets Vesper kept, then it knew a fifth.

And she was going to make Vesper tell her what it was—even if the telling broke the oath, even if the breaking broke them both.

She closed her eyes as the light came up, and the last thing she heard, faint and warm at the edge of hearing, was the voice saying, kindly, as if it had read her mind:

*Yes. Go to them. Bring me what you learn.*
