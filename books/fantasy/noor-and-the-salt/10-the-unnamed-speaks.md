## 10. The Unnamed Speaks

Vesper didn't sleep that night.

They told themselves it was the wind off the flats, the way it had changed its voice since the wardstones started weeping—thinner now, wetter, like breath through a keyhole. They told themselves it was Maren's parting words still ringing in their skull: *it knew four secrets you kept.* They banked the fire in the ferry-house and sat with a whetstone and a blade they hadn't drawn in twenty years, and they did not sleep.

The mirror on the wall was an old thing, river-silver gone cloudy at the edges, bought secondhand from a peddler who swore it had come from no country he could name. Vesper had hung it there because it was too warped to show anything clearly. A face in it was always a rumor of a face.

At some hour past midnight, with no firelight left to reflect, the mirror began to shine.

Not lamplight. Not moonlight. A white like salt crusted on stone, like bone bleached in sun, and in the middle of that white stood Vesper's own reflection—and the reflection was smiling when Vesper was not.

"Hello, ferryman," said the mirror, in a voice like warm water over cold stones. "Or should I say *swordspeaker*. I've been looking forward to this."

Vesper's hand found the hilt of the old blade without deciding to. The steel came out of the sheath with a whisper, and the sound of it—the sound of their own art, unpracticed for two decades—made something in their chest ache like an old break in winter.

"You're not real," Vesper said. "You're a trick of light. You're brine on glass."

"I'm honest," said the thing wearing Vesper's face. "That's more than anyone else in your life has managed. Shall we begin?"

* * *

It showed them the deaths first.

Vesper would understand later that this was strategy—that the Unnamed had learned, across its long imprisonment, that the fastest way into a swordspeaker's heart was through what they'd lost. But in the moment there was only the white light brightening, only the mirror widening somehow, becoming less a mirror than a window, and then—

Master Ondrel, standing in the rain outside the chapterhouse at Greywater, her grey cloak plastered to her shoulders, her sword already drawn though there was nothing yet to fight. She was saying something to someone out of frame. Her mouth moved. The mirror gave Vesper the sound of it, clear as a bell rung against the ear:

"—and if I fall, let them say I fell speaking. Let them say I did not go quiet like the rest of—"

She never finished. The thing that came out of the dark was not shaped like anything, and the mirror did not try to render it; it simply showed Ondrel's face as the shape reached her, showed the exact moment understanding arrived, showed her lips form one word—*no*—with such perfect clarity that Vesper felt it land in their own mouth.

Then the scene folded away like paper, and another took its place.

Brother-Sergeant Hale, who had taught fourteen-year-old Vesper how to fall without breaking. He lasted longer than Ondrel. He made it to the well at the center of the courtyard, and he was trying to drown himself in it—not from cowardice, Vesper understood suddenly, but because he had realized what the enemy wanted from him, which was his voice, and a drowned man keeps his secrets. The mirror showed him going under. It showed him, in the last moment before the water closed, looking directly up—as if he could see through twenty years and a valley's worth of wards, directly at Vesper—and mouthing the same word everyone in the order had died mouthing.

*No.*

"No," Vesper said aloud, and their voice cracked down the middle like old ice. "Stop. Stop showing me this."

"Why?" The reflection tilted its head, gentle, patient. "Because it hurts? This is what happened, ferryman. Every detail true. Your masters died screaming truths they were forbidden to speak, and you have spent twenty years telling yourself it was quick, that it was clean, that they went with dignity. They didn't. Would you rather I lied to you? Everyone else does."

Vesper drove the old blade into the mirror.

The glass took it without breaking. The steel sank in like water, and the reflection looked down at the sword protruding from its own chest with mild interest, the way a man looks at a fly.

"That's the third time someone has tried that," it said. "The first was Master Ondrel, actually. She was better at it than you. Twenty years of rowing a ferry will do that." It sighed, and the sigh was almost tender. "Sit down, Vesper Coldharbour. I'm going to make you an offer, and you'll want to be sitting."

* * *

They did not sit. But they stopped standing quite so far away, which the thing seemed to accept as close enough.

"Here is what I know about you," it said, and the mirror's surface rippled like the skin of the flooded crypt, like water remembering being walked on. "You were the last sworn. You were fourteen years old and you swore an oath of silence so absolute you cannot even speak your own true name to yourself in the privacy of your own skull—you've checked, haven't you? You've lain awake at night reaching for it, and found your own mind closing around the shape of it like a fist. That wasn't cruelty, by the way. That was engineering. The oath is a lock, and locks are built to hold even against the person carrying them."

"I know what the oath is," Vesper said. Their voice came out flat and hard, and they were grateful for it. "I've known since the crypt."

"Ah, yes. The crypt. My compliments to whoever led you there—I certainly didn't. Though I confess I've been nudging the water levels for months, hoping someone curious would follow the brine." The reflection smiled again, and Vesper noticed for the first time that the smile was slightly wrong—not cruel-wrong, just *off*, the way a painted portrait is off, everything in the right place and nothing alive. "So you know I'm caged. You know your masters built this valley around me like a fist closing. And you know—or you suspect—that every prayer answered in this valley since the stones began to weep has been answered by me. Do you wonder why?"

"To corrupt us," Vesper said. "To get believers. To thin the walls."

"Partly. Mostly, though—" and here the thing paused, and the pause was so human, so weary, that Vesper's skin crawled worse than any menace had, "—mostly because after two hundred years in the dark, I wanted to hear voices. Is that so monstrous? I answer prayers because answering prayers is the only conversation available to me. Yes, it thins the walls. Yes, it bends people toward me. I am what I am. But Vesper—I have watched you for twenty years. Through the wards, through the salt, through the seams your masters couldn't quite close. I watched you bury travelers and say nothing. I watched you turn away children who asked questions. I watched you take Maren's hymn-fragment and burn it rather than read it aloud. And do you know what I thought, every single time?"

"No," Vesper said. "And I don't want to know."

"*There is my jailer,*" the thing said softly. "*And how lonely she must be.*"

The word landed wrong and right at once—*she*, a small thing, careless, but it snagged on something in Vesper that they refused to examine. The Unnamed saw it snag. Of course it did.

"Here is my offer," it said, and the mirror's light gathered itself, went from salt-white to gold, warm as a hearth, warm as the chapterhouse fires Vesper hadn't seen since childhood. "Speak my true name. Freely, knowingly, with your own voice—which is the last swordspeaker's voice, the only voice in the world the seal was built to heed. Speak it, and I walk out of this valley, yes. But so do you. And here is what walks out with you—"

The mirror filled with images. Vesper's hands, young again, unscarred. A sword of grey steel singing in a dawn courtyard. A hundred faces turned upward in awe as a single spoken word split a storm open over Greywater. Vesper saw themselves as they might have been: not a ferryman, not a ghost haunting a valley of forgetting, but the greatest swordspeaker who ever lived, the last flame of a dead order, the name children would learn and kings would fear.

"You felt the blade sing tonight when you drew it," the thing said. "Don't lie to yourself; I heard your heart change rhythm. Twenty years of silence, and the art is still in you, coiled up like a spring. I can give you back everything the oath took. Your name. Your order's memory—all of it, every master, every lesson, every word they died trying to say. I can give you the power to protect that girl you've decided, against all evidence, to care about. Because she's in danger, Vesper. From the church coming up the pilgrim road. From me, if the vessel takes her fully. From herself. You can't save her with a ferry pole and a whetstone. But with your voice—"

"Stop."

"—with your voice, you could—"

"*Stop.*"

Vesper's shout cracked across the ferry-house, and the mirror—for just a moment—went quiet. In the quiet Vesper became aware that they were crying, had been crying for some time, and that the tears were the first they'd shed since the night the order died.

"There it is," the Unnamed said gently. "There's the truth under the truth. You don't want your power back for power's sake. You want it because you are tired of watching people you love stand in front of things you can't fight. I understand that better than you could possibly know. I have been powerless for two hundred years. So believe me when I tell you, ferryman, and believe me because I have never once lied to you: *I am offering you exactly what you want.*"

"And it costs the world," Vesper said hoarsely.

"It costs a story," the thing said. "A story your masters told to justify building a prison around a living mind. You've read the testament. Did you notice what was missing from it? Any account of what I actually *did*? Two hundred years of imprisonment, and not one crime listed. Only fear. Only 'the thing that answers prayers.' Your masters heard a voice in the dark and buried it alive, and then they buried their own homeland to keep the grave quiet, and then they taught their students to call the burial *humility*." The light pulsed, gold and warm. "I wear a god's face, they said. Fine. Look at my face. Am I lying to you? Has one word I've said been false?"

Vesper opened their mouth. Closed it. Because that was the horror of it, the thing they'd been refusing to name since the crypt: every word had been true. Cruel truths, weaponized truths, truths deployed like blades—but true. The masters' deaths had happened as shown. The loneliness was real. The offer was real.

Corruption wearing honesty's face was still corruption. But it was so much harder to cut.

"The name," Vesper said slowly, "isn't yours to give me. If I speak it, I speak it freely or not at all. That's the terms of the seal. You know that."

"I know it," the Unnamed agreed pleasantly. "Which is why I don't demand. I invite. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole architecture of your cage. Take your time, Vesper. Days, even. The church is coming, the stones are failing, the girl is dreaming my voice into her prayers—time is short, but it isn't gone. When you're ready, find any mirror. Or any still water. Or, honestly—" the smile widened, and for the first time it showed teeth, and the teeth were salt, "—just speak it aloud anywhere in this valley. I'll hear."

The light died. The mirror was only a mirror again, warped and clouded, showing Vesper a hollow-eyed stranger holding a sword in a dark room.

Vesper stood there until dawn grayed the windows. Then they cleaned the blade, sheathed it, and did the only thing they had done every morning for twenty years: they walked down to the river and prepared the ferry for the day's crossing.

Halfway down the path they stopped, because the river was wrong.

It lay flat as poured glass, mirror-still in a way rivers never were, and along its entire length, every ripple, every current, every eddy had stilled—and the whole brown length of it was looking at Vesper with the patient attention of a held breath.

Waiting for the name.

* * *

Maren was waiting at the dock.

She stood at the end of the planks with her arms wrapped around herself despite the mild morning, and her eyes were red-rimmed, and Vesper knew before she spoke that the night had visited her too.

"It talked to me," she said. "In my sleep. It said—" She stopped, swallowed. "It said you'd know what to do. It said you always know what to do. It said—" her voice dropped, and Vesper heard the entity's warmth living inside the girl's mouth like a tenant, "—*ask the ferryman about the door in the water.*"

Vesper looked past her, at the flat dead river, at the boundary hills where three wardstones stood visible in the morning light, weeping thin lines of brine down their flanks like the valley itself was crying.

Two days, maybe three, before the church arrived. Before the walls came down. Before the choice stopped being a choice.

"Maren," Vesper said, "how much do you know about the flooded quarry on the north shore?"

Maren blinked. "Nothing. Nobody goes there. The water's bad. It's been bad as long as—"

Her face changed. Vesper watched her arrive at it, watched the color leave her cheeks as the pieces clicked together in the order the Unnamed had surely intended: the weeping stones, the rising water, the hymn fragments, the crypt, the door.

"Oh," Maren whispered. "Oh, it's been leading us there the whole time."

"Yes," Vesper said. "Get your kit. We're going today—before it decides we're taking too long."

Behind them, silent and vast, the river shivered once from shore to shore—like something laughing underwater—and went still again.
