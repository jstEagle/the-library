## 11. Wardfall

**Vesper**

The stone cracked at dawn, and Vesper heard it in their teeth.

They were on the river, mid-crossing, the ferry pole sunk to the shoulder in green water, when a sound came up through the current—deep, mineral, like a mountain clearing its throat. The water shivered. Not rippled: *shivered*, as if it had been asleep for twenty years and something had just spoken its name.

Vesper had spent two decades learning the valley's silences. This was not one of them.

They poled hard for the far bank and stood there, boots in the mud, watching the eastern ridge where the first wardstone stood—the old one, the one they'd found weeping brine, its sigil worn to a ghost of itself. Even from the river they could see the light coming off it now. Not sunlight. Sunlight didn't pulse.

*It's failing,* Vesper thought, with the strange calm that had carried them through every disaster of their life. *Or it's being opened.*

Both possibilities ended the same way.

They ran.

Through the village square, past the bakery where Toma was already standing in his doorway with flour on his hands, staring east. Past the well, where the water had gone white—not clouded, *white*, salt rising through the aquifer like blood through a bandage. Children were crouched at the well's edge, dipping fingers, tasting, laughing. Vesper wanted to scream at them to stop. Didn't have time.

"Maren." They said it aloud, uselessly, to no one. She lived on the far side of the village, near the healing house. Near the second wardstone.

The ground bucked under their feet. Not an earthquake—an *adjustment*, the whole valley settling like a man shifting in chains. And then, rolling down from the ridge, across the fields, through the open windows of every house:

Singing.

No. Not singing. That was what it sounded like to people who'd never heard real singing. To Vesper it sounded like the order's choir hall before the end—forty voices trained to hold a single note until the note became a place you could stand inside. Except this voice was one voice, multiplied wrong, coming from everywhere, from under the ground, from the salt itself.

*You kept my house so well,* it said, in no direction at all. *Twenty years. I remember every kindness.*

"Shut up," Vesper said, running, and knew how it sounded—a ferryman shouting at the sky—and did not care.

**Maren**

Maren felt the change come into her hands first.

She was binding a cut on Joss's forearm—he'd fallen off a hay cart, nothing, a scratch—and the wound closed under her fingers faster than it should have. Faster than salve. Faster than anything. She pulled her hand back and watched the skin knit, smooth and pink, and Joss laughed and said, "You're getting good, Maren," and she couldn't answer because her hands were glowing.

Faintly. Like light through a closed eyelid.

"No," she said. "No, no—" Because she had spent three weeks telling herself the prayers were coincidence, that the hymn fragments in her head were just a song, that the thing answering her in the dark behind her eyes was her own conscience wearing a kind voice. But her hands did not lie. Her hands were full of light that had never come from her before, and outside the window the sky over the eastern ridge was pulsing like a heartbeat, and somewhere beneath everything, patient as tide, the warm presence stirred and said:

*It's time, little healer. Open the door.*

"I don't know how."

*You do. You've known since the first prayer. Sing me the rest of the hymn.*

"It's not finished. I don't *have* the rest—"

*You do.* And the warmth pressed closer, tender, terrible, and Maren understood with a lurch that it wasn't asking. It had never been asking. Every answered prayer had been a key turned a little further in a lock, and she had held out her hand each time and called it grace.

The door of the healing house banged open. Vesper stood there, chest heaving, river mud to the knees, and their face—Maren had seen Vesper calm through a drowning, calm through a fever, calm through everything the valley could throw at a body—but their face now was the face of someone reading their own death warrant.

"Away from the windows," Vesper said. "Now. Is anyone else here?"

"Just Joss. His arm—I healed it, Vesper, I didn't mean to, it just—"

"I know." Vesper crossed the room in three strides and took her by the shoulders, and their grip was shaking. That frightened her more than the sky did. "Listen to me. The wards are going. All of them, maybe today. When they go, things come through that door that your prayers will not stop—your prayers are *why* the door is opening. Do you understand? Everything that's been answering you is on the other side of that stone, and it is about to be on this side of it."

"The hymn—"

"Forget the hymn." But Vesper stopped. Looked at her. Something moved behind their eyes, quick and pained, like a fish under ice. "Unless you've remembered more of it."

"Some. Fragments. It comes when it wants to."

Vesper closed their eyes for one breath. Two. When they opened them, they said, quietly, like a confession: "Then keep them. Whatever happens next, whatever I say or don't say—keep them. They might be the only true words left in this valley."

Outside, the first wardstone shattered.

The sound was not loud so much as *total*—it passed through the walls, through the floor, through the bones of everyone in the valley at once, and every glass jar in the healing house rang one clear note and held it. Through the window Maren saw the light on the ridge collapse inward, fold into itself like a star dying in reverse, and then—

Color.

That was the only word her mind offered. Color poured down off the ridge and across the fields, a flood without water, and where it touched the grass the grass grew knee-high in seconds and flowered and seeded and died and grew again. Where it touched the well, the white water began to glow. Where it touched old Halvard's goat pen, the goats spoke.

One of them said, in a dry, reasonable voice, "Oh, *finally*," and Maren made a sound she would be embarrassed about for the rest of her life.

And over the hills, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of horns.

**Cassian**

Brother Cassian heard the ward fall from two miles out, and knelt in the road to give thanks, and hated himself for how much the thanks cost him.

*There it is,* said the voice, warm as banked coals at the base of his skull. *My door. My faithful servant. Bring them in before the swordspeaker runs.*

"The soldiers are ready," Cassian said. Around him, fifty men of the Church's Third Penitent Company checked straps and blade-hilts in silence. They had marched nine days from the coast. They had been promised miracles. What they'd gotten was Cassian's sermons, and the voice that only he heard, and a list of names folded against his heart.

He drew the list out now. Unfolded it in the morning light.

Every name crossed out in red except one. He had crossed them out himself, town by town, over four years—old men in poorhouses, a woman selling charms in a border market, a child who had never even been marked, crossed out because the voice insisted thoroughness and Cassian had learned not to ask what happened after he reported an address. One name left. One name the voice said with something almost like hunger.

*Vesper Coldharbour.*

"He'll run," Cassian said. "When the wards fall, he'll run. Twenty years he's hidden. A cornered animal—"

*He won't run yet.* The voice was serene. *He has someone to carry.*

Cassian looked at the name a long moment. Then he thought—as he thought every day now, in the small traitor hours—of Elodie. Of the year of silence while she burned with fever, his prayers going up like sparks into wet thatch. Of the night the Answered Prayer first spoke to him, and the terrible mercy of finally being *heard*. Of the price, which he had paid in installments, each one reasonable, each one final.

He knew the voice was wrong. He had known for a year. Knowing changed nothing; that was the horror of it. Silence had been a desert. This was water, and the water was poisoned, and he drank because thirst was the only self he had left.

"Sound the advance," he said, and stood, and led his soldiers toward the burning valley.

---

**Vesper**

The village burned from the inside out.

Not all at once—that was the nightmare of it. First the church roof, struck by a lance of blue fire that fell out of a clear sky, and the villagers forming a bucket line with the dumb heroism of people who still believed this was an accident. Then the granary, when the grain itself woke—Vesper saw wheat rise out of the sacks like a swarm, golden and hissing, and old Toma go at it with a flail, shouting, until the wheat took him too and Vesper looked away.

And then the soldiers came through the western pass, and the burning acquired purpose.

Vesper fought them in the lane outside the healing house with a ferry pole, because twenty years of hiding meant no sword, and the pole lasted exactly long enough to break two spears and a jaw before a shield-boss caught them across the temple and the world went white and sideways. They went down in the mud. A soldier raised his blade.

The soldier froze.

Not hesitated—*froze*, mid-motion, and Vesper, bleeding into one eye, saw why: the air around the man had gone thick and shining, and in the shine stood shapes. Tall ones. Wearing the faces of the dead. The soldier screamed once, a short torn sound, and fled, dropping his sword, and all along the lane Vesper could see it happening—soldiers breaking and running from things only they could see, and among the fleeing villagers, some few standing perfectly still with expressions of unbearable peace as the color flooded into them and lifted them off the ground like offerings.

The valley was eating both armies. It didn't care which.

*Maren,* Vesper thought, and got up.

The healing house was already alight when they reached it. Through the smoke in the doorway: Maren, backed against the far wall, arms around Joss, and filling the doorway opposite her—filling it *wrong*, too tall for the frame, edges smoking—stood a shape in the general shape of a man.

It turned its head to Vesper, and it wore the face of Master Ellery, dead nineteen years, smiling with dead patience.

"You came," it said, in Ellery's voice, warm as a hearth. "I hoped you would. I've missed your silence most of all, little one. It's the loudest thing in this valley."

Vesper's body moved before their mind did. Later they would understand that; later they would take it apart, examine it, decide whether it was courage or cowardice or simple animal refusal. In the moment there was only this: the thing between them and Maren, the beam overhead groaning, the roof coming down in fire, and one word in their chest that they had sworn at fourteen years old, kneeling in salt, never to speak.

Swordspeak. The true art. Words that are acts.

The oath forbade it absolutely. The oath had been the lock, and the masters dead, and the world ending, and Maren's face in the smoke turning toward Vesper with no fear in it at all, only trust, which was worse than fear, which was unbearable—

Vesper spoke.

"*Aldh skerren vael—*" and the words came out of twenty years of silence like a blade leaving a body, and the world *obeyed*. The fire bent away from Maren and Joss, a corridor of clean air carved through the inferno. The thing in the doorway recoiled, Ellery's face flickering through a dozen others, surprised for the first time. The falling beam stopped falling. The very heat stood aside.

Three words. That was all Vesper managed before the oath itself rose up in them like a wall—the oath was not a rule, they realized, gasping, it was a *structure*, it had weight, it had been built into their bones at fourteen and it fought back—and the third word tore loose half-spoken and the corridor of air collapsed and the ceiling came down in a roar of ember and slate.

Vesper caught Maren's wrist. Joss's collar. Dragged them both through the burning doorway into the yard, into the salt-white light, into the noise.

Behind them the healing house folded. Ahead of them the village burned. Above them the second wardstone, visible now over the rooftops, was cracking like ice in spring, hairline fractures crawling across it, shedding light instead of dust.

And down the western road, banners moving through the smoke, the Church's soldiers were regrouping, and at their head rode a thin man in gray who was not looking at the burning village at all.

He was looking at Vesper.

"There," Maren coughed, clutching Joss, her eyes streaming. "Vesper—you *spoke*—I heard it, the words, they were—"

"Don't." Vesper's throat was raw, as if the oath had scoured it on the way out. Their hands wouldn't stop shaking. Twenty years of silence, broken in three words, and the valley had noticed—the salt wind had *turned* to listen—and somewhere beneath the ground something ancient had just revised its plans. "Don't thank me. Don't ever thank me for that. There's a road out of here, the old pilgrim road, north along the brine flats. We're taking it. Now. Before that stone finishes falling and before that man in gray finishes crossing the square."

"What's on the brine road?"

Vesper looked once at the burning village—at Toma's bakery, at the well where the children had tasted the water, at everything they had guarded by doing nothing for twenty years—and found they had no answer that wasn't a lie, and had just broken one oath today already.

"I don't know," they said. "But it's the only direction left that isn't behind us."

The second wardstone sang one high, clear, breaking note.

And they ran.
