## 14. Hymn for the Drowned Order

Maren had learned the hymn the way she'd learned everything else in her life: in pieces, from people who didn't know they were teaching.

A phrase from Old Sefa, who used to hum it while grinding feverroot. A line from a funeral, sung by a woman whose name Maren had never caught. Four bars she'd found herself humming at nine years old and could not remember learning. She'd spent years collecting them the way other children collected river stones—without purpose, without thought, just because they fit together in her mouth.

She had eleven fragments now. She'd counted them on the road out of the valley, on the nights when Vesper slept and the brine wind kept her awake. Eleven fragments, and for the first time, sitting in the archivist's flooded archive with a candle guttering against the damp, she laid them all end to end.

It should have been simple. It was not.

The trouble was that the hymn didn't want to be finished. Every time she sang it through, it stopped short of itself, like a stair missing its last step. Her voice would climb toward something—a resolution, a landing—and find nothing there. Just air, and the taste of salt, and the sense that somewhere far below her, something enormous was listening through the floor of the world.

"You've been at that for hours." The archivist stood in the doorway, a coil of gray hair pinned up with what appeared to be an actual fishhook. Ondrey. He'd introduced himself three days ago as "the last man who remembers countries that no longer exist," and he said it the way other men said *I own a farm*.

"I'm missing the ending," Maren said.

"Most things are." He came in anyway, dragging a stool with one hand and a leather folio with the other. He set both down among the maps—the drowned coastlines, the erased borders, the country called Coldharbour Reach that existed only here, inked in faded brown. "But endings are my trade. Show me what you have."

She almost refused. She'd been refusing for days, keeping the hymn folded inside her where even Vesper couldn't reach it, because it was hers, because it was the first thing in twenty years of quiet that had ever felt like it belonged to her specifically, that had chosen her instead of the other way around. But the stair was still missing its step. And she was tired of falling.

So she sang it. All eleven fragments, in the order they wanted to go, which was not the order she'd collected them.

The candle flame leaned toward her as she sang. Not away. Toward. She hadn't noticed that before, and now she would never unnotice it.

Ondrey listened with his eyes closed, his lips moving slightly, following along. When she reached the break—the place where the hymn fell off into nothing—he opened his eyes and said, quietly, "Again."

She sang it again. This time he hummed beneath her at the break, low, four notes that weren't in any fragment she knew, and the hymn *leaned* into them, the way a leaning person leans into a shoulder, and for half a breath the whole thing hung complete in the wet air—

—and then it collapsed, because he stopped.

"Why did you stop?" Her voice came out sharper than she meant it to.

"Because I don't know what comes after those four notes," Ondrey said. "Only where to find it."

* * *

Where to find it was forty feet below the archive, down a spiral stair that predated the valley, predated maybe everything, in a chamber Ondrey called the listening room. He carried the candle. Maren carried the folio, because he said the folio went where she went now, though he wouldn't say why.

The chamber was round and dry despite being sunk in earth, and its walls were covered in script—not written, Maren realized after a moment, but *sung into* the stone. The letters were grooved at varying depths, and when she ran her fingers over them they felt less like carving than like sound made solid. Sound that had been poured in while the stone was soft.

"What is this place?"

"A chapel," Ondrey said. "Of the kind that isn't built anymore. Before your Church of Answers, before the old gods went quiet, there were orders who believed that a song could hold weight. That words, rightly arranged, could bear more than their meaning." He touched the wall almost tenderly. "This is a hymnal. Sung into stone so it couldn't be burned, couldn't be forgotten, couldn't be *corrected*."

Maren's skin prickled. "Whose?"

"Read the signature."

There was a signature. Of course there was. Carved deep at the base of the eastern wall, larger than the rest, worn by centuries of hands touching it: a sword crossed over a wave, and beneath it, in the old liturgical script she'd seen only once before—on the wardstone, on Vesper's face when they spoke of the dead—

*The Order of the Salted Blade.*

Her knees went loose. She sat down hard on the chamber's single bench without deciding to.

"That's—" She stopped. Started again. "Vesper's order. The swordspeakers."

"The same," Ondrey said. "Though I doubt they'd claim this one. This is early work. This is from before the valley. From before the *oath*."

"The oath of silence."

"The oath of silence," he agreed, "which everyone believes was always an oath of silence. Which is what makes it such good work." He set the candle on the floor and straightened, and for a moment the old man seemed taller than his stoop allowed. "Sing me your fragments again, girl. Here. In this room. And watch the walls."

She sang.

And the walls answered.

Not with sound—with *light*. As her voice moved through the fragments, the grooved letters filled faintly with luminescence, each phrase glowing in sequence, a trail of cold fire following her voice around the circular room. Fragment one, the wall behind the door. Fragment two, the wall left of it. She turned as she sang, following the glow, and the glow led her, and she understood suddenly why the room was round: the hymn was meant to be walked.

At the break—the missing stair—the light stopped. The last lit fragment pulsed once, twice, expectant.

"Those four notes you hummed," she said. "They're the next verse. Where's the rest of it?"

Ondrey pointed at the floor.

Maren looked down. The stone beneath the bench, beneath her feet, was carved too—but differently. Deeper. The characters down there were older, cruder, cut rather than sung, and they spiraled inward toward the center of the chamber like water going down a drain.

"The ending isn't part of the hymn," Ondrey said. "The ending is what the hymn was *for*."

* * *

She knelt on the cold stone and read.

It took her a long time. The old script fought her; half the characters were cognates of the liturgical forms she knew and half were something earlier, something that predated the Church, predated maybe language itself, or at least language as the Church permitted it. But Ondrey helped, translating slowly, and piece by piece the spiral gave itself up.

It was instructions. That was the horror of it—that was the first horror, and she would later understand it was the smallest. The spiral was *instructions*, precise as a recipe, for how to finish the hymn. Which notes closed the fourth verse. Which syllable bound the fifth. How many repetitions. What the singer must hold in mind at the final cadence.

And then, at the center of the spiral, in characters cut deepest of all:

*A cage is a song sung completely.*

Maren read it six times. The candle burned. Somewhere above them, water dripped through the ruined archive at long intervals, patient as a clock.

"It's not worship," she said finally. Her voice sounded wrong in her own ears, flattened, like someone else speaking through her mouth. "The hymn. It was never a prayer."

"No."

"It's a binding. The whole thing. Every fragment—" She was counting now, frantically, her healer's mind sorting symptoms into patterns. "Every fragment I've ever heard. Sefa grinding her herbs. The woman at the funeral. The four bars I couldn't remember learning. They're not pieces of a song. They're pieces of a *cage*. Scattered. Deliberately scattered. Sung into the valley generation after generation until nobody knew what they were singing anymore."

"Until the singers forgot," Ondrey said, "but the song didn't."

Maren pressed her palm flat against the floor, against the deepest-cut characters, as if she could hold them down. "How much of it do I know? How close am I?"

Ondrey crouched beside her, and his face, in the candlelight, held none of the archivist's detachment. He looked frightened. She realized with a distant, floating surprise that he was frightened *of her answer*.

"You know the opening verses entire," he said. "You know the fourth verse except its final cadence. And you know—" he hesitated— "you know the closing invocation. The last lines. You've known them since childhood, haven't you? The ones that feel like coming home."

The ones that felt like coming home. Yes. There was a passage at the hymn's heart—or where its heart would be, if she completed it—that she had never been able to sing without tears, without the sensation of a door opening somewhere inside her chest. She'd assumed it was beauty. She'd assumed it was grief for something she'd lost before she could remember losing it.

"When did you learn the closing invocation?" Ondrey asked gently.

"I told you. I don't remember learning it. It's just *there*."

"That's because it wasn't taught to you, child. The closing invocation can't be taught. It has to be *carried*. Passed down in the blood, or the bone, or whatever part of us holds songs we don't choose." He rose, joints cracking, and stood looking down at her with terrible pity. "Your family were wardens. Valley-born, valley-bred, back further than the valley keeps records. The hymn was seeded into families like yours so that if the seal ever weakened—if the thing beneath ever stirred—someone would be able to sing the cage shut again without knowing they could. Without the Church finding them. Without the *god* finding them."

"And now it's stirring," Maren whispered.

"And now it's stirring. And now you've been singing pieces of it since you were a child. And now—" Ondrey spread his hands— "the prayers have started being answered, and every miracle weakens the seal, and the seal was always, underneath everything, a *song*."

Maren stood. Her legs held. That surprised her.

"So my prayers," she said. "When I prayed for the ferryman's fever to break. When I prayed for rain. When I prayed for—" she couldn't say Vesper's name, couldn't say what she'd prayed for on the night the soldiers came— "those prayers worked. Because the thing answering them wanted the cage sung *loose*. It heard me carrying the binding and it—it answered me so I'd keep singing. So I'd trust it. So I'd sing *more*."

"Or," Ondrey said softly, "it answered you because it loves you, in the way a prisoner loves the lock that might someday rust. Both things can be true. With gods, both things are usually true."

The candle flame leaned toward her again. She watched it lean, and understood at last what the flame was reaching for: the closing invocation sleeping in her chest. Everything in this drowned world leaned toward her the way iron leans toward a lodestone. She had spent her whole life thinking she was ordinary. She had spent her whole life being the most dangerous object in the room.

"How do I finish it?" she asked. "If I need to. If the seal breaks. Can I finish it?"

"You could," Ondrey said. "That's the cruelty of it. The cage-song needs a carrier willing to sing it whole, and the singing will hollow you—it will take every song you'll ever carry, every word you'll ever love, and pour them into the stone. You'd live. You'd walk out of here. And you'd never again be able to pray, or sing, or say the word for the color of the sea, because the cage takes language itself as payment." He paused. "The masters who sealed the valley paid that price. All of them. That's why they swore silence. Not humility. Payment."

Twenty years. Vesper's twenty silent years. Maren thought of them standing at the ferry rope, saying so little, watching everything, hoarding words like a miser hoards coins they're forbidden to spend—and she understood, finally, what the silence had cost and why Vesper paid it daily without complaint.

And underneath the understanding, colder: *and I'm already halfway hollow, aren't I. I've been singing the cage in pieces my whole life. How much of me is already in the walls?*

She looked down at the spiral. At the instruction for the final cadence. *What the singer must hold in mind:* the old script said, and then a word she translated twice to be sure, because she wanted it to mean something else.

*Home.* The singer must hold home in mind.

She had no home. The valley had been a lie. The Church had burned the village. The archive was a cellar full of dead countries. And yet the word fit her chest like a key fits a lock, and she hated it, and she sang the fourth verse's missing cadence under her breath, just once, just to see if she could—

—and every candle in the archive went out at once, forty feet overhead, and the drip of water stopped, and in the perfect silence Maren felt something beneath the world shift its weight, slow and vast, the way a sleeper turns toward a familiar voice.

Ondrey gripped her arm hard enough to bruise. "Don't," he breathed. "Not here. Not alone. Never alone."

Above them, the candles relit themselves, one by one, in reverse order, as if time were being politely rewound.

Maren's hands were shaking. She let them shake. Innocence, she understood, was not something you lost all at once; it left the way a tide leaves, in stages, each withdrawal showing you another stretch of seabed you'd been walking on all along without knowing what was underneath.

She had been walking on a cage her whole life. She had been the cage. Or the keeper of it. Or its choir.

"We have to find Vesper," she said.

"They went to the shore," Ondrey said. "To look at the door."

"Then we go to the shore." She took the folio. She took the candle. At the foot of the spiral stair she paused, one hand against the stone, and felt—faintly, unmistakably, the way you feel a second heartbeat when you press your wrist to the right throat—the hymn continuing without her. Rising from the valley behind them, from hundreds of throats in the burning village square where Cassian's faithful had gathered, the fragments rising off a thousand tongues that didn't know what they carried, the congregation singing the Answered Prayer's new liturgy, which was her hymn, which was the cage, which was being sung *wrong*—sung open, sung outward, sung as invitation.

Sung, note by note, as a key.

"Oh," Maren said, to no one, to the stone, to the turning sleeper beneath the world. "Oh, he's making them sing it backwards."

She ran for the stairs.
