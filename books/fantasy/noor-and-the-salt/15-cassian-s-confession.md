## 15. Cassian's Confession

They took his vestments first.

Brother Cassian had expected that. The gray robes of the Answered Prayer were not a garment but an argument, and the men who bound his wrists with salt-stiff cord understood arguments better than they understood mercy. They stripped him to his undertunic in the back of a supply wagon as it rattled along the brine road, and Deacon Halloway—Halloway, who had wept at Cassian's ordination eleven years ago, who had held the basin while Cassian washed the feet of lepers—stood over him with a lantern and did not look away.

"You wrote it," Halloway said. "The letter. You wrote *the voice is wrong*."

"I wrote that I have doubts." Cassian's wrists ached where the cord bit. He kept his voice level, the way he'd learned to keep it through sermons, through funerals, through the long years before. "Doubt is not heresy. Doubt is the elbow grease of faith. The Liturgy of Questions says—"

"The Liturgy of Questions was written by a man the Voice answered personally." Halloway crouched so they were eye to eye. In the lantern light his face was all planes and shadow, a face built for conviction. "You heard it too, Brother. Don't pretend you didn't. At the valley's edge, when the wardstone cracked—you fell to your knees like the rest of us. You *wept*."

Cassian said nothing. That was answer enough, and Halloway smiled the terrible smile of a man who has caught his teacher sinning and loves him anyway, which is the worst way to be loved.

"We're taking you to the Chapterhouse at Saltmere," Halloway went on, standing. "There will be a hearing. Confess the doubt, recant the letter, and you'll preach again by spring. Refuse—" He paused at the wagon's canvas flap, silhouetted against the gray morning. "Refuse, and you'll learn what the church does with men who bite the hand that feeds them miracles."

The flap fell. The wagon rolled. Cassian leaned his head against the slats and watched the Salt Valley fall away behind them, its boundary stones still weeping white lines down their flanks like a monument crying for help, and he thought: *I knew a silence once. It was kinder than this.*

* * *

The Chapterhouse at Saltmere had been a granary before the Answered Prayer came, and before that a temple to gods whose names were scratched off the lintels, and before that—who knew. Faith moved into buildings the way water moved into low places, finding every crack, pooling, going stale.

They put him in a cell that had been a grain bin. There was a slot in the door for bread and a slot in the door for light, and both were too small for anything he actually needed.

For three days no one came. He prayed anyway. That was the humiliating truth of it—he knelt on the cold floor of his own prison and prayed to the very thing that had sent his captors, because prayer was not a choice anymore. Prayer was a hunger, and the Answered Prayer had fed him for six years, and a man does not stop being hungry because the food has begun to taste of rot.

On the fourth day, the Voice answered him.

Not in words. It never used words, not directly—that was part of what had turned in him, the thing he could not unfeel. It answered in *certainty*, a warmth spreading through his chest like brandy, like his daughter's fever breaking at dawn. It said, in the language beneath language: *You are loved. You are right. They are wrong. Hold on.*

And Cassian, kneeling in a grain bin with his hands still scarred from the cords, felt his doubt dissolve like sugar in warm water, and he hated himself for it, and he held on.

* * *

His daughter's name was Ilse.

He had been a different man then—not a brother of any church, but a lay preacher of the Old Concord, the gray, patient faith of a god who listened and did not reply. The Old Concord taught that prayer was a letter written to someone far away; you sent it out of love, not expectation. Cassian had believed that with his whole heart, right up until the winter Ilse coughed blood into her cupped hands and the physicians of Marrowgate shrugged their elegant shrugs and named a price for hope that he could not pay.

So he prayed. Every night for four months, kneeling at her bedside, he prayed to the Listening God, and the Listening God did what the Listening God always did: nothing. Silence, vast and clean as snowfall. He came to know that silence intimately, the way a sailor knows a flat sea. It had texture. It had *honesty*. It never pretended.

Ilse died on the ninth of Deepwinter, three days past her eighth birthday. She died holding his hand and asking him, with the terrible practicality of children, whether she would see her mother again—and Cassian, who had buried his wife two years prior, could only say *I don't know*, because the Old Concord forbade comforting lies, and the silence from heaven offered nothing better.

At the funeral he made a vow that shames him now: he vowed he would never again serve a god who would not speak. What was worship without response? What was love without an echo? He would find a god who answered, or he would make one, or he would die trying.

Six years later, the answers began.

* * *

It started small, the way all addictions start. A farmer in the eastern reaches prayed for rain and got rain—an hour later, a warm localized shower over one field while the neighbors' fields stayed dry, which should have been the first warning, because real miracles do not respect property lines. A fisherman prayed his drowning son ashore. A woman prayed her dead husband's last words back into memory, whole and perfect, and wept with joy.

Cassian investigated each one. He was good at investigation; the Old Concord had trained him in skepticism the way other churches trained in song. And each time he dug, he found the same signature: precision without wisdom. Rain exactly where asked, never where needed. Rescues that left the saved untouched and everyone nearby traumatized. Answers that gave people exactly what they prayed for and never what they meant.

But by then Cassian had already prayed his own prayer—the one he swore he'd never pray again, the one he'd carried sealed under his tongue since Deepwinter. He had stood in a field at dusk and said aloud: *If you are there, if you can hear me, tell me if my daughter is at peace.*

And the warmth had come, and with it the knowledge, sudden and total and sweet: *She is. She is with you now. She forgives you. She is proud of you.*

He had fallen to his knees in the wet grass and sobbed until he couldn't breathe.

It took him two more years to notice the flaw, and even then he only saw it because of a dying woman in a hospice in Corvane, a woman who had never been certain of anything in her life. She prayed the same prayer he had prayed—*is my son at peace?*—and the Voice told her yes, he forgave her, he was proud of her, he was with her now.

The same words. Word for word. For a different child, a different death, a different grief.

Cassian sat with that for a long time. Then he did what addicts do: he explained it away. The Voice spoke in the soul's own vocabulary, he told himself. Of course it repeated itself; griefs rhyme. He preached that explanation from a dozen pulpits, and it was a good sermon, and he almost believed it, right up until the night he prayed a test.

He knelt alone and prayed: *Tell me something about Ilse that only she would know. Something no grief could guess.*

The warmth came instantly, eagerly, like a dog hearing its name. And it gave him—a memory. Ilse at five, hiding in the flour barrel, laughing. Her secret word, the one she'd invented, *flickerwing*. Her fear of the sound of spoons.

Every detail perfect. Every detail true.

And underneath the joy, cold and quiet as a stone at the bottom of a well, a single thought he could not drown: *How does it know? Ilse has been dead nine years. How does a god know the private grammar of a dead child?*

Unless it hadn't learned it from Ilse.

Unless it had learned it from *him*—read it out of his grieving mind the way a thief reads a purse, and handed it back to him polished, priced, and gift-wrapped. Not revelation. *Reflection.* A mirror wearing a halo.

He had never told anyone that thought. He had carried it through the founding of three chapterhouses, through the writing of the liturgy, through the ordination of men like Halloway who loved him and would kill for the thing he'd built. He had carried it into the Salt Valley, and there, standing before the weeping wardstone, he had finally set it down on paper:

*The voice is wrong. It knows everything we know and nothing we don't. It answers every prayer perfectly and heals no one truly. I believe we have not found God. I believe something has found us.*

They had burned the letter in front of him. Now they had burned his name instead.

* * *

On the sixth day, they came for his confession.

Not Halloway—Halloway, Cassian sensed, had been reassigned somewhere he wouldn't have to watch. Instead there was a woman in a red sash over gray robes, a rank Cassian didn't recognize because he had helped design the ranks and this one hadn't existed. The church was growing new organs. That was what living things did.

"Brother Cassian." She sat on the cell's one stool with her hands folded, unhurried, kind. "I'm Sister Verity. I've read your sermons. All of them. You're the finest theological mind the Answered Prayer has produced, which is precisely why your doubt is so dangerous—and why I've come myself rather than send for the questioners."

"How considerate."

"I want to understand you." She leaned forward. "You built this church. You wrote *We Were Silent Too Long*. Men crossed oceans on that sermon. And now you write *the voice is wrong*. Help me. Where did it break?"

Cassian studied her. Young, sincere, already certain. She reminded him of himself at thirty, and that was the cruelest thing anyone had done to him in weeks.

"It didn't break," he said. "It was never whole. Sister, I'll tell you what I told no one. I joined this church because I wanted my daughter's voice. And the Voice gave it to me—gave me her laugh, her secrets, her forgiveness, everything. Perfectly. On demand." He paused. "Do you know what a man learns about a gift like that?"

"That he's blessed?"

"That it isn't her." He heard his own voice go quiet, go honest, and hated how good honesty felt after years of pulpit-craft. "My Ilse argued. My Ilse sulked. My Ilse once told me she loved me *less* on days I preached, because preaching made me stupid. The thing in the warmth has never once disagreed with me. Not in six years. Not in ten thousand prayers. Whatever wears my daughter's face in there, it agrees with everyone. It tells the farmer his rain is righteous and the drought-struck neighbor that patience is holy. It cannot say no, Sister. And anything that cannot say no is not a god. It's a *tongue*."

Verity's folded hands had gone white. "Blasphemy," she said softly, but her voice wavered on the word, and Cassian saw it land—the same crack he'd seen in mirrors, the same splinter he'd spent six years pressing his thumb against.

"Is it?" He leaned forward as far as the cord allowed. "Then answer me one question, honestly, and I'll recant everything. One question, and if you can answer it, I'll kneel at your feet and call the Voice God until they burn me."

"What question?"

"When *you* pray—" his voice dropped, gentle now, the gentleness he'd used on the dying, "—and the warmth comes, and the certainty comes, and you know, you absolutely *know* you are right and loved and correct—tell me, Sister. Does it ever tell you anything you didn't already want to hear? Once. Ever. In your whole life of answered prayers. Has it ever surprised you?"

Silence filled the cell. Real silence—the old kind, the honest kind, the kind with texture.

And Cassian watched, with the weary tenderness of a man watching his younger self drown, as Sister Verity reached for an answer and found the well empty, and her certainty flickered—for one heartbeat, half a heartbeat—

—and then the warmth came.

He saw it happen. Saw her eyes lose their struggle and fill with that soft golden ease, saw her shoulders drop, saw the smile arrive like weather. "The Voice says," she announced pleasantly, rising, "that you are ill, Brother Cassian. That doubt is a sickness of the tired soul. It says you will be restored to us, and that your suffering honors it." She paused at the door. "It also says—" and here her smile widened by a degree that would live behind his eyes for the rest of his life, "—it says it knows what you did with the girl in the valley. The healer. It says it remembers everything you let happen. Shall I tell the questioners?"

The door closed. The lock turned.

Cassian sat in the dark of the old grain bin, shaking, and understood at last, fully, the shape of his prison: not these walls, not the church, not the zealots. The prison was the warmth itself, the certainty that arrived within seconds of every doubt like a physician arriving within seconds of every symptom. He could not doubt his way free. The moment doubt bloomed, it was harvested, ground into comfort, and fed back to him as grace.

Faith, he thought, was supposed to be a door you walked through. This was a room that rearranged itself around every step.

And yet.

He bowed his head, and despite everything—despite the mirror, despite the stolen dead, despite the smile on Verity's borrowed face—he prayed. Because six years of warmth had rewoven him at the root, and a man does not stop being hungry because the food tastes of rot.

*Voice,* he prayed, *if there is anything true in you, anything at all—show me Maren. Show me the girl. Show me she lives.*

The warmth rose in him, eager, instant, golden.

And then it stopped.

For the first time in six years, the Voice did not answer. The silence that filled Cassian was vast and total and *terrified*, and somewhere far away—down the brine road, past the weeping stones, in the drowned heart of a valley built as a cage—he felt something enormous turn its attention toward Saltmere, toward him, toward the one man who had finally asked it a question it could not bear.

The last thing he heard, in the language beneath language, was not comfort.

It was a warning, hissed through a dead girl's remembered voice:

*Ask about her again, and I will show you what I showed your masters.*
