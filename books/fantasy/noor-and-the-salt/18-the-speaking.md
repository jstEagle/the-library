## 18. The Speaking

The heart of the valley had been a shrine once, before the salt took it. Vesper could still see the bones of it under the ruin: the ring of standing stones, the sunken basin where pilgrims had knelt, the channels cut into the rock that had carried clean water from the mountains down to the fields. Now the channels ran with brine, and the standing stones were cracked like teeth in a broken jaw, and at the center of it all stood Maren.

Or something wearing Maren.

She hung a hand's width above the flooded basin, her bare feet trailing in water gone white as milk. Her eyes were open and they were not hers—too old, too patient, lit from behind like lamps in a house where no one lived. The Unnamed had dressed her in white, because gods liked their vessels clean, and around her the air shimmered with a heat-haze of returning magic, the first the valley had felt in twenty years.

Vesper walked toward her through the crowd.

They had come in their hundreds—the whole valley, it seemed, plus the Church's soldiers in their gray-and-gold, rank on rank along the ridgeline, crossbows leveled but not loosed, because Cassian had ordered them to wait. Cassian himself stood near the basin's edge, and when Vesper passed him he did not reach for his sword. He only watched with the expression of a man watching a fire he had lit finally reach the house.

"Vesper Coldharbour," the Unnamed said, and its voice came out of Maren's mouth in Maren's voice, which was the cruelest thing about it. "You've come far for someone who swore never to speak."

"I've come to talk," Vesper said. "That's different."

Laughter moved through the faithful—not mockery, exactly, but the nervous laughter of people who had come expecting a miracle and were getting a conversation. The soldiers shifted. Somewhere on the ridge, an officer barked a command to hold.

Vesper stopped ten paces from the basin. Close enough to see the salt crust on Maren's lips. Close enough to see that beneath the wrongness of her eyes, her hands were trembling—her hands, still hers, still fighting.

*Hold on,* Vesper thought at her. *I'm going to tell them everything.*

"You should know," Vesper said, raising their voice to carry, "that I was going to do this differently. I spent last night deciding whether to speak this creature's true name and end it, or stay silent and keep my oath and lose everything. Those felt like the only choices. Silence or power. The oath or the world."

The Unnamed smiled with Maren's mouth. "And now?"

"Now I understand those were the choices *it* wanted me to have." Vesper turned, slowly, so the whole crowd could see their face. "Because both of them are lies. Both of them keep the truth underground. And the truth is the only weapon here that has ever worked."

"Careful, swordspeaker," the god said softly.

"No." Vesper's voice cracked out across the basin like a blade leaving a sheath. "You don't get to hush me. You have had twenty years of my silence. You have had *centuries* of my order's silence. And every year of it fed you."

The shimmer around Maren flickered.

It was small—a stutter in the heat-haze, a guttering like a candle in a draft—but Vesper saw it, and Cassian saw it, and the front row of the faithful saw it, and a murmur went through the crowd like wind through wheat.

Vesper kept speaking.

"My order was called the Order of the Spoken Word. We were swordspeakers—we carried truth the way other soldiers carry steel, and our truths could cut. When we spoke a lie aloud, the lie died. When we spoke a name, the named thing obeyed. We were not humble people. Remember that. Remember we were proud, and certain, and very sure of God."

They began to walk the basin's rim as they spoke, pacing like a preacher, and the crowd turned to follow them, hundreds of faces, farmers and fishwives and gray-and-gold soldiers all turning together.

"And then, three hundred years ago, we found something answering prayers. A voice in the dark that gave people what they asked for. Healing. Rain. Justice. It sounded like God. It *wept* like God. Half my order fell to their knees and worshipped it on the spot." Vesper stopped pacing. "The other half listened more carefully. And what they heard, underneath the mercy, underneath the love, was hunger. It didn't answer prayers because it loved us. It answered prayers because every answered prayer is a hook. Every miracle is a debt. It was farming us, the way we farm sheep—kindly, patiently, right up until the shearing."

"So they lied to you," Vesper said, gesturing at the crowd. "All of you. Your grandparents and theirs, back three hundred years. They told you the valley was sanctuary. They told you the wards kept magic *out*, to protect you from a dangerous world. The truth is the opposite. The wards keep nothing out. They keep one thing *in*. This valley is not your home. It is a cell. And you—" their voice broke, and they let it break, let the crowd hear it, "—you were born inside the lock."

The murmur became a roar. Soldiers on the ridge were shouting; Cassian had raised both hands, trying to hold his own faithful steady; and above it all the Unnamed's voice rang out of Maren's throat, sweet and wounded:

"They are lying to you now. I have only ever given. Ask them what your masters took."

"Oh, I will," Vesper said. "Thank you for asking."

And they told them.

They told them about the sacrifice. About how the seal required feeding—not blood, not gold, but *existence*. How the order had taken a whole country off the map to power it: Vesper's homeland, the salt-white country beyond the northern pass, its cities and songs and kings, unmade so thoroughly that no one outside remembered it had ever been there, and everyone inside forgot they'd ever wanted to leave. How the swordspeakers had done it themselves, with their own spoken art, and then sealed the memory of doing it inside their own oath of silence, so that even they could not confess what they'd done.

"I was fourteen years old," Vesper said, and their voice went quiet, and somehow that quiet reached farther than any shout. "I stood where you're standing. I swore an oath I wasn't old enough to understand, on an altar I wasn't old enough to question. My master put her hands on my head and told me silence was holy. She believed it. That's the worst part. They weren't monsters. They were good people who did an unforgivable thing and then made themselves forget it, because the alternative was living with it, and they couldn't bear that, and neither can I, and I am bearing it *right now*, in front of all of you, because someone has to."

Maren's body shuddered in the air. The white light around her pulsed—and dimmed.

"The vessel weakens," the Unnamed said, and for the first time its voice frayed at the edges. "Stop him. Stop—"

"No one has to stop anyone," Vesper said. "That's the whole point. For three hundred years everything has been done to you without your consent—the sealing, the forgetting, the lying. Even your faith was managed, rationed, curated by dead people who decided you couldn't handle the truth. Well. Here it is. All of it. Ugly and unforgivable and *yours*."

They spread their arms.

"I don't ask you to forgive my order. I don't ask you to forgive me—I broke my oath two nights ago to save a life, and I'd break it again. I ask you one thing only. Look at the thing in that girl. Really look. It has shown you miracles. Has it ever once shown you the truth? Not comfort. Not answers. *Truth.*"

Silence, vast and ringing.

Then, from the crowd, an old woman's voice—Talla, who sold eels at the ferry dock, whom Vesper had carried across the water a thousand mornings. "The hymn," she said hoarsely. "My grandmother sang it. I taught it to my daughter. Was that the cage? Were we singing the *bars*?"

"Yes," Vesper said gently. "You were. And you didn't know. None of us knew. That's what it means to live inside a lie—it isn't that you believe falsehoods. It's that the true things are taken away and replaced with beautiful ones."

The shimmer around Maren tore like wet paper.

She dropped—one instant hanging in glory, the next simply falling—and hit the shallow brine with a splash that sounded obscene in the silence. The white drained from her eyes. She lay gasping, human, small, alive, and the crowd made a sound that had no name in it at all.

But the Unnamed was not finished. Vesper felt it the way you feel a storm change direction—the pressure in the air reversing, gathering, the shattered haze re-knitting into something darker and denser above the basin. Stripped of Maren, stripped of the crowd's belief, it had nothing left to wear. So it stopped wearing anything.

It rose out of the water as absence. A shape of not-shape, a hole in the world the size of a kneeling man, and every prayer anyone in that crowd had ever had answered screamed silently in the space around it.

"*Nameless now,*" it said, and its voice was everywhere, in the stones, in the water, in Vesper's own skull. "*You have unmade my face. But I was old before faces. I need no vessel. I need only a name, swordspeaker, and you know it—you have always known it. Speak it, and I am bound to obey. Refuse, and I take this valley apart stone by stone, soul by soul, starting with the girl at your feet.*"

Maren lifted her head from the water, hair plastered to her face, and looked at Vesper with her own eyes—terrified, clear, and utterly unbroken.

"Don't," she rasped. "Whatever it offers. Whatever it threatens. Don't give it the—"

"*SPEAK,*" the god roared, and the standing stones began to crack, and the brine in the channels boiled up white, and three hundred years of patience came down to one word on one tongue—

Vesper opened their mouth.

And did not say the name.

Instead, they looked at Maren, drowning in air and terror, and spoke—to her, only to her, in the old cadence of the liturgy, the words passing between them like a key passed hand to hand:

"Maren of the Salt Valley. Daughter of no order, sworn to no one. Here is the name it hides behind. Take it—it was never mine to keep."

And the name poured out of Vesper—not as a command, not as a chain, but as a gift, freely given, freely held—and Maren's eyes went wide with it, and the Unnamed understood, one heartbeat too late, what was happening, and shrieked loud enough to split the sky.
