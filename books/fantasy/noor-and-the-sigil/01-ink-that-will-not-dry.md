## 1. Ink That Will Not Dry

The ink in Noor was made from ash and tallow and the boiled-down roots of a weed that grew only where nothing else would grow. It was gray-black, joyless, and it dried faster than any ink Sable Reyes had ever worked with—which was fitting, because everything in the valley dried fast: grief, grass, gossip, hope. Nothing was permitted to stay wet long enough to leave a stain.

He had been copying ledgers for six hours when he noticed that his ink had stopped obeying him.

It began as a small wrongness, the kind of thing a careful man trains himself to overlook. A letter that sat too heavy on the page. A stroke of the pen that seemed to continue after he lifted it, a hair's breadth further than his hand had carried it. He told himself it was fatigue. His eyes were tired; the lamp was low; the scriptorium above the counting-house smelled of cold tallow and older men's coughs, and tiredness explained everything if you let it.

Sable had learned, over twenty-six years, to let it explain everything.

He sat at the last desk in the last row, where the light from the high windows died before it reached him and no overseer bothered to look unless something needed punishing. The ledger open before him was the grain-tithe for the eastern terraces, forty years old, its original hand cramped and furious. His task was simple: copy it clean, copy it exact, add nothing, omit nothing. Exactness was the whole of his trade. In a valley that had traded away its gods for peace, exactness was the closest thing left to prayer.

*Debtor: Reyes, line of.* *Principal remaining: eleven years, four months.*

His own name, in his own hand, staring up at him from the margin of someone else's arithmetic. That was how the temple wrote people—not as souls but as sums, entries to be worked down year by year until either the number reached zero or the person did. His father had entered the ledger owing for a failed harvest and a borrowed ox. His father had exited it owing nothing, because dead men's debts passed down like eye color, like the flat set of his jaw, like the careful way he held his shoulders so as never to take up more room than necessary.

Sable dipped his pen. Wrote. Dipped again.

And then, on the thirty-first line, the ink moved.

Not smudged. Not bled. *Moved*—the way a worm moves under wet earth, a slow muscular shift beneath the surface of the page. He watched a comma he had placed after *barley, threshed* crawl sideways, dragging itself out of position with the patience of something that had all night and no fear of being caught.

He put the pen down very carefully, the way you set down a cup when you suspect there is something alive in the tea.

Around him the scriptorium went on breathing its dry paper breath. Old Haskel two rows up, wheezing through his copies of the marriage rolls. The twins, Pell and Pella Ostrin, indentured since they were nine, their pens scratching in unison like insects in a wall. Somewhere below, the counting-house clerks were arguing about a shortfall in the wool tithe, their voices rising and flattening into the particular rhythm of men who had argued the same argument every season of their lives.

No one else saw anything. Of course no one else saw anything. Sable looked at the comma for a long time, and the comma looked back at him in the only way a comma can, by refusing to be where he had put it.

*You are tired,* he told himself. *You have copied four thousand words today and your eyes are lying to you, which is what eyes do when a man cannot afford new ones.*

He blotted the line, squared the page, and kept working. This was the discipline of his life: keep working. When his mother died in the fever-year, he had kept working. When the temple raised the interest on inherited debts—quietly, in the third month of winter, when the poor were least able to read—he had kept working. Wonder was expensive. Curiosity got men summoned to Magistrate Corren's office on the second floor, and men who were summoned to Corren's office came back with longer debts than they'd arrived with, or didn't come back at all.

So he kept working, and the ink kept moving, and by the time the bell rang for the evening meal he had stopped pretending not to notice.

* * *

He stayed late. That was allowed; staying late earned credit against the principal, quarter-hours scratched into a slate beside his desk by a clerk who trusted the slate more than any man. The others filed out into the blue dusk, and the lamps guttered one by one, and soon Sable was alone in a room full of sleeping paper with a single flame and a page that would not hold still.

Because now it wasn't commas. Now it was words.

They assembled themselves slowly, the way frost builds on glass—not written so much as *grown*, each letter rising out of the drying ink like a blade of grass pushing through soil. They were not letters he knew. They were older than the temple's script, older than the trade-language of the valley markets; they were angular and hungry-looking, all downward strokes and closed gates, a language built by people who expected to be interrupted.

Sable knew what it was anyway. Everyone in Noor knew what it was, the way everyone knows the shape of a thing they've been taught never to look at.

Old Tongue. Godscript. The language of the Founders' Bargain, reproduced in exactly three places in the valley: the sealed doors beneath the temple, the monument stones on the northern ridge, and nowhere else, ever, on pain of a debt so large your grandchildren would inherit it still owing.

His pen hovered. The lamp hissed. And the words finished themselves across the bottom of the grain-tithe ledger, forty years of barley and threshing and bushels measured to the half-liter, ending in a sentence in a dead language that no living man in Noor could read—and yet Sable found, with a horror that started in his teeth and sank slowly through him like well water, that he could almost *hear* it. Not understand it. Hear it. The way you hear your name called from another room: the meaning arriving ahead of the words.

Something was answering him. Something had been listening to the scratch of his pen for six hours, patient as debt, and had decided to speak.

"No," Sable said aloud, to the page, to the room, to whatever was wearing his ink like a mouth. "No. I didn't ask for anything."

The words did not change. They sat there glistening, wetter than the rest of the page, wetter than ink had any right to be an hour after writing, shining faintly in the lamplight like something breathing under a sheet.

He thought about Corren. He thought about the interrogation rooms on the second floor, about the scribe named Aldous Vey who had been dismissed eight years ago for "irregularities" and whose family still paid off the fine. He thought about the word *vessel*, which appeared in the oldest law-scrolls and was never explained, and about how the temple's punishments were always described in terms of accounts—*forfeiture*, *settlement*, *closure*—as if a man could be balanced out of existence.

Then he picked up the ledger, carried it to the iron stove in the corner that heated the scriptorium's water, and fed it to the fire page by page.

It felt like drowning in reverse. Forty years of records going up in yellow flame, his own careful hand curling black at the edges, and underneath the panic a small, appalled voice noting that this was arson, that this was the destruction of temple property, that the penalty would take him the rest of his life to work off and possibly several lives beyond it. He burned it anyway. He stood there watching the godscript blister and vanish, and the last thing to go was the final word—the longest one, the one that had taken the ink the most effort to grow—and just before the flame took it, Sable heard it clearly, inside his skull rather than his ears, in a voice like a door opening in a house he had lived in all his life without knowing it had a cellar:

*Scribe. I have waited a long time for a hand steady enough to write me.*

The stove door clanged shut. The fire settled. The scriptorium was silent except for the ordinary sounds of an ordinary building, and Sable stood in the middle of it shaking, waiting for the voice to come again, waiting for the walls to sprout words, waiting for the temple bells to begin ringing alarm.

Nothing. The valley of Noor kept its famous silence, the silence that had been purchased at a price recorded on the northern ridge in letters of blood and old tongue: *no god shall enter here*. The Founders' Bargain. The one miracle the valley permitted, and only because it was a miracle of absence—a hole shaped exactly like the divine, maintained for three hundred years by law and fear and the tireless copying of ledgers.

Sable laughed once, a short ugly sound with no humor in it, and began gathering his things. He would report a lamp accident. A spark from the stove. Papers lost. It was thin—it was paper-thin—but it was the best lie available, and lies, like ink, dried fastest in Noor.

* * *

He washed his hands at the courtyard pump before walking home, because ink-stained fingers drew comments, and comments drew eyes, and eyes were the one currency Sable had spent his whole life refusing to earn.

The water ran gray off his fingers. He scrubbed harder. The gray wouldn't lift entirely—it never did, scribes wore their trade in their skin like farmers wore soil—but there was more of it tonight than usual, a staining that climbed past his knuckles, and when he turned his wrist to scrub the underside, the pump-light caught something on the inside of his forearm that stopped his breath.

A mark. Small, no bigger than a thumbnail, sitting just below the heel of his palm where the pulse lives. It had not been there that morning. He was certain of it the way you are certain of your own heartbeat. It was angular and hungry-looking, all downward strokes and closed gates—one character of the dead language, rendered not in ink but in something darker, something that lay *under* his skin the way a root lies under a road, visible only where it breaks the surface.

He pressed his thumb against it. It was warm. Warmer than skin. Warm the way a stone stays warm after the fire is out, holding a heat that has nowhere left to go.

Ink dries in Noor. Everything dries. Grief, grass, gossip, hope.

But this would not dry, and it would not wash, and when Sable pulled his sleeve down over it and walked home through streets where every lamp was lit and every window shuttered against wonder, he caught himself doing the one thing he had trained himself never to do.

He was praying. Wordlessly, desperately, to no one—to the empty air of a godless valley, to the hole where the divine used to be—

*Please let this be nothing. Please let me be invisible. Please don't let them see me.*

And somewhere beneath his skin, patient as debt, warm as a banked fire, something heard him—and answered.
