## 2. The Valley of Noor

There was a lesson every child in Noor learned before they learned their letters: *do not ask.*

Do not ask the sky for rain beyond what the aqueducts give. Do not ask the river for more than its banks allow. Do not ask for luck, for love, for vengeance, for mercy. The asking itself was the crime—not because it offended anyone living, but because of who might be listening.

My mother taught me this with her hands pressed over my ears on the night my grandmother died, when the old woman's keening had turned into something that sounded almost like prayer. "Not in this house," my mother whispered, though we were alone with the dead and the dead could hardly tell. "Not in this valley. You want something, Sable? You work for it. You bleed for it if you must. But you never *ask*."

I was six years old. I did not understand then that she was not warning me against rudeness. She was warning me against being heard.

* * *

Noor sits in a fold of mountains like a coin dropped into a purse—shut away, kept safe, worth nothing to anyone outside. The valley runs forty miles end to end, green where the meltwater reaches, gray-brown everywhere else. Terraced fields climb the lower slopes in steps like the ribs of some enormous sleeping animal. The temple stands at the center of everything, white-walled and windowless, not a house of worship but a house of *accounting*, because in Noor the only thing anyone worshipped was the ledger.

The Founders' Bargain was carved above the temple gate in letters worn nearly smooth by four hundred years of wind:

*NO GOD ENTERS HERE. NO VOICE ANSWERS HERE. WE KEEP OUR OWN SOULS.*

Below it, smaller, added by some later magistrate with a taste for practicality: *ALL DEBTS ARE SACRED.*

That second line governed my life. It governed everyone's life, but mine most thoroughly. My father had borrowed against his own hands—against the labor they would do, the years they would carry—to buy seed after the blight year, and then he had died before the seed became anything but debt. Debt passed down like eye color. Mine came to eleven years, four months, and some odd days of scribe-work at the temple scriptorium, copying ledgers in a hand so careful it looked printed.

Eleven years. I had made my peace with it the way you make peace with weather. Some people raged at Corren's audits, at the interest compounded in silence, at the way a missed day multiplied like rot. I did not rage. Rage was a kind of asking—*why me, why this*—and asking got you heard.

Or so I believed, until the ink moved.

* * *

Three days after I burned the page, I woke before dawn out of habit and lay still in the cold, listening to Noor wake around me: the creak of the aqueduct gate, the first cart on the limestone road, the water-seller's bell. Ordinary sounds. Sacred sounds, in their way—the sound of a valley that needed nothing from anywhere else.

The mark on my wrist itched.

I turned my arm to the gray light coming through the shutter. The sigil sat just below the pulse-point, fine as engraving, a closed knot of lines like a key drawn by someone who had only had keys described to them. It had not faded overnight as burned skin should. If anything it had deepened, darkened to the color of dried blood, and around its edges the skin was smooth and unbroken, as if the mark had grown there rather than been written.

I pressed my thumb over it hard enough to hurt. Nothing. No voice. No warmth. Just the itch, faint and patient, like an insect walking under cloth.

*It was nothing,* I told myself, and began the morning ritual of becoming invisible. Plain clothes, mended twice. Hair cut close. Shoulders slightly stooped—not hunched, which drew pity, but stooped, which drew nothing at all. In Noor, invisibility was a craft, and I had been apprenticed to it longer than to letters.

* * *

The scriptorium smelled of oak gall and tallow and the dust of ten thousand pages. Forty desks in rows, forty scribes bent like reeds under snow, and at the front of the room the overseer's dais, empty until the hour bell.

I took my seat, unwrapped my bandage in the privy beforehand and replaced it with a fresh one—a scribe's wrist strain was common enough, no one questioned it—and set to work on the grain-tithe rolls for the eastern terraces. Numbers. Safe, dumb numbers. Ink went where I put it and stayed there, obedient, and for two hours I almost convinced myself the night three days past had been a fever, a dream, a trick of candle-smoke.

Then Magistrate Corren came down the aisle.

He did it sometimes without warning, appearing between the desks like frost on glass—suddenly, silently, everywhere at once. He was a narrow man, dry as the valley in high summer, with a face built entirely of judgments. His footsteps made no sound on the stone. People said he practiced that. People said a lot of things about Corren, all of them quiet, all of them true enough to be dangerous to repeat.

He stopped beside my desk.

"Reyes." My family name in his mouth sounded like an entry in a ledger. "Your output is down."

"I've been unwell, magistrate. A wrist injury." I lifted the bandaged arm a few inches, the way you'd show a wound to a physician—honestly, briefly, already lowering it.

Corren looked at the bandage. Then he looked at my face, and his eyes did the thing that made even the debt-free nervous: they went still, completely still, while the rest of him stayed ordinary, as if behind them a man were reading a very long document very fast.

"A wrist injury," he said. "In your writing hand?"

"The other wrist."

"Mm." He picked up my finished page, held it to the light, tilted it. Checking the line-weight, the margins, the spacing of numerals. His inspection took perhaps four seconds and felt like four hours. "Your hand has changed," he said at last. "Since last month. The descenders are shorter. You're compensating."

"It will pass."

"Everything passes," Corren said, setting the page down with terrible gentleness. "That is rather the point of this valley. Nothing here is permitted to *last*—not grief, not fever, not faith. We keep our own souls, Reyes. Carved over the gate. Have you read it lately?"

"Every day, magistrate. I walk under it."

"And yet." He straightened his cuffs, a small precise motion. "There have been anomalies in the tithe rolls. Entries corrected after sealing. Ink behaving—" he paused, choosing the word with the care of a man defusing something, "—irregularly. Three scribes report dreams. One reports hearing her name called in an empty room." His eyes came back to mine and stopped again, that long slow reading. "You'll forgive the question, but have you asked for anything lately, Reyes? Even quietly. Even once."

The word *asked* went through me like a hook through cloth.

"No," I said. "Never."

"Good." He moved on down the aisle, silent as frost, and I watched the back of his narrow head and understood, with the particular clarity of a man watching his own trap close, that Corren had not been asking me a question at all. He had been checking a snare he'd already set. Somewhere in those sealed tithe rolls was a page with my handwriting on it, and somewhere in that page was something that should not exist in Noor.

Something that had answered.

* * *

At midday I walked the aqueduct road to clear my head, up along the channel where the meltwater ran bright and shallow over white stone. From there you could see the whole valley laid out like a map: the terraces, the roofs, the temple's blind white walls, and beyond them the tombs of the Founders cut into the mountain's root, seven doors of black basalt that no one entered and no one spoke of, except once a year, when the whole valley observed the Hour of Silence—one hour, sundown to dark, in which no one in Noor spoke at all, in memory of whatever price the Founders had paid.

We were taught the shape of the story and never its flesh. Four hundred years ago, the valley had been a battlefield. Gods had walked here openly—so the schoolroom said—gods of harvest and storm and war, and mortals had been their dice, their fields, their fuel. Then came the Founders: seven men and women whose names we knew and whose deeds we did not, who lured the gods into the valley's heart and *closed the door*. Sealed it in blood and silence. Every god, every whispering power, every answering ear—shut outside or shut beneath, the stories disagreed, and the disagreement was itself forbidden ground. What mattered was the result. For four hundred years, no prayer in Noor had ever been answered.

Four hundred years of peace. That was the phrase. They taught it to us before they taught us to fear asking, and they taught us both before they taught us to wonder why a peace needed guarding so jealously, hour by hour, generation by generation, like a fire that might catch if left alone.

I stood on the aqueduct with the water talking its endless indifferent talk below me, and I pressed my bandaged wrist against my chest, and I thought: *the door is shut. Whatever scratched at my ink three nights ago, it is outside the door. It cannot be inside. Nothing is inside.*

The sigil itched, patient as arithmetic.

And far off, at the valley's mouth where the road climbed toward the pass, I saw a figure on the road. Small at this distance, wrong-colored—a smear of saffron and silver where no traveler wore color, moving steadily down toward Noor, toward the gate, toward the carved words that were supposed to mean the same thing to everyone who read them.

Travelers came rarely. Travelers in priest's colors came never. The toll-keepers would turn her back by nightfall; the Bargain saw to that; it always had.

I watched her descend anyway, for a long time, with the itch burning on my wrist like a second pulse, and I found myself thinking a thought so forbidden I didn't dare finish shaping it even in the privacy of my own skull:

*I hope she gets through.*
