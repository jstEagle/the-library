## 4. What the Gods Want

The sigil did not sleep.

Sable had hoped, in the three nights since it burned itself into his wrist, that it would fade the way ink fades, the way all strange things faded in Noor if you simply refused to look at them long enough. But each morning he woke to find it darker, a whorl of black lines like a spider's web drawn by someone who hated spiders, and each morning it was warm—warm as skin, warm as something living pressed against him from underneath.

He wrapped it in linen. He wore his sleeves buttoned to the knuckle even in the swelter of the scriptorium, where Magistrate Corren's overseers walked the aisles with their counting-sticks and their eyes like scales. He told himself: *it is nothing. It is a rash. It is a stain from bad ink.* He told himself this while copying columns of grain-tithes in his small, careful hand, the hand that had kept the Reyes family invisible for two generations, and he almost believed it.

Almost.

Because at night, alone in his room above the tannery, the ink on his desk moved again.

* * *

It began with the smell. Old paper, older smoke—the scent of a library burning somewhere very far away. Then the inkwell would shiver, a fine ring trembling across the surface of the black liquid, and one drop would climb the side of the pot against every law of water and weight, crawl down onto the desk, and begin to walk.

Sable sat very still the first time, hands flat on the table, watching the droplet drag itself into lines. Not the dead language from the ledger—not entirely. This was slower, more deliberate, as though whatever wrote it were choosing each stroke with care, the way Sable himself chose strokes when the words mattered.

He could not read it. But he could feel it, the way you can feel someone reading over your shoulder—a pressure between the shoulder blades, a breath on the neck.

"You're not real," he said aloud, to the empty room, to the tannery stink seeping through the floorboards, to the drop of ink forming letters on his desk. "There are no gods in Noor."

The ink stopped.

And then, inside his skull—not in his ears, never in his ears, because then it would be madness and madness could be endured—something answered.

*No,* it agreed, and the voice was vast and patient and terribly tired, like a bell that has been ringing underground for a thousand years. *There are no gods in Noor. That is rather the point.*

Sable's chair went over backward. He lay on the floorboards among the splinters and the spilled ink, heart slamming, and the voice continued, unhurried, as if they had all the time in the world—as if, indeed, it had already waited longer than the world.

*I have been called many things, scribe. Vesh is the oldest. War is what they made me into, which is not the same as what I am. I am the wall that holds when everything else breaks. I am the answer men scream for in the last hour. Your founders knew me once. They sang to me. They fed me their firstborn's names written in barley-oil, and I kept them safe, and then—*

The voice paused. When it resumed, the patience in it had a hairline crack running through, like glaze on old pottery.

*Then they built a valley with no doors for me, and called it peace.*

"Noor isn't a prison," Sable said to the ceiling. His voice came out steadier than he felt. "It's a home. The only one anyone here has ever needed. We don't *want* gods. That's the whole—that's the bargain. That's why there's no famine, no war, no burning temples. The Founders gave up wonder so we could keep our children."

*Yes.* A sound that might have been laughter, if bells could laugh. *I have read your histories, scribe. I taught your great-grandmother's great-grandmother how to write them. Do you know what I find most remarkable about the Valley of Noor?*

"What."

*That not one of them ever asked what the gods got out of the bargain.*

Sable sat up slowly. Ink had soaked through his shirt; the sigil on his wrist pulsed against the wet cloth like a second heartbeat.

"I don't care what you got," he said. "I don't want anything from you. I want my ledgers copied and my debt paid and my mother's grave left undisturbed, and none of those things require a god."

*Your debt,* the voice said, and now it went soft, soft as a hand extended palm-up. *Eleven years of it. Fourteen hundred marks to the temple of the Silent Accord, accrued the day your father signed for medicine he could not afford, transferred to you the day he died. You have paid nine hundred and sixty. At your current wage, you will be free at forty-one years of age.*

Sable said nothing. There was nothing to say. The numbers were correct to the mark; he had checked them himself a thousand times, in his own careful hand.

*I can unmake that ledger,* Vesh said. *Every line. Every mark. One word from me through your mouth, one sigil traced in the temple's own book, and you walk out owing nothing—to no one, forever. Is that not what prayers are? Men asking for exactly this. And here I am, offering it freely, asking nothing but to be carried.*

"Nothing but to be carried," Sable repeated. "That's how it starts."

*How what starts?*

"How it always starts." He pulled himself upright, set the chair right, and looked at the ink-letters drying on his desk—letters he still couldn't read but suddenly understood anyway, the way you understand a threat in any language. "A god offers mercy and calls the price nothing. My father took medicine on credit. The temple called the price fair. Everyone who is owned was offered something first."

The silence that followed was long. When the voice came again, it had gone quiet in a new way—not patient now, but *hungry*, and Sable understood with cold clarity that he had wounded something enormous, and that enormous wounded things do not stay wounded.

*You will change your mind,* Vesh said. *Not because I force you. Because the world will. Watch.*

The candle guttered out. The sigil burned cold, then hot, then cold again, and Sable slept—or did not sleep—and dreamed of walls holding against a tide that never stopped coming.

* * *

Market day. The third hour, when the fish-sellers' ice was melting and the whole lower square smelled of brine and coriander and hot oil.

Sable had come for lamp oil and salt, nothing more, keeping to the edges of the crowd the way he had kept to the edges of everything for twenty-six years. The linen wrap on his wrist itched. He had begun, in the last day, to feel the sigil the way you feel a tooth—not constantly, but waiting underneath, ready to announce itself.

He heard the screaming before he understood it.

A woman near the well, on her knees in the trampled cabbage leaves, and in her arms a boy—five years old, maybe six, gray-lipped, eyes rolled back, his small chest hitching in that terrible rhythm that is not breathing but the memory of breathing. Fever, the crowd murmured. The wasting fever, come up from the river settlements. The temple physicians did not treat the wasting fever; the temple physicians charged more than a house cost, and the woman's hands were a laborer's hands, cracked and rope-veined, and everyone in the square knew what the ending of this story was, because everyone in Noor had seen this story end before.

*Here,* said the voice inside Sable's skull, gentle as falling snow. *This is what I meant.*

"No," Sable breathed. He stood frozen at the edge of the gathering crowd, salt packet forgotten in his hand. "No. Don't. Whatever you're going to do—"

*You misunderstand me. I cannot do anything. I am a prisoner behind glass, remember? I have no hands in this valley. Only yours.* A pause, and in the pause, Sable felt it: the sigil waking, warming, the black lines beginning to move beneath the linen like worms under bark. *But you, scribe. You are kneeling beside her before you have decided to kneel. Look at your feet. Tell me whose choice that was.*

He looked down. He was moving. He had been moving since the first scream, shouldering through the crowd, and now he was on his knees in the mud and cabbage leaves beside the weeping woman, and the boy's breath was failing, the gaps between each hitching gasp growing longer, and there was no physician within a mile and no money in Sable's pocket worth the name.

*One touch,* Vesh said. *That is all I ask. Not worship. Not freedom. Not yet. Just—let me help. Let me be wanted, once, for something other than war.*

"This is a trick," Sable whispered. His hand was rising. He watched it rise as though it belonged to someone else. "This is how you get out."

*Everything is a trick, little scribe. The question is whether the trick works.* And there was something raw in the vast voice now, something that sounded—gods help him—like pleading. *The child dies in ninety heartbeats. Count them, if you doubt me. You are a man of ledgers. Count.*

Sable counted. Sixty-eight. Sixty-nine.

The mother looked up at him—at this stranger in a buttoned sleeve kneeling in the filth beside her—and her eyes were beyond hope, past the far border of hope, in the country where mothers go when there is nothing left to do but hold on and witness. She did not ask him for anything. That was the worst of it. She had already asked everyone, and everyone had already said no, and she simply held her son and rocked him, and the crowd around them was silent in the way Noor was always silent, godless and orderly and helpless, the silence of people who had traded wonder for safety and discovered that safety did not cover this.

Seventy-four. Seventy-five.

"Damn you," Sable said—to Vesh, to the Founders, to the bargain, to the whole bloodless architecture of Noor—and he pulled the linen from his wrist and laid his bare hand, sigil-down, on the boy's small burning chest.

The world went white.

Not light—white heat, white noise, the roar of a furnace door opening in his bones. Power poured out of him like water through a broken dam, and he felt it leave, felt it *go*, streaming down his arm and into the failing body under his palm, and where it passed it mended: he could feel the fever breaking apart like ice, feel the lungs unfurling, feel the small stuttering heart find its true rhythm and hold. The boy arched. Drew a breath—a real one, deep and whole—and opened his eyes, and blinked up at his mother with the mild confusion of a child woken from a nap.

The square erupted. The mother was sobbing, clutching the boy, laughing, kissing his hair, and the crowd was pressing in, and someone was shouting *a miracle, a miracle in Noor* in a voice that cracked with disbelief, because there had been no miracles in Noor for three hundred years, that was the entire covenant, that was the whole point—

Sable crawled backward out of the crush. His arm was dead to the shoulder, cold and heavy as a felled tree, and the sigil on his wrist was glowing faintly through the skin, satisfied, like a cat after feeding.

*Well done,* Vesh said softly. *You see? Mercy. Nothing bought, nothing owed. Ask the mother whether the price was too high.*

"It wasn't mercy," Sable muttered. He was shaking. He could not stop shaking. "It was advertising."

*Perhaps.* The voice receded, sinking back beneath the floor of his mind, but its last words rose clearly through the closing dark, and they were almost kind:

*But they saw you, scribe. And in Noor, being seen is the one debt that cannot be paid in ink.*

Across the square, above the heads of the celebrating crowd, Sable caught a flash of gray robes and a counting-stick held like a baton. Magistrate Corren's overseer had stopped mid-stride. Was staring—at the boy, at the mother, and then, slowly, deliberately, at the space in the crowd where Sable Reyes had been kneeling.

And turning away, unhurried, to report.
