## 6. Two Kinds of Faith

*Ilan*

The valley smelled wrong to her.

Not foul—wrong the way a locked room smells wrong, all its air gone stale with disuse. Ilan had crossed three mountain passes and half a desert to reach Noor, and every mile of the journey had been loud with divinity: roadside shrines humming, rivers that remembered their names, the low constant chorus of small gods jostling for attention like beggars at a feast. Then she had crested the last ridge at dusk and felt it fall away beneath her, clean as a blade drawn from flesh. Silence. A silence so complete it had teeth.

She had wept, the first night, in the room above the dyer's shop she'd rented with coin she could not afford. Not from grief. From the sheer strangeness of quiet—of lying down without something holy pressing against her ribs, asking, asking, always asking.

Now, four days later, she walked the market terraces of Noor in the gray light before the stalls opened, and she watched him.

The scribe moved through the crowd like water through reeds, careful never to touch anyone. He carried his ledger case against his chest the way other men carried children. He was thin in the way of people who had learned to eat slowly, and he flinched—not visibly, not enough for most eyes, but enough for hers—every time he passed one of the blank shrines that dotted the valley like blind sockets where eyes had been put out.

And beneath his left cuff, wrapped in linen he thought no one noticed him adjusting, the sigil burned. She could feel it from twenty paces. It sang to her the way a struck bell sings after the sound has supposedly ended—a vibration in the silence, a wound in the world's skin.

*Vesh*, she thought. *You found yourself a vessel in the one place you were buried.*

She did not approach him. Not yet. She had learned patience in the temple schools beyond the mountains, where the gods came to the novices in dreams and taught them that hunger was the beginning of all wisdom. The gods outside were waking, starved by centuries of thinning faith, and they were growing careless, and desperate, and cruel. Her order had sent her east with a simple charge: find the source of answered prayers in the godless valley. Find out how a god was feeding inside a cage.

She had expected a heretic. A hidden shrine. A corrupted priest.

Instead she had found a scribe who burned his own pages and hid his wrist like a boy hiding a stolen sweet, and who, two days ago, had knelt in this market and laid his hands on a dying child—and the child had lived.

Ilan bought roasted chestnuts from a stall whose owner blessed her in the flat, careful way of a man reciting a law rather than a prayer, and she ate them slowly, and she waited, and she watched the scribe pretend, so beautifully, so desperately, to be no one at all.

* *

*Sable*

He knew she was following him. He had known since the second day, when he'd caught her reflection in the dye-vats' coppery water—tall, dark-robed, foreign, watching him with an expression that was neither suspicion nor pity but something far worse: recognition.

Today she stopped pretending.

"Scribe," she said, stepping into the alley behind the ledger-house just as he passed, and her accent turned the word into music, into something old. "You dropped this."

She held out nothing. Her hands were empty. But Sable's heart slammed once against his ribs, because there was only one reason a stranger would invent a pretext so clumsy, and that reason was wrapped in linen under his cuff.

"I didn't drop anything."

"No." She smiled, and it was a terrible smile, warm and knowing. "You didn't. You burned it. I watched the ash come out of your chimney for three nights. You burn everything now, don't you? Everything the ink says."

"Who are you?" His voice came out steadier than he felt. He had spent his whole life being invisible; invisibility was a craft, and he was good at it, but this woman looked at him the way Magistrate Corren looked at discrepancies in a ledger—as if she could see straight through to the sum underneath.

"Ilan." She said her own name like an offering. "Of the Order of the Waking Word, from beyond the Sereth Mountains. And you are the answer to a question my whole order is asking." She stepped closer. Up close her eyes were the brown of river-stones, patient, worn smooth. "May I see your wrist?"

"No."

"Good," she said softly. "A wise refusal. It means you understand what you're carrying." She glanced up the alley, toward the ledger-house windows, then back. "There's a shrine on the western terraces—the empty one, the one called the Mouth. Meet me there tonight, after the lamps are lit. If you don't come, I'll leave you alone. That's a promise. But if you do come, I'll tell you what's happening outside this valley, and why the silence you've been breathing your whole life isn't peace. It's a held breath."

She walked away before he could refuse again, which he suspected was the point.

Sable stood in the alley a long time, listening to his own pulse. He should report her. Foreigners were registered at the gate; unregistered contact with temple property—he was temple property, ink and sinew both, until the debt was paid—was a fineable offense. Corren would want her name.

He thought about the child in the market. The fevered little girl with the gray lips, whose mother had been screaming, and how his hands had moved on their own, and how the heat had poured out of him like water through a broken jar, and how the girl had opened her eyes and asked for bread.

He thought about how he had run home afterward and scrubbed his hands until they bled.

That night, after the lamps were lit, he went to the Mouth.

* *

*Ilan*

The empty shrine was a bowl of black stone, older than the Founders' Bargain, older perhaps than the valley itself. Its god—if it had ever had one—had been the first thing Noor devoured. Ilan sat on its rim with her boots off, her bare feet on cold stone, feeling the deep hum of the imprisoned thing below the valley floor, faint as a heartbeat through a wall.

He came. Of course he came. The curious always came; that was the tragedy and glory of mortals, that they could be starved of wonder for generations and still walk toward it in the dark.

"You came," she said anyway, because some things deserved to be said aloud.

"Don't flatter yourself," the scribe said. "I came to tell you to leave Noor. Whatever you're selling, no one here is buying." He stood at the edge of the lamplight, arms crossed, chin up—a man performing courage he did not feel. She liked him enormously for it.

"All right," Ilan said. "Then listen while I tell you, and then send me away."

She told him.

She told him about the mountains, where the river-gods had grown so hungry they had begun taking the memories of fishermen as offerings. About the plains kingdoms, where a harvest god had answered a drought-prayer so thoroughly that the wheat grew through the coffins of the dead. About the war-god Ashkarat waking in the south, and the priests who fed him battles the way farmers fed oxen. About the thinning, everywhere, the long starvation, the gods grown strange and grasping because faith had become a currency hoarded by temples instead of a fire tended in every hearth.

"And here," she said, laying her palm flat on the black stone, "here is the cruelest joke of all. Your Founders didn't banish the gods, scribe. They took one hostage. Vesh—the greatest of them, the one the others loved and feared. They sealed him under this valley and made his imprisonment the price of the others' distance. *Stay away, or we hurt him.* Every god beyond those mountains has kept that bargain for three hundred years. They hate this place. They grieve this place. And lately—" she met his eyes— "lately they've begun to wonder if the bargain still holds. Because something in Noor has started answering prayers. Something has started spending power. And a caged god does not spend power unless someone has opened the cage door from the inside."

"That wasn't me," Sable said. His voice cracked on the second word. "I didn't open anything. I copied ledgers. I paid my family's debt one page at a time. I never prayed—I *never*—"

"I know." She gentled her voice. "It doesn't work that way. Vessels aren't chosen for their prayers. They're chosen for their wounds. A god looks for a soul shaped like a keyhole." She rose, slow, giving him time to flee. He didn't flee. "Your peace is real, scribe. I won't lie to you—it's real, and it's precious, and I have seen what its absence looks like. But it is also a cage, and you are standing inside it holding the lock, and sooner or later the temple that owns your debt is going to notice the rattling."

"The temple keeps the peace," he said, but it sounded rehearsed, a line learned from childhood, and they both heard it.

"The temple keeps the *silence*," Ilan said. "Those aren't the same thing. Peace is something living. Silence is just the absence of anyone daring to speak."

* *

*Sable*

He should have walked away. Instead he heard himself say: "Come with me. Tomorrow. There's someone I want you to see."

She came.

The child's name was Mirei, and she lived in the weavers' quarter, in a room that smelled of wool-grease and woodsmoke. Her mother, Talla, had been a laundress in the temple before her husband's debts folded into Sable's family's like two streams meeting; Sable had known her since he was small enough to sleep under ledger tables while his father worked. When they arrived, Mirei was sitting up in bed eating porridge, pink-cheeked and furious about the taste, and Talla wept when she saw Sable in the doorway and pressed her forehead to his hand and called him blessed, and he stood there rigid with shame while the word *blessed* rang in a valley where blessing was contraband.

"She was dead," Talla whispered. "Or near enough. Three days of fever, and the physicians said make her comfortable. And then you came through the market, and—" She kissed his knuckles. "Whatever saint walks with you, scribe, thank it for me."

Afterward, in the street, Ilan was quiet a long moment.

"You saved her," she said finally.

"It wasn't me."

"No. It was Vesh, leaking through you like light through a cracked shutter. I know. But you *knelt*. The god gave the power and you chose where it went." She turned to face him fully, and the evening light caught the gold rings at her ears, and Sable realized he had been looking at her mouth while she spoke and forced his eyes up, too late, because she had noticed, and she was smiling—not the terrible knowing smile from the alley, but a smaller one, startled, almost shy. "Do you understand what you are, Sable Reyes? Not the vessel. *You.* You're proof it can be otherwise. A god's power in merciful hands. Outside these mountains, that's a heresy they burn people for saying. Here it's a miracle nobody will admit happened."

"My debt," he said, because it was the truest wall he had. "Corren owns eleven years of my life. Whatever I am, I'm collateral first."

"Is that all you are?" she asked quietly.

And because the question was asked gently, and because the evening smelled of bread and cooling stone, and because he had not been *seen*—truly seen, wounds and keyhole-shape and all—since his father died, Sable heard himself answer honestly.

"No. But I've never been allowed to find out what the rest is."

Ilan reached out and touched his wrapped wrist, feather-light, over the linen, over the sigil, over the sleeping god. The mark flared warm between them like a coal breathed upon, and Sable gasped, and she withdrew her hand at once, apology in her face—but neither of them stepped back.

"Two kinds of faith," she murmured. "Mine says the gods deserve freedom. Yours says the valley deserves safety. We're going to break each other's hearts over this, you know."

"Probably," Sable said.

Neither of them moved away.

They stood in the amber light of the weavers' quarter while the lamps of Noor came alight one by one, and it was Ilan who saw them first—three men in magistrate gray turning onto the lane behind them, moving with the unhurried purpose of people following a scent. One of them carried a ledger open in his hands, reading as he walked.

"That's the third time today," Sable breathed. "They were at the market this morning. Asking questions."

Ilan's hand found his, hidden between their bodies, and gripped it.

"Then we'd better decide quickly," she said, "which kind of faithful you are—because I think someone just started counting."
