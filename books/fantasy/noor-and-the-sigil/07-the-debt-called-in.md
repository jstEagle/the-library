## 7. The Debt Called In

The summons came at dawn, folded into the hand of a temple courier who would not meet my eyes.

I had learned to read people the way I read ink—by what bled through the surface—and this boy's silence was a confession written in a language I knew too well. He stood in the doorway of my room above the chandler's shop, rain dripping off his oilskin cloak onto floorboards I scrubbed every seventh day to keep my rent honest, and he held out the letter as though it might bite him.

"From Magistrate Corren," he said. "He asks that you come before the third bell."

*Asks.* Corren did not ask. Corren summoned, and men summoned by Corren came back changed—lighter of purse, or lighter of something harder to name.

I took the letter with steady hands because I had practiced steadiness my whole life. Inside, on paper heavy enough to be official, was a single line in Corren's narrow, precise script:

*Present yourself to the Hall of Ledgers. Bring nothing. Tell no one.*

Beneath it, smaller, almost an afterthought: *Your account has been reviewed.*

My account. My debt. The number that had hung over my family for three generations like weather—the eleven hundred crowns my grandfather borrowed to buy grain during the blight year, the interest that grew even as we starved it, the scribe's indenture I had signed at fourteen because it was the only way to keep my mother's house from the temple's hands. Eleven hundred crowns had become nine hundred and forty. Nine hundred and forty became eight hundred and twelve. I could recite the figure the way other men recited prayers.

*Reviewed.* As if it were a thing that could be reviewed. As if it were not simply true, fixed, eternal as the valley's stone.

I burned the letter in the chandler's stove and watched the ash curl, and I told myself the dread in my chest was only about money.

It was not about money. It had not been about money since the night ink moved under my hand and spelled out words in a tongue dead two thousand years, since the sigil took root on my wrist like a seed of fire. Since Vesh began to speak to me in the dark behind my eyes, patient as erosion: *They caged me, little scribe. They wrote me into their ledgers and called the writing peace.*

I dressed. I told no one. And all the way down the wet streets of Noor, past the shuttered shrines and the blank-faced temples where no god had been welcome for six centuries, I felt the mark beneath my sleeve warming like a coal wrapped in wool.

* * *

The Hall of Ledgers smelled of tallow and old vellum, that smell I had once loved more than any perfume—the smell of order, of everything in its place, of a world that could be counted and therefore controlled. I had spent ten years of my life inside that smell. This morning it made me think of tombs.

Corren sat alone at the long magistrate's table, and the room around him was conspicuously empty. No clerks. No witnesses. Just the two of us and forty years of Noor's accounts stacked in shelves along the walls, the whole valley's history of borrowing and repayment, sin and settlement.

"Sable Reyes." He did not look up from the page before him. "Sit."

I sat. My hands, I noted distantly, were perfectly still in my lap. Ten years of copying had taught them discipline my heart had never learned.

"I've been going through the market records," Corren said. He turned the page toward me at last. His face was mild, gray, unremarkable—a man designed by some careful god to pass unnoticed, which was perhaps why he noticed everything. "Third month. A child, name of Tessaly Wren, carried into the herb-seller's row dying of lung-rot. Two physicians had already refused her. Her mother paid a copper for a blessing she didn't believe in, because mothers do that." He tapped the page. "The child walked home before sundown."

"The herb-sellers mix willow and honey into most of their tonics," I said. "Children rally. It's well documented—"

"It is," Corren agreed pleasantly. "And I would have let it go. One miracle is a rumor. But then there was the well on Tanner's Lane that ran sweet again after six years of brackish water. And the fever that broke across the Weavers' Quarter in a single night, all at once, like a candle snuffed. And—" his finger moved down the column, each entry a small precise wound, "—the fact that every one of these events falls within four streets of wherever you happen to be working that day."

The sigil pulsed against my wrist. Warm. Almost affectionate. *Tell him nothing,* Vesh murmured, and for once the god's voice and my own survival agreed.

"You're suggesting I'm healing people," I said. "Magistrate, I copy ledgers. The last living thing I touched was a moth I accidentally drowned in my inkwell."

Something flickered at the corner of Corren's mouth—not quite a smile. "Do you know why the Founders built no statues?" he asked. "Every other city in the world raises its gods in bronze and marble. Noor has none. Because the Founders understood that a god does not need a statue. A god needs only a door." He rose, unhurried, and came around the table until he stood close enough that I could smell the ink on his fingers, the same ink I used, the same trade, and hated how much we were alike. "Show me your left wrist, scribe."

The room went very quiet. Rain ticked against the high windows.

"If you refuse," he continued, in the tone of a man reading terms from a contract, "I will invoke Statute Nine. First Founding. It has never been repealed, because it has never been needed." He recited it from memory, and I heard in his voice that he had loved this law his whole life the way I had loved ledgers—as proof the world could be made safe by writing things down. "*Any vessel bearing the mark of an imprisoned god is forfeit to the Temple of Noor, body and soul, without trial, appeal, or term of debt.*"

Forfeit. Body and soul. I thought of my mother's house, of the debt that would die with me unpaid, of Ilan's face in the lamplight saying *the gods are starving, Sable, and your valley built the famine.*

Slowly, because there was no other move left on the board, I pushed back my sleeve.

The sigil lay on my skin like a brand of black fire, its lines shifting when you weren't looking directly at them, rearranging themselves the way water rearranges around a stone. It had grown since the night it first appeared. It was nearly complete. I had known that for weeks and had done nothing, because doing nothing was the one skill Noor had ever taught me well.

Corren looked at it for a long moment. Whatever he felt, he kept it behind his gray face, locked away like everything else. Then he straightened and said, almost gently, "You understand that I cannot allow this."

"You understand," I said, and my voice surprised me by being steady, "that if you take me, the mark completes anyway. Your statute doesn't stop the god. It just delivers him to the temple in a convenient wrapper."

"That is a matter for wiser heads than mine." He turned away, already writing, already filing me into the great machine of the ledger. "You'll be held in the undercroft until the Conclave assembles. Three days. Confess nothing. Sign nothing." A pause. "And Sable—if you run, they will take your mother's house. That is also in the statutes."

* * *

They should have bound me. In fairness, they tried—but the moment the iron touched the sigil, the metal went soft as wax, and the guard who held the shackles screamed and dropped them, and after that no one wanted to be the one to touch me. So they simply locked the undercroft door and trusted in stone, which is how the gods have always gotten in.

Ilan came to me at moonrise, the way water finds a crack.

She didn't knock; the lock simply opened for her, or perhaps she'd bribed the guard, or perhaps her foreign goddess had opinions about doors. She slipped inside smelling of cold air and road dust and something resinous, incense from beyond the mountains, and when she saw me sitting in the dark she crossed the cell and knelt and took my chained hands in hers, and her palms were warm, impossibly warm, warm as a hearth in a country I'd never seen.

"They're going to give me to the Conclave," I said. "Statute Nine. Forfeit."

"I know. I listened outside the hall." Her thumb moved over my knuckles. "Sable. There's a way out. The catacombs under the Founders' Tomb—the old pilgrim roads, predating the Bargain. My maps show a passage that surfaces beyond the eastern scree. Three days' walk and you're over the mountains, out of Noor, out of their law."

"And into yours."

She didn't flinch. That was the thing about Ilan; she never flinched from truth, even when it cut her. "Yes. Into mine. Where the gods are waking, and hungry, and where a completed vessel would be—" she exhaled, "—welcomed. Revered. Used. I won't lie to you about what waits out there. But it isn't a cell, and it isn't a knife, and it isn't Corren deciding your life in a room that smells of tallow."

"And if I leave," I said quietly, "the sigil completes. You told me. Distance, danger, desperation—it feeds on all of it. Every mile east is a line filling in. I walk out of Noor a man and arrive somewhere else as a door."

"Yes."

"Then I don't just betray Corren. I betray everyone who ever prayed in a shrineless city and slept soundly because nothing answered. Six hundred years of peace, bought with a cage, and I carry the key out of the valley in my own skin."

Ilan knelt back on her heels. In the dark her eyes caught what little light there was, and I saw she was crying without any drama at all, tears simply present, like weather. "And if you stay," she said, "you betray yourself. You betray the child you saved. You betray every soul outside these mountains whose gods are dying of thirst while Noor hoards the spring." Her voice cracked and she mastered it. "There is no clean choice. I have crossed half the world looking for one, and I am telling you now, from experience: there is no clean choice. There is only the choice you can live inside."

The sigil burned. Not painfully anymore—that was the horror of it; it had stopped hurting weeks ago, the way a lie stops feeling like a lie once you repeat it enough. *Go,* Vesh whispered, silk-soft. *Let them chase us. Let the mountains ring with war. You were never meant for ledgers, little scribe. You were meant for legend.*

"No," I said aloud, to both of them.

Ilan looked up.

"Not the legend. Not the cage either." I pulled my hands free of the chains—they opened for me now too, which should have frightened me more than it did—and stood, and the mark on my wrist threw faint black light against the undercroft walls like a lantern made of absence. "But I can't stay. If I stay, Corren hands me to the Conclave, and the Conclave does something desperate, and desperate men complete prophecies. So I'll run. I'll run knowing what running costs." I reached down and took Ilan's face in my hands, this woman from a world of waking gods who had seen me truly before anyone else dared to. "Take me through the tomb. Take me out. And when we get there—to whatever waits beyond the mountains—I want your word that when the time comes, you'll help me end this. Not wield it. End it."

She searched my eyes for a long moment. Outside, far above, the third bell began to toll, and somewhere in the temple a door was surely already opening, and men with lamps were finding an empty cell.

"You have it," she whispered. "Whatever it costs."

We went down into the dark together, and behind us Noor slept on, peaceful and blind, dreaming its six-hundred-year dream of a world with no gods in it—while beneath its streets, its last god's chosen vessel ran for the border, carrying the ending of everything in the lines of his own skin.
