## 8. The Founders' Tomb

The dark beneath Noor was older than the city above it.

Ilan had walked catacombs before—in the bone-halls of Ashkarat, in the drowned shrines of the southern coast—but those had been places of worship, tended and candle-lit, their dead arranged with love. This was different. These tunnels had been made to be forgotten. The air tasted of dust and old iron, and every breath she drew felt borrowed from something that did not want to give it back.

Sable moved ahead of her with a stolen lantern held low, its flame guttering in the draft that breathed up from somewhere deeper. His shoulders were drawn tight beneath his scribe's coat, and his free hand kept drifting to his wrist—to the sigil—as though checking whether it still burned. It did. She could feel it from three paces back, a heat like a coal wrapped in linen, a second pulse out of rhythm with his own.

"Left here," she said, consulting the map she'd memorized from the temple archive—the one page Corren's clerks had never thought to guard, because no one in living memory had cared what lay beneath the valley.

"You're certain?"

"No." She let him hear the honesty in it. "But the map is, or it was when it was drawn. Four hundred years ago."

He glanced back at her, and in the lantern light his face was all hollows and shadow, the face of a man who had spent one day becoming a fugitive and already wore it like an old coat. "Four hundred years," he repeated. "And no one comes down here."

"No one who wants to remember."

They went left.

* * *

The stairs descended in long switchbacks, each step cut low and shallow, built for processions rather than flight—or perhaps for prisoners being led down, Ilan thought, and could not unthink it. The walls changed as they descended: first dressed stone, fitted tight and smooth; then rougher blocks, hurried; then, near the bottom, the raw black rock of the valley's bones, carved only where carving was needed.

Somewhere above them, muffled by forty feet of stone, Noor was waking to the news that a scribe had run. She imagined Corren at his desk, ink on his fingers, writing Sable Reyes's name into a ledger with the same unhurried hand he used for grain tariffs. She imagined the gates closing. They would not be caught above ground. That was the one mercy of this place: a city built to keep gods out was also built to keep everything else in, and its people had forgotten how to look down.

"Sable," she said softly. "Tell me again what Vesh said to you. Before we reach whatever we're reaching."

His lantern paused. "You think I'll forget between here and there?"

"I think you heard a god speak inside your own skull, and you've been carrying it alone for two days. I think words spoken once can lie to us, and words repeated start telling the truth." She stepped closer, until the warmth of the sigil reached her like a hand held near a hearth. "What did he promise?"

"That he'd erase my debt." A dry, bitter almost-laugh. "That he'd make me *free*, Ilan. A god offering a scribe freedom. He knew exactly which wound to press."

"And what did he ask?"

Sable was quiet for so long that the drip of water somewhere in the dark counted ten beats for her.

"He asked me to want him," he said finally. "Not to serve. Not to obey. He said—" His voice thinned. "He said worship is just wanting that's been made afraid of itself. He wanted me to want him without the fear."

Ilan closed her eyes. In the temples beyond the mountains, they taught that the gods were hunger given names, that prayer was feeding, that faith was a bargain struck in both directions and honored by neither party more than it must be. But this—this was not hunger. Hunger takes. What Sable described sounded closer to loneliness, vast enough to crack a valley open.

"Keep walking," she said gently. "We're close."

* * *

The tunnel ended at a door.

It was not grand. That was the first wrongness. For the tomb of the Founders—the nine men and women whose Bargain every child in Noor learned before they learned their letters—Ilan had expected bronze and basalt, wards carved thick as armor. Instead there was a slab of plain stone, unmarked except for a single line of script worn nearly smooth, in a language she recognized only because she had spent six years studying dead tongues: Old Nooric, the speech of the founding generation, abandoned within a century of the Bargain itself.

Abandoned deliberately, she suspected. Languages could be killed like anything else.

"Can you read it?" Sable asked.

"Some." She traced the air above the letters without touching them. "It says: *We were nine. We are sorry.*"

Sable stared at her. "That's it? No blessing? No warning?"

"That's it."

He put his shoulder to the stone. It ground aside on hinges that had not been oiled since the founders died, shrieking like something alive, and cold air rolled out over them—air that had been sealed away for four centuries, air that smelled of wax and blood and, beneath both, of something scorched. Something burned very badly, very long ago.

The lantern flame bent toward the opening, as if eager.

They went in.

* * *

The chamber beyond was round, and it was wrong in ways Ilan's eyes took a long moment to catalog. Nine alcoves ringed the wall, and in eight of them lay skeletons laid out with ceremony—robed, hands folded, faces turned toward the center of the room. The ninth alcove stood empty, its floor stained dark, its occupant evidently removed or never interred.

But the center. The center of the room was not a sarcophagus, as the temple diagrams had promised. It was a pit—a shaft sunk into the rock, perhaps twenty feet across, ringed by a low parapet of stone. Chains ran down into it. Not iron chains; even in the lantern light Ilan could see they were something else, links of a dull grey metal that seemed to drink the light rather than reflect it, and they did not hang slack. They hung *taut*. All of them. Straining downward into the dark with the tension of ropes holding something that pulled.

From the shaft rose a sound. Low. Rhythmic. She might have taken it for wind in a deep place, if wind had a heartbeat.

"Oh," Sable said. Very quietly. "Oh, no."

"What is it? What do you see?"

"Nothing. That's—" His voice cracked. "There's nothing down there. There's nothing *left* down there, and the chains are still pulling."

Ilan approached the parapet the way one approaches a sleeping animal, heel to toe, breath shallow. She looked over the edge.

The shaft descended farther than the lantern's light could follow. But partway down, carved into the inner wall in letters taller than a man, the same Old Nooric script marched around the circumference—and this time the words had been cut deep and filled with lead, filled so they would last, because someone had needed them to last:

*WE DID NOT IMPRISON HIM. WE INVITED HIM DOWN, AND CLOSED THE DOOR.*

Below that, smaller, in a different hand:

*HE CAME WHEN WE CALLED. HE LEFT HIS WAR BECAUSE WE ASKED. THIS IS HOW WE THANKED HIM.*

And below that, smallest of all, half-eroded, almost pleading:

*DO NOT WORSHIP WHAT YOU HAVE CHAINED. IT TEACHES HIM TO HATE THE HAND THAT FEEDS HIM.*

Ilan gripped the parapet and made herself breathe. Around her, the taut chains hummed faintly, a note below hearing, below bearing.

"They didn't defeat him," she said. Her voice came out strange, flattened by the weight of the room. "Sable. Do you understand what this is? The Founders' Bargain—the peace of Noor—it isn't a victory. It's a hostage-taking. They called a god down with prayers, with four hundred years of their ancestors' devotion, and when he set his war aside because they asked—because they *asked*, and he trusted them—they shut the door behind him and built a city on the lid."

Sable hadn't moved. He stood among the eight kneeling skeletons with the lantern raised, and his face had gone the color of the old wax pooled in the alcoves.

"My ancestor," he said.

"What?"

"The ninth alcove." He pointed with the hand that didn't hold the lantern, and she saw now that his finger was shaking. "Eight bodies. Nine founders. My family has been temple scribes since the founding—debt-bound since the founding, do you understand? Every record, every ledger, every law. We kept the accounts." His laugh was terrible. "Someone had to write down what they did. Someone had to *keep* it. And the name on the founding charter—the first signatory, the one my grandmother used to recite like a prayer—Reyes. *Reyes.*"

He turned to the empty alcove as if it might deny him.

"It wasn't eight of them who betrayed him. It was nine. And mine wrote it all down afterward and called it scripture."

* * *

Ilan did not know how long they stood there. Time behaved strangely in the tomb; the heartbeat-sound from the shaft ate the hours. At some point she found herself beside him, and at some point her hand found his, and neither of them remarked on it, because there are rooms in which touch is the only honest language left.

"Ilan," he said at last. His voice was wrecked and steady at once, the way a man sounds after deciding something enormous. "When I refused him—when I told Vesh I wouldn't be his vessel—I said the valley didn't need gods. I said it proudly. I thought I was defending the peace." He looked down at their joined hands. "I was defending a prison. I've been defending a prison my whole life. My debt, my servitude, my careful invisible little life—all of it built on top of a god who came when he was called and got chained for his trouble."

"Grief is allowed," she said. "Even for him. *Especially* for him."

"But don't you see what it means?" He faced her fully then, and the sigil on his wrist flared through his sleeve, throwing red light up across his jaw. "If the Bargain was a betrayal, then everything Vesh said was true. He doesn't want conquest. He wants—he's been down there four hundred years wanting to be *wanted*, and the ones who wanted him first are the ones who locked the door. And I'm—" His breath hitched. "I'm the blood of the man who wrote the lock. Of course it's me. Of course the sigil chose a Reyes. It's not a gift, Ilan. It's a *bill*. Four hundred years overdue, come to my family's account."

The chains shivered.

All of them, at once, a sound like a thousand bells struck under water, and the heartbeat in the shaft stumbled—quickened. Ilan felt the change in her teeth, in the wet of her eyes, in the sigil's heat blooming through Sable's skin and into her palm where she held it.

Down in the dark, something that had slept through four centuries of silence turned its attention upward, toward the two small warm lives standing at the lip of its grave.

And far above them, muffled but unmistakable, stone boomed against stone: the tomb door, grinding shut.

Footsteps followed. Many. Descending the switchback stairs with the unhurried cadence of soldiers who knew exactly where their quarry had gone—because their magistrate had known about this room all along.

Sable's grip tightened on her hand. "Corren," he whispered.

The sigil burned like a brand, and from the shaft below rose a voice—not heard so much as remembered, ancient and patient and terribly glad:

*At last. Someone has come to pay.*
