## 10. Noor, Unwritten

The sigil did not want to die.

Sable felt that truth in the marrow of his own bones as he stood inside the burning architecture of his own mind, where Vesh had made a palace of him. The god wore Sable's body like a crown wears a head—beautifully, and without permission. Through eyes not entirely his own, Sable saw the tomb's antechamber: Corren's soldiers scattered like dropped dice, Ilan on her knees with her hands raised, not in surrender but in something older than surrender. Supplication. Or warning.

*Stop,* Sable thought at the god. *You don't have to do this.*

Vesh laughed, and the laugh was an avalanche somewhere behind Sable's ribs. *I have waited nine hundred years in the dark of your valley, little scribe. I will not stop for your sentiment.*

But Sable had spent three days inside his own skull with the god now, and he had learned to read Vesh the way he read ledgers—line by line, looking for what was written between the entries. And what was written between them, always, everywhere, in every boast and every threat, was this: *look at me.*

Not *obey me.* Not even *fear me.* Just—*look.*

A god who wanted to win would have taken Corren's soldiers apart already. A god who wanted to win would have marched on the temple by now, would have cracked the Founders' seals and called the old war up out of the ground like a harvest. Instead Vesh stood in the tomb of the mortals who had betrayed him, wearing a scribe's scarred hands, and let himself be seen.

That was the flaw in the anchor. That was the crack in the sigil.

*I know what you are,* Sable said, and he made his voice quiet, the way one speaks in a sickroom. *You're not a war-god. You're a lonely one.*

The palace around them shuddered. Walls of memory and stolen fire flickered like lampflame in a draft.

*Careful, vessel.*

*Your worshippers chained you because you needed them too much.* Sable walked forward through the god's mindscape, through halls built from the prayers of dead soldiers, and the halls trembled at his passing. *They couldn't bear being loved that much. So they bought peace with your imprisonment and called it wisdom. And you've been down here ever since, waiting for someone—anyone—to pray again. Even if it was only a debt-bound boy copying ledgers, whose ink moved because you were so starved you'd speak to anyone.*

*Stop.*

*You don't want Noor,* Sable said. *You never wanted Noor. You want to be wanted. And I am so sorry, Vesh. I'm sorry for all of it. But I can't give you that. Nobody can give you that. It isn't mine to give.*

He found the center of the palace. Of course it was small. Of course it was plain. A single room, and in it a single image: a circle of worshippers around a fire, faces lifted, hands open, singing a name. His name. Nine hundred years ago, and still the god kept this room swept clean, kept the fire lit, kept waiting for his people to come home.

Sable knelt in front of it the way one kneels before a grave.

*This is beautiful,* he said, and meant it, and felt the god flinch at the sincerity. *And it's over. They're gone. All of them. The ones who loved you and the ones who feared you and the ones who betrayed you—they're dust, Vesh. And the valley they built doesn't need a god. It needs bread and ink and someone to heal its children. It needs to be left alone.*

*If you unmake the anchor, you unmake yourself,* Vesh said, and there was no thunder in it now. There was something almost like pleading. *The sigil is woven into your blood. Shatter it and you shatter with it.*

*I know.*

*You will be nothing. Ordinary. Empty.*

*I've been empty before,* Sable said, thinking of a ledger hall, of careful invisible years, of a life measured out in other people's debts. *It turns out emptiness is just room.*

He reached into the heart of the sigil—the true one, the one burning beneath his skin in the world outside, the anchor that held a god to a valley—and he did what he had done every working day of his life.

He broke the seal.

* * *

Pain first. Pain like a page being torn lengthwise, like a book pulled apart at the spine, like every word he had ever written being scraped off the vellum of him at once. He heard someone screaming and understood distantly that it was his own mouth, his own body, folded on the tomb floor while Ilan held his head and shouted words in her mountain tongue that were either prayers or curses or both.

And inside, Vesh screamed too—not in rage but in grief, the long keening howl of a thing losing the last room it had in the world. *Wait—wait—I was patient, I was so patient—*

*I know,* Sable said, and held on to the god as the sigil came apart, because no one should come undone alone. *I know. Go gently. Go wherever gods go when no one remembers their names. I'll remember yours. For a while. As long as I can.*

The sigil shattered like ice in warm water.

The light went out of the tomb. The pressure went out of the air. Somewhere far above, Sable thought he heard the temple bells ring—though perhaps that was only his blood leaving his head—and then there was nothing at all, a darkness so complete and so quiet that for one absurd moment he thought: *finally, some peace and quiet.*

Then, from very far away, a voice he knew, fraying at the edges: *"Stay. Stay, you stubborn, impossible man, stay—"*

He stayed. Barely. But he stayed.

* * *

He woke to the smell of bread.

Not incense, not blood, not the cold mineral breath of the catacombs—bread, ordinary yeast-and-grain bread, baking somewhere nearby, and beneath it the particular dusty sweetness of the ledger hall, which meant he was in his own narrow room above the scriptorium, which meant—

"You're awake." Ilan's voice, hoarse, from the chair beside his bed. She looked terrible. She looked wonderful. Her priestess's braids had half-collapsed and there was an ink stain on her jaw that he suspected was his fault. "Three weeks," she said. "You slept for three weeks. I aged forty years. Don't do it again."

"The sigil?"

"Gone." She took his hand and turned it over. His wrist was bare except for an old scar from a penknife, years old, ordinary. Where the mark had burned, the skin was smooth and pale and utterly unremarkable. "There's nothing left. I've checked every way I know. The magic didn't move elsewhere—it didn't flee into the walls or another vessel. It's simply... ended. Whatever Vesh was, he isn't anymore. Not anchored, not caged. Unwritten."

Sable lay still and listened to himself. His heartbeat. His breath. The small sounds of a body with exactly one occupant.

It should have felt like loss. It felt like setting down a weight he'd forgotten he was carrying.

"Corren?" he asked.

Ilan's expression did something complicated. "Alive. Demoted past demotion—he's been assigned to the granary tallies. He comes by sometimes." She paused. "He stands in the doorway. Doesn't come in. Last week he left a jar of that honey you like. He didn't say anything about it."

"He thinks I saved the valley."

"He knows you saved the valley. That's worse, for a man like him. He built his whole life on keeping the cage locked, and you showed him the door was already open and nothing came through." She squeezed his hand. "The temple burned the vessel laws. Publicly. In the square. Magistrate Oyel read the proclamation herself. Your debt—" she hesitated, and her voice went carefully neutral, "—your family's debt was struck from the rolls. All of it. Every entry since your grandfather. The temple calls it restitution."

Sable stared at the ceiling. Nineteen years of ledgers. Nineteen years of copying other men's numbers in other men's hands, buying back his family's freedom one careful line at a time. Erased. Not earned away—erased, given, forgiven.

"I didn't want it forgiven," he said quietly. "I wanted to pay it."

"I know." Ilan smiled, tired and crooked. "That's the most Noor thing about you. But listen to me, Sable Reyes: you paid. You paid more than any ledger ever asked. The temple just finally wrote it down."

* * *

Spring came to Noor the way it always did, grudgingly, the valley holding its mists until well past the equinox, and Sable walked out into it on legs that ached and worked.

He was ordinary now. He tested it the way a man tests a healed bone, cautiously, expecting the old pain. He copied letters; they stayed letters. He prayed—once, experimentally, alone, half-hoping and half-dreading—and the silence answered with silence, vast and clean and utterly indifferent, and he found he could breathe inside it.

Noor was still godless. The Founders' Bargain was ash and the Founders' Tomb was sealed with new stone, and the valley went on being what it had always been: a place of ledgers and orchards and arguments in the market, of children who would grow up never knowing what had nearly woken beneath their feet. Some nights Sable grieved for Vesh, quietly, the way one grieves a cruel parent. Most nights he simply slept.

Ilan stayed.

She had every reason to go—her order beyond the mountains, her waking starving gods, her whole life's mission—and she stayed anyway, and when the elders of her faith sent a delegation to retrieve her, she met them at the gate and told them, in front of witnesses, that the gods outside would have to learn to live unfed, because she had found a valley that had, and she intended to study how. They left without her. She watched them go with her hand in Sable's, and neither of them spoke until the road was empty.

"Well," she said. "Now what?"

"Now," Sable said, "I owe the scriptorium nothing, the temple nothing, the gods nothing, and you—" he turned to face her fully, this woman who had seen him truly when being seen was the most dangerous thing that had ever happened to him, "—you I choose to owe everything. If you'll have it."

Ilan laughed—a real laugh, unguarded, the first he'd ever heard from her that wasn't aimed at anyone. "Everything," she repeated. "That's a terrible contract. No terms, no exit clause. Any scribe worth his salt would strike it."

"I'm not a scribe anymore," Sable said. "I'm just a man in a valley with no gods in it. And I find I like the terms."

She kissed him there in the gate-road dust, with the mountains standing witness and no power in heaven or under the earth watching, because there was none, and it didn't matter, and it mattered more than anything.

That evening he sat at his desk by the window, in his narrow room above the scriptorium, and dipped his pen, and wrote—not a ledger, not a copy, not another man's words in another man's hand, but his own, in his own ink, drying at its own pace:

*In the valley of Noor there lived a scribe, who owed nothing to anyone, and chose everyone.*

He looked at the line for a long time. Then he blew on the page, watched the ink set, and smiled—because it stayed exactly where he had put it, and it was enough.
