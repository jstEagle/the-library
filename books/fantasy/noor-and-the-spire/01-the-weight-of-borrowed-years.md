## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Years

The night market in Grayhollow opened the way a wound does — slowly, and only after dark.

Wren Eastwick knew every seam of it. The tallow-lamps strung between the fishmongers' stalls, their light the color of old honey. The smell of brine and burnt sugar that clung to everything, even memory. The particular hush that fell over the crowd when someone walked past wearing a Warden's grey coat, the way water goes still before it freezes.

Wren had been coming here for fifteen years, and in all that time had never once let themselves be seen doing what they did. Selling was simple. Anyone could sell. What Wren did was *give* — and giving, in Grayhollow, was the closest thing to magic left above ground.

The stall was a fortune-teller's, which was the best kind of cover, because everyone already assumed fortune-tellers were frauds. Madame Serrit sat behind her velvet-draped table with a cracked crystal ball she'd bought at auction, and Wren stood beside her in the shadow of her awning as her "assistant," holding a lacquered box of charms that were, to any casual eye, nothing but pretty trinkets: bits of sea-glass, knucklebones, river pebbles drilled through with cord.

They were not river pebbles.

"Three coppers for the small ones," Serrit murmured to a dockhand leaning over the table, his breath thick with ale. "Five for anything blue."

The dockhand picked up a chip of blue glass and turned it in his fingers. Wren watched him the way they watched everyone — not his face but his hands, the tremor in them, the way his thumb worried the edge of the charm like a man touching a scar he couldn't remember earning.

"That one's a summer," Wren said quietly. "A real one. A woman on a roof in Highmoor, hanging washing, and her daughter laughing so hard she falls into the sheets. You'll have it whole. Ten seconds, maybe twelve."

The dockhand's eyes came up, wet and hungry. "Whose?"

"Gone from them." That was the part people always needed to hear first. "Taken clean. They won't miss it. Most folk don't even know what they've lost until you hand it back to them."

He paid. Everyone paid. Wren touched the charm with two fingers, felt the small warm pulse of it — a memory was a living thing, even stripped from its root, and it always woke a little when it sensed a new mind near — and pressed it into his palm. His eyes went distant. His mouth softened. For eleven seconds the dockhand stood in the middle of a fish market smelling of tar and offal, and he was on a rooftop in Highmoor with sunlight on his arms and a child laughing.

When it passed, he blinked, looked at the dead glass in his hand, and said, hoarsely, "Another."

"They're not wine," Wren said. "Come back next week."

That was the rule, and Wren enforced it harder than any law. One charm a customer, one week apart. Memory was a hunger like any other, and Wren had seen what happened to people who fed it without limit — the hollow-eyed regulars who drifted down to the underlevels chasing feelings they'd sold their own lives to rent. Wren stole memories for a living. Wren refused to make thieves of anyone else.

*Steal.* Even now, fifteen years on, the word snagged something in Wren's chest. Because that was the other thing about the trade, the thing no one in the market knew: the memories had to be taken from a living mind, willingly or no, and the taking left a mark. Wren could feel a stranger's grief the way a pickpocket feels a purse — a weight, a warmth, a shape — and close their hand around it, and draw it out. It didn't hurt the donor. That was the mercy of it, and also the horror. People gave up their worst days to Wren's touch like coins falling out of a torn pocket, and most of them never noticed the hole where the day had been.

The Council called it theft of the self. The Wardens called it resonance, and burned you for it.

Fifteen years ago, the last sanctioned mages of Grayhollow had done something at the Spire — the great tower at the city's heart, older than the walls, older than the kingdom, its crown dark now these fifteen years like a snuffed candle. Whatever covenant the Spire had anchored between the human realm and whatever lay beyond it, it had broken, or been broken, and the Council's answer had been to outlaw magic entire. As if the tower's death were a contagion carried in spells. As if burning hedge-witches could relight it.

Wren didn't know if that was true. Wren only knew that the night the Spire went dark was the same night Wren's own childhood went missing — and that the two facts had never once let each other go.

* * *

Business was good that night, which should have been the first warning. Good nights made Wren careless; lean nights kept the edges sharp. By the third bell past midnight the box held four charms fewer than it had at dusk, and Serrit was counting coin with the contented clink of a woman who would eat well this month, and Wren was letting their attention drift to the crowd — cataloguing sorrows, as always, half out of habit. A widow by the eel stall carrying a grief shaped like a wedding ring. Two brothers who hadn't spoken in years, each radiating the same argument like heat off a stove.

And then the hush.

It moved through the market the way cold moves through a room — not walking so much as arriving. Conversations thinned. A knife dropped somewhere and nobody laughed. Wren turned, slow, unhurried, an assistant with nothing in his hands but a box of trinkets, and saw them: three grey coats moving up the aisle between the stalls, lanterns hooded, batons holstered but hands resting near.

Wardens. Not the usual patrol — those were bored men collecting bribes from unlicensed gin-sellers. These three moved like hounds on a line. The tall one in front held something cupped in his palm, and every few steps he'd glance down at it, and every few steps his head would turn a degree, correcting course.

A compass. A resonance-needle. Wren had heard rumors of them for a year — instruments that could taste magic the way a tongue tastes copper — and had told himself, night after night, that the rumors were just that. Rumors traveled fast in Grayhollow precisely because they were usually wrong.

The needle pointed, faintly and steadily, toward the fortune-teller's stall.

Wren's heart did not speed up. Fifteen years had trained that out of it; fear arrived now as clarity, cold and total, the world going sharp-edged and silent. In that silence Wren took stock with the dispassion of a merchant reading a ledger. Four unsold charms in the box — evidence enough to hang them both. Serrit beside them, guilty by proximity, an old woman who had never stolen anything but a few dishonest fortunes. Thirty feet of open market between here and the alley behind the fishmongers'.

"Madame," Wren said softly, "your crystal ball is about to be very lucky."

Serrit's eyes flicked up. She was seventy if she was a day, and she had survived Grayhollow longer than Wren had been alive, and she understood in one look. She swept the lacquered box off the table and into her skirts in a motion smooth as pouring tea, and raised her voice into a patter: "Come, come, the ball does not lie tonight! Who will hear their fortune? You, sir? You have the look of a man crossed in love—"

The tall Warden stopped ten feet away.

Up close he was younger than his coat, with the flat patient face of someone who had learned to wait for a living. His gaze went over the stall — the ball, the velvet, the coins, Wren — and settled there with the weight of a hand on a shoulder.

"Assistant," he said. It wasn't a question. "Open the box."

"The box is madame's property," Wren said, mild as milk. "I only hold it."

"Then hold it out."

Behind the Warden, his companions had drifted wide, closing the angles, easy and unhurried, men who had done this a hundred times. Somewhere in the crowd a mother pulled her child back behind a stall of dried cod, and the gesture told Wren everything about how this would go if it went badly. Nobody in Grayhollow intervened for a thief of selves.

Wren's fingers found the box through Serrit's skirts — no, Serrit had it now, tucked against her hip beneath the velvet, and Wren's hand closed instead around the one thing at their belt that mattered. The little pouch. Inside it, wrapped in oilcloth, hung on a cord worn thin as thread: a single charm unlike all the others.

Not stolen. Not purchased. Found — or so Wren had always believed — fifteen years ago, in the ash-fall the morning the Spire died: a sliver of pale stone that held a fragment of a childhood. Sunlight through a window. Someone singing, low, in a language Wren had never heard anywhere else. Small hands. A name being called — *their* name, surely, though the memory cut off before the sound resolved, and always had.

Wren's own memory. The only piece of their own first six years that existed anywhere in the world. Every other charm in Wren's keeping belonged to somebody else; this one was the single plank of a self, and Wren had starved rather than sell it, had frozen rather than burn it, had slept with it under their tongue some nights like a coin saved against ruin.

If the Wardens took the box, they would take the pouch. If they took the pouch—

"Last chance," the young Warden said. "The box."

And then the market itself intervened, because Grayhollow loved no one but occasionally tripped over its own feet in your favor. A dray cart bound for the fish docks chose that exact moment to lose a wheel-rim against the cobbles; the driver cursed; the cart slewed; a barrel of ice-melt went over and flooded the aisle in a sheet of slush and silver light. The crowd surged back with a roar of complaint. Lanterns swung. Shadows leapt everywhere at once.

Wren moved inside that chaos the way water finds a crack. Left shoulder, low, three steps, the gap between the eel stall and the rope-seller's cart — a seam no wider than a coffin, known to Wren for fifteen years and used twice before. Behind them, Serrit's voice rose in magnificent outrage about ruined merchandise, drawing every eye, spending her safety like coin to buy Wren's.

Wren slid into the alley, into the dark, into the reek of the tide-flats, and ran — not fast, not panicked, but with the long steady stride of someone who had rehearsed this night in their head a hundred times and never once believed it would come.

At the mouth of the alley Wren paused, one breath, and looked back. The young Warden stood in the lamplight, slush to his knees, scanning the crowd. He wasn't looking at the fleeing shape. He was looking at his needle.

And the needle, even from here — even across thirty feet of screaming market — was still turning. Slowly. Following.

Following not the path Wren had fled, but the direction of Wren's garret. Of home.

Of the child who was, at that very moment, climbing the stairs to Wren's door.
