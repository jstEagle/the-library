## 2. A Knock at the Wrong Hour

Dawn in Grayhollow came the way debt collectors did — announced by no one, arriving anyway.

Wren Eastwick was still awake to meet it. They sat cross-legged on the floor of their garret above the tannery row, sorting the night's takings into their cases: a memory of first snowfall, bottled in a bead of amber glass; a sailor's last sight of his mother's face, folded into a curl of copper wire; a bride's terror and joy braided together in a knot of black thread. Each charm gave off a faint warmth when Wren's fingers passed over it, the way coals hold heat long after the fire has gone. Fifteen years of practice had taught them to sort by feel alone, eyes half-closed, letting the charms murmur against their skin like sleeping birds.

The knock came at the wrong hour for it.

Three soft raps, spaced too evenly — not the frantic hammering of someone fleeing Wardens, nor the drunken pounding of a tanner's apprentice who'd lost his key. Just three small knocks, patient as rain.

Wren went very still. In Grayhollow, you learned the grammar of doors. A knock at midnight meant trade. A knock before dawn meant trouble. And a knock that didn't demand entry but simply *asked* — that meant something had already decided to find them, and knocking was only a courtesy.

They set down the amber bead, slid a knife from their boot, and crossed the garret on silent feet. The room was a museum of other people's lives: shelves of labeled jars, bundles of ribbon-wrapped memories hanging from the rafters like dried herbs, a single cot with a blanket worn thin from fifteen years of one body turning in it. Through the salt-fogged window, the Spire stood against the graying sky at the city's heart, black and vast and dead as a burnt finger pointing at heaven. It had been dead longer than anyone could remember. The Wardens said it had never been alive at all.

Wren pressed an eye to the peephole they'd drilled years ago and felt the knife go loose in their grip.

A child stood on the landing. Eight years old, perhaps nine — underfed in the way of Grayhollow's foundlings, wrapped in a coat two sizes too large with the sleeves rolled and re-rolled. Dark hair cut raggedly, as if with a fish knife. And eyes that caught the first gray light and held it, pale as river ice, fixed on the door with the unnerving certainty of someone who knew exactly who was on the other side.

No parents in sight. No Wardens. Just the child, and the fog, and the far-off groan of the tannery vats beginning their day.

Wren should have waited until the child gave up. That was the rule. You didn't open your door to strays; strays brought questions, questions brought attention, attention brought the white-cloaked patrols with their iron censers and their quiet, terrible ledgers. Fifteen years of survival reduced to a simple arithmetic: *open nothing, want nothing, be no one.*

Wren opened the door.

Later they would tell themselves it was because the child looked half-starved. Later they would invent better reasons. But in the moment there was only the child's face tilting up toward theirs, and a silence so complete it seemed to lean against Wren's chest like a hand.

"You shouldn't be here," Wren said quietly. "Whatever you were told, whoever sent you — this is a tannery loft. Nothing else."

The child opened their mouth. What came out was not common tongue, not the gutter-slang of the docks, not the high speech of the Wardens' proclamations. It was something older — vowels that rolled like water over stones, consonants that clicked and hummed in the throat, words that seemed less spoken than *remembered*, as if the language itself had been asleep and the child was waking it syllable by syllable.

Wren understood none of it. And yet the back of their neck prickled, because some buried part of them — some part older than Wren Eastwick, older than the name — stirred at the sound like a sleeper hearing a voice through a wall.

"I don't understand you," Wren said. "Can you write? Can you—"

The child simply stepped forward, past the threshold, past Wren's outstretched arm, into the garret, with the unhesitating air of someone entering a room they had seen a thousand times in dreams. And then they stopped, and turned, and held out both hands.

In their palms lay a disc of gray stone the size of a saucer, cracked clean through from edge to center. The fracture glowed faintly, the way a dying coal glows — not with light exactly, but with *attention*. Wren had spent a lifetime around objects that held echoes. Charms whispered. Sealed letters ached. But this thing did neither. It *listened*.

"Touch it," the child said — and though the words were still in that impossible language, Wren heard them land inside their own skull, translated by something that had no business existing.

"No," Wren said. "Absolutely not. I don't touch strange magic. I especially don't touch strange magic that talks."

The child's expression didn't change. They simply held the seal out further, patient as the tide, and the crack in the stone brightened by a degree, and Wren felt the pull of it in their teeth, in the hollow behind their sternum where they kept the one charm they would never sell.

*Don't,* said fifteen years of instinct. *Close the door. Put the knife away or use it. Be no one.*

Wren touched the seal.

* * *

The hum began in the stone and ended in Wren's blood.

It wasn't pain. It was worse than pain — it was *opening*. Their gift worked one way and one way only: they took memories, drew them out of a willing seller like thread from a spool, wound them into vessels. They had never once felt a memory reach back for *them*. But the seal reached, and Wren's carefully built defenses — the mental architecture of a lifetime of hiding — parted like fog before a boat.

And then Wren was falling through someone else's dreams.

*A tower, whole and blazing, its crown a ring of white fire against a night sky crowded with unfamiliar stars. Voices rising around its base in that same old rolling language, thousands of voices, singing the tower upright out of the earth itself. A covenant — Wren felt its shape rather than saw it, a bond woven between the tower's heart and the world's, two hands clasped so tightly they had become one flesh.*

*Then the dream aged. Centuries in the space of a breath. The voices thinned. The fire at the crown dimmed to embers, embers to ash. The tower's living stone went gray, then black, and Wren watched — helpless, weightless — as cracks spread through the covenant like frost across a window, and on the far side of those cracks another world flickered and guttered like a candle in wind.*

*And always, always, the same image returning like a refrain: the tower's crown, dark now, hollow, waiting. Not dead, the dream insisted. Dying. There is a difference. Dead things cannot be saved. Dying things can only be answered.*

Wren tore their hand back and staggered into the shelf of jars. Glass rattled. A jar of summer rain — someone's memory of it, anyway — toppled and rolled to the floorboards, unbroken by luck or habit.

The child watched them with those ice-pale eyes, saying nothing, offering nothing. As if what Wren had just seen were a letter they'd been asked to deliver, and the reading of it was merely postage.

"What *was* that?" Wren's voice came out rough. "Those weren't your memories. They were older than you. Older than—" They stopped, because the next word was *older than me*, and they had no idea why they believed that, and believing it frightened them more than anything the Wardens had ever done.

The child pointed at the seal. Pointed at the window, at the black shape of the Spire against the brightening sky. Then they laid one small hand flat over their own chest, and said a single word in the old language — and again it arrived pre-translated, dropped whole into Wren's mind like a stone into a well:

*Noor.*

"That's your name." Wren crouched slowly, putting themselves at the child's eye level, keeping their voice level too, the way they did with sellers who trembled. "Noor. And you're dreaming about the Spire. Every night?"

Noor nodded once.

"And the crack in that stone — it's the same crack you see in the dreams."

Another nod. Then Noor's composure slipped, just barely — a tremor in the small hands, a quickness of breath — and Wren recognized it with a lurch, because they had worn that exact expression in a mirror once, fifteen years ago, on the night they'd first realized what they were and what possessing it would cost. It was the look of someone carrying something far too heavy who has finally found a door and is terrified it will close.

"You can't stay here," Wren said, and meant it, and heard how hollow it sounded even as they said it. "There are people — Wardens — they burn what I am. They'd burn you twice over. Whatever you think I can do—"

Noor reached out and caught Wren's wrist. Small fingers, cold from the fog, gripping with startling strength. And whether it was the seal's residue still humming in Wren's veins or something else entirely, the contact opened a second, smaller door — a sliver of the child's own memory, offered rather than taken:

*A long road through wet grass, walked alone. A city gate closing behind them. Nights of sleeping in doorways while the Spire grew larger on the horizon, pulling them like a hook pulls a fish. And threaded through it all, a single repeated instruction, felt rather than heard, ancient and exhausted and desperately hopeful:*

*Find the thief of memories. Find the one who keeps what others discard. The covenant needs a keeper who knows the weight of borrowed things.*

Wren pulled free and stood, heart slamming. "I'm not a keeper. I'm a criminal. Those aren't the same thing, whatever your tower thinks."

But even as they spoke, their eyes went — traitorously — to the shelf where a single plain wooden box sat apart from all the others, unlabeled, locked, holding the one charm Wren had never sold and never dared open: a fragment of their own childhood, stolen from themselves so long ago they no longer remembered what it contained. The box that made them lie awake some nights wondering if a person could be built entirely out of other people's leftovers.

Noor followed their gaze. Looked at the box. Looked back at Wren.

And smiled — small, certain, terrible in its certainty — as if the child had just read *Wren's* label.

Outside, below the fog, a bell began to ring. Not the tannery bell, not the harbor clock. The sharp triple-strike of a Warden patrol post, followed by the low horn-call that every soul in Grayhollow knew and dreaded: *resonance detected. Search authorized.* The sound rolled up through the streets, and Wren felt it answer in the cracked seal on the floor, which flared briefly, brightly, like a struck match.

Noor picked it up and hid it inside the oversized coat without being told.

"They're not looking for you yet," Wren breathed, already moving, snuffing lamps, sweeping charms into hidden compartments with fifteen years of practiced speed. "They're looking for the hum. And you are *made* of hum."

The horn sounded again, closer. Somewhere down the tannery row, boots were finding their rhythm.

Wren looked at the child standing calm in the middle of their crumbling life, and understood, with the clarity of a man watching the tide come in, that the door they had opened could not be closed — that they had already chosen, the moment the latch lifted, and everything after was only the shape of the choosing.

"Stay behind me," they said. "And whatever happens — don't let them hear you speak."
