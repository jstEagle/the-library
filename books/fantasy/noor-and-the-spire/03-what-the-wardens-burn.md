## 3. What the Wardens Burn

The knock came at dusk, and it was wrong.

Not the soft triple tap of a buyer — buyers never knocked, they drifted in like moths and stood too close to the lamp. Not the flat fist of the landlady demanding rent she knew Wren couldn't pay in coin. This was iron on wood, measured and patient, the knock of someone who had all night and the law behind them.

Wren blew out the lamp.

In the dark of the garret, Noor sat perfectly still on the pallet, the cracked seal cradled against their chest. The stone had hummed all day — a thin, insectile whine that Wren could feel in their back teeth — and it did not stop now. If anything, in the darkness, it grew louder.

*Quiet,* Wren thought at it uselessly. *Quiet, you wretched thing.*

They crossed to the window in three silent steps and looked down into Candlemaker's Row. The night market was kindling itself the way it always did: paper lanterns blooming one by one, braziers coughing alight, the smell of frying dough and tallow smoke rising through the cold. Ordinary. Beautiful, even, if you didn't know what was folded inside the ordinary — the charms moving hand to hand under shawls, the little tin reliquaries full of other people's Tuesdays.

And standing at the mouth of the row, where the street widened into Gullmarket Square, stood six figures in slate-grey cloaks, and their lanterns burned white.

Warden-lights. No flame in them at all, only that pale, sourceless glow that made everyone they touched look drowned.

Wren's mouth went dry. Fifteen years of careful, quiet work, and the Wardens had come to their street.

Behind them, Noor made a sound — not a word, not quite, but the old language stirring in the child's throat like water finding its way downhill. The seal flared warm against the child's ribs. Wren felt it from across the room, the way you feel a struck bell in a wooden floor.

"No." They were beside the pallet before they'd decided to move, hands closing over the small cold fingers. "No singing. Not tonight. Not ever tonight."

Noor looked up. In the dark the child's eyes caught light that wasn't there, and Wren remembered what they'd seen when they'd accidentally read the child two nights ago: a tower black against a burning sky, roots pulling out of the world like teeth, dreams leaking across the border between realms. The Spire, dying. The covenant, cracking. And this small mute vessel carrying half of it like a coal carried in cupped hands.

The knocking came again. Not at Wren's door — below. At the market itself.

Boots on cobbles. A voice pitched to carry: *"By writ of the Vigilance, this market is closed. All persons will remain where they stand. Charms will be surrendered peaceably. Any who run will be assumed to carry resonance, and treated accordingly."*

*Treated accordingly.* Everyone in Grayhollow knew what that meant. There was a yard behind the Vigilance Hall with a drain in the middle of it, and the drain ran down into the canals, and nobody spoke about what colored the water some mornings.

Wren pressed their forehead briefly against the cold window glass. Below, the market was dissolving — not running, running got you killed, but folding itself away with the practiced speed of people who had done this before. Lanterns pinched out. Awnings dropped. The woman who sold fried dough became just a tired woman closing early; the boy with the bootlaces became a boy loitering. And beneath the performance, charms vanished into mouths, into sleeves, into cracks in the wall where they would wait out the century if they had to.

But the Wardens weren't sweeping for trinkets tonight. Their white lanterns swung in slow arcs, and where the light passed over a person, Wren saw it snag — saw men and women freeze mid-motion as the light *read* them, hunting for the hum of stored memory. Hunting for resonance.

And somewhere among all those honest liars and petty thieves was a chain of buyers who had purchased pieces of Wren's work. Forty? Fifty? Every one of them marked now by association, every charm a thread leading back —

Back to the garret above the chandler's shop. Back to the door with the bad latch. Back to the mute child holding a humming stone older than any kingdom.

"They traced it," Wren whispered. The words tasted like ash. "Whatever the seal does, whatever *you* do — they felt it. It's my street. My customers. Everyone I've ever sold to is going to burn for—"

Noor reached up and touched Wren's wrist. Just that. Small fingers on the pulse point, deliberate, and through the touch came something that wasn't language and wasn't quite memory either — an image, clean and simple as an icon painted on glass: a single lamp lit in an enormous darkness, and hands cupped around it, sheltering it from wind.

*I know what I cost,* the image said. *I have always known.*

"Don't," Wren said roughly, pulling back. "Don't be noble, you're seven years old, nobility is wasted on—" 

The door downstairs broke open. Not theirs — the chandler's. Then the widow Aldsey's, two doors down, her scream cut short by a Warden's clipped command. The sweep was working up the row, house by house, methodical as a comb through hair.

Wren turned to the room. One bed. One trunk. Under the floorboard beside it, wrapped in oilcloth: the working kit — needle-thin silver hooks, the salve of crushed moonwort, and forty-one unsold charms in their little lead coffins, each one a stolen hour from someone's life. First snowfall in the hill country. A mother's voice reading aloud. The taste of a peach in a summer fifteen years dead.

And underneath those, in a separate wrapping, the one charm Wren would never sell. The fragment of childhood. Their own, they had always believed. Their only possession that was truly—

There was no time to think about that. There was barely time to breathe.

Wren pried up the board, took the kit, left everything else. Then they knelt and pulled the loose brick from the chimney breast — the cavity no bigger than a bread loaf, lined with felt, built for exactly one purpose.

"Noor." Wren held out both hands, palms up, and tried to make their voice steady. "I need you to trust me, and then I need you to forgive me, and I'm sorry the order goes that way round."

The cavity wasn't big enough for a child. But it didn't need to be — not for what Wren had in mind. They took three charms from the kit without looking at which ones, and pressed them into Noor's hands along with the seal. Then, gently, they laid their fingertips against the child's temple.

Reading a person was theft. But there was another trick, rarer, uglier — one Wren had only ever used twice, and hated both times. You could fold a person into a charm's shape. Not their whole self. Just enough. An hour's worth of stillness and silence, packed tight around the seed of them, while their body went quiet as a doll's.

"It will feel like falling asleep in winter," Wren said. "It will feel like nothing. When I wake you, none of it will have happened yet. Can you let me?"

Noor studied them for a long moment with those impossible eyes. Then the child nodded once, solemn as a magistrate, and closed their own fingers over Wren's hand — and *helped*. That was the worst part. Noor poured themselves willingly into the shape Wren made, folded down and down into something small and dense and silent, until what lay in Wren's palm was a charm like any other, warm as a living thing, humming faintly with the seal's buried note.

Wren wrapped it in oilcloth and hid it in the chimney cavity behind the loose brick. Then they scattered the remaining charms across the floor — deliberately, carelessly, a spill of contraband any panicked smuggler might leave behind. If the Wardens found product here, they'd stop looking for anything else.

Then Wren opened the window onto the roof slope, and climbed.

* * *

Captain Ilsa Vane stood in the middle of Candlemaker's Row and let the market hate her.

She was used to it by now. Fifteen years wore most of the edges off a person, and what edges remained, the job sanded daily. Around her her Wardens moved with the white lanterns, and the good folk of Grayhollow watched her with faces like shutters pulled closed, and somewhere in that row of houses was the source of the strongest resonance reading the Vigilance instruments had recorded in a decade.

A reading that had spiked again tonight. Twice. From a garret above the chandler's shop.

"House to house," she said quietly to Sergeant Brell. "Start at the square end. Anything that hums, bag it. Anyone who resists—" She didn't finish. He knew.

"Captain." Brell hesitated. "The instrument. It's climbing again."

Vane looked down at the brass disc in her gauntlet. The needle trembled against its cage, straining toward the upper registers, toward numbers she'd only seen once before — the night, eleven years ago, when they'd put down the Bellfounder cell and burned everything they found, including a girl of nineteen who had begged them, weeping, not to erase her sister, who hadn't done anything, whose only crime had been sleeping in the same room as a forbidden thing.

Vane had held the lantern that night. Vane had watched the erasure take the sister and not the girl, because the instruments said so, because the law said so, because saying otherwise meant joining the erased.

The needle climbed. Somewhere above her, in that garret or on those rooftops, whatever carried the resonance was moving — or hiding — or waiting.

"Brell," she heard herself say. "When we reach the chandler's garret — I go in first."

He gave her a strange look. "Captain?"

"You heard me."

Because she needed to see it herself. Because eleven years of drained canals and ash-yard mornings had taught her that the law was a wall, and walls kept things out, and sometimes the thing they kept out was the truth. Because the needle was climbing and some stubborn splinter of her, buried deeper than the uniform, wanted to know *what* the city was so afraid of that it had outlawed wonder entire.

She followed her Wardens up the row, white lantern high, hating the night and loving the law and unable, after all these years, to tell the difference anymore.

* * *

Wren lay flat against the cold slates of the chandler's roof and listened to the Vigilance break their life apart below.

Through the floor came muffled sounds: the scrape of the trunk lid, boots crossing boards, a Warden's voice calling up the stairwell — *"Clear below, checking the loft"* — and then footsteps on Wren's own stairs, and Wren pressed their cheek to the wet slate and breathed as shallowly as a corpse breathes.

Below, the Wardens found the spilled charms. Found the empty chimney cavity — and the felt lining, and the faint warmth of it, and nothing else. Wren heard the frustration in the silence, the suspicion, the long pause while someone decided whether an empty hiding place meant the prize had flown or simply been overlooked.

*"Instrument says the resonance is still here, Captain. Faint, but here. Could be residue."*

*"Residue fades,"* said a woman's voice, low and hard. *"This doesn't feel like residue. Sweep the roofline."*

Wren rolled sideways off the slates as lantern-light swept the ridge above, dropped the six feet into the chandler's yard among the stacked osier baskets, and ran — low, fast, hood up, one more grey shape in a city full of shadows. Behind and above, the white light swept the rooftops they'd left bare.

The plan, such as it was, had one line in it: get the seal out of Candlemaker's Row before it hummed again, get clear of the sweep, and then — then what? Run to the coast? Hide in the marshes? The covenant didn't care where Wren ran. The crack in the world didn't narrow because its keeper's guardian was frightened. Sooner or later, Noor would need to sing, and when Noor sang, every Warden instrument in Grayhollow would point at them like a compass needle.

Unless someone knew how to hide a resonance. Unless someone had spent fifteen years moving forbidden things through this city without a single trace reaching the Wardens.

Wren slipped through the cut between the cooperage and the canal, down the slick steps into the drowned underlevels where the tide muttered and the lamps burned green, and told themselves the sick feeling in their stomach was fear of the Wardens.

It wasn't. It was the other thing. The thing they'd been swallowing since dawn.

Corvane. The fence. The only soul alive who had known what Wren was since the beginning — who had found them half-starved at seventeen with stolen memories in their pockets and a price on their head, and instead of selling them had said, *you'll need someone to move your goods, little thief, and I'll need someone worth moving them for.* Corvane, who had shielded them through fifteen years of raids. Corvane, who knew every safe route, every blind instrument, every way to make a resonance vanish.

Corvane, who had known about the garret on Candlemaker's Row.

And the Wardens had come straight to it.

Wren stood in the green dark with water dripping somewhere ahead, and made themself say it plainly, because lying to yourself was the one theft you could never fence:

Either Corvane had been careless.

Or Corvane had sold them — and everything Wren had given him, every secret, every year of trust, was already in the Vigilance's ledgers, priced and filed.

Ahead, the tunnel branched: right, toward the marsh gates and the open country and a life spent forever running. Left, up through the underlevels, toward Gullmarket, toward the shop with the brass scales and the smell of pipe-smoke, toward the only family Wren had ever had.

Wren chose left.

If Corvane had betrayed them, Wren needed to know. And if he hadn't — if there was any chance at all — then he was the only person in Grayhollow who could hide a humming stone and a miracle-child from Captain Vane's white lanterns.

The green lamps guttered as Wren passed, and far above, through a grating open to the sky, the first stars were coming out over the dead black silhouette of the Spire.
