## 4. The Fence Who Knew Too Much

Corvane's shop sat where the Lantern District forgot itself — a crooked wedge of a building wedged between a shuttered tannery and a chapel that had been a brothel before it was a chapel and would probably be both again someday. The sign above the door read *Curiosities, Bought & Valued*, and beneath it, in smaller letters someone had scratched long ago: *Nothing Asked About Origins.*

Wren had read those words a thousand times over fifteen years. Tonight they read them like a stranger.

Noor's small hand was locked in theirs, cold as river stone. The child hadn't spoken since the market — hadn't made a sound at all except that impossible language, murmured once in sleep against Wren's shoulder like a prayer said to no one listening. The cracked seal hung at Noor's throat on a leather cord, and even through two layers of coat Wren could feel it humming, faint and patient, the way a struck bell keeps ringing after the sound has left it.

The Wardens had burned three stalls in the night market. Three families' livelihoods, ash by dawn. Because of a resonance traced to Wren's street. Because of Noor.

*Run rather than let others pay.* The decision had hardened in Wren somewhere between the smoke and the rooftops, and now there was only one door left to knock on.

"Stay behind me," Wren whispered. "Whatever you see in there. Whatever he says."

Noor looked up. Dark eyes, older than the face around them. The child nodded once.

Wren knocked the pattern — two, pause, three, pause, one — and heard the old familiar shuffle inside, the drag of a bad knee on good floorboards, and felt something in their chest loosen for the first time since the patrol's lanterns had swept the market square. Corvane's knee. Corvane's floorboards. Fifteen years of them. The man had found Wren half-starved at seventeen with a pocketful of charms they couldn't sell and no name anyone would buy, and he had taught them everything: how to price a first kiss, how to spot a buyer who'd report you after, how to keep your own memories out of your own inventory. He'd never asked what Wren was. He'd only ever said, *What you are is mine to not know.*

The door opened on a chain. One eye, wet and brown, in a seam of lamplight.

Then the chain dropped so fast it clattered, and Corvane filled the doorway whole — big as a barrel, bald as an egg, smelling of pipe smoke and vinegar as always — and pulled Wren inside by the collar like a father hauling a son out of a flood.

"Girl, girl, girl." He shut the door, threw the bolt, shot it home with the little brass pin he thought nobody knew about. "You've brought a fire into my house and you don't even smell the smoke yet."

"Corvane—"

"I know." He was already moving, snuffing lamps, dragging the heavy curtain across the front window. His hands were shaking. In fifteen years Wren had never once seen his hands shake. "I know about the market. I know about Vane. I know she pulled the resonance ledgers from the harbor office this morning, and I know your street is written in them twice."

"Then you know why I'm here." Wren kept Noor pressed close, back to the wall. "I need passage out. Barges, tunnels, anything. And coin."

"Coin." Corvane laughed, but there was nothing in it. He turned, and in the one lamp he'd left burning his face looked suddenly old, suddenly carved. "Sit down, Wren."

Nobody used that name aloud. Not here. It was the rule above all rules — names were bait, names were hooks, and Corvane had taught Wren that better than anyone.

"Sit down," he said again, gently, terribly, "and let me tell you what I know about Wren Eastwick."

* * *

The shop smelled different tonight. Wren noticed it only now, too late: under the pipe smoke and vinegar, something sharp and mineral. Ink. Fresh ink, and the waxy ghost of a sealing stamp.

On the counter, half-hidden under a ledger, lay a folded paper. Warden paper — that particular gray stock, that particular fold. Corvane saw Wren see it, and did not move to hide it.

That was the worst part. That he didn't move.

"Fifteen years ago," Corvane said, settling onto his stool with the groan of the bad knee, "a boy came up from the Saltmarsh road with nothing but a knife, a hunger, and a head full of other people's lives. He didn't know what he was. Thought he was cursed. Thought if he sold enough of it, the curse would empty out and leave him clean." He tapped ash from his pipe with a hand that had steadied now, which was somehow worse than the shaking. "He was wrong on both counts. Stealing memory doesn't empty a man. It fills him. With everyone but himself."

"Stop." Wren's voice came out flat. "How do you know that? Nobody knows that. I never told—"

"You told me everything, girl. That was the arrangement." Corvane's eyes were kind, and kindness from him now was unbearable. "Every charm you brought me, I logged. Every buyer, every price, every year. And every quarter, I sent a copy down to the Citadel. Names redacted, mostly. Yours always redacted. My one act of decency, I told myself, year after year. Look how well it held."

The room tilted. Wren gripped the counter's edge and the wood bit cold into their palms and still the tilt wouldn't stop.

"You reported me." Each word cost something. "Fifteen years. You fed me, you hid me, you buried my mother's—" They stopped. Swallowed. "You sat at my table."

"And ate every meal knowing I might be signing your death warrant with the next one." Corvane's voice cracked on it, finally, just once. "You think I slept? You think the knee is the only thing that aches at night?" He leaned forward. "But listen to me, because this is the part that matters. This morning, Vane's clerks stopped accepting my reports. This morning, a courier came instead. A summons. They have my ledger, Wren — the real one, the unredacted one, the one I kept because keeping it was the only leash I had on my own guilt. And in that ledger, fifteen years deep, is a page describing exactly what you are. What the gift does. How it works. Everything Vane needs to understand what walked into her city wearing a child's skin and humming like a struck bell."

Outside, far off, a bell began to ring. Not the hour bell. The watch bell — three quick tolls, a pause, three more. The pattern every smuggler in Grayhollow knew. *Cordon closing.*

"They gave me until moonset to deliver you," Corvane said quietly. "Or they come for us both."

Wren couldn't speak. There was a whole childhood of grief backed up behind their teeth — not their childhood, never their childhood, that was the joke, that was the whole rotten joke of their life — and none of it would come out. Only this: "Why are you telling me? Why not just open the door?"

Corvane looked at them for a long moment. Then he reached under the counter and set something down between them: a small iron key, green with age, stamped with a symbol Wren had seen only once before — scratched into the wall of a drowned tunnel, years ago, when Corvane had drunk too much and talked too much about the old days. The mark of the underlevels. The mark of the people the city had flooded and forgotten.

"Because whatever else I am," he said, "I am not going to hand a child to Vane. Take the key. Take the girl. Go down through the tannery cistern, follow the water until the walls start singing — you'll know it when you hear it. And Wren." His voice caught. "When you find whoever keeps the archive down there — ask them about the theft. Ask them whose memory you've been carrying all these years. Ask them who you were before you were mine."

"The archive?"

"There are places even I don't control, girl. Places where the confiscated things go to wait." He pushed the key across the counter. "Go. Now. Before I do the arithmetic of my own survival and come up with a worse answer."

Wren took the key. Their hand should have struck him. Their hand should have thrown the lamp, shattered the glass, burned the ledger, burned it all. Instead their hand closed around cold iron, and their voice, when it came, was small and strange:

"I loved you, old man."

"I know." Corvane turned away, toward the curtain, toward the window, toward the gray light coming up over the rooftops where the Wardens would already be gathering. "That was the cruelest thing either of us ever did."

* * *

The tannery cistern swallowed them in darkness and the smell of a hundred years of hides and lime. Wren went first, boots finding the slick stair-cut stones by feel, one hand holding Noor's wrist in a grip that must have hurt and the child never flinched. Behind them, muffled by earth and brick, the watch bell kept its three-and-three, closer now.

At the bottom, water. Black, waist-deep in the channel, moving with a slow tidal breath. Somewhere ahead it would drop into the drowned levels — the streets the river took two centuries ago, the vaults and chapels and markets of the city beneath the city, where the ban had never reached because the Wardens had never bothered to look.

Noor tugged Wren's sleeve. Pointed back the way they'd come, then pressed the cracked seal flat against their own chest, over the heart. Then pointed forward, down, into the dark water.

"Yes," Wren said. "I know. We go down."

They waded in. The cold climbed Wren's body like a verdict. Above and behind, faint through stone, came a new sound: boots on the cistern stairs. Many boots. Lantern light bleeding down the shaft in a trembling yellow line.

And threaded through it all — so faint Wren might have imagined it, except that Noor heard it too and went rigid, gripping Wren's arm — a voice echoing down the flooded corridor ahead of them. Not a Warden's voice. Older. Dry as pages turning.

It was speaking the same language Noor spoke in dreams.

The water ahead began, very softly, to glow.
