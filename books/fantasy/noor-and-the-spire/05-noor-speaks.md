## 5. Noor Speaks

The water in the drowned streets sang.

Noor heard it first as a hum beneath the soles of their feet, the same note as the cracked seal that hung against their chest on its leather cord. Then, as Wren led them down a stairway that had once been a street and was now a riverbed of slick black stone, the hum became something almost like music — old music, the kind the Spire used to make before it began to die. Noor knew the melody the way they knew their own heartbeat: without knowing how.

They could not say any of this aloud. That was the cruelty of it. The words were all there, whole and shining, an entire language folded inside them like letters pressed between the pages of a book — but every time they tried to speak in the tongue of Grayhollow, the words came apart like wet paper. Their mouth made shapes. Nothing came out but breath.

So they followed Wren instead, and watched, and listened.

Wren moved through the underlevels like someone who had done it many times and trusted none of them. The thief — no, not a thief; Noor had seen enough inside Wren's sleeping mind to know better, though *thief* was the word Wren used when they spoke about themselves, always with a flinch hidden behind it — carried a small lamp hooded with tin, tilted so its light fell only on the next three steps. Beyond those three steps the dark went on forever, and the water dripped from ceilings that had been sky once, and things rustled in the flooded doorways of houses where people had lived before the city grew over them like moss over a fallen tree.

Noor liked it here. That surprised them. It was the first place since the Spire had sent them walking that felt honest. Above, in Grayhollow's crooked streets, everything pretended — the lamplighters pretended the lamps burned for beauty rather than fear, the merchants pretended their wares were only cloth and fish, and Wren pretended hardest of all, wearing a face like a coat buttoned to the chin. But down here nothing pretended. The water had taken what it wanted and left the bones of the old city open to anyone who cared to look. Noor cared to look. Looking was most of what they had.

They walked for hours. Or maybe less; time moved strangely underground, stretched thin as tallow. Wren stopped twice to listen for pursuit, pressing them both into alcoves while distant boots echoed down through grates. Once, far above, there was a sound like a struck bell and a smell of burning pitch, and Wren's jaw tightened in a way Noor had learned meant *the Wardens*, and meant *guilt*, though Wren would never have said the word guilt either. Noor had begun collecting Wren's unspoken words the way other people collected coins.

At last Wren stopped at a doorway set into the side of the channel — a real door, wooden, impossibly dry, carved with a spiral that Noor recognized down to the grain. The Spire's mark. Someone had carried a piece of the Spire down here, long ago, and built a room around it.

Wren knocked a pattern on the wood. Waited. Knocked again.

Nothing answered except the water.

"Empty," Wren muttered, and the sound of their voice in that dead place was loud as a landslide. "Should be a boat behind this one. Corvane keeps—" They stopped themselves, lips pressing white. The name Corvane sat between them like a knife left on a table. Noor had felt it in Wren's mind that morning, sharp and grieving: a man who had been father and fortress and family, who had sold them anyway. Noor did not fully understand betrayal yet. But they understood that Wren carried it like a stone in the belly, heavier than the seal.

Wren shouldered the door instead of knocking a third time, and it gave with a groan, and beyond it was not a boat but a long gallery of standing water, knee-deep and mirror-still, lit from above by cracks in the vaulting where the drowned city let in threads of gray dawn.

And in the water, reflected, the Spire was waiting for them.

Noor saw it before Wren did. The great tower stood in the reflection upside-down, rooted in the ceiling of the world, and even rendered in gray water-light it was unmistakable: the Hollow Crown, the anchor, the thing that had chosen Noor out of every child in every village and poured a language into their mouth and a seal into their hands and then gone quiet, going quieter, dying by inches while the border between the realms thinned like ice in spring.

"Noor," Wren said carefully, the way one speaks to a frightened animal. "What is it? What are you looking at?"

Noor pointed. Wren looked. Wren's face did the thing faces did when they refused to believe themselves.

"It's just water," Wren said. "It's just a reflection of the—"

Of the vaulting. Of the cracks. Of anything except a tower that no longer stood anywhere in the world above, having burned to a crown of ash years before Noor was born. But the water remembered. Water was older than burning. And the Spire, dying or not, still had friends in low places.

Noor stepped into the gallery. The cold climbed their legs and they did not care. They knelt, and put both palms flat on the surface, and the hum in the seal rose up through their arms into their throat like a tide coming in.

*Speak,* said the hum. *You were made to speak.*

And for the first time since the night the Wardens' fire had lit the horizon behind their village, Noor opened their mouth and found that the words fit.

Not the old language — or not only. Something stranger happened, something that made Wren take a step back and grip the doorframe: the words came out in broken, halting common tongue, each one shaped clumsily, a child learning to walk on legs that had only ever flown. But they came out. They came out in *Wren's* language, and Noor understood dimly that this too was the seal working, translating, spending itself a little more with every syllable the way a candle spends its wax.

"The tower is not a tower," Noor said. Their voice sounded rusted. Wren stared as if the water itself had spoken. "It is a — a promise. A promise that got a body."

"A promise." Wren's voice was flat with disbelief, but they crouched slowly at the water's edge, lamp lowered, listening. Listening was something Wren did well, Noor had noticed. Perhaps because no one had listened to Wren in a very long time.

"The old peoples made it. Before kingdoms. Before the ban." Each phrase cost effort; Noor paused between them, breathing. "Two worlds, touching. Like — like two hands, pressed together. The Spire was where they touched. It held the door so neither world could swallow the other. There were keepers. Singers. One for each side of the door."

"What happened to them?"

"Died. Forgot. Were — erased." The last word came out hard, and Noor watched it land on Wren like a blow, because *erased* was a word with teeth in Grayhollow, a word that meant Wardens and pyres and neighbors suddenly gone from every memory but the physical ones, the empty chairs, the unpaid debts. "The ban killed the singers. So the Spire took the promise and hid it. In children. In — " Noor touched their own throat. "In me. I am the last speaker. I am half of the promise. The seal is my half."

"And the other half?"

Noor lifted the cracked stone. In the gray light the fracture through its heart glimmered faintly, and the water around them shivered.

"It broke when the last keeper on your side died. Fifteen years ago." Noor watched Wren's face do something complicated and terrible. Fifteen years. Everyone in Grayhollow counted things from fifteen years ago, Noor had learned; it was the year the ban bit deepest, the year of some wound the whole city shared. "Since then the Spire has been dying. When it dies, the door does not close. The door *rots*. Both worlds bleed together until neither can hold its shape."

Silence, except the drip of the vaulting and the faint song of the water.

"So why me?" Wren asked quietly. "Why come to my door? Out of everyone in this city."

Noor considered how to answer, and found the answer had been forming for days, in dreams and in the way Wren's charms clicked softly against each other in their pocket, each one holding somebody's morning, somebody's grief, somebody's name.

"You keep memories that are not yours," Noor said. "You give them back to people. You carry other lives inside you and you do not lose yourself." A pause. "That is what a keeper is. The Spire has been watching you for fifteen years, Wren Eastwick. It sent me to the one person already practicing."

Wren laughed — a short, wounded sound with no humor in it. "I'm a criminal. Magic is banned. I'm exactly the kind of person your tower's enemies burn."

"Yes," Noor agreed simply. "The Spire does not choose the safe. It chooses the willing."

Then Wren asked the question Noor had been dreading, leaning closer, lamplight catching the fracture in the stone: "What does it need? To be mended. What does the covenant need?"

Noor closed their hand around the seal and felt its warmth, and its hunger, and told the truth because the water was listening and the water did not permit lies.

"It needs a second bearer. A singer for your side of the door. And it cannot take one." Their voice dropped; the words came harder now, dragged up from somewhere deep. "It must be *given*. Someone must give the Spire something irreplaceable. Not blood. Not life. Worse than life." Noor met Wren's eyes. "A memory. The memory that holds you together. The one you would refuse to lose if losing it meant dying. Freely given, or the door stays broken."

The water stilled. Even the dripping seemed to hold its breath.

"I don't have one of those," Wren said, too quickly, too lightly. "I steal memories. I don't keep them."

But Noor had been inside Wren's sleeping mind, and had seen the charm Wren touched at night through the fabric of their pocket like a talisman, the one never sold, the fragment of childhood Wren believed was their own. Noor said nothing. Some truths were not theirs to give. Yet.

Instead Noor reached across the still water and took Wren's hand — the first time they had dared — and turned the palm upward, and set the cracked seal upon it.

The hum changed. Deepened. Split into two notes, one Noor's and one new, tentative, searching, like a second voice joining a song after centuries alone.

Wren went very pale. "It's — choosing me. Noor, I didn't agree to anything—"

"You don't have to agree tonight," Noor said, and was astonished to hear their own voice steadying, growing easier, the common tongue loosening in their mouth now that the worst of it was spoken. "The seal only asks. It never takes. That is the whole of the old law, and the reason the ban fears it."

Wren stared down at the stone resting in their palm, humming its two-note question, and Noor watched fifteen years of careful pretending crack down the middle the way the seal was cracked.

Then, from far above, muffled through fathoms of drowned city, came a sound that made both of them freeze: horns. Three of them, in the pattern Noor had heard outside Wren's window two nights past. The Wardens' hunting call. But wrong — not sweeping the streets above. Descending. Someone had told them where the underlevel entrances were, and someone had known Wren would run below.

Wren closed their fist around the seal, snuffed the lamp, and pulled Noor toward the far end of the gallery, where the water flowed into darkness.

"There's an archive," Wren breathed, "an old smuggler's vault past the sunken chapel. If we're fast—" They stopped. Swallowed. "If we're fast, we might learn what I gave away fifteen years ago. And what I'll have to give now."

Noor ran beside them into the dark, the seal singing against both their hearts, one note and its answering note, and above them the horns came closer.
