## 6. The Archive of Stolen Names

Noor led us the way water finds a crack.

We had been in the underlevels for two days by then — two days of wading through drowned streets where the old city slept beneath the new one, its windows bricked over, its doorways breathing cold air that smelled of river silt and extinguished candles. I knew these tunnels the way a thief knows lock mechanisms: intimately, and without affection. I had run through them once before, fifteen years ago, with someone else's memories burning a hole in my chest and no name I could say aloud.

But Noor walked them like a child walking home.

The child never hesitated at branching passages. Never paused to listen for the drip-count that told you which tunnel flooded at high tide. When we came to a wall that looked like every other wall — wet brick, green-slicked, unremarkable — Noor pressed both small palms flat against it and stood there, head bowed, lips moving around words in that language older than kingdoms. The seal at their throat hummed. And somewhere behind the brick, something answered: a low, grinding shudder, like a great door remembering it had once been a door.

The wall split along a seam I would have sworn wasn't there.

"Noor," I said carefully — I had learned a handful of the child's gestures by now, enough to ask *what is this place* — "what's behind there?"

Noor looked up at me with those eyes that held dying towers, and touched two fingers to my sternum, then drew them away slowly, as if pulling a thread out of my chest.

*Something you lost,* the gesture said. Or maybe I only wanted it to mean that.

I lit my last candle stub and followed the child into the dark.

* * *

The Archive did not look like an archive. It looked like the inside of a whale that had swallowed a library and died of it.

Shelves rose out of standing water, warped and bloated, sagging under the weight of things that were not books — or not only books. Glass jars in which small lights swam like trapped fireflies. Bundles of letters tied with hair. A birdcage containing nothing visible, yet rattling softly whenever I looked away from it. Confiscated magic, hoarded by outlaws across generations: everything the Wardens had ever burned a marketplace to find, hidden here beneath Grayhollow's feet by people who believed — as I had once believed — that memory was too heavy a thing to let any government hold all of it.

And everywhere, catalogued in careful ink on tags of yellowed vellum, were names.

Not the names of objects. The names of owners.

"Stolen years," whispered a voice from the dark, and I nearly put my knife through a throat before I saw her: an old woman seated on a dry crate amid the floodwater, so still she might have been part of the archive herself. Her eyes were white as boiled milk. Blind, or close to it. She was winding thread around a spool with hands that didn't need light. "That's what we keep down here, sparrow. Stolen years. You've come about yours."

"I haven't," I said, because saying otherwise felt like stepping off a roof.

Noor tugged my sleeve and pointed, insistent, toward a shelf near the far wall — low, half-drowned, marked with a tag reading **EASTWICK, W.** in handwriting I recognized because it was mine. Fifteen years ago I had catalogued my own theft here, following instructions given to me by a woman with milk-white eyes, because even then — especially then — I had needed some record to exist outside my own skull. Proof that what happened to me had happened.

"You filed it yourself," the old woman said. She hadn't turned her head. "Fifteen years back. You were bleeding. You asked me to keep it where no Warden would ever burn it." Her thread-pulling hands went still. "You said: *if I ever come back asking who I am, tell me the truth.*"

The candle flame leaned in air that had no draft.

I waded to the shelf. The water was waist-high there and cold enough to make my breath go shallow. The bundle came free of its wrappings with a sound like old leaves — oilcloth gone stiff, tied with cord — and inside were papers, and inside the papers was a charm.

My charm. The one I never sold. The fragment of childhood I had carried against my heart for fifteen years, warm as a swallowed coal, certain — *certain* — that it was the last true piece of myself, the one thing in my possession that had not been taken from someone else.

Except here was its twin. Its record. Its receipt.

And the receipt bore another name.

* * *

I read it three times. The ink didn't change. The name didn't change.

**RECOVERED CHARM — FRAGMENT, CHILDHOOD (APPROX. AGE 6–9).**
**ORIGINAL BEARER: MERCY CALLOWAY, WEAVER'S ROW.**
**TAKEN UNDER DURESS. TAKEN UNWILLINGLY.**
**SUBSEQUENT POSSESSION: UNKNOWN.**

Mercy Calloway. Weaver's Row.

I sat down in the floodwater because my legs decided the matter without consulting me. The cold climbed through my clothes and I barely felt it. Somewhere above us, muffled by forty feet of brick and earth, Grayhollow went about its evening — cart wheels, gull cries, the bells of the Lantern Quarter marking the hour — and none of it reached this room, this drowned library, this moment where the floor of my entire life quietly opened.

Mercy Calloway. I turned the name over the way you turn a coin you suspect is counterfeit. I had no face to attach to it. No street. Nothing. But the charm in my pocket — the one I had carried through raids and betrayals and fifteen years of careful, lonely competence, the one thing I'd have let them cut off my hand before surrendering — that charm sang to me of summer. Of a garden behind a dyer's shop where the sheets hung bleaching white. Of a woman's hands teaching small hands to wind thread. Of a voice saying a name — *my* name, I had always believed, the name I'd lost —

— and the voice in the memory said *Mercy. Come in before dark, Mercy.*

It had always said that. Fifteen years I had heard it and called it an echo, a distortion, the damage done when a memory is torn loose from its root. I had built an entire self on top of that sound and called the sound mine.

"Who was she?" My voice came out wrong. Scraped. "Mercy Calloway."

The blind woman wound her thread. "A weaver's daughter. Died in the Sorrowfever, year after you took her summers. Whole row of Weaver's Row went into the ground that winter." She tilted her head, listening to something beyond hearing. "You were running from the Wardens with a hole in your side, and you grabbed the nearest charm off a dead woman's table because you needed strength to walk. That's what taking does, sparrow. It doesn't ask whose life it borrows."

"And mine?" I heard myself ask. "My real memory. My real childhood. Where is it?"

Silence. Even Noor had gone still beside me, the seal at their throat pulsing faintly, like a second heartbeat in the room.

"There isn't one," the old woman said gently, the way you tell a child that a pet has died. "There never was. You weren't robbed, sparrow. You were emptied. Whatever they took out of you before you ever came down here — it's gone past recovery. What you've been carrying all these years—" she gestured with her chin at the bundle in my lap, at the papers, at the whole sagging archive of other people's lives, "—is patchwork. A coat sewn from stolen cloth. And you wore it so long you forgot you were naked underneath."

The candle guttered. In its failing light I looked at my own hands — Wren Eastwick's hands, thief's hands, clever and scarred — and understood that I did not know whose hands they had been before. Every skill I owned had been bought or taken. My knowledge of locks: purchased from a locksmith who'd sold his apprenticeship to pay a debt. My fluency in the river-thieves' cant: lifted wholesale from a smuggler named Osk, dead ten years. Even my wariness, my loneliness, my competence — the very shape of the person standing knee-deep in cold water holding a dead woman's childhood — even those had been assembled from parts, fitted together by a girl with a hole in her side and nothing left of her own.

Who was Wren if the memories were never theirs?

The question didn't howl. That was the terrible part. It just settled, quiet and total, like floodwater finding its level.

Noor crouched beside me. Water soaked the child's hem and the child didn't care. Small fingers found my wrist — not the gesture-language this time, just touch, plain human touch — and rested there while the seal hummed its slow, patient note.

And I realized, with a clarity that felt almost like mercy, that the child was not comforting me.

The child was *reading* me. The way you read a page you already know by heart.

Because Noor had seen it, back at my garret, in the accidental spill of memory between us. Had seen the Spire dying in my dreams — except they weren't my dreams, were they? They were Mercy Calloway's summers, refracted. Everything in me was refraction. Light passing through borrowed glass.

"The covenant needs a bearer willing to give up something irreplaceable," I said aloud, testing the words, watching them hang in the dark. "That's what you showed me in the tunnels. Something irreplaceable."

Noor nodded.

"But there's nothing irreplaceable in me," I said, and laughed, and the laugh came out cracked down the middle. "Do you understand? There's no *me* underneath. I'm a coat with no body. If I give up everything, I'm not sacrificing anything. I'm just — agreeing to stop pretending."

The blind woman's spool stopped turning.

"That," she said quietly, "is exactly why the Spire will want you."

The candle went out.

In the blackness, the seal at Noor's throat flared — not humming now but ringing, a clear bell-note that ran through the floodwater and up the drowned shelves and set every jar of trapped light in the Archive glowing at once, hundreds of small imprisoned suns waking in answer. And beneath my feet, far below even this buried place, something vast shifted its weight. Turned over. Began, slowly, to climb.

"It knows you're here," the old woman breathed. "Fifteen years I've kept your file, sparrow, and in all that time the tower never stirred. Now look what a question does."

Through the high slit of a window that had not faced the sky since before my grandmother's grandmother, I saw it: a smudge of darkness rising against the stars, impossibly tall, impossibly patient.

The Spire. Climbing out of its long death. Coming to collect.

Noor's hand tightened on my wrist, and the child's eyes in the seal-light were bright and terrified and absolutely certain, and the bell-note resolved at last into a single word in the old language — a word I somehow understood without understanding, the way you understand falling:

*Come.*
