## 7. Vane's Bargain

The rain had stopped an hour before moonrise, and Ilsa Vane stood in the flooded cloister of the old Chantry of Saint Verren with water seeping through her boot soles, listening to a building breathe.

That was the word for it. Breathe. The resonance her instruments had been chasing for three weeks — the hum that had led her from the night market to a garret on Candlemaker's Row to this drowned ruin at the city's edge — it did not behave like magic. Magic, in her experience, crackled. It stank of ozone and burnt hair and fear. This was slower. Deeper. It rose out of the stones beneath her feet like heat off a summer road, steady as a sleeping animal's chest.

*Inhale,* said the cloister. *Exhale,* said the dark.

Her second-in-command, Ferren, shifted behind her. "Captain, the readings are strongest below. The underlevel cisterns. Whatever they're doing down there—"

"They're not doing anything," Vane said quietly. "It's doing something to them."

She had learned that much in fifteen years of enforcing the Ban. Most practitioners she caught were small and desperate — widows stitching luck-charms into hems, dockworkers paying a memory-thief to forget a drowning they'd witnessed. They used magic the way rats used walls: to survive along the edges. But this was different. This was something using *them*.

The reports had come in fragments all day. A fence named Corvane, found bound and gagged in his own counting-house, his ledger burned to ash around him — though not before Vane's clerks had copied three pages of it. Pages that mentioned a name. *Eastwick.* Fifteen years of small transactions, small charms, small sins. And one entry, circled in Corvane's careful hand, dated the night the resonance first appeared: *the child came to her door.*

A child. Mute, according to the market gossip Vane had bought for the price of two bottles and a threat. Speaking a language older than any kingdom, if you believed the drunkards. Carrying a cracked stone seal that sang when touched.

Vane did not believe drunkards. She believed instruments, and the instruments said the seal's signature matched something in the Warden archives she had only ever seen once — twenty years ago, as a young officer, when she'd been sent to catalog the confiscated vaults beneath the Citadel. Something labeled in a dead archivist's hand: *COVENANT FRAGMENTS. DO NOT DESTROY. DO NOT STUDY.*

She had studied them anyway. That was the first law she'd ever broken.

"Captain." Ferren again, lower now. "Movement. West gallery."

* * *

They came up out of the underlevels through a collapsed stairwell behind the Chantry's ruined altar, and Vane watched them from the shadow of a broken column and did not give the order to close.

The woman first — Eastwick, it had to be, though the reports made her sound older, harder. She moved like someone who had spent fifteen years being hunted and had gotten very good at pretending otherwise: weight on the balls of her feet, eyes sweeping every exit twice, one hand resting near a coat pocket that bulged wrong. Rain-damp hair plastered to her cheek. Mud to the knee. And beside her—

Beside her, a child of perhaps nine or ten years, barefoot, holding a cracked stone the size of a heart against their chest with both hands, the way other children held blankets.

The seal was glowing. Faintly, guttering, like a coal remembered rather than alive — but glowing, and with every pulse the whole cloister breathed a little deeper. Vane felt it against her sternum. Felt it *recognize* her, almost, the way a dog lifts its head at a familiar footstep.

"Positions," she murmured. "On my word. No bolts. I want them breathing."

Lantern-flare bloomed across the gallery — six Wardens stepping out of the dark in perfect unison, white-lit, crossbows leveled, the sigil of the Ban blazing on their pauldrons. Eastwick spun, shoved the child behind her, and went still in the way that Vane knew meant *calculating*, running exits, counting bodies, pricing her own life against the odds.

"Ilsa Vane," Eastwick said. Her voice was rough but level. "I wondered how long Corvane's leash would run."

"You knew?"

"I suspected. He flinched whenever I mentioned the Wardens. Guilty men flinch. Loyal men flinch too, sometimes, when they're hiding which side they're on." A thin smile without any humor in it. "You're here for the child."

"I'm here for the resonance," Vane said, and stepped into the lantern-light herself, letting Eastwick see her face, letting her see that she came alone to the front line. "The child is part of it. So, apparently, are you."

"Then take me," Eastwick said. "Whatever the charge is — unlicensed practice, trafficking, all of it, I'll sign whatever confession your clerks want. Let the child go. They walked into my life four days ago. Before that they were no one's problem, least of all yours."

*Four days.* And already the whole city's buried nervous system was singing. Vane kept her face flat.

"The child isn't a problem to be released, Eastwick. The child is half of something that killed a tower." She let that land. Watched Eastwick's jaw tighten. "You've seen it. In the dreams the seal gives. Don't lie to me — I've read what it does to people who touch it. The Spire, falling. Both worlds coming apart at the seams. You've seen it every night since Candlemaker's Row."

Silence. Rain dripped somewhere far above, through the broken roof, into the flooded nave. The child peered out from behind Eastwick's shoulder, and their eyes — gray, ancient, wrong for a child's face — met Vane's across the water, and the seal pulsed once, softly, like a heartbeat skipping.

And Vane thought, with a clarity that felt like vertigo: *it knows me too.*

* * *

There was a file in the Citadel archive with her family name on it. Vane had signed the requisition slip herself, eleven years ago, using her authority as Captain of the Third Patrol, and she had told no one, and she had read it exactly once, standing alone in the cold vault with a hand-lantern, and then she had resealed it and never gone back.

*Mira Vane. Age nineteen. Convicted of unlicensed practice, second offense. Sentence: full mnemonic excision per the Ban's third clause.*

Her sister. Not executed — the Ban had done away with executions by then, having discovered something cleaner. Erasure. They took everything. Every memory, every skill, every scrap of the person who had been Mira Vane, and they left behind a body that could be taught to sweep floors in a Citadel laundry, placid and obedient and empty, humming work-songs it had never learned.

Their mother had visited her twice. The second time, Mira had smiled at her with total politeness, the way one smiles at a stranger offering directions, and their mother had died eighteen months later of something the physicians called grief of the heart, and Vane had stood at the funeral in her Warden uniform because she owned nothing else to wear, and she had sworn — privately, furiously, in the part of herself no instrument could read — that the Ban was right. That it had to be right. Because if it wasn't, then she had spent her whole adult life feeding people into a machine that ate her sister and called it mercy.

That was the thing about laws, Vane had learned. You didn't believe in them because they were true. You believed in them because the alternative was admitting what you'd done.

And now she stood in a drowned chantry looking at a child holding half of a covenant older than the Ban itself, and the seal pulsed toward her like a hand reaching across a table, and she thought: *Mira used to sing. Before. What language was it? I can't remember. I can't remember her voice.*

* * *

"Here is what happens now," Vane said aloud, and her voice carried flat and clear across the water. "You walk out of Grayhollow tonight, both of you, and you keep walking until the seal stops singing. You go north past the salt marshes, beyond the patrol roads. And every Warden in this city forgets your faces, because I will make them forget."

Eastwick stared at her. "That's not how the Ban works. You don't get to just—"

"No," Vane agreed. "I don't. Which is why there's a price."

She watched the woman understand it before she said it. Saw it move through Eastwick's face like weather.

"The child stays. Surrendered to the Citadel, into my custody — mine personally, not the archive clerks', not the Excisors'. I will keep the seal and the child both locked where no one studies them and no one destroys them, and I will spend the rest of my career making sure that holds. You walk free. Clean record, new papers, a purse to start over somewhere the rain doesn't know your name." She paused. "Or you refuse, and I take you both by force, and the child goes to the Excisors with everyone else, and whatever the covenant is, it ends in a vault under the Citadel next to my sister's empty body."

"That's not a bargain," Eastwick said hoarsely. "That's a knife with a ribbon on it."

"Most bargains are." Vane took one step closer. Water rippled around her boots. Close enough now to see the exhaustion under Eastwick's defiance, the four sleepless nights, the way her hand hadn't left the pocket full of stolen lives. "Let me tell you something I've told no one, Eastwick, because you're going to make a choice in the next hour and you deserve to make it knowing what kind of woman is pointing the knife."

She told her. Briefly. Without ceremony. About Mira, about the laundry, about the work-songs. About the file she'd read once and reseal. Eastwick listened without moving, and the child listened too, head tilted, and the seal glowed a little brighter with each sentence as though it were drinking the words.

When Vane finished, the cloister was very quiet.

"So you doubt it," Eastwick said at last. "The Ban. You doubt the thing you enforce."

"I doubt it the way a man doubts a bridge he's standing on," Vane said. "Doubting doesn't stop the fall. It just means you fall knowing." She straightened. "Moonset is in three hours. After that, my orders resume, and I follow them, because that is the only thing I have ever been good at. Three hours, Eastwick. Give me the child, or find a third door I haven't thought of. But if you find one—" her eyes flicked, despite herself, to the dark stairwell rising out of the flooded nave, the one that climbed toward the hills outside the city, toward the broken silhouette on the skyline that every map of Grayhollow marked with a warning instead of a name, "—know that I'll be behind you. One way or the other. I'm always behind you. It's the job."

She turned and walked back into the dark, and gave the order to withdraw, and heard behind her the seal begin — very faintly, very sweetly, in a language older than any kingdom — to sing.

By moonset, the Chantry of Saint Verren stood empty.

By dawn, the watchtowers reported two figures climbing the salt road north, toward the Spire — and a third set of hoofprints following, unhurried, at a distance no order had authorized.
