## 10. A Name With No Owner

The city woke me before the sun did.

Grayhollow has a sound it makes at first light — the groan of bridges settling, the clatter of fish-carts on wet cobbles, the bells of the Tide Chapel ringing a hour that no longer exists. I lay in the window of Wren's garret and listened to all of it, and I thought: *this is what a world sounds like when it decides to keep going.*

Below me, the Spire stood against the paling sky.

It was still scarred. Its crown was black where the covenant had burned through it, and half its shrines were rubble, and moss climbed its flanks like slow green fingers trying to close old wounds. But it breathed. I could feel it from here, a pulse under the soles of my feet, faint as a sleeper's heartbeat — in, and out, and in again. Fifteen years it had been dying. Three days ago, someone had given it back its breath.

Someone who did not remember doing it.

I turned to look at the cot by the cold hearth, where Wren slept.

* * *

They had woken twice since the Giving, and both times they had looked at me with the flat, polite wariness of a stranger meeting a stranger. The first time, they had asked me who I was. The second time, they had asked me who *they* were, and when I said *Wren*, they had repeated the name like a word in a foreign language, tasting it, finding nothing familiar in it.

"It's yours," I told them then. "It was always yours."

"That's not possible," they'd said. "I don't remember anyone ever calling me that."

And that was true, and it wasn't, and I didn't know how to explain the difference to someone with an empty house where their life used to be. So instead I had done the only thing I knew how to do anymore. I had opened my hands, and I had remembered for them.

I told them about the night market under the tarpaulins, the way Wren's fingers moved over a charm like a thief counting another man's silver. I told them about the childhood fragment they kept in a locket and never sold, the one that hummed with snowfall and a woman's voice singing low. I told them about Corvane, and the betrayal, and the drowned underlevels where we'd run hand in hand while the water rose. I told them about Vane on the Spire's flank, holding her own Wardens at bay with nothing but her rank and her grief, buying us the minutes we needed.

Wren listened to all of it without expression, the way you listen to a story about people you've never met. But at the end, they asked one question.

"Why would someone give up everything? All of it? For a tower?"

"Because," I said, "the tower was holding up two worlds. And because—" and here I stopped, because the true answer was not mine to give. It belonged to the person Wren had been, and that person had poured themselves into the covenant like wine into dry ground.

But I remembered. That was my work now. I remembered that Wren had spent fifteen years built out of other people's memories, a patchwork self stitched from charms bought and stolen, and that in the end they had chosen to give away even the seams. Not because they hated what they were. Because they finally understood that what they were had never depended on what they remembered.

"You'll have to tell me again tomorrow," Wren had said, pulling the blanket up. "I'll forget most of it by morning."

"I know," I said. "That's why I'm here."

* * *

On the fourth morning after the Giving, we went down into the city together.

Wren walked like a stranger because they were one — hesitating at corners, startled by the noise, gripping my sleeve at the mouth of Sallow Lane where the night market was already setting up its stalls. The memory-charmers were out in force: a woman selling the taste of a summer orchard, a boy hawking first kisses and last words, an old man with a tray of small comforts — the smell of bread, the weight of a sleeping child, the feeling of being forgiven.

Magic, all of it. Illegal, all of it. The ban had not died with the old covenant; if anything, the Wardens were more nervous now, patrolling in threes, jumping at echoes. But the market was fuller than I had ever seen it. People came to buy not just memories but *proof* — proof that something had changed, that the air itself felt different, that the dread which had sat on Grayhollow for a generation had lifted like fog off the river.

A charm-seller caught sight of Wren and went white. Then she pressed a charm into their hand — unasked, unpaid — and hurried away before they could speak.

"What is it?" Wren asked, turning the little glass bead in their fingers.

"The day the Spire breathed," I said. "She's giving it back to you. You made it."

Wren held the charm up to the light. I watched them try to feel what everyone else felt looking at it, and fail, and set it carefully in their pocket anyway. Keeping it. That, too, was becoming a habit: keeping things they couldn't remember earning.

We were halfway across the market when the crowd parted, and Ilsa Vane stepped through it.

My whole body went rigid. But Warden-Captain Vane was out of uniform, in a plain gray coat, and she carried no irons, and the Wardens behind her — there were three — kept their hands well away from their weapons. She stopped an arm's length from Wren and studied them for a long moment, this stranger wearing the face of the outlaw she had hunted across half the city.

"Do you know who I am?" she asked.

"No," Wren said honestly.

"Good." Something moved behind her eyes — relief, or envy, or both. "Then let me introduce myself. Ilsa Vane. I tried very hard to arrest you, once. I failed." She glanced up at the Spire, its crown still dark against the morning. "I've spent three days telling my superiors that the resonance they tracked to your street has dissipated. That whatever caused it is gone. They believe me, mostly, because they want to. The alternative is admitting the tower lives again, and the law has no answer for that."

"And if they find out?" I asked.

Vane looked down at me, and for a moment the captain's armor slipped, and I saw the woman whose sister had been erased by the ban — erased so thoroughly that even Vane's own memories of her had been confiscated and burned. "Then I'll lie again," she said quietly. "I've gotten good at it. It turns out enforcing a bad law and breaking one require the same skill: knowing exactly how much truth a thing can survive."

She reached into her coat and drew out a folded paper — a warrant, I realized, bearing Wren's face and the name *WREN EASTWICK* in heavy official ink. She held it out. Wren took it, frowning at it like a riddle.

"Burn it," Vane said. "Keep it. I don't care. As far as I'm concerned, Wren Eastwick died on the Spire, and whoever you are now, you're no one I have jurisdiction over." She turned to go, then paused. "There are others like me. Wardens who doubt. If your tower ever needs friends inside the walls—"

"It will," I said.

"—then leave a mark on the third shrine from the river gate. The one that still stands." She pulled her collar up and melted into the crowd, and the crowd closed around her, and that was that.

Wren looked at the warrant a long time. Then they folded it small and put it in their pocket next to the charm.

"Why keep it?" I asked.

"Because it's the only document in the world that says who I was," Wren said. "Even if I can't read it like a name. Even if it belongs to someone else." A pause. "Maybe especially then."

* * *

The outlaws found us before sundown.

They came quietly, in ones and twos, the way people move when they're used to being hunted: the charm-seller who had given Wren the bead, the boy who sold first kisses, a lamplighter, a smuggler with river-mud on his boots, a dozen others whose faces I knew from the edges of the night market. They filled the garret stairwell and spilled into the room until there was hardly space to stand, and none of them said *thank you*, because thanks were dangerous words in Grayhollow. Instead they brought things. Bread. Lamp oil. A blanket still warm from someone's hearth. A jar of honey. A child's wooden bird, offered shyly to me, because word had spread that the mute child spoke now, and the city's forgotten had decided that was a miracle worth feeding.

"We can't stay here," the smuggler said. "Wardens walked the street twice today. But there's a place under the Tanner's Bridge — dry, hidden, big enough. Yours, if you want it. Ours, too, if you'll have us."

Wren looked at me. I could see the question forming — *why are they doing this, who do they think I am* — and I answered it before they could ask.

"They think you're the one who saved them," I said. "They're right."

"But I don't remember saving anyone."

"They remember enough for both of you." I looked around the crowded room, at the faces turned toward us — wary, hopeful, hungry for something to belong to. "That's how it works now. That's how it's always worked, maybe. Nobody keeps their own story alone."

* * *

Night came, and the garret emptied, and Wren stood at the window looking at the Spire while I lit the lamp.

"Tell me again," Wren said. It had become our evening ritual, these three days. "Tell me what I gave up."

So I told them. The locket with the snowfall. The false names, the purchased pasts, the patchwork self. The climb up the dead tower, shrine by shrine, past the bones of every keeper who had come before. The moment at the crown when the broken covenant had opened like a wound and asked for a life's worth of memory, freely given — and Wren had knelt, and unstrung every charm from their neck and wrists and pockets, and poured them out one by one, and finally given the last thing, the truest thing: their own name, the memory of being *Wren Eastwick*, released into the covenant like a key turning in a lock older than kingdoms.

And the Spire had breathed. And Noor had sung. And somewhere below, a Warden had held the line against her own soldiers, and the world had not ended, and the morning had come anyway.

When I finished, Wren was quiet for a long time.

"There's something you're not telling me," they said at last. "Something about the giving. I can tell, because you always stop in the same place, right at the end, like a door you won't open."

I hadn't known I did that. But they were right. There was a door, and I had been standing in front of it for three days.

At the crown of the Spire, in the last moment before the covenant closed, it had spoken — not in the old language, but directly, into the marrow of both of us. It had accepted Wren's gift. And it had named its keepers. Two of them. One who sang, and one who gave.

And then it had said a third thing, meant for Wren alone, which the covenant had sealed inside *me* because Wren no longer had anywhere to keep it. A message from the person they had been, entrusted to the tower, to be delivered whenever the empty-handed stranger was ready to hear it.

I had been waiting, these three days, for them to be ready.

Wren turned from the window. The lamplight caught their face — a stranger's face, open and unguarded, belonging to no one and therefore able to become anyone.

"Tell me," they said.

I took a breath, and opened the door.

"The covenant kept one thing back," I said. "One memory, out of everything you gave. It says a keeper's price must be paid in full, but a keeper's *purpose* is never taken — only stored. And it wants you to know—" My voice caught, and I steadied it. "It wants you to know that the name wasn't the real gift. The real gift was choosing to give it. And that choice doesn't live in your memory. It lives in you. It's the reason the strangers downstairs fed you tonight. It's the reason the Spire still breathes. Whoever you are now — you're still the one who did that. Memoryless. Unbroken. *Chosen.*"

Wren stood very still. Outside, far off, the Spire pulsed — in, and out — and beneath our feet, so faint it might have been imagination, the stones of Grayhollow pulsed with it.

"I don't remember any of that," Wren said softly.

"No," I agreed. "But I do. And starting tomorrow—" I smiled, and took their hand, the way I had in the flooded dark when we were running for our lives, "—we're going to make some new ones. Both of us. Keepers watch the border between realms, and the border runs straight through this city, and nobody watches it alone."

Wren looked down at our joined hands, then up at me, and something kindled in the stranger's face that I recognized instantly, because I had seen it a hundred times in the person they used to be:

Not memory. Not a name.

Belonging.

"Then we'd better start at dawn," Wren said. "Because tonight, for the first time since the Giving, the Spire is humming. And I think—" they turned to the window, where the tower's dark crown had begun, very faintly, to glow, "—I think it's trying to tell us something."

I joined them at the glass. Far below, at the third shrine from the river gate, a light was moving that was not a lantern.

And it was climbing.
