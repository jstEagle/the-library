## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The morning of the poisoning began, as all the worst mornings did, with honey.

Juniper Thackeray stood in the antechamber off the king's private dining hall, hands folded, spine straight, wearing the grey-and-gold livery of the royal household like a second skin that had never quite stopped chafing. Three years in Vellamere's palace, and she had learned that danger rarely announced itself with a blade or a shout. It came dressed as breakfast. It came sweet.

"Thackeray." Master of Kitchens Odell bustled in, flour to his elbows, breath smelling of onions and anxiety. "His Majesty requests the pear compote this morning. The one from the southern jars."

Her stomach tightened. The southern jars. The ones that had arrived three days ago, wax-sealed, unmarked, no manifest. In a well-run household, that would have been refused at the gate. But the king had been in one of his moods lately — restless, thin-skinned, snapping at servants over imagined slights — and when Aldric Vellamere wanted pears, pears appeared on his table regardless of their provenance.

"Of course, Master Odell," she said, and heard her own voice come out steady, which surprised her. She'd had practice at steady.

The tasting chamber was small and cold, lit by a single high window that let in a blade of pale morning light. A long table ran its length, and on it the dishes would arrive one by one, each portioned for her before it ever touched the king's plate. She was, technically, the fourth taster of the day — there were others who handled the wine, the bread, the meats — but she was the one they trusted with everything else. The one whose tongue could find what the others missed.

That reputation had been earned slowly, dish by dish, and it had cost her more than anyone at court knew. Because Juniper Thackeray's tongue was not merely careful. It was *gifted* — though gifted was a word she kept locked in a box at the bottom of her soul, along with every other word that might get her hanged. Magic was outlawed in Vellamere. Had been for sixty years, since the Purge of the Thorn, when the last hedge-witches were dragged to the pyres and the old pacts burned with them. What remained of the craft survived in whispers: charms sold for copper in night markets, blessings murmured over cradles, and one food-taster who could taste things no tongue should taste.

She could taste intent. That was the truth of it, the secret she carried like a stone behind her breastbone. Poison announced itself to her plainly enough — bitter, metallic, a wrongness on the back of the tongue — but so did magic worked into food or drink. Charmed wine tasted of summer storms. A blessing baked into bread came through as warm and yeasty-sweet, like standing in a doorway of a bakery at dawn. And malice, malice had a flavor all its own: cold, mineral, like licking an iron fence in deep winter.

The first dishes came and went without incident. Porridge with cream — clean, tasting only of oats and the cook's heavy hand with salt. Smoked river trout — clean, though faintly of the smokehouse's tar. Bread, butter, a compote of damsons from the autumn stores. She swallowed each measured bite, waited the required span, watched by the silent steward with his ledger, and pronounced each safe with a nod. Routine. Survival performed so smoothly it looked like nothing at all.

Then the pears.

Odell himself brought the jar, carrying it the way one carries a sleeping viper, and set it before her with a small apologetic cough. "Wax seal's intact," he said. "No marks on the glass. But you'll want to look sharp, Thackeray. His Majesty's been peculiar about the fruit lately."

"Peculiar" was a word that had been following the king around for months now, Juniper thought. Peculiar moods. Peculiar appetites. Peculiar wasting — though the court physicians called it melancholy, and the queen's household called it grief, and nobody called it what it might actually be, because nobody else could taste what she could.

She took up the silver spoon. The compote inside the jar was a soft amber gold, pears poached to translucence, syrup thick and glistening. Beautiful. Innocent-looking. Her mouth had already begun to water, which was the body's stupid betrayal, because her tongue knew better than her stomach ever would.

She dipped the spoon. Lifted a single pear slice with a thread of syrup. Touched it to her tongue.

Cold.

Not the cold of chilled fruit — the pears sat at cellar temperature, nothing more. This was cold that bloomed *backward*, spreading from the tip of her tongue up into her sinuses, behind her eyes, down her throat like a swallow of winter air. Underneath the honey and the soft cooked fruit lay something else entirely: a mineral wrongness, iron and frost and something older beneath both, something that tasted the way a frozen pond looks from underneath — dark, still, waiting.

Malice. Old malice. Worked and layered and patient.

Juniper did not flinch. Three years of training held her face smooth as poured cream while the cold spread through her skull and her heart began to slam against her ribs hard enough that she felt it in her ears. She set the spoon down. She took a slow sip of water. She breathed.

Then, because the steward was watching and the ledger was open and a taster who spat was a taster who answered questions, she swallowed.

It went down like a coin of ice, settling somewhere beneath her sternum, radiating cold outward in slow pulses. *Don't panic,* she told herself. *Panic raises the pulse. Raised pulse spreads it faster.* She didn't know if that was true. She knew it sounded true, and right now she needed true-shaped things to hold onto.

"How is it?" the steward asked, pen poised.

"Excellent," Juniper said. "Sweet. The pears are very fine this year."

The steward made his note. Odell beamed and hurried off to see the compote plated for the king.

Juniper stood very still in the cold little room and understood, with terrible clarity, that she had just approved a poison for the King of Vellamere.

* * *

She should have spoken. That was the thought that circled her, hawk-like, through the entire interminable hour that followed — through the serving of the meal, through the king's pleasure at his pears (he ate two full servings, sighing with something close to contentment, and Juniper watched him from her station by the wall and felt each spoonful he took like a blow), through the clearing of the table and the dismissal of the household. She should have dropped the spoon. Should have cried out, clutched her throat, done *something*. A taster's whole purpose was that moment. Her whole purpose, her whole dangerous value, hung on the instant where she said yes or no.

But she hadn't. And she knew why, even as she hated herself for it.

Because if she spoke, there would be questions. Questions led to investigations; investigations led to interrogations; interrogations, in Vellamere, led to the questioner's name being written into ledgers that Wren Corliss kept. And somewhere in those ledgers, if anyone pulled hard enough on the thread of Juniper Thackeray, orphan of the Lowbridge tenements, they would find the other thing she was. The charm-seller. The witch. The girl who broke the law nightly to keep six people fed and sheltered in a rotting building above the tanneries.

One word from her about magic in the king's food, and the inquiry would not stop at the pears. It would walk backward through her whole life until it found the night market stall, the copper charms, the whispered cantrips. They would burn her. And worse — far worse — they would start pulling threads near the people she loved, and Tam would lie badly, and Maren would lie bravely, and either way the tenement family would be unraveled thread by thread in front of witnesses.

So she had swallowed the king's poison and smiled and said the pears were excellent, and now she walked the long gallery toward the servants' stair with a coin of ice melting slowly under her sternum and her mind moving fast and cold as a weasel.

*Think. Think.*

First: how bad was it? She catalogued herself the way she'd catalogue a dish. Tongue: numb at the tip, fading. Throat: tight but easing. Chest: the cold coin, pulsing slower now, weaker. Whatever it was, it wasn't a killing dose — or it wasn't meant to kill *her*. That was the part that turned her blood to slush. The dose in the jar was calibrated. Measured. A slow poison, built to accumulate. One serving wouldn't fell a man. Ten would. Twenty. The king had been eating charmed food for months — she was suddenly, horribly certain of it, remembering all the "peculiar" moods, the wasting, the way his hands had begun to tremble at council meetings. Someone had been feeding him winter, spoonful by spoonful, and today was simply the day her tongue finally caught it.

Second: who? Not Odell — the man sweated honesty. Not the steward. The jar itself had come from outside, unmarked, unmanifested. Which meant someone with access to the provisioning chain, someone patient, someone who wanted the king weak rather than dead. Dead kings made martyrs and wars. Sick kings made compliant ones.

Third — and here her thoughts snagged, caught like wool on a nail — third was the flavor itself. She had tasted worked magic before, dozens of times, in charms and blessings and once, memorably, in a love-philtre some fool had slipped into a lordling's wine. All of it had tasted *warm*. Human magic, hearth-magic, the kind her grandmother's generation had practiced before the pyres. This was nothing of the kind. This was cold as a grave in January, structured as architecture, and threaded through with an intent so vast and patient it made human malice feel like a child's tantrum. Whatever had been worked into those pears, it hadn't been made by any charm-seller she knew.

Which left only the impossible conclusion, the one she kept shoving away and it kept coming back: somebody at court had access to magic that shouldn't exist anymore. Magic from before the Purge. Or magic from somewhere else entirely.

By the time she reached the servants' hall, she had made her decision, and it frightened her more than the poison did.

She went back for the jar.

The dining hall was being reset for the evening meal, footmen moving tables, maids shaking out linens. Nobody paid attention to a taster returning for her kit — tasters were furniture, mostly, tolerated fixtures. She retrieved her satchel from beneath the sideboard, and while adjusting its strap, with the ease of long practice at making herself invisible, she tipped the nearly-empty compote jar into her satchel along with the spoon, wrapped in her spare apron. A smear of amber syrup clung to the glass. Enough. She hoped it was enough.

Her heart hammered the whole way down the servants' stair. If anyone asked, she was carrying kitchen leavings to the pig-buckets — plausible, dull, beneath notice. No one asked. Furniture was never asked anything.

Only at the postern gate, where the afternoon light came slanting gold through the arch, did she allow herself one breath that shook. Somewhere behind her, the King of Vellamere was digesting his pears, spoonful by patient spoonful, and the woman who could have warned him had instead stolen the evidence and run — because warning him meant burning, and burning meant leaving her family alone in a city that ate orphans.

*Gods forgive me,* she thought, and did not believe forgiveness would come cheaply.

* * *

The Lowbridge district received her the way it received everyone: with a shrug and a smell. Tannery reek and woodsmoke, cabbage-water and horse dung, the eternal damp that rose from the river flats and softened everything — boots, mortar, resolve. Juniper moved through it with the automatic economy of someone who had grown up counting corners: the spot by the cooper's where the watch gathered to gamble dice and could be safely ignored, the alley shortcut that stank less than it looked, the drainpipe she could climb in four seconds if running became necessary.

The tenement rose at the end of Tanner's Row, four stories of sagging timber and stubbornness, leaning against its neighbor like a drunk leaning on a friend. Home. The word still caught strangely in her throat sometimes, even after three years.

Maren was in the ground-floor kitchen when she came in — Maren who had been a laundress before her hands gave out, broad and grey-streaked and immovable as a dock bollard, currently attacking a mountain of mending with a needle that moved like a striking adder. She looked up, and her eyes narrowed immediately, because Maren missed nothing.

"You've gone white as whey," she said. "Sit down before you fall down."

"I'm fine."

"You're lying, which is worse." The needle paused. "Tam's upstairs. Eat something first."

"I couldn't eat—"

"Maren says eat," said a voice from the doorway, and Tam slid in — Tam, seventeen and quicksilver, all elbows and grin, hair cropped short for the work he did hauling at the docks. He took one look at her face and his grin died. "Juni. What happened?"

She sat. She let Maren push a bowl of barley soup at her. And then, because these people had fed her when she was starving, had taken her in off the street three years ago when she'd fled the palace the first week with a taster's wage and nowhere to sleep, she told them. Not everything — never everything — but enough. The pears. The cold. The swallowing. The jar in her satchel.

The kitchen went very quiet. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"Say that again," Maren said slowly. "Slower. The part about the taste."

"Cold. Mineral. Like—" Juniper searched for words and found only images. "Like the air over a frozen river at dusk. And underneath it, intent. Patient intent. I've tasted malice before, in food. This was malice the way a cathedral is a hut. Something enormous, and it doesn't care about the king at all. The king is just... the door it's walking through."

Tam had gone still in the way he did when his quick mind caught fire. "You took the jar?"

She nodded.

"That's evidence," he said. "Evidence gets people hanged. Evidence also gets people *answers*." His grin flickered back, thinner, sharper. "I know a glassblower's apprentice who owes me. We can seal it proper, keep the residue from spoiling."

"And what do we do with answers?" Maren demanded. She'd put down the mending entirely now, needle abandoned like a sheathed knife. "Girl, listen to me. You tasted poison in the king's food and you said nothing. That's a hanging offense if it ever comes out — and it will come out, because secrets in this city are like fish: they go off fast and they stink up everything nearby. So we don't sit on this. We move. Tonight."

"Move where?" Juniper asked. "To whom? Who do we tell — the watch? Half of them take Wren Corliss's coin. The physicians? They'd bleed him with leeches and call it melancholy, same as always. There's no one, Maren. That's the whole shape of the trap. There's no one to tell."

"There's you," said Tam quietly.

The fire popped. Juniper stared at him.

"You can taste it," he said. "You can taste what it is, maybe where it comes from. Nobody else in this city can do that. You're not just the witness, Juni. You might be the only instrument in Vellamere that reads this particular language."

"I'm a food-taster," she said. "I sell luck-charms for coppers. I am not—"

"You are," Tam said, "the person who knows the king is being murdered by something that isn't human, and the person most likely to figure out why. Those are the same person. You."

Maren snorted, but it was a thinking snort, not a dismissive one. "He's got the right of it, much as I hate admitting a boy of seventeen has sense. But sense isn't safety. Corliss's spies are everywhere, and if you start asking questions about magical poisonings—"

"I won't ask questions," Juniper said. The plan was assembling itself in her mind, piece by piece, the way she assembled a stall display: carefully, beautifully, deceptively innocent. "I'll *listen*. Tomorrow night's market. Charm-trade draws all kinds — hedge-folk, herb-wives, the odd renegade priest. Someone in that crowd knows this flavor. Someone's grandmother tasted it before the Purge. I sell my little charms, I keep my ears open, and I pay for stories the way I always do. With copper and silence."

"And the jar?" Tam asked.

"We hide it. Properly hidden. Not here." She glanced around the kitchen — at the worn table, the patched chairs, the kettle Maren had owned for thirty years — and felt the weight of what she was about to say settle onto her shoulders like a yoke. "If anyone connects me to this, the first place they'll search is here. With you. I won't have that."

"So we split the risk," Maren said flatly. "That's what family does."

"That's what family does," Juniper agreed, and hated how much it cost her to say it, and loved every one of them for meaning it.

Later — much later, after the soup and the plans and Maren forcing a second bowl on her "for the cold in your chest, whatever it is" — Juniper climbed to the narrow room under the eaves that she shared with two others, and sat by the window with the city spread below her, smoking and murmuring in the dark.

Under her sternum, the coin of cold pulsed once, faintly, like something turning over in its sleep.

She pressed her palm flat against her breastbone and thought about flavors. About the architecture of that malice, layered and patient and *old*. About the way it hadn't cared about the king at all — the way it had tasted, almost, of *elsewhere*.

Tomorrow night, the market. Tomorrow night, questions dressed as silence.

Below her window, far off across the rooftops, the palace glowed with candlelight, and somewhere in its golden heart a dying king slept the sleep of the gently poisoned, and dreamed, perhaps, of pears.

Juniper watched until the candles guttered low, and then she took out the jar one last time, looked at the amber smear of syrup catching the moonlight, and whispered the oldest charm she knew — the one her grandmother had used for keeping, for holding, for *remembering* — over the glass, sealing the residue against time and spoilage.

The charm took hold with a soft warmth she felt clear through her chest, and for one strange moment the cold coin beneath her sternum pulsed in answer — as if the poison recognized the working, as if it *knew* her.

As if it had been waiting for her.

Juniper sat very still in the dark, heart hammering, and understood that she had just crossed a line she could not uncross.

Whatever came next, it had already noticed her.
