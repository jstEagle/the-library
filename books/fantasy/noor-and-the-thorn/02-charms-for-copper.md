## 2. Charms for Copper

The night market of Vellamere woke the way a cat wakes: all at once, and with its claws already out.

Juniper felt it stir beneath her feet as she came down the tenement stairs — the cobbles of Cooper's Row warming with lamplight, shutters folding back like eyelids, the smell of fried onions and tallow smoke rising to meet the river damp that rolled in off the Vell after dark. By day this street belonged to coopers and chandlers and the king's tax collectors. By night it belonged to everyone else.

She carried her wares in a basket lined with wool, and she carried them the way other women carried eggs: carefully, close to the body, aware that one wrong jostle could break everything.

There were nine charms tonight. Nine small stitched bundles of linen and thread, each no bigger than a sparrow's heart, each holding what Juniper could only describe as *a held breath* — a whisper of the old magic, caught and folded and sewn shut before it could escape and be noticed. A charm against fever for the washerwoman Marta, whose lungs rattled like a cart on cobbles. A charm against bad luck for the fishwife Odile, who had lost two husbands to the river and did not intend to lose a third. A charm against nightmares for a child whose name Juniper didn't know, paid for by a father who counted out his coppers one at a time and apologized with every coin.

Magic was outlawed in Vellamere. Had been for sixty years, since the last of the hedge-witches burned in the King's Square and the edicts went up on every gate: *No charms. No cantrips. No commerce in the unseen arts.* The penalty was the pyre, or the deep cells under the Citadel where they said the walls drank sound.

But the poor of Vellamere had never stopped believing in the unseen arts. They had only stopped believing in the law.

"Thimble," said Odile, materializing at Juniper's elbow the way she always did, smelling of brine and pipe smoke. That was Juniper's market name — Thimble, because she was small and because she mended things, though what she mended couldn't be seen with eyes. "You have it? The river-charm?"

"For you, always." Juniper passed the little bundle across, their hands cupped together around it like children sharing a coal. She felt the faint warmth of the magic answering Odile's grip, recognizing a believer. "Sew it into your left sleeve. Left side, over the heart. And Odile —"

"I know, I know." The fishwife's voice dropped to a rasp. "Don't thank you out loud. Don't say where I got it. Don't let the priest see me cross myself twice."

"Don't cross yourself twice," Juniper agreed. "Once is piety. Twice is confession."

Odile laughed, a bark like a gull, and pressed coins into Juniper's palm — too many coins, as always, and as always Juniper pushed half of them back. They performed this argument every week, a liturgy older than either of them, and every week Odile won by exactly the same margin: enough for bread, never enough for comfort.

The market flowed around them. A knife-grinder worked his wheel in a shower of orange sparks. Two brothers sold roasted chestnuts from a brazier shaped like a dragon's mouth, and children dared each other to reach into the flames. Somewhere a fiddle played a tune that was probably illegal too, though nobody had gotten around to outlawing joy outright — not yet, not in so many words.

Juniper moved through it all with her basket on her hip and her smile fixed in place, and underneath the smile, as always, ran the cold wire of fear.

Because here was the truth she carried with the charms: she was not merely selling trinkets. Every bundle in that basket held a thread of real working, drawn out through techniques her grandmother had died rather than surrender, refined across three years of trial and terror into something small enough to hide in a hem. She was committing high sorcery forty times a month in the shadow of the Citadel, and the only thing between her and the pyre was the fact that she was very, very good at not being seen.

Mostly.

* * *

Her buyers came to her in a rhythm she'd learned to read like weather. The washerwoman Marta always came early, while the lamps were still being lit, hurrying as if shame might catch her if she dawdled. The chimney-sweep Pell came late, near midnight, when the market thinned and he could stand close without being noticed. The night-soil men bought in bulk and paid in silence. The whores of Sable Lane bought more than anyone — charms against violence, against disease, against the particular darkness that lived in some men — and they paid in silver, and they never once asked how the charms were made, which was its own kind of mercy.

"You look tired, Thimble," said Sera from Sable Lane, counting out coins with painted fingers. "Tired and thin. You eating?"

"I'm eating."

"Liar. Here." Sera tucked a honey-cake into the basket beside the charms, as if contraband sweetness were less dangerous than contrabile magic. Perhaps it was. "My girls slept whole nights this week. Whole nights. Do you know what that's worth?"

"I know what it costs me," Juniper said lightly, and Sera's smile flickered, because even the ones who didn't ask still understood.

That was the arrangement Juniper had built, stitch by careful stitch, over three years. Not just a trade but a web. Every buyer was also a lookout; every charm sold was a debt of silence owed. If the king's men ever came for her, word would travel down Cooper's Row faster than boots could follow. The washerwomen would see. The chestnut-brothers would signal. The whole crooked, warm, unlawful organism of the night market would close around her like a hand.

It was a good system. It had kept her alive.

Which is why it unsettled her deeply when, halfway through the evening, she felt the system go quiet.

Not silent — the fiddle still played, the sparks still flew — but *quiet*, in the way of a room where someone has stopped breathing. The buyers began to drift away from her stall a few minutes earlier than usual. Odile finished her transaction and melted backward without her usual parting joke. Even the fiddler, she realized, had shifted his tune into something louder, something with more noise in it, as if trying to fill a space that wanted swallowing.

Juniper kept her eyes on her stitching and let her awareness spread the way her grandmother had taught her — not looking, but *listening with the skin*. The market's attention had turned, all of it, gently and without any single person moving, toward the mouth of Copper's Row.

Someone had come in who frightened people more than the law did.

She knew who it was before she saw him. You always knew Wren Corliss by the way rooms rearranged themselves around him.

* * *

He stood at the edge of the lamplight like a man who had been carved out of the city's shadow and given a coat. Grey wool, grey gloves, a face that was handsome the way a well-made lock is handsome — precise, closed, designed to keep things out. He was not young anymore; there was frost in his dark hair now, and lines around his eyes that had been cut by decades of watching people. But he stood the way he had always stood, weight even on both feet, hands loose, a man who had never once in his life needed to hurry.

Wren Corliss. Spymaster of Vellamere. The king's left hand, they called him, because the right hand was the sword and the left was the one that signed the warrants.

And once — in another life, in a smaller, warmer room — he had been hers.

Juniper had been fourteen when the Citadel took her. Not as a prisoner; as an *apprentice*. They gathered orphans with quick tongues and tested them against poisons, because a royal food-taster had to be trained young, trained thoroughly, trained until the tongue itself became an instrument of the crown. Wren Corliss had overseen that training. He had taught her the bitter alphabet of alkaloids, the sweet lie of belladonna in wine, the way arsenic fogged the breath. He had taught her to read a room the way she was reading this one. He had taught her, in his dry and patient voice, that fear was information and panic was waste.

He had also, once, found a stolen sweetmeat in her pocket and said nothing about it, and she had loved him for three years afterward with the fierce, stupid love of a child for the one adult who was kind on purpose.

Then she had survived her first year of tasting, earned her post, and discovered that the woman she was becoming could not exist inside the walls he'd built for her. She had left the Citadel quietly. He had let her go quietly. Neither of them had ever spoken of it.

Now he was standing twenty feet away in the night market, watching her sell illegal magic, and the quiet in the air had teeth.

*He knows,* she thought. Her needle did not pause; her grandmother's ghost would have struck her dead. *He has known for years.*

Because that was the terrible arithmetic she'd done long ago, sleepless in the tenement with Tam snoring in the next room: Wren Corliss did not miss things. A network of charm-buyers operating weekly in the same market for three years, under the nose of the man who had trained her own senses — either he knew, or he was worse at his work than she'd always believed. And he was not worse at his work than anyone.

So why was she still breathing?

*Because he tolerates it,* she told herself, for the ten-thousandth time. *Because the charms are small. Because he decides what crimes exist in Vellamere, and mine are beneath his notice.* It was a comfortable theory. It had kept her sleeping, mostly. But theories, her grandmother used to say, are like charms: they hold until they don't, and then they fail all at once.

Wren Corliss did not approach her. He never approached her. He stood at the edge of the light for the length of one transaction — Pell the chimney-sweep, arriving late, receiving his charm against lung-rot with a bow — and then he turned, unhurried as tide, and walked back out of Copper's Row, and the market exhaled around her like a held breath released.

Juniper's hands were steady. Her heart was not.

*He's counting,* she thought. *He's always counting. One day the count will matter.*

* * *

It was nearly midnight when the stranger in grey arrived, and by then Juniper's nerves had been worn down to bright wire, and she almost missed him entirely.

He came from the direction of the river, which was itself strange — nothing good came off the Vell at this hour except fog and drowned things. He was tall and stooped at once, the stoop of a man carrying weight inside his body, and his cloak was grey the way ash is grey, the way old frost is grey. Not Wren Corliss's tailored grey. This grey had been rained on and slept in and bled through.

He walked past the chestnut-brazier and the sparks leapt sideways, away from him, as if flinching.

Juniper's skin prickled. That was not wind. Sparks did not flinch from wind.

He stopped at her stall. Up close she could see that under the hood his face was wrong — not injured exactly, though there was blood at his lip, but *faded*, as if someone had begun erasing him from the world and grown bored halfway through. His eyes were the pale grey-green of ice over water, and they fixed on her face with an urgency that made the market noise fall away.

"You're the taster," he said. His voice was a ruin, gravel dragged over gravel. "The king's taster. The one with the *tongue*."

Every instinct she owned screamed at her to deny it, to laugh, to call him mad. Instead she heard herself say, quietly, "What do you want?"

"The map." His gloved hand went into his cloak and came out clenched around something she couldn't yet see. "I've carried it thirty years. Carried it through six kingdoms and both realms and every purge between. There's no one left to carry it. Do you understand? *There is no one left but you.*"

"Sir, I think you're ill—"

"I'm dying." He said it the way other men said *it's raining*. "Frost-wounds. From the court's own alchemist. The king is killing the land, girl, and no one with the blood to stop it knows—" He swayed. The brazier-sparks bent away from him again, harder this time, and somewhere behind Juniper a dog began to howl.

"Sit down." She reached for his arm to steady him and the cold that came off him sank straight through her sleeve into the bone, a cold with a *texture* to it, a crawling, crystalline wrongness that her tongue recognized before her mind did — the same signature, the same frozen poison-note she'd caught in the king's wine three nights ago and told herself she'd imagined.

The world tilted slightly. *Oh,* she thought, absurdly calm. *So I didn't imagine it.*

The stranger pressed the thing in his hand into hers. It was stiff and heavy and slick in places, and even in the dark she knew what the slickness was. A map. Folded many times, stained dark, warm from his body and colder than his body everywhere his blood hadn't touched.

"Thorn-heir," he whispered.

The word went through her like a hook through cloth.

"What did you say?"

"Thorn-heir. Find the stones. Wake what sleeps. Before the last stone goes dark, before the severance—" He coughed, and the cough had frost in it; she saw the breath of it glitter, actual glitter, tiny stars of ice falling from his lips onto the cobbles, where they did not melt. "They'll come for it. The grey men. The spymaster's hounds. Burn it rather than be taken. Or better — *read* it. Read it and run."

Around them, the market had gone truly silent now. The fiddle had stopped. Doors were closing. Whatever the night market of Vellamere was willing to ignore — illegal charms, illegal songs, the whole soft economy of the desperate — it was not willing to ignore whatever was happening at Juniper's stall, and she felt her precious web of watchers dissolving around her like sugar in rain.

"Please," she said, gripping the man's sleeve. "Who am I? What am I heir *to*?"

He smiled. It was the worst thing she had ever seen, that smile — relieved and terrified and grateful all at once, the smile of a man setting down a weight he had carried so long he'd forgotten his own shape without it.

"You're the last," he said. "That's all that matters. You're the *last*."

Then his knees folded, and Juniper — royal food-taster, charm-seller, keeper of nine small illegal breaths of magic — caught a dying stranger in her arms in the middle of Cooper's Row, and felt the frost of him biting through her bodice into her skin, and heard, from the mouth of the street, the unmistakable cadence of boots approaching at a run.

Guards. Citadel guards, lantern-light swinging, voices sharp with the particular excitement of men who have been sent for something specific and have just spotted it.

The stranger's hand spasmed once against her wrist — pressing the map's shape into her flesh like a seal — and then the pressure was gone, and the cold in his body changed quality, became the honest cold of death instead of the dishonest cold of frost-magic, and Juniper Thackeray knelt in the night market holding a corpse and a bloodstained inheritance while the lanterns came closer.

*Run,* said the voice of her grandmother, of Wren Corliss, of every survival instinct she had ever sharpened. *Now.*

She stuffed the map down the front of her shirt, snatched up her basket of charms, and ran.
