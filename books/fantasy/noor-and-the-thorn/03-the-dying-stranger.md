## 3. The Dying Stranger

The night market smelled of wet wool and frying onions, of tallow smoke and the river's green rot, and Juniper Thackeray loved it the way a drowning woman loves the surface — desperately, and with no illusions about how long it would hold her up.

She had claimed her corner between a pie-seller whose crusts were legendary and a mender who could make a coat last three winters, and she had spread her wares on a scrap of black velvet that had once lined a coffin, according to its previous owner, who had sworn this made it luckier. On the velvet sat her charms: knotted threads dyed with madder and weld, little clay beads fired in Tam's bread oven, sprigs of rowan tied with red string. To any passing watchman they were trinkets, peasant nonsense, the sort of thing sold at every crossroads in Vellamere. Only her buyers knew that the knots held more than string.

"Against bad dreams," she murmured to the laundress haggling over a blue-knotted cord. "Tie it to the bedpost. If the dreams come anyway, untie one knot each night until they stop."

"And if I run out of knots?"

"Come back. I'll tie you another."

The laundress paid her two coppers and a heel of yesterday's bread, which was worth more than the coppers, and moved off into the crowd with the small, furtive relief of someone who has purchased hope on credit.

Juniper watched her go and felt the familiar arithmetic turn over in her chest. Three coppers for the charm, four already tonight from the chimney-sweep's wife, one from a boy too young to need what he'd bought but old enough to know fear when he saw it in his mother's face. Eight coppers, a heel of bread, and half a sausage someone had pressed into her hand without explanation. Rent was six days away. The tenement needed coal. Little Wren — not *that* Wren, thank all merciful saints, but Wren-the-orphan, seven years old and sharp as a fishhook — needed new boots before the frost set in properly, because the child had grown out of everything except appetite.

It was a good night. It was almost enough. And because it was almost enough, Juniper had learned long ago to distrust it.

The market shifted around her like water around stones. Lantern-light turned faces amber and anonymous. Somewhere a fiddler played something in a minor key that made even the pie-seller pause in his ladling. And at the edge of the light, where the stalls gave way to shadow and the shadow gave way to the river stairs, a man in grey stood watching her.

He had been there an hour. Maybe longer. She'd noticed him the way you notice weather — peripherally, as a pressure rather than a presence. He wasn't dressed like a watchman or a Corliss agent; agents wore good boots and stood with their weight forward, hungry. This man stood like someone holding himself upright by argument alone, his grey cloak beaded with river mist, his hood low. He was tall. He was very still. Stillness like that cost something, and whatever it cost him, he was paying down to his last coin.

Juniper sold a charm against rheumatism to a grandfather with hands like tree roots, and did not look at the grey man again, and thought about him constantly.

*Wren Corliss?* No. She knew Corliss's silhouette the way she knew her own heartbeat, and besides, if Corliss wanted her he wouldn't send a stranger to stand in the rain and stare. *A buyer?* Buyers approached. They fingered the wares, asked leading questions, pretended skepticism. They didn't haunt the edges of lamplight like ghosts waiting for an invitation.

*Something else, then.*

The fiddler finished his tune. The crowd applauded thinly and dispersed into other entertainments. And in the space the music left behind, the grey man moved.

He came toward her stall the way a falling tree comes toward the ground — inevitably, badly, with a terrible gracelessness that was somehow worse than speed. Two steps, and she saw that his left leg wasn't working properly, dragging at the ankle. Three steps, and she saw the dark stain spreading down his side beneath the cloak, black in the lantern-light, glistening. Four steps, and the market seemed to contract around him, conversations faltering, the pie-seller going quiet, everyone smelling blood the way dogs do, without needing to see it.

He reached her stall. He fell.

Not gracefully. Not dramatically. He simply stopped being able to stand, folding over her table of charms like a puppet whose strings had been cut mid-performance, scattering knotted threads and clay beads across the cobblestones. His hands came down on the velvet — on the coffin-lining, on the lucky cloth — and Juniper heard herself say, absurdly, "That's two coppers extra," because terror had always made her flippant, and then his hand shot out and closed around her wrist.

His grip was cold. Not sick-cold, not dead-man's-draught cold. Cold like January iron, like the inside of a well, like something that had never known warmth at all. She gasped. He pulled her down, and up close she saw his face under the hood and forgot every clever thing she had ever thought.

He was young. Thirty, perhaps, though the wound had aged him past that in hours. Grey eyes gone glassy. Skin so pale it looked translucent, veined faintly at the temples with lines of silver-blue, like frost climbing a windowpane. And along his jaw, spreading down his neck beneath the collar, the same pattern — delicate white traceries, crystalline, beautiful in the way that dangerous things are beautiful, creeping outward from some central ruin with every labored breath he took.

Frost-wounds. In high summer. In Vellamere, where magic had been outlawed for sixty years and the only ice anyone saw came from the ice-house carts.

"Thorn-heir," the man said.

His voice was ruined, scraped down to gravel and breath. But the words came out clear as a bell struck once, and they went through Juniper like the bell's vibration goes through bone.

"No," she said. "No, I think you've got the wrong—"

"The wrong what?" A ghost of a smile. "Wrong girl? Wrong city?" His fingers tightened on her wrist, and where they touched, cold sank through her skin and settled in the joint like a splinter of winter. "There is no wrong. There is only late. Listen — I am out of time, and you are out of choices, and both of those are my fault, so take the map."

"I don't want your map."

"Nobody wants the map." Another ghost-smile, flickering and gone. "I didn't want it. My mother didn't want it. It goes back further than wanting." He fumbled at his cloak with his free hand, and Juniper saw now that his right side was soaked through, that the blood was not entirely red — that threaded through it ran filaments of silver, catching the light like ore in stone. "Take it. Hide it. Don't read it where walls have ears, and in Vellamere, girl, every wall has ears."

"What is it? What's a Thorn-heir? Who are you?"

"My name was Aldous Vane." He said it carefully, like a man setting down something heavy for the last time. "Once. Before the Court took it back." He pressed the bundle into her hands — oilcloth, stiff with age and stained dark, tied with a cord that had been white once. It was lighter than it should have been. It hummed, very faintly, against her palms, the way a struck tuning fork hums after the sound has faded beyond hearing. "Seven stones. One oath. Find them before the king does, or before the frost does — whichever comes first."

"The frost?"

But his eyes had drifted upward, past her shoulder, fixed on something in the middle distance that was not the market, not the lanterns, not the living world. "You smell like her," he whispered. "Did you know that? You smell like the hedge-rows in spring. Like the old magic. She said the line would thin, but it never broke, it never—" A shudder took him, head to foot, and the frost-lines on his throat visibly advanced, silver creeping toward his cheekbones like a tide coming in. "Listen. Last thing. When they ask what you're carrying, you're carrying nothing. You're a trinket-seller. You sell knots and clay. Be small. Be nothing. Being nothing kept me alive for eleven years."

"Being nothing isn't keeping you alive now," Juniper said, and heard her own voice crack on it.

"No," he agreed, wonderingly. "Nothing like this ever happened to me either."

Then the bells started.

Not the hour-bells — those had rung already, marking the ninth hour, and the quarter-bells wouldn't come for another stretch of minutes. These were the watch-bells from the river gate, two quick strikes and a third, the pattern every market-child in Vellamere knew in their bones: *watch converging, clear the streets.* The crowd reacted like a flock of starlings, a ripple of motion and murmur, and down the lane toward the river stairs came torchlight and the rhythmic slap of boots, four men moving fast, moving together, moving with purpose.

Aldous Vane's grip on her wrist went slack.

"No," Juniper heard herself say, stupidly, uselessly, as if the word could argue with death. "No, wait, you can't — I haven't asked you anything yet, I don't know anything, you can't just—"

"Be small," he whispered. The frost had reached his mouth. His lips were going grey-blue, silver-blue, and his breath came out faintly visible in the summer air, a tiny ghost of fog that hung between them like a secret. "And Juniper Thackeray—"

She hadn't told him her name.

"—the thorn remembers. Whatever else forgets, whatever else rots, whatever they take from you — the thorn remembers."

The light left his eyes. It didn't fade; it *left*, the way a candle leaves a room when pinched, sudden and total, and what remained was a young man's face wearing frost instead of peace, silver traceries complete now, covering his throat and jaw and climbing one cheek like ivy claiming a wall. His hand slid from her wrist. The cold stayed.

* * *

Juniper did not remember deciding to run. She remembered the decision already made, already in her legs, already carrying her: bundle crushed to her chest, velvet abandoned, charms scattered in the mud like fallen stars, her body doing what three years of tasting royal food and selling outlaw magic had taught it to do, which was vanish.

She went left, away from the river stairs, into the tanner's warren where the lanes narrowed to shoulders' width and the smell of lime pits covered everything including pursuit. Behind her, boots. Shouting — "*Body! Body in the market!* Someone fetch the watch-surgeon!" — and then, worse, quieter, a voice she knew: "Seal the lanes. Nobody leaves. Whoever was nearest that man stays nearest."

Corliss. Of course it was Corliss. The spymaster didn't sleep, or slept standing up in shadows like a sword sleeps in its sheath, and a death in the night market with frost on the corpse would pull him out of whatever den he haunted faster than a fire in the palace.

Juniper ducked through the gap behind the tannery where the wall had crumbled years ago and been "repaired" with a board that swung aside if you knew its trick. She crossed the rendering yard, stepping in things she refused to identify, and came out on Cooper's Row with her heart slamming and the oilcloth bundle clutched so hard against her ribs she'd bruise herself with it. Above, the sky was clear and warm and indifferent, stars scattered across it like salt, and the moon hung fat and low over the palace hill, lighting the king's banners to a dull gold.

Somewhere behind her, Wren Corliss was asking questions. Somewhere ahead of her, home waited at the top of four flights of rotten stairs, full of people she would burn the world down to protect, people who had no idea what she carried pressed against her heart.

*The thorn remembers.*

She walked. Not fast — fast drew eyes. She walked the way Aldous Vane had told her to walk, small and nothing, a tired trinket-seller heading home with empty pockets, and only the bundle humming against her chest betrayed her, a sound below hearing, a pulse that was not hers.

At the mouth of Thackeray's Lane — her grandmother's name on the street sign, a coincidence she'd always found either funny or ominous, depending on the day — she stopped. Looked back once. The market glowed in the distance, torches clustering where the body lay, and above them, on a rooftop, a single figure stood motionless against the moonlight, and even at this distance, even without seeing his face, she knew the set of those shoulders.

*He'll come for me,* she thought. *Tomorrow, or the day after. He'll knock on the tenement door with his hat in his hands and his eyes full of apology, and he'll ask me what the dying man gave me, and I will have to decide whether the man who taught me to survive is the same man who'll hand me to the block.*

Inside the bundle, beneath the oilcloth, something shifted. Not the map — something else, folded with it, small and hard. She didn't look. Not here, not in the open, not with the moon watching and the spymaster's silhouette printed on the night.

She climbed the four flights. She knocked the family knock — two, pause, three — and heard Tam's feet hit the floorboards inside, heard little Wren's voice piping *"Is she bleeding?"* and Tam's answering *"Go back to bed"* delivered in the tone that meant neither of them believed it.

The door opened. Warmth and lamplight and the smell of yesterday's stew spilled out over her, and Juniper stood on the threshold with a dead man's inheritance humming against her ribs and frost-ache still sunk deep in her wrist where his fingers had been, and said:

"Everyone up. We need to talk."

Behind her, far off across the sleeping city, the first covenant-stone — though she didn't know its name yet, couldn't have spoken its name to save her life — felt her carry the map across the threshold of her home, and began, very slowly, to wake.
