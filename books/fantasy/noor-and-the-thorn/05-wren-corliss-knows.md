## 5. Wren Corliss Knows

The interrogation room in the Spire smelled of tallow and old fear, and Wren Corliss had long ago stopped noticing either. What he noticed was the boy's hands.

They were a pickpocket's hands, quick and clever, and they would not stop moving. The boy — Ferro, sixteen, a market runner with a face like a ferret and debts in three quarters of Vellamere — sat across the scarred oak table and fidgeted with a loose thread on his sleeve as though he could unravel his way out of the room.

"Again," Wren said. "From the moment the man in grey entered the night market."

"I told you everything, master. I swear it on my mother's—"

"On your mother's what?" Wren let the silence stretch. He was good at silence. It was the first thing they taught you in the Spire, before knives, before codes: how to make a room so quiet that a man could hear his own guilt breathing. "Your mother sold watered wine to sailors. I know her. I know you skipped her funeral to run dice for the Kettle Street boys. Swear on something true."

Ferro flinched. Outside the narrow window, dawn was coming up over Vellamere the colour of pewter, and the bells of the Low Temple began their slow counting of the hour. Six. Wren had been at this since midnight.

"The grey man came up Rowan Street," Ferro said, quieter now. "Walking like his boots hurt him. He went past the charm-sellers' row — everyone does, it's the fastest way to the fish gate — and then he stopped. At the juniper stall."

"At whose stall?"

"You know whose stall." The boy's voice dropped to almost nothing. "Juniper Thackeray's."

Wren looked at him for a long moment. Then he took out his notebook — a small, grease-stained thing bound in grey leather, the twin of a dozen others in his coat — and wrote nothing at all, just held the pen above the page until Ferro understood that the pause itself was a question.

"She sells charms," Ferro said finally. "Little ones. Knots and beads and such. Everyone knows. Nobody says."

*Everyone knows. Nobody says.* That was the whole architecture of the low city in six words, and Wren Corliss had built half his career on knowing exactly which silences to leave standing.

"And the grey man bought one?"

"He didn't buy anything. He just stood there. And then—" Ferro's hands started moving again, plucking at the thread. "And then he fell down. Right against her table. Like a puppet with cut strings. And she caught him, or tried to, and there was blood, and people started screaming, and the watch came, and I ran. That's all. That's everything. On my life."

"Your life," Wren said dryly, "is worth about four coppers in this market, but I'll take it." He closed the notebook. "You never saw her take anything from him. You never saw a map."

"No map," Ferro said eagerly. "No map at all."

"Mm." Wren rose. "Then you have nothing further to tell me, and I have nothing further to pay you for. Go home by the long way. If anyone asks where you've been, you were drunk in a gutter on Kettle Street, which is at least plausible."

The boy fled. Wren stood alone in the interrogation room while the bells finished striking six, and he did not move for a long time.

He had known Juniper Thackeray since she was nine years old.

Not well — never well; that was not the way of things between the Spire and the city it watched — but the way a falconer knows a hawk that will not come to the glove. She had been a kitchen girl at the palace when he'd found her, small and sharp-eyed, sorting grains of poisoned rice from safe ones with a certainty that made the old tasting-master weep with relief. *A tongue like yours is wasted on porridge,* he'd told her, and he'd been the one to recommend her elevation to the royal table, because a girl who could taste henbane in honey was worth more to the crown than a company of soldiers.

He had also been the one who noticed, three years ago, that the same girl was selling charms in the night market.

It had been a small thing. A knot of red thread sold to a fishwife against miscarriage. A bent iron nail wrapped in wool, hung over a door against bad luck. Trifles. Superstitions. By every law of Vellamere — and the law was absolute, written in the king's own hand after the Sundering Edicts — it was magic, and magic was death.

Wren had gone down to the market himself, in a plain coat, and stood across the lane and watched her work. She'd had a patter even then, easy and warm, laughing with the buyers, pressing a charm into a beggar's hand and waving off the coin. And when she'd glanced up — once, quickly, the way prey animals glance — and seen him, she had not run. She had met his eyes across the crowd, and lifted her chin, and gone on selling.

*Dare me,* that look had said. *I dare you.*

He had reported nothing. In his ledger of tolerated offences — a ledger that existed nowhere but his own head, and which would hang him if it were ever read aloud — Juniper Thackeray's charms occupied a single line: *small mercies, no harm in them, watch only.*

For three years he had watched only.

Now the king's seal sat heavy on the desk upstairs, and the order beneath it was written in the chancellor's hand, and it said: *Find the map. Find the woman who has it. Bring both to the Spire unspoiled.*

*Unspoiled.* Wren turned the word over. It meant alive. It meant intact. It meant that someone above him wanted whatever the dying stranger had pressed into Juniper Thackeray's hands badly enough to keep her breathing — and in Wren's long experience, when the crown wanted something kept breathing, it was because someone else wanted it dead.

* * *

The frost-wound lay under glass in the Spire's cold room, and it was beautiful, and Wren hated it.

The body of the grey-cloaked man had been brought in at dawn, laid out on a slab of black marble while the physicians argued over it. Wren stood at the edge of their argument and studied what remained of the stranger's right hand, where the flesh had gone white as candle-wax and veined through with lines of silver-blue, like frost ferns growing under skin. Where the pattern touched, the flesh was not dead, exactly. It was *elsewhere*. When Wren touched it — briefly, with one gloved finger — the cold that came back through the leather was not the cold of ice. It was the cold of a door left open in winter, the cold of an absence.

"Frost-burn," said Physician Hale, who was old and half-deaf and had been wrong about important things before. "Some alchemical agent. We'll have the analysis by evening."

"We won't," Wren said. "Because it isn't alchemical." He did not say the rest: that he had seen frost-burn once before, twenty years ago, on a smuggler pulled from the Siltmarshes with a fae blade in his chest, back when Wren had still been young enough to be sent into the field instead of merely sending others. The old files called it *thorn-touch*, and the files were sealed, and the reason they were sealed was that the last time anyone had catalogued it honestly, the crown had burned the catalogue and hanged the cataloguer.

Hale blinked at him. "Then what—"

"Have the hand packed in salt and sent to my office. Not to the archive. To *my* office." Wren pulled his gloves tighter. "And the analysis you promised — lose it. All of it. If the chancellor asks what killed this man, the answer is 'a knife,' and if he asks whose knife, the answer is 'another madman's,' and if he asks anything else, you are a deaf old fool who cannot help me. Can you be a deaf old fool, Hale?"

"I have been one for years," Hale said placidly. "It's why you keep me."

Wren almost smiled. Almost. Then he went out into the grey morning and gave orders that scattered his people across the low city like spilled ink: find the map, find the woman, watch the tenements on Rope Walk, watch the gates, watch the sewers. And then, because there was one errand he would trust to no one else, he went down into the night market himself, in daylight, when the stalls were shuttered and the lanes were empty.

* * *

The charm-sellers' row by daylight was a sad country of bent poles and faded awnings, and at the end of it, wedged between a shuttered pie stand and a wall stained black by generations of lamp-smoke, stood Juniper Thackeray's stall.

Wren had never actually stood at it before. Watching from across a lane was one thing; standing in the footprint of a crime was another. He looked at the little table with its worn grain, at the hook where she must hang her satchel, at the scrap of red thread caught on a nail — red thread, always red, the cheapest dye and the oldest superstition — and he let himself see it the way he should have seen it years ago.

She had been good. That was the thing he had never let himself fully admit. The knots were tied in patterns he recognized now, looking with the eyes the Spire had given him instead of the eyes he'd chosen to use: not random decoration but structure, intention, *grammar*. A loop here, a crossing there, each charm a small sentence written in a language the Edicts had outlawed and the Spire had pretended to forget. She had taught herself, or someone had taught her, and she had done it quietly, patiently, in the cracks of a city that would kill her for it, and she had used it for — what? Miscarriage wards. Luck for fishermen. Fever-knots for children in the fever season.

Three years of small mercies, and he had filed them under *watch only*, because the alternative was to file them under *arrest*, and some part of him — the part that had been poor once, before the Spire found him — had refused.

Now the chancellor's order sat in his breast pocket like a stone, and the frost-hand waited in salt in his office, and somewhere in Vellamere a girl he had mentored and spared and betrayed in equal measure was running with a dead man's map in her possession, and the crown wanted her *unspoiled*.

Wren picked up the scrap of red thread from the nail and turned it in his fingers. It was knotted. Of course it was. A tiny warding knot, the kind she must tie a hundred times without thinking, the way a scribe doodles letters.

He thought: *she learned this from someone, and that someone learned it from someone, and the chain goes back and back, to before the Edicts, to before the Sundering, to a covenant that the histories call broken and the archives call sealed.*

He thought: *the grey man walked past a hundred stalls and stopped at hers.*

He thought: *thorn-heir,* which was what a second witness — a fruit-seller who had seen nothing useful and everything expensive — claimed to have heard the dying man whisper. The Spire's transcriptionists had dismissed it as delirium. Wren had not. Wren had gone to the sealed archive at four in the morning and read, by the light of one guarded lamp, a fragment of a document older than the palace, and the fragment had used the words *thorn-heir* exactly once, in a context that made his blood run cold:

*When the seven stones fail, a heir of the thorn shall rise to hold the door — or the door shall fall, and what waits beyond it shall not knock.*

Below that, in a different hand, a later archivist had added a note in ink gone brown: *The king knows. The king has always known. Ask why the poisonings began the year the stones were surveyed.*

*Ask why the poisonings began.*

Wren stood in the empty market lane with the red thread in his fist, and the morning wind came off the river carrying the smell of fish and tar and coming rain, and he felt the thing he had felt only twice before in his career: the ground shifting under his feet. Once had been the day he'd realized his masters would burn a whole quarter of the city to catch five smugglers, and had done it. The second had been the day he'd understood that the girl with the tasting-tongue was the best thing he had ever given the crown, and the crown did not deserve her.

Loyalty, he had always believed, was not a feeling. It was a discipline. You did your duty whether your heart agreed or not, because the alternative was chaos, and chaos ate the poor first. For thirty years he had practiced that discipline the way monks practice prayer.

But a discipline requires something to be loyal *to*, and the crown of Vellamere had become, in his long service, a rotting thing wearing a crown-shaped hat. A king who was being poisoned — Wren was certain of it now; the residue the tasting reports described matched the frost-fern pattern too closely for coincidence — and who responded to his poisoning by ordering his spymaster to hunt a kitchen girl. A court that severed realms and burned catalogues. An order marked *unspoiled* that Wren would bet his life meant *alive until questioned*, and questioning, in the Spire, was a thing one rarely survived with one's mind intact.

And somewhere ahead of his hunters, Juniper Thackeray was running with a map to seven stones, and every instinct Wren possessed — the instincts that had made him spymaster, that had never once been wrong about the shape of a conspiracy — told him that the map was real, the stones were real, and the door they held shut was opening whether the crown liked it or not.

He put the red thread in his pocket, next to the chancellor's order, and went back up to the Spire.

* * *

In his office, the salt-packed hand waited on his desk, and beside it lay the things he had gathered in one sleepless day: the sealed fragment, copied out in his own careful hand; the tasting reports from the palace, three months of them, showing trace-residues no physician could name; and a single sheet of new paper, blank, on which he had begun — twice — to write his report to the chancellor.

Both attempts lay crumpled in the wastebasket.

The truth was simple enough to write: *The woman Juniper Thackeray possesses the item. My agents are closing. Expect her within two days.* It was even mostly true. His agents *were* closing; he had made sure of that, spreading them thin along the obvious roads while keeping the sewer mouths and the river gates — the routes a clever runner would choose — understaffed, underwatched, a negligence so carefully constructed it would pass any audit.

He was buying her time. He knew he was buying her time. And he knew, with equal clarity, that he could not buy much of it, because the chancellor was not a fool, and because there were other hunters in the field besides his own — quiet men in plain coats who did not report to the Spire, who answered to someone behind the chancellor, and who had already asked him, once, with terrible politeness, why the sewer gates had been left unwatched.

That question had been two days ago. He did not have many days left.

Wren sat in the lamplight with the frost-hand in its box of salt, and he took out the red thread, and he set it on the desk beside the copied fragment, and he looked at the three objects together — the warding knot, the prophecy, the poisoned king's silent testimony — and he admitted, at last, in the privacy of his own skull, what he had been refusing to admit since midnight:

He was not going to bring her in.

He might delay. He might misdirect. He might spend every favour and every dirty trick of thirty years' service buying her road out of the city, coin by coin, lie by lie. But the moment his hunters put hands on Juniper Thackeray, he would have to choose in front of witnesses, and he had just understood — sitting in the dark with a dead man's frozen hand — which way he would choose.

The lamplight guttered. Somewhere below, the Spire's bells rang the change of watch, and Wren Corliss, spymaster of Vellamere, loyal servant of a rotting crown, took a fresh sheet of paper and wrote his report:

*The woman Thackeray has fled the city. Pursuit continues. The item was not recovered.*

It was the first lie he had ever written to his masters that was also, he suspected, the truest report he had ever filed.

He signed it, sealed it, and set it aside — and then, because he was still Wren Corliss, because thirty years of discipline do not dissolve in a night, he drew a second sheet toward him and began, in the small precise script no one else could read, a different kind of document. A private ledger. The ledger he had kept in his head for thirty years, now committed to paper for the first time, because a man who is about to betray his crown should know exactly what he is betraying it *for*.

At the top of the page he wrote: *Small mercies, no harm in them.*

And beneath it, the names began.

He was three names deep when the knock came at his door — soft, particular, the knock of the quiet men in plain coats — and Wren closed the ledger with one hand and opened the door with the other, his face already arranged into the mask they expected.

"Master Corliss." The man in the doorway was wet with river fog and carried himself like a drawn crossbow. "A word. About the sewer gates."

"Of course," Wren said pleasantly. "Come in. Mind the salt."

And far away, past the city walls, in the dark throat of the world beneath Vellamere, a girl with a tasting-tongue and a dead man's map was already moving toward the first stone — and something on the other side of it had begun, very quietly, to wake.
