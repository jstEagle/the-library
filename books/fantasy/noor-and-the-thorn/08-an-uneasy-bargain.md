## 8. An Uneasy Bargain

The fae knight did not kill her.

Juniper kept waiting for it — for the flash of that thorn-forged blade, the cold slide of it between her ribs, the way the dying stranger had gone. She stood with her back against the first covenant-stone, its light still humming through her bones like a struck bell, and watched Noor sheathe his sword with the deliberate slowness of a man setting down something he would very much like to pick up again.

"You're supposed to be dead," he said. His voice was strange to her ears — low, but with a resonance underneath it, as though he spoke through a hollow reed, or a winter forest. "Or bound. Those were my choices."

"Those are terrible choices," Juniper managed. Her legs had not entirely stopped shaking since the stone had flared beneath her palm and spoken her name in a voice like roots splitting stone. *Thorn-heir.* The words were still written somewhere behind her eyes. "I decline both."

Something moved across his face — not quite a smile, but the ghost of where one might live on a mortal face. He was beautiful in the way that made her skin prickle: too symmetrical, too still, his eyes the deep green of water over moss, his hair dark as wet bark. Bramble-crested armor of blackened silver clung to him like grown things rather than worn ones. Thorns curled at his collarbone, small and living, and Juniper thought absurdly of the charm-vines she sold in the night market, three coppers apiece, and how far out of her depth she suddenly was.

"The Court does not accept declinations," he said.

"Then the Court can send someone who speaks less like a tax collector."

That earned her a longer look. His head tilted, birdlike, and for a moment she saw him truly *consider* her — weigh her the way she weighed a suspect dish on her tongue, cataloguing every note.

"You touched the stone and it named you," he said. "It has not done that in three hundred years. The last heir it named—" He stopped. The thorns at his collar shifted, almost imperceptibly, like a man's shoulders tightening. "The last heir died badly."

"They usually do, in my experience."

Behind them, the covenant-stone pulsed once more, slow as a heartbeat, then dimmed to a dull ember-glow. The clearing around it — a hollow in the hills south of Vellamere, ringed by hawthorn so old and twisted it looked more animal than tree — seemed to exhale. Juniper felt the silence settle back into place, thick and watchful.

Tam was somewhere back in the city, probably pacing a hole in the tenement floor. Wren Corliss's hunters were certainly already moving; she'd heard the hounds baying at the sewer grate before dawn, and Tam had shoved her up the ladder with shaking hands and a whispered *don't you dare die before you come back*. And now she stood alone in an enchanted hollow with a fae knight whose orders, by his own admission, had been to kill her.

"So," she said, when the silence had stretched long enough to ache. "Verify me. That's what you said."

Noor's hand rested on his sword hilt still — not gripping, just resting, the way a man rests his hand on a fence post. "Your claim must be tested against the second stone. Then the third. The Court will not move on a single flare of light. Stones have flared falsely before." A pause. "Once."

"And if I fail?"

"Then I do what I was sent to do." He said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. It was weather to him. Rain falling. A task like any other.

Juniper looked at the sword. Looked at his face. Made the calculation she'd been making her whole life — the taster's calculation, the one that weighed what was certain against what was merely survivable. Alone, she was a girl with a bloodstained map and no food, no shelter, no knowledge of the road, hunted by the best spymaster in Vellamere. With him, she was a girl with all those things plus a blade at her back that might turn around.

"With him," she decided aloud, "at least I'll see it coming."

"I beg your pardon?"

"Nothing." She pushed off from the stone and brushed the dirt from her skirts with as much dignity as she could salvage. "We have a bargain, knight. You escort me to the second stone. You don't kill me unless your stones tell you to. In exchange, I don't run, and I don't poison you."

His eyebrows rose a fraction. "You could poison me?"

"I'm a royal food-taster," Juniper said. "I could poison you eleven ways before you finished drawing that sword, and you'd never taste any of them. Consider this my demonstration of good faith: I haven't."

For the first time, Noor laughed. It was a short, startled sound, quickly mastered, as if laughter were a coin he wasn't permitted to spend — but it escaped him nonetheless, and Juniper found herself absurdly pleased at having stolen it.

"A bargain, then," he said. "Heir."

"Don't call me that."

"What should I call you?"

"Juniper. If we're going to travel together, you may as well use the name my mother gave me instead of the one a rock did."

Something flickered across his face at *mother* — there and gone, quick as a fish under ice. "Juniper, then." He said it carefully, tasting it, the way she tasted wine. "Walk beside me. Not ahead. The hollow watches strangers, and it is kinder to those it can see clearly."

* * *

They walked south through country she had only known from maps and smugglers' gossip. The land changed as they went — the hard, plowed fields of the Vellamere outskirts giving way to rougher pasture, then to moorland where the heather grew in colors she had no names for, purple shading into a blue so deep it looked bruised. The boundary between realms ran somewhere out here, thin as old cloth, and Juniper could feel it now that the stone had woken something in her blood: a faint wrongness in the air, like a note held just below hearing.

Noor did not speak much. When he did, it was in short, precise sentences, each word chosen like a soldier choosing ground. But he watched everything. Twice he stopped her with a raised hand and stood listening to nothing she could hear, and twice, when they resumed walking, their path had bent away from something unseen.

"What's out there?" she asked the second time.

"Hunters." He didn't mean Wren's men. Or maybe he did. His face told her nothing.

"Yours or mine?"

"For now, mine are worse." He glanced at her sidelong. "The Queen's hounds run ahead of her knights. They smell oath-breaking. They will smell you soon — the stone marked you, and marked things carry their scent across borders."

"Wonderful. So I'm being hunted by two realms."

"Three," Noor said. "The Court counts the king's men as a third pack, since they serve the same end whether they know it or not. Your spymaster's hunters are thorough. I've watched them work." Something in his tone made her stop walking.

"You've been watching Wren Corliss's hunters? Since when?"

"Since the grey-cloaked man crossed the boundary bleeding frost." Noor turned to face her fully, and the afternoon light came through the hawthorn behind him and turned his eyes to green glass. "He was one of ours, once. Long ago. A covenant-bearer who fled his stone and hid in your city, wearing mortality like a coat. He carried the map because someone had to, after the sixth bearer fell. He was making for you, Juniper. Do you understand? Out of everyone in Vellamere, the dying magic of that map went looking for *you*."

She hadn't known that. Hadn't let herself know it. The stranger's weight against her stall, the bloodstained parchment, the whisper — *Thorn-heir* — she had wrapped all of it in urgency and fear and refused to ask the question underneath: *why me?*

"He chose you," Noor said quietly. "Stones choose heirs, but bearers choose successors. He watched you sell your little charms in the night market for three years. He watched you survive the court. Whatever he saw in you, he trusted it with the last of his life."

Juniper's throat was tight. She thought of the charm-sellers' network, of Tam, of old Marta who bought luck-knots for her grandsons, of the whole quiet web of small magics she'd spun beneath the ban because people needed protecting and no one else would do it. Had the grey man seen that web? Had he understood what it meant?

"He never spoke to me," she said. "Not once."

"He wouldn't. Bearers of the Thorn learn early that speaking to what you love brings the frost down on it." Noor's jaw tightened. "It is why I was given my orders alone."

They camped that night in the lee of a standing stone half-swallowed by gorse. Noor built no fire — "light draws the hounds" — but he showed her how to find warmth in the boundary-thin air, breathing slow, drawing heat up from the ground itself, a trick he called *borrowing*. It wasn't magic, exactly, or not the kind that got people burned in Vellamere. It was older and politer than that. Asking rather than taking.

Juniper sat with her knees drawn up, the map spread across her lap even though the darkness made it useless, tracing the route to the second stone by memory. A drowned chapel, the grey man's notes said, in the fens beyond the Marches. Two days' walk, if the fens allowed crossing.

"Tell me about the price," she said, not looking up.

Noor was silent long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. When he did, his voice had gone careful, the way voices go around open graves.

"No."

"You said the Court judges heirs. You said there's a price. I'm walking toward it blind, knight, and I don't walk well blind. Ask anyone I've ever served dinner to."

"The Court will tell you at the seventh stone. Not before. Those are the terms of my charge."

"Then tell me what you're permitted to tell me. You're good at precision. Try it."

Another silence. Then, low: "The covenant was forged in blood freely given. One life, bound into seven stones, holding the door between realms shut — or open, as the bearers willed. The door has been held shut for three hundred years by a dead woman's sacrifice, and the binding is failing, and when it fails completely, either a new life goes into the stones or both realms bleed into each other until there is nothing left of either." He paused. "That is what I am permitted to say."

Juniper stared at him across the dark. "One life. Freely given."

"Yes."

"And you were sent to make sure I give mine."

"I was sent to kill you if you refuse," Noor said, "and to see you reach the stones if you don't. Both outcomes serve the covenant. Only one serves you." His green eyes caught the faint starlight. "I am aware of the arithmetic, Juniper. I have been aware of it since the moment the stone named you and I smelled the old magic on your skin and could not—"

He stopped himself. Closed his mouth. The thorns at his collar curled tight, like fists.

"Could not what?" she pressed.

"It is not relevant to the bargain."

"It's relevant to whether I trust you."

"Then don't trust me," he said simply. "Trust the bargain. Trust that I want the second stone reached more than I want you dead, and that I will keep you alive until it judges you. Everything past that—" He shook his head once, sharp, like a horse refusing a bit. "Everything past that, I have not yet decided. I will tell you when I have."

It was, she realized, the most honest thing anyone in power had ever said to her. Kings lied beautifully. Wren Corliss lied with kindness. This fae knight lied not at all and called it a flaw in himself.

"All right," she said. "Bargain stands."

* * *

They made the edge of the fens by the second noon, and that was where Juniper first heard the hounds.

Not the Queen's — these were mortal dogs, deep-chested and baying, and behind them came the thinner music of hunting horns. Noor went rigid as a drawn bow, his head snapping west toward the sound.

"Corliss's riders," he said. "Four days behind us as of yesterday. Someone rode ahead with fresh scent." His hand found her arm — the first time he had touched her, and even through her sleeve his grip was cool as riverstone. "How many days to the chapel?"

"One, if the fens are kind. Two if they aren't."

"The fens are never kind." He pulled her off the path, into a stand of black poplars whose leaves shivered without wind. "There's a causeway. Old, drowned in places, but a rider can't take it at speed and horses hate the fen-smell. We can gain half a day if we cross tonight."

"In the dark?"

"In the dark, with the drowned things watching." He almost smiled again — that ghost-smile, there and gone. "You said you could poison me eleven ways. Can you also walk on rotten stone over black water in the dark?"

"My mother taught me to swim in a millpond," Juniper said. "And I've survived three years of court dinners. I can do anything required of me while terrified."

This time the smile stayed a moment longer. "Then we are well matched," Noor said, and offered her his arm across the broken ground, and after a heartbeat's hesitation, she took it.

They went down into the fens as the light failed, two figures on a drowned causeway with hunters' horns fading behind them and the second stone waiting somewhere ahead in the dark water — and Juniper could not have said, even to herself, whether the quickness of her heart came from the hounds, or the dark, or the cool strength of the fae knight's arm beneath her hand.

Behind them, far to the north, a signal fire bloomed on a hilltop. Then another. Then a third, ringing the fens like a crown of embers.

Noor stopped. Watched. His voice, when it came, was very quiet.

"They're not driving us off the causeway," he said. "They're penning us in. Corliss knows about the chapel."

Juniper turned to look back the way they'd come, at the fires closing like a fist.

"How could he possibly know about the chapel?"

"Because," Noor said slowly, "someone told him. And there is only one person besides us who has read that map."

Tam. The thought landed like a stone dropped down a well — she heard it fall and fall and never heard it strike.
