## 9. The Poisoner's Trail

The frost did not melt.

Wren Corliss had kept the dead man's glove for six days now — cut from the grey-cloaked stranger's hand in the market square before the body was hauled away to the potter's field, before anyone thought to ask why a corpse in high summer should be rimed white as a winter branch. He kept it wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of his document chest, beneath false passports and coded correspondence and the small black book of names he was never supposed to have written down. And every morning he unwrapped it, and every morning the frost was still there, glittering, patient, *wrong*.

Frost melted. That was the first law of frost. It surrendered to warmth the way everything in Vellamere surrendered eventually — to coin, to fear, to the slow grinding weight of the crown. But this frost held its shape on wool and skin alike, a lace of white crystals that caught the lamplight and threw it back in colors no ice should know: violet, then green, then a bruised gold that made his teeth ache to look at.

He had seen magic before. Three decades in the spymaster's trade meant he had seen everything men could do to one another, and a fair amount of what they could do when they stopped being entirely men. He had burned charm-sellers' stalls and watched the embers curl into shapes that fire does not make. He had interrogated a woman once who wept pearls instead of tears, and he had never learned whether it was glamour or grief or some third thing with no name in any of the eleven languages he spoke.

But he had never seen cold that refused to die.

*It's not weather,* he wrote in his private ledger, the one locked behind his own cipher. *It's an argument. Something is arguing with the world, and the world is losing.*

The residue from the king's table told the same story in a different tongue. Juniper Thackeray — and yes, he knew whose hands had pocketed that sample; he had trained those hands, taught them the palmer's pass and the wrist-turn and the hundred small thefts of a servant's life — Juniper had brought him nothing, but she hadn't needed to. His own tasters' reports carried the shape of it: a bitterness like burnt sugar at the back of the throat, a numbness in the gums that outlasted the meal by hours, and in two of the undercooks who'd handled the royal platters, fingertips gone pale as candle-wax that never quite regained their color.

Someone was poisoning the king. Slowly. Elegantly. With something that did not exist in any apothecary's ledger in Vellamere.

Which meant it came from somewhere else.

* * *

Wren started where all trails in Vellamere started: with money.

The crown's alchemical accounts were a swamp, deliberately so — Master Aldous Vane, the King's Alchemist, kept his ledgers in a shorthand of his own devising, and the treasury clerks had long since given up pretending to audit them. But Wren had spent thirty years reading what powerful men tried to hide, and hiding left marks the way dragging a trunk leaves furrows in a carpet. Vane's requisitions had changed eighteen months ago. Before that: salts, quicksilver, the ordinary furniture of a court alchemist's vanity. After: quantities of silver nitrate that would have embarrassed a mint. Cold-iron filings sourced from three separate foundries so no single smith would know the total. And — this was the entry that made Wren sit very still in his chair until the candle guttered — *river-glass*, forty pounds of it, purchased through a broker in the Salt Quarter who dealt exclusively with smugglers out of the Thornmarch.

River-glass was fae material. Mortal sand, swallowed by the boundary between realms and excreted back changed — smooth, faintly warm, humming if you held it to your ear the way children hold seashells. Possession of it was a hanging offense. The ban on magic was absolute, and the ban on *materials* was more absolute still, because the crown understood — had always understood — that you could hang a hundred charm-sellers and the craft would sprout again like grass, but starve the craft of its substance and it died in a generation.

So why was the King's Alchemist buying it by the sack?

Wren went to see the broker himself, alone, without his usual shadows. There were questions he did not want his own people answering later, under someone else's questioning. The broker was a ferret-faced man named Quill who kept his shop behind a curtain of dried eels and who sweated the moment Wren's boot crossed his threshold.

"Spymaster," Quill said, in the voice of a man greeting his own funeral.

"Relax. I'm not here for you." Wren turned a chair around and sat astride it, which he had learned young made people talk; there was something about a man who sat backwards that suggested he intended to stay a while. "Forty pounds of river-glass, moved through you in the last year. I want the chain."

"I never ask my buyers—"

"You never ask because I've paid you never to ask. I'm unpaying you." He set a coin on the counter, then another, then let his hand rest flat over the little stack without pushing it forward. "Chain, Quill. Names and doors."

Quill's eyes went to the coins, to the hand, to the window, calculating exits the way his kind always did, and finding none. "Vane's man collects monthly," he said at last. "But the orders come written. In this." He produced a scrap of paper from beneath the eel-curtain, folded into a square small enough to swallow.

Wren unfolded it. The handwriting was precise, clerkish, and wrong in a way he couldn't immediately name — until he could. The letters leaned *backward*. Not mirror-writing exactly, but a script that seemed to have been learned by someone watching a mortal hand write from the far side of a pane of glass.

Cold walked up Wren's spine and settled between his shoulder blades like a patient bird.

"And the glass goes where?"

"Vane's tower. All of it. Every sack."

Every sack. Forty pounds of a banned substance, delivered openly to the heart of the palace, while Wren's own agents hanged fishwives in the Marsh Market for selling love-charms woven from horsehair and spit. The hypocrisy was so vast it took him a moment to see it as what it was: not hypocrisy at all, but architecture. The ban wasn't failing to catch the court. The ban was a wall built around everyone *except* the court.

He had known that, of course. He had known it for years, the way you know a house is settling — a sound here, a crack there, never loud enough to make you leave. He had tolerated Juniper Thackeray's little charms for three years precisely because the law was a farce and punishing her for it would have been like drowning a child for stealing bread from a banquet. He had told himself his tolerance was mercy. Sitting in Quill's stinking shop, he admitted for the first time that it might have been complicity wearing mercy's coat.

He took the backward-written order with him. He left the coins. Some transactions should stay honest even when nothing else is.

* * *

That night he did something he had not done in twenty years. He went to the archives.

Not the working archive — the living one, the current files, the machinery of the service he ran. He went down. Past the sealed levels, past the flood-mark on the stair from the inundation nobody talked about, down to the Old Records, where the air tasted of stone and older stone, where the documents predated the current dynasty and were supposed to have been burned in the Purification but had been, through some clerk's cowardice or some archivist's stubbornness, merely buried.

He took a lamp and he took his time, and he found what the founders of Vellamere had wanted forgotten.

There had been a covenant. Not a treaty — treaties were things kings made and unmade over card tables. A covenant, sworn into seven stones set across both realms, binding the fae lands and the mortal lands into a shared breath: what rose in one rose in the other, what fell in one dragged the other down after it. The old texts were maddeningly oblique about the terms — the language circled the price of it the way a tongue circles a broken tooth — but they were clear about the *shape*. Two realms, one pact, and a line of keepers sworn to maintain it, heirs of something called the Thorn.

And then, ninety years ago, a king of Vellamere had broken it. Hadn't shattered it — shattering, the records implied, was impossible, or catastrophic, or both. He had *starved* it. Cut the covenant off from its keepers, scattered them, outlawed the craft that fed the stones, and let the whole great living thing go dark stone by dark stone. The ban on magic wasn't superstition. It was siege warfare, conducted across generations, against an enemy too large to see.

Wren stood in the cold dark holding a four-hundred-year-old folio and felt the floor of his profession drop away beneath him.

Because he had spent thirty years enforcing that siege. He had been, all this time, not a keeper of the peace but a picket line. Every stall he burned, every charm-seller he turned, every frightened girl he'd let flee into the night rather than take — every act of mercy he'd permitted himself had been a soldier's mercy, the kindness of a besieger toward the starving city he was slowly strangling.

And now the siege was entering its final phase. The frost-poison. The river-glass. Whatever Vane was building in his tower with silver and cold iron and the stuff of the boundary itself.

They weren't suppressing the covenant anymore. They were killing it. And killing a covenant that bound two realms together raised a question Wren did not want to ask, and asked anyway, alone, in the dark, his voice absurdly loud in the silence:

What happens to the wall between worlds when the mortar dies?

* * *

He went to the tower at dawn, when the mist off the river lay thick enough to drown a cat, because he had decided he was done learning things secondhand.

Getting in was almost insultingly easy. Vane's security was built against burglars and assassins, and Wren was neither tonight; he was a tired man with a legitimate seal and thirty years of practice looking like he belonged wherever he stood. The alchemist's apprentice — a boy of perhaps sixteen, half-asleep over a brazier — waved him up the spiral stair with the boneless deference of the very young confronted by authority.

The workroom at the top smelled of cold.

That was the only way to put it. Beneath the expected reek of sulfur and scorched metal and the sweet rot of alchemical failures, the room breathed winter. Frost furred the inside of the north windows in patterns too regular for nature — spirals, thorn-shapes, a geometry that hurt to follow with the eye. Along one wall stood racks of river-glass, each piece labeled in that backward-leaning script, and the glass was *singing*, faintly, a note below hearing that Wren felt in his fillings and the roots of his hair.

And in the center of the room, on a table of black iron, sat the machine.

He had no other word for it. Rings within rings, silver and cold iron, etched with script that crawled slowly when he wasn't looking directly at it. At its heart floated a single drop of liquid that was not falling and not frozen and not, Wren suspected, entirely present in the world. Around it, the air bent. Looking at the drop too long gave him the sensation of standing at the top of a staircase that wasn't there.

"It's beautiful, isn't it."

Vane stood in the doorway to his sleeping chamber, dressed, composed, a thin man with ink-stained fingers and the mild face of a schoolmaster. He did not seem surprised. That was the worst of it. He looked at Wren the way a craftsman looks at a delivery arriving slightly ahead of schedule.

"Master Corliss. I wondered when you'd come. Sit — no, don't touch anything. Please."

"What is it?" Wren asked, though he already knew, the way you know a diagnosis before the physician says it aloud.

"The Severance." Vane said it the way other men said *cathedral* or *coronation* — with the hushed pride of a life's work approaching completion. "Or rather, its engine. The covenant can't be broken from outside; believe me, ninety years of effort has established that thoroughly. It must be *unclaimed*. Its keepers must fail, its stones must starve, and above all—" he smiled, gently, terribly, "—the land itself must stop wanting it. Grief ends covenants faster than swords ever could. So we are teaching the land to grieve. The poison works upward, you understand. From the king's table into the king, from the king into the soil he's anointed over, from the soil into the roots, the rivers, the rain. A kingdom's health and its sovereign's are one flesh. We are making that flesh cold."

"The frost-wounds. The dead man in the market."

"A courier who developed scruples." Vane shrugged. "The boundary resists transport of certain materials. He resisted with it. Regrettable."

Wren's hand had drifted, without his permission, to the knife at his belt. Vane watched the drift with interest, without alarm.

"You're going to ask yourself what you do now," the alchemist said. "I'll save you the labor. You deliver me to the crown — except I am the crown, in this matter; the king knows, the chancellor knows, the ones who matter know. Or you destroy the engine, and the next one is already half-built beneath the chapel in the Lower Ward, and the next beneath that. Or—" and here his voice softened into something almost kind, "—you do what you have always done, Master Corliss. You serve. You have served beautifully for thirty years. Why stop now, at the end, when the prize is a realm forever free of the fae? No more bargains in the dark. No more children stolen behind the hedge. No more *magic*. Think of it. A clean world."

And Wren thought of it. That was the shameful truth he would carry afterward — that for three full breaths, he thought of it. A world with no more frost-wounded couriers, no more tenement witches, no more of the low constant dread that had hummed under Vellamere his entire life like a bad tooth. A clean world.

Then he thought of Juniper Thackeray at fourteen, showing him her first charm with her heart hammering, waiting to find out if the man she trusted would burn it. Then he thought of the dead courier's glove in his chest, frost that refused to surrender, an argument the world was losing. Then he thought of the Old Records, and the word *heirs*, plural, scattered — and of how, six days ago, a grey-cloaked stranger had pressed something into a servant girl's hands in a market square, and how his own agents had reported the map missing from the body, and how he had quietly buried that report rather than forward it.

He had already chosen, he realized. Somewhere between the archive and the broker's shop, between the glove and the singing glass, he had chosen. Everything since had just been the long ritual of admitting it.

"No," Wren said.

Vane tilted his head. "No?"

"It's not a clean world you're building. It's a dead one. I've read the old terms, Master Vane — the realms breathe together or they suffocate together. You're not severing a limb. You're cutting the throat of everything, and calling the bleeding freedom." He drew the knife after all, but not for Vane; he crossed to the workbench where the alchemist's confession lay — because of course there was a confession, of course a meticulous man like Vane had written it all down, the schedule of doses, the source of the poison, the names of the knowing, the engineering of the Severance, forty pages in that backward-leaning hand — and he picked it up.

Vane moved for the first time, fast, reaching for the bell-pull. Wren was faster, the way old men are sometimes faster than they have any right to be, and he had the boy-apprentice's poker in his other hand, and he swept the brazier's coals onto the desk in a single motion that thirty years of fieldwork had made smooth as a signature.

The confession caught. Backward script curled and blackened. Vane screamed — not for help, Wren noted, but in grief, lunging into the flames barehanded to save his masterpiece, and Wren stepped back and let him burn his clever fingers and did nothing at all, and found the doing-nothing the easiest thing he had done in years.

"You're finished," Vane gasped, cradling his hands, smoke rising off his sleeves. "You understand that? There is no place in either world for what you've just become."

"There never was a place for what I was," Wren said. "That was rather the point of me."

He left through the mist, down the spiral stair, past the sleeping apprentice, out into a dawn that was turning gold and ordinary over the rooftops of Vellamere. Behind him the tower windows glowed with firelight, and he did not look back to see whether the whole workroom burned or only the paper. It didn't matter. Engines could be rebuilt. What could not be rebuilt was a spymaster's obedience, and that was ash now, and he felt lighter than he had in thirty years and expected to feel terrified by nightfall.

By the time he reached his office, the first reports were already waiting: riders dispatched from the palace at first light, hunting warrants drawn in a hand he recognized, hunters moving toward the Thornmarch roads. Toward a drowned chapel. Toward a servant girl and whatever fae shadow walked beside her.

The king's purge had begun without him.

Wren Corliss sat down at his desk, took out a fresh sheet of paper, and began — for the first time in his long, gray, obedient life — to write down where he was going, and why, and signed it with his own true name.
