## 11. Hunted Across the Marches

Noor had walked the mortal marches before, in the old days, when the covenant still held and the crossing was a matter of courtesy rather than trespass. He remembered them green then — hedgerows humming with small agreements, every stream a signed treaty, the very soil thick with the memory of bargains kept. The land had *known* its obligations, and so had the people.

The marches he walked now were a wound wearing a landscape's clothing.

He felt it in his teeth first, that absence. Mortal lands had always been quieter than the Bramble Court, thinner in magic the way winter air is thin, but this was different. This was amputation. Three days out from the drowned chapel, following the river road east toward the high country where the third stone waited under a cairn of older stones, Noor could map the damage without the bloodstained parchment at all. Whole stretches of field simply *ended* — not fallow, not wild, but blank, as though someone had scraped the idea of growing off them. Hedgerows stood like rows of pulled teeth. And the birds — there were no birds. He had not heard a single song since they crossed the boundary-marker at the ford, and Juniper had noticed too; he'd seen her tilt her head at the silence the way she tilted it at suspicious sauces, cataloguing, tasting the air for what didn't belong.

She did not trust him yet. He could smell it on her, sharp as vinegar — and beneath it, something else he refused to name, because naming things in the mortal realm gave them weight, and weight was dangerous when you were already carrying an oath you meant to break.

*You were sent to kill her,* the Queen's voice reminded him, patient as rot. *Or bind her. Those are the only mercies the Court permits.*

He walked three paces behind her instead, watching the way she moved through dead country like a woman reading a menu of poisons.

"The third stone is close," she said, without turning. She had learned to feel them faster than he'd expected — or perhaps she'd always been able to, and the taster's tongue was only one of her instruments. "I can tell because my wrist itches."

"Thorn-marks itch before they burn," Noor said. "When the stone wakes fully, you'll wish it back to itching."

"Wonderful." She glanced over her shoulder, and the late light caught the grey in her eyes — storm-grey, hedge-grey, the color of magic that survived by being small and overlooked. "Anything else I should dread? Feel free to be thorough. It helps me plan."

"You should dread iron," he said, and had the satisfaction of seeing her smile die.

"Iron's a blacksmith's problem."

"Iron is *everyone's* problem, on this side of the veil. Cold iron doesn't just cut us. It unmakes the holding-together. A fae knight gutted by an iron blade doesn't bleed out, Juniper. He comes apart. Slowly. From the edges inward." He flexed his right hand, feeling the old ache where centuries ago a ploughman's scythe had grazed him — a scar from a gentler age, when such wounds healed clean. "Your king has been forging. We've counted the smoke from his foundries all season."

She was quiet for a dozen steps. Then: "How many hunters?"

"That," Noor said, "is the correct question, and I hate that you asked it first." He lifted his face to the wind coming down off the high country, and let his senses open the way he hadn't dared since the chapel — wide, reckless, drinking the air. Wind carried everything if you asked it politely enough: woodsmoke, wet wool, horse sweat, and beneath it all, faint but unmistakable, the sour tang of quenching-oil and the flat dead smell of iron freshly ground.

"Horses," he said. "Six, maybe eight. Behind us and to the north, moving to cut the road ahead. They've been tracking us since the chapel."

Juniper stopped walking.

"How long have you known?"

"Since dawn."

"And you were going to tell me *when*, exactly?"

"When knowing would help rather than frighten you."

She turned fully then, and her anger had a heat to it that no mortal ought to carry so casually, and Noor thought — not for the first time, and with increasing inconvenience — that the Court had misjudged this woman entirely. "Here is what your chivalry gets us," she said. "A shorter head start. Next time you decide what I can bear to know, remember that I spent three years swallowing poison for a living. I can bear quite a lot."

"I will remember," Noor said, and meant it, which frightened him more than the horses did.

* * *

They left the road at dusk, cutting up through a combe of gorse and bracken toward the ridge, because Noor knew — as the hunters likely did not — that the third stone lay not beyond the hills but *in* them, sunk in a hollow where a shepherd had once left offerings and now left nothing at all. If they could wake it before the riders closed the gap, the stone would shelter them; covenant-stones remembered their heirs, and even half-lit, one would blur their scent and trail.

But the riders were better than he'd estimated.

The ambush came at the moment the light failed — which was itself a message, because mortal soldiers feared the dark, and these did not. They came over the ridge crest in a wedge, torches unlit, and the first thing Noor knew of them was the smell: oil, grindstone, and iron, iron, iron, a stench like a nail driven through the world's tongue.

"Down—" he began, and the world went white.

Not fire-white. *Cold*-white. A net of fine-linked chain sailed out of the dark and settled over him like a spider's shawl, and every link sang against his skin with a sound only he could hear — a sound like names being erased. He went to his knees. The bracken froze around him in a perfect circle, silvered with frost that bloomed outward from where he knelt, and somewhere above him a voice said, conversationally:

"The knight first. The girl after. Captain wants the heir breathing."

Noor knew that voice. Not the man — the *training*. Wren Corliss's school of hunters spoke exactly so, unhurried, courteous, treating murder as correspondence. He had heard men trained this way once, long ago, before the courts closed against each other, and the recognition chilled him worse than the net.

Through the frost-silvered blur he saw Juniper. She had dropped flat as he'd commanded — some instinct of hers obeying before her pride could argue — and now she lay in the gorse shadow with her eyes fixed on him, and he watched her do the arithmetic he dreaded: one knight, netted, kneeling; six riders dismounting; herself, unarmed except for a satchel of charms worth copper apiece and, apparently, far more than that.

A hunter approached him with a drawn blade, and Noor saw the metal catch the starlight and knew it for what it was — forged cold, quenched in something that was not water, worked by men who had been told precisely what they were hunting. The blade came up. Noor, whose body had forgotten how to be afraid across four hundred years of service, discovered fear again all at once, and it tasted of Juniper's name unsaid.

Then the night filled with light.

Not torchlight. Charm-light — a handful of Juniper's cheap market trinkets, the ones she sold for coppers to servant girls who wanted sweet dreams, cracked open and hurled into the gorse, where they detonated into blooms of foxfire-bright glare that seared every eye on the ridge. Hunters cried out, reeling, and in the strobing after-images Noor felt hands on the net — quick, clever, taster's hands, working the links apart with a hairpin and a curse.

"Get *up*," Juniper snarled, hauling, and he got up.

What followed, Noor would remember afterward in fragments, the way mortals remember storms. Running. Gorse raking his arms. A crossbow bolt that passed close enough to part his hair, and Juniper's hand shoving his shoulder so they both stumbled sideways into the hollow — and the blade.

It found him as they fell together down the slope into the stone-hollow, a hunter's desperate lunge from above, and the cold iron slid along his ribs like a pen writing a sentence he could not read. There was no pain at first. That was the mercy and the horror of it. Iron wounds in mortal lands announced themselves slowly, politely, the way frost announces itself on a window — and by the time you see the pattern, the glass is already ruined.

He heard Juniper scream his name — his true name, the one he'd given her only two nights past, and hearing it in a mortal mouth made it ring like a struck bell — and then the hollow lit from within.

The third covenant-stone woke.

It did not blaze. It *answered*. The old shepherd's hollow filled with a low amber glow rising out of the ground itself, and the hunters on the ridge-top stopped as one, because whatever they had been told about the stones, they had not been told *this*: light with a heartbeat in it, light that looked back. A crossbow bolt loosed in panic shattered midair into moths. The riders fled — Noor heard the hoofbeats scatter northward, toward report, toward Wren Corliss, toward consequences — and then the hollow was quiet, and Juniper was kneeling over him with her hands pressed to his side, and her hands were red.

"No," she was saying. "No, no — stay — look at me—"

He looked at her. It seemed important to say so.

"It doesn't hurt," he told her, truthfully. "That's how you'll know it's bad."

* * *

The wound came apart the way he had promised her it would: from the edges inward.

By full dark he lay against the stone itself, which hummed against his spine like a sleeping animal, and watched the flesh around the iron-cut begin to forget its own shape. Frost crept along the cut in delicate fern-patterns, and where the frost passed, the skin went smooth and pale and wrong, like wax. His left hand had begun, very quietly, to lose its definition — fingers blurring at the tips, as though a painter were losing interest.

Juniper worked by charm-light, tearing strips from her shift, packing the wound with moss she crushed between her palms, doing everything mortal medicine knew and a few things it only suspected. None of it mattered. He could see her realize it, watch the understanding arrive in her face like a slow tide, and he wished — absurdly, selfishly — that she would stop looking so much like grief and start looking like fury, because fury kept people alive.

"There has to be something," she said. Her voice was steady. Her hands were not. "In the satchel. I make charms, Noor. Small ones, stupid ones — luck-charms, fever-charms, charms so a milk-cow doesn't go dry. Nothing like *this*." She laughed, one broken note. "I sell them for coppers. Coppers."

"Then sell me one," Noor said.

She stared at him.

"You are a maker," he said, and each word cost breath he did not have to spare, and he spent it anyway. "Small magic is still magic. The covenant does not measure gifts by their size — it never did. That was the courts' vanity, thinking power must be vast to be true. A charm that keeps a cow's milk sweet is a charm that says: *let what is whole remain whole.* So make it say that about me. Make it badly. Make it cheap. Make it the smallest spell ever sworn over a dying knight — and mean it."

Her eyes were wet and furious and, underneath, catching the amber light of the stone, beginning to burn.

"I don't know the words for fae wounds."

"Then use yours. Taster's words. You know a hundred ways to name a poison, to call a thing by its true nature. Call the iron what it is. Unmake its sentence."

For a long moment she did not move. Then she emptied the satchel onto the stone between them — a scatter of knotted threads, wax-sealed buttons, dried rowan berries, scraps of sigil-paper worth perhaps three silvers total, the entire inventory of three years of secret defiance — and chose, of all things, a plain brown twist of thread she'd knotted for a scullery maid who suffered nightmares.

"This one's for bad dreams," she said, half-laughing, half-crying. "It's supposed to turn nightmares into nothing. Into *forgetting*." She looked up at him, and the storm-grey of her eyes had gone bright as lightning about to strike. "Iron is a nightmare, isn't it? To you. So we make the wound forget it was ever made."

She set the thread-knot over the frost-ferns spreading along his ribs, pressed both hands flat, and spoke.

She spoke in no language Noor knew — kitchen-gutter Vellamere, the argot of servants and sellers, words about broths and fevers and the price of bread — and it was the most beautiful incantation he had heard in four centuries. Because it was hers. Because it was *meant*. The knot warmed. The warmth spread. And the frost — the beautiful, ruinous frost — hesitated, and then, link by invisible link, began to come undone, forgetting itself backward along the cut until the wound was merely a wound, bleeding and ugly and *mortal-healable*, and his left hand sharpened back into fingers, five of them, trembling.

Noor lay against the humming stone and breathed, and the breath came easy, and he understood that something had changed between them that could not be unwound any more than her knot could.

"You saved me with a charm worth a copper," he said.

"Two coppers." She slumped beside him, shaking now that it was done, and he put his newly-solid arm around her shoulders because it seemed the correct use of it. "And don't you dare tell the Court a hedge-witch's nightmare-charm beat their cold iron. Their pride couldn't survive it."

"The Court will never hear it from me." He paused. "Though I confess I intend to think about it constantly."

She laughed — exhausted, ragged, real — and leaned against him, and above the hollow the stars came out over a land that had forgotten how to sing, and the stone beneath them pulsed slow and amber, waiting to be woken properly.

It was Juniper who went still first, her head lifting from his shoulder like a deer scenting weather.

"Noor," she said quietly. "The stone's awake enough to show things, isn't it? Like the second one did?"

"It can be asked questions, yes. At a price."

"Then ask it this." She turned to face him, and in the amber light her expression had gone grim and certain. "Ask it where Wren Corliss is right now. Because those riders weren't running away, Noor. They were running *to report* — and I want to know what our old teacher intends to do when he learns his hunters found us alive."

Noor laid his palm against the warm stone, and it answered before he finished asking — a flare of images behind his eyes: a road, a rider, a sealed letter, and Wren Corliss himself standing at a crossroads inn with the letter unopened in his hand, looking north toward Vellamere, then west toward them, his face the face of a man standing on a cracking floor, choosing which way to fall.

The image faded. Noor exhaled slowly.

"He's coming," he said. "Alone. And Juniper — he's bringing something with him. Something he stole from the palace."

Her eyes narrowed. "What kind of something?"

"The kind," Noor said, "that burns."
