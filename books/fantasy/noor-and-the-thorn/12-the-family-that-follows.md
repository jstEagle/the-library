## 12. The Family That Follows

Tam had always known the tenement would not hold them forever. Nothing in Vellamere held anything forever — not the river's smell, not the price of bread, not the lives of people who sold charms in doorways and tasted poison for coin. But he had hoped, in the stubborn way he hoped about everything, that when it finally came apart it would come apart loudly, with soldiers at the door and fire in the stairwell, so that at least they'd know what killed them.

It came apart quietly instead.

He woke on the third morning after Juniper fled to feel the silence of the house, that particular hush that meant someone was listening from outside. He lay still in his cot beneath the eaves and counted the sounds: Bess turning over below, old Marta's cough, the creak of the water barrel as it settled. And underneath all of it, faint as a hair drawn across a drumhead, the sound of a man breathing on the roof across the alley.

Tam had been a pickpocket before he was a cook, and a cook before he was whatever he was now, and neither trade survived long without learning what listening meant. He did not get up. He did not look out the window. He lay in the grey light and thought: *Wren Corliss knows where we live.*

Of course he did. Wren Corliss had probably always known. The spymaster had tolerated Juniper's little market for years the way a farmer tolerates mice in the walls — aware of them, amused by them, waiting for a reason to set the traps. Now there was a reason. There was a dead man in a grey cloak and a map made of blood, and somewhere in the palace Wren Corliss was reading reports with Juniper Thackeray's name in them, and Tam could almost see his face doing that thing it did, that terrible patient thing, like a man watching weather come.

He got up quietly and went down to the kitchen, because kitchens were where Tam did his thinking, and by the time Bess shuffled down with her eyes half-shut he had flour on his hands and a plan in his mouth.

"We're leaving," he said.

Bess stopped on the bottom stair. She was sixty if she was a day, a washerwoman with hands like tree roots, and she looked at him the way she looked at underdone bread. "Leaving where?"

"Away. Today. Now, actually."

"Juniper said—"

"Juniper said stay put and don't be stupid." Tam kneaded the dough harder than it needed. "And we've been staying put and being stupid for three days, and there's a man on the roof across the alley who breathes like a bellows, and I'll wager my good knife there's another one watching the well. She told us to stay so we'd be safe. Staying is going to get us taken."

Marta came down next, then the twins — Pip and Sable, sixteen and feral, who had been Juniper's runners since they were small enough to fit through cellar grates. Tam laid it out for them the way he laid out a recipe: simple steps, no flourishes. Pack light. Take the charm-stock, take the copper, leave everything that couldn't run. Out through the tanner's yard before full light, along the drainage cut south, and then — here his plan went soft at the edges — away.

"Away where?" Pip asked.

"Wherever Juniper is."

"You don't know where she is."

"I know where she's going," Tam said. "She's following a dead man's map, and dead men's maps go to old places. Stones. Chapels. Ruins. Things that were important once and got forgotten." He wiped his hands. "I've been listening to her talk in her sleep for three years. Half her nightmares are about a chapel."

That wasn't entirely true. Most of her nightmares were about the tasting table, about the king's thin face, about a spoon she couldn't put down. But some of them — the ones where she cried out words instead of just sounds — were about water over stone, about bells drowned in green light, and Tam had filed those away the way he filed everything, against a day he'd need them.

They left through the tanner's yard at first light, Bess carrying a bundle nearly her own size, Marta walking with her stick and her chin up like a duchess, the twins ranging ahead like dogs off a lead. Tam took the rear, because rears were where trouble came from, and trouble came within the hour.

The man from the roof had not been alone after all.

* * *

They lost the first watcher at the tannery by doubling through the lime-pits, which stank enough to make even Sable's eyes stream, and lost the second at the south drain by sending Pip up a chimney to drop loose slates behind them. But agents of the crown were not street boys, and by midday there were three of them on their trail, patient as ticks, keeping just far enough back to seem like an accident of the road.

"They're herding us," Marta said, resting on a milestone. Her voice was dry as August. "Corliss doesn't chase. He steers."

"Then we stop being steered." Tam looked at the country around them — hedgerows, sheep-cropped hills, a road running white toward the southern marches. Somewhere out there was a drowned chapel and a girl who thought she was protecting them by leaving them behind. "Sable. You still remember the drover's paths?"

"Every one."

"Get us off this road. Sheep tracks, field edges, anything a horse can't follow easy. We're going to be very boring people walking in circles until dark, and then we're going to be very fast people walking straight."

It worked, mostly. It worked until dusk, when the three agents grew tired of patience and came on at a jog through a barley field, and Tam learned that you cannot outrun trained men while carrying an old woman's bundle, no matter how many years you spent running from butcher's boys in Copperlane.

What saved them was Bess.

Bess, who washed the shirts of half the palace guard and knew every man of them by his collar size, simply turned around on the track, planted her feet, and said in a voice that had quelled drunk lords' sons for forty years: "*Oswin Fenwick.* I know your mother. I did her linens when you were in swaddling bands, and I will tell her exactly what you're doing in this field if you take one more step."

And Oswin Fenwick, royal agent, iron-tipped cudgel in hand, stopped as though he'd hit glass.

It should have ended there. It didn't, because the second agent wasn't from the city and didn't know Bess, and he swung at Tam, and Tam — who had never been brave, only persistent — caught the cudgel on his forearm and heard something crack and went down into the barley with the taste of pennies in his mouth. Then there was shouting, and Sable came out of the hedgerow like a thrown knife, and somewhere in the confusion of flailing bodies and crushed grain and Marta beating a grown man about the head with her stick while reciting his crimes aloud, the agents decided that three fugitive servants were not worth a report full of embarrassment, and withdrew to lick their wounds and lie to their masters.

Tam sat in the ruined barley with his arm cradled against his chest and laughed until it hurt worse.

"We're coming," he said to the darkening south, to nobody, to Juniper. "You absolute fool. We're coming."

* * *

They found the chapel road by asking questions the way servants ask questions — invisibly, usefully, of ostlers and goose-girls and a friar who owed Marta a favor older than the twins. A girl with dark hair and a fae knight, yes, seen two days past, heading for the drowned valley. And strangers on the road behind them, armed men with iron, asking after the same pair with a warrant signed in the king's own hand.

Iron-forged weapons. Tam's stomach went cold when he heard that part repeated. He knew what iron meant to the fae; everyone who bought Juniper's charms knew, because she'd drilled it into them between jokes. Iron burned them. Iron kept their wounds open.

"They'll be hurt," he said that night, over a fire built small in a hollow. "Her knight. If the hunters catch them, he'll be hurt, and she'll do something stupid trying to save him, because that's what she does, she spends herself on people—"

"So do you," Bess said comfortably.

"I'm not spending myself. I'm investing." He flexed his splinted arm and winced. "There's a difference."

"There isn't," Marta said, "and thank all the saints for it."

They caught up on the fourth day, at the edge of the drowned valley, where the land fell away into mist and the air smelled of green water and old bells. Tam saw them from the ridge — two figures small on the winding path, one dark-haired and mortal and moving like someone carrying a weight no one else could see, and one tall figure beside her whose walk was wrong in a way Tam couldn't name, too smooth, too quiet, like a cat deciding to be a horse.

Pip whistled the signal — three notes, high-low-high, the same whistle they'd used in the market alleys for years — and watched the dark-haired figure stop dead, then turn, then start *running* up the path, and Tam felt something in his chest crack in an entirely different way than his arm had.

"You *idiots*," Juniper said when she reached them, breathless, furious, her hands grabbing at Bess, at Marta, at the twins, counting them like coins she was afraid had been stolen. "You were supposed to stay. You were supposed to be *safe*. There are hunters with iron, there's a spymaster who raised me, there's a whole kingdom looking for this map, and you walked out of the city with a washing basket and Marta's stick—"

"Yes," Tam said.

"—and you're *hurt*, you're bleeding through the splint, who splinted this, a child? This is the worst splint I have ever—"

"It's holding," Tam said. "Like us."

Juniper stopped. She looked at all of them, standing in the mist with their bundles and their bruises and Bess already eyeing Noor — the fae knight, the actual fae knight — with the expression of a woman assessing whether he ate enough. And Tam watched something go out of her shoulders, something she'd been carrying since the night the grey man died, and he understood that she had believed, truly believed, that leaving them behind meant saving them, and that they had just made a liar of her in the kindest possible way.

"Noor," she said faintly, "this is my family. They don't listen. Ever. To anyone."

Noor inclined his head with courtly gravity. "I begin to understand," he said, "why you are difficult."

"He means that nicely," Juniper said. "I think."

* * *

They camped that night in the lee of the valley wall, and it was there, over Bess's stew — which Noor ate with the fascinated caution of a scholar examining a new species — that Tam finally said the thing he'd carried up four days of road in his cracked ribs.

"Not here," Juniper had said first, when he'd touched her shoulder. "Tomorrow. We move at dawn."

But there was no tomorrow that wasn't made of today, and Tam had learned that from her, ironically, back when she'd taught him that secrets kept too long went rotten in the keeping. So he waited until the twins slept, until Bess's snores kept their steady rhythm, until Noor stood watch at the valley's edge a courteous distance off, a silhouette against the stars that did not quite belong to any sky Tam knew.

Then he sat down beside Juniper at the fire and said, "You lied to us."

She didn't pretend otherwise. That was one thing about her he'd always loved — she never wasted time pretending. "Yes."

"For three years you lied. Every charm you sold, every spell you slipped into Bess's tea when her chest was bad, every warding-knot you tied in Pip's coat — you let us think we were just poor folk muddling through, and all the while you were breaking the king's law nightly to keep us alive." He poked the fire. "And then a dying man hands you a map and a destiny, and you slip out at dawn without waking a soul, and you expect us to believe that's different?"

"It was supposed to be safer for you."

"That's the lie I mean." He turned to look at her. Firelight moved on her face, and she looked so tired, so worn down to the quick, that it cost him something to keep his voice level. "You decided what our lives were worth without asking us. Same as the king does. Same as Corliss does, steering us down roads with his little herd of agents. Everyone in this story keeps deciding things for other people, Junie. I thought you were better than that."

The silence stretched. Somewhere down in the drowned valley, water moved over stone with a sound like slow applause.

"I was trying to keep you," she said at last, and her voice cracked on the word *keep*, and Tam understood suddenly how much of her life had been about keeping — keeping charms, keeping copper, keeping a family fed and hidden and alive, keeping herself whole enough to do it again tomorrow. "Everything I've ever loved gets taken. My mother. My name. My—" She gestured vaguely at the map, at the stones, at the whole unfolding catastrophe of her inheritance. "So I keep things wrong. Too tight. I know. I *know*, Tam. But if you'd been in that tenement when Corliss's men kicked the door—"

"Then we'd have been taken together, or we'd have run together, or we'd have died together complaining about your cooking." He shrugged, and paid for it in pain. "That's what family *is*, Junie. Not the thing you protect. The thing you do it *with*."

Juniper looked at him for a long moment. Then she laughed — wet, unsteady, real — and leaned her head against his good shoulder, careful of the splint, and they sat there while the fire settled and the strange stars wheeled overhead.

"I'm sorry," she said.

"I know. Don't do it again."

"I might do it again."

"I know that too." He sighed. "We'll survive it. We always do."

Down at the valley's edge, Noor turned from his watch, and Tam saw the fae knight's gaze rest on their little circle of blankets and grey heads and snoring twins with an expression Tam couldn't read — something old and hungry and grieving, like a man looking at a hearth in a house he'd been exiled from. When Juniper caught him looking, he looked away too quickly, and Tam filed that away too, the way he filed everything, against a day he'd need it.

Because he'd need it soon. He could feel it, the way you felt weather coming. Tomorrow they'd go down into the drowned valley together, all of them, toward whatever the chapel wanted, and the world was going to keep asking its prices, and someone at this fire was going to pay.

Tam banked the coals, and touched the charm-knot at his wrist — Juniper's work, three years old, frayed now, still warm — and slept.

In the morning, the mist off the valley had shapes in it. And one of the shapes, far down among the drowned bells, was wearing the grey of the king's own guard, and waiting.
