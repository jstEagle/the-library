## 14. Flight From Two Courts

The Bramble Court did not chase them with horns.

That was the first thing Juniper learned about being an oath-breaker in the fae realm: the pursuit was quieter than anything mortals could manage, and therefore worse. No shouts, no hoofbeats. Only the forest itself changing its mind about them.

They had run through the Queen's garden while the thorns were still bowing to her name, and now, a mile out, the brambles had begun to lean. Not toward them — *after* them. Hedges that had stood for centuries peeled themselves off their moorings and came on with the slow patience of things that had never once needed to hurry. Behind them, the pale lanterns of the court guttered out one by one, as if the whole realm were closing its eye.

"Don't look back," Noor said. He ran ahead of her, armor gone, wearing only a grey linen shirt she'd last seen folded in his saddlebag, and he moved through the briars like a man reading a language she couldn't see — left here, duck there, always three steps before a path closed. "The hunters read intent. If you look back, you're telling them where your heart is."

"My heart," Juniper said between breaths, "is currently trying to exit my mouth."

"Then keep it moving."

The ground beneath them was wrong in ways she was only beginning to catalog. Moss that felt warm as skin. Roots that crossed the path like knuckles. And everywhere that faint green-gold light, sourceless, that made shadows fall in directions shadows shouldn't. Three days ago this realm had been a story. Two days ago it had been a courtroom. Now it was a mouth, and they were well past the teeth.

A sound reached them then — not behind, but *above*. A whirring, like a thousand wings deciding all at once to be interested.

Noor went still. That frightened her more than the brambles had. Noor did not go still.

"The Queen's swifts," he said. "Moth-winged. They mark you with dust so the hedges can find you in the dark." He tore a strip from his shirt without breaking stride and pressed it into her hands. "Tie your hair up. Cover what you can. And whatever lands on you—"

"I know. Don't let it."

"—don't let it," he agreed, and something flickered across his face that on anyone else she'd have called a smile. On Noor it was more like weather changing. "You learn quickly for someone who argued with a queen yesterday."

"I argue with everyone. It's how I know I'm still me."

*Yesterday.* It felt like a year. Yesterday she had stood in the Court of Thorns with sap-veined pillars breathing around her and told the Bramble Queen, to her face, that she would not die for a realm that had let hers rot. She remembered the silence afterward — the particular, terrible silence of a court realizing its script has been torn up. She remembered the Queen's face, beautiful and old as winter, rearranging itself around the word *oath-breaker* the way ice rearranges itself around a drowning thing.

And she remembered Noor stepping forward. Stepping forward when every knight in that hall expected him to draw.

He hadn't drawn. He had said, in a voice that carried to the farthest pillar: *"Then I am done being the covenant's knife."*

She thought about that now, running, because thinking kept the fear at a manageable size. The Queen had not screamed. The Queen had simply looked at him the way a jeweler looks at a stone that has shown a flaw, and said, softly, *then the road will collect you*, and the lanterns had begun going out.

The trees thickened. The light failed by degrees, gold to grey to a darkness with texture to it, and Juniper's lungs burned with air that tasted of loam and old honey. Somewhere behind them the hedges were coming, patient as arithmetic.

"Noor." She caught his sleeve. "The border. How far?"

"Half a night's run if the realm lets us. Less if it doesn't." He turned, and in the failing light his eyes caught what little glow remained — fae eyes, amber shot through with green, the eyes of a man who had walked out of a story and decided to stay. "There's a ford ahead. The river runs along the boundary for a stretch. Crossing water confuses the hedges — they think in straight lines, roots do."

"And the swifts?"

"The swifts don't think at all." He was already moving. "Run."

* * *

They found the river by sound before sight — a low rushing in the dark, and then the smell of it, cold and mineral and blessedly ordinary, the first ordinary thing Juniper had encountered since crossing over. The water ran black under a sky that held no stars she recognized. On the far bank, barely visible, stood a line of stones that might have been natural or might have been watching.

The swifts found them at the water's edge.

They came down out of the dark like snow falling upward — dozens of them, moth-winged and pale, each no bigger than her palm, and where their dust touched it clung and glowed faintly, marking her shoulder, her wrist, the back of Noor's neck. He swatted them away with a flat hand, precise, unhurried even now, but there were too many. The glow spread. Somewhere behind them, in the black throat of the forest, the hedges would have seen.

"In," Noor said. "Now."

The river hit her like a wall of knives. It wasn't deep — waist-high at the center — but it was *cold*, cold past meaning, cold like the frost-wounds on the dead man in the market, and for one lurching moment Juniper was back in Vellamere kneeling beside a stranger's body while guards converged and a bloodstained map burned against her ribs. Then Noor's arm was around her, hauling her upright, and the current was trying to take her knees, and none of that mattered because the far bank was ten feet away and the hedges were not patient anymore.

She heard them coming. That was the worst part. The sound of the hedge-hunters was not footsteps. It was *growing* — a vast creaking rush, wood stretching, thorn parting soil, the forest relocating itself with terrible enthusiasm. She scrambled up the far bank on hands and knees, mud under her nails, hair plastered to her face, and Noor pulled her the last few feet and turned to face the water.

The hedges stopped at the near shore.

They arrived all at once — a wall of blackthorn twenty feet high slamming down along the opposite bank like a wave frozen mid-break — and then simply stood, quivering, thorns longer than her forearm, straining against some boundary Juniper couldn't see. The river between them ran on, indifferent. The swifts circled above the thorn-wall, dust glowing, unable or unwilling to cross.

"They won't follow?" she gasped.

"Not onto mortal soil. Not yet." Noor's voice was strange. Flat. "The Queen cannot send her hunters across while the covenant stones still hold any light at all. Which is why, heir, we are going to spend the next three nights lighting them."

Juniper looked back once — allowed herself one look. The thorn-wall stood against the starless sky like the jaw of a trap, and set into it, high up, three shapes had formed themselves out of the branches: tall, crowned in twigs, faces like knots in ancient wood. They did not move. They did not need to. She felt their attention the way she'd once felt fever — a heat behind the eyes, a certainty of being measured.

One of them spoke. The voice came across the water without troubling itself with distance, dry as leaves.

*"Oath-breaker."*

It was not looking at her.

*"You cut nothing yet, Noor of the Bramble Guard. The thread still holds. Come home, and the Queen may let the girl keep her name. Stay, and you are nothing. Less than nothing. Unwritten."*

Juniper turned to Noor. He stood at the waterline, soaked to the bone, marked with swift-dust that glowed on his skin like scattered embers, and he was very quiet in the way she had learned meant a decision was being made somewhere behind his eyes.

"What does that mean?" she asked. "Unwritten. What does it actually—" and then she saw it, because the glow of the dust lit everything whether she wanted it lit or not: the thin line running from his chest out over the water, a filament of green-gold light, fine as spider silk, stretching back across the river into the dark. His oath-thread. The bond that had made him the covenant's knife, the Queen's instrument, the thing sent to kill her. It had been fraying since the courtroom. Now it hummed taut as a harp string, singing faintly in a register she felt in her teeth.

"Noor," she said carefully. "What are you thinking?"

"What I should have thought three hundred years ago." He raised one hand, and she watched his fingers close around the thread as though around a rope.

"You don't know what it'll do to you," she said. "Cutting it. You said yourself — the knights who break oath-threads—"

"I said the ones who break them *secretly* go mad. The thread fights a lie." His eyes stayed on the far shore, on the wooden faces, on the court that had owned him since before her grandmother's grandmother drew breath. "This isn't a lie. This is the truest cut I'll ever make."

The voice across the water said, softer, almost gentle, which was somehow infinitely worse: *"Noor. You were forged for us. There is nothing else in you."*

"There was always something else," Noor said. "You just never asked to see it."

He pulled.

The sound the thread made when it parted was not a snap. It was a note — one long, clear, grieving note, like a glass rimmed by a wet finger, rising until it passed beyond hearing. The light of it washed the riverbank white. Juniper threw her arm across her eyes, and through the glare she heard Noor make a single sound, short and involuntary, the sound of a man setting down a weight he had forgotten he was carrying, and then the light was gone and the night rushed back in and the thread's severed end drifted down between them like ash.

On the far bank, the three wooden faces slowly unraveled back into branches. The thorn-wall shivered, settled, and began — with the same terrible patience — to withdraw. Not defeated. *Repositioning.* The message was legible enough: *we will wait. We are very good at waiting.*

Noor swayed. Juniper caught him before she'd decided to, one arm around his back, and felt him shaking — not fear, she realized, but the rawness of it, the phantom limb of three centuries of duty suddenly gone. His chest, where the thread had anchored, bore a pale scar in the shape of a small branching tree.

"It will ache," he said. His voice was roughened, human-sounding, and she understood dimly that this was the first time in his long existence it had ever needed to be. "For years, perhaps. The torn place doesn't heal clean."

"Good," Juniper said.

He looked down at her, water dripping from his chin, exhaustion and something else warring in his face. "Good?"

"Scars mean it was real." She tightened her grip on his arm. "Come on, knight. We've got two courts that want us dead, five stones to light, and my family is holding a door in a city that wants to purge them. We don't have time for you to be dramatic about your freedom."

Something happened to his mouth then — an unpracticed motion, crooked and brief and entirely real. "Dramatic," he repeated, tasting the word. "Three hundred years of service, and this is my reward. Insult."

"Consider it a wedding gift," she said, and then heard herself, and went hot from collar to ears, and decided with the full force of a food-taster's nerve not to take it back.

Noor said nothing. But he picked up her pack from the mud, and slung it over his own shoulder alongside his, and when they turned north along the riverbank toward the fifth stone, he walked close enough that their arms touched, and neither of them moved away.

* * *

They walked most of the night through country that grew gradually, grudgingly mortal. The sourceless light faded. Stars came out — her stars, the constellations Tam had taught her on tenement rooftops — and the air lost its honeyed undertone and began to smell of rain and woodsmoke and distance. By the time the sky went grey, they had crossed into the marches: moorland stitched with drystone walls, a land so empty it rang.

They made a cold camp in the lee of a ruined shepherd's hut. Noor built no fire — "smoke reads for miles out here, and we are, both realms agree, extremely readable" — and they sat shoulder to shoulder sharing hard bread and the last of the cheese Tam's smuggling network had pressed on them at the border, watching the light come up over hills that had never heard of either court.

Juniper was half-asleep against the stones when she saw it.

A bird, high up, circling. Wrong bird. Too steady, too deliberate, holding its pattern against the wind like a man pacing a room. Even at that distance she knew the silhouette, because she'd grown up under it: a courier hawk, royal-bred, the kind that flew dispatches between Vellamere and the border garrisons. The kind that flew for the spymaster's office.

"Wren," she said.

Noor followed her gaze. "Your mentor."

"My trainer." She corrected it out of habit, and the correction tasted like ash. "There's a difference. There was a difference." Below the hawk, far off across the moor, she could just make out movement — riders, four maybe five, fanning out along the valley line with the disciplined economy of people who had done this a hundred times. Not fae. Mortal horses, mortal steel, mortal patience. Wren Corliss did not send hunters after people unless he already knew where they were going.

"He's between us and the fifth stone," Noor said quietly.

"He's between us and everything. He trained me, Noor. He knows how I think. He knows I'll go for the stones, because he's the one who taught me to finish what I start." She watched the hawk wheel, and felt the old reflex rise in her — the taster's instinct, the survivor's calculus, the voice that said *run, hide, small, quiet* — and she looked instead at the scar on Noor's chest, half-hidden by his ruined shirt, and at the road north, and at the smoke-smudge on the horizon where Vellamere sat under siege with her family inside it.

Three days ago she had refused to die for a realm that abandoned hers. She was not going to live small for one that hunted it.

"We don't run," she said. "We ride at them."

Noor turned. In the growing light his eyes had lost their fae gleam — or rather, it had sunk deeper, banked like coals. "Five hunters. One knight whose armor is at the bottom of a fae river, and one alchemist's daughter with a satchel of charms."

"One spymaster," Juniper said, "who spent ten years teaching me every trick he knows, which means I know every trick he knows. And one knight who chose exile in front of a queen, which means he's already survived the worst thing that can happen to him." She stood, brushing moor-grass from her knees, and held out her hand. "Wren Corliss taught me that the best position in any fight is the one your enemy believes impossible. So we're going to walk straight down the middle of that valley, in plain view, and we're going to see what he does when the girl he trained refuses to play the game he dealt."

Noor looked at her hand. Then he took it, and rose, and the morning wind came off the moor carrying the smell of rain and iron.

Above them, the hawk wheeled once more — and broke its pattern, turning north, flying fast toward the riders below. Carrying word. Carrying warning. Or perhaps, Juniper allowed herself to think, gripping Noor's hand as they started down into the valley, carrying something else entirely.

They would know soon. The riders had seen them.

And one of the riders, even at this distance, wore a coat she would have known anywhere in the world — grey, travel-stained, and buttoned to the throat, exactly the way Wren Corliss had worn it the day he first told her that her tongue was worth more than her life, and that someday, if she was clever, she might get to choose what it was worth.
