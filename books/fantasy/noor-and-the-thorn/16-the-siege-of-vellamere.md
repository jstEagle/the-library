## 16. The Siege of Vellamere

The bells woke the city before dawn — not the sweet-tongued bells of Saint Alba's that rang the hours, but the great cracked bell in the Saltspire tower that had not sounded since Tam's grandmother was a girl. It had one note only, a flat iron groan that rolled down through the rooftops and into the bones of every soul in Vellamere.

*Siege,* it said. *Siege. Siege.*

Tam stood at the tenement window with his knuckles white on the sill, watching the first grey light find the walls. The city gates were already shut. He could see them from here — the Marrowgate, its twin doors of black oak drawn closed for the first time in living memory, and soldiers swarming over it like ants over spilled honey. Torches on the battlements though the sun was coming up. Wagons heaped with supplies being hauled inside while carts full of people were turned away below, their drivers shouting, their children crying, the whole mass of them pressed against the bars like river debris against a weir.

Behind him, the tenement stirred. Old Maren struck flint to her cookstove. The twins, Pim and Sable, tumbled down the stairs arguing about whose turn it was to fetch water. The ordinary music of an ordinary morning, except that nothing about it was ordinary anymore, because Juniper was gone, and Wren Corliss's agents had torn through their street two nights past asking questions no one dared answer, and now the king had sealed his own people inside the city like a man shutting a lid on something he meant to burn.

"They've posted criers," said Odile from the doorway, her voice carefully level. She was the eldest of them, the one who kept the ledger of who owed what to whom, and she held the morning broadsheet as if it might bite. Tam took it.

The print was fresh, the ink still smelling of the press-house. *By order of His Majesty,* it read, *all practitioners of unlawful arts within the walls of Vellamere are commanded to surrender themselves at the Marrowgate garrison within three days. Shelterers and abettors will be held to the same account. The realm is cleansed. The realm endures.*

"Cleansed," Tam repeated. The word sat wrong in his mouth, like meat gone off. "He's going to purge us. That's what this is. Not a siege against enemies outside — a siege against everyone inside."

Odile's jaw tightened. "Three days."

"Juniper's been gone—" He counted without wanting to. "Nine days. Nine days since she went through the sewers with that map, and no word, and now this." He looked out again at the gates, at the soldiers, at the slow machinery of the king's intention grinding into motion. Fear sat in his chest like a swallowed stone. But beneath the fear, stubborn as a weed through cobblestone, something else was growing. "She'd come back if she knew. She'd come back right into their teeth if she thought we were behind these walls alone."

"Then we make sure she knows," Odile said. "And then we make sure there's a door left standing when she arrives."

* * *

They started, as all their best work started, with the market.

Not the grand square where the patricians bought peacocks and poisoned gossip, but the night market — the crooked lane between the tanneries and the old canal where Juniper had sold her charms for three years, where a copper bought you a knot of braided thread that kept fever from your children, or a pinch of salt charmed against nightmares, or a whispered word over a sickroom door that made the sickness think twice. Magic, small and humble and illegal, sold by a girl with flour on her apron so the guards would look past her.

Juniper was gone. But the buyers remained. And so did the sellers she'd never known she had.

Tam found the first of them that same evening: a lamplighter named Brannic who'd been buying Juniper's charms for his own bad knee and had, it turned out, been twisting little protections of his own into the wicks he trimmed — lamps that burned steadier, that guttered toward trouble the way dogs growl before strangers knock. When Tam told him what the broadsheet said, Brannic didn't panic. He set down his ladder pole and looked at Tam with eyes gone very still.

"How many days?"

"Three. Then they start door-to-door."

Brannic nodded slowly, the way a man nods when a debt has finally come due. "My mother was hedge-touched. They drowned her in the canal when I was nine, and I watched from under a bridge, and I swore I'd never be worth noticing." He picked up his pole again. "I'm done being invisible. Who else do you have?"

By midnight, they had eleven. By the second midnight, forty-one.

They met in the tallow-candler's cellar, where the smell of fat and wick-smoke covered the sound of voices, and Tam stood on a crate among faces lit by a single shielded lamp: servants and sellers, a laundress with scarred red hands, a gravedigger who knew every tunnel under the city better than the men who'd dug them, two of Wren Corliss's own former informants who'd come over carrying everything they knew about how the spymaster's network worked. Ordinary people. People whose names would never be written in any chronicle. People who had spent their whole lives learning how to survive quietly, and who now discovered that quiet survival was itself a kind of training.

"The soldiers will seal the wards first," said Mistress Vetch, the laundress, who had a sister in the garrison and therefore knew things. "Then the granaries. Then they'll go house to house with the lists." She tapped the broadsheet. "Lists take time to make. Lists can be wrong."

"Can we make them wrong?" asked Pim, who at fourteen already had a forger's neat hand and no conscience whatsoever about using it.

"We can make them *very* wrong," Tam said, and felt something kindle in his chest that wasn't quite hope and wasn't quite fury but burned like both.

It was Odile who gave the plan its spine, drawing lines in spilled salt on the cellar floor. "We don't fight them. Forty of us can't fight a garrison, and anyone who tries will die stupid. We *slow* them. We hide people. Every charm-seller, every hedge-touched soul on those lists goes into the walls — the gravedigger's crypts, the old canal cisterns, the spaces behind the tannery vats. We move them at night, along routes only we know. And meanwhile—" her finger traced the streets around the Marrowgate— "the lists get lost. The ink runs. The officers' orders contradict each other. A garrison is only as fast as its paperwork, and paperwork, my dears, is something we can *touch*."

"And the gates?" someone asked. "If the king means to burn the city clean, the gates are the throat. Whatever Juniper's doing out there — whatever's coming — they'll need a way in."

Tam looked down at the salt map, at the line of the Marrowgate with its black oak doors, and heard himself say, "Then we hold the throat open."

Silence. Forty faces turning toward him.

"Not with swords," he went on, his voice steadier than he felt. "With everything else. The gatehouse garrison eats from the palace kitchens — and the palace kitchens are staffed by *our* people, women and men who've passed Juniper's charms hand to hand for years. The soldiers drink from the district wells. The watch changes at dusk, and the changeover takes a quarter hour of confusion, and in that quarter hour the inner postern has been opened twice this year already by drunk sentries who couldn't tell a key from a codfish. We don't storm anything. We *are* the garrison's food, its water, its laundry, its letters home. We're already inside every wall they think protects them." He looked around the lamplit circle of faces. "Juniper spent three years teaching this city that magic could be small enough to hide in a pocket. Let's show them what happens when all the pockets open at once."

Old Maren, who had come despite her hips, raised her cup of barley tea as high as her arm allowed. "For the girl with the tasting tongue," she said, "and for everyone they mean to cleanse."

Forty cups rose in the dark. The toast made no sound at all, and it was the loudest thing Tam had ever heard.

* * *

The message went out on the third night, written in Pim's careful hand on paper thin enough to swallow.

They had argued about what to say. Odile wanted caution — *stay away, stay safe, we'll manage.* Tam had looked at her across the candle for a long moment and then said, gently, "You know she won't stay away. So we'd best tell her the truth instead of a lie she'll see through." Because that was the thing about Juniper Thackeray that everyone always underestimated, even the people who loved her best: she could taste a lie the way she tasted poison, instantly and completely, and she had never once in her life chosen safety over family when family was in reach.

So Tam wrote the truth:

*Sister. The king has shut the gates and posted the cleansing order. They come for the charm-sellers in three days; we've moved most below ground already, and the rest we'll keep moving. We've got the garrison's kitchens and its wells and half its laundry, and Brannic says the postern key sleeps in a guard's pocket more often than on its hook. We cannot hold forever, but we can hold longer than they expect, and we will hold until you come. Come home, Juniper. We'll hold the door.*

He read it aloud once, and the cellar-full of them listened, and when he finished, nobody spoke, because there was nothing to add. It was all true. That was the whole of it.

Sable carried it out — small, quick Sable, who could cross a rooftop like spilled water and had never once been caught. She tucked the paper into the hollow brick above the bakery on Cordwainer Lane, the third brick, the dead drop Juniper herself had built, and from there it would pass hand to hand through the network of beggars and barge-men and bird-sellers that stretched out beyond the walls like roots under a road, all the way to wherever Juniper was, whatever she had become, whatever price the covenant-stones were taking from her.

After Sable slipped out into the dark, Tam climbed to the roof.

Vellamere lay below him under a curfew moon, and it did not look like a conquered city. It looked like a banked fire. Here and there a shuttered window glowed where a charm burned hidden in a jar. In the tannery lane, a figure moved between shadows with a bundle of the hidden ones, feeding them, leading them deeper. On the wall above the Marrowgate, soldiers stamped their feet against the cold, and directly beneath their boots, in the guardhouse, a kitchen woman Tam had known since childhood was ladling soup into which — if Brannic's word held true — a small protection had been stirred, so that every soldier who ate it would dream tonight of home instead of slaughter, and wake tomorrow a little less eager for the burning.

Ordinary people. Doing extraordinary things not with thunder and glory, but with soup and stolen keys and lists rewritten in the dark. Juniper had taught them that. Or maybe, Tam thought, leaning on the cold parapet, they had always known it, and she had simply given them permission.

The wind came off the mountains smelling of snow and something else — something green and wild and old, threading through the smoke of the city like a promise. Tam's thorn-charm, the little knot of braided rowan he'd carried since Juniper made it for him two winters ago, pulsed warm against his chest. Once. Twice. Like a heartbeat answering another heartbeat far away.

He went very still.

It had never done that. Not once in two years. Charms faded; everyone knew charms faded. This one was waking up.

Far to the east, past the walls, past the hills, a light bloomed on the horizon — brief, vertical, unmistakably not lightning — and then another, farther off, and Tam understood with a certainty that dropped through him like a stone through deep water that somewhere out there, Juniper was lighting stones, and the boundary between the worlds was beginning to blaze like a fuse.

He turned and shouted down into the dark of the stairwell, his voice cracking with something too big for his chest:

"Odile! Get everyone up — get *everyone* up! It's starting!"
