## 17. The Fifth and Sixth Stones

The boundary was dying the way a fire dies — not all at once, but in patches, in cold spots where the world simply stopped being one thing or the other.

Juniper felt it through her boots first. They had crossed into the Marchlands at dusk, that ragged country between Vellamere's eastern wall and the fae realm's thorned edge, where the grass grew silver on one side of a footpath and green on the other, and where, lately, whole fields had gone grey as ash. Places where nothing lived because nothing could decide what it was. The air tasted of struck flint. Her thorn-mark — the dark sigil coiled around her left wrist like a sleeping serpent — pulsed with every step, faster now, urgent as a second heart.

"It's worse than the chapel," she said.

Noor walked half a pace behind her, the way he always did, close enough to catch her if the ground betrayed them and far enough to pretend he wasn't guarding her. His armor was Bramble-work, black-green and living, leaves rustling along his shoulders even when there was no wind. In the failing light his eyes caught what little remained and held it, amber over gold.

"The boundary is not dying," he said. "It is being *unmade*. There is a difference. A wound closes. This is being unpicked, thread by thread, by hands that know the stitching." He glanced at the grey field to their left, where a shepherd's hut stood hollow-eyed, its door open on darkness that did not match the darkness outside it. "Your king's alchemists have been at this for years. We are only seeing the hem come loose."

"Then we run faster."

They ran.

* * *

The fifth stone stood on a hill that had once been holy and was now merely high — a ring of leaning monoliths, half-swallowed by gorse, with the covenant-stone itself at the center like a rotten tooth in a broken jaw. Juniper had seen four of the seven now. She knew their shape the way she knew the shape of grief: a pillar of veined grey rock taller than a man, carved all over with interlocking thorns, cold to every sense except the one that mattered.

This one was nearly dark. The thorn-carvings were filled with lichen and bird filth, and the light that should have moved beneath its surface — that slow green-gold breathing she'd seen in the others — was down to a single vein, flickering like the last ember in a banked stove.

Noor circled it twice, reading the carvings with his fingertips, his lips moving through words in a language that made the gorse shiver.

"Vessel-stone," he said at last. "It takes what you pour in. The fourth took your mother's face. The third took—" He stopped.

"My warmth," Juniper said. "I remember. I'm the one who can't feel it anymore." She held up her hands. They worked fine. They just didn't *tell* her anything — no heat, no cold, no ache. She had learned to check her own fingers for frostbite the way other people checked the time. "What does this one want?"

Noor was quiet for too long.

"Noor."

"It wants time," he said finally. "Years. It is the oldest toll and the cruelest, because it is the only one that cannot be given back. The first bearers called it the Tithe of Tomorrow." His jaw tightened. "In the old days, the heir brought a condemned criminal. Someone already dying. The stone was satisfied with whatever remained."

"And there's no one here but me."

"There is me."

She looked at him sharply. In the stone's dying light his face was all planes and shadow, beautiful the way winter is beautiful, and utterly unreadable — except that it wasn't, not anymore, not to her. Three weeks of shared road, shared bread, shared blood. She had learned to read him the way she read a dish: not the obvious flavors but the ones underneath, the bitter note under the honey, the almond hiding in the sauce that meant *poison* or meant *care*, depending on the hand that added it.

"You're going to say something noble," she said. "Don't."

"I am going to say something *arithmetic*." He stepped closer. "You have lit three stones. Each toll has taken more than the last, and each has taken it from the same well — you. Two stones remain after this one, and then the seventh, which will ask for everything. If you spend yourself here, there will be nothing left for the end. I am not noble. I am *budgeting*."

"That's worse."

"It's true, which is often worse." He pulled off one gauntlet. Beneath it his hand was fae-fair, long-fingered, the skin faintly luminous even now, even this far from home. "A year of mine is not a year of yours. Fae years are longer. Deeper. What I give will cost the stone less effort and buy it more light. It is simple economy."

"Nothing about you is simple economy." She grabbed his wrist before he could touch the stone. Her deadened fingers couldn't feel his pulse, and that small failure stung worse than any toll so far. "Listen to me. I watched my mother get old before her time because she sold charms in a banned market and paid for it in ways nobody saw. I know what it looks like when someone spends themselves quietly for people who don't notice. I am not going to stand here and watch you do it."

For a moment neither of them moved. The wind came up the hill smelling of gorse and ozone and something older, something like the inside of the thorn-hedges where Noor had been grown.

Then Noor said, very gently, "And I watched you give your mother's face to a rock and walk away without weeping, because the tears froze somewhere on the way out of you. You think I did not notice? You think I do not lie awake counting what remains of you?" His voice dropped lower. "Juniper. The covenant asks for a life freely given. It will take yours entire unless we find another way. Every piece I give instead is a piece of you that survives to bargain with."

"That's not how—"

"It is exactly how. You taught me. In your city, in your night markets, you sell charms for copper — small magics, small prices, many hands. You told me once: *no one person carries the whole weight; that's why we split it.*" His mouth curved, barely. "I am splitting it."

She wanted to argue. She was, by training and temperament, excellent at arguing. But her thorn-mark had begun to burn, and beyond the hill, east toward Vellamere, the sky had taken on a wrongness she recognized — a thinness, a translucence, as if the night were a curtain someone was pulling threads out of. The boundary was collapsing faster than any of them had guessed. Tam's message had reached them two days ago, smuggled out through a charm-seller's network: *Gates sealed. Purge coming. Hold the door.*

Two stones. One night. Or none.

"Half," she said. "We both give half. That's the deal. And if you try to sneak extra while I'm not looking, I will taste-test every meal you cook for the rest of our lives, and I will tell you honestly that it's bad."

Noor stared at her. Then he laughed — a short, startled sound, rare as sun in the Marchlands — and pressed his bare palm flat against the stone beside hers.

"Together, then," he said. "On three."

The stone woke like an animal.

Light flooded up through the thorn-carvings, green-gold and ravenous, and Juniper felt it find her — not her flesh, not her blood, but the *forward* part of her, the unwritten pages. She braced the way she braced against poison: cataloguing, measuring, refusing to panic. Years. She could feel them being weighed. Twenty-three years old, and the stone turned her future over like a merchant turning coins, tasting each one.

She thought of Tam's laugh, of Wren's gruff lessons in the spymaster's yard a lifetime ago, of Noor cooking badly in some kitchen that didn't exist yet. She chose which years to hold back and let the rest go, and it hurt the way drowning hurts, silent and total—

—and then it eased. Half of the pull simply *stopped*, cut clean, and warmth poured in from beside her. From Noor. She felt him giving, felt the strange deep-grained texture of fae years flowing into the stone alongside her mortal ones, two rivers feeding one channel. The stone drank. The vein of light became a flood. The thorns blazed, and the whole ring of monoliths rang like bells struck once, a sound that went through the ground and through her bones and out across the Marchlands, and far away — impossibly far — she felt a fifth point kindle in a chain that stretched between worlds.

Then it was done, and they were both on their knees in the wet grass, and the stone stood tall and burning above them like a green star planted in the earth.

Juniper counted her own breaths until they steadied. Then she looked at Noor.

He had gone grey. Not pale — *grey*, the color leached out of him, his luminous skin dull as pewter, and when he pushed his hair back from his face she saw silver streaks at the temples that had not been there an hour ago. Fae aged slowly, he'd told her. Glacially. He had been perhaps two hundred years old and worn it like a young man wears twenty-five.

Now he wore years.

"How much?" she whispered.

He flexed his hand, examining it like a stranger's. "Fifty of mine. Perhaps sixty." He said it lightly, the way she said *it's just a scratch*, and she hated that she'd taught him that too. "It bought the stone more than a hundred of yours would have. Economy."

"Noor—"

"*Juniper.*" He caught her face in both hands, and his palms were cool now, cool as the stone, and his eyes were still amber-gold and still full of that stubborn, banked ferocity she had first mistaken for contempt. "I have served the Bramble Court since before your grandmother's grandmother drew breath. I have never once spent myself on purpose, for anyone, for anything. Do you understand what you have made me? A knight who gives gifts." His thumb traced her cheekbone. "I find I do not regret it. That frightens me more than the seventh stone does."

She laughed, wetly, and leaned her forehead against his, and for one breath the burning chain of stones and the collapsing sky and the purge rolling toward Vellamere all fell away, and there was only the two of them kneeling in the grass on a hill that was holy again.

Then the horizon flickered.

Not lightning. Not dawn. The eastern dark simply *stuttered* — there, then not-there, then there again — like a candle flame in a draft, like reality blinking. Through the gap, for half a heartbeat, Juniper saw stars that belonged to no mortal sky.

Noor was on his feet instantly, gauntlet back on, sword half-drawn. "The boundary is failing faster than the Court estimated. When it goes entirely, the realms do not separate cleanly — they tear. Everything bleeds through. Both directions."

"Then we don't stop tonight." Juniper hauled herself upright using the warm flank of the stone. Her body felt lighter and thinner, like a garment washed too many times, but it held. "Where's the sixth?"

Noor pointed north, toward a smudge of hills and, beyond them, a darkness that was not natural darkness. "The Drowned Chapel. Old mortal ground, swallowed by a fen when the last covenant weakened. The stone lies beneath the water." His mouth thinned. "It is a listening stone. It does not take years or faces or warmth."

"What does it take?"

"Names," he said. "It takes names. And Juniper — yours is already half-spent. The third stone took your warmth, the fourth your mother's face, the fifth your tomorrows. Names are woven through all of it. I do not know what will be left to give."

Juniper looked at her hands — hands that could not feel, attached to a body running on borrowed structure, belonging to a woman whose name was the last solid thing she owned. Juniper Thackeray. Taster. Charm-seller. Thorn-heir. Found sister. The name Tam shouted across tenement roofs. The name Wren had written in his reports for three years without ever once saying aloud.

"Well," she said, and started walking north. "Then we'd better make sure it's worth spelling correctly."

* * *

The fen breathed.

That was the only word for it. The water lay black and still between tussocks of sedge, and every few minutes the whole marsh swelled and sank, swell and sink, slow as a sleeper's chest. Mist moved against the wind. Somewhere below, drowned bells hung in a drowned chapel, and when the fen exhaled, the bells stirred, and a sound came up through the water like a word remembered halfway.

They crossed on paths only Noor could see — fae-sight reading the old covenant-roads that still ran under the muck like veins under skin. Juniper followed with her dead hands out for balance and her thorn-mark blazing so brightly through her sleeve that it lit their way better than any lantern. Behind them, the stuttering on the horizon had spread. Ahead, the mist had begun to show things: a roofline under the water, a weathervane, the tip of a spire like a finger pointing accusingly at heaven.

The chapel rose around them gradually as they waded the last stretch — walls emerging from the fen like a drowning man surfacing, black stone slick with weed, windows empty of glass but full of fen-light that had no source. Inside, the water was waist-deep and warmer than it had any right to be. The sixth stone stood where the altar should have been, submerged to its crown, its thorn-carvings glowing faintly through the black water like embers under ash.

"It's alive," Juniper said. "Barely."

"Barely is enough." Noor waded to the altar and set his back against it, scanning the broken windows, the doorless doorway, the fen beyond. "Quickly, though. The hunters behind us are not the problem. The problem is that when a stone wakes fully, everything nearby feels it. Including things that live in fens." Something moved in the water off to their left, distantly, heavily. "So. Quickly."

Juniper climbed onto the altar, water streaming off her, and laid both hands on the stone.

It did not seize her the way the others had. That was the horror of it. The fifth stone had *taken*. This one *asked*. A pressure against the inside of her skull, patient and vast, like a librarian waiting for her to find the right shelf. It looked through her — through the taster's tongue, the charm-seller's hands, the heir's mark — looking for the thing she'd come to pay.

Her name.

She felt it turn the name over. *Juniper Thackeray.* And beneath it, threaded through it like dye through cloth, everything the tolls had already taken — her mother's face, her warmth, sixty years of her tomorrows — and she understood, suddenly, sickeningly, that the name was frayed. That there might not be enough of it left to give. That she might stand here forever, offering and offering, while the stone waited politely for a payment that no longer existed.

Panic rose. She crushed it the way she'd been trained to crush it — *breathe, catalogue, measure* — and in the space that panic left behind, she heard Noor move.

Water splashed. Warmth against her side. His hand closed over hers on the stone, and his voice came low and steady beside her ear.

"Take mine too," he said. "Split it. Like the hill. Like the market. *No one carries the whole weight.*"

"You don't know what it'll take from you—"

"I know what it will take from *you* if I don't." He lifted his chin and addressed the stone directly, formally, in the voice of a knight of the Bramble Court delivering terms. "Hear us, listening stone. Two bearers stand before you. One name in partnership, freely intertwined. Take from both or take from neither."

The pressure paused.

And then, for the first time in seven stones and three thousand years, the covenant hesitated — considering, the way she considered an unfamiliar dish. New flavor. Untried recipe.

Juniper seized the moment. She gathered her name together — all of it, the girl in the tenement, the taster at court, the seller in the night market, the daughter, the sister, the heir — and instead of handing it over whole, she *wove* it, deliberately, braiding it through the name Noor offered, two threads twisted into one cord. *Juniper-and-Noor. Noor-and-Juniper.* Neither name alone anymore. Both names, stronger together than either had been apart.

She pushed the braid into the stone.

The chapel *rang*. Every drowned bell in the fen sounded at once, though no clapper moved, and the water around the altar lit from beneath — green-gold, then white, then a color Juniper had no name for, the color of the boundary itself before anyone tried to cut it. The sixth stone blazed like a captured sunrise. Out across the dark, she felt the chain leap and strengthen, five bright points and now six, and felt — distinctly, unmistakably — something on the far side of the chain *answer*. A seventh point, dark but present, waking.

And the toll came due.

It took from both of them at once, evenly, mercilessly fair. From Juniper: pieces of her name she would not miss until later — she felt the diminutive go first, the childhood nickname only Tam used, and then her mother's voice saying *Juniper* in a particular way on a particular morning, gone, gone. From Noor: she could not see what it took, only watch it leave, watch him sway against the altar with his teeth bared and his eyes fixed on hers, giving and giving, refusing to stop until she gripped his wrist hard enough to hurt.

Enough. *Enough.*

The light sank. The bells quieted. The fen settled around them like a dog returning to sleep.

They clung to each other in the waist-deep water, shaking, alive, six-sevenths done. Juniper opened her mouth to speak and found she had to *search* for her own name for a moment — a horrible, bottomless instant — before it came back to her, slightly thinner, slightly worn, like a coin rubbed smooth by handling.

"Six," she managed.

"Six." Noor's voice was rougher than she'd ever heard it. Older. He touched his own face wonderingly, as if checking it was still his. "One remains."

And then the water around them went cold — truly cold, fen-cold, wrong-cold — and every bell in the drowned chapel began to swing at once, though nothing touched them, ringing out a warning across the black water.

Through the broken doorway, out on the fen, lights were appearing. Many lights. Moving fast. Some burned mortal-white, in formation, disciplined — Wren's hunters, or what remained of the king's men, come across the Marchlands on the purge's heels.

But between them, ahead of them, closing from the direction of the thorned edge of the world, moved lights of a different color entirely. Bramble-green. Cold and lovely and relentless.

The Queen's hunters had found them too.

Noor drew his sword, and the blade caught the light of six burning stones, and he said the only thing left to say:

"Hold on to me. We're going to be swimming."
