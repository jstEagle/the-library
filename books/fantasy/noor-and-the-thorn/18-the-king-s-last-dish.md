## 18. The King's Last Dish

The summons came at dawn, carried by a herald in livery so clean it looked unworn, as though the king's household had dressed a mannequin and sent it walking.

"By order of His Majesty Aldric Vellamere the Third," the herald recited, unrolling a scroll sealed with wax the color of old blood, "the taster Juniper Thackeray is commanded to attend the royal banquet on the eve of the Feast of Severance, there to perform her sworn office. Safe conduct is granted under truce until the banquet's conclusion."

*Severance.* They had named it now. Named it and set it on the calendar like a wedding or a coronation, printed it in gold ink on festival broadsheets pasted to every wall in Vellamere: *A New Age for a New Realm — Magic Severed, Order Restored.*

Noor read the scroll over my shoulder, his breath cool against my ear, smelling of frost and green things. "It's a trap."

"Of course it's a trap." I folded the scroll along its creases, precise as a surgeon. "But it's a trap with a table laid in the middle of it, and I'm being asked to taste everything on that table." I looked up at him. "Do you know how long I've waited for them to be stupid enough to put the poison directly under my tongue?"

Tam, who was mending a net by the window because his hands needed occupation the way other men's needed drink, snorted. "You're going to walk into the palace that tried to kill you three times, sit down at the king's own board, and eat his food. On purpose."

"On purpose," I agreed.

"I hate this plan," Tam said. "I want that noted. Witnessed. Written into the covenant itself if you can manage it."

"Noted," Noor said gravely, and I could have kissed him for the deadpan of it, except that kissing Noor in front of Tam still made the room feel too small, so I settled for catching his hand and squeezing once. The thorn-mark on my wrist pulsed where our oaths had begun to twine, a slow warm heartbeat that wasn't quite mine anymore.

Three stones lit. Four dark. And between us and the seventh stone stood a king who had decided that if he could not claim the covenant, he would murder the ground it grew from.

"He wants me there," I said. "That's the part that matters. He didn't send soldiers. He sent an invitation. Which means whatever he's planning, he needs a taster for it — which means whatever he's planning, he needs it tasted *safe*. Or he thinks he does." I smiled, and felt Tam flinch, because he'd told me once that my market-smile looked like a knife deciding where to go in. "Either way, they're going to serve me the truth on silver plates. All I have to do is swallow carefully."

* * *

They dressed me for court, which was its own small war.

The gown was Tam's find — grey silk shot through with threads of green that caught the light like moss on stone, borrowed from a merchant's widow who owed her life to one of my charms and repaid debts in wardrobe. Noor refused, flatly refused, to wear mortal court dress, and after an hour of argument we compromised: his Bramble Court armor, but dulled, the living bramble-vines coaxed flat and dark, the silver gone matte as old pewter. He looked less like a fae knight and more like something grown in the deep of a winter forest — beautiful, and slightly wrong, and armed.

"They will stare," he said, examining himself in the tenement's cracked mirror.

"Good. Let them stare while they're listening to me."

Wren met us at the palace gates.

I hadn't seen him since the Marches, since he'd pressed the alchemist's confession into my hands along with his resignation and walked away from thirty years of service like a man setting down a load he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it was heavy. He looked thinner. Older. He was wearing plain wool, no badge, no chain of office, and yet the guards at the gate saluted him anyway, because some men carry authority in their bones long after the title's stripped away.

"You came," he said.

"You knew I would."

"I hoped you'd be smarter than that." But there was no bite in it. He fell into step beside us as we passed through the gatehouse, his voice dropping low. "The truce is real — the king keeps his word when it suits his dignity, and a public banquet under truce suits it. But the truce ends when the banquet does. Whatever you're going to do in there, do it before the last course."

"How many guards?"

"Two hundred in the hall. More in the corridors. The king's been afraid since the fifth stone lit — he doesn't sleep, he paces, he talks to the alchemist for hours behind closed doors." Wren's jaw tightened. "Merrick Voss. That's the name you'll want. Court alchemist, twenty years in service, the man who brewed the frost. He'll be at the king's right hand tonight, presenting the final course himself. The king let it be known the Severance feast will be crowned by a dish of Voss's own devising."

My stomach went cold, colder than any poison had ever made it. "He's serving it to the whole court?"

"He's serving it to *you*, first. As a courtesy." Wren almost laughed — a dry, joyless sound. "The king remembers what your tongue caught three years ago, Juniper. Half the court calls you the girl who tastes ghosts. He wants you to taste Voss's masterpiece and declare it clean, in front of two hundred witnesses, so that when the land begins to die, no one can say the poison was ever in the cup."

"And if I refuse to declare it clean?"

"Then the truce ends early, and you die in the hall, and the feast proceeds without a taster." Wren stopped at the foot of the great stair, where his authority ended and mine began. For a moment he looked at me the way he'd looked at me when I was nine years old and burning my first charm in a soup pot, half exasperation, half something fiercer. "You were the best student I ever had. Better than me, maybe. Don't prove me right by dying stupid."

"No, sir," I said, and meant it as a joke, and watched it land instead like a vow.

* * *

The great hall of Vellamere had been rebuilt twice in my lifetime, and both times in marble pale as bone, all soaring columns and vaulted ceilings painted with clouds. Tonight the clouds had been repainted. I saw it the moment we entered: the fresco overhead no longer showed the old kings receiving tribute from river-spirits and hill-fae. It showed a single crowned figure standing alone in a field of ash, and beneath it, in letters of gold leaf, the new motto: *One Realm. One Law. One King.*

Two hundred nobles in their finest, seated at tables arranged in a horseshoe below the dais. Guards along every wall, halberds gleaming. Musicians playing something bright and hollow. And at the high table, King Aldric the Third, gaunt as a winter branch, his crown sitting on his head like a debt he couldn't pay — and beside him, slight and neat and smiling, a man in alchemist's grey with ink-stained fingers and eyes like vinegar.

Voss. I'd seen him at court a hundred times across three years of tasting, a background figure pouring measured drops into the king's medicines. I had never once looked at him properly. Now I looked, and my tongue remembered the residue from the king's wine three years ago — that faint mineral coldness, like licking a frozen key — and my whole body understood, in one sick rush, whose hand had been on the ladle all along.

A herald cried our names — mine with careful neutrality, Noor's with barely concealed distaste — and we walked the length of the hall with every eye upon us. I heard the whispers move through the crowd like wind through wheat. *The taster. The traitor-taster. They say she lights the stones. They say she travels with one of Them.*

The king did not rise. "Juniper Thackeray," he said, and his voice was dry paper. "You come under truce. Observe it, and you leave alive. Break it—" he gestured vaguely at the guards, a gesture that cost him effort— "and the truce breaks with you."

"I know how truces work, Your Majesty," I said, and curtsied exactly as deep as protocol required and not one inch further. "I've survived three years of your court. I learned manners where manners were the only weapon allowed me."

Something flickered in his face — irritation, or perhaps recognition. Kings do not like being reminded that servants watch.

We were seated at a side table near the dais, close enough to hear the king's conversation, far enough to be displayed. The banquet began.

I tasted everything. That was the office; that was the ritual the whole court knew by heart. Each dish borne in, each portion cut, each morsel placed on the golden tasting-spoon and held on my tongue for the count of ten while two hundred people watched to see if the taster lived. It was theater, and I had always hated it, and tonight I performed it better than I ever had in my life — because tonight I was not tasting for poison in the dish.

I was tasting for the poison in the *pattern*.

Course after course, I let the flavors tell me their story. The venison, rich and dark. The river-fish, delicate. The bread, the butter, the honeyed pears. And beneath it all, thread after thread of that same mineral cold — fainter here, stronger there, woven through the meal like a signature through a letter. Not enough to harm. Never enough to harm. Enough to *mark*. Every dish on the king's table had been touched by Voss's frost, the way every page of a book carries the ink of the same pen.

And then I understood what I was tasting, and my blood turned to ice water, and it took every year of Wren Corliss's training to keep my face smooth.

The frost-poison was never meant to kill the king.

It was meant to kill *through* him. A king is the land's anchor in the old law — every coronation oath in Vellamere bound the monarch to the soil, the rivers, the harvest, a marriage of crown and country older than the city walls. Three years of doses, taken daily in wine and medicine and now threaded through a sacred feast, had soaked that bond in frost. The king was dying of it, yes, slowly, the way a tree dies from a ring of bark cut away — but the tree dies too. The land drank from the king the way the king drank from his cup. Voss hadn't been poisoning a man.

He'd been poisoning the *marriage*. Killing the land's half of the covenant so that when the seventh stone called for an heir, there would be nothing left alive to answer. The covenant would die unclaimed. The realms would part forever, cleanly, legally, with the paperwork in perfect order.

That was why the king needed me here. Not to protect him. To *witness*. To stand before the court, taste the Severance feast, and pronounce it clean — so that when the fields grayed and the rivers stilled, the histories would say the taster found no fault, and the death of the land would be no one's crime at all.

The final course was announced.

Merrick Voss rose from the king's right hand and descended to the floor himself, carrying a covered dish of white porcelain on a tray of black oak, and the musicians fell silent, and two hundred faces turned. He walked to my table. He set the dish before me. And he lifted the cover with the tenderness of a man unveiling a lover.

Beneath it lay a single pear, glazed and gold, perfect as a painting.

"The king's own orchard," Voss said, and his voice was mild, pleasant, pitched to carry. "Fruit of the last harvest before the Severance. His Majesty desires that the famous tongue of Juniper Thackeray pronounce it fit — that all present may know the new age opens with a meal no fault can be found in." He smiled. His teeth were very even. "Whenever you are ready, mistress taster."

The hall waited. Two hundred breaths held. Noor's hand had gone to the table's edge, vines stirring beneath his dull armor, and I saw Wren by the far wall, rigid, reading my face the way he'd taught me to read a suspect's.

I picked up the pear.

I held it to my nose first — the audience expected theater, and theater bought me time. Honey. Ripe flesh. And beneath, so faint only a tongue trained on three years of this exact poison could find it: the cold. But different now. Deeper. This was no trace, no residue. This pear had been grown in soil steeped in the frost for three years. It was not poisoned.

It was *made* of the poison. Root and branch and fruit. The land's own dying body, served up sweet.

If I pronounced it clean, I signed the land's death warrant before the whole court. If I pronounced it foul, Voss would produce his ledgers, his assays, his gentle proofs that no harmful substance was present in any measurable degree — and I would look either mad or malicious, and the truce would end, and I would die with the truth still locked in my mouth.

Unless I did what a taster is actually for.

Not detecting poison. Anyone with a kit can detect poison. A taster's true craft is older: it is knowing a dish so completely — its history, its hands, its intentions — that you can tell the court not merely whether it will kill the body, but what it will do to everything the body touches.

I bit into the pear. Held it on my tongue. Ten counts. Twenty. Let the frost bloom against my palate, cold and mineral and *familiar*, and reached through the thorn-mark on my wrist, through the lit stones, through the covenant's living thread, and *asked the land to confirm what my tongue already knew*.

The mark answered. A pulse of grief so vast it had no words — the taste of a forest falling asleep and dreaming it would never wake.

I swallowed. Set down the pear. Stood.

"Mistress Thackeray?" Voss said softly. "Your verdict?"

"My verdict," I said, and my voice carried to the rafters because I made it carry, because I had shouted orders over a siege kitchen and sung charms above a riot and I knew exactly how loud a servant can be when she chooses, "is that this pear is the finest thing I have ever tasted, and the most obscene."

Murmurs. The king leaning forward. Voss's smile thinning at the edges.

"It is sweet," I said, "because it was grown in poisoned ground. Every dish at this feast carries the same frost I first found in the king's wine three years ago — the frost Master Voss has dosed His Majesty with, day upon day, for three years. Not to kill the king." I turned, slowly, letting the whole horseshoe of faces see me. "To kill *through* him. The coronation oath binds the king to the land. Poison the king's blood, and the land drinks the poison after. Your fields will gray. Your rivers will sleep. And when the seventh covenant-stone calls for an heir, there will be nothing left alive to answer — and the realms will sever, and Master Voss and his master will have murdered a world and called it policy."

Silence, total, the kind that rings.

Then Voss laughed — a light, genuinely amused sound. "Charming. The little market-witch tells ghost stories. Mistress, there is no substance in any assay of mine that—"

"There wouldn't be." I pulled off my left glove and held up my wrist, and let the thorn-mark blaze — green-gold light running the twisted lines like sap, bright enough to throw shadows across the white porcelain. "Because your frost isn't a substance, Master Voss. It's a *sentence*. And the land itself just dictated it back to me, word for word. Ask the harvests this spring. Ask the river at the mill. Ask the orchard your pear came from whether its trees dream of waking."

The light from the mark climbed the dais and touched the king's face, and Aldric the Third looked down at his own hands — at the faint gray threading his fingernails that I suddenly realized the whole court could see, now that they knew to look — and something in him broke. Not loudly. Quietly, the way ice lets go of a branch.

"Voss," the king said. Just the name. Nothing else.

The alchemist's composure held for one more heartbeat. Then his hand slipped inside his grey coat, and Noor moved faster than sight, and the vial of something smoking and pale shattered on the flagstones, hissing, eating a small black hole in the stone — and Merrick Voss stood pinned against the table edge with a bramble-thorn at his throat, dripping a bead of red that smelled, even to me, of frost.

"Guards!" someone cried, uselessly, from six directions at once.

"No need." Wren Corliss stepped out of the wall's shadow, holding a sheaf of papers high enough for the front tables to see the seals. "I have the assays. The ledgers. Three years of delivery records, and a confession signed in the presence of two witnesses. I was the king's spymaster. I am, as of this week, merely a citizen — and citizens may testify." His voice cracked, just once, on the word *merely*. "Read them. All of you. Read them before anyone decides what happens next."

The papers changed hands. The murmur became a roar. Somewhere a chair went over; somewhere a lord was already drafting his defection in his head. The king sat motionless amid the collapse of his design, gray to the fingertips, staring at the bitten pear as though it might explain itself.

Noor released Voss to the guards who finally dared approach, and came to stand at my shoulder, and beneath the roaring hall he spoke quietly, for me alone.

"The seventh stone," he said. "The frost has nearly finished its work. We have days, Juniper. Perhaps hours."

Above us, unnoticed by everyone but the two of us, the painted ash-field on the ceiling seemed to darken — or perhaps that was only the candles guttering.

I took his hand, thorn-mark to thorn-mark, and felt the covenant pull tight as a bowstring.

"Then we'd better not waste the truce," I said, "on the ride home."
