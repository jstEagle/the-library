## 21. After the Purge

The bells of Vellamere rang for three days when the king fell, and Wren Corliss did not ring them.

He had considered it, in the strange hollow hours after the abdication — the heralds reading the alchemist's confession from every corner-stone while the crowd's roar rose like weather — but his hands had not seemed like his hands that day. They had felt like tools left behind by someone who had moved on. So he stood in the shadow of the Chancery arch with his resignation folded in his breast pocket, already signed, already delivered to a council that no longer needed it, and he listened to the city celebrate the end of a poison he had helped administer.

Not the frost-poison. That had never been his work. But he had carried out the smaller poisons all his life: the arrests that came at dawn, the names whispered into ledgers, the charm-sellers dragged from their stalls with their fingers broken so they could not work again. He had told himself each of them was necessary, that the ban was a wall and walls kept people safe. He had taught that lesson to a sharp-eyed girl with quick hands and a talent for tasting lies, and she had used what he taught her to unmake everything he had built.

Juniper Thackeray. Even now the name sat strangely in his mouth, half pride, half wound.

* * *

Rebuilding began before the ash of the purge-fires cooled. The new covenant-law was read aloud in Copperlane Square on the first market morning of spring: magic was lawful, magic was accountable, and no one in Vellamere would again be starved or hanged for the shape of their gift. Wren heard it secondhand, standing at the edge of the square with his collar turned up, watching a woman sell protective charms openly from a table where once she would have hidden them beneath onions.

She saw him looking. He braced himself for the flinch, the fear, the hatred — he had earned all three — but instead she lifted one of her charms, a small knotted thread with a bead of river-glass, and held it up as if offering.

"I can't," he said, when he'd crossed close enough. His voice came out rougher than intended. "I'm the reason you sold these in cellars."

"I know who you are, Master Corliss." She set the charm down between them anyway. "My sister was taken in the third year of the purges. You signed the order." His stomach dropped; there it was. "And you're also the man who burned the confession that saved us all. I've decided both things are true, and I'm tired of pretending a person is only the worst thing they've done."

He looked at the charm a long time. River-glass, worn smooth, catching the grey light. "What does it do?"

"Nothing, yet. It wants a purpose. Most things do."

He paid her double what it was worth and wore it under his shirt, against the scar of an old knife-wound, where no one could see the spymaster of Vellamere wearing a charm like any frightened grandmother in Copperlane.

That was how it started. Not with forgiveness — forgiveness was a door he did not expect opened to him — but with work. There was so much work. The council needed men who knew where the old networks ran, who understood which informants could be trusted and which had only ever been hungry. Wren knew every cellar, every dead drop, every name in every ledger, and for the first time in thirty years he used that knowledge to give back rather than take away.

By summer he was teaching.

It happened without ceremony. A group of charm-sellers — the same ones whose stalls he had raided, whose fingers his soldiers had broken — asked him, through Tam Thackeray's blunt mediation, whether he would show them how to recognize a royal informant. "You know them better than anyone," Tam said, arms crossed, chin up, daring him to refuse. "You made most of them."

So Wren Corliss, once the terror of the night markets, stood in a borrowed room above a bakery and taught twelve charm-sellers how to spot a watcher: the loiterer who bought nothing twice, the beggar whose eyes tracked hands instead of faces, the way a true customer haggles and a planted one doesn't. They took notes. They argued with him. An old woman named Maren corrected his assessment of a street he had worked for two decades, because the city had changed under the new law and she had changed with it while he was still mapping the old one.

"You were good at this," she said afterward, almost grudgingly, packing up her samples of dried rowan and iron filings.

"I was good at hurting people with this."

"Mm." She tied her bundle with a practiced pull. "Well. Now you're not. Get used to it faster than I got used to my son being alive again. It goes easier."

He laughed — actually laughed, a sound rusty as an unused hinge — and walked home through streets that smelled of bread and cut lavender instead of fear.

* * *

Autumn came, and with it the first anniversary of the grey-cloaked stranger's death, though Wren doubted anyone else marked it. He remembered the date the way he remembered all his failures: precisely, and without mercy.

The man's name had been Aldous Fenwick, though Wren had learned it only weeks ago, from the covenant-records Juniper now kept. A hedge-wizard of the old line, keeper of the first stone, courier of maps and warnings. He had spent his final years carrying the truth of the king's campaign from realm to realm on legs that grew slower every season, and he had died in a gutter in Copperlane with frost blooming black in his wounds, pressing a bloodstained map into the hands of a girl who never asked for any of it.

Wren had interrogated witnesses to that death, in the early days. He remembered the stallholder who described the dying man: *grey cloak, tall, spoke like a book, kept saying a name over and over.* Thorn-heir. At the time, Wren had been hunting the map. He had put watchers on every gate. He had very nearly caught Juniper at the sewer-mouth, and would have, if Tam hadn't doubled back through the tannery run to mislead his hunters.

He thought about that often. How close the world had come to ending, not with a battle but with one more efficient arrest, one more competent servant of a rotting crown doing exactly his job well.

In the new records room — bright, open, deliberately unlike the Chancery's sealed archives — Wren found what remained of Aldous Fenwick's effects. A satchel, water-stained. A wooden cup, mended with wire. A pressed sprig of something that might have been rowan or might have been its fae cousin, silver-leaved and faintly warm even after a year in paper. And letters. Dozens of them, unsent, addressed to a daughter in a village called Marlow's Edge across the marches, written in a careful hand that grew shakier toward the end.

*I have found the heir, or she has found me. She has quick eyes and kinder hands than mine. If I do not come home by the thaw, tell Nell I tried to leave the world better thorned.*

Wren sat with the letters a long time, the afternoon light going gold and then grey around him. He had spent his career learning what people carried in their pockets — weapons, poisons, secrets — and here was a dead man's pockets laid open, full only of love and unfinished errands. He copied every letter in his own careful hand and gave the originals to the covenant's couriers to carry to Marlow's Edge. Then he sat alone in the emptying room and did the thing he had not done since boyhood, since his mother's funeral, since the last time grief had been allowed a body.

He wept for the stranger in grey, whom he had hunted in death as surely as the king's poisoners had hunted him in life.

* * *

The first stone stood where it had always stood, in the weedy hollow beyond the Northgate, but it was not the same stone. Where it had been a grey, forgotten menhir furred with lichen, it now breathed. Light moved beneath its surface like slow water, green-gold, pulsing in a rhythm Wren eventually recognized as the rhythm of the tide — or of breath, or of seasons, some vast patience keeping time. The grass around it grew lush and wrong-shaped, sweetly scented, and small white moths danced there even in the cold months, drawn by warmth that had no earthly source.

Wren came at dusk, on the anniversary, carrying flowers.

He had agonized over the choice in a way that embarrassed him. Carnations were funeral-flowers, too obvious. Lilies too grand. In the end he had cut branches of rowan from the tree outside his lodgings, heavy with red berries, and wrapped the stems in plain brown paper the way one wrapped bread, the way one wrapped anything meant to be eaten and gone.

The walk out the Northgate took an hour. The city thinned behind him — new lamps in Copperlane, the tenement with its charm-shop glowing above, the sounds of an evening that did not brace itself — and the country took over, all wet grass and crow-call and the smell of coming frost. His knees ached. He was fifty-seven years old and he had buried his whole former self within the year, and the ache felt honest, like the ache after honest labor.

The stone greeted him. He was nearly sure of it — a softening in the air, a warmth like a hand held near a candle, the sense of being noticed by something enormous and gentle. Since the Reforging, the stones did that. Travelers spoke of it in awed tones: the boundary between realms breathing easy, the old wall between worlds become a membrane, a skin, alive. Juniper Thackeray and the fae knight Noor were its living heart, bound together in whatever fashion the fae understood marriage and kingship and sacrifice all at once. Wren had seen them once, at the council's first covenant-audience, standing shoulder to shoulder — the taster-girl and the thorn-crowned knight, both watchful, both wearier than their years — and he had bowed lower than protocol required, and Juniper had met his eyes and given him the smallest nod, and he had carried that nod home like a coal in cupped hands.

At the stone's base, the ground was soft and dark. Others had left things here: ribbons, candles burnt to stubs, a child's carved horse, coins from both realms. The covenant-dead were remembered here, all of them — the hedge-wizards, the hidden healers, everyone the long severance had cost.

Wren knelt. His joints complained; he ignored them. He laid the rowan branches against the stone's warm flank and arranged them with the care he had once reserved for laying out evidence, and then, feeling foolish and doing it anyway, he spoke.

"Your name was Aldous Fenwick." His breath smoked in the cold. "I didn't know it until spring. I knew you as a corpse in Copperlane, a loose end, a map that needed finding. That's what I was, then. A man who saw people as ends and means." The moths circled him, unbothered by the cold, landing briefly on his sleeve like small pale thoughts. "I want you to know what became of your map. It saved the realms. Your heir — she's not what either court expected. She's better. Stubborn. Kind in a way that costs her. She feeds people, Fenwick. That's her magic, underneath everything else — she feeds people, and they follow her."

The stone pulsed. Warmth washed over him, brief as sunlight through cloud, and he chose to believe it was answer rather than imagination. Belief was a discipline like any other; he was old enough to learn new ones.

"Nell knows," he said. "Your daughter. The letters reached her in midsummer. She wrote back — did you know? The couriers brought her reply to the records room. She said—" and here his voice betrayed him, cracking on the ordinary words, "—she said thank you for finishing her father's road. She has a son. He shows signs of the gift. Under the new law, he'll be taught, not taken. You did that. Whatever else the world remembers or forgets, you did that."

He stayed until true dark, kneeling in the grass, telling the stone — telling the man — about the year. About the charm-law schools. About Maren and her corrections. About the king, who lived now in comfortable exile on a distant island estate, spared the axe because Juniper had argued that a realm reborn should not begin with a public killing. About Tam, who ran the tenement like a general and forgave Wren slowly, grudgingly, one grudging kindness at a time, which Wren suspected was the only kind Tam dealt in.

"And I'm staying," Wren finished. "Teaching. Guarding, in the ways that don't require breaking anyone. It's poor penance. Penance usually is. But it's mine, and I'll keep it until they put me under grass, and I hope wherever you are — whichever side of whatever wall — someone is feeding you properly. I'm told that matters."

The light beneath the stone swelled, green-gold, and held — longer than a pulse this time, longer than breath — and in that glow Wren saw, or dreamed he saw, the impression of a tall figure in the stone's depths, grey-cloaked, straightening from a long stoop, turning toward the surface as if toward a window. Not a ghost. Something quieter. A recognition. *Someone is keeping the watch I kept, and he grieves rightly, and the road is finished.*

Then it faded, and the stone breathed its slow tide-breath, and the moths settled on the rowan berries like fruit made of snow.

Wren rose, stiff and cold and lighter than he had been in a year. He touched two fingers to the stone — a soldier's salute, softened into blessing — and turned toward the lamplight of the city.

Halfway down the hill, he stopped. On the road below, a rider was coming up from the south at speed, cloak flying, and beside the rider ran — no, *paced*, effortless as falling water — a figure crowned in living thorns, silver-limbed in the moonlight. Noor of the Bramble Court, far from the palace, far from the seventh stone, moving north through the dark with urgency in every line of him.

The rider drew rein beside Wren, breathless. One of Juniper's couriers, a girl from the night market he vaguely remembered arresting years ago — she didn't seem to remember, or she chose mercy.

"Master Corliss — the Queen's summons came an hour ago. Both of them. The Lady Juniper and Sir Noor both." Her eyes were wide, reflecting the stone's distant glow. "There's trouble at the far stones. Something's waking on the other side of the deep march — something older than the courts. They ride at dawn, and the Lady says—" the girl swallowed, "—she says she needs her spymaster. Those were her words. *Her* spymaster."

Wren looked back once at the first stone, breathing its patient light over the rowan berries and the small pale moths.

"Well," he said, and found that his heart, that old scarred organ, had not forgotten how to answer a call. "Then we'd best not keep the dawn waiting."

He turned toward the city, toward the tenements and the charm-shop lights and the family that had, against all sense, made room for him — and began to walk, faster, the frost crunching under his boots like the first page of a new book.
