## 1. The Thirteenth Moon Rises

The trouble with being invisible, Ada Delacroix had learned by her twenty-sixth year, was that everyone assumed you were also deaf.

"Third cask's leaking," muttered Bramble, hauling the barrel onto his shoulder with a grunt that suggested the cask weighed more than his regrets. "Been leaking since the ferry."

"Then it's been leaking for four hours and you've only now noticed," Ada said, not turning around. She stood at the mouth of the Hollowmere pass with one hand flat against the cold stone, reading it the way other people read letters. The rock told her things. Where frost had crept overnight. Where a goat had passed in the small hours, which meant a goatherd, which meant the shepherd track above was watched, or at least occupied by someone who would talk about what he'd seen over breakfast.

"Leaking brandy is still brandy," Bramble said.

"Leaking brandy is *evidence*." She turned at last and gave him a look she'd practiced on younger smugglers for years—the one that said *I am tired, I am competent, and you are neither*. "If Inquisitor Voss's people find a trail of sticky stones smelling of plum spirit all the way up the pass, we won't need to be caught. We'll have left them a map with an apology note attached."

Bramble winced. "What do we do?"

"We do what the guild has always done." Ada pulled a stopper of wax from her coat pocket, knelt, and pressed it into the cask's seam with her thumb, sealing the crack as neatly as a seamstress closing a hem. "We become boring. Boring men don't get hanged. Boring men are just farmers moving farm things on farm roads, and no one has ever burned a farmer for carrying too much brandy."

"Except that time in Lowmarsh," offered Pip, the youngest of them, who carried the lantern and considered himself its sovereign.

"That time in Lowmarsh was a *tax dispute*, Pip, and the man was hanged for unpaid tariffs, not for boredom. There's a difference, and the difference is paperwork." She rose, brushed the frost from her knee, and pointed up into the dark. "Shepherd track's got eyes on it tonight. We take the Widow's Stair instead."

Pip made a sound like a kettle deciding against boiling. "The Widow's Stair? That's—Miss Delacroix, respectfully, that path hasn't been used since before I was born. Sorrel says it fell in."

"Sorrel says lots of things fell in." Ada smiled, and there was something in the smile that made even Bramble straighten up. "Sorrel also once told me the cellar under the Gilded Hart flooded every spring, right up until the year I found the dry passage behind the wine racks that runs half a mile to the tanner's yard. Roads don't die, Pip. They just get shy. And shy roads are the best kind, because nobody's looking for them."

She started up, and they followed, because that was the arrangement. It had always been the arrangement, as far back as anyone in Thornfell could remember: the Delacroix woman walked first, and the road agreed to be walked.

* * *

The Widow's Stair earned its name honestly—a spine of switchbacks cut into the mountain's flank so narrow in places that two people could not stand abreast, with a drop on the left side deep enough that a dropped coin never announced its landing. Ada climbed it the way she did everything: without hurry, without wasted motion, and with a running commentary delivered just loudly enough to keep the others' minds off the drop.

"Left foot here, where the lichen grows in a little beard. Not there—that stone sings when you step on it, and I don't mean musically. Pip, lantern low. If you must swing it, swing it toward the cliff face, not the valley. Light travels farther than gossip in this country, and gossip already travels scandalously far."

"How do you know all this?" Pip panted, somewhere behind her. "Who walked it before?"

"My mother walked it. Her mother before her." Ada reached the shelf where the path widened, and paused—not because she needed to rest, but because the words had come out more easily than such words usually did, and she wanted to look at the valley while saying the rest. "There's a book. Well, not a book exactly. A ledger. Guild property, older than the guildmaster's chair, older than the guildmaster's teeth, and he has very old teeth. Every hidden road in Thornfell is written in it, and every Delacroix for six generations has added to it. My mother's hand is in there. Mine will be too, someday."

"And your father's?" Bramble asked, carefully casual, the way men asked questions they'd been sitting on for years.

Ada looked out at the valley, where the lights of three villages glimmered like embers raked apart, and said nothing at all, which was itself an answer, and an old one, and everyone knew better than to press it.

They made the top of the Stair an hour past midnight and came down through the birch stands on the far side, where the trees grew pale and close together and the ground was soft enough to swallow footsteps whole. By the grey hour before dawn they had delivered the brandy to the miller at Coldbrook—who paid in coin and bread and a lecture about the state of the world—and taken on the return cargo: salt, needles, a crate of books for the shrine-keeper's daughter, and, wrapped in sacking at the bottom of a grain sack, a bolt of red silk that Ada chose not to ask about, because asking about things was how smugglers became witnesses, and witnesses were visible.

It was on the return leg, cutting across the high moor to avoid the toll bridge at Stoneford, that they came upon the shrine.

* * *

Everyone in Thornfell knew the shrine on the moor, and everyone pretended not to. It was a ruin, officially—one of the dozens of little way-shrines the old faith had raised along the roads in the years before the Edict, when travelers still stopped to leave offerings for whatever god kept the passes. This one had belonged to Noor, though hardly anyone remembered that anymore; the name-stone at its heart had been defaced so long ago that the erosion had finished what the chisels started. What remained was a ring of standing stones, a broken altar, and a roofless cell where wind came to think its thoughts.

Guild law said: walk past. Don't look. Don't touch. The Edict had made shrines dangerous, and the guild survived by being the least interesting thing in any given mile.

But the wind had come around sharp from the north, and Pip was shivering, and the shrine's broken walls broke the wind beautifully, and Ada told herself that sheltering in a ruin was not the same as praying in one.

"Just until the light," she said. "Nobody speaks above a whisper. Nobody touches the stones."

They settled into the lee of the walls. Bramble produced bread and hard cheese and divided it with the fairness of a man who had once lost a finger to an unfair division of food. Pip curled against his pack. Ada sat with her back to the defaced name-stone, ate her share, and let the quiet settle over her like a coat.

That was when she heard the whispering.

Not Pip—Pip snored. Not Bramble, who chewed like a millstone. The whisper came from beyond the broken wall, thin and cracked and desperate, and Ada was on her feet and moving before she'd decided to move, pressing herself into the shadow of the doorway, one hand signaling the others to stillness.

An old woman knelt at the altar. She was rag-wrapped and snow-dusted, and she had clearly walked a long way, because her boots were wrong for the moor—lowland boots, valley boots—and her hands, pressed flat on the broken stone, were shaking.

"*Please,*" the old woman whispered, to a god three generations outlawed, at a shrine three generations dead. "*Please. My son. He went into the mines at Greyhollow and the shafts closed and they say there's nothing to be done, they say leave him, they say—please. You used to listen. They told me you used to listen. Please.*"

Ada should have stepped out. Should have cleared her throat, frightened the poor creature off, reminded her gently that kneeling at old altars was how villages ended up on lists. That was the protocol. That was survival.

Instead she stood in the shadow and listened, because some part of her—the part that kept the ledger, the part that was her mother's daughter—wanted to know if anyone still spoke to the dark.

And the dark answered.

It began as warmth. Ada felt it before she saw anything—a pressure against her skin like standing near an oven door, sudden and enormous, in a night that had been biting cold. The old woman gasped. And above the broken ring of stones, in the black belly of the sky between the clouds, a sigil bloomed.

It was made of fire, or of something that behaved like fire but burned without smoke, without heat, without sound—a wheel of white-gold flame hanging over the moor, vast as a harvest moon, turning slowly. At its center burned a shape Ada had seen only once before, inked in faded pigment on the oldest page of the guild ledger: a circle crossed by seven lines, like a wheel with seven spokes, like a cage with seven bars, like a star that had been *bound*.

The sigil held for the length of three heartbeats. In that time Ada felt—there was no other word for it—*attention*. Something enormous and starving had heard a whisper in the dark and turned its whole blind face toward it, the way a man lost in a cellar turns toward a crack of light.

Then it was gone, and the sky was only sky, and the cold rushed back in like water filling a footprint.

The old woman knelt where she had knelt, weeping, laughing, whispering *thank you, thank you* to the empty air. And Ada stood in the doorway of a ruined shrine with her heart slamming against her ribs, understanding two things with terrible clarity.

First: the prayer had been answered. Actually answered. Not by coincidence, not by weather, not by any trick of the honest world.

Second: the answer had been *wrong*. A burning sigil in the sky over open moor, bright enough to be seen from Stoneford, from Coldbrook, from the high shepherd tracks—from everywhere. A god had heard a whisper and replied by shouting, and gods who shouted were gods who had forgotten how to whisper, and gods who had forgotten how to whisper—

Gods who had forgotten how to whisper got people burned.

"Miss Delacroix?" Bramble was beside her, having followed silent as a man who'd spent thirty years following silent. His face was grey in the pre-dawn light. "Tell me you saw that."

"I saw it."

"What was it?"

Ada looked at the old woman, still kneeling, still thanking. She looked at the defaced stone at the altar's heart, and for the first time in her life she wondered—not idly, not academically, but with the cold drop of a trapdoor opening beneath her—what name had been chiseled away, and why the guild ledger marked this shrine with a small symbol in the margin: a circle crossed by seven lines, annotated in her great-grandmother's hand with a single word.

*Warden.*

"It was a mistake," Ada said quietly. "Somebody made a very large mistake, and I intend to find out whose."

Behind them, unnoticed by all three smugglers, the old woman rose from her knees and began the long walk home, and where her tears had fallen on the broken altar stone, the frost had melted into seven small, perfect circles, arranged like a wheel.

* * *

They made Coldbrook's edge by full light, and Ada sent Pip ahead with the cargo and kept Bramble back with a hand on his sleeve.

"You'll speak of this to no one," she said. "Not to the miller, not to your wife, not to the priest if he asks you directly whether the sky caught fire last night. Especially not to the priest."

"The priest asks me everything," Bramble said mournfully. "He's very thorough since the new one came. Father Anselm. Talks about purity a great deal. Has opinions about card games."

"Then lose at cards cheerfully and know nothing about the sky. Can you do that?"

"I can do that." He hesitated, shifting his pack. "You're going to Sorrel."

It wasn't a question, but she answered it anyway. "I'm going to Sorrel."

"He won't tell you anything. He never tells anyone anything. Man's built like a locked cellar."

"Yes," Ada said, and thought of dry passages behind wine racks, of roads that didn't die but only got shy, of a word in a dead woman's handwriting beside a burning wheel. "But I've been picking his locks my whole life. He taught me how. He just doesn't remember teaching me the important ones."

She left Bramble at the crossroads and took the last miles alone, down through the birches, along a stream that ran backward to the town, through a gap in a wall that existed on no map and appeared on no survey, because Ada's great-grandmother had bribed the surveyor and her grandmother had bribed the surveyor's son, and invisibility, the Delacroix women liked to say, was not a talent but a *maintenance schedule*.

Thornfell woke around her as she walked—shutters banging open, bread-smoke rising, the first carts creaking toward market. Ordinary sounds. Ordinary town. And yet now she saw it differently, the way you see a room differently after learning the house has a second floor: every shuttered window might hide a warden's descendant. Every ignored ruin might be a station on a road she'd never known existed. Three generations of careful, profitable, comfortable lying, buried under her feet like the passages under the Gilded Hart.

The guildhall stood where it had always stood, squat and unremarkable behind the cooper's yard, its sign painted to read DELACROIX & SONS, FREIGHT AND CARTAGE, which was funny twice over, since there were no sons and very little freight.

Sorrel was in his office, doing what he did every morning: losing an argument with a ledger. He was seventy if he was a day, built like a rain barrel, with eyebrows that had gone entirely white and independent of each other, and he looked up when she entered with the expression of a man who had been dreading precisely this visit and was disappointed only that it hadn't come later.

"You're up early," he said.

"The sky was on fire," Ada said. "Over the moor. Over Noor's shrine, if that ruined heap on the high road ever had another name, and I think it did, because I've read page forty-one of the ledger you keep chained in your cellar, the one you told me was a list of grain prices."

Sorrel set down his pen. Very carefully. The way a man sets down a pen when his hands want to do something else with it.

For a long moment the only sound was the cooper's mallet next door, knocking staves into place, building something round and sound and whole.

"Sit down, Ada," said Guildmaster Sorrel, and his voice was not the voice of a man about to lie. It was the voice of a man who had been carrying something heavy for thirty years and had just heard the floorboards start to crack. "And shut the door. It's time you knew what our family has been guarding—and why the thirteenth moon is the worst possible moon for it to wake."
