## 2. What the Guild Buried

The sigil had faded by morning, but the mountain hadn't forgotten it.

Ada felt it in small ways as she walked the high road back toward Thornfell: a stillness in the air that should have held wind, birds taking a different line across the valley than they'd flown her whole life, and once—she would swear to this later—a moment when her own shadow lagged half a step behind her boots, like a dog unsure whether to follow.

She told herself it was exhaustion. She'd been awake for thirty hours guiding the Merrow wool run through the passes, and even the steadiest mind wobbled at the end of a run. But she was Ada Delacroix, and Ada Delacroix did not wobble. She catalogued. She filed. She kept the guild invisible.

So she catalogued: one burning sigil over a ruined shrine, shaped like nothing she knew and yet familiar the way your own name is familiar; one prayer—*hers*, whispered without thinking, more habit than faith; and one answer, immediate and impossible.

Three items. None of them fit anywhere.

Thornfell proper came into view by late afternoon, all slate roofs and chimney smoke tucked into the fold of the hills like a coin hidden in a palm. To anyone passing through, it was a market town of no consequence—wool, tin, apples, and a great deal of gossip about other towns. Nobody looked twice. That was the point. That had always been the point. The guild's whole art was being overlooked, and Ada had spent twenty-nine years perfecting it, first as Sorrel's errand girl, then as his right hand, then—though the title was never spoken aloud—as the person who decided which roads existed and which did not.

She went straight for the Counting House, which was not a counting house at all but a tannery with excellent acoustics and a cellar full of ledgers no tax assessor would ever be permitted to see.

Sorrel was where he always was on a rest day: in the back room, feet on a barrel, arguing with Tamsin the cooper about whether last quarter's numbers were "creative" or "a crime."

"They're *aspirational*," Tamsin was saying as Ada pushed through the door.

"They're fiction," said Sorrel, "with good posture." He was sixty-odd and built like a knot in old timber, gray-bearded, missing two fingers on his left hand from an incident he described differently every time he told it. When he saw Ada's face, he stopped smiling mid-way, which for Sorrel was practically a scream.

"Bad run?" he asked.

"Good run," Ada said. "Clean roads, no soldiers, paid in full." She set her pack down carefully, the way you set down something that might go off. "But there was a sign."

Tamsin glanced between them and had the sense to leave, though not before muttering that aspirational was a legitimate accounting philosophy.

"Sign," Sorrel repeated.

"A burning sigil. Over the shrine at Hollowmere. It answered a prayer." She heard how it sounded and hated it. "I know how it sounds."

"Sit down," Sorrel said.

"I don't want to sit down."

"Ada." And there was something in his voice she'd heard maybe three times in her life—the night the Vell ferry burned, the night her mother died, and once more she couldn't place. "Sit down."

She sat. He poured two cups of plum brandy from the bottle he kept behind the false ledger shelf, gave her the fuller cup, and drank his own in one swallow, which was also unlike him. Sorrel sipped. Sorrel savored. Sorrel performed moderation the way other men performed piety.

"Tell me exactly," he said.

So she did. The shrine, the crack in the altar stone, the prayer she'd made half-laughing—"old gods, if you're listening, give me a sign"—and the sky opening like a struck bell. She described the sigil as best she could: interlocking lines, like a knot drawn by someone who understood knots too well, burning white-gold and utterly silent.

When she finished, Sorrel was looking at the table.

"You've seen it before," Ada said. "Haven't you."

"No," said Sorrel. Then, because he was honest in the way that cost him most: "Not burning. In ink. Once. A long time ago."

"Where?"

He got up, checked the door, checked the window, and then did something she had never once seen him do in twenty years: he moved the brandy bottle aside and lifted the floorboard beneath it. From the cavity he took a flat iron box, unlocked it with a key from around his neck, and drew out a piece of vellum so old it had gone the color of weak tea.

On it, drawn in faded silver, was her sigil. Exactly. Every line.

The room got very quiet. Somewhere above them, the tannery vats creaked through their afternoon rotation, and the smell of oak bark and lime drifted down, ordinary and grounding, while Ada's whole understanding of her life quietly changed shape.

"What is this?" she asked.

"It's called the Warden's Mark," Sorrel said. "And this—" he tapped the box, heavy with whatever else it held, "—is what's left of what we used to be."

* * *

He talked for a long time. He talked the way he taught routes: slowly, in pieces, making her earn each turn.

"We weren't always smugglers," he began. "Or rather—we were always smugglers, but smuggling was the coat, not the body. Three hundred years ago, this guild was founded to keep something safe. Not goods. Not gold." He turned the vellum toward the lamplight. "A covenant. Between the mortal realm and the divine one. You've heard the stories—gods walking among us, answering prayers, demanding worship. Most of that is embroidery. But the thread underneath is real. There was an agreement. Terms. The gods could hear us, answer us, draw strength from our devotion—and in exchange they were bound. Bounded. Kept from doing what gods do when nobody holds their leash."

"Bounded how?"

"That's the part I don't have. The terms were written somewhere I've never seen, sealed somewhere I've never been. What I have are fragments. My predecessor's predecessor's notes. Half a ledger. This." He laid out the contents of the box one item at a time, like a man laying out tools before surgery: the vellum; a scrap of ledger page in a spidery hand listing *tithes received, thresholds maintained, keystone secure*; and a small stone disc, cracked clean through, carved with the same knotting pattern as the sigil.

"The covenant broke," Ada said. It wasn't a question. Everyone knew the gods had gone silent—everyone's grandmother had a story about the last miracle, usually involving a sainted aunt or a well that healed warts.

"Three generations ago. Before my time. My master, Old Brannoc, told me it broke suddenly, like a rope parting under load. One day the gods were listening; the next, silence. The order that kept the terms scattered or died or—worse—decided silence was safer and buried everything." His jaw worked. "Including us. We were told to become invisible. To forget on purpose. The ones who wouldn't forget were... encouraged to retire early."

"And you just *obeyed?*" Ada heard her own voice rise and didn't care. "You found out the guild was founded to guard a pact between mortals and gods, and you just—kept smuggling wool?"

"What else was I going to do?" Sorrel spread his hands. "I was a young man with eight fingers and a debt. Brannoc handed me a broken thing and told me to keep it quiet, and quiet was the only skill I had. I kept the box. I kept the mark. I taught every route my master taught me—including six that make no sense unless you're moving something that isn't cargo." He tapped the map wall, where decades of routes hung in colored thread. "Why do you think the Shepherd's Cut exists? No toll road runs parallel. No village needs it. It exists because a threshold needed maintaining, and maintaining meant someone walking it, regularly, for centuries. We thought we were keeping old paths open out of sentiment. We were keeping watch. We just didn't know what for."

Ada stared at the threads crisscrossing the wall. All those years learning them. All those nights memorizing blind turns and false summits, believing she was mastering her grandfather's craft, her mother's craft, *her* craft—and it had never been craft at all. It had been liturgy wearing a smuggler's coat.

"The omen," she said. "Last night. If the covenant broke, why is anything answering prayers now?"

"I don't know." He said it plainly, no performance of wisdom. "Brannoc wrote that when the covenant broke, the gods didn't die. They starved. Silence goes both ways, Ada. They couldn't hear us—but we couldn't feed them either. Whatever's up there now has been starving for three generations, and last night, something heard a prayer for the first time since your grandmother was a girl." He met her eyes. "A starving thing heard a voice and answered. Think about what that means."

Ada thought about it, and didn't like any of the shapes it made.

"There's one more thing," Sorrel said, and here his voice changed, went careful, the way it did when he was about to quote a price he knew she'd hate. "The ledger fragment. *Keystone secure.* The covenant wasn't just words and boundaries. It had a keystone. An anchor. Something living, held in trust, passed down a bloodline. Without it, the terms can't hold even if the gods wanted to honor them. Without it..." He hesitated.

"Without it, what?"

"Without it, there's nothing to reforge. Nothing to rebuild with. The covenant doesn't just stay broken—it stays *unfixable*. And the gods stay starving. Forever." He closed the box. "Which brings me to the question you're already asking, because you're your mother's daughter and you ask questions like drawing knives."

"Sorrel." Her heart had begun to beat somewhere up near her teeth. "What role did my family play?"

* * *

He refused.

That was the thing she couldn't get past, later, turning it over in the dark. Not that he lied—he didn't lie, that was almost worse. He simply declined, with the gentle immovability of a boulder, to answer.

"My mother's name is in that ledger," she said. "I saw it. Third column. *Delacroix, M., attested.* Attested to *what*, Sorrel?"

"Ada—"

"You've known me since I was nine years old. You taught me to read a ridge line and pick a lock and lie to a customs man with a straight face. You stood at my mother's funeral next to me and held my hand, and you knew—didn't you. You knew what she was. What I am." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated that too. "Attested. Keystone. Those aren't random words. You looked at me my whole life and you *knew*, and you said nothing."

Sorrel sat very still. For a moment she thought he might actually tell her. His mouth opened; his hand went to the iron box; and then—she watched it happen—something older and stronger than honesty closed over his face like water over a stone.

"Some things," he said quietly, "are heavier than secrets. Some things are burdens. Your mother made me promise, before the fever took her, that you would live your life without carrying it until carrying it became unavoidable. Last night, a god answered a prayer over a ruin. That makes it unavoidable that *something* is coming. It does not yet make it unavoidable that it's coming for *you*. When it does—if it does—I'll tell you everything. Every word I have. On my life."

"Your life," Ada repeated. "That's supposed to comfort me?"

"It's supposed to bind me." He met her eyes, and for once there was nothing jocular in him at all, nothing of the genial fence-of-the-neighborhood guildmaster. Just an old man who had kept a terrible promise for thirty years and was tired all the way down. "I loved your mother. I love you. And I am telling you, as the only true thing I can give you tonight: the less you know right now, the longer you stay alive. There are people who killed to bury what's in that box. If they learn a Delacroix is asking questions—"

"Then let them come," Ada snapped. "I've spent my whole life being invisible for people who never told me why. Maybe it's time somebody saw me."

The words came out fiercer than she intended, and truer. Sorrel flinched as if she'd thrown them.

They sat in silence after that. The tannery vats groaned overhead. Down in the town, evening bells rang the hour, and lamplighters worked their rounds, and Thornfell went on performing its smallness for the world, and Ada sat in the cellar beneath it all feeling like the floor had been pulled out and replaced with something shaped almost exactly like a floor.

Because that was the worst of it—not the covenant, not the gods, not even the danger. It was the arithmetic of it. Twenty years of trust, and it summed to this: the foundation of her identity was a coat over a duty she'd never agreed to, stitched by dead women, enforced by the man she loved best in the world.

"I need air," she said, standing.

"Ada." Sorrel rose too, awkward, hands half-lifted like a man approaching a frightened animal. "Whatever you decide to do with this—whatever anger you carry—remember one thing. The guild kept the roads open. Through plague, through war, through the silence. Somebody walked those thresholds every month for three hundred years so that when this night came, there would still be *paths*. Whatever else we were, we were faithful. Even blind."

"Blind faith," Ada said at the door. "Is there any other kind?"

* * *

She walked the walls until midnight.

It was an old habit—when her head got loud, she walked the rooftops and wall-tops of Thornfell, the routes no customs man knew, and let the town's small sounds settle her. Tonight it didn't work. Every lantern looked like a vigil candle. Every shadow under the eaves looked like a place a sigil might burn.

She ended up, without quite deciding to, at the shrine of the Twin Sisters on Bellows Lane—the little working shrine, wax-crusted, where townsfolk left copper coins for safe births and fair winds. As a child she'd prayed here with her mother. She could almost hear the lullaby her mother used to hum on these steps, some tune about a river and a door, the words worn away by time.

Ada stood in the dark and looked at the little altar, and found herself afraid of it. Afraid of a bowl of copper coins and a stub of tallow candle. Because last night she'd learned that prayers were letters that actually got delivered, and she did not know what she'd been saying all these years, or to whom, or what a starving listener might do with the sound of her voice.

"Don't," she told herself. "You're Ada Delacroix. You don't frighten."

But Ada Delacroix, it turned out, had been a story someone else had written, and she was only now holding the pen.

She was still standing there, jaw set, arguing with herself, when she noticed the candle.

She hadn't lit it. The shrine keeper banked the candles at dusk; she'd watched him do it a thousand times. Yet the stub on the altar burned—steadily, in windless dark, with a flame that cast light the wrong color. Not orange. White-gold. The exact shade of a sigil burning over a ruin.

And as she watched, the flame bent—slowly, deliberately, unmistakably—toward the north. Toward the high passes. Toward the Veil.

Somewhere beyond the hills, a dog began to howl, and then another, and then the whole valley was singing with them, and Ada Delacroix stood alone in a shrine she'd known all her life and understood, with total clarity, that hiding was over.

The gods had remembered the way in.
