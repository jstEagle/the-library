## 3. Ash on the Wind

The trouble with answered prayers, Ada would later reflect, was that nobody had asked for them.

She was three days out of Thornfell with a mule named Duchess and a cargo of salted eel, lamp oil, and—because Sorrel insisted on keeping up appearances even as the guild starved—one crate of fine Vellish wine bound for a widow in Lowmarsh who paid promptly and complained beautifully. The roads were hers. Every switchback and sheep-track from the Thornfell passes down to the river country lived in her head like a second skeleton, and she moved through them the way other people moved through their own houses: without thinking, without fear, without ever once bumping into a wall.

Which is why it unsettled her so deeply when the road itself began to misbehave.

It started small. A footpath she'd walked since she was nine had grown an extra bend overnight—a gentle curve where there should have been a straight run between two boulders, as if someone had taken the landscape by both ends and wrung it slightly. She stopped, set down Duchess's lead, and stared at it for a long moment.

"You see this too?" she asked the mule.

Duchess, who was a pragmatist with ears, ate some grass.

Ada backtracked half a mile and came at the path again. The bend remained. Worse, when she crested the hill beyond it, she could see the valley below—and the village of Harrow's End sitting exactly where it ought to sit, except that its church spire was wrong. Not damaged. *Wrong.* It had been a squat stone tower her whole life, topped with a slate cap. Now it rose slender and pale, crowned with something that caught the morning light like bone.

"Right," Ada said to no one. "That's not good."

* * *

Harrow's End smelled of woodsmoke and panic.

She led Duchess down the main street past doors standing open, past a woman scrubbing the same clean doorstep over and over, past three children sitting in a row on a fence rail with the perfect stillness of children who have been told not to move and are obeying with their whole souls. Nobody questioned a smuggler with a mule. In hard times, people who arrive carrying things are assumed to be help until proven otherwise.

The inn—the Drowned Ox, which had always struck Ada as an ill-chosen name for a place forty miles from any water deep enough to drown anything larger than a duck—was full of people not drinking. They sat around tables with untouched cups while a man in a farmer's smock stood near the hearth and told his story for what was clearly not the first time.

"—and I only said it once," he was insisting. "Once! You don't kneel in your own field and beg unless you mean it, and I meant it."

"What did you say?" someone asked. "Exactly?"

The farmer—broad, sunburnt, hands like shovels—looked down at those hands as if they belonged to a stranger. "I said, *Lords of the sky, we've had no rain in six weeks. Send us water or we'll lose everything.*" He swallowed. "That's all. That's all I said."

"And then?" This from a sharp-faced woman at the corner table, whom Ada recognized distantly as the schoolmistress, Mistress Corren.

"And then," the farmer said, "it rained."

"Well," said someone, with the desperate cheer of a man grasping at normalcy, "that's a blessing, isn't it? Six weeks dry, and the old gods finally—" He stopped, because everyone else's face had already told him the rest of the story.

"It didn't stop," the farmer whispered. "Three days. Three days and nights. The millrace burst. Took the lower fields, took the barley, took the bridge. Took Tam." His voice cracked on the last word, and Ada understood without being told that Tam was not a field or a bridge. "I asked for water. I got the whole sky let down on my head. And now every night I can hear it—that sound, under the ground, like something enormous turning over in its sleep, and I think, *it's still listening. It heard me once and it's listening for me to ask again.*"

Silence held the room. Then, softly, Mistress Corren said: "Show me your hands."

The farmer held them out. Across both palms, faint as old scars, ran lines of silver—not ink, not ash, but something that shimmered when it caught the firelight, tracing the creases of his skin like veins in marble.

"Mine too," said the woman who'd been scrubbing her doorstep, appearing in the doorway with her hands held out like an accusation. "Since the night my husband prayed for our boy's fever to break. The fever broke. The boy woke up healthy as a foal. And these appeared, and now everywhere I look I see—" Her voice wobbled. "I see things moving at the edge of things. Shadows with intentions."

Ada stood very still by the door, her hand resting on Duchess's neck, and felt the careful architecture of her life shift another inch toward collapse.

Prayers were supposed to go nowhere. That was the arrangement. That had been the arrangement for three generations—you spoke to the sky because speaking was human, and the sky kept its silence because the sky was dead, or sleeping, or gone, and everyone agreed not to examine which. Sorrel had told her enough, that terrible night after the burning sigil, to know the silence had been built rather than natural. A covenant. A wall. Wardens whose job was to keep the door shut.

And now the door was open a crack, and something on the other side was reaching through, blind and hungry, grabbing whatever prayers came near and crushing them in its fist like fruit.

"I'm looking for work," Ada announced to the room, because a smuggler who stands silent in a doorway too long becomes interesting, and interesting was dangerous. "Salted eel, lamp oil, and one crate of genuinely excellent wine. Also, if anyone needs goods carried anywhere far away, quickly, and without questions, I'm your woman."

It broke the tension the way a stone breaks a window. People surged toward her crates. The farmer bought lamp oil he did not need just to have something to do with his hands. And when the crowd thinned, Mistress Corren drifted over, sharp-eyed, and said quietly:

"You're not from the order."

"No."

"But you know something. I watched your face during that man's story. Everyone else looked frightened. You looked like a woman doing arithmetic." She tilted her head. "Who are you, really?"

"A merchant," Ada said, "of flexible inventory."

"Mm," said Mistress Corren, who had clearly spent thirty years extracting confessions from children and found adults easier. "Well, merchant. If you're heading toward the Veil country—and your mule's shoes are shod for mountain work, so don't lie to me about that—there are four more villages up there with stories like Brann's. Rain, fire, a well in Coldfern that started granting wishes. One girl wished to be beautiful and woke up beautiful and hasn't spoken since; she just sits by the window and cries, and her mother says she doesn't remember being anyone else." The schoolmistress's voice stayed level, but her knuckles were white around her cup. "Whatever's answering, it isn't wicked. That's the thing nobody wants to say out loud. It answers *exactly*. Like a child doing sums. Like something that has forgotten how to understand us and is trying very, very hard."

Ada thought of the burning sigil over the shrine. Of Sorrel's face, gray as porridge, saying *the guild were wardens, girl, and we stopped warding.*

"Something like that," she said. "Keep your people indoors at dusk. Don't pray. Don't wish. Don't even hope loudly."

"And you? What will you do?"

Ada looked out the window toward the mountains, where the Veil lay hidden somewhere in those high gray shoulders, and felt the road pull at her like a current.

"What I always do," she said. "Take the hidden paths."

* * *

She meant to pass through Coldfern by night and be gone before dawn. Instead she arrived at midday, because the roads had other opinions now.

The extra bend outside Harrow's End had been a warning shot. By the time she reached the high country, the landscape had begun rearranging itself with the cheerful lawlessness of a dream. A ravine she'd crossed a hundred times via a fallen pine trunk simply wasn't there anymore—in its place stood a meadow, green and impossible, dotted with wildflowers that did not grow at this altitude. An hour's detour brought her to a stream flowing uphill. When she finally picked her way down into Coldfern's valley, she did it along a path she had never seen before and yet somehow knew, the way you know a tune you're certain you've never heard.

The village sat in a bowl of terraced fields, and half of it was burned.

Not destroyed—burned, precisely, surgically, as though fire had been applied by a careful hand to exactly the buildings that needed burning and nothing else. The granary: gone. The tannery, whose reek had offended Coldfern for a century: gone. The house of a man named Aldous Pike, who by all accounts was a miser and a bully: a smoking rectangle of foundation. Around the edges of each burn, chalk-white handprints marked the walls of the surviving houses, dozens of them, overlapping, like a congregation pressing palms against the world.

"They wished," said the old woman who'd found Ada standing in the square staring. Her name was Maret; she fed Duchess apples and talked the way lonely people talk, in a continuous stream with no expectation of response. "That's how it started here. Somebody wished, and next morning there's a handprint on their door, white as milk. And then the wishes start coming true. Old Pike used to beat his apprentices. One night the whole village, every soul, went to bed wishing that man would get what was coming to him." She offered Duchess another apple. "Fire took his house at midnight. Him in it. Nothing else touched. Not even his neighbor's fence."

"And the handprints?"

"Our thanks," Maret said simply. "Or our apology. We haven't decided which." She squinted up at Ada. "You've got guild boots on, girl. Delacroix routes?"

Ada went cold. "You know me?"

"I knew your grandmother." Maret's eyes crinkled. "She carried salt through here the winter of the great snows, when the pass was closed and everyone said it couldn't be done. Sat where you're sitting. Ate my stew. Said the same thing you're about to say, which is 'how do you know my grandmother,' and the answer is the same too: because she was kind when kindness cost something, and people remember." She patted Ada's arm with a hand like a dried leaf. "Your family's been walking these roads longer than the roads have had names. So tell me, Delacroix. You're the ones who kept the old bargains. What's happening to us?"

And there it was—the question Sorrel wouldn't finish, wearing a stranger's face, waiting patiently with a basket of apples.

"I don't know yet," Ada said. It was the most honest thing she'd said in years, and it tasted strange. "But I'm going to find out."

* * *

She stayed the night in Coldfern because Maret fed her stew and because the alternative was the dark hills, and the hills had started making sounds at night—a low, vast exhalation, rhythmic, almost breathing, coming from the direction of the Veil.

Near midnight, unable to sleep, she climbed to the ridge above the village and looked north.

The moon hung fat and low, one turn shy of its thirteenth. And beneath it, in the high country where no fires should burn, the Veil's approach glimmered—not with flame but with a soft, sourceless luminescence, like moonlight pooling in a bowl. As she watched, the glow pulsed. Once. Twice. Slow, patient, hungry.

And Ada understood—or began to, standing alone on the ridge with the wind pulling at her hair—with the particular horror of a person who realizes the monster in the story has been starving in a cage the whole time.

The gods weren't invading. They weren't punishing. They were *famished.* Three generations sealed behind a covenant built to keep mortals safe from divine whim—and divine whim, apparently, ran on mortal prayer the way mills ran on water. Cut off from worship, they'd dwindled in the dark, and now the wall was cracking, and everything leaking through was desperate, mindless appetite. A farmer begged for rain and received a flood, because whatever heard him had forgotten what rain was *for* and remembered only that it was wanted. A child wished to be beautiful and lost herself, because beauty was given without asking what it would displace.

They were like dogs gone feral after years chained—still aching for the hand that fed them, no longer able to tell it from the whip.

Ada sat down on the cold rock and put her face in her hands, and discovered, to her considerable irritation, that she was crying. Not for herself. For the thing in the dark, starving and groping and trying so hard, breaking everything it touched because it had forgotten how to hold anything gently.

"Someone has to feed you properly," she said to the distant glow, "or put you down kindly. And either way—" she laughed wetly, wiped her nose on her sleeve, "—either way it's going to be me, isn't it. Because it's always me. Because my family built the stupid wall."

Behind her, on the path down to Coldfern, a figure stood where no figure had been a moment before. Tall, travel-worn, silhouetted against the village lights, watching her with the stillness of someone who has been listening for a long time.

"That," the stranger said pleasantly, "depends entirely on whether you take the right companion."

Ada was on her feet with a knife in her hand before the sentence finished—which, she would later admit, was the correct response, and also completely useless.
