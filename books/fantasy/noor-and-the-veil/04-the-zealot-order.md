## 4. The Zealot Order

The smoke reached Ada before the news did.

She was in the counting room of the Drowned Lantern, haggling with a wool merchant over tariffs he had no idea he was paying, when the smell drifted through the shutters—pine pitch and something sweeter underneath, like honey left too long on a hot stove. She knew that smell. Everyone in Thornfell knew it from childhood bonfires.

But this was not a bonfire season.

"Finish the ledger," she told the merchant, whose name was Pellam and whose arithmetic was a crime against numbers. "You'll pay eight percent on the northern route or you can explain to your wife why the winter coats are sitting in a warehouse in Lowmark."

"You're a tyrant," Pellam said fondly.

"I'm a bargain." She swept out before he could argue further.

The smoke was coming from Chapel Row—or what Thornfell called Chapel Row, which was really just four shrines and a leaning bell tower that hadn't rung since anyone's grandmother was young. By the time Ada reached the corner of Tanner's Way, a crowd had gathered at a respectful distance, the way crowds do when they want to watch something terrible but don't want to be blamed for enjoying it.

The shrine of the Threshold Mother was burning.

It was a small thing, that shrine—a stone alcove with a carved lintel worn smooth by centuries of travelers' hands, a niche where people left buttons and ribbons and, if they were feeling generous, actual coins. Ada had passed it ten thousand times. She'd once hidden a stolen signet ring behind its altar for six weeks while a magistrate's men searched her mother's house.

Now three men in grey-and-white stood around it watching it burn, and their faces were not angry faces. They were *satisfied* faces, which was somehow worse.

At their center stood a woman in a white cassock trimmed with ash-grey, tall and straight-backed, her hair bound up so tightly it looked architectural. She held a book—not reading it, just holding it, the way a soldier holds a shield—and she watched the flames with the expression of someone checking work off a list.

"Inquisitor," someone whispered near Ada. "They say she's Inquisitor Maren Voss. From the capital."

Ada did not whisper. Ada melted backward into the crowd, then out of the crowd, then through the gap between the cooper's shop and the tannery that only existed if you were thin, determined, and had been squeezing through it since you were nine years old.

Her mind was already moving faster than her feet. One shrine burned. That was theater—that was a message nailed to a door. But an Inquisitor didn't travel alone to nail messages. An Inquisitor traveled with soldiers, writs, and lists.

And somewhere in Thornfell there was a list, and Ada Delacroix's guild was almost certainly on it.

* * *

By nightfall, the Lantern's common room had the particular silence of forty people trying very hard to look ordinary.

Ada stood behind the bar pulling ale nobody had ordered, watching the door. Sorrel sat in his usual corner pretending to be asleep, which was his way of being extremely awake. Tam the ferryman was telling a story about a fish, loudly, to an audience that wasn't listening, because Tam always talked too much when he was frightened and everyone knew it and loved him for it anyway.

The door opened at the ninth bell.

Two soldiers came in first—big, bored-looking, hands resting on sword hilts with the casualness of men who had done this fifty times and been thanked for it more often than not. Then the woman in white.

Maren Voss was younger than Ada expected. Thirty, perhaps thirty-five, with a sunburned nose and ink stains on her fingers that no amount of scrubbing would fully remove. She looked less like a zealot and more like a scholar who had wandered into the wrong story—which, Ada would learn later, was exactly what she was.

"Good evening," Maren said to the room, and her voice carried without effort. "I'm looking for smugglers."

A beat of silence. Then Tam, bless him, laughed. "Aren't we all, love? Half of Thornfell's looking for them and the other half is hiding them."

A few nervous chuckles. Maren smiled—it was a real smile, tired at the edges—and crossed to the bar.

"And which half are you?" she asked Ada.

"The thirsty half. Ale's two coppers."

Maren set a coin down. "One ale. And information, if it's free."

"Information's never free. That's the first rule of Thornfell."

"The second rule?" Maren asked, taking her ale.

"There is no second rule. We couldn't afford one."

Something flickered across the Inquisitor's face—amusement, quickly disciplined. She drank, set the mug down, and studied Ada with eyes that were pale grey and entirely unblinking. "Three nights ago, a sigil burned in the sky above the Thornfell passes. Witnesses in three villages. Two days ago, a farmer outside Millbrook prayed for rain and drowned in his own barley field. The Order of the Severed Hand has been sent to determine whether these events constitute heresy, miracle, or plague." She turned the coin on the bar with one finger. "Heresy I can burn. Plague I can quarantine. It's the third thing that worries me."

"And what's the third thing?"

"That it might be real."

The words landed in the room like a dropped glass. Ada kept her face pleasant and empty, the face of a barkeep who had heard everything and understood nothing.

"Mm," she said. "Sky-signals and weather. Sounds like a bad harvest and good gossip."

"Perhaps." Maren finished her ale. "I'll be in Thornfell for some weeks. I'm told there are old shrines in the high passes. I'm told some families here still leave offerings at them." She stood, smoothed her cassock, and left a second coin beside the first. "For the information. You gave me some after all—you told me this town protects its own. That's useful to know."

She walked out. The soldiers followed. The door swung shut, and the common room exhaled all at once, forty people breathing together like a congregation.

Sorrel appeared at Ada's elbow so fast he must have crossed the room on his knees. "Well?"

"Well," Ada said quietly, "she paid for her drink, complimented my wit, and told us exactly how much trouble we're in. She's not stupid, Sorrel. Stupid zealots I can outrun. Smart ones build roads."

"Then we'd best make sure ours stay hidden." He gripped her shoulder, and his hand was shaking. Not with fear, Ada realized. With something older. Guilt, maybe. She made a note of it and filed it away with all the other things Sorrel wouldn't tell her.

* * *

Over the next six days, the Inquisitor worked.

She worked methodically, politely, and with a thoroughness that made Ada's skin crawl. She interviewed the miller about his grandmother's habit of leaving bread at the river stone. She questioned the schoolteacher about a children's rhyme that named the Threshold Mother. She burned two more shrines—the river stone and the little moss-covered waymarker on the Lowmark road—and paid each owner of the adjacent property for the scorch damage, in full, with a receipt.

"She *pays*," Tam marveled one evening, sprawled across two chairs in the Lantern's back room. "Whoever heard of an arsonist with receipts?"

"That's what frightens me," Ada said. She was cleaning the same tankard for the fourth time. "Fanatics who enjoy cruelty are predictable. They gloat, they escalate, they get sloppy. This one thinks she's a surgeon. Surgeons cut precisely."

"And we're the—" Tam began.

"Don't finish that sentence in my establishment."

On the seventh day, the soldiers found the cache.

It was a small one—a waystation under the roots of a lightning-struck oak, holding contraband silk, three kegs of unlicensed brandy, and, tucked into a false bottom because Sorrel insisted on sentimental nonsense, a box of pre-covenant prayer tokens that the guild had confiscated from pilgrims over the years and never quite gotten around to destroying.

Ada watched from two hundred yards up the slope, flat against a granite shelf, as the soldiers laid the contents out in the snow-melt mud. Watched Maren Voss kneel and lift one of the tokens—a pressed clay disc stamped with the threshold spiral—and go very still.

Then she looked up the slope.

Not searching. Looking. Directly at the granite shelf, directly at the space where Ada lay, as though she could feel attention the way a spider feels a fly land on its web.

Ada slid backward off the shelf, rolled into the drainage gully, and was gone before the first shout went up.

* * *

They ran her that night.

Not a chase, exactly—chases were loud, and loud things got people hurt—but a long, cold game of pressure and release through the dark streets of Thornfell. Soldiers at the Tanner's Gate. Soldiers at the harbor stairs. A lantern signal from the chapel tower that meant the north roads were watched. Whoever ran the Inquisitor's network, they were good; within a week they'd mapped half the obvious ways out of town.

Fortunately, Ada had spent her whole life making sure the obvious ways were the least interesting ones.

She went out through the cooper's yard, over the brine vats, down the drain channel that smelled like a crime against the gods (the new ones and the old), and surfaced in the salt marsh beyond the palisade. From there, the marsh path—not a path, really, a sequence of hummocks and deadfalls that only held weight if you stepped on them in the right order, a rhythm more than a road, taught to her at age seven by a mother who called it "dancing." She danced the marsh in the dark, boots wet to the ankle, heart hammering a rhythm of its own.

Halfway across, crouched in the shadow of a wrecked fishing skiff, she heard the soldiers behind her reach the marsh edge and stop. Argue. One of them threw a torch out onto the flats; it hissed and died in the wet.

"Lost her," someone said, disgusted.

"No," said another voice, quieter, carrying strangely across the water. "Look at the ground. Nobody *lost* her. She was never here at all."

Ada lay in the cold mud under the skiff's ribs and breathed shallowly and thought: *that one's dangerous.* And beneath the fear, unwelcome as a splinter, a small hot coal of something else. Pride, maybe. Her invisible roads, holding. Her invisible life, holding.

For now.

* * *

Inquisitor Maren Voss did not sleep well that night, though she would have denied it fiercely if accused.

She sat in the rented room above the chandlery with her ledger open, candle burning low, and wrote by the light with the careful hand of a woman who believed records were a form of prayer. *Item: clay token, threshold spiral, recovered from cache beneath lightning-struck oak. Age indeterminate. Craftsmanship: devotional, not commercial.*

Someone had collected these. Someone had *kept* them—for decades, judging by the wear. Kept them the way misers keep coins, or the way widows keep letters.

Maren rubbed her ink-stained fingers and thought about her father.

He had been a stonemason in Corvale, and when the fever took her mother and both her brothers in a single autumn, he had carried Maren, aged nine, to the shrine of the Shepherd of Resting Fields and made her kneel on the cold stones while he begged. All night. His voice going raw. Promising anything, offering anything, laying their last silver piece on the altar like a man paying a ransom.

The god did not answer. Of course the god did not answer—the gods had been silent for three generations, everyone knew that, everyone *said* they knew that, and yet her father had knelt until dawn because knowing a thing in your head and knowing it in your knees are different knowings.

He died in the spring. Quietly, of the same fever, having spent everything they had on a prayer that fell on nothing.

Maren had been taken in by the Order, fed, educated, given a purpose, and she had repaid them with thirty years of perfect service, because the Order had told her the truth no shrine ever had: *no one is coming.* No one was ever coming. The silence overhead was not a test of faith; it was simply the truth, and every burning shrine was a mercy compared to letting people kneel all night on cold stones waiting for an answer that rotted in heaven.

Except.

Except the sky-sigil. Except the farmer in his drowned barley, whose widow had shown Maren the field herself, weeping, saying *he only wanted water for the crop, and it came, it came all at once, like something reaching up from underwater.* Except the clay token in her ledger, worn smooth by hands that had believed.

If the gods were waking—if the silence was ending—then everything her father had knelt for might suddenly be *listening again*, and Maren Voss did not know whether that was salvation or the cruelest joke the heavens had ever played.

She blew out the candle and sat in the dark.

*Somewhere in this town,* she thought, *is a person who moves through walls. Who owns the ground and lets the rest of us walk on it. Find that person, and you find the root of it.*

She did not think of the barkeep with the quick wit and the empty pleasant face. Not yet.

* * *

Ada reached the safehouse above the old tannery an hour before dawn, soaked, muddy, and grinning despite herself.

Sorrel opened the door before she knocked—he'd been listening for her rhythm on the stairs, the little double-tap she'd used since she was small. He looked at her, took in the mud, the marsh smell, the grin.

"They didn't catch you."

"They didn't even see me." She peeled off her boots and dumped them upside down; a surprising amount of marsh poured out. "Sorrel. Their maps are good but they're mapping a town that doesn't exist. They're drawing lines between doors. My roads aren't doors. They're—" she waved a hand, hunting for the word, "—they're the spaces between the lines. As long as I've got those, she can burn every shrine in Thornfell and never touch what matters."

Sorrel's face did something complicated. Relief and grief sharing a chair.

"What matters," he repeated softly.

"The guild. The routes. The people." Ada wrung out her hair. "Look—I know there's something you're not telling me. About the omen, about why the guild really keeps those old tokens, about why you flinch every time someone says 'covenant.' And I'm not asking tonight. Tonight I'm going to sleep for eleven hours. But Sorrel—" she fixed him with a look, and it was the look her mother used to give him, and he actually stepped back half an inch, "—if the Inquisitor finds it before I do, whatever 'it' is, I will never forgive either of us."

Sorrel opened his mouth. Closed it. Nodded, slow and heavy, like a door settling on old hinges.

"Get some sleep, girl," he said. "Tomorrow I'll tell you about the map."

"What map?"

But he was already shuffling away, and Ada was too tired to chase him, and besides—there would be time. There was always time. Thornfell hid its secrets the way the mountains hid their roads, patiently, generation after generation.

She slept. And in her dream, a woman in white stood at the edge of a salt marsh, holding a lantern out over the water, and the light found footprints where no one had walked.

When Ada woke, past noon, Tam was pounding on the safehouse door hard enough to crack the frame.

"Ada! Ada, wake up—they've taken Pellam. The wool merchant. They're questioning him in the chapel square right now, and he's—" Tam gulped air. "He's telling them about the counting room. He's telling them about *you*."
