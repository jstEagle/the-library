## 5. Kaelen at the Crossroads

The trouble with secret roads, Ada had learned over twenty years of keeping them, was that eventually someone else found one.

She'd known it was coming. Nothing stayed hidden forever—not the guild's routes, not the smugglers' marks on the stones, not the small carved sigil above the Hollow Gate that only looked like a knot in the wood if you didn't know to look. But knowing didn't make it easier, three days after Sorrel's half-truths had left her feeling like the ground beneath her feet was borrowed, to find boot prints on *her* road.

Not just any boots. Fresh ones. This morning's frost still clung to the shadowed side of each print, which meant whoever made them had passed before dawn—and they'd walked the Widow's Stair without once stepping on the loose scree that would announce them to anyone watching from below. That took either luck or skill, and Ada had never once met luck that careful.

She followed the prints up through the pines, hand resting easy on her knife, cataloguing as she went. The stride was long. The weight favored the right foot slightly—old injury, maybe, or habit. And at the third switchback, where the road narrowed between two leaning boulders, the stranger had stopped. Long enough to notice the guild's mark scratched into the stone. Long enough to understand it.

Because beneath it, in charcoal, someone had drawn a second mark: a circle with a line through its heart, like an eye closed by a blade.

Ada stood very still.

She knew that mark. Every child in Thornfell knew it, though most couldn't have said from where—the way you know a lullaby without remembering learning it. It was the sign of the Veilstone. It was the sign Sorrel had drawn in spilled tea three nights ago, his hands shaking, when he'd finally told her what the guild had been before it became a smuggling outfit with delusions of respectability.

*Wardens,* he'd said. *We were wardens first.*

And now someone was leaving warden-signs on her roads like invitations.

She should have turned back. She knew that too. But curiosity had always been her worst flaw and her best tool, and besides—if someone was poaching her routes, she needed to know how much they'd seen, and whether she'd need to reroute the autumn silk run, and whether she'd need to bury a body in the soft ground past the falls, which was honestly the least convenient part of the whole business.

So she kept climbing.

* * *

She heard him before she saw him—which was wrong, because nobody heard anybody on the Widow's Stair unless they wanted to be heard, and this man was humming.

Humming. On a smuggler's road. In broad morning light, sitting on the flat stone at the crossroads where the Widow's Stair split toward the salt pass and the old shrine road, with his pack off and his boots off and his bare feet dangling over a drop that had killed two guild runners in Ada's lifetime, roasting something over a fire he'd built out in the open like a man who had never once been hunted in his life.

He was younger than she expected. Late twenties, maybe. Dark hair going every direction, a jaw that hadn't seen a razor in days, and clothes that were expensive in the way that tried not to look expensive—a traveler's coat that fit him too well, boots worth more than most of Thornfell's roofs. He looked, Ada thought, like a merchant's son playing at adventure, except for the eyes. The eyes were older than the rest of him. Old in a way that made her think of the burning sigil in the sky, and the shrine that had answered a prayer with fire, and everything else she'd spent three days trying very hard not to think about.

He didn't startle when she stepped onto the stone. That was its own kind of answer.

"You're on my road," she said.

"Am I?" He smiled up at her, warm and unhurried, and turned the spit. "It didn't say so anywhere."

"Roads don't. I do." She let him see the knife. Not brandishing it—just letting it exist in the conversation. "This is Delacroix territory. Every route from the Hollow Gate to the salt pass. You've been walking them since before dawn."

"Have I?" He sounded genuinely delighted. "Then I owe you thanks. They're beautiful roads. I've never seen anything maintained so carefully by people who officially don't exist." He pulled the spit free, blew on the bird—some poor grouse that had picked the wrong morning—and held it out toward her. "Grouse? It's the least I can do for trespassing."

"I don't share meals with strangers on my roads."

"Then let me fix that." He set the grouse down on the stone between them, wiped his hand on his coat, and extended it. "Kaelen."

She didn't take it. "Ada."

"Ada," he repeated, and there was something strange in the way he said it—not testing the name, but *recognizing* it, the way you recognize a word from a song you thought you'd forgotten. Something flickered across his face, quick as a fish under ice, and was gone before she could name it. "That's a good name."

"It's a name. You're poaching my routes, Kaelen, and you're doing it well enough that I want to know who taught you. Guild-trained men don't walk like you. Soldiers walk louder. So who are you?"

"Nobody taught me," he said. "I've just always known where roads want to be."

"Roads don't *want* to be anywhere."

"Yours do." He nodded back down the Stair, toward the switchback with the boulders. "That stretch between the rocks—it doesn't follow the mountain's grain. Someone cut it against the slope on purpose, generations ago, so it would look impassable from below. Whoever built your roads loved them. You can tell. A road built out of fear looks like a scar. Yours look like handwriting."

Ada had spent her whole life hearing outsiders describe the Thornfell passes as a maze, a trap, a godforsaken tangle. Nobody had ever called them handwriting. She hated how much she liked it.

"Flattery," she said. "From a trespasser."

"Observation. Flattery would be telling you the view from here is the finest in the mountains, which it isn't—that honor belongs to the ridge above the Veil, and we both know it." He tore the grouse in half and set the better portion on the stone again, closer to her this time. An offering. Or bait. "You know the way to the Veil."

It wasn't a question, and that was what finally put real cold in her spine. Because everyone in Thornfell knew *of* the Veil—the white-crowned peak at the far end of the range, the one the maps marked with nothing, the one even the zealot orders mostly ignored because there was nothing there to burn. But nobody asked the way to it. Nobody *wanted* the way to it. The Veil was where the old shrines pointed and where no road went, and the guild's oldest rule—one Sorrel had finally explained to her three nights ago with ash still falling from the sky—was that the way there stayed lost on purpose.

"I don't know what you're talking about," she said.

"Yes, you do." No smile now. He looked at her steadily, and those too-old eyes did something unsettling—they seemed almost sad. "But you're deciding whether to lie to me, and I'm asking you not to, because I'm tired, Ada, and I've been walking your beautiful handwriting for six days, and I think you might be the only person in these mountains who can help me, and I would very much like to stop pretending."

"Pretending what?"

"That I'm a pilgrim." He laughed, short and self-mocking. "I told the ferryman at Lowbridge I was a pilgrim. He believed me. People believe what's easiest. But you won't—you've already read my boots and my fire and my face, and you've decided I'm trouble, and you're right, I am absolutely trouble—but I'm honest trouble, which is rarer."

"Say what you want."

"The Veilstone." He watched her flinch and, to his credit, didn't pretend he hadn't seen it. "Yes. I know what it is. I know what it does. And I know that in—" he paused, lips moving faintly, counting something only he could feel, "—a little more than eight turns of the moon, if it isn't reached, both sides of the sky go dark. Not metaphorically. I mean the gods starve, and then the land starves, and then everyone you've ever smuggled flour to starves, in roughly that order."

The wind moved through the pines. Somewhere below, a hawk cried out over the valley, indifferent.

"You sound," Ada said slowly, "like a priest."

"I sound like a priest." He winced. "Gods, don't do that to me. Priests are worse than inquisitors—at least inquisitors are honest about wanting you dead." He picked up his half of the grouse, considered it, and set it back down. His appetite seemed to have left him. "Do you know why prayers started being answered lately? Really answered? Fire-signs and floods and wishes granted by things that don't understand mercy?"

"Sorrel says the covenant broke. Three generations ago."

"Sorrel says a great many things he learned secondhand." Kaelen's voice gentled. "The covenant didn't break all at once. It's been fraying since before your grandfather was born, thread by thread, and everyone who should have mended it decided instead that forgetting was safer. Your guild included. Especially your guild." He tilted his head. "Has he told you yet what your family was? What the Delacroix blood actually carries?"

"No."

"No," he echoed softly. "Of course not. They never do. They love you, and they bury you, and they call the burial protection." Something raw crossed his face—grief, or recognition, or both—and Ada felt, absurdly, like she'd said something that hurt him. "Sorry. That wasn't fair. I don't know your Sorrel."

"He's not mine to give away," she said, "and neither is my family. And neither is the road to the Veil, which I still haven't admitted exists, so you've wasted your morning."

"Have I?" He smiled again—smaller this time, and more tired, and somehow worse for her composure than the full-wattage version. "Then here's my offer, since we're being honest trouble together. I know another way out of Thornfell. South, past the salt marshes, over the border into Callowmere, where the inquisitors have no writ and the zealots have no reach and a woman with your particular talents could be rich inside a year. I'd fund it. I'd travel it with you. No covenant, no keystone, no burning sky, no dying guildmaster feeding you truths in teaspoons. Just roads. New ones. Ones nobody's ever written on."

Ada stared at him. "How do you know about Sorrel?"

"How do you know about the Veilstone?" He spread his hands. "We're even."

"We are not remotely even."

"No," he agreed cheerfully, "we're really not. I'm holding considerably more cards, and I'll play them when it costs less to do so, and meanwhile I'm offering you the single thing I suspect nobody has ever offered you: a door out. You keep this whole range invisible, Ada Delacroix. Who keeps *you* invisible? Who's ever asked what you want, instead of what you can hide?"

And that was the cruelest part, the part that made her want to throw the grouse at his head: for one bright, treacherous second, she wanted it. She saw it whole—Callowmere, warm and low and lawless in comfortable ways, a house with a door that locked from the inside, mornings where the only thing she had to smuggle was bread. She saw it so clearly it hurt, and she hated him for putting it there, and she hated herself for looking.

Because underneath the wanting sat the farmer's field outside Millbrook, drowned by an answered prayer. Sat the burning sigil over the shrine. Sat Sorrel's shaking hands around a cup of tea, saying *wardens*, saying *your family*, saying *forgive me*. Sat the simple arithmetic she'd been doing since she was nine years old: somebody keeps the roads, or nobody crosses the mountains, and the somebody has always been her.

"You're offering me escape," she said quietly.

"I'm offering you a choice. Escape is only one of the doors."

"And what do you get? Nobody walks six days through hostile mountains and funds strangers' retirements out of charity. What's in it for you, Kaelen?"

For a moment he didn't answer. He looked past her, down the long fall of the Stair, at the sea of pine and shadow and hidden roads, and his face did something complicated—guilt and longing tangled together like roots.

"I get to stop running," he said at last. "And I get—" he stopped himself, visibly reorganizing, "—I get to the Veilstone, one way or another. Preferably with someone who knows these mountains better than the mountains do. That's the trade. My coin and my company for your roads. And my honesty, such as it is, which I'll admit is currently a currency in short supply."

"So you admit you're hiding things."

"Constantly. Enormous things. Things that will make you furious when you learn them." He said it lightly, but his eyes didn't. "I'm telling you now so you can't say I deceived you later. That's the best I can offer today. Maybe tomorrow I'll be braver."

"Generous of you."

"I'm told I'm charming." He gathered his boots, began pulling them on, unhurried, as though she weren't holding a knife and a grudge. "Well? Roads for passage, Ada. Yes or no. And if it's no, I'll go on alone, and probably die somewhere picturesque, and you'll spend the rest of your life wondering what was in my pack."

"There's nothing in your pack."

"There's something in my pack."

Against her will—against every professional instinct she owned—Ada laughed. It escaped before she could catch it, short and rusty, the first laugh in three days that wasn't covering for fear. Kaelen's whole face lit at the sound like she'd answered a prayer correctly, and that was alarming too, how pleased he seemed, how *personally*, as if her laughter mattered to him in some ledger she couldn't see.

"Don't do that," she said.

"Do what?"

"Look at people like that. It's unsettling."

"Noted. I'll look at you normally from now on, with great effort." He slung his pack, stamped his boots settled, and stood at the edge of the crossroads stone waiting—patient as a road himself, she thought uncharitably, a road that wants to be walked. "Well?"

Ada looked south, where the passes ran down toward Callowmere and the life she could have had. Then north, toward the Veil, white-crowned and silent, where the oldest rule of her guild said no road should go.

Eight turns of the moon. Both sides of the sky.

"North," she said, and hated how easily the word came. "The salt pass, then the high shelf road. We move at dusk—in daylight you walk like a man announcing himself to every eye in the range. And Kaelen—" she held up one finger, "—you carry your own food, you sleep downstream of the fire, and if you draw that closed-eye sign on any more of my stones without asking, I'll leave you for the inquisitors."

"A reasonable and proportionate response," he said solemnly. "I accept all terms."

He bent to kick dirt over his fire, and as he did, his sleeve rode up his forearm, and Ada saw it: a mark on his skin, fine-lined and silver-pale, like an old scar in the shape of a threshold—a doorway, drawn small, on the inside of his arm.

Old scars didn't shimmer. She'd have sworn it didn't shimmer. But the light caught it strangely, the way light catches water, and then his sleeve fell and it was gone, and Kaelen straightened up smiling with his pack on his shoulders as if he carried nothing heavier than bread.

"Ready when you are," he said.

Ada turned north and started walking, and did not let herself wonder—yet—what kind of man wore a god's mark like an old wound, or why, when he'd said her name at the crossroads, he had looked at her like someone reading the last page of a book he'd lost long ago.

Dusk was four hours off. The road was long. And she had the sudden, unwelcome certainty that whatever was in his pack, she was going to find out.
