## 6. Blood Remembers

The rain had stopped by the time Ada reached the guildhouse, but the smell of it clung to everything—wet stone, wet wool, and underneath it all, the copper-and-ash scent that had followed her down from the pass like a dog she couldn't shake off.

She found Sorrel in the counting room, which was wrong before she even understood why. The counting room was where he held court at midday, feet on the table, arguing with the ledger as though it were a stubborn mule. Now it was dark except for one candle, and Sorrel was on the floor.

Not sitting on the floor. *On* the floor, propped against the strongbox with his legs out straight and his hands pressed flat against his belly, and between his fingers something dark and glistening moved with each breath.

"Sorrel—" Ada was across the room before she'd decided to move, kneeling in the spilled ink and scattered papers. "What happened? Who did this?"

"Door," he wheezed, "was locked."

"Locked doors don't do *that*." She pulled his coat open. The wound beneath was long and ugly, a slash from hip to ribs that some blade had opened with terrible patience. "Who did this?"

"Three of them." He laughed, and the laugh cost him; blood bubbled at the corner of his mouth. "Came for the records. Thought I'd be an easy old man." His hand fumbled along the floor beside him and came up holding a poker from the cold hearth, bent now into a shape a poker should never make. "They weren't entirely wrong. But I'm a stubborn easy old man."

Ada pressed her palms hard against the wound. The blood welled up hot between her fingers, insistent, indifferent. "I need Brannoc. He knows stitching. He stitched that gash you got from the Ferrowdale customs men—"

"Brannoc's dead, love."

The words landed somewhere below her ribs, deeper than the wound in Sorrel's belly. "Dead how?"

"The same three lads. On their way in." He said it simply, the way he reported everything—the price of tin, the mood of the toll-keepers, the weather coming over the mountains. "Wren too, probably. They went for the archive first. I heard the shouting."

*Wren.* Nineteen years old. Still owed Ada two coppers from a card game and never let her forget it. Ada's hands kept pressing, steady, because if they stopped being steady they would stop being useful, and if they stopped being useful they would start shaking, and she could not afford shaking. Not yet.

"You're not dying," she told him. "You're going to sit there and be difficult about it, the way you always are."

"I am always difficult." His eyes crinkled. Even now, even gray-lipped and leaking life onto the floorboards, there was mischief in him, banked but not out. "It's my best quality. Ask anyone."

"Everyone who could answer is dead or bleeding, so I'll take your word for it." She looked around wildly. Cloth. There had to be cloth. She stripped off her own outer shirt, wadded it, jammed it against the wound. Sorrel hissed through his teeth but didn't complain, which frightened her more than complaining would have. Sorrel complained about everything. It was practically liturgy.

"Listen to me," he said, and caught her wrist—not the wounded hand, the other one—and his grip was still strong, shockingly strong, the grip of a man who had hauled cargo up scree slopes for forty years. "We don't have much time, and I have spent thirty years not telling you things, and I find I've run out of the luxury of silence."

"Sorrel—"

"*Listen.*" And there was something under the word she had never heard from him before. Not the guildmaster's bark. Something older. Something almost afraid.

She listened.

* * *

He made her build the fire first. "If I'm going to die," he said, "I'll die warm, and you'll want light for what I'm about to show you." So she fed the hearth while he bled slowly into his folded coat, and when the flames stood up properly he nodded her toward the strongbox.

"Third false bottom. The one you've never found, because I built it after you picked every lock in this building at fourteen, little magpie, and I decided some things needed hiding even from you."

Her fingers found the seam by feel, the way he'd taught her—*the wood remembers where it's been cut, if you're patient enough to ask*—and the panel came away. Inside lay a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord gone brown with age, and beneath it a flat thing wrapped in leather that clinked faintly, like coins, but wasn't coins.

"Open the flat one."

She unwrapped it on the floor between them. A map—or what had been a map once. It was parchment of a fineness she'd never seen outside a lord's library, and it had been broken. Deliberately, it seemed: torn into seven pieces, its edges charred, and someone had tried to mend it since, stitching the fragments together with thread and wax until it resembled a shattered face pieced back together by someone who loved it. Mountains she knew. Rivers she knew. And roads she did not know at all—roads drawn in silver that caught the firelight and seemed to hold it, roads that appeared to run *through* mountains rather than around them, roads that ended in nothing and began again on the far side of the nothing.

"What is this?" Her voice came out strange.

"The honest map," Sorrel said. "The one we don't show anyone. The one your grandmother drew, and her mother before her, and hers before *her*, all the way back to a woman named Noor, who walked these roads when they were young." He coughed, wet and deep. "You asked me once why we call ourselves smugglers instead of just thieves. You were eleven. You wanted to know why we bothered with the pretense."

"And you said it was because thieves steal and smugglers *move* things, and moving things takes craft."

"I lied to you," Sorrel said gently. "It's what I do. It's what the guild has done for three generations. We lie because the truth got people killed, and the truth is this: we were never smugglers first. We are wardens. Or we were. The roads on that map aren't roads through Thornfell. They're roads through the *Veil*. And the covenant that made them—" his breath hitched, "—is breaking. Has been breaking since before you were born. Since before *I* was born."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs, wind worried at a loose shutter, patient as a debt collector.

"The burning sigil," Ada said. "Last night. In the sky over the shrine."

"That was the covenant cracking another joint. Like a door settling in a rotten frame. Every year it groans louder. Every year the gods on the other side wake a little more, and they wake *hungry*, Ada, because worship is food and nobody's been feeding them. Three generations of wardens pretending to be criminals, keeping the keystone safe without knowing what it was, and the debt has come due."

"What keystone?" She looked down at the map, at the silver roads, and felt the question form before she wanted it to. Felt it the way you feel a stair that isn't there in the dark—that lurching drop in the stomach. "Sorrel. What is the keystone?"

He looked at her then with such tenderness that she understood, in one sick falling moment, everything.

"No," she said.

"It's not an object." He dragged himself upright against the strongbox, grimacing, refusing her help. "Never was. That's why they never found it. That's why Maren Voss's zealots can burn every shrine from here to the sea and never touch it. The keystone is a memory. A living memory, carried in blood, passed mother to daughter down a single line—a memory of the covenant itself, of the day it was forged, of the *terms*, so that when the time came to reforge it, someone would know what they were agreeing to." His voice dropped. "Your line, Ada. The Delacroix line. Your grandmother didn't die of fever. She died carrying it, same as her mother, same as theirs, all the way back to Noor herself. And now it's in you."

The room tilted. Ada put her hand flat on the floorboards, on the cooling blood, to prove to herself the floor was real.

"That's not possible. I don't remember any—I don't know anything about gods or covenants or—"

"No. You remember your mother's kitchen. You remember learning the passes. You remember me teaching you cards and cheating badly enough that you'd catch me." He smiled, and it broke her heart, that smile. "The memory doesn't live in your head, love. It lives deeper than that. In the blood. It sleeps. But it's waking, same as everything else. Why do you think the sigil burned over *your* shrine? Why do you think the veil-things have been following you for a week and leaving everyone else alone?" He tapped the map with one bloody finger. "Because the mountain knows its keystone. And the mountain is calling you home."

* * *

Afterward—she would never be sure how much later, minutes or an hour—she sat beside him with the map across her knees and her arms wrapped around it like a child with a stolen loaf.

"Why hide it?" she asked. "Why lie for thirty years? If I had known—"

"If you had known, you'd have gone looking. At fifteen. At twenty. Head full of stories and no sense in it, straight up the Veil with your pretty knives and your clever routes, and the zealots would have taken your head, or worse, the *gods* would have taken it, hungry as they are." He shifted, and the movement cost him; she saw him decide, deliberately, to stop conserving himself. "Your grandmother made us swear. *Let her be a smuggler*, she said. *Let her be small and quick and invisible. The covenant will find her when it's ready. Don't you dare hand her the burden early.*" His breath rattled. "So we buried it. Built a guild on top of it like a house on a grave. And I let you believe the lie because loving someone means lying to them sometimes, and I have never been sorry, and I have always been sorry, both at once, every day."

"Then why tell me now?"

"Because the zealots found us tonight, which means they'll find *you* tomorrow. Because the moon is turning its thirteenth turn, and when it completes, either the covenant reforges or both realms starve each other into silence—every god dead, every answered prayer gone, every harvest blessing and healing spring and mercy-wind snuffed out like candles. Because—" he caught her hand again, and this time his grip was weaker, and they both pretended it wasn't, "—because I promised your grandmother I'd tell you when it was time, and I have run out of ways to delay, and I find, at the end, that I would rather you hate me knowing the truth than love me inside the lie."

"I don't hate you." Her voice cracked clean through. "Sorrel, I could never—"

"You might." He said it steadily. "When you learn what it costs. When you're standing at the Veilstone and you understand what the forging asks. You might hate me then, and I want you to know, before you go, that whatever you feel on that mountain—I earned it. Every hour of it. And I'd do it all again, because you're alive, and free, and the finest warden this guild ever produced, and you learned all of it thinking you were only learning to smuggle." He laughed, soft and terrible. "Best trick I ever ran. Made a guardian out of you and never once let you see the strings."

Outside, the wind changed. Ada felt it against the shutters—a new note in it, thin and high, like a voice remembering how to sing after decades of silence. The mountain, calling. Patient. Certain.

"Sorrel." She leaned close. "Tell me what to do."

"Follow the silver road east from the Hollow Gate. First waypoint is a shrine under the split peak—your grandmother marked it on the map, look for the little star. After that, the road remembers itself as you walk it. It wants to be found." He closed his eyes. Opened them. "And Ada. When you meet the others on the road—and you will, the faithful and the faithless and everything between—remember that you were a smuggler first. Smugglers survive. Whatever the covenant asks of you, survive it. Promise me."

"I promise."

"Good girl." His eyes drifted toward the fire, and something in his face eased, the way a fist eases. "You know, I cheated at cards that first night too. The one where you won two coppers off me. Let you win. You were so pleased with yourself." His smile was far away now. "Told Wren afterward, *that one's going to run this guild someday, mark me.*"

"She does mark you. Two coppers. She never lets me forget it."

"I know." His hand tightened on hers, briefly, with all his remaining strength. "Give it back to her. Tell her it's from me."

"Sorrel—"

"Ah, love." He looked at her, and his eyes were clear and calm and full of thirty years of lies told out of love, and he said, quietly, the way he'd say it about the weather: "Forgive me. For all of it. For the hiding and the lying and for making you carry what I wouldn't carry myself. Forgive an old man who loved you the only way he knew how—which was badly, and completely."

"Don't ask me that yet," Ada whispered. "Ask me when you're better. Ask me over breakfast. You always say debts are settled over breakfast—"

But his eyes had gone to the fire, and his grip had gone gentle, and the rattling breath slowed, and slowed, and the wind outside rose into something that was almost, almost singing.

Ada sat with him a long time while the candle burned down. Then she wiped her face on her sleeve, folded the map into her coat, took two coppers from the strongbox, and stood up in the empty counting room of a guild that had never really been a guild at all.

Somewhere east, under the split peak, a shrine was waiting. And somewhere behind her, already on the wind, she could hear hoofbeats—many, disciplined, coming up the valley road with the patience of people who believed the world belonged to them.

Maren Voss. Coming for the records. Coming for the heretics. Coming, though she didn't know it yet, for her.

Ada blew out the candle, let herself out the back way—the way only a Delacroix knew—and started walking east into the dark, carrying a map of impossible roads, two coppers, and a debt of forgiveness she wasn't ready to settle.

The mountain sang to her the whole way.
