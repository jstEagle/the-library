## 7. Roads That No Longer Exist

The trouble with roads that don't exist, Ada had learned early, was that they were the only ones worth taking.

She rose before dawn in the hollow beneath the Widow's Tooth, where a stream ran cold and honest over slate, and she watched Kaelen sleep for exactly as long as it took to decide she didn't like doing it.

Not because he looked dangerous asleep—though he did, in the way of men who'd learned to sleep with one ear open—but because he looked *comfortable*. Sprawled against his pack like the mountains belonged to him, dark hair falling across a face that was too handsome by half and knew it even unconscious. Nobody slept that comfortably on smuggler's ground unless they were either very stupid or very well protected.

Ada had ruled out stupid somewhere around the second day.

"Up," she said, kicking the sole of his boot. "We lose the light if we're not through the Saddle before noon."

Kaelen opened one eye. "Good morning to you too."

"It's not morning yet. It's barely an apology for night." She tossed him a heel of bread. "Eat while you walk."

They climbed out of the hollow into grey air that smelled of pine and something else underneath—something faintly sweet, like honey left too long near a fire. Ada had been smelling it for two days now and hadn't said so aloud, because saying things aloud made them real, and real things required plans, and her plans were already stretched thin as wet paper.

The first waypoint lay three days east: the Hollow Chapel, cut into the shoulder of Mount Serel, where Sorrel's fractured map swore a door still stood. The map had been drawn before Ada was born. The map, Sorrel had told her with his dying breath, would be wrong in places, because the world itself was wrong in places now.

He hadn't been lying.

* * *

By midmorning they reached the Saddle—the narrow spine of rock between two peaks that every courier in Thornfell had crossed for sixty years, the guild's oldest artery, worn smooth by generations of soft-soled boots.

It wasn't there.

Ada stood at the edge of the meadow where the trail should have begun and stared at a wall of unbroken forest. Not young forest. Old forest, deep-rooted, the kind that took centuries to grow. Trees stood where she had personally walked four months ago carrying contraband prayer-candles and a strongbox of guild silver.

"That's not possible," she said.

Kaelen came up beside her and was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had lost its usual silk. "How old are those pines?"

"Older than me. Older than you." She crouched, pressed her palm to the moss at the treeline. The ground hummed—faintly, wrongly, like a struck bell heard through water. "Kaelen. What's happening?"

"The veil is thinning." He said it simply, the way you'd say *it's raining*. "The border between what is and what was. It's getting... porous. Places that were erased are leaking back through. Or this place is leaking forward. I'm honestly not sure which direction the leak runs."

"Erased," Ada repeated. "Places get erased?"

"The covenant didn't just bind the gods, Ada. It bound everything they touched. Shrines, roads, whole valleys. When it broke three generations ago, the wardens sealed away more than worship. They sealed away the geography of the divine." He gestured at the impossible forest. "This was probably a pilgrim road once. Before your guild turned it into a smuggling route."

"My guild turned it into a smuggling route *because* nobody else remembered it existed," Ada snapped, but the anger rang hollow even to her. She pulled out Sorrel's map, unfolded it against her knee, and felt her stomach drop.

The Saddle was marked in Sorrel's careful hand. So was the Hollow Chapel. And between them, in ink so faded it might have been written in ash, ran a second line—a road she'd never seen on any version of the map, curving north through country that had no name.

Beneath it, in Sorrel's writing: *When the old road fails, the older road remembers.*

"He knew," she whispered. "The stubborn old goat knew this would happen."

"Guildmasters usually do. It's why they die tired." Kaelen peered over her shoulder, close enough that she caught woodsmoke and something sweeter beneath it—that same honey-and-ash smell from the air. She stepped sideways without making it obvious. Mostly.

"We go north," she decided. "And if the older road turns out to be a river or a dragon's front porch, I'm haunting you specifically."

"Noted. I'd be honored."

* * *

The older road found them before they found it.

One moment they were pushing through trackless forest, Ada cutting blazes into bark with her knife and then watching, unsettled, as the blazes seemed to drift behind them faster than their walking could explain. The next, the trees simply stepped aside—and there it was: a road of pale stone, wide enough for six abreast, running arrow-straight through the wilderness. It was spotless. No leaves, no moss, no hoofprints. It looked less built than *remembered*, as though the idea of a road had been given just enough substance to bear weight.

"Well," Ada said. "That's either very good or very bad."

"Historically," Kaelen said, stepping onto the stone, "roads like this tend toward both."

They walked. And the road gave them back the world that had been taken away.

First it was small things: a waystone carved with a seven-pointed star, its inscription legible in a script Ada didn't know but somehow half-recognized, the way you recognize a lullaby you can't name. Then a shrine, whole and white-washed, candles burning in its niche though no hand had lit them. Then—Ada stopped dead—a village.

It sat in a valley that should have been empty pasture. Slate roofs, smoke rising from chimneys, laundry strung between windows. People moved along its single street. A woman laughed at something; the sound carried up the hillside, warm and ordinary and utterly impossible.

"I know that village," Ada said slowly. "I mean—I don't. But I've heard of it. Callowmere. It burned when my grandmother was a girl. Everyone died. There's a cairn up the valley. I've drunk from the stream beside it."

Kaelen's jaw tightened. "Then the cairn is down there too. Somewhere under the smoke."

They stood at the edge of the road and watched the dead live their afternoon. A boy chased a dog through a puddle. An old man repaired a shutter, muttering at it. None of them looked up. None of them ever would, Ada understood suddenly. This wasn't a village returned—it was a village *preserved*, a memory wearing flesh, replaying its last ordinary hours forever at the edge of a thinning veil.

"They don't know," she said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended.

"No. And they never will. That's the mercy of it, if you want to call it mercy." Kaelen's face had gone carefully blank, the way it did whenever the subject drifted anywhere near the gods. "Come on. The road won't hold all day."

She followed him, but she looked back twice. At the second look, the woman who'd laughed was standing in her doorway, gazing up the hillside directly at Ada—unseeing, smiling at nothing, at everything, at some joy thirty years gone.

Ada walked faster after that.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a ruined archway that the road passed beneath—two pillars and a fragment of lintel, carved with stars. Ada got a fire going small and smokeless, guild-fashion, and they ate hard cheese and apples and argued about whether the moon had looked wrong last night (it had; Kaelen claimed this was expected; Ada informed him that "expected" was doing a lot of work in that sentence).

"You're better company than you pretend to be," Kaelen said, apropos of nothing.

"I'm exactly the company I pretend to be. You just have low standards."

"Three days ago you held a knife to my throat for standing on your road."

"And I haven't had to do it since. That's called training. It works." She fed the fire a twig. "Why do you actually want the Veilstone, Kaelen? And don't give me the speech about restoring balance. You've got a tell. Your left eyebrow does something insincere."

His left eyebrow, traitorously, did something. He laughed—a real laugh, surprised out of him—and for a moment the firelight made him look younger, and lonelier, and much more dangerous than charm ever had.

"Someday," he said, "I'll tell you. Properly. Not today."

"Today would be convenient."

"Someday," he repeated, and there was something in it—not evasion exactly. More like a man rationing out a wound. Ada let it go. Guild rule: never press a partner on the job. Press them after, when you've got leverage and distance.

She took first watch. Somewhere past midnight, drowsing against a pillar, she heard him murmur in his sleep—one word, over and over, soft as a prayer: *"Mama."*

She decided not to have heard it.

* * *

The ambush came at the worst possible hour: that grey sliver of false dawn when the world can't decide whether to exist.

Ada woke not to sound but to silence—the birds stopping all at once, which in Thornfell meant one thing and one thing only. She had Kaelen's shoulder gripped and her other hand over his mouth before his eyes finished opening.

"Soldiers," she breathed. "Downslope. Six, maybe eight. They've circled the archway."

Through the pillars she saw them: Maren Voss's order, in their grey-and-white, moving with the terrible patience of people who believed absolutely. And at their center, torchlight catching steel—no, not steel. A censer. They were swinging a censer, filling the air with incense, and where the smoke drifted, the pale road *steamed*, like snow touched by a hot iron.

"They're not hunting us," Kaelen whispered against her palm. "They're hunting the road. Unmaking it as they come."

"Maren doesn't leave loose ends. And we're tied to this road now." Ada's mind was already moving, laying the ground over in her head the way Sorrel had taught her: *the land is always telling you where to run, girl, if you shut up long enough to listen.* "There's a ravine east of here. The stream feeds it. If we drop before they close the circle—"

"If we drop, we drop blind."

"We drop *first*." She grinned at him, sharp and thin. "Blind beats dead. Guild maxim number nine."

"How many maxims are there?"

"Forty-one. Number twelve is 'never explain the maxims to outsiders,' so consider yourself privileged."

The circle tightened. A soldier's boot crunched on consecrated stone twenty yards out. Ada squeezed Kaelen's arm once—*now*—and they went over the eastern lip together, sliding down scree in a controlled collapse of gravel and curses, hitting the streambed and splashing upstream into the ravine's throat.

Behind them, a horn sounded. Then shouting. Then—worse—the strange, tearing silence of the road being erased, a sound like a held note snapping.

"They'll fan out along the rim," Ada panted, hauling herself over a fallen slab. "But ravines funnel searchers. We need to be out of the channel before—"

An arrow sparked off the stone above her head.

"*Before that,*" she finished, and ran.

They ran the streambed in the failing dark, boots hammering shallow water, arrows whispering past—one punching into the pack between Kaelen's shoulders with a crack that made Ada's heart seize until she saw it was the cheese, heroically sacrificed. The ravine bent, narrowed, and spat them into a birch grove just as the sky finally committed to dawn.

And there, in the open, they were caught: three soldiers breaking from the trees ahead, swords drawn, and the horn sounding again behind. Ada put her back to a birch and drew her knives, doing the arithmetic she hated—three ahead, four behind, two of them hers if she was lucky, zero if she wasn't.

The lead soldier raised his sword and said, in the flat voice of a recitation: "In the name of the Order of the Severed Hand, surrender the god-touched and be spared—"

The birch grove *answered*.

Every tree shivered at once, though there was no wind. Light came up through the roots, gold and slow, like sunrise from below, and the soldiers' swords dimmed in their grips—not extinguished, just *outshone*. Ada felt it too: a warmth in her blood, in the keystone the gods had buried in her family line, humming like a plucked string finding its chord.

The soldiers fled. She couldn't blame them. Faith that burns shrines has no answer for faith that grows trees.

When the light faded, Kaelen was on his knees in the moss, both hands pressed to the ground, breathing hard. The warmth in the air was pouring out of him—she could see it now, see the faint glow guttering under his skin like a lamp running low.

"Kaelen." She crouched beside him. "What did you just spend?"

"Nothing. Nothing important." But he wouldn't meet her eyes, and his hands, when he lifted them, trembled like a drunkard's.

"You drove off six armed men with a feeling. That's not nothing."

"Ada." He looked up at last, and whatever mask he wore—and he wore several, she was beginning to catalogue them—this one had slipped clean off. Underneath was exhaustion, and fear, and something that looked unbearably like guilt. "I will tell you everything. I swear it. But not here, and not while I'm like this, because you'll make a decision about me, and I—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I need you to make that decision later. Please."

Ada studied him for a long moment. Guild maxim seventeen: *a partner who lies to you on the road will lie to you at the gate.* Guild maxim eighteen: *but judge them by what they do when the arrows fly, not by what they say around the fire.*

The arrows had flown. He had spent something precious shielding a woman he'd known five days.

"Later," she said. "But Kaelen—when 'later' comes, you don't get to choose which parts to tell me. All of it. Even the parts that make me put the knife back to your throat."

"Especially those," he said, with a ghost of his usual smile. "Those are the interesting ones."

She hauled him to his feet. Above them, on the ravine's rim, the horn sounded one last time—then stopped, cut short, as though the hand blowing it had thought better of announcing their position to whatever lived in these woods now.

Ada went still, listening. The woods listened back.

"Move," she said quietly. "Waypoint's a day east. And Kaelen? Whatever's following us through these trees—it isn't Maren's soldiers."

Because there, far off through the birches, where the pale road had been unmade, the air hung in a shimmering curtain—and through it, faint as memory, she could hear singing. Dozens of voices. Human voices, joyful and distant, singing a hymn she almost knew.

Coming from a village that had burned before her grandmother was born.

And it was getting closer.
