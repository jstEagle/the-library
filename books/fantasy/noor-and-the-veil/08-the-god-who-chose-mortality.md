## 8. The God Who Chose Mortality

Kaelen had lied to exactly three people in his life with any real conviction: a customs officer in Vell, a priestess who had deserved better, and Ada Delacroix.

The first two had been easy. The third was becoming impossible, and that was the problem. He sat across their small fire on the shoulder of the Graystep pass, watching her clean her boots with the focused violence of someone who considered mud a personal insult, and felt the truth pressing against his teeth like a bad tooth.

"You're staring," she said without looking up.

"I'm admiring your technique."

"No one scrubs boots that admirably." She flicked a pebble at him. It bounced off his knee. "You're working up to something. You've been working up to something since the ford, two days ago. You do this thing where you get very charming and very quiet at the same time, and I've learned it means you're deciding how much of the truth you can afford me."

He laughed, because she was right, and laughing was easier than answering. "You make me sound like a merchant."

"Merchants are more honest. They at least tell you the price upfront."

* * *

He had been born on a threshold. That was not poetry; it was fact. His mother had chosen the doorway of a shepherd's hut on the last night of autumn, halfway between inside and out, halfway between her divinity and its abandonment, and she had delivered him there herself, kneeling on cold stone, so that his first breath would be taken in neither world entirely.

Veyra, goddess of thresholds, keeper of doors, warder of the spaces between—she had held her newborn son in the exact center of a doorway and wept, and the weeping had been the last truly divine thing she ever did.

He had never told anyone this. Not Sorrel, who would have burned the records rather than hold them. Not the priests of Vell, who would have made him a relic or a target depending on the day. And certainly not Ada, whose whole life was built on the assumption that the gods were distant weather, dangerous but impersonal, and whose trust he needed if he was going to get her to the Veilstone.

Because here was the arithmetic he could not escape: every mile she walked toward the Veilstone was a mile walked because of what he wanted. And what he wanted was not the covenant reforged.

What he wanted was for it to fail quietly, or break cleanly, or dissolve into nothing—anything, so long as his mother's sacrifice stayed finished. So long as Veyra remained half-mortal and free instead of being dragged back into the machinery she had spent thirty years clawing out of.

And Ada Delacroix, keystone of the covenant, blood-carrier of the old wardens, was the instrument by which the gods would wake again. She just didn't know it yet. He'd known it from the first night, watching her read the fractured map by firelight, her brow furrowed, her fingers tracing roads that only her blood remembered.

He was using her. He was also, inconveniently and increasingly, falling in love with her. The two facts did not cancel each other out. They compounded.

* * *

They made camp that night in the ruins of a waystation that had been abandoned so long the mountain was trying to digest it—walls slumped into green shoulders, a hearth full of moss like some enormous patient tongue. Ada built the fire with the efficiency of habit while Kaelen pretended to gather wood he didn't need, buying time, delaying the conversation he'd already decided to have and already dreaded.

She handed him tea. It was terrible. She made it the same way every night—too many leaves, boiled too long—and he drank it the same way every night, gratefully, because it was hers.

"The shrine back at the ford," she said suddenly. "The little one, the one Maren's people hadn't found yet. You stood in the doorway of it for a long time."

"It was a nice doorway."

"Nobody stands in doorways like that." She set down her cup. "You put your hand on the frame. Like you were greeting it. Like it was—" she searched, "—like it was family."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, wind moved through pines with a sound like the sea rehearsing.

"My mother," Kaelen said, "is a goddess."

Ada did not laugh. He had expected her to laugh—a short, sharp smuggler's laugh, the kind she used on liars. Instead she went very still, the way she went still when a patrol passed on the road below, all her attention narrowing to a point.

"That's not funny," she said quietly. "So either you're crueler than I think you are, or you mean it."

"I mean it."

"Which goddess?"

"Does it matter?"

"Of course it matters. If you're the son of Ilvara of the harvests, I should be leaving offerings around our campsite. If you're the son of one of the hungry ones, I should be running." Her eyes were steady on his face. "So tell me which, and tell me why you're telling me now, three waypoints into a journey you've been suspiciously helpful about."

There it was. The question underneath the question. *Why are you really here?*

He looked into the fire and told the truth, or the shape of it.

"Her name is Veyra."

He watched the name land. Ada knew her lore—he'd seen that early on, beneath the smuggler's pragmatism, a childhood's worth of shrine-visits and grandmother's stories. Everyone in Thornfell knew Veyra. Thresholds were the one god ordinary people still touched daily without thinking: they knocked wood, they paused at doorways, they said *in and out again* when someone left the house. Small courtesies to a small-seeming goddess.

"She was the warder of the Veil," Kaelen said. "Before it broke. Before your guild's grandfathers' grandfathers decided the terms were too heavy and let the whole thing rot. She kept the boundary between what mortals asked for and what gods gave. She was—" he smiled despite himself, "—she was very good at it. Fair. Patient. Three thousand years of listening to prayers and weighing them, and she never once granted one out of spite."

"What happened?"

"I happened." He turned his cup in his hands. "Not me personally. Mortals generally. Do you know what the old terms required? Every prayer answered in kind. Every wish weighed and paid for. A farmer asks for rain, the rain comes, and somewhere else the rain is *taken*. Balance, always balance. Your chapter of the covenant—the wardens' part—was to decide which prayers got heard at all. To ration grace. And when the wardens started taking bribes, when they started granting the prayers of rich men and silencing the desperate—"

"We know what happened," Ada said softly. "We're living in it. The flood at Marrow's Bend. The fire that answered a prayer for warmth."

"Yes. But before that, before the breaking, my mother saw the cruelty of the system itself. Not corruption—design. A covenant that made gods into scales and mortals into weights. She argued for reform for a century. Then she did something no god had done." He met Ada's eyes. "She stepped down. She poured her divinity out—not into the covenant, not into another god, but into the threshold itself. Into every doorway in the world. She scattered herself thin. And what was left over, what pooled at the bottom of that emptying, was enough to make one mortal child."

The wind moved again. Ada's face was unreadable, but her hands had gone tight around her cup.

"So you're half of what remained," she said. "A residue."

"A residue," he agreed, and huffed a laugh. "That's the least flattering thing anyone's ever called me, and I once courted a woman who described me as 'adequate.'"

"And your father?"

And there it was. The part he'd been circling for two days. He set the cup down.

"I don't have a father in the way you mean. Gods don't—I was made, not begotten. But there was a god who loved her. Who had loved her for millennia across the boundary of their domains. When she emptied herself out, he—" Kaelen's jaw tightened. "He took it as an answer. As permission. He began walking out of his own divinity too, following her down, step by step, domain by domain. And as he descends, the covenant frays faster, because he was one of its pillars. His name was written into the terms. Every year he gives up more of himself, the terms lose more ink."

"Which god?" Ada asked. Her voice had gone careful.

"He was called Orenn. God of the deep roads. Of passage and return. The one travelers swore by when they meant to come home." He shrugged, aiming for lightness and missing. "You'd know him as the reason lost things sometimes find their way back."

Ada was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was strange.

"The map Sorrel gave me. The first waypoint—it opened onto a road that shouldn't exist anymore. A road that came *back*. I thought it was the veil thinning."

"It is. And it's him. He walks the old roads as he falls, and where he walks, erased things return." Kaelen fed a stick to the fire. "He'll reach the bottom soon. Full mortality. And when he does, he intends to find her. And I intend to make sure that when he finds her, she isn't dragged back up into the sky to be a scale again. Thirty years, Ada. Thirty years of being a person. Of burning toast and getting rheumatism and learning to love things that die. I will not let the covenant take that from her twice."

He stopped. Heard what he'd said. *Take that from her twice.*

Ada heard it too. He watched her assemble it, piece by piece, the way she assembled anything—quickly, completely, without mercy.

"The covenant reforged means the gods restored," she said slowly. "All of them. Bounded by new terms, but awake. Whole." Her eyes came up. "Including her."

"Including her."

"And you've known since the beginning that I'm the one who does the restoring. You've been guiding me straight toward it." Not accusation. Worse than accusation—understanding. "Every shortcut. Every warning about Maren. Every meal. You've been keeping your investment alive."

The fire cracked. Kaelen discovered that his mouth had gone dry.

"Yes," he said. Because lying now would be worse than useless; she'd smell it, and whatever else survived tonight, honesty might. "Yes. I have been using you. I found the keystone's carrier and I befriended her and I led her toward the thing that will unmake my mother's freedom, and I told myself the whole way that I'd find another answer before we arrived. Some third road. There's always a third road; I'm my mother's son, thresholds are practically the family trade." He laughed, and it came out broken. "There isn't one. I've looked. I've looked for weeks and there isn't one, and I hate myself, and I keep walking anyway, because the alternative is letting the realms starve each other into silence, and I can't carry that either."

Silence. Long enough that he counted his own heartbeats and lost track.

Then Ada reached over, took his terrible tea out of his hands, drank it herself, grimaced, and handed it back.

"You make this too strong," she said. "It's undrinkable."

"I—what?"

"It's a distraction. I need a moment and you need something to do with your hands." She pulled her knees up, stared into the fire. "You should have told me at the crossroads."

"I know."

"You should have told me before I started trusting you on the roads."

"I know that too."

"But you didn't lie about the important parts." She ticked them off, fingers moving. "Maren's order, the ambush routes, the waypoint warnings—all true. All useful. All things that kept us alive. And you just handed me the one secret that could make me turn on you." She glanced sideways at him. "A smart manipulator keeps that in reserve. Deploys it at the Veilstone, when it's too late for me to change course. So either you're a very bad manipulator—"

"I am, actually. Historically."

"—or you wanted me to have the choice." She let that sit between them. "Which is a strange thing for a man to do, if he's using me."

"It doesn't un-use you," Kaelen said miserably. "Knowing my reasons doesn't change my reasons."

"No. But it changes mine." She was quiet a moment. "I grew up on stories about the gods, you know. My mother's stories. She used to say the gods weren't in the sky, they were in the *doorways*—that every kindness done at a threshold was a prayer whether you meant it or not." Her voice softened. "I thought those were just stories. Pretty ones. The kind you tell children so they say *in and out again* when Pa leaves for the passes."

"They are pretty ones," Kaelen said. "I can confirm. I was there for most of them."

Something almost like a smile crossed her face and vanished. "If I reforge the covenant, your mother goes back. Back to being a scale. Back to three thousand years of weighing other people's grief." She looked at him fully then, and her eyes were fierce and sad at once. "And if I don't—if I walk away, take your offered road out of Thornfell, let the whole rotten structure fall—then Marrow's Bend drowns, and the loops spread, and everything starves. There's no third road for me either."

"No," he admitted. "There isn't."

"So we're both damned, and we're both honest now, and the tea is terrible." She lay back against her pack. "Get some sleep, son of Veyra. We reach the second waypoint tomorrow, and I want to see what your father's been doing to my mountains."

He lay down and did not sleep. He listened to her breathing even out, and he stared at the stars through the ruined roof, and he hated himself a little less and a little more in alternating waves, which he suspected was simply what love cost, charged nightly.

Near dawn, he dreamed—not a mortal dream but a memory wearing one's clothes. His mother, standing in a doorway, thirty years younger, saying: *A door is not a wall, little one. A door exists so that both sides may be chosen.*

He woke to grey light and Ada shaking his shoulder, and her face was wrong.

"Kaelen," she said. "Look at the moon."

He looked. Low in the western sky, pale as a coin left in a dead man's pocket, the moon hung where it should not be—for last night it had been full, and now it was not, and the thirteenth turn had begun.
