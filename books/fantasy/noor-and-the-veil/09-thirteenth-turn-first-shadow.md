## 9. Thirteenth Turn, First Shadow

The thirteenth moon did not rise so much as arrive, the way an unwelcome relative arrives—suddenly, and already in your kitchen.

Ada noticed it first as a wrongness in the light. She had been mending a strap on her pack at the Hollowbrook waystation, one eye on Kaelen, who was ostensibly gathering firewood but actually arranging sticks in patterns that looked suspiciously like sigils, when the dusk simply... stopped. The sun hung low and orange over the western peaks like a coin someone had forgotten to take out of their pocket.

"Kaelen," she said.

"Hm?"

"The sun has stopped."

He looked up. For once he didn't have a clever answer ready, which frightened her more than the frozen sunset did. "That's early," he said quietly. "I thought we'd have until the turn proper."

"When is the turn proper?"

"Three days ago, by my count." He dropped the sticks and came to stand beside her, squinting westward. "Which means my count is wrong, or time is. Given where we're standing, I'd bet on time."

The mountains around Thornfell had been misbehaving for days—paths that doubled back on themselves, a stream that ran uphill for a hundred paces before thinking better of it—but this was different. This was not the land forgetting its shape. This was the sky holding its breath.

"We need to move," Ada said. "If time is pooling anywhere, it'll pool where people are. Where prayer is."

"Bramblewick," Kaelen said, and she hated him a little for reading her mind before she'd admitted it to herself.

Bramblewick was three hours off her hidden roads—a village of forty-odd souls tucked in a fold of the hills, famous for nothing except an apple festival that had run every autumn for two hundred years, and for old Marta Tulliver's cider, which Ada would have smuggled across borders at considerable personal risk if anyone had ever asked her to. She'd slept in Bramblewick's haylofts a dozen times. The miller's daughter had taught her a card game. The baker gave her day-old rolls and pretended they were fresh because he knew she'd refuse charity but not a bargain.

"They'll be right in the middle of festival season," she said.

"Yes." Kaelen was already shouldering his pack. "And if what I think is happening is happening, they will be *in* that festival. All of them. Forever."

* * *

They smelled Bramblewick before they saw it: woodsmoke and cinnamon and roasting pork, thick enough to chew. On any other evening it would have made Ada's stomach growl. Here, with the sun nailed to the horizon behind them, it made her skin crawl.

The village looked perfect. That was the first wrong thing. The second was the light—golden, honeyed, coming from everywhere and nowhere, the light of a memory rather than an evening. Lanterns glowed without flickering. Banners hung motionless in a wind that wasn't blowing. And the people—

The people were dancing.

Not the exhausted, wine-loosened shuffling of a real festival's third night, but dancing the way you dance in a dream: flawlessly, joyfully, endlessly. The fiddler played the same eight bars over and over, and nobody noticed, because nobody could notice. Children chased each other through the same game of tag, laughing the same laugh, falling over the same root and being caught by the same grandmother, again and again and again.

Ada stood at the edge of the square with her heart going cold in her chest.

"It's the happiest day any of them can remember," Kaelen said softly. "Or perhaps the happiest day the *land* remembers for them. When the veil thins and time pools, it looks for something worth keeping. Something the prayers here were always about." He nodded toward the shrine at the square's far end—an old stone, older than the village, older than the guild, crusted now with ribbons and apple offerings. "Harvest prayers. Gratitude. *Let this day never end.*"

"Somebody prayed that?" Ada whispered.

"Somebody prays it every year. It's practically liturgy. And now something is answering."

She found faces she knew. There was Tam Broadhurst, the miller, mid-toast with a mug raised to lips that never quite drank. There was his daughter Wren, seventeen and gap-toothed no longer, spinning with her sweetheart in a circle they had clearly completed four hundred times. There was the baker, Aldous, pressing a fresh roll into the hands of a boy who had already received it, by Ada's count, eleven times since she'd been watching.

"Aldous," she said, stepping forward. "Aldous, it's me. Ada."

The baker turned. His face split into genuine delight—the delight of a man seeing a friend—and he pressed a roll into her hands. "Ada! You made it! You always make it, don't you? Best part of the festival, when the roads bring you back to us." He beamed. "Have you eaten? Sit down, sit down, the dancing's just starting."

"The dancing started hours ago," Ada said.

"Has it?" He laughed, easy and unbothered. "Well. It feels like it's just starting. It always feels like it's just starting. Isn't that wonderful?"

It was wonderful. That was the horror of it. He meant it. Every word of his happiness was real, and it was a cage built out of exactly that happiness, and he would live inside it until the world starved.

Ada took the roll. Her hands were shaking.

"Kaelen," she said, not turning around. "How do I get them out?"

* * *

They tried gentleness first.

Kaelen walked the square touching shoulders, murmuring words in a language that made the lantern-glow bend toward him, trying to wake them the way you wake a sleepwalker—with patience, with softness. Ada went door to door, shaking people, shouting names, dragging Tam Broadhurst bodily toward the village well. Tam thanked her warmly for the dance, asked if she'd eaten, and wandered back to the square before she'd finished blinking.

The loop held them like water holds a fish. They couldn't see the edges because the edges were made of contentment.

By full dark—which never fully came, the golden glow only deepening toward amber—Ada sat on the shrine steps with her head in her hands while Kaelen crouched beside the old stone, reading markings she couldn't see.

"There's a seam," he said at last. "In the loop. A moment where it's thinnest."

"Where?"

His jaw tightened. "You won't like it."

"I haven't liked anything since the sky caught fire, Kaelen. Tell me."

"The moment the day ends. The last hour of the festival, when the fires burn down and everyone goes home. The loop skips over it—it can't hold an ending. Endings are what it's *for* avoiding." He looked up at her, and for once there was no charm in his face at all, only apology. "To reach the seam, we have to end the festival ourselves. Loudly. Irreversibly. We have to make it impossible for them to pretend the day isn't over."

"How?"

"Break the thing the day is built around." He nodded at the shrine stone. "Or—" and here his voice did something complicated, "—the cider barrels. The toast. The whole village drinks together at moonrise. If the cider were gone—if the toast failed—the day would collapse. They'd feel the lack. Grief wakes people faster than sense does."

Ada stared at him. "You want me to dump out Marta Tulliver's cider."

"I want you to decide whether forty people are worth one barrel of tradition. Because that's the arithmetic the covenant runs on, Ada. It always has been. Somebody pays. The only question is whether we choose who, or let the gods choose for us."

She thought about arguing. She thought about looking for a third option, some clever smuggler's route around the toll. But she had spent three weeks learning that the old roads ran straight through things you couldn't go around, and this—she understood suddenly, with a sick lurch—this was practice. This was the covenant showing her its terms in miniature, written small enough to read.

Somebody pays.

"Show me the cellar," she said.

* * *

Marta Tulliver's cellar smelled like twenty autumns stacked in rows. The barrels gleamed in the lantern light, each one chalked with its year, and Ada stood among them feeling like a burglar in a temple.

"She'd understand," Kaelen said from the stairs. He hadn't come down. Maybe he knew she needed to do this alone. Maybe he couldn't watch.

"She'd hex me," Ada said. "She'd hex me with that spoon she keeps behind her ear, and she'd be right to." She put her palm flat against the oldest barrel, the festival reserve, the one tapped only at moonrise when the whole village drank together and old grievances drowned and new promises got made. "This is the happiest day they've got. And I'm about to be the reason it ends."

"That's not what you'll be."

"No?"

"You'll be the reason it *was*. There's a difference. Nothing real survives by refusing to end. Not days. Not people." He paused. "Believe me. I've looked into it."

Ada almost asked him what he meant. She was learning that Kaelen's throwaway lines were often doors left open on rooms she wasn't ready to enter. She filed it away, the way she filed away every unlit road, and picked up the tap hammer.

"Marta," she said aloud, to the cellar, to the woman upstairs frozen mid-laugh with a mug in her hand, "put it on my account."

The first barrel took three blows. The smell that rose up was so beautiful she nearly wept—apples and honey and smoke and the particular warmth of a thing made slowly by someone who loved making it. It spread across the flagstones and ran toward the drain, and with every gallon that escaped, the golden light above them dimmed a shade.

The second barrel she opened with one blow, angry now, efficient, a smuggler breaching cargo. The third. The fourth. With each one the music upstairs faltered—the fiddler's eight bars stuttering, stretching, the dancers' laughter developing gaps like a song with teeth knocked out.

On the fifth barrel, the screaming started.

Not terror-screaming. Worse. It was the sound of people waking from a good dream into a room they didn't recognize, reaching backward for something warm and finding only air. Ada climbed the stairs two at a time and came out into a square where the amber light was guttering like a dying lamp, where forty people stood scattered among dead lanterns, holding mugs of nothing, staring at each other as though meeting after years apart.

In a way, they were.

Wren found her mother and clung to her, sobbing, saying *it was so good, why did it stop, it was so good.* Old Marta stood in the doorway of her house with her spoon still behind her ear, looking down at the empty barrels Ada had dragged into the square as proof, as confession, as evidence for a trial she expected to face.

For a long moment nobody spoke. Then Marta looked at Ada—really looked, with eyes that remembered every year of every festival—and said, in a voice like gravel wrapped in flannel:

"You poured out my reserve."

"I did."

"To save us."

"I did."

Marta Tulliver considered this for the length of one long exhale. Then she took the spoon from behind her ear, pointed it at Ada, and said, "You'll help me press next year's crop. Every day. No arguments." And then, before Ada could answer, the old woman turned and bellowed at her frozen-and-thawing neighbors: "Fires are out, you great weeping fools! Someone get a flame from the hearth and let's have soup. Soup fixes what cider can't."

The square exhaled. People moved. Grief became bustle became, gradually, something survivable. Ada watched Wren wipe her face and pick up a basket and start gathering dead lanterns, and she understood that this too was a kind of answer to prayer—not the wish granted, but the wish survived.

Kaelen appeared at her elbow with two bowls of soup that had achieved existence with miraculous speed. "You know," he said, "most people would've hesitated longer."

"Most people haven't spent their lives moving goods they shouldn't through places they shouldn't. You learn when a road is closed for good." She took the bowl. Her hands were still trembling, but less. "It doesn't mean you don't mourn the place you can't go back to."

"No," he said quietly. "It doesn't."

They ate standing up, shoulder to shoulder, while Bramblewick rebuilt its ordinary evening around the hole where paradise had been. And Ada told herself the ache in her chest was only tiredness.

She was lying, and she knew it. The ache was tuition. The covenant had just shown her the price list, and somewhere ahead, at the top of a mountain, there was a Veilstone waiting to charge her the full amount.

* * *

They left before dawn, taking the high road east, because Kaelen said the waypoint stones would show clearest in the hour before sunrise and Ada said the hour before sunrise was the only hour Maren Voss's soldiers weren't awake, and between the mystical and the practical, practical had kept her alive longer.

They were both wrong.

The soldiers were in the pass at first light—six of them, grey-cloaked, methodical, spreading across the road with the unhurried confidence of people who had been told exactly where to wait. At their center stood a woman Ada recognized from a dozen terrified reports: tall, iron-grey, with a burn scar along one cheek and eyes that held no cruelty whatsoever, which somehow made everything worse.

Inquisitor Maren Voss lifted one hand, and six crossbows rose with it.

"Ada Delacroix," she called, her voice carrying clean down the pass. "The guildmaster's records name you. The shrines name you. And now a village in a time-loop names you, because whatever broke it left a mark on the air that my order can read from thirty miles away." She stepped forward. "I've burned a hundred false prophets. I don't think you're one. That's why I'm asking instead of shooting. Come down, and talk to me."

Behind Ada, very quietly, Kaelen murmured, "There's a side path. We can run."

Ada looked at the crossbows. She looked at the narrow goat track that might or might not exist anymore, given how the mountains had been behaving. She looked at Inquisitor Maren Voss, who had asked instead of shot, and who was the first person in authority Ada had met in this whole cursed season who seemed interested in conversation.

Her legs decided before she did.

They carried her forward, one step, into the shadow of the order that wanted all the gods dead—and wanted, apparently, to start with a chat.
