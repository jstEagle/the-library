## 10. Maren's Sermon

The cell smelled of wet stone and lamp oil, and Ada had decided within the first hour that if she was going to be imprisoned, she was at least going to be insufferable about it.

"Your guards have terrible posture," she called through the bars. "The tall one leans on his spear like it owes him money. You should fix that before someone takes it off him."

From the corridor came the measured click of boots. Inquisitor Maren Voss appeared with a lantern in one hand and a book in the other—a real book, leather-bound, its spine cracked from use—and settled herself on the stool across from the cell as though she'd been assigned there by fate.

"You've been awake for two days," Maren said.

"I sleep when I'm bored. Your sermons are keeping me very awake."

Something flickered at the corner of the woman's mouth. It might have been amusement. It might have been indigestion. With Maren it was impossible to tell, and Ada suspected that was deliberate.

"You're not what I expected," Maren said, opening the book across her knees. "The Delacroix of Thornfell. The ghost of the passes. I imagined someone harder."

"I'm plenty hard. I'm just also hungry, and hunger makes me chatty."

Maren studied her for a long moment, then did something that genuinely startled Ada: she passed a heel of bread and a wedge of pale cheese through the bars.

"Eat," she said. "I don't interrogate starving people. They agree with anything."

Ada took the bread. "That's the most sensible thing any zealot has ever said to me."

* * *

**Maren**

She had burned forty-one shrines in three years, and she remembered every one.

Not the fires—fires were forgettable, fire was just work—but the shrines themselves. The little clay figure of the harvest mother in Aldenmere with a child's thumbprint pressed into its shoulder. The river shrine where an old man left cups of tea for a god who never drank them. The way people looked at her afterward: not hatred, mostly. Relief, sometimes. Grief, often. And once—only once—gratitude, from a woman whose sister had drowned herself at a prayer-stone because the god of that stone had demanded it.

Maren wrote all of it down. That was the discipline. A faith you could not audit was a faith that would eat you.

The smuggler in the cell was complicating things, which annoyed her. Prisoners were supposed to be simple: terrified, or defiant, or bargaining. Ada Delacroix was *conversational*. She asked questions back. She'd made the tall guard laugh twice yesterday, and he'd apologized to Maren both times with the stricken face of a man who'd petted a wolf by accident.

Tonight Maren brought the question she'd been saving.

"The sigil over Thornfell," she said. "You saw it. Everyone in three valleys saw it. Tell me what you felt."

Ada chewed her cheese thoughtfully. "Like watching a door open onto a room where something enormous has been waiting in the dark. And hungry."

"Good. Honest." Maren leaned forward. "Now tell me why that frightened you less than it should have."

"It didn't frighten me less. I'm just good at walking scared. It's most of my job." Ada set the cheese rind down. "Why do you want to know how I felt? You already know what you believe. Gods come back, gods starve, gods take. Burn everything, sever the realms, mortals stand on their own feet. I could recite your sermon better than your guards can, and they've heard it more."

"Recite it, then."

"'We were children who learned to pray before we learned to walk,'" Ada said, pitching her voice low and solemn, doing a fair impression of Maren's cadence, "'and no child survives who is never weaned. The gods are not our parents. They are our crutch, our leash, our open vein. Severance is not cruelty. Severance is the last kindness we do ourselves.'" She stopped. "Did I get the ending right? In the square you said something after that. Something quieter."

Maren was silent for a moment. "I said: 'I am sorry.'"

"That's the part your soldiers don't repeat," Ada observed.

"No," Maren agreed. "It isn't."

* * *

**Ada**

By the third day, Ada had mapped the prison entirely by ear.

Two guards outside, changing at dawn and dusk—the lean one whistled off-key, the tall one didn't whistle but hummed when he thought no one listened, some marching song gone soft and domestic. A kitchen below, so the walls carried sound up through old flues. A drain in her cell floor that gurgled toward somewhere lower, which meant somewhere out. Sorrel would have been proud. Sorrel would also be furious with her for getting caught, and the thought landed in her chest like a stone, so she set it aside and went back to needling her jailer.

Maren came every evening with the book. On the fourth evening she brought two stools, sat inside the cell itself, and lit a second candle.

"You realize," Ada said, "that sitting in here with me is either very confident or very stupid."

"Both can be true." Maren folded her hands. "Today I want to talk about the farmer."

Ada went still. "What farmer?"

"Floodmere. Three weeks ago. A man prayed for rain for his fields, and the sky gave him his whole river at once. His wife and daughter were swept away." Maren watched her carefully. "You were seen near Floodmere. You pulled a boy from the water."

"He was drowning. I didn't check his theology first."

"No. You wouldn't. That's what interests me." Maren's voice stayed level, but something underneath it shifted, tectonic and slow. "I have watched faithful people run from their own answered prayers. I have watched priests refuse to look at what their gods actually do. But you walked into the floodwater after it happened. Why?"

Ada looked at the candle flame for a while.

"Because the gods aren't cruel," she said finally. "They're starving. There's a difference, and nobody in your order seems to know it. A fed god grants rain gently. A starving god grabs whatever worship it can feel and pours everything it has through the crack. That farmer got a flood the way a beggar given a whole loaf might choke on it. The sin isn't theirs. It isn't even really the god's. It's ours—we broke the thing that used to feed them properly, and now they're feral, and feral things hurt people whether they mean to or not."

"And your solution?"

"To feed them properly again. Reforge what was broken. Boundaries, terms—they wake up *whole*, Maren. Not dead, not severed. Fed and bounded, like a hearth instead of a wildfire."

Maren was quiet for a long time. Then she laughed—one short, startled breath of it, quickly suppressed, as though laughter were contraband she'd forgotten she was carrying.

"What?" Ada demanded.

"You sound like my mother," Maren said. "She kept a hearth-god's little statue on our stove. Fed it crumbs. Said a cold house makes cruel people." Her jaw tightened. "She died praying to it during the Grey Fever. It didn't save her. It didn't even *answer*."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be useful." Maren stood, gathering her book. "Tell me where the Veilstone road begins, and I'll see you released. Not freed—released. Watched. Alive."

"And if I don't?"

"Then in six days I march for the Veil, and you stay here, and when the severance comes you'll be safe and everyone you ever smuggled for will be free of gods forever." She paused at the cell door. "You'd survive it, Delacroix. That's the worst part. Severance doesn't kill anyone. It just leaves the world quieter. Emptier. Like a house after a funeral."

She left. Ada sat in the dark doing arithmetic: six days, a drain, two guards, one whistler.

And somewhere out in the hills, a charming liar with a dwindling supply of miracles, who absolutely knew she'd been taken, and who was constitutionally incapable of leaving well enough alone.

"Hold on, Kaelen," she murmured. "And please, please be smarter than you look."

* * *

**Maren**

On the fifth night, Maren dreamed of the tea cup.

The old man at the river shrine, setting out tea for a god who never drank. She had confiscated the cup. She still had it, wrapped in cloth at the bottom of her baggage, and she could not have told anyone why.

She woke before dawn and went to the cell, earlier than her habit, and found Ada awake—as always—as though the woman slept in shifts around her own thoughts.

"I had a teacher once," Maren said, without preamble. "Before the Order. A village priestess. Kind woman. Foolish woman. She taught me every rite of the old covenant, and she believed every word, and when the Fever took half our village she prayed until her hands bled, and the god she served—" her voice caught, and she hated it, and continued anyway, "—the god she served was already too weak to answer. Do you understand? I don't hate the gods because they're evil. I hate them because they are *weak*, and we built our whole world on their weakness, and when it fails us we will call it mystery and forgive them, and our children will die of the same forgiving."

Ada was quiet. Then: "Your priestess. Did she know the covenant was broken?"

"Everyone knew something was wrong. No one knew why."

"But you might have known why, if anyone had told you the truth. Three generations ago the wardens failed—or fled, or were killed—and the covenant broke, and the gods began to starve, and everyone since has prayed into a silence and called it mystery." Ada pressed her hand flat against the stone wall. "Maren. I'm not asking you to believe me. I'm asking you to consider that the thing you hate—the silence, the weakness, the dying children—it has a cause, and the cause is fixable, and you are marching to destroy the only tool that fixes it."

"Or the tool feeds them and binds us to them for another thousand years of floods and fevers and prayers that cost more than they give."

"Yes," Ada said simply. "That's the wager. Both of us are betting lives on what we can't prove. The difference is I've read the wardens' records and you've read your own grief."

Maren stood in the doorway a long moment. The lamp between them guttered and steadied.

"You argue like a smuggler," she said at last. "Everything's a toll, everything's negotiable, everything has a back road."

"And you argue like a woman carrying a body she can't put down." Ada's voice gentled. "I'm not your enemy, Inquisitor. I'm the person trying to make sure your mother's hearth-god wakes up strong enough to answer the next fever-prayer. Think about which of us your priestess would have wanted at that altar."

Maren turned to go. At the corridor's end she stopped, and did not turn around, and said:

"The column marches tomorrow at first light. There's a drain behind your cell's east wall. The flue carries kitchen smoke; the mortar's rotten; I've known for two days." She paused. "The tall guard sleeps heavy. The whistler doesn't. Choose your moment."

Then she was gone, boots clicking away into the dark, and Ada stared after her with her mouth slightly open.

* * *

**Ada**

Well.

Ada sat down on the cot and laughed until her ribs hurt, quietly, helplessly, the way you laugh at a joke told by someone who'd deny it under torture.

The zealous inquisitor had just handed her an escape route. Either this was a trap—which it wasn't; Maren Voss didn't have a deceptive bone in her formidable body—or it was something far stranger: a woman so honest she couldn't bear to win dishonestly, giving her prisoner a fair chance because some splinter of the argument had gotten under her armor.

"Grudging respect," Ada murmured to the empty cell. "Who knew that was a door too."

She worked the loose mortar with her thumbnail while she thought. Six days of debate, and here was what she'd learned: Maren couldn't be defeated, because Maren wasn't fighting to win. She was fighting to pay a debt—to every villager who'd prayed and been abandoned, to her mother, to the priestess with bleeding hands. You couldn't out-argue grief. You could only show it a better grave.

But grief could hesitate. Grief had hesitated tonight.

The mortar crumbled. Cold air breathed up from the flue, smelling of ash and pine and, faintly, impossibly, of woodsmoke and something sweeter underneath—ozone, the smell Kaelen left behind him like a signature.

Somewhere out there, he was already moving. She'd have bet the guild's last coin on it.

Ada pulled the grate free, wedged it back to look innocent, and started counting the hours until the whistler's shift ended.

"Coming, Kaelen," she whispered into the dark. "Try not to spend any more of yourself getting me out. I've got plans for what's left of you."

Above the mountains, unseen behind cloud, the thirteenth moon turned another degree toward full—and in her cell, in the corridor, in the sleeping camp beyond, every lamp flame bent briefly westward, toward the Veil, like grass leaning into wind.
