## 11. Escape Through the Hollow Road

The cell smelled of lye and old blood, and Ada had spent three days memorizing it.

Three days of stone walls sweating cold, of Maren Voss's measured footsteps passing her door at hours that had no pattern—deliberately no pattern, because Maren understood that a smuggler's mind craved rhythm and would starve without it. Three days of bread and water and questions delivered in that infuriatingly gentle voice, as though they were two neighbors discussing the price of wool.

"You say you've never felt a god's touch," Maren had said on the second day, seated across from her cell with a lantern between them, her hands folded like a woman at prayer. "And yet the sigil burned over Thornfell the night you were in the shrine. Coincidence is a theology too, Ada Delacroix. It simply requires more faith than mine."

Ada had said nothing. She was good at saying nothing. She'd been raised on it, practically weaned on silence and hidden doors.

But three days was long enough. On the third night, when the torches in the corridor guttered low and the guard outside her door began to snore, she heard something else beneath the snoring—a sound like wind moving through a place where wind should not be.

She knew that sound. Every smuggler in Thornfell knew it. It was the sound of a door opening where no door had been drawn.

The back wall of her cell rippled.

Not melted, not broken—*rippled*, like the surface of a dark pond disturbed by a dropped stone, and then Kaelen stepped through it, solid and impossible, with soot on his jaw and a grin that suggested he found his own cleverness delightful.

"Evening," he said. "Terrible accommodations. I've stayed in nicer tombs."

Ada crossed the cell in four strides and hit him—not a blow, exactly, but the full weight of her relief dressed up as violence, her fists against his chest, his arms coming around her before she could decide whether she meant it. "You're supposed to be halfway to the waypoint," she said into his shoulder. "I told you to go."

"And I told you I'm terrible at following directions from beautiful women who are trying to get themselves martyred." He pulled back just far enough to look at her. In the dark, his eyes had that faint luminous quality again, the one she'd been telling herself for weeks was lamplight or imagination. "Sorrel's map says the Hollow Road's mouth is under the chapel Maren burned. The one with the shrine to Veyra in the crypt. We can be through before dawn if we run."

"The Hollow Road." Ada kept her voice flat. "That's a story we tell new recruits to scare them off the mountain passes. Toll-roads and ghosts and—"

"Mostly true, actually." He caught her hand. His fingers were warm, warmer than they should have been, warm enough that she noticed it now when she hadn't before. "Come on. Argue with me while we walk."

They argued while they walked. It was, Ada would think later, the most honest conversation they'd ever had, conducted in whispers through the belly of Maren's fortress—past sleeping barracks, past a storeroom where Kaelen paused to steal an apple with the unashamed delight of a boy, down a stair that wasn't on any plan of the building because Kaelen had *asked the building*, which was a sentence Ada filed away to panic about later.

"The tolls take memories," Kaelen said quietly, as they crouched in the shadow of the ruined chapel, moonlight falling through its broken roof onto scorched stone. "Small ones, usually. The taste of a meal. The name of a childhood dog. The road decides what it wants. You pay, you pass. Refuse, and it keeps you."

"Keeps you how?"

"It doesn't matter how. Nobody comes back to describe it." He looked at her, and something in his face made her stomach drop. "Ada. If I tell you to keep walking, you keep walking. Whatever happens. Promise me."

"I don't make promises I intend to break."

"That's what worries me."

Then the torches bloomed.

A dozen of them, ringing the chapel ruins, and Maren Voss stepped into the light with her white cloak immaculate despite three days of siege, her soldiers fanning out behind her with the smooth discipline of people who had done this many times. She did not look surprised. That was the worst part. She looked *sad*.

"I hoped it wouldn't come to this," Maren said. "I truly did. You're not a heretic, Ada. You're a good woman being used by something that wears a man's face."

"Maren," Ada said, edging backward toward the crypt stair, "you're standing between me and a door. You know how I feel about doors."

"I know exactly how you feel about doors." Almost a smile. "It's why I liked you."

An arrow sang past Ada's ear and buried itself in the chapel wall. Then another. The soldiers were closing, boots crunching on ash, and Kaelen put himself in front of her with his arms slightly out—the oldest gesture in the world, and absurd, twelve men against one body—and Ada saw him close his eyes and *reach*.

What happened next, she would never fully be able to describe, and she had described it many times over the years since, always imperfectly.

The air changed pressure. The torch flames all leaned toward Kaelen at once, like flowers turning to sun, and the nearest soldier—a young man, hardly older than Ada—stumbled backward with his hands over his ears, weeping without knowing why. Kaelen raised one hand, and light came off his skin the way heat comes off a forge, gold-white and wrong and *beautiful*, and every shadow in the ruin bent away from him as though afraid.

"Veyra's son," Maren breathed. Her face went through something complicated—horror, recognition, and beneath both, a terrible flicker of vindication. "So it's true. So the god-touched are real."

"He's not god-touched," Ada heard herself say. "He's god-*forsaken*. There's a difference. Ask his mother."

Kaelen laughed—a real laugh, startled out of him—and the light around him stuttered like a candle in wind. And Ada saw it then, clearly, in that unforgiving glow: the fine tremor in his fingers. The way his knees locked to hold him upright. The gray creeping at the edge of his aura like frost on a window.

He was spending himself. Burning divinity he barely had left, buying seconds with coins he couldn't replace.

"Stop," she said. "Kaelen, stop, you'll—"

"Go," he said, and his voice cracked down the middle. "Crypt. Now. I'm right behind you."

"You're lying."

"I'm right behind you," he said again, gentler, and turned back to face the soldiers, and Ada ran.

She took the crypt stairs three at a time, into cold and dark and the smell of old incense soaked into stone. Behind her, light flared through the doorway like a second dawn. She heard shouting, and then she heard nothing at all, which was worse, and she reached the shrine of Veyra—a small carved threshold-stone, humble, worn smooth by centuries of anonymous hands—and pressed her palm against it because she didn't know what else to do.

*Let him follow me,* she thought, with the desperate clarity of a prayer she didn't believe in. *Whatever you gave up for us, whatever it cost you—let him follow.*

The stone was warm under her hand.

Behind her, footsteps on the stairs—two sets, uneven, one dragging—and Kaelen came down into the dark half-carrying himself, singed and swaying and grinning like an idiot, with Maren's soldiers hammering uselessly at a sealed door above.

"How?" Ada demanded, grabbing his arm to hold him up.

"Bought us a corridor." He winced. "Cost me... hm. I'll tell you when I remember what it cost. That's rather the point."

The wall behind the shrine was already open. Not rippling this time—*open*, yawning, a passage of absolute darkness breathing slow cold air that smelled of rain on deep stone and something older underneath, something like dust and like sleep. The Hollow Road. Real. Waiting.

Kaelen looked at it the way other men looked at gallows.

"Together?" Ada said.

"Together," he said, and took her hand.

* * *

Walking the Hollow Road was like walking inside a held breath.

There was floor under them—smooth, pale, faintly warm, like skin—and walls that were sometimes stone and sometimes something softer, and light that came from nowhere, a gray luminescence like the last minute after sunset stretched into eternity. Their footsteps made no echo. When Ada spoke, her voice sounded close even when she whispered from ten paces ahead.

Toll-gates stood across the road at intervals: arches of black wood, older than any tree, each hung with small bells that rang without wind. At the first arch, Ada stopped, and the darkness gathered itself in front of her into a shape—not quite a figure, more a suggestion of attention, patient and vast.

"What do you want?" Ada asked it, because her mother had taught her you address powers directly or not at all.

The answer arrived without words, settling into her mind like silt into still water: *a small thing. a warm thing. give.*

Ada searched herself. She found the memory waiting, almost offered: a market morning years ago, the smell of cinnamon buns from the baker's stall near the guildhouse, Sorrel buying her one with exaggerated ceremony, flour on his knuckles. She held it up mentally like a coin.

The toll took it.

It didn't hurt. That was the horror of it—it simply *left*, a clean little absence, and when she tried afterward to picture the baker's stall she got the shape of it but not the smell, and Sorrel's face but not the flour, and a vague sense that something sweet had once mattered to her on a morning she could no longer name. She stood there grieving a grief with no object, and Kaelen watched her with an expression she couldn't read.

"You didn't have to pay that one," he said quietly.

"Tolls are tolls."

"At the next gate—" he started, and stopped, and she saw him decide not to say whatever he'd been about to say. "Never mind."

At the second arch, the toll wanted something from Kaelen. She knew because he went still in a different way, a deeper way, and the gray attention hung before him for a long time, longer than it had lingered for her. When it withdrew, he stood blinking in the sourceless light, and when Ada asked him what he'd paid he said, "Something about a harbor," with a small puzzled frown, as if the word *harbor* were a stranger's name.

At the third arch, everything went wrong.

The toll didn't approach Ada at all. It went straight past her—she felt its attention slide over her like water over glass—and settled on Kaelen, and this time it didn't suggest. It *demanded*. The pressure of it filled the corridor; the bells on the arch shivered; Ada's teeth ached with it.

"No," Kaelen said aloud, to the darkness. "Not that one."

The pressure increased.

"What does it want?" Ada grabbed his sleeve.

"My name." His voice was very steady, which is how she knew he was terrified. "The first one. The one my mother gave me before I had any other. It's the only thing left of me that's still—" he gestured vaguely at himself, at the fading gold under his skin— "*mine.* Everything else has been spent or traded or burned. If I give it up, there won't be enough left of Kaelen to reach the Veilstone. There'll just be a polite stranger who looks like me."

"Then refuse. Walk away. We'll find another way."

"There is no other way. This is the road, or it's Maren's cells, or it's the end of everything." He laughed, short and bitter. "You know, my mother warned me about this road. Said it takes exactly what you can least afford. Gods built it, back when gods still bargained honestly."

"Kaelen—"

"Wait." He held up a hand, and turned to the darkness, and Ada watched him negotiate—watched his lips move, watched the pressure bend and shift, watched him argue with something ancient in a language that made her fillings ache. And then, slowly, the pressure eased, and the arch's bells rang once, softly, satisfied.

"What did you give it?" Ada asked. "What did you trade?"

"A fragment." He flexed his hand, and the light that answered was dimmer than before, thinner, a coal instead of a flame. "Divinity. What's left of it. A piece of the piece." He said it lightly, but his legs buckled as he stepped through the arch, and Ada caught him, and for a moment he let his whole weight rest against her, his forehead against her hair.

"You're killing yourself," she whispered. "Gate by gate. For me."

"For the covenant," he corrected, muffled. "For the world. For the general principle of things continuing to exist." A pause. "Also for you. I've stopped pretending the order of those matters."

Ada pulled back far enough to look at him. In the gray light his face was hollowed, younger and older at once, and there was flour on his jaw—no, ash, ash from the burning chapel—and she realized with a lurch that she could not remember whether she'd ever seen him eat, properly, at a table, and that some part of her mind had already begun keeping a ledger of him the way the road kept ledgers of everyone.

"Why?" she asked. It was the question she'd been carrying since the cells, since the crossroads, maybe since the first night he'd poached her roads with that insufferable smile. "You could have gone beyond the veil's reach. Your mother offered you that. You told me. Why are you here, spending yourself down to nothing?"

"Because I watched my mother give up everything she was to fix what the gods broke," he said, "and I swore I'd never understand her, and then I met a woman who hides an entire city inside her roads and calls it survival, and I understood her completely." His hand came up, hesitated, and touched her cheek—carefully, the way you touch something you're afraid of breaking or of being broken by. "I love you, Ada Delacroix. Terrible timing. No expectations. I just needed you to hear it once, in case the road takes the rest of me and whatever walks out the other side forgets to say it."

The words landed in her chest like a struck bell.

She should have said something wise. She was, by reputation, quick-tongued; she had talked her way past customs officers and cutthroats and one memorable tax collector. Instead she stood in the underworld's antechamber holding a dying god's son by the front of his shirt and said, with tremendous dignity:

"Well. That explains the apple theft. Sentimental idiots steal fruit."

And then she kissed him, because some doors you don't draw on a map—you just walk through.

When they broke apart, the road had gone very quiet, in the way of places that are politely pretending not to watch. Kaelen was smiling, and crying a little, and trying to hide the crying, and failing, and Ada decided to let him fail.

"Come on," she said, taking his hand. His fingers were cooler now. She noted it and hated that she noted it. "We've paid our tolls. Let's see what your mother's mountain looks like from below."

They walked on together into the gray, and the road received them, and somewhere far behind them the bells of the third arch rang once more—soft, almost kind—as if marking their passage the way a shopkeeper marks a debt.

Ada did not look back. But she counted the gates ahead, and there were more of them than Kaelen had promised, and the fourth arch was already visible in the distance, its bells hanging silent, its darkness gathering itself to ask its question.

And she understood, with a smuggler's instinct for a rigged game, that the road had not finished pricing them yet—that it saved the steepest tolls for those closest to their destination, and that whatever it would ask of her at the final gate, she was beginning to fear she already knew the shape of the answer.

Kaelen squeezed her hand. "Almost there," he said.

The bells began to ring.
