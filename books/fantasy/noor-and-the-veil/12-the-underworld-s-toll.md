## 12. The Underworld's Toll

The entrance to the Hollow Road did not look like a road at all.

It looked like a mistake—a seam in the cliff face where the stone had been sewn together badly, the edges puckered and wrong, as though the mountain had once been cut open and healed by someone in a hurry. Ada had known it was there for years, the way she knew most of her roads: as a shape in the landscape that didn't quite belong. She'd never once considered going through it. Smugglers had rules about places like this, the same way sailors had rules about green flashes on the horizon and whistling below decks. You didn't need to know *why*. You just needed to know not to.

"You're staring at it," Kaelen said behind her.

"I'm assessing it."

"You're staring at it with your whole face."

Ada turned. He stood a few paces back with his pack slung over one shoulder, looking infuriatingly unbothered for a man who had, three days ago, traded a piece of his own soul to buy her out of an Inquisitor's cell. He'd been quieter since. Not grim—Kaelen didn't do grim, or rather he refused to, the way some men refused to drown—but there were pauses in him now where there hadn't been before. Little silences he filled too quickly.

"It's a toll road," she said. "Everything is. I've paid passage fees my whole life. Coin, favors, information." She looked back at the seam. "This one takes memories."

"And you know this how?"

"Sorrel's records. Page forty-one of a book he made me promise never to read until I absolutely had to." She tapped her temple. "I absolutely had to."

"Of course he did," Kaelen said softly. "That man left you instructions like a mother leaving notes in a lunchbox."

"He left me a map, a debt, and a guilt complex. Very thorough."

But she didn't move toward the seam yet. Because here was the thing Sorrel's book had said, in his cramped, furious handwriting—the handwriting of a man writing fast because he wanted to be done before he lost his nerve: *The Road takes what you offer, but it weighs what you carry. Pay small. Pay often. Never let it choose for you.*

And beneath that, underlined twice: *Do not go in loving someone. It will take the wrong things first.*

Ada glanced at Kaelen, who was examining the cliff face with the studied interest of a man pretending not to watch her watch him.

"Well," she said briskly. "Good thing neither of us loves anyone."

"Tragic, really. We'll have to muddle through."

She almost smiled. Then she stepped through the seam, and the world folded shut behind them like a book closing.

* * *

Inside, the Hollow Road was not dark, which somehow made it worse.

Light came from everywhere and nowhere—pale, sourceless, the color of the sky just before snow. The road itself was smooth grey stone, wide enough for four abreast, curving gently downward into a distance that refused to resolve. There were no walls exactly; the light simply thickened at the edges until seeing further became something the eye declined to do. And along both sides, at intervals, stood the tollgates.

They were beautiful. That was the trap of them, Ada thought. Little arches of white wood strung with bells that made no sound, each one attended by a figure that might have been carved from candle wax—smooth-faced, sexless, patient. They did not speak. They held out their hands, and when you gave them what they asked, they nodded, and the gate swung open, and you walked through feeling very slightly lighter than before.

The first gate stopped them an hour in.

The attendant extended its palm, and into Ada's mind rose—not images, exactly, but *labels*. Memories presenting themselves for inspection like goods at customs. The taste of burnt porridge from her seventh winter. The name of a boy who'd kissed her behind the tannery when she was fifteen and whose name she had, until this moment, been able to recall perfectly. The route through the reed marshes south of Thornfell, low-value, easily replaced.

Small things. Cheap things. Exactly what Sorrel had promised.

"The porridge," she said aloud, because saying it made it real. "I'll pay the porridge."

The attendant's hand closed. Something slid out of her—gently, without pain, the way a splinter leaves—and she felt the absence immediately, a small smooth hole where seven-year-old Ada's breakfast disaster used to live. She tested the gap the way you test a missing tooth. Gone. She couldn't even remember what she'd missed.

"That's horrifying," Kaelen said quietly.

"It's efficient." She walked through the gate. "Come on."

At the second gate, the attendant turned its blank face to Kaelen first, and Ada watched him go still in a way she'd learned to recognize. His hand drifted to his chest—an old habit, she'd noticed, touching the place where his divinity used to sit like a coal under the skin.

"No," he said to the attendant, pleasantly. "Not that one."

The hand stayed open. Patient. The bells hung silent.

"Kaelen." Ada kept her voice level. "What's it asking?"

"Nothing important." He smiled at her—that easy, crooked smile that had gotten him fed in a dozen villages and forgiven in half of them. "It does this. Pokes around. Finds the shiny thing and asks for it."

"What's the shiny thing?"

"A song my mother used to hum." He said it lightly, but his fingers pressed harder against his sternum. "It can't have it. That's the whole point of—" He stopped. Recalibrated. "I'll pay something else."

He reached into himself, visibly, the way a man reaches into his coat for coin, and whatever he pulled out, he gave without ceremony. The gate opened. But as they passed under the arch, Ada saw him falter—one half-step, barely a stumble—and when he straightened, he was frowning faintly at his own hands.

"What did you give?" she asked.

"I don't remember." He laughed, and it came out wrong, thin at the edges. "Which is rather the point, isn't it."

* * *

By the fourth gate, Ada had developed a system.

She catalogued herself as they walked, between tolls, sorting her own mind like cargo. Here: the guild routes, load-bearing, irreplaceable. Here: Sorrel's voice saying *check the knot twice, girl, the mountain doesn't forgive*, load-bearing in a different way. Here: her mother's face, which she touched only briefly and then wrapped in seven layers of refusal, because the attendant at the fifth gate had already asked for it once, and she had paid three other memories instead—the smell of the Thornfell market in autumn, her tenth birthday, the feel of her first successful solo run—to make it stop asking.

The attendants always accepted substitutes. They weren't cruel. That was the worst part. They were *fair*, in the way of scales and ledgers, and fairness offered no purchase for anger. You could hate a thief. You couldn't hate arithmetic.

Kaelen, meanwhile, was bleeding.

Not visibly. Visibly he was fine—joking about the attendants' fashion sense, inventing backstories for them ("that one's called Bramwell, he collects regrets about unsent letters, look at him"), keeping her spirits up with the relentless competence of a man who had decided cheerfulness was a job. But every time a gate faced him, he paid big. She watched it happen. Where she paid in coppers, he paid in silver—no, more than silver. She paid in memories; he seemed to pay in *pieces*. Small structural elements of himself. After the sixth gate he no longer whistled while walking, and when she asked why, he said, "Do I usually whistle?" with genuine curiosity, and then went pale when he heard his own voice say it.

After the seventh gate, he forgot the word for dusk. He knew the concept—pointed at the dimming, sourceless light and called it "that time"—but the word was gone, a hole shaped exactly like *dusk*, and he stood there turning the absence over like a man patting his pockets for keys.

"Stop," Ada said.

"It's fine—"

"*Stop.*" She caught his sleeve. "You're paying double for me. Every gate. I've been watching. When it asks me for something hard, you step in front and give it something worse so it'll leave me alone."

The silence stretched. Somewhere ahead, the eighth gate waited, bells silent, attendant patient as a held breath.

"It's not double," Kaelen said finally. "It's just... concentrated. My memories are worth more. Fewer years in them, denser packing." He tried the smile again. It didn't hold. "You have decades of little things. Porridge. Birthdays. I've got maybe thirty years total, and half of those are—" he touched his chest again, that coal-cold place, "—not really mine to spend anyway."

"So you're spending the rest."

"I'm spending what keeps you whole." He said it simply, without drama, and that simplicity was what frightened her. "Ada. In a few days you're going to walk up a mountain and give away everything you love. Every person, every warmth, every reason you get up in the morning—dissolved into a rock so the gods don't starve and we don't starve with them. I've done the arithmetic on that. I've had longer to do it than you have." His voice dropped. "So no, I'm not going to stand aside and let this road take your mother's face a few days early because I was busy protecting my whistling."

Ada discovered she was gripping his sleeve hard enough to hurt them both. She let go.

"You idiot," she said, and her voice cracked in the middle, and she hated it. "You absolute, self-sacrificing, *god-touched* idiot."

"There it is. The tenderness."

"I will *hit* you."

"Please don't, I've forgotten how to duck."

And that was the thing—she couldn't tell anymore how much was joke. That was the dread of it, walking beside him through the pale light: she was watching her own future performed in miniature. This was what the Veilstone would make of her. A person with smooth holes where the load-bearing pieces used to be, smiling too quickly over the gaps, forgetting the word for dusk. She looked at Kaelen's careful, bright face and saw the woman she was scheduled to become, and the sight of it made her chest ache in a way no wound ever had.

Because here was the other thing, the thing Sorrel had underlined twice, the thing she'd been carrying since the first gate:

*Do not go in loving someone.*

Too late. Far, far too late. And the road knew it. She could feel it knowing—every time the attendant's blank face turned toward her and presented its labels, the labels had started including him. His laugh. The specific way he said her name, like it was a place he was glad to arrive. The third gate had asked. She'd paid her grandmother's kitchen instead. The sixth had asked. She'd paid the sound of rain on the guildhall roof.

The road was teaching her the price of the mountain in installments.

* * *

They made camp at what Kaelen insisted was a waystation, though it was only a wider stretch of road where the light pooled warmer and the stone rose into something like benches. Ada built no fire—there was nothing to burn—and they ate cold travel bread while the sourceless glow held steady, dayless and nightless.

"How many gates left?" she asked.

"Three. Maybe four if the road's feeling literary."

"And after that?"

"After that we come up under Veyra's threshold." He tore his bread with unnecessary focus. "My mother. Who is going to be *thrilled* to see what I've spent."

"She'll understand."

"She'll understand *exactly*, which is worse. She gave up everything too. She'll look at me and see the family resemblance." He chewed. Swallowed. "Ada."

"Mm."

"When we're through—if I've lost things, important things—I want you to tell me. Whatever you notice. Don't be kind about it. Kindness lets holes stay hidden until you fall in one."

She looked at him across the warm pool of light: this exiled scrap of a god, spending himself down to mortal change to keep a smuggler's memories intact, asking her to audit his erosion because he trusted her to be honest more than he trusted himself to notice.

"All right," she said. "But same terms. If I start losing things I shouldn't lose, you tell me. Even if it's ugly. Especially if it's ugly."

"Deal." He hesitated, then added, lightly, too lightly: "For the record, there are things I'd pay gladly to forget. The cell Maren put you in. The sound the door made. Those can go anytime."

"Keep them," Ada said. "If you forget why you hate her, you might start trusting her."

"God forbid." He smiled. Then, softer, almost to himself: "There are also things I'd pay anything to *keep*. Just so you know. The road keeps asking. I keep saying no. It's becoming a negotiation."

"What things?"

He looked at her for a long moment, and the sourceless light made his eyes strange, pale as the road itself, and she thought—he's going to say it, he's finally going to say it—

The bells rang.

All of them, down the whole length of the Hollow Road, every silent bell on every silent gate ringing at once, a sound like ice cracking in deep water. Kaelen was on his feet instantly. Ahead, in the direction they needed to go, the light had changed—gone bruised and violet, and out of that color came a pressure Ada felt in her teeth, in the scar tissue of old memories already paid, a sensation like being *remembered* by something enormous.

"Oh no," Kaelen breathed. "No, it shouldn't be able to reach in here—"

"What is it?"

"The thirteenth turn." He grabbed her hand, and his grip was shaking. "The moon's turning, Ada. It's reaching *into the underworld*. The gates are opening—all of them—and everything the road has ever collected—"

Down the long curve of the Hollow Road, in the bruised violet distance, the tollgates were swinging open one by one, and out of them, pale as candle wax and moving with terrible patience, the attendants were stepping free of their arches—hundreds of them, thousands, all facing the two small figures by the dead fire, all extending their open hands.

Not collecting now.

*Coming to collect.*
