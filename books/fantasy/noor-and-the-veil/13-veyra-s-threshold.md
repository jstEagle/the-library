## 13. Veyra's Threshold

The mountain stopped pretending to be a mountain on the third morning.

Ada had climbed the Thornfell passes since she was six years old, barefoot in summer, booted and swearing in winter, and she knew every switchback the way she knew the lines of her own palm. But the path to the Veil did not behave like a path. It unspooled ahead of them only as far as Kaelen's lantern-light reached, and behind them it closed like water over a dropped stone. Twice Ada turned to check their backtrail and found nothing but cliff face, smooth as a closed door.

"It does that," Kaelen said, when she swore at it the second time. "The Veil doesn't want to be found by accident."

"Nothing about this has been an accident," Ada said. "I've been dragged here by a dying man's map, a zealot army, and you."

"You flatter me. I'd say I'm at least two of those."

She almost smiled. Almost. The nights were getting stranger — the moon overhead had begun its thirteenth turn, and time moved oddly beneath it, hours stretching like taffy or snapping shut between one breath and the next. Her body ached with a tiredness that didn't match the miles they'd walked. And underneath everything, quiet as a coal under ash, the keystone memory sat in her blood, humming. She could feel it more clearly now, the way you feel a splinter once you know it's there: a weight of other people's centuries, waiting to be spent.

They came around a shoulder of black rock at midmorning-that-felt-like-dusk and stopped, both of them, without meaning to.

There was a door in the mountainside.

Not a carved doorway, not a ruin — a *door*, ordinary and domestic, oak and iron hinges, set into raw stone as if someone had hung it there the way you'd hang a picture. A wreath of dried rowan berries decorated it. Someone had recently swept the threshold; a small broom leaned against the jamb.

"Is that—" Ada began.

"She's home," Kaelen said softly. All the charm had gone out of his voice, leaving something young and frightened underneath. "Ada. Whatever happens in there — whatever she shows you — remember it isn't a judgment. It's just... seeing."

"Comforting. Thank you." She wiped her palms on her coat. "Your mother lives behind a door with a broom against it."

"She liked sweeping. When she was still..." He didn't finish. He knocked instead, three times, and the sound went through the stone like a bell through water.

The door opened before the echo died.

* * *

Veyra was smaller than Ada had imagined, which was absurd, because Ada hadn't imagined her at all — or rather, she had imagined everything *but* this. She had pictured vastness, radiance, a presence that filled a cathedral. What stood in the doorway was a woman of perhaps fifty, grey-streaked hair pinned carelessly, wearing a patched woolen dress and an apron dusted with flour. Her eyes were the problem. They were the color of the sky just before snow, and looking into them was like looking into a well and realizing the well went down through the world and out the other side.

"You brought her," Veyra said to Kaelen. Not warmly. Not coldly. With the terrible evenness of someone who has waited a long time for news they already knew.

"I brought her," Kaelen said. "Mother—"

"Don't." She held up a hand, and Ada saw that it trembled. "Come in, Delacroix girl. Wipe your boots. The floor is old and so am I, and neither of us appreciates mud."

The inside of the door was not the inside of a mountain. It was a cottage — low-beamed, firelit, smelling of bread and rosemary and something older underneath, something like rain on hot stone. Shelves crowded the walls, but they held no books. They held jars. Hundreds of them, each labeled in a slanting hand, each holding what looked like ordinary things: a feather, a knot of ribbon, a child's milk tooth, a folded letter gone brown with age.

"What are these?" Ada asked.

"Prayers," Veyra said. "The ones nobody claimed. Sit. Eat. You'll need your strength, and I don't perform miracles on empty stomachs — I can barely perform them on full ones anymore."

There was bread and honey and tea that tasted of every herb Ada had ever smuggled past a customs house, all at once. She ate because her body insisted, though her mind was spinning. Kaelen sat across from her and did not eat, and Veyra watched him not-eat with an expression Ada couldn't read and didn't like.

When the plates were cleared, Veyra folded her hands.

"You've come to reforge the covenant. You carry the keystone. Before you take one step further up my mountain, you will understand exactly what you're carrying, and who paid for it, and why." Her snow-colored eyes fixed on Ada. "This will hurt. I won't lie to you about that. Lying is how the last covenant broke."

"Then show me," Ada said, and was surprised by how steady her voice came out.

Veyra reached across the table and took her hands.

* * *

The visions came like weather.

First, sunlight: a harvest festival three hundred years gone, golden grain stacked high, a priestess of the old covenant walking the rows blessing each sheaf, and the gods answering gently — rain when rain was asked, sun when sun. A world where asking and giving were a conversation, not a gamble. Ada felt the rightness of it in her bones, the way she felt the true line of a hidden road. *This is what it was,* something in her said. *This is what we broke.*

Then the breaking. Three generations ago — she saw it now from above, from a god's-eye distance that made mortals look like ants arguing over crumbs. She saw the old terms torn up, saw which god tore them (a vast shape of hunger and grievance whose name she could not hold in her mind, only its feeling: *betrayed, betrayed, betrayed*), saw the covenant shatter like a dropped plate, saw the shards scatter into mortal bloodlines to be carried and hidden and forgotten until only one remained.

Hers.

And then the cost. Oh, then the cost.

She saw the wardens — her guild, her people, before they were smugglers — standing watch over the shards, generation after generation. She saw a warden named Delacroix burn a shard rather than let Maren's order's grandmother seize it, and burn with it. She saw villages starve when the gods went silent and the rains stopped answering. She saw a mother pray to a half-dead goddess for her fevered son and receive silence, and bury him, and never pray again. She saw the other side too: a god starving for worship so badly he answered a drunkard's careless wish and drowned a valley, and wept afterward, and no one heard him weeping.

She saw Sorrel, young, taking an oath in a cellar with six others, swearing to keep the last shard hidden even from themselves. She saw him break that oath tonight-by-vision, telling Ada everything, and die for it, and in the vision his face as he died was relieved.

She saw Kaelen. Small, dark-haired, human, being born to a goddess who chose mortality's edge rather than watch the covenant's cruelty continue — saw Veyra give up most of herself so she could stand at the threshold and turn back the hungry ones, saw her diminish year by year, jar by jar, sweeping her doorstep, waiting.

And threaded through all of it, everywhere, the same pattern: gods failing, mortals paying. Gods breaking, mortals bleeding. Every vision ended the same way — some ordinary person, a farmer, a ferryman, a girl with a smuggler's map, standing in the gap where the divine should have been, holding the world together with their hands and their graves.

The visions released her.

Ada came back to herself in the cottage chair, gasping, tears on her face she didn't remember crying, Kaelen gripping her shoulder, saying her name.

She pulled away from both of them and stood up, and the awe that had filled her chest curdled as it drained, and what it left behind was anger.

"No," she said.

Veyra tilted her head. "No?"

"*No.*" Ada's voice cracked on it. She pointed at the shelves of jars, at the woman in the apron, at the whole impossible cottage. "You're a *goddess*. Or you were. You watched all of that — centuries of it — and you let *us* carry it? You let my family die for your broken promises? You let Sorrel take an oath that got him killed in a cellar, and you sat here — *sweeping* —"

"Ada," Kaelen started.

"Let me finish." She was shaking now, and she let herself shake. "You had power. Real power. And when your own kind went mad with hunger, when the covenant shattered, you scattered the pieces into *mortal blood* and asked us to hide them, carry them, die for them — and called it faithfulness. Called it wardenship. We built a whole guild, a whole life, a whole *identity* on keeping your secret, and none of us even knew what we were keeping! Sorrel died not knowing if it would ever end!" She swiped at her eyes, furious at the tears. "Every life I just saw — every grave — that wasn't fate. That was a choice. *Yours.* Yours and the rest of them. And now you sit there in your flour-dusted apron and tell me it will hurt, as if that's honesty, as if honesty excuses anything—"

"Do you think I don't know?" Veyra's voice didn't rise, but the fire did — every flame in the hearth standing straight up, the shadows leaning away from her. For one heartbeat Ada saw her as she must have been: vast, threshold-wide, a door between worlds with a heartbeat. Then it passed, and she was a tired woman again, and somehow that was worse. "Do you think I gave up my divinity because it was *comfortable*, girl? I stand at this threshold because someone had to turn back the hungry ones, and every god left either couldn't or wouldn't. I pay tolls of myself every day. I have forgotten my own first name. I sweep the step because it is the last thing I remember how to do that matters." She looked down at her hands. "You think I let mortals carry the burden. I *am* the burden. I am what's left when a god tries to carry it alone and fails."

The silence stretched. The fire settled. Somewhere in the walls, the cottage creaked like any old house.

"That's not an answer," Ada said finally, quieter. "That's just your version of the same story. Everyone sacrificing everyone else and calling it necessity."

"Yes," Veyra said simply. "It is. That's what a broken covenant *is*, Ada Delacroix. Necessity eating love and calling itself duty. The question in front of you is whether you reforge it the same way — or differently." She rose, joints cracking like green wood, and walked to the shelf. She took down one jar, unlabeled, and held it out. Inside was a single dried rowan berry, identical to the wreath on her door. "The Veilstone is a half-day's climb. The thirteenth turn completes in four days. Maren's army is two days behind you — yes, I know, I see thresholds, and hers is coming whether she knows it or not." 

Ada took the jar. It was warm.

"One more thing," Veyra said, and now her snow-eyes went to her son, and softened into something ancient and grieving. "Kaelen. Come here."

He crossed the room like a boy summoned by his mother, which, Ada realized, was exactly what he was. Veyra cupped his face in her hands and studied him for a long moment.

"You've been spending yourself," she said. "On the road. On her. I can see the holes in you."

"It was worth it," Kaelen said.

"It always is. That's the trap." She kissed his forehead, and light ran briefly under his skin like water under ice — and then faded, and he swayed, and Ada caught his arm. "I can't give it back. No one can. But I can tell you what I told no one else in three hundred years." She looked at Ada over his head. "The covenant doesn't have to be reforged with surrender. That is the old way — the way that broke. There is another term. Harder, stranger, but it doesn't hollow the giver. The Veilstone will accept it, if the keystone offers it."

Ada's heart slammed. "What term?"

Veyra smiled, and it was the saddest smile Ada had ever seen.

"Climb," she said, opening the door onto the wind and the waiting dark. "And ask the stone yourself. But climb quickly, child. Your inquisitor has stopped burning shrines." 

She paused in the doorway, letting the cold pour in.

"She's started building something."
