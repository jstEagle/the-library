## 14. The Keystone's Weight

Kaelen had stood before his mother perhaps a dozen times in his life, and every time she had been a different size.

When he was six, she had filled the doorway of their cottage like weather. When he was sixteen and furious with her for leaving his father, she had been small enough to hold in two hands—small enough that his anger had nowhere to go. Now, at the threshold above the Veilstone's approach, Veyra was neither large nor small. She was simply *present*, the way a mountain is present, the way a decision you have been avoiding is present, and Kaelen hated her a little for it, the way he always did, the way he suspected she had designed him to.

"Sit," said Veyra.

"I'd rather stand."

"You would rather stand because sitting feels like surrender, and you have your father's spine about surrender." She gestured anyway, and a shelf of stone rose out of the mountainside beside Ada, flat and inviting as a kitchen bench. "But this will take some time, and I have watched mortals faint standing up. It embarrasses us both."

Ada sat. She had been quiet since they crossed into Veyra's threshold—an hour of climbing through air that tasted like the moment before rain—and Kaelen knew that particular silence. It was the silence of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like.

He stayed standing. Out of spite, mostly. His mother raised him to know when spite was all he had left.

* * *

"The covenant," Veyra began, "was not built of stone."

"No," Ada said. "Sorrel told me. It was built of memory."

"Your guildmaster was more honest than most wardens. Yes. Memory. When the realms were young and the gods walked freely among you, we learned what your scholars have never quite forgiven: that divinity starves without belief, and belief curdles into worship, and worship curdles into cruelty. We asked too much. We took too much. The first covenant bound us—not with chains, but with remembering. A mortal line agreed to carry the covenant's keystone: a living memory, passed in blood, that held the terms of our restraint. As long as one of that line remembered what we owed you, we could not take more than we were given."

"And when the covenant broke," Ada said, "three generations ago—"

"When it broke, the last keystone-bearer died without passing it on." Veyra's voice did something complicated there, something Kaelen recognized from his own throat when he spoke of his father. "Or so we believed. But blood keeps its own records. The memory went dormant. It slept in a smuggler's daughter who thought her family's gift was knowing which mountain passes don't exist."

Ada looked at her hands. "The roads. The hidden roads. That's why Delacroixes always find them."

"You are a map of everything the gods agreed to forget," Veyra said gently. "And now the veil is thinning, and the thirteenth turn is nearly done, and the keystone must be carried to the Veilstone and surrendered willingly—or the covenant dies unreforged, and my kindred wake with no terms at all. Blind. Starving. Answering prayers with floods and fire until there is nothing left on either side of the veil but silence."

"All right," Ada said. "So I carry it up the mountain. I put my hand on the stone. What does 'surrendered' mean, exactly? Because everyone keeps using that word like it's a technical term, and nobody will tell me the price."

Kaelen closed his eyes. He had begged his mother, on the climb, not to be the one to say it. He should have known better. Veyra had never once in his life spared anyone a hard truth, including herself. Especially herself.

"It means this," Veyra said. "The keystone is not an object you carry. It is woven into you—in your recollections, every one of them tied to the people you love. Your mother's voice. Your guildmaster's lessons. Every kindness ever done to you by someone whose face you can still see. To reforge the covenant, those memories must be dissolved into the stone. All of them. Willingly. You will walk down that mountain whole in body, and hollowed in heart, and you will not know why the world feels emptier. You will not know *who* made it feel full."

The wind moved through the threshold. Somewhere below, a bird complained about the weather, absurdly ordinary.

"That's the price," Ada said. "Everyone."

"Everyone you love," Veyra confirmed. "The covenant must be paid in the only coin gods cannot mint themselves."

* * *

Kaelen had planned this conversation in his head for three days, and in all his versions, he was eloquent.

In none of them did he hear himself say, "There has to be another way," in the voice of a boy asking if the dog could please come inside.

Veyra turned to him with an expression he knew intimately—the look of a mother watching her child insist the stove is not hot. "You've known since the Hollow Road."

"I hoped—" He stopped. Started again. "I hoped the tolls had taken enough of me that I'd stop caring. They haven't. Apparently I'm stubborn past the point of function."

"You get that from both of us, unfortunately."

"Don't do that," he snapped. "Don't be funny. Not now."

His mother regarded him for a long moment, and then—mercifully—she stepped back through the threshold and left them alone on the ledge, which was either wisdom or cowardice, and Kaelen had long ago stopped trying to sort which was which in his family.

Ada hadn't moved. She sat on the stone bench with her elbows on her knees, looking out over the Veil country, where the light came up gold and wrong and beautiful, and Kaelen realized with a lurch that she was already grieving. Already saying goodbye to things in advance, quietly, the way she did everything—without witnesses.

"No," he said.

She looked up. "No?"

"No. Whatever you're deciding in there—and you're deciding, I can see the ledger behind your eyes—you're not allowed to decide this alone, and you're not allowed to decide it *that* fast."

"Kaelen—"

"There's another way. There's always another way; I'm the son of a goddess of thresholds, thresholds are literally the family business, and I am telling you there is a door out of this." He crouched in front of her so she couldn't look anywhere but at him. "Beyond the Veil's reach. Past the far ranges, past where the old names were ever spoken. My father's power doesn't stretch there. None of theirs does. We could go tonight. You, me, the guild's fastest mules. By the time the moon finishes turning, we'd be so far away the gods couldn't find us with both hands and a lantern."

Ada was quiet for a moment. Then, horribly, she smiled—a small, tired, real smile that made everything worse.

"Mules," she repeated. "You've thought about the mules."

"I've thought about everything. I've had three days and no sleep. There's a pass I found when I was poaching your routes—I mean, respectfully surveying your routes—and it runs south along the dry riverbed, and if we leave before the second watch—"

"Kaelen."

"—and I know a man in Lowmarsh who owes me and doesn't ask questions, which granted describes most of the people I know, but this one also owns mules—"

"*Kaelen.*"

He stopped. His hands had found hers somewhere in the middle of the speech without his permission. Her fingers were cold. Threshold-cold. Mountain-at-dusk cold. He held on anyway.

"If we run," she said softly, "what happens to the villages? The ones in the loops. The farmer whose well ran dry last month because the local river-god is starving and confused. The children born this year under a thinning veil who'll grow up praying to things that answer like broken machinery. What happens to them?"

"They survive. People survive. People always survive—that's the entire miracle of people."

"People survive *worse*," she agreed. "That's not the same as surviving *well*. And I know—" her grip tightened on his hands, "—I know how this sounds. I know you think I'm being noble, and I promise you I have checked, thoroughly, and there is no nobility in here. There's just arithmetic. One woman's memories against two realms going dark. Even I can't fudge those numbers, and I once balanced Sorrel's books while drunk on festival wine."

"Then let me fudge them for you." His voice cracked and he let it; there was no dignity left to protect. "Let me carry half. Let me pay the tolls again, all of them, everything I have left—it isn't much, I'm mostly mortal now anyway, what's one more hollowing—"

"You'd forget me."

"Not all at once."

"Eventually." She reached up and touched his face, and her hand wasn't steady, and that undid him more than anything else had. "You'd trade your memories of me to keep mine intact, and then in ten years you'd be some stranger in Lowmarsh wondering why a woman with my face makes your chest hurt. That's not saving me, Kaelen. That's just moving the wound."

"Then we both run and keep everything—"

"And the covenant dies. And the gods come back unfettered. And somewhere down the road, years from now, a flood takes a village while we're safe beyond the Veil's reach, and we know—we *know*—there was a door and we walked through it." She exhaled. "I've spent my whole life keeping Thornfell invisible. Hiding roads, hiding people, hiding the guild. It worked. It kept us alive. And it turns out invisible isn't the same as safe, it's just safe *for now*, and the bill comes due, and the bill is always bigger than you budgeted."

"This isn't a smuggling debt, Ada. This is *you*. Your mother's lullabies. Sorrel teaching you to read a ridge-line. Everything that made you—gone, and you won't even mourn it properly, because mourning requires remembering what was lost—"

"I know what it costs." Her voice was very steady, which is how he knew she was holding on with both hands. "I've known since the Hollow Road, when I watched you pay tolls you thought I didn't see. You gave up pieces of yourself to shield me, and you've been lighter ever since—do you know what I mean? Lighter, like a book with pages torn out that still sits square on the shelf. I watched it happen to you and I thought, *so that's the shape of it.* And I thought, *if it comes to that, at least I'll know what I'm choosing.*"

"You shouldn't have to choose."

"No one should. That's rather the whole complaint I have with gods." She almost laughed. "Present company excluded. Mostly."

* * *

They sat together on the stone bench as the light went long and amber, and Kaelen did the thing he was worst at in all the worlds, mortal or divine: he stopped arguing.

Instead he said, "Tell me what you remember best. Of your mother."

Ada blinked. "Why?"

"Because I want to know what I'm losing. If you're going to do this, I want to have heard it. From you. While you still know it." He swallowed. "Consider it a toll. Mine. Tell me something the stone can't have."

She was silent a long time. Below them, the Veil country breathed gold.

"She used to sing off-key," Ada said finally. "Deliberately, I think, though I've never been sure. A song about a fox who trades his tail for a secret and spends the whole ballad regretting it. And whenever she got to the fox's part she'd look at me like the fox was me, and I'd protest, and she'd say, 'Well, did you give away the tail?' And I'd say no, and she'd say, 'Then you're smarter than the fox, aren't you,' and go back to singing badly." Ada's mouth trembled around the smile. "I must have heard that song a thousand times. I could sing it right now. I could sing every wrong note."

"Sing it," Kaelen said. "Here. Once. For me."

"For you?"

"So that when you've forgotten, and I haven't—if I haven't, if the far ranges are kind—someone in this world will have heard it sung by the daughter, and not just read about it in some warden's record. Someone will know it was real."

Ada looked at him for a long moment. Then she leaned her shoulder against his, faced the gold-lit valley where the Veilstone waited like a held breath, and sang her mother's song about the fox and the tail, every note wrong, perfectly wrong, while the son of a goddess of thresholds memorized the melody of a thing already slipping away.

When she finished, neither of them spoke. The wind moved. Far below, at the edge of the Veil's approach, something glinted that was not gold and not sunset—steel, ranks of it, banners Kaelen recognized with a cold drop in his stomach.

Maren's army had reached the mountain.

Ada saw it too. She stood, brushed off her trousers, and rolled her shoulders like a woman preparing for a long day's work, which, Kaelen supposed with a grief that had gone quiet and permanent, was exactly what it was.

"Well," she said. "Better hurry, then. Before the zealots get to the good parts."
