## 15. Maren at the Gates

The army of the Severance reached the foot of the Veil on a morning so beautiful it felt like an accusation.

Maren Voss stood at the head of her column and looked up at the mountain that had haunted her sermons for twenty years—the mountain she had drawn in charcoal on the walls of three different chapter houses, the mountain she had dreamed of since she was a girl watching her mother die of a prayer gone wrong—and felt nothing like triumph.

She felt cold. That was all. Cold, and tired, and aware of the smell of her own army: six hundred souls who had marched through thaw-mud for eleven days, smelling of wet wool, horse sweat, and the tallow they'd burned through the nights. Somewhere behind her, Brother Anselm was arguing with the supply wagons again, his voice carrying up the column in indignant fragments.

"—I don't care what the manifest says, Sister Odile, we have *fourteen* barrels of salt pork and *one* barrel of actual pork—"

"Anselm," Maren called, without turning around, "the gods are not going to wait for our inventory."

"The gods," Anselm said, catching up to her at a trot, breathless and round and utterly unafraid of her, which was one of the reasons she kept him, "have waited three generations already. They can wait while I establish whether we can feed six hundred people or four hundred and eighty."

"We'll know by lunch," Odile said grimly, riding past with the manifest clamped under one arm. "And it'll be four hundred and eighty."

Maren almost smiled. Almost. Her mouth made the shape of it without committing.

The Veil's approach opened before them like a throat: a valley between two shoulders of black stone, terraced with old shrines long toppled, their stones scattered as if a giant child had lost interest mid-game. At its far end, half-hidden in cloud, the mountain rose to the summit where—according to every fragment of scripture the Order had spent decades burning, collecting, burning again—the Veilstone sat like a heart in a chest.

Kill the stone. Kill the covenant. Kill the gods.

*Severance*, her order called it. The great mercy. The final kindness of mortals to themselves.

Maren had believed it the way other people believed in sunrise: completely, without examination, because it had always been there when she woke.

She was beginning to examine it now, and she hated herself for the lateness.

* * *

It had started small, the way rot starts small, in a place you never think to look.

Three days ago, in the village of Harrow's Bend—a name she remembered because it was also the name of a hymn, and she had found that coincidence unbearable—they had burned a shrine. Standard practice. The shrine was a squat thing of riverstone, older than the Order, older than the kingdom probably, and it had a hollow in its base where villagers left offerings. Bread, mostly. A few copper coins. A child's knitted mitten, dropped there by someone too young to understand why their mother snatched it back and then put it back anyway when no one was looking.

Maren had ordered the shrine fired, as she had ordered a hundred shrines fired. The villagers watched from their doorways with the flat, careful faces of people who had learned not to object where soldiers could see them.

But an old woman had come out. Old enough that the Order's rules barely registered to her; old enough, perhaps, to remember things the Order preferred forgotten. She had walked straight through the cordon—straight through, and no soldier had stopped her, Maren noticed later, though none of them would say why—and stood before the burning shrine and said:

"You're burning her kitchen."

Maren had turned. "Whose?"

"The hearth-goddess. Brannoc's wife. The little one." The woman's hands were flour-white. "She keeps the bread from burning. She keeps the milk from turning. She doesn't ask anything. We leave her crusts because it's *polite*." The old woman looked at the fire, at the shrine's stones cracking in the heat, and her face did something terrible and quiet. "You're burning the kitchen of the only god who ever did us a kindness, Inquisitor, and you're doing it in the name of saving us."

Maren had ordered her taken inside, given soup, and questioned gently. The woman had answered everything honestly and nothing usefully, and at the end she had patted Maren's hand—patted it, like Maren was a child at a wake—and said, "You have kind eyes. It's a shame about the rest of you."

That night Maren had lain awake in the requisitioned loft above the tavern and listened to the village dogs, and somewhere past midnight she had heard, very faintly, a sound she had no framework for: singing. Not human singing. Something underneath the wind, wordless and thin and *hungry*, coming down off the mountains, and every dog in Harrow's Bend had begun to howl at once, and Maren had sat up in the dark with her heart slamming and understood, with total clarity, that the old woman was right about one thing at least.

There was something up there. And it was starving.

*Severance* would kill it. That was the point. But standing in the dark listening to that threadbare song, Maren had thought: *it sounds like my mother sounded, at the end.*

She had hated the thought. She had prayed—irony noted, filed, ignored—for the strength to unthink it.

The strength had not come.

* * *

Now the army made camp in the valley of broken shrines, and Maren walked among the toppled stones while her officers argued about siege positions.

"There's no garrison," Captain Ilsa Rennick said, falling into step beside her. Rennick was the best soldier Maren had ever fielded: competent, humorless, loyal in the way of a well-made axe. "No walls. No defenders. The smugglers' scouts melted into the hills when they saw our numbers. This isn't a siege, Inquisitor. It's a walk."

"It's never just a walk," Maren said.

"With respect—it has been, so far. Thornfell fell in a day. The passes are open. Whatever wardens the old covenant had left, they're dead or scattered." Rennick consulted her slate. "We reach the summit approach in two days. Sappers estimate the Veilstone can be cracked with charges and leverage within a day of arrival. Five days, Inquisitor. Five days and the realms are severed forever, and every prayer in the world goes unanswered until the end of time."

"And every harvest-spirit dies with it," Maren said quietly. "Every hearth-bringer. Every healer at every threshold."

Rennick frowned. "Those aren't real, Inquisitor. With respect. Those are stories the faithful tell to soften the doctrine."

"I've been telling myself the same thing for twenty years," Maren said. "Lately it sounds less like doctrine and more like a man covering his ears."

Rennick studied her for a long moment. "You've changed since Harrow's Bend."

"Harrow's Bend didn't change me. It *noticed* me." Maren stopped beside a fallen shrine-stone, half-buried in moss. Carved into its face, worn nearly smooth, was a figure with arms raised—not in menace, she realized, but the way a mother raises her arms to catch a toddler. "Tell me, Captain. When we sever the realms. When the last god chokes on our silence. What happens to the fields that grew fat because something under the world loved them? What happens to the fever-breaks? To the mothers who pray at thresholds?"

"They manage," Rennick said. "As they always should have. Dependence is the disease."

"Yes," Maren said. "That's the sermon. I wrote most of it." She crouched and touched the moss-covered stone, and the stone was warm. Not sun-warm. Warm the way a sleeping animal is warm. She pulled her hand back as if scalded. "But I keep thinking about the old woman's kitchen."

* * *

The trouble arrived at dusk, as trouble does, wearing a familiar face.

They brought her to Maren's tent bound at the wrists—one of the smugglers' people, caught probing the valley's eastern edge, caught *deliberately*, Maren suspected, because no one that bad at stealth survived this long in Thornfell. Young. Freckled. Terrified in the specific way of someone trying very hard to look more terrified than they were, because terrified prisoners got fed.

"Inquisitor Voss," the girl said. "I'm told you're reasonable. For a burner of kitchens."

Word traveled fast. Maren gestured for the guards to leave, which they did reluctantly, and poured tea for the prisoner, because her mother had raised her to do that even for enemies, especially for enemies, and because it gave her hands something honest to do.

"You're Delacroix's people," Maren said. "The guildmaster's network."

"Was." The girl's chin came up. "Sorrel's dead. You want to know what killed him? Zealots with torches, months ago, over some records. Records about a covenant your Order pretends never existed." She leaned forward despite the bonds. "Here's the thing, Inquisitor. Here's why I let your scouts catch me, because yes, obviously, I let them. Ada Delacroix is climbing that mountain right now to fix what's broken. To reforge the covenant. And your army is marching up the other side to smash the only thing keeping every starving god from waking up *unbounded*. Do you understand what happens if you crack that stone before she reaches it?"

"Nothing happens," Maren said. "That is the entire point."

"Nothing happens *to you*." The girl's eyes were bright and furious. "The gods starve, yes. Good riddance, you'd say. But they don't starve *cleanly*, Inquisitor. They starve the way starving things do—wild, blind, grabbing. Every spirit of field and threshold and hearth, every small benign power that's kept this land green out of habit and love, dies thrashing. And the last thing a dying god of the harvest does—" she swallowed— "is take the harvest with it. There won't be a famine after Severance. There'll be a *reckoning*. One bad year becomes ten. One blight becomes the end of seed-corn everywhere. Your mercy kills the patient."

Maren set down her cup very carefully, because her hands wanted to shake and she refused to permit it.

"You're lying," she said. "Or repeating lies."

"Probably," the girl agreed cheerfully. "I'm a smuggler. Lying's the trade. But here's what isn't a lie: Ada Delacroix could have run. Kaelen offered her a way out—beyond the gods' reach, past the edge of the maps. She's the keystone of the whole covenant. She could have saved herself and damned everyone else, and instead she's climbing a mountain to give up every memory she loves. Her mother's voice. Her teacher's lessons. The laugh of a man she—" the girl faltered, glanced away— "of someone. She's trading her entire heart to fix what our grandfathers broke. And you're going to march up there and break it worse, in the name of freedom, and call it kindness."

Silence. The tent-flap breathed in the wind. Under the wind, faintly, that threadbare hungry song.

"Why are you telling me this?" Maren asked finally. "If I believe you, I stop your enemy's enemy. If I don't, you've wasted your one audience."

The girl smiled, and it was Sorrel's smile, Maren realized—she'd seen it in the guildmaster's face once, across an interrogation table, years ago. Guilds were families. Families left marks.

"Because Ada said to tell you the truth whether you believed it or not. She said, 'Maren Voss has kind eyes. Someone should talk to them.'" The girl shrugged. "Also because if you don't believe me, you'll find out at the summit, and by then it'll be too late, and I'd rather be right than comfortable."

Maren dismissed her with food and an escort to the picket line's edge—loose enough to escape, tight enough to pretend otherwise—and sat alone with the tea going cold and the doctrine going colder in her chest.

*Kind eyes.* The old woman and the smuggler girl, agreeing across a canyon of belief. It was almost funny. Maren had built her life on the idea that faith was a lens that clarified, and lately every lens she owned showed her the same smudged, doubting face.

* * *

At midnight, she climbed alone to the ridge above the camp, as she did most nights, to be away from the snores and the horses and Anselm's sleep-talking (he delivered sermons in his sleep; they were better than his waking ones).

From the ridge she could see the summit approach: a pale scar of switchbacks climbing toward cloud, and above the cloud, faint as breath on glass, a light. Not starlight. Not moonlight. Something steady and gold and *waiting*.

The Veilstone. Alive. Lit like a lamp in a window for someone expected home.

Maren stood in the wind and took out the small iron medallion every Inquisitor carried: the Severance sigil, a sword cutting a circle. Twenty years she'd kissed it. Tonight she turned it over and over in her fingers and thought about her mother, who had prayed herself to death—literally, kneeling at a shrine, begging a deaf and broken godling to save her fevered son, praying until her heart gave out while the boy lived and the god did nothing. Maren had been nine. Everything she believed had grown from that grave like thorns.

*Dependence is the disease.* She had sworn it over her mother's body.

But here was the thing she had never let herself think, thinking itself now in the dark with the weight of inevitability: her mother had died praying to a god that was already *starving*. Already blind. Already broken by a covenant three generations dead. The Order's answer was to kill the patient. Ada Delacroix's answer was to heal it.

One of those answers would have saved her mother.

Maren closed her fist around the medallion until the edges bit.

Below her, the camp fires dotted the valley like fallen stars, six hundred points of mortal warmth, each one a person who trusted her. Behind her, the mountain light burned patient and gold. Somewhere on those switchbacks, a woman was climbing to surrender everything she loved, and Maren's army was climbing to make sure the surrender bought nothing at all.

"Inquisitor?" Rennick, at the base of the ridge, lantern raised. "Orders for dawn?"

Maren looked at the light on the summit a moment longer.

"Dawn formation as planned," she said, and heard her own voice sound like a door closing on a room she wasn't done searching. "Full column. We move at first light."

Rennick saluted and went. Maren stayed on the ridge until the sky went grey, turning the iron sigil over and over, sword cutting circle, circle waiting to be cut.

And in the hour before dawn, when the starving song beneath the wind swelled suddenly—joyful, urgent, *alarmed*—and every sentry in the camp came awake shouting that there were lights moving on the mountain road, dozens of them, torches and lanterns and something brighter, coming *down* toward the valley with the speed of desperate people—

Maren Voss stood at the head of her army, medallion in her fist, and gave the order that would decide everything:

"Battle stations. And someone find me that smuggler girl. *Now.*"
