## 16. Battle of the Broken Shrine

The mountain had a smell before it had a sound. Ada had learned that young, on her first run through the Thornfell passes—Sorrel tapping her nose and saying, *Snow smells like nothing, girl, but trouble smells like iron.* Now the whole approach to the Veil reeked of iron: Maren's soldiers, three hundred strong at least, their armor oiled and gleaming in the grey dawn light, their campfires smoking like a wound.

She crouched beside Kaelen on the ridge above the Broken Shrine, an old waystation whose roof had collapsed a generation ago, leaving only its stone archway standing like a crooked doorframe to nowhere. Below them, the smugglers were gathering. Her smugglers. Hers and Sorrel's, though Sorrel was two weeks dead and his grave was a cairn of river stones that Ada still couldn't look at directly.

"They're going to come up the Pilgrim's Stair," Kaelen said. His voice was quiet, but there was something underneath it—the hum he carried now, fainter every day, like a bell heard through a wall. "Three hundred against what, sixty?"

"Forty-one," Ada corrected. "Old Tam's got a bad knee and Fen's only fourteen, so really thirty-nine and a half."

Kaelen gave her a look. It was the look he'd been giving her for days, ever since they'd come down out of Veyra's threshold with the keystone burning cold in Ada's chest—a look that said *you are doing the thing where you count instead of feel.*

"I'm allowed to count," she said. "Counting is how I keep everyone alive."

"And how do you keep yourself alive?"

"That's not on the manifest."

Below, the villagers from Hollowmere were arriving too—the ones she'd pulled out of the loop, blinking and weeping and furious with her for shattering their perfect day, who had followed her up the mountain anyway because, as old Marta put it, *if the gods are waking up, somebody ought to be there when they do.* They came with pitchforks and kitchen knives and one ancient hunting bow, and they arranged themselves among the rocks with the cheerful incompetence of people who had never fought anything angrier than a stubborn goat.

And behind them, half-hidden, the remnant faithful. A dozen men and women in patched robes, keepers of shrines nobody had tended in decades, carrying candles and hymnals and, in one memorable case, a ceremonial censer that Ada suspected had never been used for anything but storing onions.

"We're going to lose," Kaelen said.

"Probably."

"You say that very calmly."

"I'm a smuggler, Kaelen. I've never once won a fight. I've only ever avoided them, delayed them, or bribed my way out of them." She watched the soldiers break camp below, banners snapping. "Today none of those are on offer. So we hold the pass long enough for me to climb to the Veilstone, and then whatever happens, happens."

He caught her wrist. His hand was warm; it hadn't always been. When she'd met him he'd been cool as riverstone, god-touched, and now he was just a man, mortal as bread, spending what was left of himself buying her passage through the world.

"When this is over," he said, "I'm teaching you to fish. Properly. You'll hate it."

"That's your promise? Fishing?"

"It's the only future I've got that doesn't require divinity." He smiled, and it cracked something in her that she'd been carefully not looking at. "Hold the pass. Climb fast. Don't die counting."

* * *

They came up the Pilgrim's Stair at midmorning, and the mountain taught them what Thornfell smugglers knew.

Ada had spent her life making roads invisible—not destroying them, just folding them away, hiding switchbacks behind scree and false trails behind true ones. Now she unfolded everything. The stair itself split into three ghost-paths, two of which dead-ended at sheer drops or, in one case, a hornet nest the size of a wine barrel that Old Tam had been protecting for years with the devotion other men gave to shrines.

The first hundred soldiers took the hornets. The screaming was biblical. Marta said afterward that it was the most answered prayer she'd ever witnessed.

But three hundred is three hundred, and zealots burn. The second wave came up the true path in disciplined silence, shields locked, and the battle became what battles are: mud and breath and the terrible arithmetic of bodies.

Ada fought the way she did everything—efficiently, unglamorously, with a smuggler's short blade and a smuggler's ethics, which is to say she struck from behind whenever possible and apologized to no one. Kaelen fought beside her, and even diminished he was terrifying: the last ember of Veyra's blood let him step *between*, appearing a stride ahead of where he'd been, and soldiers learned to fear the space around him more than him.

It was the guild brothers who held the pass proper.

Brannic took the narrows with six others and a wall of stacked cargo crates—smuggler's crates, full of smuggled brandy, which caught fire beautifully when Marta's nephew tipped a lamp into them. Brannic stood in the smoke with his axe and his missing teeth and laughed like the end of the world, and Ada, fighting her way toward the upper trail, saw him go down under four shields and take two of them with him.

Wick, who could pick any lock in Thornfell and had once stolen a bishop's hat purely to see if he could, held the shrine archway itself. He'd wedged himself into the broken stonework with a crossbow and made the arch a tollgate no soldier passed cheaply. When they finally flanked him, he stood up on the archway—on top of it, silhouetted against the sky—and shouted down at them, clear as a bell:

"This road has a toll! Always has! You're paying it in blood because you're too stupid to pay in coin!"

Then he jumped into the middle of them, knives out, grinning.

Ada didn't see him fall. She chose not to. There would be time later, she told herself, for seeing things. There would be a whole lifetime of seeing things, if she lived, and right now the Veilstone was above her and the thirteenth turn was nearly done and grief was a luxury she would invoice later.

But her eyes burned anyway, climbing. Fury and gratitude tangled together until she couldn't tell them apart. These people—her invisible people, the ones she'd spent twenty years keeping unseen, unnamed, deniable—they were dying in plain daylight, loudly, gloriously, *seen*. Old Tam was roaring somewhere below. Fen, fourteen and a half by now, was dragging wounded behind the shrine wall with a ferocity that would have made Sorrel proud and Sorrel also extremely cross about the child being in danger.

*We were never nothing,* Ada thought, cutting down a soldier who'd gotten between her and the upper trail. *You just never bothered to look.*

* * *

The last stretch to the Veilstone was a stair of white stone older than any kingdom, and Maren had saved her best for it.

Twelve of her inquisitors stood in formation at the base, and above them, on the steps themselves, Inquisitor Maren Voss waited with her sword drawn and her face pale with something that wasn't quite certainty anymore.

Ada climbed into the space between them and stopped, breathing hard, blood on her sleeves that was mostly not hers.

"Maren."

"Delacroix." The inquisitor's voice was steady. Her eyes weren't. "Your people are dying down there for a lie."

"My people are dying down there for each other," Ada said. "Which is the only thing worth dying for that I know of. Move aside."

"The gods starved us first," Maren said, and there it was again—that conviction like bedrock, the thing Ada had argued against through days of captivity and never once dented. "Every prayer they answered, they answered with a hook in it. Every blessing was a debt. Severance is mercy."

"Is it?" Ada stepped forward, and the keystone in her chest flared, and she didn't fight it. She let it rise. "Then look. Just look, Maren. That's all I'm asking. Not surrender. Witness."

She opened the memory.

It poured out of her like light through a cracked door: centuries of covenant, kept and betrayed. Farmers' prayers answered with rain that fell gently, exactly where needed. Midwives calling on Veyra at the threshold of birth, and the goddess coming small and kind. And the betrayals too—kings who'd tried to chain gods, gods who'd grown greedy with worship, the slow rot that had led to the breaking, three generations ago. All of it. The whole ugly, beautiful ledger.

Maren's sword arm trembled.

Her twelve inquisitors stood rigid, waiting for orders that didn't come. Down the mountain, the battle roared and dimmed and roared again. Somewhere below, someone was singing—one of the remnant faithful, off-key, defiant, a hymn about thresholds.

Maren looked at the memories the way a starving woman looks at bread.

"I burned a shrine in Aldenmere," she said quietly. "There was a girl tending it. Eleven years old." Her voice didn't waver, but something behind it did. "I told myself the fire was cleaner than dependence."

"I know," Ada said. "I saw it in you, in the cells. You carry every single one of them. That's why you'll never be free of them, Maren—but you can stop adding to the count."

For a long moment, the inquisitor stood at the center of her own storm. Then she turned to her soldiers.

"Fall back to the treeline," she said. "That's an order. No one touches the mountain."

"Maren—" one began.

"*No one touches the mountain.*" She sheathed her sword and looked at Ada, and her eyes were wet and furious and lost. "Go. Do whatever it is you're going to do. And Delacroix—if it costs you what I think it will—"

"It does."

"Then I'm sorry," Maren said, "for every hour I made you waste arguing with me. You'd have won anyway. You always argue like a smuggler."

"Thank you," said Ada, who had no idea if that was a compliment, and turned, and climbed.

* * *

The Veilstone waited at the summit like a held breath.

It was smaller than she'd imagined—a pillar of grey crystal no taller than a man, veined with light that pulsed slow as a sleeping heart. Around it, the air shimmered, and beyond the shimmer she could see them: the gods, waking, vast and hungry and blind, pressing against the thinning veil like faces against frosted glass.

The moon hung above the peak, swollen, one sliver from completing its thirteenth turn.

Kaelen reached the summit behind her, bleeding from a cut above his eye, alive, mortal, hers. He didn't beg. He'd begged last night, and she'd held him, and they'd both known how it ended.

"You could still run," he said, without hope.

"So could you," Ada said. "Fishing. Remember?"

His face broke. She kissed him—quickly, fiercely, the way you kiss someone when you're stealing the memory for later—and pressed her palm to the Veilstone.

The light rose up to meet her.

And Ada Delacroix, keeper of hidden roads, began to give away everything she had ever loved.
