## 17. Confrontation at the Veilstone

The last stretch of the climb was not a road at all. It was a staircase of black stone that had no business standing on a mountainside this steep, each step worn smooth in the middle by feet that had stopped climbing centuries before Ada was born. The wind up here did not howl. It *sang*—a low, wordless hum that Ada felt in her teeth more than her ears, like the mountain itself had been holding one long note since the covenant broke and could not let it go.

Behind her, far below, the battle for the pass still raged. She could hear it: the clash of steel, the crack of burning shrines, and beneath it all a sound she would carry to her grave—the voices of smugglers and farmers and freed loop-dwellers shouting her name. *Delacroix. Delacroix.* Her invisible people, choosing to be seen, dying to buy her an hour.

She did not look back. Looking back was a luxury she couldn't afford, and besides, Sorrel had taught her better. *The road behind you is already paid for, girl. Walk the road ahead.*

The Veilstone waited at the summit.

She had imagined it a hundred ways—a jewel, a door, a great cracked bell—and none of them were right. It was simply a circle of standing stones, nine of them, tall as old pines, arranged around a hollow basin of white rock at the center. The stones were veined with light, faint and starving, pulsing out of rhythm with one another like nine hearts that had forgotten they were supposed to beat together. Snow fell upward here, drifting lazily from the ground into the sky, as if even gravity wasn't sure whose rules applied.

And standing among the stones, alone, her army held below by a wall of smugglers and stubborn villagers, was Inquisitor Maren Voss.

Ada's hand went to her knife out of pure habit. Then she made herself take it off her belt and drop it in the snow.

Maren watched her do it. The Inquisitor stood with her grey cloak snapping in the singing wind, her iron-tipped staff planted before her, and she looked—tired. That was the thing that undid Ada's prepared speech before she'd spoken a word of it. Maren Voss looked like a woman who had climbed a mountain on conviction alone and found the summit colder than advertised.

"You came," Maren said. Her voice carried strangely in the thin air, close and clear at once.

"You're surprised?"

"I'm surprised you came *alone*." Maren's eyes flicked to the pass below, where smoke rose. "I expected your god-son. I expected an army. Instead I get a smuggler who drops her knife in the snow like a peace offering."

"It is one."

"Knives don't worry me, Delacroix." Maren lifted her staff, and the iron tip began to glow with that flat, hungry light Ada had seen it burn shrines with. "What worries me is what you're carrying in your blood."

Ada took a step forward. The upward-falling snow swirled around her ankles. "Then let me show you what I'm carrying."

"No."

"You haven't even heard—"

"I've heard everything." Maren's voice cracked like ice on a pond. "I've heard the gods are waking. I've heard the covenant must be reforged. I've heard the keystone memory will save both realms. Do you know where I heard those things first, Delacroix? From my mother. On her knees, praying to a goddess of harvests who never once answered her, while my brothers starved through three failed winters. Faith is a beautiful story. I have heard it my whole life. It is always told by people who want something from you."

The words landed harder than any blow could have. Because Ada had heard that sermon too—not from Maren but from Sorrel, in his gruffer way, about every priest and lordling who'd ever tried to tax or tame the guild. She understood, suddenly and horribly, that she and Maren had been raised by the same lesson, taught by different mouths.

"My mother prayed too," Ada said quietly.

Maren's jaw tightened. "Don't."

"She prayed to Noor, actually. Goddess of thresholds and small mercies. My mother lit a candle every night of my childhood and asked for nothing grand—just that I'd come home safe when I ran the roads." Ada took another step. "Noor never answered. Not once. And do you know why?"

"Because your gods are dead or sleeping or *invented*, depending on which scholar you ask—"

"Because the covenant was broken." Ada kept walking, slow, hands open at her sides. "Three generations ago. The gods didn't abandon us, Maren. They were *cut off*. Blinded. Starved. Everything your order has burned for forty years—every shrine, every prayer-scroll, every god-touched child driven into the sea—it all served the breaking. Your order didn't save humanity from dependence. It blinded the powers that were keeping our harvests alive, and now they're waking up mad and starving, granting wishes with floods and fire because nobody ever taught them gentleness again."

"And your solution," Maren said, "is to hand them a living bridge straight back into our world. To reforge the chain." Her knuckles whitened on the staff. "You'd shackle us again. Forever. And call it mercy."

"I'd call it a *bargain*. Which is what it always was." Ada stopped ten paces away, close enough to see the exhaustion carved under Maren's eyes. "A bargain has terms. Both sides give. Both sides get. What your order wants isn't freedom, Maren. It's silence. Total severance. You'd kill every god—not just the cruel ones. The ones who heal fevers. The ones who turn the seasons. You'd cut the realm off from all of it and call the resulting quiet peace."

"The quiet *would* be peace."

"The quiet would be starvation wearing peace's clothes." Ada spread her arms. "Look around you. Look at the stones."

For the first time, Maren hesitated. Her gaze moved across the circle—the nine monoliths, their veins of guttering light, the basin at the center where snow fell upward and hung suspended like breath held too long.

"What am I looking at?" she asked, and there was something almost young in her voice.

"Dying hearts." Ada touched the nearest stone. It was warm. Warm as a sleeping animal, warm as a hand. "Each one is bound to a god. This one—" the light under her palm pulsed, weak as a candle in wind "—this is Noor. Thresholds and small mercies. The one my mother prayed to. She's been trying to answer prayers for sixty years, Maren, and she can't reach us. Every unanswered prayer your mother ever spoke, every candle that guttered out without so much as a sign—your order did that. Not the gods' indifference. *Your order's success.*"

"You're lying."

"I'm a smuggler. Lying is my trade, and I'm telling you I've never been more honest in my life." Ada turned to face her fully. "You want proof. Fine. You want something better than my word. I can give you the keystone itself."

Maren's staff came up. "Stay back."

"I'm not going to attack you with memories, Inquisitor." A laugh escaped Ada, ragged and unexpected—genuine humor, the kind Sorrel would have appreciated, surfacing at the worst possible moment because that was where it always surfaced. "Though I'll warn you, some of them are tedious. There are four hundred years of guild ledgers in my head. If witnessing doesn't work, boredom might."

"That's not how the keystone works," Maren said sharply. "It shows what it wants to show. It *seduces*. The texts are clear—"

"The texts your order wrote after burning everyone who remembered otherwise?" Ada shook her head. "Here's what actually happens, as far as I understand it, and I only learned it two weeks ago from a dying man, so forgive me if I fumble. The keystone holds the covenant's memory. All of it. Every term, every renewal, every keeping and every betrayal, on both sides. Mortal and divine. It isn't a seduction. It's a *witness*. And right now, standing on this mountain, you're the only person alive who has the power to end everything—and no idea what you'd be ending."

The wind sang its long note. Somewhere below, faintly, a horn sounded—Maren's army, frustrated at the pass.

"If I refuse," Maren said slowly, "you'll force it on me."

"I can't force anything on anyone. That's rather the point of the whole covenant." Ada held out her hand. "You have to choose to see. Same as I did. Same as everyone the keystone has ever asked."

Maren stared at the offered hand as though it were a serpent. And then—because underneath the iron and the doctrine and the forty years of ash, Maren Voss had always been, at bottom, a woman who needed to *know*—she stepped forward and took it.

* * *

The world went white, then went *everything*.

Ada had braced for visions, for images, for a pageant. What came instead was presence. She stood inside centuries the way you stand inside weather.

She felt the first forging—cold morning, a circle of mortals and shapes of light negotiating in a tongue older than language, and the terms laid down like stones: *the divine shall answer the honest prayer; the mortal shall tend the flame; neither shall feed on the other unasked.* She felt four hundred years of tending—generations of Delacroixes and their kin walking hidden roads, carrying messages between shrine and shrine, smuggling not goods but *remembrance*, keeping the names alive in villages where it was dangerous to keep them. She felt the small mercies flow back: rain arriving the day after a farmer's whispered plea, a fever breaking, a lost child walking out of the woods toward a light only she could see.

And then she felt the betrayals, and these she felt in her bones.

A king who demanded his dead son returned, and when the gods explained that death was part of the terms, declared them tyrants and raised armies against their shrines. A priesthood that grew rich selling absolution the covenant never promised. An age of famine in which desperate mortals offered *anything*—and a young god of the harvest who took them at their word and fed a city on a price no city should pay, and the horror that rippled through the divine host afterward, the shame, the first whispers of *perhaps we should not be trusted with this.*

She felt the zealot order born—not as villains, but as grief. She felt Maren's own history woven into it: the starving winters, the mother on her knees, the girl who swore that no power would ever hold her family's survival hostage again. She felt the order's founding charter written in genuine love, twisted over decades into iron.

And she felt—she felt Maren feeling all of it beside her. Felt the Inquisitor's breath catch at the first forging's tenderness. Felt her flinch at the harvest god's crime, and Ada made no move to soften it, because softening it would be a lie, and the keystone did not lie. Felt her go very still at the memory of the order's founding, seeing her own conviction reflected back across centuries in a dozen other faces, some of whom had also begun in love and ended in ash.

When the vision released them, they were both on their knees in the snow. Ada's nose was bleeding. She wiped it on her sleeve and found Maren staring at the stones with an expression Ada had last seen on her own face in Sorrel's office, the night she learned her whole life was built on a buried truth.

"How long?" Maren rasped. "How long has the order known—all of this—and chosen to burn it?"

"Some of them never knew. Some knew and couldn't bear it." Ada got to her feet, legs shaking. "Grief does that. It picks one truth and guards it like a fortress, because the alternative is admitting the fortress was pointed the wrong way."

"Don't." Maren pushed herself upright, staff trembling. "Don't psychoanalyze me on a mountaintop, Delacroix. I have spent forty years—my *whole life*—"

"I know."

"—and you're telling me the gods I've hated since childhood include ones who walked children out of forests. And ones who ate a city. *Both.* How am I meant to carry both?"

"You aren't." Ada said it gently. "That's what the covenant is *for*. Neither side carries it alone. That's the entire bargain, Maren. We watch each other. We hold each other to the terms. The moment either side stops watching—mortal or divine—that's when cities get eaten and winters starve families. Severance isn't safety. Severance is just making sure no one's watching when it happens again."

The staff lowered an inch. Then another.

Below them, the fighting had gone strangely quiet. Ada risked a glance down the mountain and saw why: along the pass, soldiers and smugglers had stopped swinging at each other and were simply staring upward, because the nine stones had begun to glow—really glow, their veins brightening, their arrhythmic pulses beginning, slowly, to find a shared tempo. The thirteenth turn was nearly complete. The moon overhead had swollen huge and low, and its light fell through the circle of stones and pooled in the white basin like molten silver.

"It's time," Ada said. "I have to go to the basin now. And I need to know what you'll do when I do."

Maren was silent for a long moment. The wind sang. Snow drifted up past them both, glittering.

"When I was nineteen," Maren said at last, "I burned my first shrine. A little roadside thing, a goddess of thresholds. There was an old woman tending it, and she didn't run, didn't fight. She just looked at me while it burned and said, *child, you'll wish someone had answered your mother's prayers.*" Maren's voice was flat. "I told myself she was a fanatic. I have told myself that for thirty years." She looked at Ada, and her eyes were wet and furious and free, all at once. "I am not joining your covenant. Understand that. I am not kneeling to any god tonight."

"I wouldn't dream of asking."

"But I am done being blind." Maren drove her staff into the snow—point-down, iron quenched, its hungry light extinguished—and stepped aside from the path to the basin. "Go. Reforge your bargain. And if your gods break it—if *one* city goes hungry by divine whim—I will spend what's left of my life tearing it down again, and this time I'll know exactly what I'm doing."

"That," Ada said, "is the most covenantal thing anyone's said to me all year."

Despite herself—despite everything—Maren huffed something that was almost a laugh. "Your god-son said you were insufferable."

"He talks about me?" Ada was already moving toward the basin, heart hammering, the moonlight pulling at her like a tide.

"Constantly. It's disgusting." Maren's voice followed her, roughened by emotion she would deny to her grave. "Delacroix. Wait."

Ada turned.

"The memories you're about to give up." Maren's jaw worked. "The people. Whoever they are. I've seen enough of the keystone to know what it costs." For a moment the Inquisitor looked years younger, and lost, and human. "Is it worth it?"

Ada thought of Sorrel's lessons. Her mother's lullabies. Kaelen's laugh, that crooked, conspiratorial sound. All of it, about to dissolve into silver light, and she would walk down this mountain not knowing what she'd lost—only that it had mattered.

"No," she said honestly. "It isn't worth it. Nothing would be. That's what makes it a sacrifice instead of a trade." She smiled, and found the smile was steady. "But worth isn't the measure. Love isn't a ledger, Inquisitor. Even yours."

Maren Voss closed her eyes. When she opened them, she nodded once—Inquisitor to warden, enemy to something not quite enemy—and stood aside among the stones, witness to the end of the world she had made and the beginning of whatever came next.

Ada stepped into the basin. The moonlight rose to meet her like water closing over her head, and the last thing she heard, before the keystone opened and the giving began, was the mountain finally letting go of its long-held note—

—and somewhere below, faint and clear, Kaelen shouting her name.
