## 19. A Stranger's Kindness

The road out of Thornfell smelled of wet pine and woodsmoke, and Ada Delacroix had never been happier to smell either.

Three weeks since the mountain. Three weeks since she had walked down from the Veilstone with snow in her boots and a lightness in her chest that she could not account for, like a room swept clean of furniture—beautiful, and echoing. The guild had taken her back without questions, mostly because Sorrel's old brothers had decided that anyone who came down off that mountain alive deserved a hot meal and no interrogation. She had eaten the hot meal. She had slept for two days. And then, because there was nothing else in her that knew how to be, she had gone back to work.

"Left at the split birch," she murmured to herself, ducking under a branch that had grown across the path since—apparently—the last time she'd come this way. Her feet knew the turn before her mind did. That was the strange arithmetic of it: everything procedural remained, every trick and switchback and hidden door, while the *why* of things had been scooped out like the flesh of a gourd.

She kept a list now. It lived in the inside pocket of her coat, written in her own hand but in moments she only half recalled composing:

*You like honey in your tea but not in your porridge.*
*You whistle when you're nervous. Badly.*
*There is someone you are missing. You will know him when you see him.*

That last line was in different ink. She had no memory of writing it. She had checked the handwriting four times to make sure it was hers, and it was, down to the little hook she put on her d's, which everyone had always told her looked like a fisherman's lure.

She did not know who "him" was. But the note sat against her ribs all day, warm as a coal.

* * *

The smuggling run went clean, which felt almost insulting after everything. Six crates of salted eel and one very illegal barrel of southern brandy, moved through three passes and past two toll stations without so much as a raised lantern. Her crew—Bramm and the twins, Ferren and Fenn, who were not actually twins but had decided during the war-scare that matching names frightened customs men—kept looking at her sideways.

"You're humming," Ferren said on the last stretch.

"I am not."

"You are. Same four notes, over and over." He demonstrated, badly. It took Ada a moment to place why the tune made her chest ache. It was a lullaby. She didn't know whose. She didn't know where she'd learned it. She only knew that somewhere behind the swept-clean room of herself, a door was locked, and music leaked under it.

"Huh," she said.

"That's it? *Huh?*"

"Some songs don't need explaining, Ferren."

"They usually do when they make the guildmaster cry into her eel money."

She threw a pinecone at him. It was good, being able to throw pinecones at people. Whatever else had been taken from her—and she'd stopped trying to inventory the loss, because inventorying it was like counting water with a net—she still had this: the road, the crew, the small daily comedy of moving forbidden things past people paid to notice nothing.

Still. At night, in whatever inn or waystation or hollow under a rock shelf the road provided, she would lie awake and feel the shape of the absence. It wasn't grief exactly. Grief has an object. This was more like the ache in a tooth that's already gone—the body insisting on a pain with no source. Sometimes she'd wake with tears drying on her face and no idea what she'd dreamed, only that it had been important, and kind, and gone.

*Someone you are missing,* said the note. *You will know him when you see him.*

* * *

It rained on the fifth night out, a proper autumn deluge that drove them to shelter early. Bramm and the twins pressed on toward Thornfell with the brandy—someone had to keep the guild solvent, and Ada had volunteered to take the long way home, alone, checking the eastern waypoints. She told herself it was diligence. She suspected it was something else: a hope she couldn't name, walking the roads ahead of her like a dog that scents its master coming home.

She made camp in the ruin of an old wayshrine—one of the small ones, a circle of standing stones with a firepit in the middle, older than any god anyone currently worshipped. Since the covenant's reforging, these places had changed character. Before, they'd been cold. Neglected. Now, if you built a fire in the pit and spoke honestly, the flames would lean toward you like a listener.

Ada built her fire, boiled tea, added honey, and sat wrapped in her cloak watching the rain stitch silver threads through the dark beyond the stones.

"You always did pick the driest corner of any ruin," said a voice from the shadows. "Even when we were running from Maren's outriders. You'd find the one spot where the roof held."

Her knife was in her hand before she'd decided to draw it. The firelight reached maybe ten feet into the dark, and the voice had come from just past that edge—low, warm, tired in a way that sounded less like exhaustion than like long practice at being patient.

"Who's there?"

A pause. Then footsteps, unhurried, and a man stepped into the light.

He was tall, dark-haired, travel-worn, with a face that might have been handsome before sorrow got its hands on it and rearranged some of the architecture. His coat was soaked through. His eyes—

His eyes stopped her breath. Not because they were threatening. Because looking into them was like pressing her ear to that locked door inside herself. Something on the other side stirred. Something enormous and gentle and grieving.

"I'm not going to hurt you," he said. "I know how that sounds. Everyone says it. But I've been waiting three weeks for the right night to approach you, and I promise I chose this one because you'd have dry socks and a full belly and a fire, and because Bramm isn't here to put a crossbow bolt in me before I can say my piece."

"How do you know Bramm?" Her knife hand stayed steady. Her voice nearly didn't.

"He doesn't know me. But I know his tell—he rubs his thumb along his belt buckle when he's about to shoot. You'll want to watch for that; he does it even when he likes people." The man spread his hands, empty, palms up. "My name is Kaelen."

The name landed in her like a stone dropped down a well. She waited for the splash. It took a long time coming, and when it did, it wasn't a memory—it was just weight. Her eyes stung. Her throat closed around words she couldn't find.

"I know you," she said slowly. "I don't—I don't know *how* I know you. But my whole body knows you. Who are you? What are you to me?"

Kaelen smiled, and it broke her heart, that smile, because it was the smile of someone receiving exactly what they expected and finding it both merciful and unbearable. "That's rather a long story," he said. "May I sit? I've been walking behind you for six days, working up the courage, and my knees have opinions about standing."

"You've been—" She lowered the knife an inch. "Six days?"

"I wanted to be sure. Sure of you, sure of myself, sure I could do this without making it worse." He eased himself down onto the stone bench across the fire, wincing, wringing rain from his sleeve. Up close he looked younger than his face suggested and older than his voice. "The truth is, Ada Delacroix, I loved you. Before the mountain. Before the Veilstone. And you gave up—"

He stopped. Started again, quieter.

"You gave up every memory tied to the people you loved, to reforge the covenant. That was the price. Everyone you cared about, dissolved into the stone. Your mother's lullabies. Sorrel's lessons. Me." He said the last word simply, without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. "I'm not here to demand anything back. The gods woke whole and bounded by new terms—that worked. The world is mending. I checked. I've spent three weeks checking, because I needed to be certain your sacrifice bought what it was supposed to buy before I came and complicated your life with my face."

Ada discovered she was crying. It seemed to be happening to someone slightly adjacent to her; she watched it with a strange detachment, wiping her cheek with the heel of her hand as though correcting a smudge.

"Why would you come back at all, then?" she managed. "If I can't remember—if loving you cost me—" She gestured vaguely at the space between them, at the whole impossible geometry of it. "Why stand in front of someone who looks at you like a stranger? That's cruelty. To yourself, I mean."

"Is it?" Kaelen considered the fire. "I've thought about that. For three weeks, mostly at night, mostly in the rain, so you're getting the distilled version." He looked up, and his eyes were wet too, though his voice held. "Because love that only exists when it's remembered isn't love. It's bookkeeping. What I feel doesn't require your recollection to be true. And because—" he laughed, short and helpless, "—because I promised someone I'd help you remember. Slowly. On your terms. If you ever want that. And if you never want it, then I promised someone I'd be kind to you anyway, and I intend to keep both promises."

"Who did you promise?"

"My mother." Something flickered across his face—grief, and beneath the grief, a tenderness so old it had worn smooth. "She's a goddess. Was a goddess. Long story. Also on the list of things I can explain whenever you ask, or never, whichever serves you."

The rain filled the silence, soft against the stones. The fire leaned toward them both, the way it did now, listening.

Ada looked at this stranger—this man her bones insisted she knew, whose name sat in her chest like a key in a lock that no longer had a door—and felt something she could only describe as homesickness for a house she was sitting inside.

"The note," she said suddenly. She pulled it from her coat, unfolded it, read the last line aloud. "*'There is someone you are missing. You will know him when you see him.'* Did you write this?"

"No." He leaned forward, and his expression shifted to something like wonder. "You wrote that? When?"

"I don't remember writing it. It was in my pocket after the mountain. Different ink. My handwriting." She turned it toward him. "But it's you, isn't it. It was always going to be you."

Kaelen took the paper with both hands, carefully, the way one handles a relic. He read it twice. When he looked up, he was laughing and crying at once, and it was such an unguarded thing, such a human thing, that Ada understood viscerally—without a single memory to support the understanding—that this man had once been more than human, and had given it up, and had never once regretted the trade if the trade ended here, at a wet fire, being handed a note in her handwriting.

"You kept walking toward me," he said. "With no reason to. With a blank space where I should be, and you kept walking toward it anyway."

"Well." Ada sniffed, retrieved the note, tucked it away. "In my defense, I'm told I'm stubborn. Sorrel used to say—" She faltered. "Sorrel used to say something. About me. I know he used to say it. I can almost hear it." She pressed her knuckles briefly to her mouth. "It's like that. All of it. Like hearing a conversation through a wall. I know the shapes of things. I just can't open the doors."

"I know," Kaelen said gently. "I watched you pay the tolls. In the Hollow Road, I paid some of them for you, and I paid far too many for myself, and I know exactly what those walls feel like from the inside." He hesitated. "Which brings me to the other thing. The reason I tracked you for six days instead of just walking up and saying hello like a normal person."

"Which is?"

He reached into his coat and drew out an oilcloth packet, bound with cord, fat and soft-cornered from handling. He set it on the stone between them but did not push it toward her.

"Letters," he said. "Sketches. Songs, some of them, though I warn you my singing hasn't improved. Everything I could write down about what you lost—not to force it back on you, but so it exists outside both our heads. So that if you ever want to go looking, there's a map. And if you never want to look—" his voice caught, steadied, "—then at least the record exists. Somewhere. Even if it's only us."

Ada stared at the packet. Her hands ached to touch it and shied away from touching it, both at once, like a tongue probing a missing tooth.

"What's in it?" she whispered.

"Your mother's lullaby—all four notes of it, the ones you've been humming. Sorrel's saying, the one you can't quite hear. The story of how we met, which is genuinely embarrassing for both of us and involves a stolen goat. The way you look at dawn, which I described in frankly excessive detail because I had time on my hands and feelings I didn't know what else to do with." He rubbed the back of his neck. "And the last page is blank. I left it blank on purpose. For whatever comes next. New memories. Ones nobody has to sacrifice for."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the rain, an owl called, and the sound of it slid under the locked door inside her and lay down there, warm, like a cat that remembered the house even when the house forgot itself.

Ada realized she had made a decision somewhere in the last minute without consulting herself, the way her feet took the split-birch turn before her mind caught up.

"Stay tonight," she said. "By the fire. It's miserable weather and you're soaked through and I'm apparently the sort of person who doesn't leave people in the rain, even strangers. Especially strangers." She pointed a finger at him before he could speak. "But tomorrow, you walk the eastern waypoints with me. All of them. And you tell me the goat story, because I refuse to believe I was involved in anything involving a goat until I've heard the evidence."

Kaelen's whole face changed—hope arriving like sunrise, cautious, disbelieving its own luck. "You want me to stay?"

"I want to know why my chest does a thing when you smile," Ada said, with the brisk honesty she suspected was native to her. "That seems worth investigating. Over several days. Possibly weeks." She poured a second cup of tea, added honey, and handed it over. "Fair warning: I'm told I'm stubborn, I hum badly, and I take my honey in tea but not porridge. Start there. We'll fill in the rest as we go."

He took the cup. Their fingers touched around it, brief and ordinary and seismic.

"To filling in the rest," Kaelen said softly, and drank.

Above the shrine, through a tear in the clouds, the moon rose—whole and round and, if you counted carefully, one turn shy of new. The firelight held steady. And in Ada's coat pocket, against her ribs, the note with the last line in unfamiliar-familiar ink seemed, very slightly, warmer than the body next to it.

She didn't notice. But the fire did, and leaned toward it, listening.
