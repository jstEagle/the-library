## 20. What Kaelen Kept

Kaelen had carried the satchel for six weeks before he could bear to open it.

It was not a heavy thing. That was the cruelty of it. A lifetime of loving someone ought to weigh more than a shoulder bag of paper and string. But there it sat in the bottom of his pack, beneath the spare shirt and the dried plums Ada liked, riding along the hidden roads of Thornfell like any other piece of contraband.

He knew something about contraband. He had spent the better part of a year smuggling memories out of a burning world — his own, mostly, and then, when the Veilstone took hers, hers too. Every letter he'd written her. Every sketch he'd made of her hands. The songs. Gods, the songs. He'd sung them into being on cold nights while she slept, because he had been afraid that if he didn't set them down somewhere outside his own skull, the Hollow Road's toll would take them too.

The road had already taken plenty. He no longer remembered his mother's face — only that she had one, and that it had been kind, and that she had given up everything so that he could be born with a heartbeat instead of eternity. He no longer remembered the color of the sky over the First Threshold. He suspected it had been blue, but only because everyone insisted skies usually were.

What he remembered was Ada.

Not all of her. The tolls had seen to that. But enough: the way she said *no* as though it were a complete philosophy; the exact pitch of her laugh when she was surprised into it, which happened perhaps four times a year and which he had once treasured more than divinity; the morning at the crossroads when she'd caught him poaching her routes and looked at him like a lock appraising a very ambitious key.

And now she traveled beside him and felt none of it.

She was cheerful about it, which was somehow worse. She treated him the way one treats a well-mannered stranger met at an inn — polite, curious, faintly amused by his habits. She had learned that he hummed when nervous. That he talked to horses as though they were colleagues. That he could not cook, a fact she found endlessly funny and reminded him of nightly, standing over the fire stirring whatever she'd salvaged from his ruin of a pot.

"You're staring at your bag again," she said now, without looking up from the stew.

Kaelen started. "I'm looking at the fire."

"You're looking at the fire *through* your bag." She glanced up. Firelight caught the small scar on her jaw — new since the mountain, from a stonefall during Maren's retreat. He had not been there to catch her. He thought about that scar more than was probably healthy. "You've been doing that for weeks. Either there's something in it you want me to steal, or there's something in it you're afraid I'll find."

She was still a smuggler to the bone. Even hollowed out, even missing whole years of herself, she read people the way other folk read weather. It was one of the thousand things about her he loved and had never told her, because telling her would be a kind of theft — taking her grief and making it his conversation.

"Ada," he said. His voice came out rougher than intended. He cleared his throat. "There is something. And I think — I think it's time."

* * *

They built the fire higher, because she asked, and because he wanted her comfortable for what came next. Then he lifted the satchel onto his knees and untied the drawstring with fingers that would not entirely cooperate.

"I need you to understand something first," he said. "I'm not giving you these back. Not exactly. They're yours, but they won't do what you might hope. Reading them isn't remembering. It's more like—" he searched for the smuggler's vocabulary she'd appreciate— "it's like holding a map of a road that's been washed out. You can see where it went. You can't walk it."

"Then why keep them?"

"Because I couldn't throw them away." He said it plainly, because anything softer would have been a lie. "And because someday — I don't know when, I don't know how — some part of you might want proof. That you were loved. That it wasn't nothing."

Her expression did something complicated. He watched her decide, deliberately, to meet it with humor, the way she met most unbearable things. "Was it nothing?"

"It was," Kaelen said, "the single best thing that ever happened to a god's son who had spent thirty years expecting to be disappointed by the world."

That landed. He saw it land. She looked down at her hands, and for a long moment the fire cracked and the mountains held their silence around them.

Then she said, quietly, "Show me."

* * *

He began with the letters, because they were the least dangerous.

There were forty-one of them. He'd written the first the night after Sorrel died, when she'd come to him shaking with grief and fury and inheritance all tangled together, and he had not known what to say aloud, so he'd said it on paper instead and never sent it. After that he hadn't been able to stop. Letters about her. Letters *to* her. Letters he'd composed in his head while walking point through passes that shifted like dreams, dictating silently to himself: *today she argued with a mule and lost; today she hummed a lullaby without noticing and I nearly wept in front of two guild brothers.*

He handed her the bundle. She read the first one slowly. Her lips moved slightly. He looked at the fire instead of at her face, because watching felt like trespass.

"'You argue with livestock,'" she quoted eventually. "'You win roughly half the time, which the livestock respect.'"

"He was a very opinionated mule."

"And this one says—" she flipped a page, and her voice changed, went careful— "'I don't know if she'll ever look at me the way she does at dawn on a high pass, but I hope she knows someone sees her the way the dawn does.'" She stopped. Turned the page back. Read it again.

Kaelen kept his eyes on the flames. "I was drunk on altitude," he offered. "People write nonsense at altitude."

"That's not nonsense."

"No," he admitted. "It isn't."

She worked through the letters one by one, and he watched her the way you watch someone stand in shallow water over something deep — waiting to see what stirs. Mostly she smiled. Twice she laughed, short and startled, at things he'd forgotten writing. Once, at a letter describing the night in the loop-village when she'd shattered a hundred people's paradise to free them and then sat alone shaking afterward, she went very still, and her hand rose halfway to her chest and stopped, hovering, as though reaching for a weight that wasn't there anymore.

"I remember *doing* that," she said slowly. "I remember it like a report. Here is what happened, here are the numbers. But this says I cried until my ribs hurt. This says you sat with me until dawn and neither of us spoke." She frowned at the page. "I know it's true. I can tell it's true. It just — doesn't feel like mine."

"It's yours," Kaelen said gently. "It's just in storage."

"What a terrible warehouse."

"The worst. I've filed complaints."

That earned him half a smile, and he counted it a victory worth the whole evening's ache.

* * *

Next came the sketches, and those were harder, because he had drawn her without meaning to — margins of maps, backs of letters, the flyleaf of Sorrel's fractured guidebook. Dozens of her. Her profile against a ridge. Her hands sorting cargo seals. Her asleep against a saddle, mouth open, undignified, perfect.

She spread them across a blanket between them like a merchant laying out wares, and studied each one with the narrowed eyes she'd once used on forged bills of lading.

"These are good," she said accusingly.

"I had excellent subject matter."

"You drew me *asleep*. That's surveillance."

"That's devotion," Kaelen said, "with charcoal."

She snorted — actually snorted, a graceless wonderful sound — and held up the sketch of her sleeping. "My own mother wouldn't recognize me from this. Mouth open. There's drool implied."

"There's drool *rendered*, yes. Art demands honesty."

"You're a menace." But she kept that one in her lap rather than returning it to the pile, and he pretended not to notice, and noticed everything.

It went on this way for hours. She interrogated each drawing, each letter, cross-referencing them against the hollow spaces inside herself like a woman probing the socket of a lost tooth. Sometimes she found an echo — a flicker, a warmth with no source. Once she touched a sketch of the two of them laughing in the doorway of a shrine and pressed her palm flat against the paper and closed her eyes, and Kaelen held his breath and hoped and hated himself for hoping, and nothing came, and she opened her eyes and said, "Nothing. It's like reading about strangers," and he said, "Then let me introduce you," and began telling her the story behind the picture, and she listened with her whole body, hungry and wary at once.

Last, near midnight, he took out the songs.

There were nine. He sang the first one badly — his voice was made for argument, not music — and she let him get four lines in before she held up a hand.

"You're flat."

"I'm aware."

"You're *very* flat. Give it here." She took the lyric sheet from him, frowned at the notation he'd scrawled, and tried it herself — and her voice caught on the third line as though it had snagged on a hook below the waterline of her memory. She stopped. Her eyes had gone wide.

"What is it?" His heart was hammering. "Do you—"

"No." She shook her head slowly. "Not remembering. But my throat knew where the note went before I did. Like — like a door my hand remembers the latch of, in a house I've never lived in." She laughed, unsteady. "Gods. That's the strangest feeling of my entire life, and I'm told I once walked through the underworld arguing with a toll-keeper."

"You haggled with it," Kaelen said. "The toll-keeper. You got it down to two memories instead of three. It was deeply offended."

"I would have." Something moved through her face — grief, maybe, or its ghost. "I wish I could remember being that person. She sounds insufferable."

"She was magnificent," he said, and meant every syllable, and watched her hear it.

* * *

She was quiet for a long time after that. She stacked the letters, squared their edges, retied the bundle with the same care she gave to sealing crates. When she finally spoke, her voice was level in the way he'd learned meant she'd done her crying in advance, internally, where no one could tax it.

"Why are you showing me this now? You've had weeks."

"Because I was a coward for six weeks," he said. "And because today, when we passed the burned shrine at Miller's Bend, you stopped and stared at it for a full minute, and then you asked me why you cared about it, and I realized I was letting you grieve without letting you know what you were grieving *for*. That's not kindness. That's just a slower cruelty."

She absorbed that. Then: "What do you want, Kaelen? Honestly. Because a man doesn't carry forty-one letters up a holy mountain and back unless he wants something."

It was the question he'd dreaded, and she asked it the way she did everything — straight down the middle, no cover, no flank. He made himself answer it honestly, because she deserved that much and he owed her considerably more.

"I want you to be whole," he said. "However that happens. If wholeness means these papers help — good. If it means building past them — also good. I'm not asking you to love me again. I'm not even asking you to believe that you did." He turned the song sheet over in his hands. "I spent years watching gods take from mortals and call it covenant. I won't do the same. These are gifts. No strings. You could burn the lot tomorrow and I'd still walk your roads, if you'd have me."

"And if I send you away?"

His chest tightened around something old and familiar. "Then I'll go," he said. "Miserably, and probably writing letters about it, but I'll go."

She studied him for a long moment — that smuggler's appraisal, weighing him like uncertain cargo — and then she reached over and picked up the sketch of herself asleep with her mouth open, the one she'd kept in her lap all evening.

"I'm keeping this one," she said. "For evidence. In case I ever need to prove what a menace you are." She tucked it into her coat, over her heart, which he chose not to remark upon because remarking would have ruined it. "The rest you carry. You're the archivist. I'm apparently just the subject matter."

"That's not—"

"Kaelen." She looked at him across the fire, and there was something in her face that wasn't memory and wasn't quite its absence — something new, growing in the cleared ground where the old thing had stood. "I don't remember you. But I've spent six weeks traveling with you, and here's what I know. You talk to horses like colleagues. You sing flat. You give gifts with no strings and then sit there looking wounded when people accept them. You watch me like—" she paused, chose her word with care— "like the dawn watches a high pass." She tapped the coat pocket where the sketch lay. "I may not recall loving you. But I like you. Right now, today, on present evidence. And I'd rather build forward with a man like that than send him off to be miserable in the snow."

Kaelen discovered that his eyes were wet, and did not particularly care.

"So you'll keep traveling together?" he managed. "Us. The roads."

"For now," Ada said. "Ask me again at the next waypoint. I'm a smuggler, Voss. I deal in the next waypoint, not forever."

It was, he reflected later, lying awake under stars that had recently learned gentleness, the most hopeful thing anyone had ever said to him.

* * *

Two days on, they came down out of the high passes toward the valley where Thornfell's first rebuilt shrine stood — a low stone circle raised by the villagers Ada had freed from the loop, tended now by a widow named Bess who left the flame always lit.

Word had gone ahead of them, as word does along smuggler roads. As they crested the last rise, Kaelen saw the crowd gathered at the shrine — farmers and guild-folk and two of Sorrel's old brothers — and saw them turn as one, and saw their faces change.

They remembered her. All of her. The woman who had shattered their paradise to save them, who had climbed the mountain, who had paid the price none of them could name but all of them sensed. And she stood there among them, beloved and celebrated, holding the memory of it the way you hold smoke.

A little girl broke from the crowd and ran to her, tugging her sleeve, saying something Kaelen couldn't hear. Ada knelt. Whatever the child said made her go still, then look up — past the crowd, past the shrine, straight at him, with an expression he had never seen her wear in this life or the last.

"Kaelen," she called across the gathering. "Come here. I think—" she hesitated, and laughed at herself, and the sound of it was almost, *almost* the old pitch— "I think there's something I'm supposed to pray for. And I don't know what it is yet. Help me find out?"
