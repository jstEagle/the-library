## 21. The First New Prayer

The shrine at Millbrook had been three things in its long life: a place of worship, a hay barn, and briefly—according to Kaelen's records, which Ada read the way other people read old love letters, slowly and with a strange ache she couldn't name—a hiding spot for a stolen reliquary that turned out to be a very convincing cheese wheel.

She'd laughed at that one. She always laughed at that one.

Now it was a shrine again. The villagers had spent the whole of autumn rebuilding it, and they'd done it wrong in a dozen small ways—the altar stone was set crooked, the threshold faced east instead of north, and someone had carved a duck into the lintel where the rites called for a wren. Ada had pointed out each error gently, expecting them to care, because everything she knew about the old covenant came from documents and from Kaelen, who remembered her remembering, which was not the same thing as knowing.

But the villagers had only shrugged. "The duck stays," said Old Marta, who was ninety if she was a day and had outlived four priests, two floods, and one husband who'd deserved it. "Ducks brought us through the famine. Wrens did nothing."

And the flame had lit anyway.

That was the thing Ada still couldn't get used to, eight months after the mountain, after the Veilstone, after whatever it was she'd given up. Under the new covenant, the gods answered *gently*. A prayer for rain brought soft gray drizzle that lasted exactly as long as the fields needed it. A prayer for a safe birth brought a midwife's steady hands and a warmth in the room that no one could quite describe afterward. A child's prayer for a lost dog brought the dog home with mud on its paws and an expression of profound guilt.

No fire in the sky. No wishes granted with cruel literalism. No blind, starving powers clawing at the veil between worlds. Just... attention. The difference between a drowning man clutching at you and a friend taking your hand.

"You're staring at the flame again," said Kaelen.

Ada blinked. She'd been doing that more lately—losing minutes to the shrine's hearth, where a small fire burned day and night without ever consuming quite all of its wood, as if the god within it were being politely frugal. Veyra's people, Kaelen said. Threshold-keepers. They understood doors better than anyone, including the door between what burns and what remains.

"I'm allowed to stare," Ada said. "I'm the guildmaster. Staring thoughtfully is most of the job."

"Your guild smuggles wool now."

"Tax-free wool. It's practically heroic."

He smiled—that easy, crooked smile that made something flicker at the edge of her mind like a fish turning under ice. It happened sometimes. A flicker, a warmth, a sense of a word sitting just behind a wall. She'd learned not to chase it. Chasing it was like pressing on a bruise to see if it still hurt. It did. It always did.

Kaelen sat down across the hearth from her, folding his long frame onto a bench that creaked in protest. He carried a satchel everywhere now, stuffed with paper. In the eight months since he'd appeared at her fire—a stranger, patient and grieving, whom she did not remember—he had filled eleven notebooks with letters, sketches, songs, and accounts of everything she'd lost. He never pushed them on her. He left them stacked in her quarters like patient birds, and she read them when she was ready, which was usually late at night, which was usually followed by crying, which was usually followed by feeling foolish for crying over people and moments she couldn't recall.

Sorrel's handwriting. Her mother's lullaby, transcribed in his careful script with the notes drawn out beside it because he couldn't carry a tune and knew it. A sketch of her own face, laughing, done so well it frightened her.

"Mart asked me something today," she said. "The boy. The one whose family joined the wardens last month."

"Mm?"

"He wanted to know why we rebuild shrines if the gods can hear us anywhere." She poked the fire with an iron rod, sending up a scatter of sparks. "Good question. I gave him the answer from the records—sacred ground, old agreements, thresholds honored. All true, I think. But it wasn't the real answer, and he knew it. Kids always know when you're giving them the answer from the book."

"What's the real answer?"

Ada considered the flame. It leaned toward her, faintly, the way a cat leans toward a hand it trusts.

"Because you don't pray to someone across a room," she said finally. "You sit with them. The gods spent three generations starving in the dark, Kaelen. Whatever they are now—whatever we made them into with this new covenant—they know what it is to be alone on the far side of a wall. A shrine is just... proof we'll come sit on this side of it."

Kaelen was quiet for a moment. When she glanced over, he had that look he got sometimes—the one where grief and pride were fighting for the same territory and calling a truce. "They should make you write the doctrine," he said.

"The doctrine would be four pages long and mostly about wool smuggling."

"Two pages. You'd spend the rest on the duck."

"The duck is load-bearing, Kaelen. Morally speaking."

* * *

The festival of the First New Prayer was Marta's idea, because Marta's ideas had a way of becoming everyone's ideas before anyone could object. Every soul in Millbrook and half the surrounding valleys came up the hill at dusk carrying candles, and the shrine—which held forty people comfortably, sixty uncomfortably, and two hundred only by the theological argument that the gods would make room—held two hundred.

Ada stood at the back. That was habit older than memory; she'd been keeping herself invisible since before she could write, and some arts didn't leave you even when the reason for them did. But tonight she watched the crowd instead of cataloguing exits: farmers with dirt still on their knuckles, weavers, two of her own wardens awkward in their first proper cloaks, a cluster of children daring each other to touch the hearthstone. An old woman wept quietly near the door, and nobody minded, because half the room was quietly weeping too, the way people do when something broken has been mended and they're still not sure they trust the seam.

Kaelen found her there. He always found her. It was one of the small mysteries she'd stopped questioning—how he could locate her in any crowd, any dark road, any crowded inn, as if he'd mapped her years ago and kept the map current.

"Nervous?" he asked.

"Why would I be nervous?"

"Because Marta has announced that you'll be leading the prayer."

"She *what*."

"She stood on the bench about ten minutes ago and told two hundred people that the woman who reforged the covenant would offer the first prayer of the new age. There was applause. There may have been a hymn. I believe someone has already composed a verse about your hair."

"That is—" Ada pinched the bridge of her nose. "I am a smuggler. I have dedicated my life to avoiding crowds, priests, and public speaking, usually simultaneously."

"Yes," Kaelen said, and his voice went soft in that way it did, the way that made the fish turn under the ice again. "And then you walked up a mountain and gave away everything you loved so that strangers could keep theirs. You've been avoiding crowds your whole life, Ada. You've never once avoided what matters."

She looked at him. He looked back, steady and sad and warm, wearing his mortality like a coat that fit him better every year.

"How do you always know what to say?" she asked.

A shadow crossed his face—brief, quickly folded away, the shadow he wore whenever she asked anything about himself. "Practice," he said. "Years of practice."

Then Marta's voice rang out—"Lady Delacroix! To the front!"—and there was no more time for shadows or questions, because two hundred people were turning to look at her, and the hearth-flame rose a hand's width higher as if it, too, were curious.

* * *

Walking to the front felt like walking a smuggler's road in fog: every step taken on faith that the ground continued past where she could see. The crowd parted. Children stared. Someone's baby grabbed her sleeve and declined, with tremendous conviction, to let go, so she completed the journey with a small determined passenger attached to her arm, which improved her dignity not at all and her courage considerably.

The hearth waited. Behind it, through the shrine's open doorway, the thirteenth moon—whole now, whole forever, its cycle complete and begun anew—hung low and gold over the hills.

Ada knelt at the altar stone. It was still crooked. The duck still gazed down at her with its carved, serene stupidity. And she found, kneeling there, that she had no idea what to say, because every prayer she'd ever witnessed had been a transaction—give us rain, give us safety, give us back what we lost—and she had learned, on the mountain, at a cost written in someone else's handwriting in eleven notebooks, exactly how dangerous it was to ask the divine for returns.

So she prayed the truth instead. Quietly. Only loud enough for the flame.

"I'm not going to ask for my memories back," she said. "I've thought about it. I could ask, I think. The covenant owes me, and the new terms are kind, and maybe you'd find a way to give them gently—her lullabies, his lessons, his laugh—one at a time, like medicine."

The flame flickered. Listening. Gods under the new covenant were good listeners; it was practically their defining feature.

"But here's what I figured out," Ada went on. "If you gave them back, I'd stop building. That's the trap in wishing for the past—it makes the present a waiting room. Sorrel—" the name caught, the way it always caught, grief for a man she knew only on paper— "Sorrel taught me roads I don't remember learning. My mother sang songs I can't recall. Fine. Then I'll learn new ones. New roads. New songs. New reasons." She swallowed. "What I want isn't what I lost. What I want is the strength to build new ones. To keep going up the hill when the hill is steep and the crowd is large and there's a duck judging me from above. Give me that. If you're inclined to give a smuggler anything, give me that."

For a moment, nothing. The crowd breathed behind her. The baby on her sleeve snored.

Then the flame leaned toward her—all the way, unafraid, the way fire leans toward the thing it intends to warm—and poured upward, gold and green and a color she had no name for, and the heat of it touched her face like a hand on her cheek, and somewhere in the light she felt—not saw, not heard, *felt*—an answer arrive the way spring arrives: gradually, then all at once, unmistakable.

*Yes,* said the warmth. *Yes, little road-walker. Build.*

It was not words, exactly. It was permission. It was a door opening onto a morning. It lasted three heartbeats and then settled back into the hearth, modest and crackling, leaving the smell of cedar and sun-warmed stone and, inexplicably, bread.

The crowd made a sound Ada had never heard before—half sigh, half cheer, wholly involuntary. Old Marta blew her nose like a war horn. And Ada knelt a moment longer with her eyes closed, holding the warmth against her face, feeling something in her chest that wasn't a memory at all but was perhaps its foundation: the simple, animal knowledge that she was loved, by someone, for reasons she'd been trusted to rediscover in her own time.

When she opened her eyes, Kaelen had come to stand beside the altar. He offered her his hand, formal as a dance, eyes suspiciously bright.

"Well led," he said. "Though you forgot to thank the duck."

"The duck knows what it did."

She took his hand and let him help her up, and the flicker came again—stronger than usual, a whole chord instead of a single note. His hand in hers. Not this fire, another one. A road through darkness. Her own voice saying words she couldn't retrieve, saying *stay*, or maybe *don't*, or maybe both. It passed through her like weather and left her standing very still, gripping his fingers harder than she'd meant to.

"Ada?" Concern in his voice, careful as glass.

"I almost had something," she said. "Just now. With your hand."

Something moved through his face like wind through wheat. Hope, she thought. And fear of the hope, which was worse. "What kind of something?"

"I don't know. A road at night. A fire that isn't this one. Me, talking to you about—" She shook her head, and it was gone, dissolved back behind the wall where all of it lived. "Sorry. It's nothing."

"It's not nothing," Kaelen said quietly. "It's never nothing." He didn't let go of her hand. Neither did she. "Do you want to know what it was?"

"No." She surprised herself with how certain her voice was. "Not yet. If it comes back, I want it to come back whole, not secondhand. Is that—" She hesitated. Eight months of reading his notebooks had taught her that this question mattered enormously to him, though she couldn't say how she knew. "Is that all right? With you?"

Kaelen smiled, and this time the grief in it had made peace with something. "Ada," he said, "I once waited three hundred years for my mother to open a door. I can wait as long as it takes for you to remember why you're glad I'm here. Take all the time in the world. I've got nothing but."

"That," said Ada, "is either the sweetest or the saddest thing anyone has ever said to me, and I resent that I can't tell which."

"It's both. Most true things are."

Behind them, Marta was already organizing the feast, on the theological grounds that the gods had provided bread-smell and it would be rude not to follow through. The crowd began to flow out toward the trestle tables, candlelight spilling down the hill like a river deciding to be generous. Somewhere a fiddle started up, badly, then less badly.

Ada stayed a moment longer at the hearth. The flame had settled into its ordinary smallness, frugal and content. On the lintel above, the duck presided over everything with carved serenity.

"New ones," she told it, and herself, and the listening god in the fire. "We're building new ones."

And the flame answered warmly, and the night smelled of bread, and down the hill Kaelen was waiting to walk her home along a road she'd learn by heart tomorrow.

She just didn't know yet that she'd be teaching it, someday soon, to others—or that one clear dawn was coming, years off but already on its way, when a snatch of song would catch her mid-stride and her mother's voice would rise through her like water finding its old channel, one perfect note, and she would smile and keep walking, letting the rest come slowly, the way spring does.

Gradually. Then all at once.
