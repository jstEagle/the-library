## 22. Noor and the Veil

The morning fog had a smell to it that Ada Delacroix knew better than her own name—which was fortunate, because some mornings she wasn't entirely sure of the name.

Wet stone. Pine resin. The faint iron tang of the old shrine spring, three switchbacks below. She stood at the mouth of the Hollow Step—the road that didn't exist, the one that ran behind the waterfall and came out in a valley that half of Thornfell believed had burned down twenty years ago—and she waited for her students the way she'd once waited for smugglers: with one eyebrow raised and no patience for lateness.

They came up the trail in a straggling line, five of them this season. Wren, the miller's daughter, who walked like she was apologizing to the ground for touching it. Two brothers from the coast whose names Ada could never keep straight and had privately decided to call Salt and Pepper until corrected. A solemn boy named Tam who took notes on everything, including, Ada suspected, his own breathing. And Ilsa, sixteen and furious about it, who had been thrown out of two apprenticeships before Sorrel's old guild brothers—what remained of them—had sent her up the mountain with a note that said only: *This one bites. Good luck.*

"Roads," Ada said, when they'd gathered. "That's what we teach here. Not swords. Not prayers. Roads."

Tam raised his hand.

"You don't need to raise your hand. This isn't a temple school."

He lowered it halfway, then thought better and raised it again. "But the roads *are* prayers, aren't they? That's what Kaelen wrote. In the third chronicle. That the hidden ways were cut by the wardens as a kind of—of walking devotion. Every path a promise kept."

Ada looked at him for a long moment.

"That," she said, "is exactly the sort of thing Kaelen would write." She turned back to the fog. "It's also wrong, or half-wrong, or right in a way that will get you killed if you believe it too hard. The roads were cut by people who needed to move flour past a tax station without being hanged. If there's holiness in them, it got there the way holiness gets everywhere—by somebody loving something ordinary enough, long enough."

She heard Wren scribbling and suppressed a smile. Let her write it down. Half of teaching was saying things you didn't fully mean so that someone smarter than you could write them down and make them true.

"Now," Ada said, "the Hollow Step floods in spring melt. It floods *sideways*, which is worse. So we go through in single file, left hand on the wet wall, and if the water starts singing—"

"Singing?" said Salt, or possibly Pepper.

"Humming. Low note, like a kettle deciding. That means the pool above has backed up and you have about forty heartbeats to clear the narrows. You count your heartbeats. You do not run, because running on wet stone is how we find out which of the gods still owes me a favor."

Ilsa snorted. "And which ones do?"

"Veyra, mostly. We're on good terms." Ada stepped into the dark of the pass. "She sends flowers now. It's unsettling."

* * *

The road behind the waterfall was everything it had always been—narrow, dripping, lit by seams of pale lichen that the wardens had cultivated for six generations because torches drowned in the spray. Ada moved through it the way other people moved through their own kitchens. Her hand found every hold without asking her eyes. Her feet knew the false step at the second bend, the one that looked solid and dropped into cold black water, and she stepped over it while talking.

"—and that's why we mark nothing," she was saying. "No cairns, no blazes. The road lives in you or it doesn't live anywhere. Which brings us to the actual lesson, the one nobody likes: memory is a road too, and most of yours are washed out and you don't even know it."

"I know all my memories," said Ilsa.

"Do you? What did you eat on your ninth birthday?"

Ilsa opened her mouth. Closed it. Glared.

"See. There's a gap where a day should be, and you never noticed it until I asked. Now imagine whole years like that. Imagine looking at a man across a fire and knowing—he *knows*, the way you know the Hollow Step—that he matters to you more than anyone alive, and having no idea why." Ada's voice stayed light. It had taken years to make it stay light. "Imagine he's patient about it. Imagine he writes everything down."

They emerged onto the ledge above the hidden valley, and the morning broke open around them—gold light pouring over the ridge, mist burning off the terraces, the little village below with its smoke rising straight and unhurried. The village that had burned down, according to everyone who wasn't standing in it.

Tam stopped dead. "This is Fenlow's Reach. Fenlow's Reach burned."

"Fenlow's Reach burned," Ada agreed, "in the year the moon turned its thirteenth time. And then it unburned, because the new covenant keeps gentler terms than the old one, and because a lot of people held a pass that day so that a girl with a hole where her life used to be could climb a mountain." She let that sit. "You're not just learning roads. You're learning what the roads are *for*. Come on. Fenlow's wife bakes on Thursdays, and I intend to be shameless about it."

* * *

Kaelen was already at Fenlow's table when they came down, which was how it usually went. He had a genius for arriving places first without seeming to hurry—a last residue, Ada sometimes thought, of being the son of a goddess of thresholds. Doors probably liked him.

He sat with his chronicles spread around his plate like a man defending a small paper kingdom: the bound volumes, the loose leaves, the little sketches he did of people's hands because he claimed faces were too easy and hands told the truth. He was writing now, pen moving in that quick careful script, and he didn't look up until Ada dropped into the seat across from him, at which point he looked up the way he always did—as if she were the thing he'd actually been waiting for all along, and the chronicles were just how he passed the time.

"Wardens survived the Hollow Step," he said.

"Barely. Salt fell in."

"Pepper fell in," said one of the brothers.

"See, this is why I can't tell you apart."

Kaelen laughed—not the big laugh, the quiet one, the one that lived mostly in his eyes—and Ada felt the familiar thing happen in her chest. The pull. Like a door in her standing slightly ajar, wind moving through it, carrying a scent from a room she'd never seen.

She knew him. Not remembered—*knew*. The way her feet knew the false step. Six years now, and the knowledge hadn't dulled; if anything it had deepened into something with weight and grain, like wood worn smooth by handling. He was a stranger who was not a stranger. He was the shape of an absence that had somehow become a presence.

Fenlow's wife set down a plate of Thursday buns and Kaelen slid the chronicles aside to make room, and Ada caught a glimpse of today's page. Sketches of the students—Wren's apologetic shoulders, Ilsa's mutinous chin. Below them, writing:

*Year six of the new covenant. The thirteenth moon wanes full tonight. Ada taught the Hollow Step. She counted heartbeats aloud so Wren wouldn't have to count alone.*

Ada read it twice.

"I don't do that," she said.

"You do it every time," Kaelen said mildly, buttering a bun. "You think you're counting for yourself. You've never once needed to count for yourself."

She stared at him. He smiled, entirely unbothered, and pushed the bun plate toward her, and the moment folded itself away into the great quiet archive of moments like it—the ones he kept and she couldn't.

Six years. He had never once made her feel the size of the gap. That was the miracle she couldn't remember asking for and received daily anyway: that living beside her own absence, he had never once made her feel hollow. He treated the missing years like a country she'd traveled to, not a wound. He told her about them the way travelers tell stories—generously, with detail, leaving room for her to ask or not ask.

Once, early on, she had asked him the direct question. *Did I love you? Before?*

He had gone very still, and then he had said, "Yes. I'm not going to pretend otherwise, because pretending would make it smaller, and it wasn't small." And then he'd added, gently, "But you're not her, Ada. You're the woman who climbed down the mountain after giving everything away, and started again, and learned to laugh at my jokes as if they were new. Which they weren't, but you laughed anyway. I'm not waiting for her to come back. I'm very fond of the one who did."

She had not known what to do with that. She still didn't. So she'd done what she always did with things too large to hold: she'd built a road through them, one day at a time, and walked it.

* * *

They stayed at Fenlow's through the afternoon because the students were useless with full stomachs and because Ada had learned—relearned, rather, the hard way—that rest was part of the craft. Kaelen interviewed the villagers for the chronicle, perched on a fence with his notebook, asking questions no one else thought to ask: what the well tasted like before the covenant broke, whether the ghosts of the burned year still walked (they didn't; Veyra had swept the thresholds herself), what songs the children sang now.

One of the songs stopped him mid-sentence.

It was a skipping rhyme, the kind children make out of anything—rope rhythm and nonsense—but underneath the nonsense there was a melody, four notes rising, and Kaelen's pen went still against the page.

"What is it?" Ada asked.

"It's—" He paused. "It's a lullaby. Or it was. Your mother sang it. It's in the records—I transcribed it from Sorrel's papers, years ago. Four notes, then the turn." He hummed it under his breath, careful and exact, the way he did everything. "The children must have gotten it from somewhere. Songs travel like that. They fall out of books and into rope games and nobody knows how."

Ada stood very still in the lane.

Four notes, then the turn. The children chanted it again, laughing, and something happened in her chest that had no business happening on a sunny Thursday in a village that had unburned. It wasn't memory. Memory was a room she could walk into, and this was not that. This was a single candle lit in a window of a house she'd never entered—one clear note of a voice, low and warm and amused, singing to a child in the dark, and the child had been her, and the voice had loved her the way water loves the sea, totally, without effort—

"Gone," she said aloud, and the note faded, and she was standing in a lane with sun on her face and an ache in her throat like she'd swallowed a star.

Kaelen was beside her. He didn't touch her—she'd told him, years ago, that in these moments she needed space more than comfort, and he had remembered, because he remembered everything, that was the whole trouble with him.

"There it is," he said softly. "That's the first one, isn't it? A real one. Not an echo."

"How would you know?"

"Because echoes fade when you reach for them. That one reached for *you*." His eyes were bright and careful. "Ada. The covenant reforged on willing surrender. Willing things can be given back. Nobody knows the terms yet—maybe not even the gods. But Veyra told me once, at the threshold, that nothing truly surrendered is ever truly lost. It goes into the keeping of what it was surrendered *to*."

"And the Veilstone kept it."

"The Veilstone kept it. And the Veilstone wakes a little more every season. And every dawn, somewhere in you, a window opens a crack." He smiled, and it wobbled at the edges, and Ada realized with a jolt that he was frightened—him, who had faced zealots and underworld tolls and his own mother's judgment with nothing worse than dry humor. Frightened of hope. "You might get her back. Not all at once. But the way roads come back—"

"One stone at a time," Ada finished.

"One stone at a time."

She looked west, toward the mountain, toward the summit she had descended six years ago with a hollow where her heart's history used to be. The evening light lay gold on the snowfields. Somewhere up there, the keystone of the world hummed in its socket of stone, holding everything she'd given it—Sorrel's gruff lessons, her mother's lullabies, the sound of a young man laughing at a crossroads—and it was waking, waking, the way spring wakes, slow and inevitable and warm.

"Well," she said at last, and her voice was steady, because she had built that steadiness herself, stone by stone, and it was hers no matter what the mountain held. "Then I'd better keep walking. Roads don't build themselves."

Behind them the children sang the four notes and the turn, and the evening took the song gently, the way the covenant took all surrendered things—into keeping, not into loss.

Ada smiled, shouldered her pack, and started up the road home, letting the rest come slowly.

And far above, on the white shoulder of the Veil, the first stone of spring shifted in its sleep—and dreamed, faintly, of a lullaby.
