## 1. Charms for a Rainless City

There are three rules to selling charms in a city that has outlawed magic. One: never call them charms. Call them *keepsakes*, *tokens*, *little comforts*. Two: never let the customer see your hands shake, because shaking hands look like working hands, and working hands get reported. Three: never, under any circumstances, do the thing you are actually good at.

I had followed all three rules for twenty years, which in Vellmark made me either the most disciplined woman alive or the most cowardly. Some mornings I couldn't tell you which.

The morning the frost came, I was arranging my window display — river stones painted with fat blue eyes, knotted cords that promised safe travel, little tin wolves that did absolutely nothing except catch the light and make children smile. That last one was my best seller, which told you everything about this city. People didn't want magic anymore. They wanted the *shape* of it, the memory of it, worn smooth and harmless as beach glass.

"Yara." Old Marta from the dye-house shouldered through my door with her usual grace, which was to say none at all. "Sky's gone green over the tanneries."

"I can see that."

"You can see it. Can you *do* anything about it?"

I set down a tin wolf with exaggerated care. "Marta. We've discussed this."

"We've discussed lots of things. Doesn't mean I agreed." She planted herself in front of my counter like a woman planting a flag on hostile territory. She was seventy if she was a day, dyer's hands stained permanently indigo, and she was the only person in Vellmark who knew what I was — or half-knew, which is its own kind of dangerous. "That storm's going to sit on the Low Quarter all night. You know what sitting water does to the foundations down there."

"The foundations are the landlord's problem."

"The *people* are not the landlord's problem, which means they're nobody's problem, which means they're yours." She jabbed an indigo finger at me. "You've been calming weather out of that back room for six years and don't think I haven't noticed. My rheumatism knows a storm's been gentled before my knees even ache."

"My rheumatism," I said, "is a lie I tell my joints."

"And your shop is a lie you tell the whole city." But her voice went soft on it, the way it always did, because Marta remembered what I'd been before. Not what I'd done — she didn't know the whole of that, thank every indifferent god — but what I'd been. A girl who could put her hand flat against the sky and ask it nicely.

"I sell keepsakes," I said. "Little comforts. That's all."

"That's all," she repeated, and left the bell above my door jangling like an accusation.

* * *

Here is what I actually sold, when I wasn't selling nothing: hope in convenient portions. A cord to hang over a cradle. A stone to hold during a fever. A pressed flower from the moorlands, where flowers still grew wild, sealed in wax so it couldn't be accused of being anything more than sentimental. The crown's edicts were specific — no workings, no bindings, no calling of wind or water or fire, no communion with the old powers — but they hadn't yet figured out how to outlaw nostalgia. Give them time. This king had outlawed everything else.

Twenty years ago, magic had been everywhere in Vellmark. Hedge-witches on every corner, storm-callers licensed by the crown itself — the crown had *licensed* us once, imagine — blessing the harvest ships and sweetening the wells. Then came the Breaking, though nobody in the city talked about it honestly. They said the wolf-god Fenris turned on his human covenant-keepers. They said the storms went mad. They said someone betrayed someone, and the sky over the north still bore the scar of it, a permanent bruise-colored cloud bank that sailors called the Wolf's Grudge.

What the edicts said was simpler: magic broke the world, therefore magic was forbidden, therefore anyone caught practicing would be taken to the Gray Tower for questioning. Nobody asked what questioning meant. The people who went to the Gray Tower didn't come back to describe it.

So I buried my gift the way you bury a body: deep, quietly, and with a story ready in case anyone asks about the disturbed earth.

The afternoon wore on. I sold a travel knot to a fisherman's wife, a fever-stone to a nervous grandmother, and nothing at all to a man in a gray coat who examined my window display for too long and bought nothing and smiled too easily when he left. I told myself he was a customs clerk. I told myself a lot of things.

Then the child came in out of the rain.

She couldn't have been more than six, soaked to the skin, barefoot, one shoe lost somewhere between wherever she'd been and my doorstep. She stood dripping on my floorboards with the particular stillness of a child who has learned that being noticed is bad. Behind her, through the streaming glass, the sky over the tanneries had gone the color of a bruise going bad — that deep wrong green that means the storm isn't passing, it's *settling*, and settling right over the Low Quarter where she'd clearly run from.

"Are you lost?" I asked, gentle, the way you talk to any frightened animal.

She shook her head. Then, after a long moment, in a voice scraped thin: "The water's coming in our house. It comes in fast now. Mama sent me to find my brother and the sky went—" Her hands made a shape in the air. Children always make the shape of it correctly. A fist, closing.

Outside, thunder rolled down off the hills and didn't roll away again. It sat there, grumbling, like something deciding whether to eat.

Now. Here is where I should tell you I weighed my options like a sensible woman. Twenty years of discipline, twenty years of smallness, three iron rules — and against all of it, one wet child making the shape of a storm with her fists. There wasn't really any weighing at all. That's the thing about the ache of a life lived small: it doesn't hurt until the moment you could stop living it. Then it hurts everywhere at once.

"Come here," I said. "Away from the window."

She came. I knelt, and I did the thing I had not done in front of another living soul in six years, and only in secret ever at all: I reached past my own fear and took hold of the storm.

It's hard to describe what that feels like without sounding either mad or boastful, and I've been both, so let me try for neither. Imagine holding a rope that runs up into a hurricane. Imagine the rope is also a conversation. The storm wasn't angry, exactly — storms rarely are; they're just enormous and indifferent, like kings. But it had decided to sit on the Low Quarter, and un-deciding a storm is like arguing with a mountain. You don't win. You negotiate.

*I see you,* I said to it, in the old way, the way my mother taught me before there was anything to be afraid of. *You're heavy with rain and tired of traveling. But there's a child here, and children are under the old protections, and if you sit on their roofs tonight I will remember it.*

And the storm — this is the part that still frightens me, twenty years on — the storm *recognized* me. I felt it turn its vast attention toward the shop, toward the kneeling woman with her hand hovering an inch above a child's wet hair, and I felt something ancient in it go *ah, there you are.* Like a dog hearing its name after years.

Rain eased to drizzle over the Low Quarter. The thunder wandered off eastward, sulking. And in my little shop that sold nothing but harmless things, the temperature dropped so fast my breath showed, and frost bloomed across every windowpane in delicate white ferns, spreading, spreading, writing itself across the glass in letters no weather had any business knowing.

The child laughed. Actually laughed, one bright startled sound, and pressed her finger to the frost, and the frost *curled toward her finger* like a cat.

"You stopped it," she said. "The sky listened to you."

"The sky listens to everyone," I said, which was the first outright lie I'd told her, and far from the worst. "Go home the dry way, through the cooper's lane. And don't tell anyone—"

But she was already gone, bare feet slapping wet cobbles, one shoe forever lost, and I was alone in a shop full of frost.

* * *

I stood there a long time, watching it melt. Frost doesn't melt evenly. It retreats in patches, and for a few minutes the windows looked like maps of somewhere else — somewhere cold and honest, maybe, where a woman could kneel in her own doorway and touch the sky without burying herself afterward.

Because that was the arithmetic, and I knew it cold. One saved child against one unmistakable sign. Frost-magic in summer. Frost that moved *wrong*. Any inquisitor worth his gray coat would read those panes like a confession. I could scrape it, salt it, blame a freak cold snap — but the man in the gray coat had already come and gone today, and Marta knew, and the child knew, and knowledge in Vellmark spread faster than plague and killed slower, which was somehow worse.

I got the bucket of hearth-ash and salted the windowsill anyway. Rituals against evidence. It felt exactly as useful as it sounds.

The ache arrived on schedule, then, the way it always did after I slipped. People think the danger of hidden magic is getting caught. The real danger is quieter: you spend so long being small that smallness starts to feel like your actual size. You forget the shape of yourself. I had been the most gifted storm-caller of my generation — my mother said so, the wolves had said so, the sky itself had said so, once, in a voice like breaking stone — and now my greatest public achievement was a fever-stone that worked through placebo and a display of tin wolves.

A life lived small leaves marks the same way a life lived large does. They're just harder to point to.

I was sweeping the last of the ash when the bells started.

Not the market bells. Not the harbor bells that rang when a ship came in drunk on good news. These were the tower bells, all of them at once, the pattern they used twice in my lifetime — once for the old king's death, and once for the Breaking. I went to the door. Down the street, people were stopping in doorways, looking up, looking at each other. Somewhere a crier was already running, and I caught the words as he passed, breathless, hoarse:

*"By royal decree! By order of His Majesty! All persons harboring or concealing the gift of workings shall present themselves or be sought out! The hunt begins at dawn!"*

The hunt. Not an inquiry. Not a registry. A *hunt*.

In my doorway, in the smell of rain and ash-salt, I watched my neighbors look at each other — really look, the way you look at a door you might need to bar — and I understood that the city I had spent twenty years hiding inside had just changed species around me.

Marta appeared at my elbow, indigo hands gripping my sleeve, her face gone the color of old paper. "Yara," she said. "Pack your things."

"I'm not leaving."

"Yara—"

"They'll take the ones who stay," I said. "The ones who can't run. The old ones. The marked ones. If everyone gifted runs, they'll burn through the rest of us by winter just asking questions." I heard my own voice and didn't recognize it — steady, low, stubborn as bedrock. Twenty years of smallness, and apparently some part of me had been waiting the whole time for a reason to stop.

Marta stared at me. "You'll be found. Frost on your windows, girl. Frost in *summer*."

"I know," I said.

Above the rooftops, the bruised sky was finally clearing — clearing, I noticed, in a pattern too neat to be natural, clouds peeling back from the north as if something up there had finished speaking and was waiting to see whether I'd answer.

Somewhere far off, at the edge of hearing, beneath the bells and the criers and the drumming of my own stubborn heart, something howled.

It sounded, God help me, like it knew my name.
