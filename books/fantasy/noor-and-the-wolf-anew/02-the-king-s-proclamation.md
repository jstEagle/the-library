## 2. The King's Proclamation

The criers came on a Tuesday, which felt like a personal insult. Tuesdays were market days, and market days were the one morning a week I allowed myself to feel like an honest woman.

I was arranging my charms in the window of the shop—harmless things, mostly: knotted cords for safe travel, dried lavender sewn into linen hearts, little tin wolves that did nothing but look handsome and remind people of a time when wolves weren't a metaphor for anything worse than hunger—when the first bell rang from the Bell Tower square.

Not the market bell. The other one. The one they only ring when the crown wants everyone listening at once.

I kept my hands moving through the display because that is what you do. You keep your hands moving. A shopkeeper with still hands is a shopkeeper thinking about running.

Through the glass I watched Torvald the crier climb the steps, unroll his parchment with the ceremony of a man who has done this forty times and will do it forty more, and begin to read in the flat, carrying voice they train into them at whatever academy produces professional bad news.

"By decree of His Majesty Aldric the Third, Everlasting, Protector of the Realm—" *Everlasting*, I noted. Someone in the chancery had been paid to write that word, and someone else had been paid not to laugh. "—a great hunt is hereby proclaimed throughout Vellmark and all her territories, for the finding and surrendering of persons gifted with unlawful arts."

Unlawful arts. As if magic were a craft guild you'd failed to register with. As if the storm in my blood had ever asked me for my papers.

"Persons so discovered who present themselves within seven days shall be shown the crown's mercy and conveyed to places of assessment."

*Places of assessment.* Four words doing the work of a hundred. Nobody knew what happened at the places of assessment because nobody came back from the places of assessment—or if they did, they came back quiet, and thin, and looking at doors as though the doors might say something.

"Persons harboring the gifted shall be judged accomplices. Persons concealing knowledge of the gifted shall be judged accomplices. Reward is offered for information leading to discovery." He paused there, and I swear the pause had been rehearsed, because it let the last line land alone in the cold air. "The realm will be made clean."

The crowd in the square didn't cheer. That was the strange thing, the thing I turned over all day like a stone looking for what lived underneath it. They didn't cheer, but they didn't leave either. They stood in their coats and their shawls and listened to the whole proclamation read twice, and then they went back to their stalls and their haggling, and every single conversation afterward had a new shape to it. People stood slightly farther apart. People laughed half a beat too quickly. Old Marta, who sells eels and has sold eels since before I was born, looked at her neighbor Halvard's stall for a long moment with an expression I couldn't name, and then looked away, and I understood that something had already begun, right there between two eel sellers, something small and irreversible.

Neighbor turning on neighbor doesn't start with knives. It starts with looks held a moment too long.

* * *

By afternoon, three people had come into the shop who had never bought anything before and never would.

That's how it works. They come in under some pretext—a charm for a sick mother, a knot for a fisherman's net—and they browse slowly, and they ask questions with soft edges, and they watch your hands. I've been watched by professionals; I know the amateur version when I see it. These three were amateurs. One of them picked up a tin wolf and asked me where I got them, and I said "from a tinsmith in Lowbridge," which was even true, and he set it down very carefully, the way you set down something that might be evidence.

I smiled at all of them. I have a good smile. Twenty years of practice. It says *I am exactly what I appear to be, a tired woman selling trinkets, please buy the lavender heart, it's two coppers.*

The lavender heart sales were up, actually. Fear is wonderful for the charm business, which is one of those ironies I'd laugh about if it didn't pay my rent.

When the last of them left, I sat down behind the counter and did arithmetic I didn't want to do. Seven days to surrender. Which meant eight days before the searching started in earnest, and the searching wouldn't be soldiers kicking in doors—not at first. At first it would be exactly what I'd just seen: neighbors, browsing. And I had spent twenty years building the one thing a hunted person cannot afford: a community that knows where I live.

They didn't know what I was. But they knew I was kind in specific ways. They knew that when the lightning storms came off the moor in autumn, the ones that should have burned the Tanner's Row roofs every year, somehow the strikes always fell on the empty lots, the old quarry, the river. They knew that Widow Brenna's fever broke on a night no medicine could have managed it. They knew the frost on my windowpanes last week, the frost shaped like nothing natural, spreading out from a child's nightmare like a scream made visible—and they knew I'd told them it was a drafty window, and most of them had chosen to believe me, because believing me was easier and kinder and safer.

But safer for whom? That was the question underneath the stone now. If they believed me, they were accomplices. The proclamation had made kindness itself a crime, retroactively, by decree.

I put my head down on the counter, on top of the account book, and breathed the smell of old paper and lavender, and thought: *twenty years, Yara. Twenty years of being small, and it ends anyway.*

* * *

Mira came at dusk, the way she always does, letting herself in through the back without knocking because she has been letting herself in through that door since we were nine years old and it belonged to the woman who taught us both our letters.

She's a seamstress, Mira. The best in the district, though she'd deny it with her whole body. She has hands that can mend anything and a face that gives everything away, which is why she came in already talking.

"Don't," she said.

"Don't what?"

"Don't give me that face. Don't sit there being calm. I heard it, everyone heard it, the whole city heard it, and you're sitting here dusting your little wolves like it's a normal Tuesday, and I need you to not be calm for five minutes so I don't have to carry both our panics."

"I'm not panicking," I said. "Panic is inefficient."

"There it is. There's the face." She dropped into the chair across from me—the customer chair, the one she's never once sat in like a customer—and put her sewing bag on the counter with a thump. Inside it, I noticed, was not sewing. Inside it was bread wrapped in cloth, a stoppered jug, and a knife with a blade the length of my forearm.

"Mira."

"It's for cutting bread."

"The bread isn't armed, Yara. You are. You're armed, and you're leaving tonight, and I've come to make sure of it because I know you, and I know that if I don't come, you'll find seventeen reasons to stay, and reason number one through seventeen will all be named after other people."

I looked at the knife. I looked at her. Outside, the lamplighter was working his way down the street, and his flame caught the frost-faint ghost of last week's panic still etched faintly in the corner pane, the mark the glazier couldn't quite polish out.

"You heard the proclamation," I said. "Harboring the gifted makes you an accomplice. You're sitting in the shop of a—"

"A trinket-seller," Mira said. "A harmless, boring, slightly stingy trinket-seller who waters my plants when I'm ill and once talked a debt collector out of taking our roof. That's who I'm harboring. That's all anyone in this district can prove I'm harboring, provided you're gone before they start looking properly."

"And if they find the frost? If someone talks about the storms?"

"Then they talk about a kind neighbor who calmed weather, and I'm an accomplice to kindness, and I'll live with it." Her voice cracked on the last word, just barely, the way old plaster cracks—hairline, structural. "Yara. Please. I buried your mother. I promised her—I promised her something I've never told you, and I'm telling you now, and it's this: I said I'd get you out of any city that turned on you, and I have been waiting twenty years for this exact evening, and I will drag you north myself if I have to."

Something in my chest went tight and hot. Not fear. Something older and stupider.

"North," I said. "To what? The Wolf Reaches are a story mothers use to stop children wandering. There's nothing north but pine and cold and—"

"—and distance," she said. "Distance from criers. Distance from amateurs browsing your shelves with soft questions. Distance from the places of assessment." She leaned forward. "What do you think happens there, Yara? Truly. Because I've been asking around today, quietly, and do you know what I found? Nobody knows. Twenty years of the hunt existing in whispers and nobody, not one person, can name someone who came back. Doesn't that—that should terrify you more than the proclamation does."

It did. Of course it did. But terror is a currency and I've learned to spend it carefully.

"I'm not the only one they'll come for," I said.

"No," she agreed. "You're not. And that's exactly why you going changes nothing for them and everything for you. You can't calm a storm for a whole city, Yara. You've tried. Last autumn, during the big one—you nearly killed yourself holding that lightning off the tanneries, and what did it buy? One season. One season of safety bought with a nosebleed that lasted two days, and next autumn the storms come again, and next year there won't be a you to stand in the square and argue with the sky." She was crying now, quietly, furiously, the way she does everything. "You've been the wall for twenty years. Walls don't get thanked. Walls get torn down or they crumble, and either way the people behind them have to learn to build their own."

"That's a terrible speech."

"It's true, though."

"It's a terrible truth, then." I stood up and walked to the window, because I couldn't have this conversation facing her. Down the street, life was performing its usual pantomime: the baker pulling his shutters, children chasing each other around the well, a dog with strong opinions about the dog across the street. All of it ordinary. All of it now shadowed, every ordinary thing stamped with the proclamation like wax on a letter. "There are eleven of them, Mira. Eleven people in this district who are like me—smaller, mostly, thank the gods, a hedge-healer, a boy who can find lost things, the twins who dream true. Eleven people who will be browsed at and informed upon and taken to places of assessment while I'm safely north eating your armed bread. And every one of them knows me. Every one of them came to me when their gift frightened them, because I was the only person in Vellmark who wasn't afraid of it. If I run, I teach them what I actually believe: that running works, that hiding works, that being small works. Twenty years of small, and look what it bought. Look what Tuesday bought."

"So you'll stay and be taken."

"I'll stay and be useful until I can't be."

"That is the same sentence with better grammar," Mira snapped, "and you know it."

We stood there in the lamplight, two women in their forties arguing like the girls we'd been, and outside, somewhere across the rooftops toward the Bell Tower, another bell began to ring. Then another. Then a third, from the direction of the harbor, and a fourth from Gate Street, and I realized with a cold slide in my stomach that these weren't alarm bells. They were the proclamation bells, ringing district by district, walking the king's decree through the city street by street like a tide coming in. By midnight there would be no corner of Vellmark that hadn't heard it twice.

"They're not going to wait seven days," I said quietly. "Are they. The seven days is for the ones who'll surrender themselves. For everyone else—"

"—the searching starts tomorrow," Mira finished. "Maybe sooner. Maybe tonight." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, hard, the way she'd wipe flour off in her mother's kitchen thirty years ago. "So. North. Tonight. With me. Say it."

And here is the shameful truth, the thing I have turned over every night since: I almost said it. I had the bread in my hand. I had my mother's ring off the shelf and into my pocket. I was, in the way that matters, already gone.

But then I heard the screaming.

Faint, from the direction of Tanner's Row—one voice, then several, the particular ragged chorus of a neighborhood watching something happen to one of its own. Mira heard it too. We looked at each other, and I saw her do the same arithmetic I was doing, and I saw her lose.

"That's the Kessler house," she whispered. "Old Anders Kessler. He's seventy. He mends boots. What gift could a boot-mender possibly—"

I was already moving, snuffing lamps, pulling my coat, and Mira grabbed my arm and I shook her off, gently, the way you shake off someone you love.

"Yara—"

"Go north," I said. "Take the bread. Wait for me at the shrine past the north gate—if I'm not there by dawn, go on without me, and live long, and hate me a reasonable amount." I paused at the door, at the threshold of my small life, and gave her the smile, the real one, the one I keep underneath the shopkeeper's. "Twenty years of small, Mira. Let's find out what I'm worth at full size."

Behind me, over Tanner's Row, the sky was beginning to flicker—and not with lightning.

With frost. Frost climbing the walls of a burning house, frost blooming against fire like a white flag raised by someone who had finally stopped waving it.

Tomorrow, I knew, the criers would have new news. But tonight belonged to the eleven, and to me, and to the wolf-god whose name I hadn't spoken aloud in twenty years and whose attention, I felt suddenly and terribly certain, had just found me again.
