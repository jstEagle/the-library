## 3. Frost on the Doorstep

There are three ways to find a mage in a city that has outlawed magic, and I have used all of them in my eleven years of service to the Crown.

The first is money. Everyone betrays everyone for enough coin, and Vellmark's poor betray each other faster than most, because their betrayals come with bread attached. The second is fear, which works on the same principle but costs less. The third—the one no inquisitor likes to admit to—is attention. You simply watch. Mages are people who cannot help themselves. They flinch at thunder they didn't call. They touch sick children and then burn the cloth they wiped their hands on. They live small lives with an unnatural, desperate precision, like actors who have rehearsed one role too long and know every exit by heart.

The frost told me where to look before I ever set foot in the Lantern Quarter.

Frost in midsummer is not subtle. The Crown's weather-readers had flagged three anomalies in as many days: a cold snap over Copperlane that killed a fever outbreak dead in its tracks, a hailstorm that fell on exactly one rooftop and nowhere else, and—most damning—a shopfront on Wren Street whose windows bloomed white ferns of ice on a morning when the rest of the city sweated through ninety degrees. The pattern was amateurish, scattered, almost tender. Whoever it was wasn't defending territory or making a point. They were *helping*. Which meant either a fool or a saint, and in my experience the two categories overlap more often than the Church would like.

I went alone. That was my first mistake, though I didn't know it yet. Protocol said take six men and a writ. But six men and a writ announce themselves from four streets away, and I wanted to see the place before it knew it was seen. I told myself it was tactics. I have since learned that this particular lie is the load-bearing wall of every bad decision I've ever made.

Wren Street smelled of tallow and wet stone, the way all of Vellmark's poorer quarters smell in summer when the drains can't keep up. The shop sat between a cooper's yard and a shuttered chapel, narrow-fronted, its sign hand-painted and slightly crooked: *Charms & Small Blessings — Y. Eriksen, Proprietor.* The E was smudged, as if someone had traced it often. I noted that. I noted everything, back then. It was the only prayer I knew.

I stood across the street for the better part of an hour, doing what I do best, which is nothing, convincingly. A woman came out with a jar of pickled something-or-other and walked away lighter than she'd arrived, which is not how commerce usually leaves people. A boy of perhaps eight ran in without knocking and ran out again clutching something small to his chest like a stolen relic. And once—I would have missed it if I'd blinked—a man pressed his forehead briefly against the doorframe on his way past, the way you'd touch a shrine. Not the shopkeeper. A stranger. Gratitude, then. In a city where magic could get you a cell beneath the Citadel, someone was being thanked for it in broad daylight.

So. Not a fool, then. Possibly a saint. I crossed the street.

* * *

The bell above the door gave a single flat note, like a coin dropped on wood.

The shop was smaller inside than out, which shouldn't be possible and yet was—one of those spaces that seems to fold itself apologetically around its contents. Shelves of charms, mostly harmless: knotted cords for safe travel, dried lavender bundles "for restful sleep," copper coins bored through and strung on leather "against rheumatism." The kind of inventory that exists precisely because it does nothing. Every city has a dozen shops like it. They are the camouflage of our trade, and honest ones, in their way; a person who buys a knot against nightmares is telling you where it hurts even if the knot never works.

Behind the counter stood the proprietor, and I will be honest, because honesty is the only currency I have left: my first thought was that the Crown's intelligence was wrong. Yara Eriksen looked like exactly what the sign promised—a charm-seller. Slight, quick-eyed, hands stained with dye and copper polish, wearing the harried smile of someone interrupted mid-task. No storm-caller's bearing. No hunger behind the eyes. Just a shopkeeper watching an inquisitor walk into their shop and doing the arithmetic of survival behind a pleasant face.

"Good morning," I said, and produced my writ. "Soren Vale, Royal Inquisitor, Third Circle. A routine inquiry."

"No one's routine inquiry is routine," Eriksen said. The voice was level. Almost. There was a hairline fracture in it, the kind only another frightened person hears—and I am always a frightened person, whatever my uniform says. "But welcome anyway. Lavender? On the house. You look like a man who doesn't sleep."

That landed harder than it should have. I took the sprig because refusing would have been a statement, and statements are expensive.

"I'm here about frost," I said.

"Terrible season for it."

"On your windows. Three days ago. Midsummer frost, ferning across the glass, melting by noon. The neighbors remember it. Neighbors always remember it."

Eriksen's hands kept moving—straightening a cord, squaring a tray of buttons—but slower now, the way water slows before it freezes. "Windows fog. Glass is old. Cold nights on the river, warm days—"

"It was ninety degrees."

"Then my windows are very talented." A beat. "Inquisitor, if you're asking whether I froze them on purpose, I sell knots and lavender. If I could make frost in July, don't you think I'd charge more?"

It was a good answer. It was, in fact, the exact right answer, delivered with exactly the right blend of indignation and weariness, and I have heard a hundred guilty people give it and slept soundly after. So why did my hand, resting on the counter, notice that the wood beneath my palm was cold? Not cool. *Cold.* Like a stone in January. Like something recently wept over.

I looked down. Between us on the counter lay a child's drawing, done in charcoal on cheap paper, the kind of thing a parent keeps pinned up until the next drawing replaces it. A crooked house. A crooked sun. And wolves—three wolves, drawn with the fierce, unskilled devotion children give to things they love, all legs and teeth and impossible eyes. One of the wolves wore a crown of scribbled lightning.

I should have taken it. Evidence protocol was clear: any wolf-imagery in proximity to suspected gift-work was to be catalogued and reported. Wolves were covenant-matter. Covenant-matter was treason-adjacent. I had filed forty such drawings in my career. They go in a drawer in the Citadel archive, next to a hundred other drawers, and no one ever looks at them again, and somewhere a child grows up learning that the thing they loved was evidence.

"Inquisitor?" Eriksen said quietly. "You've gone somewhere."

"I'm deciding whether you're lying to me," I said.

"And?"

"And I genuinely don't know." I picked up the drawing. The charcoal had smudged under someone's thumb—Eriksen's, I guessed, touched often, the way you touch a wound. "Who drew this?"

"A neighbor's girl. She comes in after school while her mother works the looms." Something moved behind Eriksen's eyes—not guilt. Fear, but a strange, specific flavor of it, the fear of someone standing between a threat and something smaller than themselves. "She likes wolves. Children like wolves. Is that a crime now too?"

"It's becoming one," I said, before I could stop myself.

The silence that followed had weight. Eriksen looked at me—really looked, the way you look at a door you're considering—and I understood, with the cold certainty of eleven years' training, that I was being measured. Weighed against some private scale. Found—what? I still don't know. I only know that whatever verdict was reached, it was reached quickly, and it changed the temperature of the room.

"You should go," Eriksen said softly. "Before someone sees an inquisitor's coat in here and decides my neighbors need reminding what happens to shops that talk."

"That's usually my line."

"Yes," Eriksen said. "I imagine you say it a lot."

* * *

I did not arrest anyone that day. I want that on the record, wherever records go when no one reads them.

I walked the quarter instead. That's the other discipline they teach you at the Citadel—walk the ground, let the ground tell you things—and Wren Street told me plenty. The cooper mentioned, unprompted, that Eriksen's charms had gotten his wife through a winter fever. The loom-girl's mother bought a travel-knot every month and swore her boy hadn't coughed since. An old woman pressed a coin into my hand—into an *inquisitor's* hand—and asked me to thank the charm-seller for the rain-calm over her roof during the last storm, because her thatch couldn't have taken another night of it.

Rain-calm. Frost in July. Fevers broken. All small. All deniable. All stitched together into a pattern so careful, so *humble*, that it took a professional to see it at all: twenty years of tiny mercies, performed at enormous personal risk, for people who could offer nothing but gratitude and silence.

And the silence held. That was the remarkable part. I interviewed nine neighbors formally, informally, and by the methods I won't dignify with description, and not one of them gave me Eriksen. In Vellmark, where a man will sell his brother for a sack of flour, an entire street had agreed to keep one secret. I have thought about that a great deal since. At the time, I filed it under *anomaly* and told myself anomalies resolve.

They don't, usually. They accumulate.

* * *

I wrote the report that night in my rooms above the Citadel annex, with the window open because the summer heat wouldn't break and the paper stuck to my sweating hand.

Here is what I should have written:

*Subject Eriksen, Y., proprietor, Wren Street, displays probable unregistered gift-work consistent with weather-influence (frost manifestation, storm-dampening). Neighborhood testimony corroborates pattern of minor interventions. Recommend immediate detention and transfer to Citadel for assessment under Proclamation protocols.*

Here is what I actually wrote:

*Subject Eriksen, Y., charm-seller, investigated following reports of anomalous frost. Findings inconclusive. Premises consistent with legitimate trade; window phenomenon attributable to defective glazing and river-fog conditions. Subject cooperative; no evidence of gift-work. Recommend no further action. File closed.*

I read it four times. Each time, the words stayed the same words, and each time they felt less like mine.

Understand: I had lied before. Small lies, procedural lies, the lies inquisitors tell suspects to loosen their tongues—*your neighbor already confessed*, *the penalty doubles at dawn*. The job runs on them. But this was different. This was me, Soren Vale, Third Circle, eleven years of clean service, setting my own hand against the machine I served, choosing—with ink and signature and full knowledge—to bury a living person inside a closed file.

Why? I've turned it over every night since, and the honest answer is that there isn't one answer. Partly it was the drawing—the wolves with the lightning crown, charcoal-smudged by loving thumbs. Partly it was the old woman's coin, warm from her pocket, given to a man in a coat she had every reason to fear. Partly it was arithmetic: one frightened shopkeeper against a Proclamation that had already filled three wagons this week, and the growing suspicion—unexamined, unspoken—that the wagons were not going where the proclamations said they went.

And partly—this is the part I dislike most—it was the cold spot on the counter. Because I have stood in a hundred rooms with a hundred liars, and I know what lying feels like from the outside: the sweat, the over-rehearsed calm, the eyes flicking to exits. Eriksen had none of it. What Eriksen had was the stillness of someone who decided long ago exactly how much truth they could afford, and had made peace with the price. You don't learn that stillness hiding crimes. You learn it carrying something heavy for a very long time.

I signed the report. Filed it. Watched the clerk stamp it CLOSED with a thump that sounded, in the quiet of the archive, like a door shutting on a cell that would not, this once, be occupied.

Then I went home and did not sleep, which surprised no one, least of all the lavender wilting in my breast pocket.

* * *

Three days later, the criers came again, and this time the King's voice had teeth in it.

*"Let it be known,"* they shouted, from every corner in the city, *"that the hunt for the gifted extends to all quarters, all trades, all persons. Shelterers are as guilty as practitioners. Silence is complicity. Reward follows information. The Crown's mercy is finite."*

Silence is complicity. I stood in the annex courtyard and heard my own filed report called a crime waiting to happen. My closed file was now an open wound in the law: by my own signature, I had become a shelterer. A complicit man. If anyone pulled the thread—if Captain Brandt, meticulous as a surgeon, ever re-examined the Wren Street frost—the lie would unravel backward onto me, and it would not stop at me. It would reach the shop. It would reach the girl who drew wolves.

I found myself walking Wren Street that evening without meaning to, the way a tongue returns to a cracked tooth. The shop was shuttered early. Lamplight leaked gold through the shutters' seams, and inside, faintly, I heard voices—not Eriksen's alone. Neighbors. A whole quiet congregation of them, gathered in a charm-shop in an outlawed city, keeping each other company in the dark the way people do when the official word for their existence has changed overnight.

I stood across the street in the shadows like a thief casing my own conscience, and I understood two things with terrible clarity.

First: I had not buried a suspect. I had chosen a side. There is no neutral ground anymore; the Proclamation saw to that. Silence is complicity, the criers sang, and for once the Crown and my own heart agreed on the terms.

Second: whatever was coming for this street—and something was coming; I could feel it the way you feel weather behind mountains—it would not stop for my paperwork. Files close. Hunts don't. Somewhere in the Citadel's ledgers, the anomaly of Wren Street was already accumulating interest, and Captain Ilse Brandt, my protégé, my pride, the best of what I made, had a habit of reopening closed files just to check the seals.

I turned my collar up against a wind that hadn't existed that morning, and walked home through streets that smelled, faintly and impossibly, of coming snow.

The frost was back on the windows of Wren Street by midnight. I know, because I watched it bloom from my post across the lane—white ferns spreading pane by pane, delicate as breath on glass, in a city where such things were now worth a wagon ride and a name on a list.

And I knew, signing nothing, saying nothing, that my first lie would not be my last.
