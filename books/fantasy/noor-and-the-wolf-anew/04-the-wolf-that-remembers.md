## 4. The Wolf That Remembers

I hadn't dreamed of wolves in twenty years.

That's not quite true. I'd dreamed of them the way you dream of anything you've buried alive—briefly, guiltily, usually after too much plum brandy. A shape at the edge of a field. Eyes in a hedgerow. The kind of dream you wake from with your heart going like a trapped bird and your hand already reaching for a charm that wouldn't help.

But I hadn't dreamed *of* Fenris. There is a difference between a wolf and the Wolf, and my sleeping mind had kept that distinction sacred for two decades, the way it had kept everything else about that night locked in a room with no door.

So when I fell asleep on the third night of the proclamation—after nailing a horseshoe over my door out of pure superstition and then feeling foolish about it, because the danger wasn't coming through the door, it was coming down every street in Vellmark wearing royal livery—I expected nothing. I expected the usual: rain, ledgers, Marta's laugh, the small gray anxieties of a small gray life.

Instead there was snow.

Snow falling upward.

I stood in a pine forest I knew the way you know a scar—by touch, not sight—and the snow rose past me into a sky the color of a bruise healing. The trees were wrong. Too tall. Their shadows fell toward me instead of away, and somewhere under the smell of pine was the old blood-smell, iron and winter, the smell of the borderlands where the world thins to paper.

"You are late," said a voice that was not a voice.

It came from everywhere, the way thunder does. It came from inside my ribs. Twenty years had not softened it. Twenty years had not changed it at all, and that—that more than anything—was what made my knees go.

"Fenris," I said. My mouth worked around the name like a key rusted in a lock. "You're supposed to be silent."

"And you were supposed to be dead." The snow swirled, gathered, took a shape. Not a wolf, exactly. Something older than the idea of wolf, drawn by someone who had only ever heard wolves described. Too large for the space between the trees, which obligingly became larger. Eyes like the moment before lightning. "Yet here we both are, breaking our vows together. How sentimental."

"I didn't break any vow. You did."

The forest went very still. Even the upward snow hung suspended, each flake a small white accusation.

"Did I," Fenris said.

"You broke the covenant." I'd rehearsed this argument for twenty years in bathhouses and on long walks and in the dead hours behind the shop counter, and now that I finally got to deliver it, my voice cracked in the middle like a dropped plate. "You went silent. The stone shattered. The storms went wild and the gifted started dying and *I*—" I stopped. Started again. "*I* was left holding the pieces."

"Yes," said the wolf-god, gently, which was somehow worse than anger. "Let us speak of who held what."

And the forest folded itself around me like a page turning, and I was twenty years younger and running.

* * *

You have to understand something about memory: it lies. Not maliciously. It lies the way a shopkeeper rounds down a price—it wants the transaction to be bearable. For twenty years my memory of that night had been a smear of storm-light and shouting, my mother's hand torn from mine, the covenant-stone blazing white, and then silence, silence, silence, the god gone out of the world like a snuffed lamp.

Fenris gave it back to me whole. That was the cruelty of it. No rounding down.

I was eleven years old again, standing in the great hall at Hjartdal with rain hammering the windows, and the lords of the human realm were arguing about me as if I were a shipment of grain.

"The child calls storms. Let the child pay."

"She's a *child*, Lord Aldric."

"She is a vessel. That is better than a person. Persons argue."

My mother stood between me and them, and even now, even knowing everything, I could feel the shape of her back against my forehead—the way she'd pulled me close so I'd hear her heartbeat instead of their voices. She was storm-caller of the northern reaches, the strongest in three generations, and she had been chosen for the sacrifice because the covenant demanded a willing gift from the human side, and the lords had decided that willingness could be manufactured if you applied enough pressure to the right family.

"They will take you or they will take me," she whispered into my hair. "And I have already lived."

"That's not fair," eleven-year-old me said, with the devastating moral clarity of children.

"No," she agreed. "It isn't."

What actually happened—what my memory had spent two decades sanding down to a blur—was this: my mother walked to the covenant-stone with her chin up and her hands open, the way you approach a thing you mean to meet honestly. And the lords watched. And the wolf-kin watched from the treeline, silver-eyed and solemn. And Fenris watched from everywhere, because gods watch from everywhere.

And I ran forward.

Not bravely. Let me be clear about that, since memory has been trying to make it brave for twenty years. It was not brave. It was an eleven-year-old's panic, pure animal refusal, the same thing that makes a foal bolt. I threw myself between my mother and the stone and screamed at the god—at *God*—with all the authority of a soaked, terrified child:

"Take me instead! Take *me!*"

And the storm answered.

Not Fenris. The storm. Because a covenant is not a contract between two parties; it is a contract between two realms, and the sky itself had been listening, and the sky has never once cared about protocol. Lightning came down and struck the stone while two willing hearts stood before it—one offered, one self-offered—and the covenant, which had been fed properly for nine hundred years, drank deep of both and *overfilled*.

The stone shattered. Fenris screamed—I felt it in my teeth, in the roots of my hair, in the marrow the way you feel a bell—and the bond between human magic and wolf magic tore down the middle like wet cloth. Every storm-caller within a hundred miles felt it. Most of them died of it, their gifts turning feral in their chests without the wolf-side to hold the leash. My mother survived the lightning but not the tearing; she was dead before she hit the ground, and I was alive because I was small and the excess poured through me like water through a sieve.

The lords called it betrayal. The packs called it betrayal. Everyone needed it to be someone's fault, because the alternative was admitting that nine hundred years of careful ritual had been undone by a frightened child and a sky that loved too much.

I fled north. They hunted me. I fled south. I buried my gift under twenty years of charm-seller's smiles, and the god went silent, and I told myself the silence was punishment, and I deserved it, and I carried it, and I carried it—

"—and you carried it beautifully," Fenris said, and the forest came back, and I was on my knees in the rising snow, and I was forty-one years old again, and crying like the child I'd been.

"You let me believe it was my fault," I said.

"It *was* your fault." The great head lowered until those lightning-moment eyes were level with mine. "As it was your mother's fault, and the lords' fault, and mine. A covenant breaks the way a bridge breaks—not at one plank, but at the place where all the weight has been allowed to gather. You were the last plank laid. Do not flatter yourself with the whole collapse."

I laughed. It came out broken, but it came out. "Twenty years. You couldn't have told me this twenty years ago?"

"Twenty years ago you would not have listened. You were busy surviving. Survival requires a simple story." The wolf's breath moved through me like weather. "Now you require the truth, because the truth has come to collect."

* * *

"What do you mean, collect?"

The forest shifted. The trees drew back like courtiers, and the snow began to fall downward again, normally, which in this place felt obscene.

"Look," Fenris said. "See what your king does with his hunt."

And I saw.

I saw it the way you see things in dreams—directly, without the mercy of distance. I saw wagons moving south from Vellmark by night, black-canopied, guarded, their wheels wrapped in wool to muffle them. I saw the fortress at Grindal that officially housed "relocated persons," its census rolls listing four hundred souls fed, housed, accounted for.

And underneath the accounting, I saw the truth: the cells were empty. Had always been empty. The gifted taken in the purge were brought down into vaults beneath Grindal and laid on tables of black iron, and machines of brass and glass—machines I recognized with a sick lurch, because I'd seen their ancestors in the king's laboratories twenty years ago, before I ran—were set humming against their skin.

They weren't imprisoning the gifted.

They were *draining* them.

I watched a fire-tender from the night market—a man whose gift was no bigger than lighting his own pipe, a harmless, gentle man named Oskar whom I'd sold a luck-charm to not three weeks ago—laid out under glass while his warmth was siphoned off into vials of liquid light. I watched him age. Not die—age. His gift left him the way life leaves a body, gradually and then all at once, and what remained was a husk that the clerks dutifully crossed off the census roll as "deceased of natural causes."

The vials went up. Up and up, floor by floor, to a chamber at the top of Grindal keep where an old man sat drinking them.

The king.

King Aldric the Second—who had signed the proclamation, who had ordered the hunt, who had been young and golden in the portraits when I was a girl and was *still* young and golden in the portraits now, thirty years later, because portraits can be commissioned but bodies cannot be argued with—drank distilled magic the way other men drink wine, and had done for decades, and it was keeping him alive and hollowing him out in equal measure, and the hunt was not a hunt at all.

It was a harvest.

"He is emptying the land," Fenris said, and the god's voice had a sound in it I'd never heard before, and it took me a moment to recognize it as fear. "Every gift he takes unbinds the wild places a little further. The moors are going quiet. The rivers forget their names. And when the wild is gone entirely, the veil between realms goes with it—and what stands behind the veil will not be me."

"What's behind the veil?"

"Nothing good. Nothing old and good." The wolf turned its head, and for a moment I saw through its eyes: the borderlands far north, the Wolf Reaches, and beyond them a thinness in the air like the skin of a soap bubble, and pressure against it from the other side. Patient pressure. "Your king thinks he is stealing immortality. He is actually removing the nails from a door. And the door does not open inward."

I sat down in the snow. It seemed appropriate.

"Why show me this?" I asked. "Why now? Why not—gods, why not ten years ago, five years, why wait until the city's full of criers and informants and my face is on every post in Vellmark?"

"Because the covenant cannot be reforged by a hidden thing." The eyes fixed on me, and in them I saw the pines reflected, and the bruise-sky, and something else—something that looked unbearably like hope, which is the most frightening thing a god can show you. "A covenant is a meeting. Two sides, seen by each other. You have spent twenty years making yourself unseen, little storm. Unseen by the crown. Unseen by the packs. Unseen by yourself, most thoroughly of all. I could call you a hundred times and you would not have heard me, because hearing me means admitting what you are, and what you are is the last living piece of the day the world broke."

"That's a terrible reason to let people get harvested."

"Yes," Fenris agreed, without apology. Gods are like that. "But also: the time is simply now. The door thins whether you are ready or not. The king harvests whether you grieve or not. The only variable left in the whole equation is you."

"And what does the equation want from me?"

The wolf smiled. I want you to understand that a god smiling at you is not a comforting thing. It is the sky deciding to be friendly.

"The same thing it wanted twenty years ago," Fenris said. "A willing heart, walking north, to the stone. To reforge what was broken. The price has not changed, Yara Eriksen. The price is the price. One life, freely given, from each realm."

The cold went through me then, real cold, dream-cold, the cold of understanding.

"No," I said.

"You haven't asked what the human side must give."

"I don't need to ask. I know how covenants work. I've known since I was eleven years old standing in front of a stone." I got to my feet, and my legs shook, and I stood anyway, because some part of me had apparently decided that if I was going to be terrorized by a deity in my sleep, I was going to do it upright. "You want me to walk north and die. Again. Except this time it'll stick."

"I want you to walk north and *choose*," said Fenris. "There is a difference. Your mother knew it. It is the difference the lords never understood, and the difference your king will never understand, and it is the only currency the covenant has ever accepted. But yes. The price is a life. I will not lie to you about that. I have lied to you by silence for twenty years, and look what silence built."

* * *

I woke with frost on the inside of the window.

Not outside. Inside. Feathering across the glass in patterns I recognized with a jolt—wolf-print spirals, the old sigils of the Reaches, drawn by my own leaking gift the way a wound leaks blood. In the corner of the room, my kettle had frozen solid. In high summer.

I sat on the edge of the bed for a long time, breathing, watching my breath fog and the fog freeze mid-air and drift down like the world's saddest confetti.

Here is what nobody tells you about being haunted by a god: it doesn't feel like horror. Horror is clean. Horror has a shape you can run from. What it actually feels like is homesickness—a terrible, specific homesickness for a place that may kill you, braided through with the bone-deep relief of finally, *finally* hearing a voice you'd convinced yourself you'd imagined.

For twenty years I had thought the silence was abandonment. It wasn't. It was a held breath. And now the breath had gone out of the god, and it had said my name, and it had shown me my mother's back and my own small screaming face and a cellar full of gentle people being drunk like wine, and it had told me the price, and the price was what it had always been.

One life, freely given.

I got up. I lit the stove with a match, like a person, like a shopkeeper, like a woman who sells charms that don't work. I put the kettle on the flame and waited for the ice to surrender. Through the window, Vellmark was waking: carts, gulls, the crier's distant bell tolling the hour, and under it all the new sound the city had learned this week—the pause. The listening pause. Neighbors listening to neighbors. Everyone waiting to hear who would be given up next.

Marta's face floated up in my mind, unbidden. *Run,* she'd begged me. *Please, Yara. Run north, run anywhere.* And I'd said no, because the community needed its storm-calmer, because running meant admitting what I was, because a life lived small was still a life.

But the small life was over. The frost on the window had ended it, and the criers had ended it, and the god in my sleep had ended it most of all. There was no version of the coming months in which Yara Eriksen the charm-seller survived. There was only the question of what replaced her, and where, and at what cost.

North, then. To the Reaches. To the stone. To the packs who remembered my name and had every reason to spit on it.

And maybe—maybe—to whatever was left of the covenant, carrying the only coin it had ever accepted.

I was still standing there, kettle steaming, frost melting off the glass in slow wolf-shaped tears, when the knocking started at my door. Three sharp raps. Then a fourth, hesitant.

Not soldiers. Soldiers knock like they own the door.

This was someone afraid.

I crossed the room and lifted the latch, and Marta fell half through my doorway, gray-faced, her shawl still pinned for the night market she'd never reach, and grabbed my wrist with fingers cold as the frost on my window.

"They're burning it," she said. "Yara—they're burning the night market *now*."
