## 5. Ashes in the Night Market

The night market smelled of fried onions and fear, which was, I suppose, an improvement over the week before, when it had smelled only of onions.

I'd been selling charms at my corner stall for three nights running—copper bells, knotted thread, little glass eyes that did nothing but catch the lamplight and look pretty. Honest work, for a liar. People bought them anyway. In Vellmark, a charm that does nothing is worth more than one that works, because a working charm gets you burned and a useless one just gets you laughed at.

"Storm's coming," said Marta Halvorsen, hawking eels two stalls down. She said it every night, rain or shine, had said it for twenty years, and for twenty years it had been true more often than the weather-wives deserved credit for. She winked at me over her bucket of silver-scaled eels. "Big one. You feel it?"

"I feel my feet going numb," I said. "That's all I feel."

Which was a lie, of course. I felt it the way you feel a hand closing around your throat from very far away—a pressure in the ears, a taste like coins on the tongue. The storm out over the moorlands was real, and it wanted in, and every night it pressed a little harder against the edges of me like a dog nosing at a door it used to be allowed through.

Twenty years since I'd let it in properly. Twenty years of small things: a headache soothed here, a fever broken there, a hailstorm turned aside from a neighbor's roof while everyone slept. Small magics, deniable magics. The kind you could bury under a shrug if anyone asked.

But small magics leave frost. That was the trouble with them. You do one tiny kindness with weather in your hands and the world writes it down in white on the windowpane, and somewhere a man with a crown's coin in his pocket reads it like a letter addressed to him personally.

* * *

They came at the turn of the hour past midnight, when the market was at its fullest and its guards were thinnest.

I heard them before I saw them—the particular rhythm of boots that aren't trying to be quiet. Not smugglers. Smugglers walk soft. These boots wanted to be heard.

"Inquisition!" someone shouted from the far end of Lantern Row, and then the shouting became general, the way panic does, spreading stall to stall like fire through dry rope.

I saw the torches first. A dozen of them, moving in formation, and behind them the gray cloaks with the sun-and-chain stitched at the shoulder. And at their center, walking calm as a man out for an evening stroll, a tall figure in a black coat that had once been finer than it was now—Soren Vale, the disgraced inquisitor himself, come down from the citadel to do his own dirty work. I'd heard he'd been demoted for some failure or other. Apparently his penance was this: burning a fishmonger's market at midnight.

"By order of the Crown," his voice carried, flat and clear, "all persons found practicing or harboring the forbidden arts will be taken into custody for examination."

*Examination.* Everyone knew what examination meant. Nobody came back from examination. They came back as ash lists posted on the citadel doors, names in neat columns, three coppers paid to the family per name, which was two coppers less than the crown paid for a dead wolf.

Marta grabbed my arm. Her grip was stronger than a woman her age had any right to, eel-wrestling arms. "Yara." Her face had gone the color of her unsold fish. "Yara, they're not searching. Look at them. They're *collecting*."

She was right. That was the horror of it. The gray cloaks weren't ransacking stalls, weren't overturning baskets looking for charms and grimoires. They were going person to person, and where they stopped, they stopped, and a chain went on, and the person went away. And they were stopping at people I knew. Old Tomas who could make bread rise twice as fast as any oven had a right to. The Widow Skarre whose lullabies could put a colicky infant to sleep from across a room. Little things. Harmless things. Twenty years' worth of the same small mercies I'd sold my life to hide.

"They know," Marta whispered. "Somebody told them names. Somebody gave them a list."

I looked at her. She looked back at me, and I watched her understand, in the same moment I understood it myself, that we were both thinking the same thought: *it could have been either of us who talked, and neither of us would ever know, and it doesn't matter anymore.*

"Go," she said. "You've got legs. Use them."

"And you?"

"I'm old, Yara. My knees are bad. I never could run." She smiled, and it was the same wry smile she'd given me over eels a hundred times, and something in my chest tore along a seam I hadn't known was there. "Besides. Someone has to stand here and be counted. Otherwise how will they know what they've done?"

"Marta—"

"*Go.*"

I went. That is the fact I have to live with, and I'll carry it to whatever grave I get: she shoved me toward the alley behind her stall and I went, because twenty years of hiding teaches you that running is a reflex deeper than love.

I got maybe thirty paces into the dark of the alley before the screaming started.

* * *

It wasn't the screaming of the taken. The taken go quiet—that's the terrible thing about them, they go quiet and cooperative, because hope makes people docile and the gray cloaks are very good at killing hope quickly. No, this screaming came from the other end of Lantern Row, high and young, and I knew before I turned around whose it was.

Three children. I knew them. Of course I knew them—they came by my stall, they bought nothing because they had nothing to buy, and they sat on my step some evenings and listened to stories about wolves and storms, and I'd told myself for months that they were just children, ordinary children, hungry children, and told myself it so hard I almost believed it.

But I'd seen the oldest girl, Petra, chill her own cup of water on a hot day without touching it. I'd seen the middle boy, Anders, make a candle gutter and flare with nothing but a frown. And the littlest, Signe, four years old, who could make rain follow her around like a duckling, and whose mother had drowned last spring, and who had no one left to teach her that some gifts are death sentences in Vellmark.

They'd run the wrong way in the panic. Children do. And now they were backed against the shuttered front of the chandler's shop, and a gray cloak had Signe by the wrist, and Petra was screaming, and Anders was throwing pebbles that flared orange in midair like tiny dying stars—which was worse than useless, because every flare was a confession.

And Soren Vale stood ten feet away, watching, and did nothing.

I have thought about that moment many times since. What he saw, standing there. What he didn't do. At the time I registered only that he was the enemy, that his coat was black, that his face was unreadable, and that the world had narrowed to a single point: Signe's small wrist in a gloved hand.

Here is what twenty years of being small buys you: nothing. Here is what it costs you: everything, all at once, the moment you stop.

I stopped.

I don't remember deciding. I remember the taste of coins flooding my mouth, the pressure in my ears becoming a roar, and the sky over Vellmark—which had been holding its breath all night, that big moorland storm nosing at the walls—I remember the sky *opening.*

Lightning came down the way water goes down a drain, except reversed, except upward, except wrong, except mine. It struck the cobbles between the soldiers and the children with a crack I felt in my teeth, and the thunder that followed wasn't sound so much as the whole night being shaken like a rug. Rain hit all at once, sideways, a wall of it, and every torch on Lantern Row died together like a congregation snuffing out.

In the sudden black I moved. I don't know how I moved; the storm moves me when I let it, and I had finally, finally let it. I was at the chandler's wall. I had Signe under one arm, kicking and shrieking, and Petra's hand in mine, and Anders pressed to my side, and the gray cloaks were shouting blind orders in the rain, and somewhere in the chaos a voice—the flat, carrying voice I'd heard give the king's order—cut through:

"Let them go!"

Not at me. At his own men. Soren Vale, standing in the downpour with his torchless, useless formation, shouting *let them go*—and whether it was confusion or cowardice or something else entirely, the line broke around us, and we ran.

We ran through the fish-reeking dark of the gutting alleys, through the tannery lane where the rain made the walls bleed brown, up the switchback stairs past the tanner's drying racks, until we reached the old salt warehouse by the north wall where Marta kept her winter stores and where, I prayed, nobody would think to look for runaway children and a storm-caller who had just signed her own name across the entire sky.

Because that was the truth of it, sitting in the dark among barrels of salt herring with three shaking children and the rain still hammering the roof: I had revealed myself. Fully. Completely. There was no un-ringing that bell. Every gifted soul in Vellmark had felt that lightning and known it for kin. So had every gray cloak. So had the citadel. So had, I suspected with a coldness in my gut that had nothing to do with the wet, whatever waited for me in my dreams—the wolf-god who had been silent for twenty years and who had, three nights ago, begun to speak again, showing me broken stone and harvested souls and telling me the hunt was not a hunt at all but a harvest.

*Marta,* I thought, and the grief arrived then, late and total, folding me double. Somewhere behind us, in the ruins of the night market, they had taken her. Because I ran. Because she pushed me. Because being counted was the last brave thing an old woman with bad knees could do, and she'd done it so I wouldn't have to.

Signe climbed into my lap without asking, the way children do, as if grief were furniture and she had a right to sit on it. She was soaked and shivering and making small hiccupping sounds, and around us, faintly, impossibly, the rain began to fall softer. Not stopping. Just... gentling. The way it does when she's near.

"You made the thunder," she whispered.

"Yes."

"It was loud."

"Yes."

"Good," said Signe, with the absolute moral clarity of a four-year-old, and put her thumb in her mouth.

Petra was crying silently, the way older children learn to. Anders was staring at his hands like they belonged to a stranger. And outside, the storm I'd called was still circling the city, refusing to disperse, pressing against the walls like a living thing that had been let out after twenty years and had no intention of going back in its cage.

Good, I thought. Stay angry. Stay loud.

Because I was done being small. Marta had bought my running with her freedom, and I intended to spend it. There was a covenant broken, a god whispering, a harvest underway—and now, apparently, three children, a warehouse of salt herring, and a fugitive storm-caller with nothing left to lose and a whole sky full of ammunition.

Somewhere out in the rain, boots were regrouping. Somewhere in the citadel, my name was being written into a column. And somewhere north, past the moorlands, past the Wolf Reaches, something old was waiting for me to stop hiding.

I held the children closer and listened to the rain gentling, and made myself a promise over the smell of salt and herring and smoke:

Next time, I wouldn't run first.
