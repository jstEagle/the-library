## 6. The Inquisitor's Choice

There is a particular kind of silence that follows an order you intend to disobey. I had never heard it before that night in the Vellmark garrison yard, and I will hear it for the rest of my life: the sound of my own conscience clearing its throat.

"Vale." Captain Ilse Brandt stood beside me on the flagstones, her breath steaming in the cold, her face lit orange by the torches. "You'll want to watch this. You taught half of them."

Below us, in the yard, six prisoners knelt in a row. The gifted of Vellmark, or what remained of them after the night market burned. A fishwife whose hands could still water. Two brothers who could light lamps with a touch. An old man who mended broken bone. And two children — children, and I use the word knowing what it does to you, because it did it to me — a girl of perhaps ten and a boy younger, both taken from the ash-choked stalls with frost still melting off their sleeves.

The girl was the one from Yara's shop. I knew her drawing when I saw it pinned to the evidence board: wolves, crayoned in blue, three of them, running under a scribbled storm. She had drawn them the way children draw things they love.

"The writ says summary execution," Ilse said. "Rebellion against the crown's lawful purge. His Majesty's mercy is not extended to traitors to the natural order."

I want you to appreciate the language there. *Traitors to the natural order.* Somewhere in the Chancellery a very well-paid man had spent an afternoon finding a way to make murdering children sound like gardening.

"I know the writ," I said. "I drafted its precedent. Four years ago. The Harrowfield case."

"You did," Ilse said, and there was something almost gentle in how she said it, which was worse than cruelty. "You were proud of it. You told me law was the only magic worth having."

I had said that. I had believed it the way you believe a floor will hold you. And now here I stood on the floor, listening to it creak.

* * *

Let me tell you how an inquisitor's certainty dies, because it does not die dramatically. It dies the way a candle gutters — small failures, one after another, each one survivable on its own.

First failure: the frost on the windowpanes. I traced it to a charm-shop on Coppersmith Lane and found not a hidden archmage but a tired shopkeeper selling love-knots and luck-brass, and a child's drawing of wolves. I wrote in my report that the trail went cold. It did not go cold. I let it go cold. That was the first lie, and I have since learned that every lie after it is just the first one wearing different clothes.

Second failure: the holding cells beneath the garrison. Prisoners brought in from the purge were not held. They were *processed*. I saw the ledgers. Intake numbers, then transfer numbers, then nothing — no trial dates, no execution records, no graves. Wagons leaving at night under canvas, heading south toward the capital, and the drivers paid triple to ask no questions. I told myself it was transport to proper facilities. I told myself a great many things. Self-deception, I have come to understand, is simply bureaucracy performed upon the soul.

Third failure came tonight, when the old man who mended bones looked up at me from his knees and said, not accusingly, which was the unbearable part: "Inquisitor. I set the leg of your sergeant's son last winter. No charge. I'd have done it for you too."

He wasn't begging. He was reminding me of who I was supposed to be. That is the cruelest thing a condemned man can do.

* * *

The officer commanding the firing detail raised his hand. The six knelt straighter. The little girl began, very quietly, to hum — a lullaby, I realized, some scrap of song she was giving herself because no one else would give her anything.

And I heard myself say: "Hold."

The word came out of me like something escaping a cage. The officer turned. Ilse turned. The whole yard turned, forty faces, torchlight, frost forming on my own breath though I have never been gifted in my life — unless you count the gift of taking too long to become decent.

"Inquisitor Vale?" the officer said carefully. Men are always careful with inquisitors. It is one of the few advantages of being feared; it buys you perhaps four seconds before someone remembers they outnumber you.

"The writ requires confirmation of identity against the purge rolls," I said, and my voice did that thing voices do when they belong to someone acting bravely against their own nature — it went flat and official, a mask made of paperwork. "These detainees were processed without verification. If we execute the wrong names, the Crown's records fail, and the Chancellor will have all our heads. I'll take custody for verification. Tonight. In the secure wing."

It was nonsense. Every man in that yard probably knew it was nonsense. But nonsense delivered with confidence by a man in an inquisitor's coat is a powerful spell, and I had cast versions of it for years. The officer hesitated. Ilse looked at me for a long moment — and I will spend the rest of my life wondering what she saw, and whether she chose not to see it.

"Verification," she repeated.

"Procedure," I said. "The one shield between the Crown's justice and mere violence. Isn't that what we're for?"

Something moved behind her eyes. "Aye," she said finally. "That's what we're for." And she gave the order to stand down, and I took six prisoners into custody, and nobody — not then — understood that I had just committed treason with a signature and a clipboard.

This is the secret they don't put in the histories: revolutions rarely begin with swords. They begin with a clerk deciding a form has been filled out incorrectly.

* * *

Getting six people out of a garrison is easier than you'd think and harder than you'd hope. Easier, because everyone trusts procedure; I walked them out the sally gate past two sentries who saluted me. Harder, because the fishwife couldn't walk fast, the old man was shaking too badly to speak, and the children kept looking at me the way livestock look at the person leading them up the ramp.

At the drainage culvert beyond the wall — I had chosen it years ago during a security audit, another gift of my thoroughness — I stopped and faced them.

"I'm freeing you," I said. "Go south, scatter, change your names, bury whatever it is you do so deep you forget it yourself. Never sell charms. Never frost a windowpane. Live."

The fishwife spat at my boots. "Now he tells us to hide. Twenty years we hid. Where was the great inquisitor then?"

"Nowhere good," I said. "I won't insult you by apologizing. Apologies are for people who intend to be better, and I don't know yet if I am."

"Then why?" the old man asked.

Because of a child's drawing of wolves. Because of wagons leaving at night. Because I had built my life on the idea that law was the only honest magic, and tonight I had seen what the law was being used to conjure. But those reasons belonged to me, and these people owed me nothing, least of all my confession.

"Because it's wrong," I said. "That's all I have."

They went. All but the girl, who paused at the culvert mouth and looked back at me with the terrible directness of children.

"You're the wolf man," she said. "Yara has your paper."

"I don't—"

"She keeps it under the counter. She looks at it when she thinks nobody's watching." Then she was gone into the dark, and I stood alone in a drainage ditch understanding that somewhere across this city, the person I had failed to arrest was the only person left who might understand what I had just done.

* * *

Finding the ruins of the night market was not difficult. Finding Yara Eriksen within them nearly killed us both.

The market had burned through the small hours and now lay steaming under a gray dawn, awning-frames like ribs, the smell of char and melted luck-brass hanging over everything. I picked my way down the main aisle past the stall where I had once bought — God help me — a charm against unfaithful lovers, back when I was young and foolish enough to need one.

She was kneeling in the wreckage of a neighbor's stall, and she was bleeding from a long cut along her scalp, and she was trying to lift a beam off something underneath it.

"Don't," I said.

She didn't startle. That was the first thing I noticed. Most people, surprised by an inquisitor in the ruins of their life, jump. Yara Eriksen went very still instead, the way animals do, and then turned with a piece of broken awning-pole gripped like a spear.

"You," they said. Their voice was wrecked — smoke, grief, sleeplessness. "The wolf man comes to finish the job?"

"I freed your neighbors' children tonight. Six of them. Out of the garrison."

The pole lowered an inch. "Why should I believe that?"

"Because you're bleeding, and I've carried a field kit for fifteen years and never once used it on anyone who deserved it. Let me start."

We regarded each other across the rubble. Here is what I saw: a person who had spent twenty years being small on purpose, now standing in the ashes of the community they had protected, with blood drying black on their temple and a storm still living somewhere behind their eyes. Here is what they saw, presumably: the instrument of everything that had just happened, offering bandages.

"You lied in your report," Yara said. "About the frost. About me."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because arresting you felt wrong and I hadn't yet learned to trust that feeling." I crouched, slowly, showing empty hands. "Yesterday I would have called that weakness. I'm revising my definitions."

"They took Marta." The name came out of them like a tooth pulled. "My friend. Forty years she sold bread next to my shop. They took her to the wagons. I called a storm to save three children and I couldn't—" The pole trembled. "I could have saved her too. I chose the children. I'll choose it again. But I will feel it until I die."

"I know," I said. "I know exactly what that arithmetic costs. I did it tonight with six lives against my oath."

"That's not the same."

"No," I admitted. "Mine cost less. My oath was already rotten. Yours was love."

For a moment neither of us moved. Dawn came up properly, gray-gold, indifferent, lighting the ruin around us — and lighting, too, the poster pasted to a surviving wall, the King's proclamation curling at its edges: *THE GIFTED WILL BE FOUND. THE GIFTED WILL BE ANSWERED.* Answered. As though magic were a question and fire the reply.

Then Yara swayed, and I caught their arm, and they were too exhausted to fight me about the bandage. I cleaned the scalp wound with hands steadier than they had any right to be. Neither of us spoke. There is a shame that wants company and a shame that can't bear it, and we were carrying both, and the silence held them together.

"The road north," Yara said at last, quietly, to the bandage rather than to me. "To the Wolf Reaches. Everyone they've taken is going south, which means everything true is north. The covenant. Fenris. Whatever's left."

"You believe the god will help you?"

"I believe the god owes me twenty years of silence and I intend to collect." They looked up at me then, and their eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely clear. "Here's the arrangement, inquisitor. I don't trust you. Trusting you would require believing a man can serve the machine for fifteen years and step off it with clean hands, and I've watched too many good people get ground up to believe in clean hands. But I need someone who knows the Crown's roads, its garrisons, its mind. And you—" their mouth twisted, "—you need someone who knows what you're now running from, because you clearly haven't thought it through. So. North. Together. Until one of us decides the other is a liability."

"And then?"

"Then the moor is wide and the weather is mine."

It was, I reflected, the most honest offer anyone had made me in years. No friendship. No forgiveness. Just mutual utility with a threat attached. I had negotiated treaties with less sincerity.

"I accept," I said. "One condition."

Their jaw tightened. "Name it."

"When you decide I'm a liability — tell me to my face first. Give me an hour's warning. I've spent my career being the thing people flee in the night. I'd like, just once, to see it coming."

Something flickered across their face — not warmth, nothing so generous, but recognition, maybe. One survivor acknowledging another's terms.

"An hour," Yara said. "If the weather allows it."

* * *

We left Vellmark by the drover's gate as the bells rang prime, two figures among many — a city waking to ash and proclamation, neighbors already eyeing one another across fences, wondering who frost had touched, who had been taken in the night, who would be next. Behind us the smoke still stood over the market district like a second spire.

Ahead, the north road ran out through the fields toward the moorlands, and the wind came down it from the Wolf Reaches carrying pine and distance and something older than either.

Yara walked half a pace ahead of me and did not look back. I looked back once — at the towers, at the smoke, at the life I had dismantled with a clipboard and a lie — and then I made myself stop.

"One hour's warning," I reminded them.

"If the weather allows it," Yara said again, and for the first time since the burning, something that was almost — almost — a smile crossed their ruined face. "Pray for bad weather, inquisitor. It might be the only mercy you get from me."

Behind us, far behind, a horn sounded from the garrison walls. Someone had found the empty cells. Someone had found my signature on the transfer ledger, and understood.

The wind from the north rose to meet us, and we walked into it together, strangers bound by shared shame, while somewhere south a captain who had once been my friend read my name off a warrant and reached for her sword.
