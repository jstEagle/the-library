## 7. Northward, Against the Wind

*Yara*

The moorlands began where the road gave up pretending to be useful.

I had always imagined exile as a door closing. It turned out to be a door opening onto three hundred miles of wet grass, wind that treated your face as a whetstone, and a disgraced inquisitor who complained about his boots like a man whose boots had personally wronged him.

"Again," Soren said, from somewhere behind me.

"You tied them wrong."

"I have tied these laces for eleven years."

"And for eleven years you've been walking on cobblestones. The moor is a different teacher." I didn't turn around. Turning around meant looking at him, and looking at him meant remembering that he'd freed the children, burned his own life down, and then stood in the ash of the night market holding out a hand to me like we were old friends instead of hunter and prey. Some things were easier to forgive at a distance of ten paces and worsening weather.

He caught up anyway. He always caught up. There was something muleish about the man beneath all that court polish — a stubbornness I recognized, because I'd been wearing my own like armor for twenty years.

"The Wolf Reaches," he said, squinting north at a horizon the color of an old bruise. "How far?"

"If the weather's kind? Nine days."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then the weather will teach you to count differently."

He made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter hadn't been confiscated along with his rank. We walked. The wind walked with us, shoving, testing, the way it always did at the edge of the wild places — as if the land itself wanted to know what business we had crossing it.

I let it test him first.

* * *

*Soren*

Day two, she stopped speaking to me entirely, which I gathered was either punishment or curriculum.

I learned later it was both.

We had made camp in the lee of a stone outcrop that some ancient hand had carved into the shape of a seated figure, worn now to suggestion — a shoulder here, a folded knee there. Yara sat on its lap as though it were furniture, which struck me as either profound irreverence or deep familiarity, and built a fire small enough to hide in a cupped palm.

"Tomorrow," she said, "you learn weather."

"I beg your pardon?"

"You heard me. You walk like a man reading a warrant. Head down, eyes on the ground, waiting for the world to present itself for inspection. Out here the world doesn't present itself. It announces. And if you can't hear the announcement, you die stupid, which is the worst way."

"I was an inquisitor," I said. "I am trained to notice things."

"You're trained to notice things people are trying to hide. Weather doesn't hide. That's why you're blind to it." She threw me something — a strip of dried fish, hard as a legal writ. "Eat. Dawn lesson. Sleep."

I ate. The fish tasted of salt and spite. Somewhere out in the dark, a curlew called, long and descending, and I found myself listening to it the way I used to listen to testimony — hunting for the lie in it, the thing said underneath the thing said.

There was no lie. That was the strange part. Out here, nothing lied. I had spent my whole career in a city built of lies stacked into architecture, and it took the moorlands two days to show me what honesty sounded like: wind, bird, grass, rain coming.

Rain came at dawn. Of course it did. She'd known it would.

*Yara*

He was hopeless, is the honest answer. Hopeless in the specific way of clever men who think noticing is a talent rather than a practice.

"Look west," I told him, standing in the grey smear of morning. "What do you see?"

"Hills."

"Look again."

"Hills. Fog. A sheep."

"The sheep counts. Sheep know things. But look above the fog line — see how the cloud has gone flat? Like a lid pressed down? This morning it was piled up, lazy, full of itself. Now it's organized. Organized clouds mean organized wind means weather with intent. By midday the wind swings to the northwest, and by evening we'll want walls. So we make distance while it's dry, and we don't complain about the mud, because the mud is the price of the warning."

He stared at the clouds for a long moment. Then, quietly, in the voice of a man setting down something heavy: "In Vellmark, we paid informants for less than this. You're telling me the sky reports for free."

"The sky has no agenda. That unsettles you, doesn't it?"

"It does," he admitted, and I'll grant him this: he didn't pretend otherwise. Most men would have. Most men would have dressed the admission up in wit until you couldn't find it anymore. Soren Vale laid his failings out on the ground like tools and looked at them plainly, and I hated how much I respected it, because respect was a currency I couldn't afford to spend on him.

Not yet. Maybe not ever. But I taught him the clouds anyway.

* * *

*Soren*

By the fourth day I could smell rain an hour before it arrived, which felt less like learning and more like remembering something my body had always known and my profession had trained out of me. Yara said this was the point. "Everyone's born knowing weather," she said. "Cities just talk you out of it. They talk you out of most things."

She talked, those days, more than I expected her to. Not about herself — about everything else. Which sheep could be trusted as prophets (all of them) and which stones held warmth past sundown (the ones with lichen on the north face, counterintuitively). She narrated the moor like a woman introducing guests at a party, and I understood, gradually, that this was not chatter. It was inventory. She was counting what the land still had left, the way a merchant counts stock after a fire.

In exchange, I gave her the court. It seemed only fair, and besides — she'd earn it eventually through interrogation if I didn't volunteer it, and Yara Eriksen interrogating anyone was a prospect I wished on no one.

"The king," I said one evening, watching her feed our fire with the economy of someone who has been cold before, "has not aged visibly in thirty years. Courtiers call it piety. 'The gods preserve him.' The truth is a cellar under the palace and a guild of distillers who work with materials the rest of us are taught to report."

Her hands went still over the flames. Just for a breath. Then they moved again.

"Materials," she repeated. "You mean people."

"I mean I didn't ask, when I was rising. Asking questions in that cellar is how inquisitors stop rising." I turned my hands toward the heat. "I'm telling you because you deserve to know what's behind us. The hunt isn't zealotry. Zealotry burns hot and dies fast. This is bookkeeping. Every gifted person taken is an entry in a ledger, and the ledger serves the king's continued existence. Ilse isn't chasing us out of loyalty alone. She's chasing us because we're the first entries that got away."

Yara was quiet a long time. The fire popped. Far off, the curlew again, or its cousin.

"My mother told me stories about the old kings," she said finally. "They ruled by covenant. Give the wild its due, and it holds up your roof. Break the covenant, and you spend forever patching the roof yourself." She looked at me across the flames, and there was frost in her eyes even though her eyes were brown. "Your king has been patching with stolen years. That's not rule. That's infestation."

I had served that crown for nineteen years. I had no defense prepared. For the first time I understood that I wasn't going to find one, and that some part of me had stopped looking since the night market, maybe since the child's drawing of wolves, maybe since the beginning, and had simply lacked the courage to announce it.

"They'll send Ilse," I said. "If they haven't already."

"You speak of her like a storm front."

"She was my protégé. Best I ever trained. Better than me, honestly — she kept the parts of the work that mattered and shed the parts that rotted, or I believed she did." I fed the fire a stick. "She'll read our trail the way you read clouds. Flat, organized, full of intent."

"Then we'd better be weather worth chasing," Yara said, and lay down to sleep with her back to me, and I sat up the last watch listening to the moor breathe, and thinking about the difference between loyalty and habit, and how much of my life had been the second thing wearing the coat of the first.

* * *

*Yara*

On the sixth day the moor turned against us, and I discovered that Soren Vale, when soaked to the bone and miserable, became almost likable, which was inconvenient for both of us.

The storm came off the western fells at dusk — not one of mine, a wild one, unclaimed, all bluster and sideways rain. We sheltered in a hollow beneath a fallen waymarker, an old covenant-road stone lying face-down in the peat like a drunk god. He sat pressed against the granite, dripping, and said, with tremendous dignity:

"I have decided I hate the moorlands."

"Noted. Filed. The moorlands remain indifferent."

"There's a theme developing in my exile." He pushed wet hair off his forehead. In the grey light he looked younger without the inquisitor's coat, or perhaps just unfinished — a man mid-erasure. "Can you do anything about this? You calm storms for a living."

"For a living, yes. For free, rarely." But I reached out anyway, gently, the way you'd steady a frightened animal — not taking hold of the storm, just leaning my attention against its panic until it remembered it had somewhere else to be. The rain slackened to a drizzle. The thunder rolled off eastward, sulking.

Soren watched me do it with an expression I couldn't name at the time. Later I'd have called it hunger, but not the king's kind. The other kind. The kind that wants to understand.

"That cost you something," he said. "I've watched you do it four times now. Each time, afterward, your hands are cold for an hour."

"You watch too closely for a man who claims to be reformed."

"It's the job that won't leave." He flexed his fingers toward the little fire. "Where did you learn it? Your mother?"

The question sat between us. Twenty years of silence around it, and here was this man, six days out from burning his own life, asking after my mother like it was a reasonable thing to ask.

"Aye," I said. "My mother. Before the Reach closed." I pulled my knees up. "She was wolf-blooded. Half, maybe more. She crossed between worlds the way you cross a street. When the covenant broke — when *I* broke it — the border sealed, and she was on the wrong side of it. Or I was. Depends which side you think home is."

He didn't say anything. He had learned, somewhere in those hundred miles, when silence was the correct answer, and I hated that too, because it worked.

"We're going to the Reaches," I said, "to fix what I broke. You should know the fixing has a price. Everything out here does. The land keeps honest books."

"What price?"

"That's not mine to say yet." I met his eyes. "But when it is yours to say, you'll say it knowing I didn't hide it. Only delayed it. There's a difference, and I need you to believe there's a difference."

Something moved behind his face — the inquisitor, cataloguing; the man, deciding whether to trust the catalogue. "Delayed," he agreed. "Not hidden." And he let it lie, and I slept better than I had any right to, in a hollow, in the rain, next to the enemy the crown had sent to kill me, who was currently boiling snowmelt for tea because he'd noticed, unprompted, that I was cold.

Wary respect, Ravn would have called it later, laughing. I called it trouble. Both were right.

* * *

*Soren*

On the eighth day, I found the sign, and my stomach dropped the way it does when a case turns.

A cairn on the ridge crest, freshly rebuilt. Under its capstone, tucked with deliberate care: a scrap of blue wax, stamped with the crown's seal, broken cleanly in half. A message marker. Inquisitors' field code — I had invented half of it myself, teaching Ilse Brandt on her first commission, ten years ago, her ink-stained fingers copying my cipher into her notebook twice so she wouldn't lose it.

Half a seal meant: *target sighted, direction confirmed, awaiting reinforcement.*

The other half would be with her.

"How far behind us?" Yara asked. She'd read my face before I'd finished reading the cairn. I was becoming transparent to her, and I couldn't decide if that was safety or catastrophe.

"Two days. Perhaps less. She travels light and she doesn't sleep badly on the ground the way I do." I set the wax scrap back exactly as I'd found it. Destroying it would tell her we'd found it. "She'll have soldiers. Six, eight. Enough."

"Enough to take us?"

"Enough to try." I looked north, where the moor climbed toward a distant line of dark hills that smelled, even from here, faintly of pine and older things. "There's a river crossing ahead. The ford at Hrafnswater. If she means to catch us, it's there — open ground, one crossing, nowhere to scatter."

Yara was quiet. Wind moved through the moor grass in long silver strokes, and I watched her watch the horizon, and I realized she was doing to the weather what I had done to the wax seal: reading intent.

"Good," she said softly.

"Good?"

"Open ground. One crossing." She turned to me, and there was frost on her breath, though the air wasn't cold enough for it, and her eyes had gone bright and terrible and alive. "Then we won't have to guess where she'll be, Soren. Let her come to the ford. Let her bring her eight soldiers and her loyalty and her conscience, and we'll see which of them weighs most when the water rises."

I had trained Captain Ilse Brandt myself. I knew exactly how good she was.

Standing on that ridge, beside a storm-caller smiling at the northern sky, I understood that I was about to find out — and that whatever happened at the ford, none of us would come away from it unchanged.

Behind us, far off, a hawk cried once and fell silent.

The wind swung to the northwest, and began to gather.
