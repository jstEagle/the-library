## 10. Ilse at the Ford

### Ilse

The river was called the Aldren, which meant *old one* in the tongue of people who had died out so long ago that nobody even bothered to romanticize them anymore. I learned that from Soren Vale, years ago, on the long ride south after the Kettering affair, when he'd talked to fill the silence and I'd pretended not to enjoy it. He knew things like that. Useless, beautiful things. The names of dead rivers. The proper way to address a wolf-kin envoy. How to make a frightened child laugh with a coin behind its ear.

I thought about that last one as I sat my horse above the ford and watched the water run silver under a sky the color of an old bruise.

Three fugitives, according to the scouts. Two gifted, one a child of perhaps ten whose crime was making flowers bloom out of season in a cellar in Vellmark. And Soren Vale himself, traitor, oath-breaker, the man who had taught me everything I knew about the difference between justice and obedience — and then, apparently, decided to demonstrate it.

"You're certain it's him?" asked Lieutenant Corren, beside me. Twenty-three, ambitious, the kind of young man who sharpened his convictions like knives because he hadn't yet learned they could cut the hand that held them.

"I'd know his horse anywhere," I said. "He spoils it shamefully. Feeds it apples from his own hand."

Corren frowned, as though this were evidence of moral decay. Perhaps it was. Perhaps everything about Soren had always been evidence of moral decay, and I'd been too busy admiring the man to read the charges.

Forty soldiers. Crossbows. A narrow ford with water up to a horse's hocks and nowhere to run except north, into the moorlands, into the Reaches where even the crown's writ went to die. It should have been simple. It *was* simple, on paper. Inquisitor gone rogue, traveling with the storm-caller who'd burned half the night market district. Take them alive for questioning. The king wanted them alive.

The king wanted everyone alive, these days. That was the part that kept me awake. You don't keep prisoners alive for questioning forever. The wagons going back down the king's road didn't carry prisoners. They carried something else — I'd seen the crates being loaded once, before dawn, and the men loading them wouldn't meet anyone's eyes, and there had been a smell from inside the sealed boxes that I still couldn't put a name to. Not blood. Something sweeter. Something wrong.

"Captain?" Corren said.

"They'll come down to the ford within the hour," I said. "We hold position. Nobody fires without my order."

"And if the traitor resists?"

I looked at this boy with his clean face and his untested certainty, and I lied to him the way the whole kingdom had been lying to itself for twenty years.

"Then we do what we must," I said.

* * *

### Soren

There is an art to knowing when you've been caught, and I confess I've never mastered it. Yara spotted the ambush before I did — of course they did; you don't spend twenty years hiding a storm inside your chest without learning to feel the pressure drop — and by the time I saw the glint of steel among the birches on the far ridge, they already had the children behind the rocks and a hand raised toward the sky as if hailing an old friend.

"How many?" I asked.

"Fifty," Yara said. "Maybe sixty. And Brandt."

"You can tell that from here?"

"I can tell her horse from here." Yara's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "It has an attitude."

The children — Mira, ten, flower-hander; the twins Doss and Pellin, twelve, who could warm stone with their palms — looked up at me with the particular trust of the young who have decided, against all evidence, that adults know what they're doing. I had spent eleven years as an inquisitor cultivating exactly that expression in the faces of the guilty. Life has a sense of humor. It's just not a very kind one.

"We could run north," Yara said. "Around the ridge. Lose them in the moorland."

"And if they send riders ahead? If we scatter, they take the children one at a time." I dismounted, which surprised everyone including me. My knee made its opinion known. "No. We talk."

"Talk," Yara repeated, in the tone of someone tasting a dish that had poisoned them before.

"She was my friend," I said. "Before she was anything else. Before I was anything else. Let me try."

Yara studied me for a long moment. Wind moved through the birches above us, restless, carrying the smell of rain that wasn't falling yet but was thinking very seriously about it. When Yara finally spoke, their voice was quiet.

"You'll stand between them and us?"

"If it comes to that."

"It will come to that." They looked past me, at the ford, at the forty shapes resolving out of the tree line. "Soren. Whatever happens — whatever you decide — don't let them have the children. Promise me."

"I promise."

And then, because I am apparently incapable of leaving well enough alone: "You could end this now, you know. One word and the sky comes down. I've seen what you can do."

"So have they," Yara said. "That's why there are sixty of them instead of six. And that's why I won't. Not first." They turned away, toward the children. "Not while there's still a version of today where nobody bleeds."

I walked down to the water alone.

* * *

### Ilse

He came down to the ford with his hands open and his sword left behind, which was either the bravest thing I'd ever seen or the most insulting, and knowing Soren Vale it was both at once.

He stopped at the near edge of the shallows, where the water broke white around his boots, and he looked up at me on the ridge the way he used to look at me across the training yard — patient, faintly amused, waiting for me to arrive at the conclusion he'd reached three moves ago.

"Soren Vale," I called, because protocol is a comfort even when it's a farce. "You are charged with treason, desertion, and the freeing of condemned prisoners. Surrender yourself and your companions will be granted—"

"Don't finish that sentence, Ilse." His voice carried across the water easily. He'd never needed to shout. That was the thing about Soren; he simply assumed the world would lean in to listen, and the world usually did. "You've seen what happens to prisoners now. Don't stand there and offer them mercy you don't believe exists."

Corren shifted beside me, hand drifting toward his crossbow. I put my arm out, casual, a captain merely adjusting his stance. The bolt stayed sheathed.

"You could have just run," I said. "North. Gone over the border. Why stop? Why give yourself up?"

"Because you'd have chased us into the moorland, and someone would have panicked, and children die quickly when soldiers panic." He spread his hands wider. Water dripped from his cuffs. "So here's my offer, Captain Brandt, and I'll only make it once. Me. Alive, unresisting, delivered to Vellmark for whatever the king wants done with me. In exchange, the others walk north unmolested. All three of them. Your word on it."

"The word of a traitor's captor," Corren sneered, too loudly. "Why should we honor any bargain with—"

"Because *I'm* asking it of her, not you, Lieutenant," Soren said, and something in his tone made the boy go pale and quiet, "and because Captain Brandt has never broken her word. Not once. Ask anyone who served with her. Ask me — I trained her."

"That's enough," I said.

"It isn't, though, is it?" He took a step deeper into the water. The current pushed at him, impatient. "That's the whole problem, Ilse. Nothing is ever enough anymore. Do you remember Kettering? The miller's son with the fire-hands? You argued for three hours that he deserved trial by evidence, not summary judgment. Three hours, in the rain, and you won. We sent him to a work farm instead of the block. Do you remember what happened to him?"

I remembered. Everyone in the service remembered, eventually, in the way you remember things you wish you didn't. The work farm at Hollowmere. The wagons. The sweet wrong smell.

"He was transferred," I said.

"He disappeared," Soren said. "They all disappear. And you've started checking the manifests, haven't you? Don't bother denying it — you have the same look I had, two months ago. The look of a person doing arithmetic they hate."

My hand was steady on my reins. I want that recorded somewhere, if anything I say is recorded: my hand was steady. Inside, something was doing exactly the arithmetic he described, and coming up with sums that had no honest answer.

"Take him," I heard myself say. "Bind him. The other three hold position until we've secured—"

And that was when the sky changed its mind.

* * *

### Yara

I had promised myself I wouldn't strike first.

I want to be clear about this, because history — if there is any history, if anyone writes any of this down besides inquisitors and kings — will want to paint it otherwise. I stood behind those rocks with three children pressed against me and a storm coiled in my ribs like a second heartbeat, and I let Soren walk into the water alone, and I let Ilse Brandt give her order, and I waited. I waited because Soren believed she could be reasoned with, and because I loved him — yes, fine, loved, past tense is cowardice — and love makes you honor stupid promises.

But then a soldier on the far ridge got nervous. There's always one. Some boy with cold hands and a crossbow and orders he doesn't understand aimed at a woman standing in a river, and his finger tightened, and the bolt went wide — wide of Soren, wide of everything, except it wasn't wide of the rocks where the children hid, because bolts don't care about intent.

It struck the stone a hand's width from Doss's head.

And I stopped waiting.

I didn't call the storm. I want that understood too. Calling implies distance, deliberation, a summoner and a summoned. This was closer than that. This was opening a door in my own chest and letting twenty years of held breath through.

The pressure dropped so fast that every soldier's ears popped. The light went green-gray, old and strange, the color of weather from before the covenant broke. Lightning came down the length of the river valley — not striking anything, not yet, just walking along the water like something pacing a cage, showing its size. The Aldren rose between one breath and the next, swollen with rain that fell only on the hills upstream, and the ford became a brown roaring argument.

Horses screamed. Soldiers scattered back into the treeline, and I gave them the courtesy of letting them scatter. I split the lightning deliberately — deliberately, hear me — into four forks that struck the earth at the corners of the ford, close enough to singe the grass, far enough to kill no one. A message written in the oldest language there is:

*Go.*

Ilse Brandt didn't run. I'll give her that until the day I die, whichever death I get. She sat her terrified horse in the middle of that chaos, rain hammering her, and she stared across the flood directly at where I stood, because of course she'd worked out where I was — she was Soren's student, after all — and she mouthed something at me through the downpour.

I couldn't hear it. But I saw it. Two words, shaped slow and deliberate.

*Take him.*

Then she wheeled her horse around, shouted orders I couldn't distinguish from thunder, and the army of the crown retreated up the ridge like a tide going out — all except one rider who lingered, looking back, before vanishing into the trees.

The storm and I stood down together, breathing hard.

* * *

### Soren

The water had knocked me flat and nearly taken me with it; I came up coughing half the Aldren and found Yara wading in to haul me out by the collar like a mother cat retrieving a particularly disappointing kitten.

"You waited," they said.

"I promised."

"A crossbow bolt hit the rock three feet from a child's head, Soren."

"And you still didn't kill anyone." I spat river. "Sixty soldiers, Yara. A lesser storm-caller would have buried the ford in bodies and called it survival. You scared them off instead. Don't tell me that was easy."

"It wasn't," they admitted, and their hands were shaking now that the storm had gone, shaking the way they always did afterward, and I held onto them in the shallows and they let me, and for a moment the river roared around us and neither of us said anything true or useful.

Then Ilse's words caught up with me. Yara told me what she'd mouthed. *Take him.* And I stood in the wreckage of the crossing, soaked and half-drowned, and laughed until it hurt, because of course. Of course she'd seen it. She'd watched me offer myself and she'd watched the sky answer for me, and she'd understood, in one flash of green lightning, the arithmetic I'd been trying to force on her for twenty minutes.

She'd let us go. And she'd told me, in the only language left to us, that the debt was noted. That next time, there might not be a ford and a storm and a moment of grace.

"She'll report it as a rout," I said. "Weather-driven. No dishonor in retreating from a storm. It buys us days."

"And after the days?"

"After the days," I said, "she comes herself. With more than sixty. Or—" I hesitated, because saying it aloud made it real, "—or she doesn't come at all. Because she's started reading the manifests too."

Yara looked at me sharply. "What manifests?"

"The ones from Hollowmere." I wrung out my sleeve, buying time, an old habit. "The wagons that leave Vellmark full of prisoners and arrive empty. Ilse knows something's wrong. Maybe she doesn't know *what*, but she knows the shape of it, the way you know the shape of a storm before it breaks." I looked north, where the moorland rose dark against the failing light, and beyond it the mountains, and beyond them the Reaches, where wolves sang to a god who had been silent for twenty years. "And I think, Yara, that when she works out what the king is really doing with the gifted — when she does that arithmetic all the way to the bottom — she's going to have to choose. Same choice you forced on me on the road out of Vellmark. Conscience or crown."

"You think she'll choose right?"

I thought about a girl in a rainy training yard arguing three hours for a miller's son's life. I thought about a woman on a hill refusing to run from a storm.

"I think she already wants to," I said. "Which is more dangerous than either option, and better than most."

Behind us, the twins were teaching Mira to skip stones across the flooded ford — hopeless, joyful, the water swallowing every stone whole — and the light was going, and the north was waiting, and somewhere behind us a friend was riding back toward a king made hollow, carrying questions that would eat her alive until she followed us.

We gathered the children. We crossed the Aldren at dusk, walking, because the horses were spent and because after the day we'd had, it felt right to arrive at the far bank on our own feet.

None of us looked back. But all of us listened — for hoofbeats, for thunder, for the sound of a decision being made somewhere in the dark behind us.

It didn't come that night.

It came three days later, in the form of a single rider on the northern road, carrying no banner, wearing no colors, with the king's seal torn from her shoulder and a look on her face that meant the arithmetic was finished.

Ilse Brandt rode toward us out of the south, alone, at a gallop.

And behind her, on the horizon, the sky over Vellmark burned the color of a drained wound.
