## 11. The King's Hollow Court

I have knelt in the throne room of Vellmark perhaps four hundred times in my life. I could do it blindfolded, which is fortunate, because for most of those years I was kneeling to a man I had never actually seen.

That sounds like madness, so let me be precise. I had seen King Aldric Vane. I had seen him at coronation anniversaries, at the Feast of the Long Peace, at executions—his favorite venue, though he never attended in person anymore, preferring to watch through that strange smoked-glass window above the scaffold. I had seen his face on coins and proclamations and the enormous painted portrait that hangs in the Hall of Records, forty feet of oiled canvas depicting a man in his prime with a jaw like a cliff face and eyes the color of a winter sky.

But the last time I stood in the same room as the living king and looked directly at him was eleven years ago, when I was promoted to full inquisitor. And even then, I remember thinking: *he is standing very still.*

I thought it was dignity. Kings are taught stillness the way soldiers are taught marching; it is a language of power. A man who does not fidget cannot be read. A man who does not blink cannot be flustered.

It took me twenty years and one burned night market to understand that he wasn't being dignified.

He was being *careful*. Because if he moved too fast, something inside him might slosh.

* * *

Let me go back further. Indulge me; we have a long road north and Yara is asleep by the fire, and there are things I need to say out loud even if only to the dark and the moorland wind, because I have been carrying them alone since I was nineteen years old, and a man can only carry so much before it starts carrying him.

When I entered the Inquisition's academy, they told us the Order existed to protect the realm from wild magic. Fire that ate villages. Plagues called up from spite. The old wars, before the Covenant, when storm-callers leveled cities and wolf-kin dragged whole garrisons into the hills. We were the wall between civilization and chaos. I believed it the way you believe in gravity—not because anyone proved it, but because everything around you behaves as if it were true.

My instructor was Master Corvus Hale, and he was the finest teacher I ever had, and I want that on the record before I tell you what he actually did.

In my third year, Hale took six of us—the top of our class—down into the undercrofts beneath the palace. Not the dungeons. Deeper. There are levels beneath the dungeons that don't appear on any plan of the palace, corridors older than the dynasty itself, and at the bottom of them, behind a door banded in lead and iron, there is a room that hums.

Not machinery. Not voices. It hums the way a struck bell hums after the sound has supposedly ended—a vibration you feel in your teeth, in the wet of your eyes. Sixteen-year-old me felt it and thought, without knowing why: *this place is empty and full at the same time.*

Inside were shelves. Hundreds of them, floor to ceiling, and on the shelves stood bottles.

They were beautiful. That's the thing I can't get past, even now, even after everything—they were *beautiful*. Glass blown in shapes that seemed to move when you weren't looking directly at them, colors that had no names: a green like the moment before lightning, a red like the inside of a closed eyelid. Some bottles held light that bent toward you when you approached, the way a plant bends toward the sun. Some held darkness that was somehow worse than ordinary darkness, a darkness with an appetite in it.

"What are they?" asked Ilse Brandt, who stood beside me then the way she has stood beside me all my life, until three days ago when she pointed a crossbow at me across a river ford, and even that, I think now, was a kind of loyalty.

"Distillate," said Master Hale. "Essence. Call it what you like." He lifted one bottle—a small one, amber, unremarkable except that my hand rose toward it without my permission, the way a drowning man's hand rises toward light—and turned it so the firelight caught it. "Every gifted soul carries magic the way a well carries water. Most wells run shallow. But some—" he smiled, and I want you to know that Hale's smile was the warmest thing in my young life, "—some run deep. And the crown has been bottling the deep ones for a very long time."

I remember laughing. Nervously, but laughing, because surely this was a lesson in why we fought magic—look how it tempts, look how it hoards itself in pretty glass—

"Who drinks them?" Ilse asked. Blunt as always. She was blunt before it nearly killed her, and I loved her for it, in the way you love a blade: grateful, and careful.

Hale set the bottle down very gently, the way you'd set down a sleeping child.

"The king," he said, "takes his medicine each morning at dawn. Has done for thirty-one years. Why do you imagine His Majesty has reigned longer than any monarch in recorded history? Why do you imagine he looks thirty years younger than his father did at the same age? Why do you imagine the harvests fail a little more each year, the rivers run thin, the storms come wrong?" He looked at each of us in turn, and his voice dropped into something almost tender. "Magic does not vanish, children. It goes somewhere. And everything the land gives up, the king takes in."

The humming in the room made sense then. It wasn't the bottles humming.

It was the *land*, outside, beyond the walls, beyond the city, the whole patient green world, humming like a struck bell because something had been taken from it that could not be given back.

* * *

Here is what they told us next, and here is where I should have walked out, and here is where I didn't.

They told us it was necessary. They told us the story of the Hollowing—the slow death of the world's wild magic, the covenant broken, the realms drifting apart, the land bleeding out its power into nothing. Someone had to catch what fell. Someone had to hold it safe. The king was not a thief; he was a *reservoir*. Every bottle on those shelves was a life saved from waste, a drop rescued from the flood.

And when the reservoir was full enough—when the king had gathered enough of the world's scattered soul—he would pour it back. All of it. He would restore the covenant himself, single-handedly, a monarch so great he would not merely rule the world but *mend* it.

It was a beautiful story. I have since learned that beautiful stories are the most reliable indicator of a lie. Ugly truths tend to come dressed plainly.

Ilse believed it completely. I could see it happen—watched conviction settle over her like armor being buckled on, plate by plate. She was nineteen and furious at the world for being broken, and here at last was a fix, a grand one, worth any cost.

And I—I want to be honest, since honesty is apparently the theme of my late-life crisis—I believed it too. For about four seconds. And then I asked the question that has ruined my life:

"Where do the bottles come from?"

Hale's smile stayed exactly where it was. That was the horror. Not that it vanished, but that it *didn't*. "From the harvest," he said. "Why do you think the Order exists, Soren? Why do you think we keep the registers? Every gifted soul in the realm is already written down. Name, village, age, depth of gift." He tapped my chest, once, lightly, with two fingers, right over the heart. "Yours is in that book. Depth: considerable. You should be proud."

"We don't take children," Ilse said quickly. "We'd never—"

"No," Hale agreed. "Never children." A pause, precisely measured, the pause of a man who has rehearsed this. "Not until they're grown. A distillate drawn from a child is thin. Weak. The essence needs decades to deepen." Another smile. "Patience is the crown's oldest virtue."

I did the arithmetic right there in the humming dark, standing among the beautiful bottles, and the arithmetic went like this: the king began drinking thirty-one years ago. The registers go back at least that far. Which means that somewhere in these undercrofts, or in palaces like it, or in bottles long since emptied, are the essences of every gifted man and woman the Order has "imprisoned" for three decades.

Except we don't imprison them. I knew that even then, in some cellar of myself I kept locked. I had processed prisoners. I had seen them taken through the deep corridors, past the humming door, and I had never once seen them come back, and I had filed the paperwork, and the paperwork said *relocated for rehabilitation*, and I had signed my name beneath it in my careful inquisitor's hand.

Thirty-one years of mornings. One bottle at dawn, sometimes two, in the later years—because tolerance builds, Hale told us, because the hollow grows, because appetite is the one thing that never stops deepening.

The king was not a reservoir waiting to give the magic back.

The king was a *throat*.

* * *

Now. Back to the present, such as it is: a shepherd's hut two days south of the Wolf Reaches, rain on the turf roof, a fire down to embers, and Yara Eriksen asleep sitting up against the wall with their arms crossed like a man guarding a gate even in dreams.

I watched them sleep for a while, which sounds sinister and is mostly just insomnia. And I found myself doing the arithmetic again, the new arithmetic, the one I've been doing since the night market burned.

Three gifted children escaped that fire because Yara tore the sky open to cover them. Three souls whose names were in the register. Whose "depth" had been assessed. Who were now, per crown doctrine, not fugitives but *spillage*—bottles that had gotten up and run.

And the hunt—the great realm-wide hunt, the criers, the rewards, the neighbor-turning-on-neighbor—isn't a purge. Purges end. You purge, and then it's over, and the criers go quiet. This hunt doesn't end. It *can't* end, because it isn't hunting heresy or treason or anything so finite. It's hunting supply. It will go on exactly as long as the king keeps breathing, and the king keeps breathing because of what the hunt brings him, and so the machine turns forever: the hunt feeds the throat, and the throat drives the hunt.

I served that machine for twenty years. I built half of it. The filing systems alone—I designed the cross-referencing that let the Order track a gifted child from birth-register to assessment to harvest. Somewhere in a vault in Vellmark there are thousands of pages with my signature on them, each one a sentence of relocation-for-rehabilitation, each one a lie I wrote in a beautiful hand.

Yara stirs. Opens one eye. Storm-grey, sharp even half-asleep.

"You're thinking loudly," they mutter. "It's like sleeping next to thunder about to make up its mind."

"Go back to sleep."

"No." They sit up fully, wince at whatever the fire did to their eyes, and look at me across the embers with that expression I've come to dread and rely on in equal measure—the one that says they already know what I'm going to say and are deciding whether to let me say it. "You've been circling something since the ford. Since Brandt. You talk in your sleep, inquisitor, did you know that? Fragments. Bottles. Dawn." Their voice drops. "What happens at dawn?"

And here is the thing about secrets: they are heavy, and I am tired, and the man I was—the man who knelt four hundred times to a king he never truly saw—that man died somewhere between the night market and the moorlands, and no one has informed his ghost.

So I tell them. All of it. The undercroft, the humming, the beautiful bottles, Hale's smile that never moved, the story of the reservoir, the arithmetic of the harvest. I tell them about the registers with my name in them and my name at the bottom of ten thousand others. I watch their face as I speak, and it does what faces do in stories like this: disbelief first, then the horrible smooth slide into belief, the way ice lets go of a bank all at once.

"The children," Yara says finally, very quietly. "The ones from the market. The ones they took." They don't finish it. They don't have to. We both know where the taken ones go, now. Down the deep corridors, past the humming door, into the dawn.

"Ilse knows," I say. "She's known since we were nineteen. She chose the story anyway, because the story was easier than the truth." I feed a stick to the embers. "But something happened to her. At the ford, when she let us go—there was a second there, when I offered my surrender, where she looked at me like a woman doing arithmetic of her own. Like she'd finally counted the bottles."

Yara is silent a long moment. Outside, the rain thickens, and I feel it the way I've learned to feel weather these past weeks—Yara's lessons, grudgingly given, freely absorbed—and there's something wrong in it. Something coming down from the north that isn't a front. It's too wide, too steady, too *purposeful*. Rain doesn't march. This marches.

Then Yara goes rigid, head tilting, listening to something I can't hear—or something I can, faintly, underneath the rain: a low vibration, felt in the teeth, in the wet of the eyes.

A hum. Familiar. Impossible, this far from the palace, and yet unmistakable—the hum of the undercroft, the hum of the struck bell, the hum of a land being emptied.

"That's not weather," Yara whispers. Their eyes find mine, and they are grey as a winter sky, and I realize with a lurch that I have seen that exact color before—in a forty-foot portrait in the Hall of Records, in the painted eyes of a king. "Soren. What's coming north?"

I know the answer before I say it. I've known since the ford, since Ilse's arithmetic-face, since the hum started under the rain. The king has run low. Thirty-one years of dawns, and the deep gifts grow rarer every year, the registers thinner, the harvest leaner—and there is only one place left in all the realm where magic still runs deep and wild and unbottled.

One place left to drain.

"He's coming for the Wolf Reaches," I say. "He's bringing the army. And he's bringing the machine."

Yara stands, and the firelight catches their face, and for the first time since I've known them they look less afraid than *furious*—a storm deciding which direction to break.

"Wake everyone," they say. "We're out of road."
