## 13. The Wolf Reaches

The border announced itself the way grief does—not all at once, but in a slow change of light.

For three days the moorlands had been thinning out beneath us, heather giving way to scrub, scrub to pine, and the wind had changed its accent. Southern wind is a gossip; it carries chimney smoke and bread and other people's arguments. This wind came down from the north smelling of nothing human at all—cold stone, wet fur, resin, and underneath it something older that I didn't have a name for until I did.

Old blood.

Not fresh. Not a slaughter. The kind of blood that has soaked into ground so long ago it's become part of the soil's memory, the way a wound becomes part of a body. I stopped on the ridge with Soren a half-step behind me and breathed it in, and twenty years fell away like a coat I'd been wearing wrong.

"There," I said, though there was nothing to see yet. Just trees going on forever, black-green and patient.

"What?" Soren asked. He'd learned by now not to doubt my weather-sense, but this wasn't weather. This was worse than weather. Weather only kills you.

"The border."

He looked where I was looking and saw forest. "I don't see anything."

"You're not supposed to. That's rather the point of it." I started down the slope, boots sliding on frost-slick needles. "The Wolf Reaches don't announce themselves to people who aren't welcome. They just... decline to be found."

"And we're welcome?"

"Ask me again when we're either home or dead. It'll save time."

* * *

We'd been walking together for six weeks by then, which is long enough to learn a person's silences as well as their words. Soren Vale had a silence for regret—he carried it in his shoulders, hunched like a man perpetually ducking under a lintel too low for him. He had a silence for fear, which lived in his hands; they were always moving now, always touching things, bark and stone and the hilt of a sword he'd stopped drawing since Ilse at the ford. And he had a silence for me, which was the one I liked best and trusted least: the quiet of a man watching someone he'd decided not to lose.

"Tell me about them," he said as we walked. "The wolf-kin. What should I expect?"

"Honesty." I stepped over a root system like exposed ribs. "Brutal, ceremonial honesty. They'll want to know why you're here, what you've done, what you intend. Lying to the pack is—" I searched for the word. "It's like lying to a river while you're drowning in it. Technically possible. Deeply unwise."

"And you? What will they want from you?"

I kept walking. He let me keep not answering for about forty paces, which was generous of him, and then he said, gently, in the voice he'd learned to use on me the way you'd use a hand on a spooked horse:

"Yara."

"They'll want to know why I broke the covenant," I said. "And then they'll want to know what I'm willing to pay to mend it. And I don't have an answer for the second question that isn't—" I made myself say it, because we'd agreed somewhere on the moor, in the dark after the shepherd's hut, that we were done keeping secrets that mattered. "That isn't my life. So let's not rehearse it. Let's just get captured efficiently."

Soren was quiet for three steps. Then: "We'll find another way."

"We always think we will. It's one of humanity's better features and most of its problems."

* * *

They took us an hour before dusk, and honestly, I was almost flattered.

One moment the forest was empty. The next there were seven of them around us, and I mean *around*—no crashing through brush, no warning cry, just a circle of grey-cloaked figures that seemed to have grown up out of the ground like mushrooms after rain. Tall, broad, moving with that particular economy the wolf-kin have, every motion doing exactly one thing and no more. Their spears were old wood and new steel. Their eyes were amber, or near enough, and every single pair of them was fixed on me.

Soren's hand went to his sword. I put mine over his wrist.

"No," I murmured. "Please. Let me do the talking, and let's both survive my talking."

A she-wolf—I use the term loosely; wolf-kin are neither wolves nor women in any simple sense, but this one read female the way a storm reads east—stepped forward. She was younger than me, hard-muscled, with a scar splitting one eyebrow and hair the color of wet slate braided back tight. She looked at me the way you look at a door you've been told is locked, finding it open.

"You smell of Vellmark," she said. Her common tongue was accented, each word placed like a stone in a wall. "You smell of smoke and burned market. And underneath—" her nostrils flared, and something moved across her face that I couldn't read and didn't like, "—underneath you smell of *storm*. Of covenant. Of blood that used to walk here."

"I'm Yara Eriksen," I said. "Storm-caller. Exiled. Returning."

"I know what you are." She didn't blink. Wolf-kin rarely do, and it's unsettling in a way I'd forgotten. "The whole Reach knows what walks toward it. The birds told us. The river told us. The *stone* told us." She jerked her chin at Soren. "And that one?"

"That one is Soren Vale. Formerly royal inquisitor. Currently—" I glanced at him. "Currently a man trying to be worth the trouble he's caused."

"He wears the crown's coat."

"He wore it. There's a difference. Ask him yourself if you doubt it—the pack values honesty, doesn't it? Or is that only for guests?"

Something flickered at the edge of the sentries' formation—a huff that might have been laughter, quickly strangled. The scarred she-wolf didn't smile. But she tilted her head, considering, and I understood I'd passed some small test without knowing what it was.

"I am Ravn," she said. "Sentry of the North March. You come with us now. Bound, both of you. If you run, we will catch you. If you fight—" she looked at my hands, at the faint blue-white shimmer that never quite left my fingertips anymore, the frost-scars of six weeks' road, "—you will wish you hadn't, and so will we. Nobody wants to fight a storm-caller. It makes such a mess."

"Your diplomacy is impeccable," I said. "Bind away."

Rope went around my wrists—hemp, rough, deliberately ordinary—and around Soren's. He submitted with a stillness that cost him; I could see it costing him, the inquisitor's pride and the fugitive's terror wrestling behind his eyes. As they turned us north, deeper into the trees, he caught my gaze over the shoulder of the sentry between us.

*Well?* he mouthed.

*Home,* I mouthed back, and hated how little conviction I could put into it.

* * *

They marched us until full dark, which was unnecessary and therefore intentional. A courtesy would have camped us early. A cruelty would have marched us past exhaustion. This was theater: arrive at the heart of the Reach tired, cold, off-balance, and grateful for fire. I remembered the technique. I'd been on the receiving end of it once before, twenty years ago, a child of eleven being walked to judgment by adults who thought they were kind.

The pack's hold—if you could call it that; it was less a settlement than an agreement between buildings and trees—rose out of the dark in pieces. Turf-roofed longhouses sunk into hillsides. Firelight in gaps. Smoke that smelled of pine and roasting meat and, faintly, of herbs I knew: yarrow, and something bitter my mother used to boil for fevers. My throat tightened around nothing.

They put us in a roundhouse with a door of woven hazel and two guards outside who did not pretend not to be listening. Someone brought food—barley and venison, real salt, more generosity than prisoners deserved, which meant we weren't prisoners. We were something else. Something pending.

Soren ate mechanically. I couldn't eat at all.

"You knew that woman," he said finally. "Ravn. When she caught your scent. Something happened in her face."

"She smelled my mother on me." I wrapped my arms around my knees. The fire cracked and spat. "Wolf-kin noses are... archival. Everything a person ever was stays in the bloodline, layered like tree rings. Ravn must have known my mother. Which means she was here before the breaking. Which means she remembers what I did."

"You were eleven."

"The Reach doesn't count years the way we do. It counts wounds."

Soren set down his bowl. He crossed the small room and sat beside me, shoulder against mine, warm as a hearthstone, saying nothing—which was, by then, its own fluency between us. Six weeks of learning each other's silences, and this was the one I needed: the silence that simply stood next to the ache instead of trying to fix it.

It lasted until the hazel door opened.

* * *

Ravn came in alone, which surprised me. She crouched by the fire, fed it a knot of pine, and watched the flames take it before she spoke.

"My mother hunted with Sigrid Eriksen," she said. "Before your mother left. Before everything."

My breath went out of me like air from a punctured lung. "Sigrid."

"She taught me to read weather when I was small. Said I had the nose for it even if I lacked the blood." Ravn's profile was all planes and shadow in the firelight. "She said her daughter would come back someday. That storms always circle home eventually. The others called her a fool for it. Sentiment. Human taint." A pause. "She died defending the eastern shrine when the king's men came for the covenant-stone fragments. Eleven years ago. I held her as the light went."

I hadn't heard my mother's death spoken aloud in eleven years. In Vellmark, among humans, she was a story I'd buried so deep even I couldn't find the grave. Now a stranger dug it up with her bare hands and handed it to me still bleeding.

"Why tell me this?" I managed.

"Because tomorrow the council will try you, and they will speak of the broken covenant, and the stolen fragment, and the twenty years of dying land, and every word will be true." Ravn turned and looked at me straight-on, amber eyes catching firelight like a beast's. "And none of it will mention that Sigrid Eriksen loved you enough to curse the day you were born into her debt, and loved you enough to die believing you'd return to pay it. Both things are true. The council forgets the second kind of true. Someone in this room should remember it."

Soren made a small sound—not words, just acknowledgment, the sound of a man revising his opinion of someone upward at speed.

"Thank you," I said. It came out rough.

"Don't thank me." Ravn rose, smooth as water pouring uphill. "Tomorrow I stand witness against you, because truth requires it. And I stand witness *for* you, for the same reason. You won't enjoy either." At the door she paused, hand on the hazel weave. "One more thing, storm-caller. The stone that the council keeps—the fragment your mother hid, the one the king's men never found—it has been *singing* for six days. Since you crossed the last river. Fenris hears it. The whole Reach hears it." Her eyes found mine, and in them was neither warmth nor hatred but something harder than both: expectation. "Whatever you came here to do, the Reach already knows. Sleep well."

The door closed behind her.

* * *

Soren and I sat a long time in the fire's company. Eventually he said, "Six days. That's—that was the drowned shrine. The fragment you took from the river."

"It's been calling since I picked it up. I felt it. I hoped it was indigestion."

He laughed—one short, startled breath of it—and then pressed his knuckles to his mouth, because laughing felt obscene on the eve of a trial that might end in a death sentence. Mine, specifically. We both knew whose. We'd argued about it on the moor until arguing became its own kind of intimacy, each of us claiming the right to die for the other like children fighting over the last sweet. Neither of us had won. Both of us had lost sleep.

"Soren." I looked into the fire. "If the council demands the price tomorrow—if they name it aloud and accept nothing else—promise me you'll let me speak first. Promise me you won't do anything brave."

"You know I can't promise that."

"I know. I'm asking anyway. It's traditional."

He reached over and took my bound hands in his bound ones, rope scraping rope, and held on. Outside, far off across the dark Reach, something began to howl—one voice, then another, then a dozen, threading together into a sound that wasn't grief exactly and wasn't joy, but was the ancestor of both. Homecoming music. Or a war-song. With wolves, I reminded myself, it was always both.

"That's for you," Soren said quietly. "Isn't it. They're singing for the storm-caller coming home."

"Or they're singing for the traitor coming to judgment." I leaned my head against his shoulder and listened anyway, letting the sound pour into the hollow places twenty years had carved. "With this family, you never get to know which song it is until the last note."

The fire burned low. The singing went on. And somewhere in the dark between the longhouses, I would have sworn on my mother's grave that I heard—beneath the voices, beneath the wind, beneath even the old blood-smell of the ground—a fourth sound. Low. Patient. Amused, in the way of very old things.

Fenris, waking. Fenris, waiting.

Fenris, I realized with a chill that had nothing to do with frost, *counting the hours until the council gave him what he'd waited twenty years to collect.*
