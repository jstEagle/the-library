## 14. Judgment of the Pack

I have sat in judgment of my own mother. I have judged a man who ate his brother's share during the Hunger Winter and lied about it for thirty years. I once had to decide whether a cub who bit through the ear of the pack's eldest was guilty of insolence or simply being six years old.

None of it prepared me for judging Yara Eriksen.

Not because the case was hard. The case was, by our standards, simple: the covenant broke twenty years ago; someone must answer for it; here stands the one who walked away from the stone while it was still warm. Simple as arithmetic. What was not simple was watching them stand in the center of the council ring with their hands bound in wolf-braided cord, chin up, looking like they'd rehearsed dying and found it tedious.

And what was *not* simple at all was the human standing behind them, wearing an inquisitor's coat with the buttons torn off, glaring at every elder present as though we were the ones on trial.

"Ravn," Sigrun murmured beside me. "You're staring."

"I'm assessing," I said.

"You're staring."

Sigrun is my grandmother's age and has no sense of theater, which is why she is the best judge among us and why I will never let her narrate anything important. I turned back to the ring and did my job.

* * *

The Wolf Reaches do not have courtrooms. We have a clearing where the pines grow in a circle so old that no one remembers planting them, and the ground there is packed hard by centuries of feet—human feet, wolf feet, and the in-between kind like mine. The council sits on fallen logs arranged in a crescent. The accused stands in the middle, alone, which is supposed to feel like exposure but mostly just feels cold this time of year. Above us, the covenant-stone's broken twin rose out of the earth like a snapped tooth, half-swallowed by moss. You could still see where the old carvings ran up its face before the fracture. Wolves on one side. People on the other. A knot of joined hands in the middle, worn smooth by weather and by touching.

Yara looked at that knot of hands for a long time. I pretended not to notice. I am not good at pretending, but I have practice.

My name is Ravn, and I am what the Reaches call wolf-blooded—the child of a human woman who came north pregnant and a wolf-father she never named to anyone who asked. I have ears that hear both kinds of music and a face that belongs fully to neither country. It makes me useful as a sentry. It also means that when Yara Eriksen was dragged into camp, frostbitten and furious, and I caught the scent of their mother on their coat—old scent, ghost-scent, twenty years stale—I was the only one in the clearing whose chest hurt about it.

Because I remembered her. Maren Eriksen. She used to sing off-key around the fire and claim it was a human tradition called "melody." She taught me to whistle through two fingers when I was small enough that my hands were still mostly human-sized. And then one autumn she went south with her child and never came back, and the elders said *the covenant is broken*, and grief got filed away under politics, the way grief always does in places that can't afford to feel things.

Now her child stood in the judgment ring, and I was one of the judges.

Justice is a strange animal. It looks noble from a distance and smells like fear up close.

* * *

Elder Varg opened proceedings the way he opens everything: loudly, and slightly wrong.

"The accused," he boomed, "stands charged with the breaking of the covenant between the realms. With abandonment of the sacred charge. With—" he consulted a scrap of bark covered in charcoal marks, because apparently we'd developed paperwork since the last trial, "—with flight from duty, concealment of gift, and consorting with crown agents."

"That last one," Soren Vale said from the edge of the ring, "is me. I'm the consorting. I'd like that noted."

"Noted," Varg said, "and penalized, if you interrupt again."

I liked him immediately, which annoyed me, because I had decided to dislike him. Inquisitors don't get to be likable. Inquisitors are the reason half the scars in this camp exist. But there he stood, buttonless, unarmed, having surrendered every weapon at the treeline without being asked twice, positioning himself exactly where he could step into the ring if things went badly. He knew they might go badly. He stepped closer anyway.

Foolish man. Brave fool. There's a difference, and I've spent my whole life trying to find it.

"The evidence," Varg continued, "will now be heard."

Here is how the pack tries a thing: we don't cross-examine. We *remember*. The eldest among us walk the ring and speak what they witnessed, and the stone itself—if you press your palm to the broken twin and speak true—shows what it held. Memory is our law. It's crude and it's merciless and it has never once been fooled by a clever lawyer, because we don't have any.

Old Halvard went first. He'd been a young sentry the night the covenant broke, and his telling was all wind and panic: riders coming up from the south under a false banner of parley, lords in silver masks, and a child—a *child*—dragged toward the stone, crying, while the human lords chanted words that made the night bend wrong.

"They meant to force the renewal early," Halvard said. "To bind the storm to their bloodline instead of to the land. They needed a storm-caller young enough to shape. They brought their own sacrifice-child and told everyone it was consent."

The clearing was very quiet. Somewhere above, a crow commented, because crows have opinions about everything.

Then the stone. Varg pressed his palm to the broken twin and spoke the old words, and the moss lit faintly along the fracture lines, and what poured out wasn't sound exactly—it was memory given to whoever stood close. I stood close. I couldn't help it.

I saw it. All of us saw it.

A girl of maybe ten or eleven, in a white dress too thin for the mountain cold, being carried toward the stone by men who smelled of incense and lies. Her mother fighting through the crowd, screaming a name—*Yara, Yara*—before masked hands took her arms. The girl reaching back. The lords pressing the child's palms against the stone and speaking the binding words over her sobs, twisting the rite, forcing the covenant to accept a consent that had never been given. And the covenant—old, patient, half-asleep Fenris's great slow heart in the rock—*straining*, rejecting, cracking down its own seam rather than be bound to thieves—

And then the girl doing the only thing left to her. Pulling the storm into herself. Tearing the rite apart from the inside. Breaking the covenant herself, at ten years old, because the alternative was letting it be stolen.

She didn't run away that night. She ran *toward* her mother, and her mother threw her onto a horse and slapped its haunch and shouted *north, go north, don't stop* — and the horse went north, and the girl on it screamed the whole way down the mountain, and the scream was heard in three valleys, and wolves answered it without knowing why.

I had to look at the ground for a while after that. Judges are allowed. Nobody wrote that rule, but everybody uses it.

* * *

When the memory faded, Varg straightened and delivered the finding, and his voice had lost some of its boom.

"The accused did not break the covenant in betrayal. The accused broke the covenant in rescue. The accused was a child pawn of human lords." He paused. "There is no guilt here that the pack can name."

Yara's shoulders dropped half an inch. Soren Vale exhaled like a man surfacing.

And then Elder Sigrun, who is the best judge among us precisely because she never lets anyone off a hook, stood up and said the other thing.

"And yet."

The word landed in the clearing like a stone in still water.

"And yet the covenant remains broken," she said. "And yet the wild magic drains from the land season by season, and the storms go unruled, and the king's machines drink what's left. And yet the price of renewal has not changed, because prices set by gods do not negotiate. One life, freely given, from each realm. That was the bargain before the theft. That is the bargain now." She looked at Yara, and her eyes were old and kind and absolutely without mercy, which is the specific combination you want in a judge and never want in a friend. "We find you innocent of the crime, Yara Eriksen. We cannot find you free of the debt."

"Innocent," Soren repeated, flat. "You just said—"

"We said justice found nothing to punish," Sigrun said. "Debt is not punishment. Debt is arithmetic. The stone cracked because a child was forced. The stone will mend only when someone comes to it *unforced*. That is the whole of the law, human, and it is older than your king, older than your crown, older than the lie your courts tell about mercy." She spread her hands. "One life. Freely given. From the realm of men, to balance the realm of men's betrayal. And one from ours, when the hour comes—we do not hide our half of it."

Silence. The kind with weight.

Yara laughed. It was not a happy laugh. It was the laugh of someone who has been carrying a thing for twenty years and has just been told the thing weighs exactly what they thought it weighed.

"I know," they said. "I've known since the river shrine. I came north to pay it."

"No," Soren said.

"Soren—"

"*No.*" He stepped into the ring proper, past the boundary mark, and two sentries moved and Sigrun raised one finger and they stopped, because even fools get one moment of honesty. "You were a child. They used you. You spent twenty years hiding in a city that outlawed what you are, calming storms for strangers, and now you've walked a hundred leagues to die on a rock, and you're calling that justice? It's not justice. It's just the same sacrifice with better manners."

"It's the price," Yara said quietly. "It doesn't care what I call it."

"Then we'll find another way."

"There is no other way. I read the shrine. I read the stone. There is *no other way*." Their voice cracked on it, and I watched something pass between them—not anger, worse than anger, the thing underneath anger—and I thought, *oh*, because I have good ears and I had been hearing it for days without naming it. The way they argued like people who had already lost the argument and were only fighting about who would survive losing it.

Sigrun saw it too. Judges see everything; it's the occupational disease.

"The debt stands," she said gently. "But hear this, Yara Eriksen, and hear it well: freely given means *freely*. No one may push you onto the stone, and no one may pull you off it either. Not the pack. Not him. When you choose, it must be yours alone, or the covenant will crack again, and next time there may be no one left to mend it." She turned to Soren. "And you, human. You heard the terms. Grief is not a legal argument. If you mean to stand beside them, stand beside them—but do not stand *between* them and the stone. Nothing stands between the gifted and their debt. That road ends in ruin for everyone."

Soren's jaw worked. He looked at Yara. Yara looked at the knot of joined hands worn smooth on the broken stone.

"I need to think," Yara said.

"Then think," Sigrun said. "The pack grants you until the moon rises full. Three nights. After that, the stone waits no longer—the king's machines are drinking the land dry even as we sit here, and a mended covenant cannot wait on human hesitation forever."

* * *

They were led out—walked out, really, no one touched them; the cord had come off the moment the verdict was read—and I followed, because sentries follow, and because I wanted to say a thing and hadn't figured out how.

I caught them at the edge of camp, where the pines thinned and you could see south across the darkening moorland, a smudge of woodsmoke on the horizon that might have been the king's army and might have been weather.

"Yara Eriksen," I said.

They turned. Up close they looked tired in the way stones look tired, which is to say permanently and without complaint.

"Your mother," I said. "Maren. She sang off-key and claimed it was a human tradition. She taught me to whistle through two fingers. She used to say—" and here my voice did something embarrassing, so I finished fast, "—she used to say the border wasn't a wall, it was a bridge nobody maintained. She'd have burned the whole council ring down before she let you stand on that stone alone."

Yara stared at me. Frost bloomed briefly on the pine bark beside their hand, little ferns of it, unconscious, the way other people blush.

"She talked about the Reaches," they said finally. "All those years. I thought she was remembering a place that had rejected her."

"She never felt rejected. She felt *late*. Like she'd left a fire untended." I shrugged, which in wolf-body-language is an apology and a hug compressed into one gesture. "For what it's worth—the pack found you innocent. My mother would have found you innocent. The stone doesn't care about innocence. But I do, and I'm the one standing guard outside your door tonight, so sleep, if you can."

They almost smiled. "And if I can't?"

"Then whistle through two fingers," I said. "I'll come."

* * *

That was three nights ago, by the reckoning I keep. Tonight is the second night, and the moon is nearly fat, and I am writing this by firelight because the elders say a record should be kept of judgments, even unfinished ones, especially unfinished ones.

Here is what I know that the record doesn't show:

Soren Vale spent today in the forge-shed with our smith, asking questions no inquisitor should ask—about the stone, about the rite, about whether the covenant reads intent or merely flesh. The smith thinks he's curious. I don't think he's curious. I think he's planning something, and I think Yara knows it, and I think that's why Yara hasn't slept and keeps looking at him the way you look at a door you're afraid will open.

And here is the other thing. An hour ago, a sentry came down from the ridge at a dead run, and what she carried was not a report but a smell: iron, oil, and burnt magic, riding the south wind in quantities that mean an army.

The king has come north. And he has brought a machine that drinks the world.

The council is summoning Yara now. The stone, it seems, will not wait for the third night after all.
